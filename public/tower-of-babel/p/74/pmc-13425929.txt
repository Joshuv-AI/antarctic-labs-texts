Effects of sexual health education during pregnancy on sexual myths, sexual function, and quality of sexual life: a randomized controlled study
Authors: Zehra İncedal Sonkaya, Emine Aksüt Akçay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the impact of sexual health training on sexual myths, sexual function, and the quality of sexual life among pregnant women.MethodsThe study employed a pretest-posttest design. Participants were randomly assigned to either the intervention group (n = 45) or the control group (n = 45). Both groups completed a sociodemographic data form, a questionnaire on myths about sexual activity during pregnancy, the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI), and the Sexual Quality of Life-Female (SQOL-F) questionnaire.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 27.58 ± 5.36 years, and the mean gestational age was 35.14 ± 5.52 weeks. After receiving sexual health training, women in the intervention group showed an increase in their SQOL-F scores and a decrease in their belief in sexual myths (p < 0.05). No significant differences were observed between the groups regarding educational level, desire for the current pregnancy, and the frequency of sexual intercourse before and during pregnancy (p > 0.05).ConclusionsThe sexual function of the participants was found to be inadequate. However, after receiving sexual health/reproductive health training, their sexual quality of life and sexual function scores increased. Furthermore, their attitudes towards sexual myths significantly changed in a positive direction. These findings indicate the importance of sexual health/reproductive health training during pregnancy.Trial registrationClinical Trials NCT06735677. Date of registration 11.12.2024 (Retrospectively registered).Keywords: Pregnancy, Sexual health education, Sexual myths, Female sexual function, Sexual quality of life

Introduction

Sexuality is a lifelong concept that begins in the intrauterine period and continues throughout life, encompassing both physical and psychological dimensions. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), sexuality integrates physical, emotional, intellectual, and social aspects, contributing to personal enrichment, communication, and expressions of love. As an integral part of humans, sexuality represents a state of health that fosters not only physical well-being but also emotional, intellectual, and social harmony, promoting personal development, meaningful communication, and a positive sharing of love [1]. Sexual behavior, on the other hand, is a lifelong natural process that involves the entire body rather than being limited to the reproductive organs. Sexuality is a psychophysiological experience shaped by societal and individual attitudes, beliefs, behaviors, emotions, and personality traits, with notable individual variations [2–4]. This randomized controlled study was conducted among pregnant women in Turkey, where cultural beliefs and attitudes toward sexuality during pregnancy may influence sexual myths, sexual behavior, and perceptions of sexual health.

Sexual functions involve a complex interplay of activities in which all bodily systems, particularly the central nervous system, play a crucial role. Consequently, various factors affecting the body, such as illnesses, medications, and life stages characterized by significant hormonal changes, including pregnancy and menopause, can influence sexuality. Psychological conditions like depression also have the potential to impact sexual functions [5, 6].

Sexual dysfunction is more prevalent among pregnant women compared to non-pregnant women. Studies evaluating sexuality during pregnancy consistently show that sexual life is negatively impacted. Pregnant women, particularly in the third trimester, often avoid sexual activity due to misconceptions and fears about harming the baby [7–9]. A study conducted with pregnant women revealed that 80.1% reported changes in their sexual life during pregnancy, with the most common changes being a decrease in sexual desire and frequency of intercourse (47.3%), avoiding intercourse due to fear of harming the baby (19.8%), and experiencing pain during intercourse (5.1%). The study also found that women with pre-existing sexual problems continued to face similar issues during pregnancy, negatively affecting their sexual quality of life [10].

Although sexuality does not have a vital role in human survival, sexuality has a significant role among the factors that make up the quality of life [11]. WHO defines quality of life as “an individual’s perception of their position in life within the context of the culture and value systems in which they live, and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards, and concerns.” Building on this definition, the quality of sexual life can be described as one’s perception of their sexual life within the framework of their culture and value system, shaped by their personal goals, expectations, standards, and experiences. Sexuality is a key component of the psychological and physiological dimensions of quality of life. On the other hand, quality of life and sexuality mutually influence each other [12]. The quality of sexual life can be defined as a holistic reflection of the positive and negative aspects of sexual life and sexual health, both of which are shaped by various factors.

Sexual myths influencing sexual life during pregnancy are unfounded beliefs rooted in false, incomplete, or distorted information, lacking any scientific basis [13, 14]. Almost all sexual myths are based on negative beliefs, particularly the idea that sexual intercourse during pregnancy can be harmful, which in turn negatively impacts the quality of sexual life. According to a study by Khalesi et al., fears of harming the fetus and concerns that intercourse may lead to miscarriage or bleeding, cause pregnant women to avoid sexual activity, further diminishing their sexual quality of life [15]. A lack of sexual education and information, along with the inability to openly discuss sexual issues within the family or society, are key factors contributing to the formation of sexual myths. Cantarino et al. noted that women often interrupt their sexual activity during pregnancy, particularly due to social norms and religious beliefs passed down from their mothers [16]. In a similar study, Alizadeh et al. found that the sexual functions of pregnant women were negatively affected by the cultural structure of society and false beliefs [17].

Factors such as decreased libido in the third trimester, feelings of sexual inadequacy and weakness, fatigue, negative body image due to physical changes, fear of not being attractive to a partner, pelvic ligament pain, frequent urination, reduced mobility, breast milk leakage, strong uterine contractions during intercourse, and discomfort during orgasm all have a significant negative impact on the quality of sexual life [18]. Additionally, as the due date approaches, concerns such as the risk of infection, anxiety about harming the baby, fear of childbirth pain, and worries about potential complications lead fathers to also avoid sexual activity during this period, adopting similar attitudes as their partners [19–21]. However, the literature indicates that there is no issue with continuing sexual intercourse until the last day of pregnancy unless a medical condition is identified by the physician [22]. It has been suggested that increasing knowledge on the subject may help reduce couples’ anxiety, improve the quality of sexual life during pregnancy, and strengthen the physical and emotional bond between partners [23–25]. Consequently, a holistic approach should be adopted when supporting couples in maintaining their sexual life during pregnancy by evaluating them and identifying their problems and needs. The literature review indicates that women often do not receive training on sexual health, sexual function, and the quality of sexual life during pregnancy -the most significant period in a woman’s life- due to the fact that sexuality is a taboo in Turkish society and is viewed as a private matter. For this reason, all interventions and education during pregnancy should address sexual functioning and the quality of sexual life for couples. Sexual counseling should be offered to all pregnant women during the antenatal period, regardless of their risk status. Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of sexual and reproductive health training on sexual myths, sexual function, and the quality of sexual life.

Research hypotheses

The following hypotheses were tested in this study:

H1: Pregnant women who receive sexual and reproductive health education will demonstrate significantly lower levels of belief in sexual myths compared with those in the control group.

H1: Pregnant women who receive sexual and reproductive health education will demonstrate significantly lower levels of belief in sexual myths compared with those in the control group.

H2: Pregnant women who receive sexual and reproductive health education will have significantly higher sexual function scores (FSFI) than those in the control group.

H2: Pregnant women who receive sexual and reproductive health education will have significantly higher sexual function scores (FSFI) than those in the control group.

H3: Pregnant women who receive sexual and reproductive health education will report significantly higher sexual quality of life scores (SQOL-F) than those in the control group.

H3: Pregnant women who receive sexual and reproductive health education will report significantly higher sexual quality of life scores (SQOL-F) than those in the control group.

Material and methods

The study was designed as a randomized controlled trial involving pregnant women who attended a pregnancy school at a city hospital.

The sample size was determined using the G*Power software for power analysis. For a moderate effect size, with a minimum power of 0.90 and a margin of error of 0.05, it was calculated that a total of 90 participants were required, with 45 in the intervention group and 45 in the control group. To account for potential data loss, 50 pregnant women were included in each group. Data were collected by the researchers through face-to-face interviews.

Collection and recording of data

The data for this study were collected using a sociodemographic questionnaire, a questionnaire on myths about sexual activity during pregnancy, The Sexual Quality of Life-Female (SQOL-F) questionnaire, and The Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI).

The Sexual Quality of Life-Female (SQOL-F) questionnaire was developed by Symonds et al. to assess the quality of sexual life in women [26]. The Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient of the original scale was 0.95. The scale was adapted into Turkish by Tuğut and Gölbaşı [27]. The questionnaire is a Likert-type scale comprising 18 items within a single factor. Higher scores on the scale indicate a higher quality of sexual life.

The Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) was developed by Rosen et al. to evaluate female sexual function [28]. The index assesses the status of sexual function or problems over the past four weeks. The FSFI is a multidimensional scale consisting of 19 items. The validity and reliability study for the Turkish version of the index was conducted by Aygin and Aslan. The scale includes six subscales namely Desire, Subjective Arousal, Lubrication, Orgasm, Satisfaction, and Pain. The total score ranges from 2 to 36, with higher scores indicating better maintenance of sexual function. The cut-off point for the total score is 26.55. Accordingly, scores at or below this threshold indicate female sexual dysfunction [29].

Implementation of the study

Data collection forms were administered to 100 pregnant women who attended the pregnancy school of a city hospital in a province of Turkey and agreed to participate in the study. The participants were then randomly assigned by lot into intervention (n = 50) and control (n = 50) groups. Pregnant women in the intervention group received four training sessions over one month. These sessions covered reproductive health, sexual activity during pregnancy and the postpartum period, personal and sexual hygiene, domestic violence during pregnancy, and misconceptions about pregnancy. Following the completion of the training sessions, the Sexual Quality of Life-Female (SQOL-F) questionnaire and the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) were re-administered to all participants for final evaluation. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before the study commenced. The study was completed with 90 participants (45 in the intervention group and 45 in the control group) (Fig. 1).

Educational intervention

Pregnant women in the intervention group received a structured sexual and reproductive health education program over a one-month period. The program consisted of four sessions, delivered once weekly, and all sessions were conducted face-to-face by the researchers. The educational intervention was planned in accordance with the specific needs of pregnancy and the postpartum period and was based on evidence-based information.

The educational sessions covered the following topics:

Baseline measurements were obtained prior to the initiation of the educational program. Following the completion of the four-session intervention, post-intervention assessments were conducted using the same data collection instruments and procedures as those applied at baseline.

After the completion of the study and data collection, participants in the control group were also offered the same educational intervention in accordance with ethical principles. This approach was adopted to ensure ethical fairness and equal access to the educational content for all participants following the conclusion of the trial.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean, standard deviation, median, frequency, and percentage. The normality of quantitative data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and graphical analysis. For variables with a normal distribution, the independent samples t-test was used to compare two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons across multiple groups. For within-group comparisons, the paired samples t-test was used for quantitative data, and the Wilcoxon signed-ranks test was applied for items related to sexual myths during pregnancy. Relationships between quantitative variables were analyzed using Pearson correlation analysis. Categorical data were compared using the Pearson chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, and the Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test. The internal consistency of scale items was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Pregnancy is a period during which women often distance themselves from sexual activities. Pregnancy-related changes, such as nausea, vomiting, and fatigue in the first trimester, as well as mechanical changes in the second and third trimesters including fetal growth, fetal head engagement in the pelvis, pelvic pain due to musculoskeletal adjustments, and breathing difficulties contribute to this withdrawal from sexuality. In our study, the sexual functioning of pregnant women was found to be inadequate, with mean scores of 15.56 ± 7.77 in the intervention group and 14.05 ± 9.67 in the control group. In our study, we aimed to enhance the sexual functioning of pregnant women through sexual and reproductive health training. Although the sexual function scores of women in the intervention group showed a slight improvement after the training, the difference was not statistically significant. This finding may be explained by the predominance of participants in the third trimester of pregnancy, a period characterized by increased physical discomfort, fatigue, hormonal changes, and psychological concerns, all of which may limit improvements in sexual function despite educational interventions. In addition, the relatively short duration of the intervention may have been insufficient to produce measurable changes in sexual function. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, suggesting that improvements in sexual function during pregnancy may require longer-term interventions or approaches that also involve partners. This outcome may be attributed to the fact that the participants were in their third trimester, a stage characterized by significant physical and psychological challenges, and the training duration was limited. Consistently, a study conducted in Thailand reported no statistically significant difference between the intervention and control groups regarding sexual behavior and sexual function following training [30]. On the other hand, a single-group study conducted in Iran by Mangeli et al. demonstrated that educational interventions positively influenced marital satisfaction among pregnant women, with a notable improvement observed compared to pre-education levels [31]. Another intervention study reported an improvement in the sexual functioning of pregnant women following the provided training [32]. These findings indicate that sexual and reproductive health education contributes to improvements in sexual function, suggesting a positive correlation between education and enhanced sexual functioning [33, 34].

Studies conducted in Turkey on sexual dysfunction during pregnancy have reported that a significant proportion of pregnant women (63–91.8%) experience sexual dysfunction [7, 35, 36]. In a study conducted by Gürgün and Bal involving 400 pregnant women, the total FSFI score was reported as 24.33 ± 7.68, indicating inadequate sexual function among the participants [37]. Similarly, another study also highlighted that pregnant women exhibited inadequate sexual function (17.56 ± 2.01) [38]. Fuchs et al. evaluated sexual function during pregnancy and found that sexual function scores declined as the gestational week advanced. They found that the FSFI total score decreased from 26.1 ± 6.1 in the first trimester to 25.9 ± 8.7 in the second trimester, and further to 22.7 ± 8.7 in the third trimester [39]. Furthermore, in a meta-analysis covering studies from 1960 to 2010, Serati et al. found that the majority of the studies they reviewed reported a significant decline in both female sexual function and frequency of sexual activity during pregnancy [40]. The evaluation of female sexual dysfunction among pregnant participants in both groups, based on sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics, revealed that factors such as employment status, education level, planned pregnancy, number of pregnancies, and gestational week did not significantly affect female sexual dysfunction. These findings are consistent with previous literature [41, 42].

Women’s sexual needs, attitudes towards sexuality, and the quality of their sexual life undergo significant changes throughout the pregnancy process. In the current study, the mean score for sexual quality of life in the intervention group was 51.46 ± 10.89, while the control group had a mean score of 72.27 ± 13.89 prior to the intervention. After the pregnant women in the intervention group received sexual and reproductive health training, their sexual quality of life scores were reassessed. The difference between the posttest and pretest scores for the intervention group was 16.96 ± 16.5, whereas the control group showed a score difference of 6.05 ± 9.37. This difference after the training was found to be statistically significant (p < 0.001). The number of studies focusing on the SQOL-F questionnaire is limited in the literature. However, existing studies show that the quality of sexual life tends to decrease during pregnancy and this decline is attributed to changes in sexual function, such as reduced sexual pleasure and satisfaction, which are commonly observed during pregnancy [43, 44]. The SQOL-F scores in the literature show some variation. For instance, Çavuş and Beyazıt reported a mean score of 64.9, while Gökyıldız Surucu et al. found a mean score of 72.79 ± 15.02. The differences in these findings can be attributed to various factors, including differences in study designs, the follow-up period for pregnant women, and cultural variations [42, 45]. In the current study, however, sexual myths and sexual health/reproductive health education provided to the intervention group led to a significant improvement in the sexual quality of life score.

The concept of sexuality is considered taboo in Turkish culture, and discussing it within families is often avoided. This issue is not unique to Turkey, as many countries worldwide face similar challenges regarding open discussions about sexual health. Previous studies have shown that sexual myths, particularly during pregnancy, have a negative impact on sexual quality of life and sexual function [46–54]. Sexual myths are influenced by various obstetric factors, such as the number of pregnancies and the gestational week. According to the literature, the frequency of sexual intercourse is significantly lower among women experiencing their first pregnancy compared to those with multiple pregnancies, which may be attributed to a greater fear of harming the fetus during sexual activity. However, after receiving training on reproductive and sexual health, pregnant women’s perceptions of sexual myths show positive changes. When pregnant women understand that sexual intercourse during pregnancy is not harmful, shameful, or incorrect, their attitudes toward sexual myths shift significantly. A study conducted with Nigerian women revealed that belonging to the Anglican Christian denomination heightened concerns about the safety of sexual activity during pregnancy, which was identified as a primary factor contributing to reduced sexual activity during this period [53]. An experimental study conducted with Iranian pregnant women reported the positive impact of group sexual counseling on improving attitudes toward sexual issues and reconstructing traditional perceptions of sexual activity during pregnancy. They found that the mean score changes for sexual attitudes and traditional perceptions were significantly higher in the intervention group compared to the control group (p < 0.0001) [51].

Conclusions and recommendations

This study evaluated the effect of sexual/reproductive health education on sexual myths, sexual function, and quality of sexual life among pregnant women. It concluded that participants had inadequate sexual function, but their sexual quality of life scores improved following the education, while changes in sexual function were limited. Furthermore, attitudes toward sexual myths changed positively and significantly. These findings underscore the importance of providing sexual/reproductive health education. Health professionals offering prenatal care should incorporate evidence-based education to promote healthy sexual lives, as harmful beliefs can negatively affect relationships. Future research should explore sexual health further through quantitative studies with larger samples and qualitative studies that enable couples to express themselves more freely.

Limitations

This study was conducted exclusively with women attending a pregnancy school at a city hospital in a single province. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution and may not be generalizable to pregnant women in different healthcare settings, cultural contexts, or geographical regions. Additionally, as the study included only pregnant women and did not involve their spouses or partners, the potential influence of partner-related attitudes, beliefs, and sexual behaviors on sexual function and the quality of sexual life could not be evaluated. The study assessed only the sexual myths included in the questionnaire. Finally, as this study employed a quantitative design, it could not fully capture the participants’ subjective experiences, emotions, and contextual factors related to sexuality during pregnancy. Future studies using qualitative or mixed-method approaches may provide deeper insights into these aspects. Another limitation of this study is that the clinical trial was registered retrospectively. Although ethical committee approval and institutional permission were obtained prior to the initiation of the study and the research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, prospective trial registration could not be completed. This issue should be considered when interpreting the findings.
