Nanotechnology in Dermatology: A Comprehensive Narrative Review of Performance, Safety, and Clinical Translation
Authors: Mohammed Saleh Al‐Dhubaibi, Ghada Farouk Mohammed, Lina Mohammed Atef, Saleh Salem Bahaj, Ahmed Mohammed Al‐Dhubaibi, Ahmad Mohammed Bukhari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNanotechnology has emerged as a transformative approach in dermatology, offering potential for enhanced drug delivery, targeted therapy, and improved bioavailability for various skin conditions. However, despite significant research output, translational barriers persist due to heterogeneous study designs, safety concerns, and regulatory inconsistencies, which limit clinical adoption.AimThis narrative review aims to synthesize evidence on the performance and safety of dermatology‐relevant nanocarriers, identify methodological and standardization gaps hindering clinical translation, and outline regulatory and clinical implications for the near‐term adoption of nanotechnology in dermatological practice.MethodsA narrative review was conducted covering the period 2015–2026. Data sources included PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar. Search strategies employed terms related to dermatology, nanotechnology, nanoparticle types, and applications. Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts, extracting data on nanoparticle class, indication, model systems, endpoints, safety signals, and regulatory notes. Thematic synthesis was performed with signal‐strength grading; quantitative pooling was not performed due to heterogeneous study designs.ResultsAnalysis of 128 studies revealed three primary nanoparticle classes with distinct profiles. Metallic nanoparticles (TiO₂, ZnO, Ag, Au) demonstrate size‐dependent skin penetration (10–200 nm) with TiO₂/ZnO showing minimal systemic absorption (epidermal retention 0.47–0.53 μg/cm²; systemic < 5 μg/L) while silver exhibits dose‐dependent cytotoxicity above 6–25 μg/mL. Polymeric nanoparticles (PLGA, chitosan) enable controlled release (20–300 nm) with improved delivery for psoriasis and acne. Lipid‐based carriers (SLNs, NLCs, liposomes) enhance barrier repair and reduce irritation in atopic dermatitis. Condition‐specific benefits include enhanced follicular targeting in acne, improved plaque penetration in psoriasis, and antimicrobial pro‐healing effects in wounds. However, stability issues such as oxidative rancidity, polymorphic transitions, and microbial growth compromise clinical reliability. Regulatory frameworks diverge significantly, with the EU mandating pre‐market notification and nano‐labeling while the US relies on manufacturer‐driven assessment. Emerging technologies, including stimuli‐responsive systems, exosome‐based therapies, and AI‐personalized formulations, show promise but lack clinical validation.ConclusionNanotechnology significantly enhances dermatological therapeutics through improved bioavailability and targeted delivery, yet translational barriers persist, including formulation instability, heterogeneous methodologies, and insufficient long‐term safety data. Standardized characterization protocols, physiologically relevant toxicity models using organ‐on‐chip platforms, and international regulatory harmonization are urgently needed. Integration of artificial intelligence for formulation optimization and digital surveillance for post‐market safety monitoring offers potential pathways to accelerate safe innovation. Addressing these gaps will enable nano‐dermatology to fulfill its transformative potential for millions of patients with chronic skin conditions worldwide.

Introduction

Nanotechnology has contributed to advances in dermatology, leading to the emergence of nano‐dermatology, which utilizes nanoparticles for various applications, including therapy, diagnostics, and drug delivery [1]. Nanocarriers may improve bioavailability, stability, and targeting in certain contexts, although clinical superiority over optimized conventional formulations is not consistently demonstrated [2, 3].

Beyond cosmetic applications, nanotechnology has been investigated in phototherapy, wound healing, and the treatment of dermatological cancers [4]. However, the widespread use of nanotechnology raises important safety and regulatory concerns, as the long‐term effects of nanoparticle exposure and systemic absorption have yet to be fully investigated [5]. Studies utilizing quantitative dermal absorption models suggest minimal systemic exposure for nanoparticles like nano‐titanium dioxide (TiO₂) and zinc oxide (ZnO) in intact skin, yet significant uncertainties persist about their potential cytotoxicity [6, 7].

Debates continue over the efficacy of nanoparticles in penetrating intact versus compromised skin, with inconsistencies reported across different model types. Factors such as particle composition, vehicle, and exposure conditions contribute to these discrepancies [8]. Hence, delineating risk–benefit profiles for specific indications and formulations is vital, while emphasizing the need for standardized protocols and post‐market surveillance to ensure the safe and effective application of nanotechnology in dermatological practices [9].

Research Objectives

This study aims to (i) synthesize evidence of performance and safety of dermatology‑relevant nanocarriers; (ii) identify methodological and standardization gaps hindering translation; and (iii) outline regulatory and clinical implications for near‑term adoption.

Methodology

Design and Rationale

Narrative review (2015–2026) was selected due to the heterogeneity of study designs/endpoints, mixed preclinical/clinical/regulatory evidence, and rapid innovation that limit formal meta‑analysis (Figure 1).

Flowchart of study selection and thematic synthesis process.

Data Sources

PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore (materials/engineering relevant to skin delivery), Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, and selected high‐impact dermatology journals.

Eligibility

English‑language original research and reviews focused on dermatologic applications of nanotechnology; excluded: pre‑2015, non‑dermatologic focus, commentaries without primary or synthesis data.

Search Strategy

(dermatology OR skin OR cutaneous) AND (nanotechnology* OR nanoparticle* OR nanocarrier* OR liposome* OR micelle* OR dendrimer* OR nanosponge* OR “solid lipid nanoparticle*” OR “nanostructured lipid carrier*” OR “titanium dioxide” OR “zinc oxide”) AND (delivery OR transdermal OR efficacy OR safety OR toxicity OR photoprotection OR imaging).

Screening and Extraction

Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and full texts; disagreements were resolved by consensus. A structured matrix was used to capture platform class, indication, model (in vitro/in vivo/human), endpoints, safety signals, and regulatory notes.

Synthesis

Thematic synthesis by platform × indication with signal‑strength grading (preclinical only/early human/clinical practice evidence). No quantitative pooling.

Bias and Limitations

Potential selection and language bias (English‑only; database coverage), publication bias, and heterogeneous outcomes may influence conclusions; these are addressed in Section 7.

This flowchart illustrates the systematic approach to literature selection and data synthesis in this narrative review. From an initial identification of 1284 records, two independent reviewers performed title, abstract, and full‐text screening, resulting in 128 included studies. Data extraction captured platform class, indication, model systems, endpoints, safety signals, and regulatory notes. Thematic synthesis was organized by nanoparticle platform—metallic, polymeric, lipid‐based, and emerging technologies—followed by condition‐specific analysis, regulatory assessment, gap identification, and future directions for clinical translation.

Basic Principles of Nanotechnology and Its Application in Dermatology

Nanotechnology, which operates at the molecular and atomic level (1–100 nm), has profoundly impacted various scientific fields, particularly medicine, leading to the emergence of nanomedicine [10]. In dermatology, it enhances drug delivery, bioavailability, and therapeutic effects, especially through the development of nano‐cosmeceuticals that offer cosmetic and functional protection against environmental factors like UV radiation [11]. By 2026, over 500 nanotechnology‐based skincare products were marketed. Notable nanoparticles, such as TiO₂ and ZnO, are commonly used in sunscreens due to their effective UV protection without leaving a visible residue [4]. Innovations in transdermal delivery systems, including nanocarriers, have further improved the stability and penetration of active agents in dermatological treatments [12].

However, safety and long‐term effects pose significant concerns. Evaluating the biocompatibility, local toxicity, and potential systemic absorption of nanomaterials is crucial for consumer safety [13]. Studies indicate negligible systemic absorption of nano‐TiO₂/ZnO while raising cytotoxicity concerns regarding silver nanoparticles at higher doses. Ongoing research is exploring nanotechnology's role in treating scalp and hair diseases, expanding its dermatological applications [14, 15, 16]. As the field evolves, implementing robust regulatory policies and safety assessments will be essential to optimize the benefits of nano‐dermatology while minimizing associated risks [17]. A comparative analysis of nanoparticle classes reveals their diverse properties, with metallic nanoparticles offering antimicrobial and photoprotective benefits, while polymeric and lipid‐based systems focus on controlled release and compatibility, providing a framework for evaluating clinical suitability in dermal applications [18] (Table 1).

Nanoparticles Used in Dermatological Products

Nanotechnology has significantly impacted cosmetic technology and dermatology by utilizing the unique properties of nanoparticles to enhance therapeutic effectiveness, safety, and targeted applications [19]. Commonly used metallic nanoparticles such as gold, silver, copper, platinum, TiO₂, and ZnO are integral in skin care products and treatments due to their Localized Surface Plasmon Resonance (LSPR) properties, which improve light absorption, fluorescence, and electrical conductivity [20].

These nanoparticles support advanced drug delivery, biosensing, and antimicrobial uses; for example, silver nanoparticles are incorporated into wound ointments for their ability to produce reactive radicals that help eliminate bacteria [21]. However, practical application is challenged by issues such as batch‐to‐batch variability, immunogenicity (influenced by surface chemistry), and inconsistent findings regarding dermal penetration and local cytotoxicity [22]. While silver's antimicrobial properties are noteworthy, they raise concerns regarding resistance and toxicity associated with ion release. Safety profiles for TiO₂ and ZnO differ based on factors like coating and crystal phase [23]. The LSPR effect also facilitates photothermal therapy, where nanomaterials generate localized heat to eliminate harmful pathogens while sparing healthy tissue [24]. Additionally, their ability to scavenge UV‐induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) mitigates skin damage and may increase resistance to diseases, emphasizing their value in sunscreen formulations [25].

Beyond metal‐based systems, polymeric nanoparticles have gained popularity in the cosmetic and pharmaceutical sectors for their controlled drug delivery capabilities and low cytotoxicity. These polymeric nanoparticles enhance the stability of bioactive components and shield them from degradation, thus improving dermatological treatment outcomes. Polymelamine‐based nanoparticles, in particular, have received attention due to their stability and potential to increase the bioavailability of active ingredients [26]. Similarly, lipid‐based nanoparticles are widely used in sunscreens, anti‐aging creams, and therapeutic formulations, offering improved skin permeability and hydration [27]. Despite the advantages of nanotechnology in dermatological preparations, ongoing research and regulatory scrutiny address safety concerns regarding nanoparticle penetration, deposition, and long‐term effects [28] (Table 2).

Transfersomal or liposomal calcipotriol–betamethasone systems enhanced plaque penetration in psoriasis and lowered required steroid exposure in early clinical studies [30].

Acyclovir nanogels and thermoresponsive polymer micelles increased local bioavailability for recurrent HSV lesions while enabling less frequent dosing schedules [31]. Silver‑ or nitric‑oxide‐releasing nano‑systems demonstrated activity against biofilm‑forming bacteria in chronic wounds, while shell‑coated Ag limited cytotoxicity to keratinocytes [32]. Minoxidil or finasteride delivered in NLCs increased follicular deposition for androgenetic alopecia and reduced propylene‑glycol‐related irritation seen with legacy solutions [33]. Hydroquinone/kojic acid/azelaic acid in NLC or liposomal formats improved epidermal targeting for hyperpigmentation with lower irritation versus alcohol‑heavy creams [34]. Lipid‐based nanoparticles

Lipid nanoparticles, particularly SLNs and NLCs, demonstrate significant advantages in dermatological drug delivery by enhancing the penetration of active agents into the skin while maintaining their integrity due to their non‐toxic and biocompatible nature [39]. However, the real‐world effectiveness of SLNs/NLCs can be affected by factors such as polymorphic transitions, oxidative rancidity, and microbial growth in aqueous environments, which may impact penetration kinetics and safety profiles. Furthermore, in conditions like acne‐prone or seborrheic skin, increased occlusion might worsen comedogenesis—a factor often overlooked in trials [40].

To ensure safe and effective applications of these nanoparticles, harmonized stability testing—involving thermal, photostability, and freeze‐thaw evaluations—along with in‐use microbiological assessments are essential. The primary composition of SLNs and NLCs consists of physiological lipids like triglycerides and phospholipids, which effectively restore and reinforce the skin barrier while prolonging drug release, thus enhancing therapeutic effects [39].

Recent innovations in lipid‐based nanoparticles have led to modifications in lipid matrices and the introduction of “helper” ingredients or unique designs like photoresponsive and core‐shell nanoparticles, which optimize drug localization and retention on the skin [41]. Despite these advancements, challenges in large‐scale production persist due to issues with formulation reproducibility, stability, oxidative degradation, microbial contamination, and potential skin toxicity. Overcoming these obstacles necessitates progress in manufacturing techniques and analytical methodologies, including x‐ray diffraction and thermal analysis [42].

Researchers are now pursuing novel biodegradable and biocompatible lipid‐based nanocarrier systems aimed at improving the safety and efficacy of dermatological treatments [43, 44]. These lipid nanoparticles are integrated into topical formulations like creams and gels, significantly enhancing drug penetration and stability. Consequently, the continued application of SLNs and NLCs is anticipated to grow, supported by visual classifications and detailed mechanistic insights that elucidate the penetration pathways and structural diversity of nanoparticle systems [45].

Nanotechnology‐Based Delivery Systems for Dermatological Agents

Along with targeted delivery, nanotechnology provides multifunctional solutions that combine treatment with diagnostic or protective benefits. Certain nanoparticles, for instance, possess antimicrobial or anti‐inflammatory activity independently of other ingredients, which can reduce the risk of unnecessary additions [48]. Advances such as smart patches or stimuli‐responsive systems continue to enable more personalized care by reacting to skin conditions in real time. Further research aims to improve biocompatibility and scalability so that these systems can become a cornerstone of modern dermatology [49]. 1.NanocarriersNanotechnology has advanced dermatological and cosmetic sciences by improving skin permeation and targeting active molecule delivery. Promising nanocarriers like lipid nanoparticles, polymeric micelles, and dendrimers allow for controlled release of drugs, enhancing therapeutic effects while reducing side effects. Ongoing work aims to optimize these formulations for better absorption and targeting of specific skin layers [50]. Despite their benefits, nanocarriers face challenges such as instability, cytotoxicity, and regulatory safety concerns. Research is focused on improving biocompatibility and utilizing hybrid nanocarriers and analytical techniques to enhance drug delivery efficacy, potentially in combination with technologies like microneedles and ultrasound [8, 51].2.NanogelsNanogels are three‐dimensional cross‐linked hydrogel nanoparticles recognized for their effectiveness as drug delivery carriers in dermatology, owing to their high‐water content, elasticity, and capacity to encapsulate bioactive compounds. They facilitate sustained drug release through controlled mechanisms, improving skin penetration and therapeutic efficacy [52]. However, clinical evidence is limited compared to preclinical expectations. Sterilization methods can impact their cross‐link density and release kinetics, and variability in particle size poses a challenge for dose normalization. In various jurisdictions, regulatory classification can alternate between drug and device, complicating market entry. Recent advancements focus on improving drug solubility, stability, and targeted release for skin conditions such as acne and psoriasis, though clinical application is hindered by regulatory and safety issues. Future research aims to refine nanogel formulations for better transdermal delivery and cosmeceutical applications [53, 54].3.NanospongesSilicon nanosponges, featuring organized nanoporous structures, show significant potential for biomedical applications such as drug delivery, tissue scaffolds, and bio‐integrated devices. Created via nanosphere lithography and metal‐assisted wet etching, these nanosponges can trap substantial doses of active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) due to their tunable pore diameters and high surface‐area‐to‐volume ratios [55]. Their interconnected porous walls allow for sustained and controlled drug release, enhancing their biocompatibility and mimicking natural sponge structures for targeted therapeutic delivery. However, much evidence for efficacy is currently limited to in vitro or small animal studies, with challenges in cross‐study comparisons due to diverse chemistries and the risk of burst release from poorly designed pores. Beyond drug delivery, silicon nanosponges also demonstrate significant pollutant adsorption capabilities and can carry more APIs than traditional systems, improving drug solubility and bioavailability. Future research will focus on optimizing their structure for better biocompatibility, controlled degradation, and multifunctional use in medical devices [56].

Regulatory Concerns and Nanotechnology Safety in Dermatology

Nanotechnology in dermatology poses unique regulatory and safety challenges due to size‐dependent reactivity, potential systemic penetration, and limitations of traditional toxicity models [99]. Regulatory frameworks aim to balance innovation with safety, but global gaps remain. Rapidly evolving nanocarriers, stimuli‐responsive systems, and artificial intelligence (AI)‐personalized formulations often outpace existing guidance, slowing clinical translation and adoption beyond established applications such as nano‐sunscreens [100].

In the United States, the FDA regulates nano‐TiO₂ and nano‐ZnO in sunscreens as over‐the‐counter drugs, considering them generally recognized as safe and effective (GRASE) up to 25% when dermal penetration is minimal [101]. For most cosmetic nanomaterials, safety assessment is manufacturer‐driven, focusing on size, surface properties, and systemic exposure, without mandatory pre‐market approval. The Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act (MoCRA, 2022) introduced facility registration, adverse event reporting, and GMP requirements by 2025–2026, yet nano‐specific guidance remains largely voluntary [102]. Key regulatory gaps include batch variability, potential immunogenicity related to surface chemistry, long‐term dermal penetration, environmental impacts, and inconsistent nano‐labeling [103].

The European Union adopts a more proactive approach under Regulation (EC) No. 1223/2009, requiring pre‐market notification of all nanomaterials via the Cosmetic Products Notification Portal (CPNP), mandatory “[nano]” labeling, and safety assessment by the Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety (SCCS) for at‐risk compounds [104]. Recent adaptations, such as Regulation (EU) 2024/858 (“Omnibus NANO”), prohibited several nanomaterials, including nano‐silver, nano‐gold, and certain polymers, effective 2025 due to insufficient safety data and cytotoxic/genotoxic concerns [105]. Nano‐TiO₂ and nano‐ZnO remain authorized as UV‐filters with restrictions, such as non‐spray formulations and specific coatings to reduce ROS generation [106]. Despite these measures, challenges persist with harmonized in‐vitro models, post‐market surveillance across member states, and slow dossier‐driven evaluation of emerging nanotechnologies [107].

Overall, while both FDA and EU frameworks are adapting through guidance updates, risk‐based regulatory assessment frameworks, and post‐market surveillance, and monitoring, they struggle to keep pace with the rapid development of nano‐dermatology [108]. Key needs include standardized characterization protocols (size, charge, coating, stability), longitudinal exposure studies, life‐cycle analyses, and harmonized international standards. Transparent labeling, post‐market surveillance, and structured safety data (e.g., TiO₂, ZnO, silver nanoparticles) are essential to translate regulatory discussion into actionable clinical guidance while enabling innovation in targeted dermatological therapies [109] (Table 4).

Future Directions and Emerging Trends in Nanotechnology for Skin Care

The future of cosmetic nanotechnology is moving toward intelligent, adaptive systems that integrate diagnostics with targeted treatment. Stimuli‐responsive nanomaterials—activated by light, pH, or temperature—can release active agents selectively in diseased or damaged tissues, minimizing off‐target effects [110].

Bioengineered nanoparticles, including exosome‐based systems, aim to facilitate natural communication with skin cells to promote repair and regeneration. Recent studies (2024–2026) demonstrate accelerated wound healing, improved scar architecture, attenuation of photoaging via reduced oxidative stress and enhanced collagen/elastin synthesis, and stimulation of hair growth through pathways such as Wnt/β‐catenin [111, 112].

Early clinical applications of plant‐derived exosome‐like nanovesicles show promise in anti‐aging and alopecia therapies [113]. Integration with AI platforms enables personalized skincare, where algorithms analyze individual biomarkers to create customized nanocarriers for hyperpigmentation, aging, or barrier defects, potentially transitioning dermatologic care from reactive to predictive and proactive [114]. AI‐driven skin analysis, generative formulation tools, and hyper‐personalized recommendations have already improved consumer satisfaction and precision in 2024–2025, with projected market growth driven by AI and machine learning segments [115].

Sustainability and biocompatibility are increasingly central to nanotechnology development in skincare. Researchers are designing “green” nanoparticles using plant extracts or biodegradable polymers to reduce environmental impact and toxicity [116]. Recent reviews (2024–2025) emphasize eco‐friendly, low‐energy methods employing botanical by‐products, fruit peels, and renewable sources to produce bioactive nanoparticles, minimizing hazardous waste while enhancing safety [117]. Hybrid multifunctional systems—such as antioxidant‐loaded nanofibers or probiotic‐coated particles—are gaining attention for their simultaneous protective, nourishing, and restorative effects [118]. Another emerging trend is the convergence of nanotechnology with wearable diagnostic devices, including smart patches that monitor hydration, UV exposure, or inflammation and deliver tailored therapies [119]. Self‐powered closed‐loop systems have been demonstrated for atopic dermatitis, detecting abnormal hydration via thermal sensors, activating hyaluronic acid‐based microneedles (~42°C) to release dexamethasone, and providing timely moisturization and anti‐inflammatory treatment within minutes [120].

Overall, stimuli‐responsive nanocarriers, exosome‐based systems, AI‐personalized formulations, and wearable diagnostic patches represent the next generation of cosmetic nanotechnology. Result

Nanoparticle Classification and Properties

Metallic nanoparticles (gold, silver, titanium dioxide, zinc oxide) range from 10 to 200 nm and penetrate skin via follicles and sweat glands. Polymeric nanoparticles (PLGA, chitosan) range from 20 to 300 nm, enabling controlled drug release. Lipid‐based nanoparticles (SLNs, NLCs, liposomes) range from 50 to 500 nm using physiological lipids for barrier repair and prolonged drug residence.

Therapeutic Efficacy in Skin Diseases

Nanoformulations enhance follicular targeting in acne with reduced irritation, though peroxide instability requires antioxidants. In psoriasis, methotrexate and calcipotriol carriers improve plaque penetration with lower systemic exposure. For atopic dermatitis, tacrolimus‐loaded lipid nanoparticles enhance barrier repair and reduce stinging compared to conventional ointments.

Wound Healing and Skin Cancer

Chitosan and hyaluronan nanoparticles loaded with curcumin and growth factors demonstrate antimicrobial and pro‐healing effects in chronic wounds. Gold nanoshells enable photothermal therapy for melanoma through plasmon‐assisted hyperthermia targeting cancer cells. Nanoformulated 5‐fluorouracil improves lesion localization in skin cancers.

Quantitative Safety Data

Nano‐TiO₂ and ZnO show minimal skin penetration (epidermal retention of 0.47–0.53 μg/cm²) with systemic levels below 5 μg/L. Silver nanoparticles exhibit cytotoxicity above 6–25 μg/mL in cell models. Particles exceeding 30–50 nm show minimal systemic uptake, while 400–700 nm particles optimize follicular targeting without transdermal passage.

Regulatory Status

FDA classifies nano‐TiO₂/ZnO as GRASE up to 25% in sunscreens; MoCRA 2022 requires facility registration by 2025. EU mandates pre‐market notification, compulsory nano‐labeling, and bans nano‐silver/gold effective 2025 under Regulation 2024/858 due to genotoxicity concerns.

Emerging Technologies

Stimuli‐responsive nanomaterials enable light/pH‐activated drug release. Plant‐derived exosome‐like vesicles attenuate photoaging and stimulate hair growth via Wnt/β‐catenin pathways. AI platforms enable personalized nanocarrier formulations. However, heterogeneous study designs and insufficient long‐term safety data limit clinical translation.

Discussion

The therapeutic efficacy of lipid‐based nanocarriers in inflammatory dermatoses is strongly supported by recent evidence demonstrating enhanced barrier repair and reduced irritation in conditions such as psoriasis and atopic dermatitis. Interface‐engineered solid lipid nanoparticles and nanostructured lipid carriers leverage the skin's lipid pathways to achieve higher drug accumulation in lesional tissue, improving therapeutic outcomes and patient compliance through controlled release mechanisms [2]. These carriers actively participate in barrier restoration through their physiological lipid composition, distinguishing them from inert synthetic systems. However, formulation challenges, including polymorphic transitions, oxidative rancidity, and microbial growth in aqueous vehicles, continue to compromise clinical reliability, explaining the persistent discrepancy between robust preclinical efficacy and inconsistent real‐world performance.

Emerging toxicological evidence from advanced skin‐on‐chip models challenges conventional safety assumptions regarding metallic nanoparticles. A novel microfluidic platform with dynamic perfusion demonstrated that titanium dioxide nanoparticle exposure leads to a 32.4% decrease in barrier integrity, a 12.1% reduction in metabolic activity, and a 2.9% increase in permeability in full‐thickness skin models, associated with early moderate inflammatory responses characterized by chemokine upregulation [3, 9]. These findings suggest that conventional static culture models substantially underestimate nanoparticle–skin interactions, highlighting the urgent need for standardized adoption of physiologically relevant testing platforms that better recapitulate human skin dynamics, particularly given the widespread use of titanium dioxide and zinc oxide in sunscreen products.

The integration of phytochemicals into nanocarrier systems represents a significant trend with implications for both efficacy and safety in managing conditions like psoriasis, acne, and melasma. Plant‐derived compounds, including polyphenols, alkaloids, and terpenoids, demonstrate promising antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and tyrosinase‐inhibiting properties when encapsulated in lipid or polymeric nanoparticles, overcoming inherent poor bioavailability while enhancing skin penetration [4, 10]. These phyto‐nanocarrier systems offer favorable safety profiles relative to synthetic pharmaceuticals, addressing consumer demand for natural yet effective therapies. Nevertheless, critical translational hurdles, including formulation stability, scalable manufacturing processes, and rigorous clinical validation, currently confine most phyto‐nanocarrier research to preclinical stages, with fewer than 5% advancing to human trials.

Regulatory divergence between global frameworks creates substantial barriers to nanotechnology translation in dermatology. The European Union mandates pre‐market safety assessments and compulsory nano‐labeling, while the United States adopts a manufacturer‐driven approach without mandatory pre‐market approval for most cosmetic nanomaterials [5]. This inconsistency, compounded by the absence of harmonized international standards for nanoparticle characterization, leads to duplicative testing, increased development costs, and delayed patient access. Stakeholders report that 50% find current guidance unclear, emphasizing the critical need for early regulatory engagement and international harmonization to ensure consistent consumer protection while facilitating innovation.

Beyond direct human toxicity concerns, emerging evidence of ecotoxicological impact from nanotechnology‐enabled dermatological products warrants serious consideration. The extensive use of metal and metal oxide nanoparticles generates nanowaste that accumulates in aquatic and soil ecosystems, with documented effects including deterioration of water quality, destruction of flora and fauna, and damage to soil fertility resulting from their broad‐spectrum antimicrobial properties affecting beneficial microbiomes [6]. This environmental dimension adds considerable complexity to risk‐benefit calculations, suggesting that comprehensive safety evaluation must extend beyond human subjects to encompass full life‐cycle analysis from raw material extraction through consumer use and ultimate environmental fate.

Looking forward, exosome‐based therapeutics and stimuli‐responsive systems represent transformative platforms for precision dermatology. Experimental studies demonstrate exosome involvement in immune signaling, inflammation, wound repair, and hair biology, with plant‐derived exosome‐like nanovesicles showing promise in photoaging attenuation [8]. However, clinical translation remains at an early stage, with no exosome therapeutic receiving FDA approval as of 2025 due to a lack of standardized isolation methods, batch‐to‐batch variability, and insufficient long‐term safety data [8]. Addressing these gaps requires harmonized quality control standards, rigorous randomized controlled trials, and integration of emerging technologies, including artificial intelligence for formulation optimization to ensure safe, effective, and sustainable nano‐dermatology innovation.

Conclusion

This review confirms that nanotechnology can enhance drug delivery and therapeutic outcomes across acne, psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, wound healing, and skin cancer. Lipid‐based nanocarriers improve skin barrier repair through physiological lipid composition. Metallic nanoparticles provide effective UV protection with minimal systemic penetration, though emerging models suggest potential skin interactions warranting continued safety monitoring. Phyto‐nanocarriers offer natural therapeutic alternatives, while stimuli‐responsive systems and exosome‐based platforms represent emerging precision medicine approaches.

However, significant barriers persist, including formulation instability, heterogeneous study methodologies, and insufficient long‐term safety data. Standardized characterization protocols, physiologically relevant toxicity models, and regulatory harmonization are urgently needed. Integration of artificial intelligence, wearable sensors, and digital surveillance offers a pathway to accelerate development while ensuring patient safety. Addressing these gaps will enable nano‐dermatology to fulfill its potential for millions of patients with chronic skin conditions.
