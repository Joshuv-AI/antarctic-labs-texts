What matters to society when making decisions about reimbursing drugs for prostate cancer: a discrete choice experiment
Authors: Rafael Emilio De Feria Cardet, Stephen Goodall, Tracey-Lea Laba, Marion Haas, Ian D Davis, Deborah J Street, Christopher J Sweeney, Richard De Abreu Lourenço
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe use of surrogate outcomes to support treatment efficacy and cost-effectiveness in localized prostate cancer has the potential to shorten time to access for new medicines, but it may be associated with greater uncertainty. We sought to understand how members of society might trade off drug efficacy, safety, cost-effectiveness, and evidence strength when asked to consider funding new treatments for localized prostate cancer.MethodsA discrete choice experiment among the Australian general population was conducted. Treatment choices were described by cost-effectiveness, government cost, adverse events, patients expected to benefit from treatment, nature of benefits, completeness of data available, and the extent of follow-up. Responses were analyzed using mixed logit and latent class models to assess heterogeneity.ResultsThe survey was completed by 1003 participants. Respondents favored interventions that benefited a high proportion of patients (OR = 2.44, 95% CI = 2.10 to 2.69), were cost-effective (OR = 0.89, 95% CI = 0.82 to 0.98), low cost to government (OR = 0.75, 95% CI = 0.68 to 0.82), and with long follow-up time (OR = 1.25, 95% CI = 1.12 to 1.39). Respondents showed aversion to ongoing studies (OR = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.75 to 0.88). Respondents were willing to accept less certain evidence if associated with a lower cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER). Latent class analysis revealed 2 subgroups with different preference patterns.ConclusionsThe analysis found preference for interventions with maximum benefits while minimizing costs. Respondents preferred studies with longer follow-up time for reimbursement decisions despite consequent treatment delays. Notably, respondents were willing to accept the uncertainty associated with surrogate outcomes—reflected through study length and completeness of evidence—provided the intervention demonstrated a favorable ICER.

Introduction

In health-care resource allocation, reimbursement decision-makers face the challenge of balancing rising demand against constrained resources, underscoring the importance of timely decisions on innovative, high-cost technologies. Using surrogate outcomes in clinical trials can expedite evidence generation, as demonstrated in prostate cancer1—a prevalent condition requiring lengthy trials powered on overall survival. The ICECaP study showed 5-year metastasis-free survival strongly predicted 8-year overall survival, suggesting it could serve as a potential surrogate.2

Surrogate outcomes may allow shortening of the time required to determine the benefit and decisions for approval and/or funding of a new intervention.3 However, the benefit-to-harm ratio observed for the surrogate may not accurately reflect the ratio for the ultimate clinical measure, potentially leading to overestimation of benefits or underestimation of risks when the impact on the final outcome has not been validated by empirical evidence,4 adding another layer of potential uncertainty into the reimbursement decision-making process.5

That potential for increased uncertainty should be balanced against the promise that the use of surrogate outcomes has in achieving more timely access to care, allowing medicines to be considered for registration and reimbursement sooner. Understanding how those competing factors are considered, among others, is critical in establishing their relative importance in the decision-making process and to enhance the accountability of that process.6

Discrete choice experiments

A discrete choice experiment (DCE)7 is a method allowing quantitative assessment of the tradeoffs between relevant factors within a decision-making process. A DCE presents respondents with hypothetical choices, within which a profile (or treatment) of interest is described by its underlying characteristics (attributes), each presented at one of a relevant set of levels. In health-care decision-making, the relative desirability of treatments depends on how decision-makers trade off between the attributes describing those treatments such as cost, efficacy, and uncertainty. By eliciting preferences through choice tasks involving attribute tradeoffs, the DCE approach provides insight into the key drivers of reimbursement decisions.

This article reports on a DCE conducted to elicit preferences for new interventions for localized prostate cancer among members of the Australian general public, with a view to understanding the role of evidence maturity relative to other factors affecting reimbursement decisions.

Methods

The development of the DCE in this study involved a systematic approach in line with recommended methods.8

Attribute and level identification

The identification of attributes and levels followed a 4-stage approach:

Literature review: To identify studies analyzing stated preferences of decision-makers in the context of prostate cancer treatment, a targeted literature review was conducted searching databases including Cochrane, CINAHL, EconLit, MEDLINE, and others. Relevant attributes for patient and physician decision-making were extracted from 22 studies,9-30 identifying attributes related to cost, effect on ability to work, quality of life, source of recommendation, recovery after treatment, treatment options for recurrent disease, side effects, survival, treatment convenience, treatment effectiveness, and severity of the diseases.

Design

With attributes and levels selected, choice sets were developed to allow respondents to choose between hypothetical alternatives. The 6 attributes identified with 4 levels and 1 attribute with 2 levels translate into a full factorial design containing 8192 possible combinations of attributes and levels. A design was developed using an orthogonal array with 32 options and 2 generators following the methodology proposed by Street and Burgess.31 The design was divided into 8 blocks, each containing 8 different choice sets. Each block contained each level of each attribute the same number of times. Respondents were randomly assigned into one of 8 blocks of 8 choice sets.

Each choice set comprised a forced choice between two alternatives: option 1 and option 2. For each choice set, respondents were asked to imagine the health-care reimbursement setting while explaining characteristics of localized prostate cancer. Descriptions of some attributes, such as treatment benefits and side effects, are more familiar to the general public, whereas others, such as cost-effectiveness ratios or the monetary value of health gains, may be less familiar and required careful framing (see Supplementary Methods). An example of a choice set is provided in Table 1. Survey respondents were asked to complete questions about their own demographic characteristics and experience as decision-makers and confidence with health literacy. In addition, respondents were asked to indicate the interpretation they had placed on the concept of value for money.

Respondents were recruited from a major panel provider in Australia (PureProfile: https://www.pureprofile.com/) and were chosen to be representative (according to age, gender, and geographic distribution) of the Australian general population.

Statistical analysis

Mixed logit models (MLM) are flexible models where the coefficients (the weighted contribution of each level of each attribute to the total utility gained) are assumed to follow a multivariate normal distribution, thus allowing for variability of preferences among respondents.32 Using the standard deviations of the mean of the coefficients, it is possible to identify heterogeneity in preferences between individuals, because attributes with standard deviations that are significantly different from 0 indicate that the preferences of respondents are not homogeneous. The MLM in this analysis was estimated in Stata (version 17) using the mixlogit command.33 Coefficients of the model with the best goodness-of-fit were used to measure the relative importance of attributes.34

To estimate the impact of covariates, a latent class model (LCM) including a set of demographic covariates that could explain heterogeneity in preferences was conducted. Latent class models explore how individuals are implicitly grouped into a set of classes in which members derive the same utility function from an alternative.35 Statistical information criteria (ie, Akaike, Bayesian, and the conditional Akaike information criteria) were used to measure the relative fit of the model and assess the optimal number of classes.36 To evaluate the probability of membership for individuals with different preferences within the sample, in the LCM all the utility coefficients of attributes coded categorically were allowed to vary randomly from class to class in the model. The LCM was estimated in Stata (version 17) using the user-written command lclogit2.37

Willingness‐to‐accept analysis

The relative marginal rate of substitution for attributes coded as categorical values was estimated as the ratio of the coefficients obtained in the MLM. The attribute of the “length of the study” was used to calculate the willingness to wait (WTW), whereas the “total cost to the government” attribute was used to estimate the willingness to pay (WTP) when both were coded as a continuous variable. The WTP and WTW analysis were estimated in Stata (version 17) using the Stata user written command “wtp.”38

For the willingness to accept analysis, “cost” and “delay time” variables were included in the model as continuous variables, and their coefficients were used as the denominator relative to other coefficients (ie, ratio of coefficients).

Ethics

This study was part of a research program approved by the University of Technology Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee (UTS HREC ETH18-2507).

Results

The survey was administered between July 7 and July 8, 2020 and completed by 1003 participants. More than 23% of respondents had previously been diagnosed with prostate cancer, and 77% had a close friend or relative who had been diagnosed with cancer in the past. In general, all respondents rated their health status as good, and the majority reported that they never or rarely needed help with health literacy and were confident with medical forms. When asked about familiarity with a decision-making role, approximately 38% of respondents had previous experience making decisions about allocating budget, funds, or resources to competing uses. Respondents in the survey were generally representative of the Australian population.39 However, there were disparities in some demographic characteristics—specifically, the educational profile of the survey respondents was higher (finishing school before Year 10 = 3.19% vs 6.6% in Australia) and more having completed undergraduate (31.11% vs 23.2%) or postgraduate degrees (14.56% vs 8.1%). Willingness‐to‐accept analysis

Respondents were willing to pay $227 500 or wait 23 years for an intervention that benefited 40% of patients with localized prostate cancer. The opposite was observed for interventions with a high probability of AEs where respondents need to be compensated $105 300 or with 10 years of time.

When the evidence to support a new intervention came from an ongoing study, respondents needed compensation of 5 years or $53 300. Additionally, respondents were willing to pay $10 000 for evidence with an extra year of follow-up and be compensated by 1.2 months for every extra $1000 of the cost of the intervention. Discussion

Through the use of a DCE, this study elicited the preferences of the general population when taking the role of advisors to reimbursement decision-makers about the funding of treatments for localized prostate cancer. This included consideration of the effect on those decisions of data maturity (length of follow-up) and completeness (eg, study still ongoing) as might be associated with reimbursement applications that use surrogate endpoints. In general, the results of this analysis from respondents with a universal health-care system revealed a strong preference for benefit attributes and a dislike for high costs (to government and in terms of costs per outcomes) and rates of AEs. This is consistent with the literature indicating that attributes related to cost and benefits gained have a high relative importance to decision-makers.40-42

The need for more effective interventions earlier is driving clinical trial design to promote the role of surrogate outcomes, relegating the final outcomes to a secondary outcome reported in continuing studies during the post-market setting.43,44 In this survey, respondents preferred interventions supported by complete studies as opposed to ongoing studies (as would be the case if surrogate outcomes were used). It is unclear if respondents were aware that longer follow-up periods could potentially delay reimbursement decisions and time to access. This points to a need for better education of the general community on potential benefits and uncertainties of using surrogates to expedite access to new therapies and determine what degree of uncertainty is needed for what potential benefit to society.

The ICECaP study showed that the use of metastasis-free survival as a surrogate of overall survival can expedite the reimbursement of new interventions for localized prostate cancer by 3 years compared with waiting for the certainty of OS.2 The willingness-to-accept analysis showed respondents were willing to pay $10 000 for evidence with an extra year of follow-up, implying a preference for studies supported by more mature outcomes or an aversion to uncertainty.

Limitations and future research

The survey was conducted among members of the general public rather than reimbursement decision-makers, which might raise concerns about whether their preferences accurately reflect those of expert committees. Previous studies show divergent cost preferences between decision-makers and the public, with the public often placing less emphasis on cost considerations in health-care resource allocation.45 However, this study’s cost attribute ranking mirrors Whitty et al.’s45 decision-maker subgroup, indicating informed public tradeoffs among attributes in prostate cancer interventions. Other studies have successfully elicited preferences when respondents adopt the role of decision-makers in health-care settings with consistent results.46 Hampson et al. developed a DCE to elicit preferences in health care where members of the general public took the role of decision-makers.46 The analysis undertaken by Hampson et al. demonstrated that members of society understood and were able to trade off cost-related attributes for others that characterized interventions for LPC. Moreover, subgroup analyses in this study of respondents with prior experience in allocating budgets or resources revealed results of comparable direction and magnitude to the overall sample (Table S4). Taken together, these findings suggest that although external applicability to formal decision-making bodies remains limited, the general public in this study demonstrated an ability to engage with and trade off cost-related attributes in a manner consistent with prior decision-maker studies.

Although our results provide insight into societal tradeoffs in the Australian context, caution is required when generalizing to other countries. The weighting of attributes in this DCE likely reflects features of the Australian universal health-care system (Medicare) providing subsidized care with lower out-of-pocket costs compared with other countries. In health-care systems where patients face higher out-of-pocket costs (eg, the United States), societal tradeoffs may shift, with cost attributes gaining greater importance and extended follow-up times being valued differently. Moreover, cultural factors, such as attitudes toward risk and uncertainty, may further influence preferences. Future research could systematically explore country-specific contextual influences by replicating DCEs across diverse health-care systems, enabling comparative analyses of how system structure and societal norms shape reimbursement preference patterns.

Past controversies surrounding nonvalidated surrogate outcomes4 may explain decision-maker skepticism. Future research could investigate if decision-makers, similar to this study’s respondents, trade off decision time against the uncertainty of limited surrogate-based evidence.

It is important to acknowledge that data collection occurred in July 2020, during the COVID-19 pandemic. Public perceptions of health-care priorities and urgency may have been heightened, which could have influenced respondents’ tradeoffs between costs, time-to-approval, and expected benefits. Although our findings provide valuable insight into societal preferences, these preferences may partially reflect the unique circumstances of the pandemic. Future studies could examine whether similar patterns are observed under typical health-care conditions to assess the robustness of these tradeoffs.

Finally, side effects in the choice sets were presented in a generalized manner without specifying severity, subtype, or downstream consequences (eg, hospitalizations or additional costs). This design decision reflected the hypothetical nature of the intervention, where no particular clinical profile was assumed. However, this simplification may not fully reflect the complexity of real-world treatment decisions.

Surrogate outcomes to support decisions

The concept of surrogate outcomes in health-care encompasses elements such as the validation of such outcomes. Communicating these elements to a lay population can be challenging. Therefore, the attributes presented in this DCE did not explicitly refer to the concept of surrogate outcomes but, rather, focused on the length and status of studies providing clinical evidence as a proxy of surrogate outcomes. Future research may explore better methods to implement this concept into the general population.

Our survey findings, therefore, highlight a pragmatic tradeoff in the factors influencing reimbursement decisions. Respondents exhibited conditional acceptance of evidence derived from shorter or incomplete studies, particularly when interventions showed favorable ICERs. In practice, this suggests a willingness to prioritize therapies supported primarily by surrogate outcomes with robust ICERs, provided that provisional reimbursement frameworks are in place. Examples of such frameworks include performance-based contracts or coverage with evidence development, both of which balance early access with the requirement for further evidence. Future research should focus on optimizing the design of these risk-sharing agreements and ensuring that validation of surrogate outcome is dynamically incorporated into reimbursement policies.

Conclusion

The analysis of respondents showed that they preferred an intervention that provided maximum benefits while minimizing the financial impact on the health system. Respondents preferred the use of studies with longer follow-up time to support reimbursement decisions regarding new interventions despite the delay in time to treatment. Notably, respondents were willing to trade off uncertainty associated with the use of surrogate outcomes, as reflected indirectly though study length and completeness of evidence, for interventions with a favorable ICER to support reimbursement decisions in prostate cancer.
