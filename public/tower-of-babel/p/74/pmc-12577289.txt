Sex differences in peer victimization and suicidal ideation among adolescents: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Zhangming Chen, Duanfang Cai, Ruini He, Yudiao Liang, Liping Wen, Hongli Zhang, Jianlin Pu, Youguo Tan, Yilin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeer victimization is significantly correlated with a heightened incidence of suicidal ideation (SI) among adolescents. The current study sought to investigate their relationship among adolescents, focusing on sex differences.MethodsA secondary analysis was conducted on data from 55,815 adolescents (53.9% being female) aged 11–18 in Zigong City, China, from October to December 2020. Questionnaires were used to gather information on the individuals’ psychosocial, familial, and demographic variables. The Multidimensional Peer Victimization Scale (MPVS) was used to examine peer victimization data. Participants self-reported their SI. The association between peer victimization and SI was evaluated using logistic regression analysis.ResultsThe prevalence of SI was 6.03% among boys and 11.85% among girls, reflecting the rates calculated within the entire study population. Adolescents with SI reported substantially higher MPVS and subdimension scores than those without, regardless of sex (all p < 0.001). In boys, physical (OR = 1.062, 95%CI: 1.037–1.088, p < 0.001) and verbal victimization (OR = 1.039, 95%CI: 1.012–1.067, p = 0.004) were closely associated with increased SI risk; however, in girls, physical (OR = 1.051, 95%CI:1.026–1.077, p < 0.001) and verbal victimization (OR = 1.032, 95%CI: 1.011–1.054, p = 0.002), social manipulation (OR = 1.023, 95%CI: 1.001–1.046, p = 0.039), property attack (OR = 1.034, 95%CI: 1.009–1.059, p = 0.007) were all closely associated with increased SI risk.ConclusionsOur findings underscore the necessity for sex-specific prevention strategies to address the distinct forms of peer victimization associated with SI. Interventions targeting verbal and physical aggression among boys, and a broader spectrum that includes verbal and physical aggression, property attacks, and social manipulation among girls, are crucial for mitigating the risk of SI in adolescents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24614-y.Keywords: Suicidal ideation, Adolescents, Peer aggression, Psychological health, Sex difference

Introduction

Among adolescents, suicide has been identified as a leading cause of death [1]. Two major risk factors linked to teenage suicide behavior are suicidal ideation (SI) and suicide attempts (SA) [2–4]. Although there is considerable variation in prevalence worldwide, SI is commonly experienced by adolescents [5]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported the prevalence of SI ranging from 14.3 to 22.6% across regions in youth, while the prevalence of SA ranged from 4.6 to 15.8% [2]. According to Nock et al. [6], the transition from SI to SA typically occurs within the first year of experiencing SI. This study also shows that roughly one-third of young people with SI go on to develop a comprehensive suicide plan during their teens. Furthermore, almost 60% of people who have devised such a plan go on to attempt suicide. Studies have shown that girls report more SA than boys and are more likely to experience SI [5, 7]. The COVID-19 pandemic has introduced additional complexities to adolescent mental health, with emerging studies suggesting an increase in mental health issues, including SI and SA, among young people [8–10]. Understanding the underlying causes of the observed differences between the sexes and identifying the risk factors associated with SI is essential for developing sex-specific interventions aimed at reducing suicide plans and attempts among adolescents, ultimately preventing premature death in this population.

Peer victimization is repeated and chronic bullying or harm inflicted by one or more peers [11]. It includes multiple forms of victimization, such as physical assaults (physical victimization), verbal maltreatment (verbal victimization), bullying in interpersonal interactions (social victimization), and theft or vandalism of property (property victimization) by peers [12, 13]. In 2019, the prevalence of online and offline victimization among adolescents in the United States was as high as 15.7%, according to Kreski et al. [14]. Research indicates that peer victimization in adolescence significantly elevates the probability of anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder symptoms, non-substance-related addictive behaviors, cognitive impairment, SI, SA, and intentional self-harm [3, 5, 15–21]. The significant frequency of peer victimization among teenagers and its grave effects, including suicide, underscore the necessity of educational and intervention strategies within schools, social settings, and families to mitigate its incidence.

The consequences of peer victimization are influenced by the specific subtype of victimization and the sex of the victim. Wen et al. discovered that all three types of peer victimization (overt, relational, and reputational) were associated with heightened odds of SI, SA, and non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) [22]. Ossa et al. have revealed that, in contrast to school-only bullying, cyberbullying that happens solely online is linked to distinct mental health difficulties and risk-taking behaviors for both victims and perpetrators [23]. Fung (2021) found that the relationship between specific forms of peer victimization and subtypes of aggression was significantly influenced by the sex of the individuals involved [24]. Specifically, it was found that reactive aggression in males could be positively predicted by verbal victimization, whereas in females, it could be positively predicted by physical victimization and social exclusion. Conversely, proactive aggression in males could be positively predicted by physical victimization and social exclusion but negatively predicted by verbal victimization. In females, social exclusion could negatively predict proactive aggression [24]. A separate study revealed that sleep disturbances contributed to the association between peer victimization and depressed symptoms in adolescent females, but this was not evident in males [25]. The results from all these studies underscore the necessity of accounting for subgroups and sex differences when examining the detrimental health impacts of peer victimization.

While earlier research has documented the emergence of sex-specific internal and external issues following bullying experiences across different regions [3, 5, 23, 26], the complex interplay between distinct types of peer victimization and the manifestations of SI in adolescents remains a relatively uncharted area of investigation. The international relevance of this study is underscored by the global nature of the pandemic’s impact on adolescent mental health and the widespread occurrence of peer victimization. The existing literature on peer victimization and SI during adolescence consistently points to the existence of sex differences [27–29]. In light of these findings and the emerging post-COVID trends, our study was designed with the hypothesis that there are sex-specific differences in the relationship between various forms of peer victimization and SI. The objective of this study is to deepen our understanding of the relationship between peer victimization and SI by investigating the different types of peer victimization, exploring potential sex-based distinctions, and enabling the development of customized prevention measures for vulnerable individuals across international contexts.

Methods

Study design and characteristics of participants

This study employed data from the Zigong City Youth Psychological Screening Project, a continuous cross-sectional survey initiated in 2020 involving primary, junior, and senior high school students aged 6 to 22. The preliminary ethical approval and data-collecting methodologies are documented in prior publications [30]. All participants received written agreement from their legal guardians before completing the questionnaires. Subsequently, qualified instructors were available to furnish comprehensive information regarding the survey, including the confidentiality policy’s aims, design, methodologies, and particulars. Subsequently, participants were required to complete a series of forms encompassing demographic information, including age, sex, height, weight, visual status, educational attainment, residence, family composition (single-child status, twin status, and whether the family is original, reconstituted, or single-parent), parental educational background, and habits related to smoking and alcohol consumption. Further, they completed various questionnaires, including the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7 (GAD-7), the Prodromal Questionnaire-16 (PQ-16), the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-being Scale (WEMWBS), the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire-students (SDQ-S), the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and the Multidimensional Peer Victimization Scale (MPVS).

The GAD-7 scale was used to assess the severity of anxiety symptoms and functional effects in the past two weeks [31]. The PQ-16 scale was used to assess the risk status of psychotic disorder or other psychological disorders [32]. The PHQ-9 scale was used to evaluate the severity of depression in the past two weeks [33]. The WEMWBS scale was used to evaluate the mental well-being status [34]. The SDQ-S scale was used to evaluate the emotional and behavioral problems [35]. The PSQI scale was used to assess the sleep quality throughout the previous month [36].

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Zigong Mental Health Center (IRB: 2023062001) and was granted permission to utilize and analyze the 2020 data. A total of 95,742 students were polled between October 2020 and December 2020. Clinical psychologists determined that 18,594 of these instances were invalid based on the following standards for recognizing faulty questionnaires and surveys: (1) response time of less than “X” seconds for each questionnaire (“X”: the GAD- 7 < 7 s, PQ-16 < 12 s, PHQ-9 < 10 s, the WEMWBS < 5 s, the SDQ-S < 20 s, PSQI < 20 s, MPVS < 10 s); (2) random or malicious answering of questionnaire questions; (3) incomplete answers in the questionnaire; (4) determination made by other on-site psychologists who reviewed the questionnaire. Consequently, the data approved for use and analysis comprised a total of 77,148 cases.

For the purposes of this study, the participant inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) adolescents within the age range of 11 to 18 years; (2) adolescents who provided complete and valid demographic information and questionnaire responses; (3) participants who self-reported instances of SI. Individuals who did not fulfill these criteria were excluded from the study.

Multidimensional peer victimization

The MPVS scale [37], a tool for measuring the degree of peer victimization, was used to examine peer victimization status. It measures the frequency of 16 victimization incidents over 12 months, yielding a total victimization score. Four criteria are assessed by the tool: property attack, social manipulation, and verbal and physical abuse. Every subscale has a score ranging from 0 to 8, where higher numbers denote more victimization.

Suicidal ideation

Participants were asked if they had SI in the previous month, and all reports of SI were recorded as such.

Covariate

Covariate information was collected from enrolled participants, which include demographics (age, sex, myopia, education grade, residence, one-child, twins, left-behind status, types of families, height, and weight), parental factors (education level of parents, smoking and drinking habits of parents), emotional status, positive/negative psychological factors, and sleep health.

Statistical analyses

In this study, we categorized the continuous scale scores from various assessments into multi-level categorical factors, informed by the existing research on Chinese adolescents. The following cut-off scores were selected based on established literature and clinical guidelines to ensure relevance and comparability across different populations: the SDQ-S was categorized into three levels: Normal or healthy (score ≤ 15), Edge or moderate (16 ≤ score ≤ 19), and Abnormal or severe (20 ≤ score ≤ 40) [38]; the PQ-16 was dichotomized into two levels: No (score < 10) and Yes (score ≥ 10) [39, 40]; the PHQ-9 was divided into three levels: Symptomless or healthy (0 ≤ score < 5), Moderate (5 ≤ score < 15), and Severe (score ≥ 15) [41]; the WEMWBS was categorized into three levels: Healthy (40 ≤ score ≤ 70), Moderate (32 ≤ score < 40), and Severe (14 ≤ score < 32) [42]; the PSQI was categorized into three levels: Healthy (score < 11), Moderate (11 ≤ score < 16), and Severe (score ≥ 16) [43]; and the GAD-7 was divided into three levels: Healthy (0 ≤ score < 5), Mild or moderate (5 ≤ score < 15), and Severe (score ≥ 15) [44].

SPSS 25.0 was used for statistical analysis. All tests were two-sided, and a significance level of P < 0.05 was applied. Numbers representing categorical variables were displayed along with the corresponding percentages. On the other hand, because they were non-normally distributed data, continuous variables such as age, BMI, MPVS scores, and their sub-scales were displayed as median values with the 25th and 75th percentiles (P25, P75). Comparisons of features between the groups with SI and those without were performed using Pearson’s chi-squared test or Kruskal-Wallis tests, where applicable. A univariate logistic regression study examined the association between multidimensional peer victimization and SI among adolescents. The relationships between MPVS and SI in teenagers were analyzed using binary logistic regression analysis (forward: LR). Binary logistic regression model corrections for covariates with SI-related P-values < 0.05 were employed in the univariate analysis. In this study, we developed three models: Model 1 was adjusted for social demographics and multidimensional peer aggressiveness; Model 2 was corrected for Model 1 and parental variables; and Model 3 was adjusted for Model 2 and both positive and negative psychological components and sleep factors. To detect multicollinearity, the variance inflation factor (VIF) method was employed, with a VIF threshold of 5 or greater indicating the presence of multicollinearity [45]. As the multicollinearity diagnostics for the three models yielded VIF values below 5 for each variable in relation to SI, all variables were retained within their respective models (Supporting materials).

Results

Figure 1 illustrates the participant selection procedure employed in this investigation. A total of 77,148 valid investigations and questionnaires assessed by clinical psychologists were initially acquired. Among them, 5,991 participants were children (5,623 were under 11 years old) or adults (368 were over 18 years old), 15,312 participants lacked information on SI, 29 participants had no recorded sex, and 1 participant lacked BMI data. Ultimately, 55,815 participants were included in this study. The stringent exclusion criteria, while intended to maintain data integrity, may have introduced bias, as the excluded group potentially exhibited distinct peer victimization and SI characteristics compared to the included participants. Furthermore, adolescents with SI showed marked disparities in psychosocial well-being indicators, including social difficulties as measured by SDQ (both sexes: p < 0.001), prodromal psychotic symptoms as measured by the PQ-16 (both sexes: p < 0.001), anxiety as assessed by GAD-7 (both sexes: p < 0.001), depression using the PHQ-9 (both sexes: p < 0.001), positive psychology as evaluated by the WEMWBS (both sexes: p < 0.001), and sleep health as indicated by the PSQI (both sexes: p < 0.001). Nonetheless, specific characteristics were significant in a sex-specific manner. The left-behind status (p = 0.028) and the educational attainment of both parents (all p < 0.001) were the only significant factors differentiating male adolescents with SI from those without. In contrast, residential status (p < 0.001) was an essential differentiating factor solely among female adolescents who reported SI.

Table 4 presents the univariate analysis of SI and multidimensional peer aggression. Adolescents with SI had significantly elevated MPVS and subscale scores compared to those without SI (all p < 0.001). For males with SI, median scores were 11 (IQR 4,19) for MPVS, 2 (1,5) for property attack, 2 (0,5) for physical victimization, 4 (2,6) for verbal victimization, and 2 (0,5) for social manipulation. Males without SI had median scores of 4 (1,10), 1 (0,2), 0 (0,2), 2 (0,4), and 0 (0,2), respectively. Females with SI had median scores of 9 (4,7), 2 (1,4), 0 (0,2), 4 (1,6), and 2 (0,5), while those without SI had 3 (1,7), 1 (0,2), 0 (0,0), 1 (0,3), and 0 (0,2), respectively. Each type of peer aggression was associated with an increased risk of SI for both sexes (males: ORs range from 1.255 to 1.313; females: ORs range from 1.344 to 1.360; all p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study constitutes the inaugural extensive analysis of a substantial population investigating the relationship between peer victimization and the risk of SI among school-aged adolescents aged 11 to 18 years in Zigong city of China, with a specific focus on sex differences. Our thorough analyses show that several factors, including peer relationships, family dynamics, and demographic characteristics influence the propensity for SI in male and female teenagers. In particular, there is a significantly higher risk of SI for teenagers of both sexes who have experienced verbal and physical abuse, have anxiety and depression, are susceptible to prodromal psychosis, and have worse psychological health. For male teenagers, being the only child is also a risk factor. However, female teenagers are more vulnerable if they live in metropolitan areas, are twins, have mothers who drink, have poor sleep quality, face social manipulation and property attacks, or are from reconstituted homes. On the other hand, increasing age correlates with a diminished probability of SI. The findings suggest that extensive screening for peer victimization and mental health, coupled with prompt interventions, is essential for reducing SI in teenagers. Furthermore, the distinct risk factors between males and females indicate the need for the creation of sex-specific training and intervention programs for parents and educators to improve suicide prevention initiatives.

SI is a universal phenomenon and an urgent issue among adolescents worldwide. The high prevalence of SI in high-income countries is well-documented, with reports indicating rates of 18.8% in the United States (2019) [14], and 28.4% in South Korea (2016) [46]. Similarly, Uddin et al. reported that the overall prevalence of SI was 16.9% among 59 low-income and middle-income countries, with females having a higher frequency than boys (girls: 18.5%, 95%CI: 16.4–20.6 vs. boys: 15.1%, 95%CI: 13.4–16.7) [7]. Another study in the US indicated that girls had a 1.7 odds-ratio risk (95%CI: 1.4–2.1) of developing SI over their lifespan compared to boys [6]. In Line with these findings, our study found a similar prevalence of 17.88% for SI among all participants. Girls are 1.97 times more Likely than boys to participate in SI, according to the prevalence of SI among adolescent girls, which was 11.85% compared to 6.03% among teenage boys. These results support the idea that SI is common among teenagers, with a significantly higher frequency in girls across all geographic and socioeconomic groups. Strong suicide prevention measures, primarily aimed at young girls, are desperately needed, as seen by the persistent trend across cultural boundaries. Prioritizing and strengthening efforts to address this concerning trend in teenage mental health is imperative.

Peer victimization is a prevalent issue among adolescents [47–49]. A previous report indicates that approximately 20% of young people are affected by this phenomenon [50], which is linked to detrimental impacts on mental health, including anxiety, depression, and SI, SA, and behaviors [5, 15, 16, 19, 51], as well as biological sequelae like cortisol dysregulation and inflammation [50]. In alignment with prior research, the present study confirmed that peer victimization was closely associated with an increased risk SI in adolescents. Our findings reveal that girls are more susceptible to a wider array of peer aggression, encompassing physical, verbal, financial, and social forms, whereas boys are predominantly affected by verbal and physical attacks, contributing to the development of SI. Recognizing the implications of these sex differences in the context of SI development is crucial.

Feelings of helplessness, social isolation, and distress—all known predisposing factors to SI—can be exacerbated by any form of aggression. Aggression, both verbal and physical, can directly damage a person’s sense of security and self-worth, increasing psychological susceptibility. The combination of social and financial aggressiveness causes girls to experience more psychological stress. A previous study found that sleep disturbances mediated the relationship between peer victimization and depressive symptoms in adolescent girls but not in boys [25]. An elevated incidence of SI linked to sleep disturbances was identified solely in girls, consistent with our findings. In other words, the interplay between peer bullying, mental/psychological stress, and SI is particularly relevant for girls. These findings may also help to explain why girls have higher rates of SI than boys and implied that psychophysiological feedback pattern of sex to peer bullying differs. Moreover, sex differences play a significant role not only in the impact of peer bullying on the risk of SI but also in the dynamics of peer bullying itself (refer to S2 Table, and S1 Figure). To sum up, there is an interaction between parents’ lifestyle, family background and their own situation and peer bullying. While a deeper understanding of the risk factors related to SI is still required, including the complex interactions between peer bullying [52], internet addiction [53], personality, and SI, our study’s focus on the diverse forms of peer victimization and gender differences in SI has yielded significant insights. Our results highlight the imperative for targeted screening, preventive interventions, and safety education, explicitly addressing peer bullying.

Moreover, this study uncovered sex-specific differences in demographic and parental factors that are associated with the risk of SI. In addition to the common risk factors associated with diminished emotional and psychological well-being for both sexes, being an only child was identified as a risk factor for boys. However, for girls, elevated risk factors for the development of SI included residing in an urban environment, being a twin, not being part of a nuclear family, and having a mother who consumes alcohol. These findings yield several vital implications: (1) Schools, educators, and policymakers should prioritize the mental health of adolescents with these risk factors, as they are more susceptible to SI. Regular psychological counseling and interventions within educational and community settings could be instrumental in mitigating suicidal thoughts; (2) There is a need to enhance parental education and training efforts. For instance, promoting reduced alcohol consumption among mothers, and increasing attentiveness to the well-being of only a boy, twin girls, and girls residing in urban areas; (3) The prompt modification of the parental relationship is essential. It necessitates parental efforts, the backing of relatives and friends, and the fostering of a societal atmosphere that denounces and combats school bullying. The healthy development of teenagers requires a collective effort among parents, relatives, educators, legislators, and society.

The present study has certain limitations to acknowledge. Firstly, this research is observational in nature and as such, it is susceptible to various biases, including those related to unmeasured confounding variables and the potential for reverse causality. Secondly, the focus of the study was on participants who reported experiencing SI within the past month. Unfortunately, the study did not delve into the frequency or the chronological onset of SI, nor did it distinguish whether these were initial or recurrent thoughts. Thirdly, the reliance on self-reported data for SI introduces the possibility of recall bias, which could result in an underestimation or overestimation of the true prevalence of SI. Fourthly, the self-reported nature of SI may lead to instances of underreporting or overreporting. Additionally, the study did not include enough types of peer abuse, such as sexual victimization [54] and cyberbullying [23]. The following approaches should be considered in future research to get around these limitations. Firstly, longitudinal studies might be carried out to understand better the temporal association between SI and peer victimization, which would lower the possibility of reverse causality bias. Secondly, more detailed and nuanced data collection methods should be employed to capture the frequency and history of SI, providing a clearer picture of the development and progression of these thoughts. Thirdly, researchers should employ more objective metrics or supplementary techniques, such as diaries or psychological evaluations, to reduce recall bias and validate self-reported information. Fourthly, research studies should focus on encompassing a more comprehensive array of peer abuse categories to facilitate a more thorough understanding of the elements contributing to SI. Additionally, the development and deployment of more efficient tools for assessing the mental health of participants is essential, with the goal of reducing the number of invalid survey responses and thus alleviating potential analytical biases in the interpretation of results. Finally, future research should aim for a more inclusive sample that represents a wider range of individuals, including those without current self-reported SI, to ensure that the findings are generalizable to the broader population. By addressing these limitations, subsequent studies can enhance our understanding of the complex interplay between peer victimization and SI, thereby informing more effective preventive and intervention measures.

Conclusions

With different risk profiles noted for each sex, this study emphasizes the crucial role that peer victimization plays in the development of SI in adolescents. Our results call for focused interventions adapted to the particular circumstances of both boys and girls. Reducing verbal and physical aggressiveness should be the primary goal for boys, but treatments for girls should also address social manipulation and property attacks.

Moreover, the insights into risk factors associated with SI gained from this study provide a roadmap for action. Firstly, educational institutions and policymakers must prioritize the mental health of at-risk adolescents, offering regular psychological support to forestall the development of SI. Secondly, parental education and training programs should be enhanced to address risk factors such as maternal alcohol consumption, with particular attention to the well-being of only boy kids, twin girls, and urban dwellers. Finally, prompt interventions in familial relationships are essential, requiring a collaborative endeavor from the family and the community while fostering a school atmosphere that proactively addresses bullying. Promoting the healthy development of teenagers necessitates a unified approach that engages all stakeholders in their lives.
