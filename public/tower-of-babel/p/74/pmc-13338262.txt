Surprising cryptic cavefish diversity in a long-studied karst cave ecosystem of northern Alabama
Authors: Matthew L. Niemiller, Jonathan W. Armbruster, Michael W. Sandel, Eric C. Maxwell, Orran M. Bierstein, C. Lael Anderson, Jacob Schaefer, Coral I. Quering, Amata Hinkle, Matt Candeias, K. Denise Kendall Niemiller, Pamela B. Hart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Subterranean ecosystems host highly specialized and often cryptic biodiversity, yet even intensively studied landscapes may conceal deeply divergent vertebrate lineages. Here we describe Demogorgonichthys arcanus gen. et sp. nov., a new genus and species of cave-obligate fish discovered in Bobcat Cave, a long-monitored karst system on Redstone Arsenal in northern Alabama, USA. Phylogenetic analyses based on mitochondrial nd2, complete mitochondrial genomes, and the nuclear gene rhodopsin place D. arcanus within Amblyopsidae but reveal deep divergence from all described genera, including extensive lineage-specific degeneration of a vision-related gene. Notably, D. arcanus occurs in syntopy with the Southern Cavefish (Typhlichthys subterraneus) despite lacking a close phylogenetic relationship, providing evidence for multiple independent evolutionary origins of cave adaptation within a single groundwater system. This discovery highlights persistent detection bias in groundwater ecosystems and demonstrates that cryptic vertebrate diversity can persist even in well-characterized environments. Extreme endemism and restriction to a single cave–aquifer system further underscore the vulnerability of subterranean biodiversity and the importance of integrating evolutionary and conservation perspectives.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54315-4.

Introduction

Extreme environments impose strong physiological and ecological constraints that limit organismal persistence, yet life has repeatedly colonized habitats once thought to be inhospitable. Across the Tree of Life, organisms occur in environments characterized by extreme temperatures, pressures, chemical toxicity, hypoxia, and resource limitation, often exhibiting remarkable evolutionary innovations that permit survival under these conditions1–6. These systems have increasingly emerged as focal points for biological discovery, yielding disproportionate insights into adaptation, resilience, and cryptic biodiversity, including the continued discovery of undescribed taxa even in regions considered well explored7–11.

Among vertebrates, teleost fishes represent one of the most evolutionarily versatile lineages to occupy extreme environments. As the most diverse group of vertebrates, teleosts have colonized nearly all aquatic habitats on Earth, including the deep sea12,13, polar waters14,15, sulfidic springs4,16, and subterranean ecosystems17–21. Subterranean habitats impose particularly severe constraints, including perpetual darkness, lack of primary productivity, and reliance on allochthonous nutrient inputs11,17–19,22,23. Despite these challenges, cave- and groundwater-dwelling fishes have evolved repeatedly across teleost lineages, with representatives documented from more than ten orders worldwide18,20,24,25. In several regions, including eastern North America, subterranean systems have been colonized multiple times independently21,26–29, sometimes even within the same cave or aquifer system20,30–32.

North America harbors one of the world’s most intensively studied cavefish faunas, specifically within the family Amblyopsidae. This family includes surface-dwelling, facultative cave-associated, and obligate subterranean species distributed primarily across the Interior Low Plateau and Ozarks karst regions of the eastern United States30,33,34. Amblyopsids have long served as model organisms for studies of cave adaptation, sensory evolution, and regressive traits17,21,22,27,29,30,35–38, and their taxonomy, phylogenetic relationships, and systematics are comparatively well resolved relative to many other subterranean vertebrate groups9,21,27,29,32,38–41. As a result, the discovery of novel amblyopsid diversity, particularly at the generic level, has been assumed to be increasingly unlikely in this region.

Here, we describe a new genus and species of cave-obligate amblyopsid cavefish discovered in a cave within the Tennessee River Valley of northern Alabama: Demogorgonichthys arcanus gen. et sp. nov., the Demon Cavefish. This species was first documented at Bobcat Cave (Alabama Cave Survey no. AMD1283), located on Redstone Arsenal near Huntsville, Madison County, Alabama, USA, during biological surveys conducted in February 2025. Remarkably, D. arcanus gen. et sp. nov. occurs in syntopy with the Southern Cavefish (Typhlichthys subterraneus), another obligate subterranean amblyopsid, yet phylogenetic analyses demonstrate that the two taxa are not sister lineages. This co-occurrence represents an independent evolutionary origin of cave adaptation within the same subterranean system, underscoring the complexity of colonization history and lineage persistence in karst aquifers.

The discovery of Demogorgonichthys arcanus gen. et sp. nov. from a cave that has been visited repeatedly and from a landscape widely regarded as biologically well characterized highlights the persistence of hidden vertebrate diversity even in intensively studied regions. More broadly, this finding challenges assumptions about biological survey completeness in subterranean ecosystems and emphasizes the role of access-restricted lands, including military installations, as inadvertent refugia for undescribed biodiversity. Through morphological and molecular analyses, we describe the diagnostic features, phylogenetic placement, and ecological context of this new genus and species, and discuss its implications for cavefish evolution, biogeography, and conservation in eastern North America.

Demogorgonichthys is most similar to Speoplatyrhinus and can be separated by rostral flaring (i.e., duckbill shape) less pronounced (snout nearly as flat with the distance from the tip of snout to the point of the narrowest point of the snout about the same length as the distance from the narrowest point of the snout to the supraoccipital vs. much longer); by having a convex hump immediately posterior to supraoccipital (vs. dorsal surface gradually increasing to dorsal fin), the lack of a posterodorsal column of neuromasts on the opercle (vs. presence), a near contiguous anterodorsal and medial column of neuromasts on the opercle (vs. well separated), and little overlap of the anterodorsal and medial columns (vs. the two columns overlapping across greater than 30% of their lengths). Embedded scales on Demogorgonichthys increase in size from dorsal to midline, while scales remain relatively the same size in Typhlichthys species and Speoplatyrhinus.

Molecular diagnosis. Mean uncorrected pairwise genetic distance between the Demogorgonichthys gen. nov. mitogenome and other amblyopsid genera is 11.0% relative to Typhlichthys, 10.9% to Speoplatyrhinus, 12.3% to Amblyopsis, 14.5% to Troglichthys, 13.4% to Forbesichthys, and 17.7% to Chologaster. All sequenced individuals of Demogorgonichthys gen. nov. possess unique mitochondrial nd5 and nuclear rhodopsin haplotypes among amblyopsids. The nd5 locus is characterized by a 3-bp deletion near the 3’ terminus. Rhodopsin is characterized by a 284-bp deletion that results in a frameshift and multiple premature stop codons, as well as an additional 12-bp deletion not observed in other stygobiotic amblyopsids.

Etymology. The specific epithet arcanus (Latin adjective meaning hidden, secret, or mysterious) refers to the long-undetected existence of this cavefish despite decades of intensive biological study at the type locality, as well as its occurrence within a subterranean environment largely concealed from human observation. The name further alludes to the species’ probable primary occupancy of the underlying karst aquifer rather than the accessible cave passages themselves, emphasizing its cryptic nature. The recommended common name is Demon Cavefish.

Distribution. Demogorgonichthys arcanus sp. nov. is currently known only from the type locality, Bobcat Cave, on Redstone Arsenal in southwestern Madison County, Alabama, USA (Fig. 2A). Bobcat Cave has a small entrance that opens within a low knoll near the western boundary of the installation. The cave is developed in the Tuscumbia Limestone and consists of a short entrance passage that slopes downward into a network of low (approximately 1–2 m high), water-filled tunnels interconnected by narrow crawlways42.

The mapped length of Bobcat Cave is approximately 454 m, with much of the cave floor covered by thick mud and limited exposed bedrock. The largest chamber, known as the Shrimp Room, is approximately 60 m in length and up to 30.5 m wide (Fig. 2C). Hydrologic conditions within the cave are highly dynamic42–45. Water levels may fluctuate rapidly by as much as 3.9 m in response to heavy precipitation events, during which floodwaters inundate all but the entrance area. During drought conditions, standing water is reduced to a series of isolated pools, primarily within the Shrimp Room.

Dye-tracing studies conducted in the vicinity of Bobcat Cave indicate that the recharge basin is shallow and localized, potentially less than 1 km2, likely restricted to areas immediately west of Indian Creek, with most recharge derived from shallow groundwater flowing primarily in a northward direction43–46. The rapid hydrologic response of the cave to precipitation events suggests strong connectivity between surface runoff and the subterranean system44–46. Trace metals, including cadmium, chromium, and lead, have been detected at elevated concentrations in water samples from Bobcat Cave over the past two decades43,44,46,47, potentially reflecting historical groundwater contamination. The cave occurs within a former industrial area of Redstone Arsenal associated with the disposal of excess conventional and chemical munitions.

Several additional cave systems in the surrounding region support populations of Typhlichthys (Fig. 2A), yet D. arcanus has not been detected at these sites despite repeated surveys. While previous dye-tracing studies suggest a shallow and localized recharge basin for Bobcat Cave, genetic evidence from co-occurring subterranean taxa indicates the possibility of broader regional hydrologic connectivity9,48–50. Based on currently available information, we estimate the extent of occurrence (EOO) and area of occupancy (AOO) for D. arcanus to be 2 km2 and 4 km2, respectively. Given that the species is presently known from a single cave system and that the recharge basin has not been comprehensively delineated, all geographic range metrics represent minimum estimates and are expected to be refined as additional hydrologic and biological surveys are conducted.

Habitat. Demogorgonichthys arcanus has been observed in quiet, silt- to mud-bottomed pools within Bobcat Cave (Fig. 2C). Water levels within the cave are highly dynamic and fluctuate in response to recent precipitation events. Individuals have been observed in water depths ranging from approximately 5 cm to 1.5 m. Water quality data for Bobcat Cave are derived from monthly measurements collected between October 2019 and July 202247. During this period, water temperature ranged from 14.0 to 16.0 °C (median 15.0 °C), dissolved oxygen from 3.3 to 9.4 mg/L (median 5.3 mg/L), pH from 6.9 to 8.2 (median 7.4), and specific conductance from 171 to 386 µS/cm (median 267 µS/cm).

Ecology and life history. Most aspects of the ecology and life history of Demogorgonichthys arcanus remain unknown. Based on count data from four surveys conducted in 2025 (Supplementary Table 2), the abundance of D. arcanus within Bobcat Cave appears comparable to that of the syntopic Southern Cavefish (Typhlichthys subterraneus), with an approximate ratio of 1:1.12. Across these surveys, we observed 8–18 individuals (mean = 12.3) of D. arcanus and 6–19 individuals (mean = 13.8) of T. subterraneus. Estimating long-term population trends is complicated by the historical conflation of the two species in previous cavefish censuses. However, if a similar ratio has persisted historically, up to approximately 25 individuals of D. arcanus may have been present during some surveys conducted since the early 1990s42–44,51,52, suggesting relative population stability over the past four decades.

Individuals as small as 8 mm standard length were observed in late December 2025, indicating the presence of multiple size classes and potentially signaling reproductive episodes in late autumn/early winter. The diet of D. arcanus is unknown but is likely similar to that of Typhlichthys, which feeds on live copepods, amphipods, isopods, cladocerans, and small crayfishes17,30. We observed a juvenile attempting to feed on stygobiotic copepods in a shallow, isolated pool on 30 December 2025. The cavefish attempted to snap at three copepods that swam within 0.5 cm or touched its head. Documented predators are unknown but may include larger stygobiotic crayfishes (Cambarus speleocoopi and Orconectes australis), stygophilic crayfishes (Cambarus tenebrosus), other cavefishes (including T. subterraneus and conspecifics), and Tennessee Cave Salamanders (Gyrinophilus palleucus). Community associates of D. arcanus at Bobcat Cave include Typhlichthys subterraneus (lineages E and F), Gyrinophilus palleucus, Palaemonias alabamae, Orconectes australis, Cambarus speleocoopi, Cambarus tenebrosus, Stygobromus sp., and stygobiotic cyclopoid copepods.

Conservation. Based on its extremely restricted distribution, confinement to a single subterranean system, and small estimated geographic range, Demogorgonichthys arcanus is considered highly vulnerable to extinction. Using the NatureServe conservation status framework, the species qualifies for a global rank of G1 (Critically Imperiled). Under the IUCN Red List framework, D. arcanus meets thresholds for Critically Endangered (CR) under criterion B, based on an estimated extent of occurrence (EOO) of approximately 2 km2 and area of occupancy (AOO) of approximately 4 km2, coupled with occurrence at a single location. The species is restricted to a single cave system and its associated aquifer, rendering it vulnerable to localized threats affecting groundwater quantity and quality. Hydrologic connectivity to surface runoff, documented fluctuations in water levels, and the presence of elevated trace metals in cave waters indicate potential exposure to environmental stressors. These assessments should be regarded as preliminary and conservative, pending further delineation of the recharge basin and additional surveys to better characterize the species’ distribution and population status.

Remarks. Bobcat Cave was discovered in the early 1970s and rapidly gained biological significance following the discovery of the largest known population of the federally endangered Alabama Cave Shrimp (Palaemonias alabamae) during a mapping expedition conducted by the Huntsville Grotto of the National Speleological Society in 197342,43. Since 1990, the cave has been the focus of long-term biological and hydrological monitoring, including nearly monthly water-quality measurements and shrimp population censuses conducted by the Geological Survey of Alabama and partners42–44,47,51–54, as well as numerous additional biological investigations9,32,48–50,55,56. Despite this sustained attention and long history of biological study, the cavefish described herein had not been previously documented.

Morphological distinctiveness

In many ways, Demogorgonichthys arcanus is intermediate in morphology between Typhlichthys and Speoplatyrhinus in that its snout is elongated and compressed compared to Typhlichthys but not to the same extent as Speoplatyrhinus. Demogorgonichthys does differ in shape in that the region between the supraoccipital and dorsal fin is elevated with the highest point roughly halfway (vs. slightly raised in Typhlichthys and a gradual slope to the dorsal fin in Speoplatyrhinus). Demogorgonichthys also has relatively smaller and less numerous superficial neuromasts. For example, both Typhlichthys and Speoplatyrhinus have three columns of neuromasts on the opercle, an antero- and posterodorsal column and medial column with the space roughly equal between the three columns (Fig. 4). Typhlichthys and Speoplatyrhinus also each have a largely longitudinal row of neuromasts that proceed from the ventral edge of the posterodorsal column with the longitudinal row at a right angle and well separated in Typhlichthys and at an obtuse angle and almost connected in Speoplatyrhinus Demogorgonichthys lacks the posterodorsal column of neuromasts, the medial row is almost contiguous with the anterodorsal column, and the longitudinal row is reduced (although also angled ventrally). In addition, the anterodorsal column only slightly overlaps the medial column in Typhlichthys and barely overlaps in Demogorgonichthys while there is over 30% overlap of each column in Speoplatyrhinus. Typhlichthys has a unique, tubular sensory structure just dorsal to the opercular opening, Speoplatyrhinus has a small column of neuromasts in the same region, and Demogorgonichthys lacks any obvious structures.

Osteology. Demogorgonichthys and Speoplatyrhinus have much taller orbital processes of the anguloarticular and dentary than Typhlichthys with the interspace between the two narrow in Demogorgonichthys (approximately the width of the coronoid process of the articular; similar to that of Typhlichthys) and wide in Speoplatyrhinus (approximately three times wider than the width of the coronoid process of the articular and even wider than that of the dentary) (Fig. 5, Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Video 1–3). The severe constriction of the snout in Speoplatyrhinus causes the mesial laminae of the anguloarticulars to overlap at the midline (vs. a gap between them in other amblyopsids; Supplementary Fig. 2). The quadrate condyle to the anguloarticular is unique in Demogorgonichthys in that there are two bumps that are visible in ventral view; these bumps appear to be more developed in Demogorgonichthys (although this may be an artifact of scanning) and are angled at a 90° or less angle to one another vs. a strongly obtuse angle in Speoplatyrhinus and Typhlichthys (Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Video 1–3).

Armbruster et al.37 describe a lengthening of the anterodorsal arm of the hyomandibula, and this portion contains a large, longitudinally oval opening (Fig. 5, Supplementary Fig. 3). The anterodorsal arm of the hyomandibula is particularly narrow in Demogorgonichthys with the width of the arm narrower than the length of the opening (vs. about the same width as the length of the opening in Speoplatyrhinus and wider than the length of the opening in Typhlichthys). The dorsal lamina above the opening is narrowest in Demogorgonichthys and widest in Speoplatyrhinus. In addition, when viewed anteriorly, the hyomandibula has a medial, ventrally oriented process and a laterally directed process (Supplementary Fig. 4). The lateral process is wider in Demogorgonichthys and Speoplatyrhinus with it widest in Demogorgonichthys. The lateral process is oriented almost completely lateral in Demogorgonichthys and Typhlichthys and ventrolaterally in Speoplatyrhinus.

Armbruster et al.37 describe the mesethmoid as being elongate in Speoplatyrhinus and Typhlichthys with the length greatest in Speoplatyrhinus. Although the computed tomography (CT) imagery does not allow for resolution of the posterior border of the mesethmoid with confidence, it appears to be as elongate as in Speoplatyrhinus. The mesethmoid flares anteriorly into a roughly rectangular shape, and there are a couple of differences here in Demogorgonichthys (Supplementary Fig. 2). In Speoplatyrhinus and Typhlichthys, there is a lateral concavity in the anterior portion of the mesethmoid with the anterior edge wider than the posterior; however, the anterior portion of the mesethmoid tapers anteriorly in Demogorgonichthys and is widest posteriorly. Also, in Speoplatyrhinus and Typhlichthys, the anterior portion of the neurocranium and the mesethmoid taper to the anterior portion of the mesethmoid while there is a constriction posterior to the anterior portion of the mesethmoid in Demogorgonichthys at about the posterior edge of the mesethmoid and then the mesethmoid begins to flare laterally before the enlarged anterior portion of the mesethmoid.

Demogorgonichthys and Speoplatyrhinus share an increased size of the fifth epibranchial, which is visible from the posterior in the CT scans (Supplementary Fig. 4). Epibranchial 5 is bent in the middle forming a dorsal lamina that is especially large in Demogorgonichthys and Speoplatyrhinus. The laterally-directed ramus of epibranchial 5 from this dorsal lamina is greater than half the width of the medially-directed ramus in Speoplatyrhinus and Typhlichthys and about quarter the width in Demogorgonichthys.

Phylogenetic placement and genetic divergence

The complete mitochondrial genome of Demogorgonichthys arcanus ranged from 17,794 to 17,795 bp, which is intermediate between Speoplatyrhinus poulsoni (17,901 bp) and Typhlichthys from Bobcat Cave (17,681 bp). Gene number, order, and orientation were conserved across all taxa and matched the inferred ancestral condition for Percopsiformes. Demogorgonichthys is further distinguished from all other percopsiforms by a 3-bp deletion near the 5′ terminus of the nd5 locus.

Maximum-likelihood analyses of the complete mitochondrial genomes recovered Demogorgonichthys arcanus as sister to (Speoplatyrhinus poulsoni + Typhlichthys subterraneus), although support for deeper nodes was low (Fig. 6). Using this topology, RelTime analyses estimated the time to the most recent common ancestor (TMRCA) of these three lineages at 11.6 ± 1.6 Ma (95% CI: 10.0–13.2 Ma). The estimated divergence between Speoplatyrhinus and Typhlichthys was 10.9 ± 1.7 Ma, overlapping the TMRCA of Demogorgonichthys. These divergence time estimates should be interpreted with caution, as they are derived from a relatively small mitogenomic dataset and are sensitive to assumptions regarding among-lineage rate variation and the calibration point applied; broader taxon sampling and additional fossil or biogeographic calibrations will be necessary to refine the temporal framework for amblyopsid diversification. Both the maximum likelihood (ML) mitogenome and nd2 phylogenetic analyses recovered Demogorgonichthys arcanus as a strongly supported, monophyletic lineage distinct from Typhlichthys (including individuals co-occurring at Bobcat Cave and other previously defined lineages) and Speoplatyrhinus poulsoni. Demogorgonichthys was recovered as sister to (Typhlichthys + Speoplatyrhinus) in the mitogenome phylogeny (Fig. 6), whereas relationships among these lineages were poorly resolved in the nd2 topology (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Mean uncorrected pairwise genetic distances (p-distances) among mitochondrial genomes ranged from 8.7% between D. arcanus and S. poulsoni to 9.4% between D. arcanus and Typhlichthys from Bobcat Cave. At the mitochondrial nd2 locus, uncorrected p-distance was 10.8% between D. arcanus and syntopic Typhlichthys, and 10.9% between D. arcanus and S. poulsoni. In contrast, divergence at the nuclear rhodopsin locus was lower, with p-distances of 1.4% between D. arcanus and Typhlichthys from Bobcat Cave and 1.3% between D. arcanus and S. poulsoni. Individuals of Typhlichthys from Bobcat Cave grouped within two previously defined mitochondrial lineages, whereas all D. arcanus individuals formed a single, deeply divergent lineage across loci.

A single 572-bp rhodopsin haplotype was observed among the seven D. arcanus individuals sequenced. The coding region contained two deletions: a 284-bp deletion (94 amino acids) resulting in a frameshift, and a second 12-bp deletion (4 amino acids). The larger deletion introduces a frameshift that generates three premature stop codons, yielding a truncated open reading frame (Fig. 6) and providing strong evidence for pseudogenization of the rhodopsin locus in this lineage, consistent with relaxation of selective constraint following colonization of an aphotic environment.

Discussion

Discovery in plain sight: hidden vertebrate diversity in a well-studied cave

The discovery of Demogorgonichthys arcanus is notable not just because it represents a new genus and species of North American cavefishes, but also because it was detected in a cave system that has been repeatedly visited and intensively monitored for decades. Bobcat Cave has been the focus of long-term biological and hydrological monitoring associated with the federally endangered Alabama Cave Shrimp (Palaemonias alabamae), including nearly monthly water-quality measurements and shrimp censuses since 199042–44,47,51–54, as well as numerous targeted biological investigations9,32,48–50,55,56. In many regions, such a sustained history of study would be expected to greatly reduce the likelihood of discovering additional vertebrate lineages, particularly within a family as well-characterized as the Amblyopsidae. The presence of an undescribed cavefish lineage under these circumstances underscores a persistent and underappreciated source of discovery bias in subterranean ecosystems: even when cave systems and other subterranean sites are well studied, detection probability for cryptic aquatic vertebrates can remain low.

Several non-mutually exclusive factors may explain why D. arcanus remained undocumented. First, the hydrologic regime of Bobcat Cave is highly dynamic, with rapid and substantial water-level fluctuations in response to precipitation and prolonged periods of reduced standing water during drought. Such variability likely affects both the spatial distribution and detectability of cavefishes, particularly if individuals spend much of their time in the underlying aquifer and enter human-accessible cave passages only under certain hydrologic conditions. Second, the cave’s extensive mud substrate and episodic turbidity can limit visibility and hinder effective visual surveys. Third, researchers have often minimized entry into and disturbance of aquatic habitats in Bobcat Cave to avoid impacts on the federally endangered Alabama Cave Shrimp, which can constrain capture effort and reduce opportunities to closely examine fishes encountered in pools and submerged passages. Finally, D. arcanus occurs in syntopy with Typhlichthys subterraneus, and historical monitoring efforts have treated cavefish observations as a single taxonomic unit. This is a common challenge in subterranean biodiversity surveys, where morphologically similar or behaviorally cryptic taxa may be conflated unless detailed morphological or genetic examination is undertaken7,57. Four previous collections (7 specimens in total) have been made of amblyopsids at Bobcat Cave: UAIC 45381.01 (1 specimen), UAIC 45378.01 (1), UAIC 49098.01 (3), and UAIC 14577.01 (2); all of which are Typhlichthys. Together, these considerations suggest that additional undescribed diversity may persist even in more intensely sampled karst regions, particularly in groundwater habitats with strong surface connectivity and challenging survey conditions.

Multiple independent evolutionary origins of cave adaptation within a single karst cave system

A striking result of this study is the co-occurrence of Demogorgonichthys arcanus with Typhlichthys subterraneus at Bobcat Cave despite the absence of a sister-group relationship between these taxa. Although syntopy of stygobiotic fishes has been documented elsewhere20,30–32, such cases remain uncommon within North American amblyopsids and carry important implications for understanding the evolutionary assembly of subterranean communities. The presence of two deeply divergent amblyopsid lineages within a single cave system indicates that Bobcat Cave, or its associated aquifer network, has been colonized independently by at least two cave-adapted lineages that have persisted together over ecological timescales.

Comparable instances of amblyopsid syntopy are known from Key Cave in Lauderdale County, Alabama, where Speoplatyrhinus poulsoni and T. subterraneus occur within the same cave system20,58, and from the Mammoth Cave system, where Amblyopsis spelaea and Typhlichthys co-occur17,20,30,33. However, in the Mammoth Cave system, the two species rarely occupy the same aquatic microhabitats. In contrast, D. arcanus and T. subterraneus occur in close syntopy within Bobcat Cave, demonstrating that independently derived cavefish lineages can not only colonize the same subterranean system but also overlap spatially at fine ecological scales.

At present, limited resolution among major amblyopsid lineages in the mitochondrial topology constrains stronger inference about deeper relationships among Demogorgonichthys, Typhlichthys, and Speoplatyrhinus. Nonetheless, the strong monophyly and deep genetic divergence of Demogorgonichthys support its recognition as a distinct evolutionary lineage. The unresolved placement of Demogorgonichthys relative to Typhlichthys and Speoplatyrhinus in the nd2 topology reflects well-recognized limitations of single-locus mitochondrial phylogenetics, which can be subject to stochastic lineage sorting, among-lineage rate heterogeneity, and insufficient phylogenetic signal at deeper divergence depths. The polytomous placement of D. arcanus in the nd2 topology thus reflects genuine uncertainty in the divergence order of these lineages rather than evidence against its distinctiveness.

We acknowledge that the current phylogenetic framework relies primarily on mitochondrial markers, and that the rapid divergence of these lineages in the late Miocene means incomplete lineage sorting cannot be excluded as a contributor to topological uncertainty. Although rhodopsin was analyzed as a nuclear marker, its utility for resolving deeper relationships is limited by the extensive structural degeneration documented in D. arcanus. As such, rhodopsin is best interpreted here as evidence for unique visual system degeneration rather than as a primary phylogenetic marker. Phylogenomic analyses incorporating multiple conserved nuclear loci will be essential for definitively resolving the placement of Demogorgonichthys within Amblyopsidae, testing whether the mitogenomic topology reflects the true species tree, and differentiating between alternative historical scenarios, including whether Demogorgonichthys represents an ancient relict lineage within the Interior Low Plateau karst region or an early divergence associated with regional karst evolution in the Tennessee River Valley. Whole-genome sequencing data are currently being generated for Demogorgonichthys arcanus and other amblyopsid taxa as part of an ongoing study, and these data will provide the phylogenomic resolution needed to fully address remaining uncertainty in deeper amblyopsid relationships. A formal description of this lineage now provides a stable taxonomic foundation for that forthcoming work.

Evolutionary distinctiveness and convergent cave adaptation

The morphology of Demogorgonichthys arcanus exhibits classic hallmarks of obligate subterranean life, including complete loss of externally visible eyes, depigmentation, and elaboration of mechanosensory systems, all traits that have evolved repeatedly and convergently in cavefishes worldwide17–20,59–61. Despite these shared troglomorphic features, Demogorgonichthys is readily distinguished from Typhlichthys and Speoplatyrhinus by a suite of cranial, jaw, neuromast, and fin characters. This mosaic of convergence and lineage-specific divergence underscores that cave adaptation in amblyopsids is not expressed as a single morphological endpoint, but rather reflects multiple evolutionary solutions shaped by ancestry and ecological context.

The temporal framework inferred from mitogenomic data suggests that Demogorgonichthys, Speoplatyrhinus, and Typhlichthys diverged during a relatively narrow interval in the Miocene, consistent with a rapid diversification of amblyopsid cavefishes rather than a protracted, stepwise accumulation of lineages. Comparable Miocene diversification time frames, including the split between Speoplatyrhinus and Typhlichthys, have been inferred in other amblyopsid studies21,27,29,40, and independent subterranean colonization events have been documented in other cave and groundwater vertebrates, including Texas Eurycea groundwater salamanders62,63 and the Eurycea spelaea grotto salamander complex of the Ozark Highlands64. Together, these patterns point to a shared evolutionary history shaped by regional geological and climatic change. The middle–late Miocene transition was characterized by increasing climatic seasonality and landscape restructuring65,66, processes that likely destabilized surface habitats while favoring the persistence of groundwater refugia. Under this scenario, repeated invasions of subterranean environments by surface ancestors may have been facilitated by expanding karst connectivity and ecological release within caves and aquifers.

The nuclear vision-related gene rhodopsin provides an independent molecular perspective on this evolutionary history. Although D. arcanus lacks externally visible eyes, its rhodopsin coding region is still present but exhibits extensive structural disruption, including a large deletion that causes a frameshift and multiple premature stop codons, as well as an additional deletion not observed in other amblyopsid cavefishes examined to date (Fig. 6). Degeneration of vision-related genes has been documented repeatedly in cave-adapted fishes and is widely interpreted as a consequence of relaxed selective constraint following colonization of aphotic environments21,27,29,40,67–71.

Importantly, the specific mutational pattern observed in D. arcanus differs from that reported in co-occurring Typhlichthys and other amblyopsid genera, suggesting that rhodopsin degeneration in Demogorgonichthys did not arise from a shared ancestral pseudogenization event. Instead, these molecular patterns are consistent with an independent evolutionary trajectory following subterranean colonization. When viewed alongside pronounced morphological differentiation and deep mitochondrial divergence, the rhodopsin results reinforce the inference that multiple amblyopsid lineages have independently adapted to life underground within the same karst system.

Together, these findings demonstrate how subterranean environments can act simultaneously as arenas for strong phenotypic convergence and as long-term evolutionary filters that preserve deep genetic independence among co-occurring lineages. This dual role helps explain the surprising persistence of cryptic diversity in karst aquifers and highlights the complexity of evolutionary processes shaping groundwater communities.

Conservation implications: extreme endemism in a dynamic and vulnerable groundwater system

From a conservation perspective, D. arcanus exemplifies the vulnerability of narrowly endemic groundwater vertebrates occupying small, hydrologically dynamic karst systems. The species is currently known from a single cave and its associated aquifer and is inferred to occupy a highly restricted geographic range. These conditions support a NatureServe global rank of G1 (Critically Imperiled) and meet IUCN thresholds for Critically Endangered under criterion B, based on a very small extent of occurrence and area of occupancy coupled with occurrence at a single site. As with many described stygobiotic taxa72–74, these designations reflect both genuine biological risk and substantial uncertainty regarding the full spatial extent of occupied habitat.

Although Bobcat Cave lies within a restricted-access military installation that likely limits direct anthropogenic disturbance within the cave itself, the primary threats to D. arcanus operate at the scale of groundwater processes extending beyond the human-accessible cave passages. Hydrologic studies demonstrate strong and rapid connectivity between surface runoff and the cave’s groundwater, resulting in pronounced water-level fluctuations following precipitation events44–46. Such connectivity increases susceptibility to surface-derived stressors, including contamination, sediment inputs, and altered hydrologic regimes within the recharge basin. The repeated detection of elevated concentrations of trace metals, including cadmium, chromium, and lead, over multiple decades suggests potential legacy contamination associated with historical industrial activities on Redstone Arsenal43,44,46,47. While the biological effects of these contaminants on D. arcanus remain unknown, chronic exposure may pose sublethal or long-term risks typical of groundwater-dependent fauna.

In addition to chronic groundwater stressors, stochastic events such as extreme flooding or prolonged drought may further constrain habitat availability and impose demographic bottlenecks in species with small population sizes and limited distributions75,76. These risks highlight a common paradox in subterranean conservation: occurrence on protected or access-restricted lands can reduce some immediate threats, such as disturbance from recreational caving, yet does not eliminate vulnerability arising from processes operating at the aquifer scale. Continued water-quality monitoring and biological surveys at Bobcat Cave, combined with protection of surface recharge areas and maintenance of existing access controls, are therefore likely to be critical for the long-term persistence of D. arcanus. Further hydrologic delineation and targeted biological surveys will be necessary to refine conservation priorities and management strategies.

Future directions

Several lines of investigation would substantially refine understanding of the evolution and conservation status of Demogorgonichthys arcanus. First, fine-scale delineation of the recharge basin and hydrologic connectivity of Bobcat Cave is needed to define the likely spatial extent of the population and to identify priority areas for groundwater protection. Second, targeted surveys of nearby cave and groundwater habitat, particularly using eDNA approaches, may reveal additional occurrences or confirm strict endemism. Third, phylogenomic analyses with broader sampling across amblyopsid lineages may resolve deeper relationships and enable divergence-time estimation, thereby placing Demogorgonichthys within the temporal framework of karst landscape evolution in the Interior Low Plateau karst region. Finally, monitoring programs at Bobcat Cave should explicitly distinguish Demogorgonichthys from Typhlichthys to generate species-specific abundance datasets and better evaluate population trends through time.

Conclusions

The discovery of Demogorgonichthys arcanus demonstrates that substantial vertebrate diversity can remain undocumented even within intensively studied karst systems and long-monitored landscapes. The occurrence of this lineage in syntopy with Typhlichthys subterraneus, despite deep mitochondrial divergence and lineage-specific degeneration of the vision gene rhodopsin, provides strong evidence that subterranean fish communities can assemble through multiple independent colonization events within a single groundwater system. Together, these findings reveal how caves and aquifers promote extreme phenotypic convergence while preserving deep evolutionary independence among co-occurring lineages. From a conservation perspective, D. arcanus exemplifies the vulnerability of narrowly endemic groundwater vertebrates and reinforces the importance of protecting karst aquifers and recharge areas, even where long-term monitoring and restricted access are already in place.

Methods

Survey approach and specimen collection

We conducted comprehensive biological inventories of terrestrial and aquatic fauna at Bobcat Cave on nine occasions between 2019 and 2025: 26 November 2019, 6 October 2020, 13 September 2023, 22 February 2024, 5 February 2025, 10 February 2025, 18 April 2025, 10 July 2025, and 30 December 2025. These visits were conducted in conjunction with other ongoing research and monitoring efforts on Redstone Arsenal.

Surveys consisted of time-constrained visual encounter surveys (VESs) of all human-accessible passages within the cave, using headlamps and handheld dive lights to illuminate aquatic habitats. Survey effort focused on visually scanning pools, submerged passage margins, and structural features while minimizing physical disturbance. During surveys conducted in 2025, cavefishes were captured using handheld dip nets. Captured individuals were transported alive to the surface in water-filled 500-mL Nalgene bottles for processing. Following examination and tissue sampling (see below), specimens were either preserved or released as appropriate under permit conditions.

Morphological analyses

Collection codes are AUM for the Auburn University Museum of Natural History and UAIC for the University of Alabama Ichthyological Collection. Morphometric measurements followed Cooper and Kuehne77 and Chakrabarty et al.78. Measurements were taken to the nearest 0.1 mm using digital calipers and include: standard length (SL; tip of snout to posterior margin of hypurals), head length (tip of snout to posterior edge of opercle), head width (measured across posterior margins of opercles), snout breadth (head width at its narrowest point), snout depth (vertical depth at the narrowest constriction of the snout), body width (width at dorsal-fin origin), body depth (depth at dorsal-fin origin), predorsal length (snout tip to dorsal-fin origin), preanal length (snout tip to anal-fin origin), prepectoral length (snout tip to insertion of the ventral pectoral-fin ray), vent-to-anal-fin distance (distance from center of vent to anal-fin origin), longest dorsal-fin ray (distance from dorsal-fin origin to maximum fin extent), pectoral-fin length (base of pectoral fin to maximum extent), longest anal-fin ray (anal-fin origin to maximum extent), caudal length (base of caudal fin at midline to maximum extent of fin), caudal peduncle depth (measured at the narrowest point), and upper jaw length (tip of snout to posterior angle of the jaw).

Fin-ray counts are reported using lowercase Roman numerals to indicate that all rays are unbranched. For meristic characters exhibiting variation, ranges are provided along with the number of individuals exhibiting each count; an asterisk denotes the count observed in the holotype. Scale counts were not attempted, as amblyopsid scales are deeply embedded, irregularly arranged, and do not form consistent rows, rendering counts unreliable.

Computed tomography of Demogorgonichthys was made at the Auburn University Center for Polymer and Advanced Composites with a Zeiss Xradia 520 Versa micro-CT scanner. Bones were segmented with 3D Slicer79.

Molecular analyses

DNA extraction, amplification, and sequencing. Genomic DNA was extracted from fin clips obtained from vouchered specimens and individuals released following processing using the Qiagen DNeasy® Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, Maryland, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. We amplified a 1,041-bp fragment of the mitochondrial NADH dehydrogenase subunit 2 (nd2) locus using primers and PCR conditions described by Kocher et al.80 and employed in previous phylogenetic studies of amblyopsid fishes9,27,40,41,81. In addition, we amplified a 874-bp fragment of the nuclear vision-related gene rhodopsin, which consists of a single exon in teleost fishes82, using primers rho-1 (5′-GTCCATATGAATACCCTCAGTACTACC-3′) and rho-2 (5′-TCTTTCCGCAGCACAACGTGG-3′) following protocols in Niemiller et al.27,40. PCR products were purified using ExoSAP-IT (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) and sequenced in both forward and reverse directions at Eurofins Genomics (Louisville, Kentucky, USA). Raw reads were trimmed, ambiguous base calls verified by inspection of electropherograms, and contigs assembled in Geneious Prime v2026.0.1 (https://www.geneious.com). The mitochondrial nd2 locus provides high phylogenetic resolution at shallow to moderate divergence depths in amblyopsids, whereas rhodopsin offers an independent, biparentally inherited nuclear marker that has been widely used to investigate cavefish evolution and visual system degeneration27,40,68.

For a subset of representatives and selected outgroups, complete mitochondrial genome sequences were generated using two complementary approaches: (i) long-range PCR followed by Oxford Nanopore sequencing of whole-mitochondrial amplicons, and (ii) reference-guided assembly from subsampled Illumina whole-genome shotgun reads obtained from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA). Long-range PCR amplification and Nanopore sequencing followed methods described by Alley et al.83. Amplicons were prepared using manufacturer-recommended library preparation protocols and sequenced on Nanopore flow cells at Plasmidsaurus (Louisville, Kentucky, USA). Raw Nanopore reads were basecalled, quality filtered, and assembled into circular mitochondrial genomes using de novo assembly tools in Geneious Prime. Sequence metadata and assembly statistics for each sample are presented in Supplementary Data 1.

For Illumina-based assemblies, paired-end reads were downloaded from the SRA, randomly subsampled to enrich mitochondrial coverage while reducing nuclear background, and assembled using reference-guided mapping to complete mitochondrial genomes of closely related percopsiform taxa. Iterative read mapping with medium–high sensitivity parameters was used to improve assembly completeness and consensus accuracy. Assembly circularity was confirmed by overlap of terminal regions. All mitochondrial genome assemblies were validated by confirming gene order, absence of frameshifts or premature stop codons in protein-coding genes, and consistency with known vertebrate mitochondrial genome structure. To minimize incorporation of nuclear mitochondrial pseudogenes (NUMTs), assemblies were screened for anomalous coverage patterns, internal stop codons, and frameshifts. Accession numbers for all previously published sequences are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

Phylogenetic analyses. Previously published sequences representing other amblyopsid taxa, along with outgroup sequences of Aphredoderus sayanus and A. gibbosus, were downloaded from NCBI GenBank (Supplementary Data 1). Newly generated sequences were aligned with existing data using MUSCLE84 as implemented in MEGA v1285. All novel sequences generated in this study have been deposited in GenBank (Supplementary Data 1).

Uncorrected pairwise sequence divergences (p-distances) were calculated for each locus in MEGA. Optimal models of nucleotide substitution were selected using jModelTest286 under the corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc), with separate partitions specified for first, second, and third codon positions for nd2. Maximum-likelihood (ML) gene genealogies were reconstructed for each locus using PhyML v3.387, and nodal support was assessed using 1,000 nonparametric bootstrap replicates. The mitogenome phylogeny was inferred under the General Time Reversible substitution model using IQ-TREE as implemented in the CIPRES Science Gateway. Divergence times were estimated using the RelTime method88 in MEGA, yielding an ultrametric timetree calibrated at the node representing the divergence of Percopsis from the rest of Percopsiformes. This split corresponds to the deepest internal split within the order 60 ± 3 Ma (95% CI = 54.12–64.88 Ma)27,40.

Conservation assessment

We conducted conservation status assessments for the newly described cavefish species using both the NatureServe and IUCN Red List frameworks. Both systems assign taxa to one of seven categories along a continuum of increasing extinction risk, based on standardized criteria incorporating geographic range size, population status, threats, and trends. NatureServe conservation status was evaluated using the NatureServe Rank Calculator v3.18689, following the methodology and criteria outlined in Master et al.90. This assessment integrates measures of rarity, population size, geographic restriction, environmental specificity, and vulnerability to threats to derive a global conservation rank. The IUCN Red List category was determined using RAMAS Red List v3.091 in accordance with the most recent IUCN Red List Categories and Criteria92. Assessments focused primarily on geographic range metrics and inferred threats, given the limited information available for population size and trends typical of newly described subterranean taxa. Geographic range size was quantified using the web-based program GeoCAT93. Extent of occurrence (EOO) was calculated as a minimum convex polygon encompassing all known localities, and area of occupancy (AOO) was estimated using a 2 × 2 km grid (4 km2) following IUCN-recommended standards89,92. Potential threats were identified and categorized using the standardized threat classification scheme of Salafsky et al.94. These threat assessments were used to inform both the NatureServe and IUCN evaluations.

Permits, animal care, and ethical compliance

All fieldwork and specimen collection were conducted in accordance with applicable federal, state, and institutional regulations. Access to Bobcat Cave and all biological surveys on Redstone Arsenal were authorized by the U.S. Army Garrison–Redstone Arsenal. Collection, handling, and tissue sampling of fishes were conducted under scientific collection permits issued by the Alabama Department of Conservation and Natural Resources (permit nos. 2025112916068680 and 2025112916268680). Animal handling protocols followed established ethical guidelines for the use of fishes in research and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at The University of Alabama in Huntsville (protocol no. 2023.R03.Niemiller.Subterranean).

Efforts were made to minimize stress, injury, and mortality during capture and processing. Individuals captured for morphological and genetic analyses were handled briefly, maintained in clean, aerated cave water during processing, and either preserved as voucher specimens or released at the site of capture following tissue sampling, as permitted. The number of individuals collected was minimized and limited to that necessary to document diagnostic morphological characters and obtain genetic material for phylogenetic analyses. Surveys and collections were conducted in a manner designed to minimize disturbance to cave habitats and associated fauna, including avoidance of unnecessary traversal through aquatic habitats and strict adherence to biosecurity protocols to reduce the risk of pathogen introduction or transfer among cave systems.
