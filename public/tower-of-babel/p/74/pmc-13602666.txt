Effect of a four-month indirect vision training program on ergonomic posture and class I cavity preparation performance in dental students
Authors: Zeynep Ceren Çeli̇k, Gül Dinç Ata, Halil Sağlam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of an indirect vision training (IVT) program on dental students’ working postures and cavity preparation performance, using a modified CADEP (Compliance Assessment of Dental Ergonomic Posture) scoring system.Materials and methodsIn this single-group pre–post quasi-experimental study, The IVT group consisted of 35 third-year dental students, while the control group comprised 67 third-year students from the subsequent academic year who completed the same preclinical restorative dentistry curriculum without receiving IVT. They performed Class I cavity preparations on teeth #16 and #46 using phantom heads. Ergonomic posture was assessed using a modified CADEP scale before and after training. Cavity preparation scores were evaluated for tooth #16 and #46. Data were analyzed using Chi-square, Mann–Whitney U, and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Repeated measurements were additionally evaluated using generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsFollowing indirect vision training, significant improvements were observed in both ergonomic posture and cavity preparation performance. For Tooth 16, total CADEP scores increased from 47.86 to 62.29 (p < 0.001), while cavity preparation performance scores improved from 64.43 to 72.43 (p < 0.001). For Tooth 46, total CADEP scores increased from 42.29 to 53.86 (p = 0.006), and performance scores improved from 64.14 to 69.91 (p < 0.001). Significant improvements were detected in CADEP-2, CADEP-3, CADEP-6, and CADEP-7 for Tooth 16, and in CADEP-6 and CADEP-7 for Tooth 46. No significant differences were observed according to academic year or gender for most parameters (p > 0.05).ConclusionThis study showed that a 16-week indirect vision training program was associated with improvements in ergonomic posture and Class I cavity preparation performance, particularly in lumbar support, stool use, feet positioning, and dental light positioning.Keywords: Indirect vision training, Dental ergonomics, Cavity preparation, Dental education

Introduction

In dental practice, clinicians frequently adopt suboptimal working postures to improve visual access to the operative field, which can contribute to the development of musculoskeletal disorders [1]. Postural disorders in dentists arise due to forward head posture, excessive bending and rotation of the neck, forward bending of the waist, shoulder elevation, and, in general, the bending or rotation of the back and neck [1–3]. Tamo et al. [4], have reported a high prevalence of musculoskeletal disorders among dentists in North East India, with lower back and neck pain being most common, highlighting the critical need for ergonomic awareness and structured training early in dental education. Corrocher et al. reported that approximately 65% of undergraduate dental students were exposed to a high risk of developing musculoskeletal disorders during restorative preclinical laboratory activities, regardless of gender, type of procedure, or treated region of the mouth [5]. The occupational challenges faced by dentists can be mitigated through effective ergonomics training provided during undergraduate education.

In certain studies on indirect vision, training tools have been employed that require students to trace lines while observing through a mirror [6–8]In line with recent studies [9, 10] highlighting the importance of perceptual and psychomotor skills in dental training, Shely et al. [11] demonstrated that home-based indirect vision training using the PhantHome tool significantly enhanced students’ spatial perception, orientation, and fine motor skills, underlining the value of early mirror-based practice for improving clinical posture and performance. The Mirroprep device, developed based on principles of indirect working techniques, is a commercially available tool. Students reported feeling that Mirroprep would assist them in performing cavity preparations on actual patients [9]. However, this study did not assess the students’ improvement in executing real dental procedures following their training sessions.

The Jumpstart Mirror Trainer (Jumpstart Dental Education, LLC, Fairfax, VA, USA) has been developed as a device that allows students to engage in activities that directly replicate the orientation and reference points of human teeth [7]. It consists of a rotating jaw, a pen-like handpiece, and numerous interchangeable tooth sets that facilitate the simulation of dental procedures. In a study comparing the efficacy of the Jumpstart Mirror Trainer with that of Mirroprep in teaching indirect motor skills, it was concluded that students could improve their indirect motor skills using the Jumpstart Mirror Trainer without requiring in-class practice sessions, and that this device was more effective than other existing methods. Studies related to indirect vision typically utilize large mirrors or mouth mirrors [7]. However, these set of trainings are assessed through line tracing on paper or plastic sheets.

Mirror-based indirect vision training, such as that provided by the Mirrosistant device, has been shown to enhance students’ spatial awareness and operational coordination. However, the tool’s limited reflection angles—restricted to vertical and frontal positions—fail to simulate the full range of indirect vision demands encountered in clinical settings. Although virtual simulation systems like Voxel-Man, Kobra Simulator, and SIMODONT are increasingly incorporated into preclinical education and offer promising advancements in fine motor skill development and hand–eye coordination, they also present certain drawbacks, including high setup costs, limited realism in tactile feedback, and lack of communication skill training. These findings suggest that while both traditional and virtual methods have their respective advantages, mirror-based training remains a valuable and accessible component of preclinical dental education.

Although previous studies have demonstrated the importance of ergonomic education [12–15] and have evaluated various indirect vision training methods, most have focused primarily on psychomotor performance, mirror skills, or tracing exercises. The modified version of Compliance Assessment of Dental Ergonomic Posture Requirements Scale (CADEP) used in this study was introduced by [16] from the original CADEP instrument developed by Garcia et al. [17] The original CADEP underwent face and content validity assessment by a panel of eight experts in dental ergonomics and demonstrated satisfactory intra- and inter-observer reproducibility following evaluator calibration. In the present study, the instrument was adapted for use in a preclinical phantom-head setting while maintaining its core ergonomic domains and scoring structure.

Limited evidence is available regarding the simultaneous assessment of ergonomic posture and cavity preparation performance during standardized operative procedures. Furthermore, few studies have investigated these outcomes using a structured ergonomic assessment tool such as the modified CADEP. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of indirect vision training on both ergonomic posture and cavity preparation performance in dental students.

The null hypothesis was that indirect vision training would not significantly affect ergonomic posture or cavity preparation performance among dental students.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Social and Humanities Research and Publication Ethics Committee of Bursa Uludag University Faculty of Medicine (Approval No: 2022-07/26). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

Study design

This study was designed as a controlled quasi-experimental before–after educational intervention including an indirect vision training (IVT) group (n = 38) and a concurrent control group (n = 67). IVT participants underwent an indirect vision training program, and their working postures were assessed at two time points: immediately before and immediately after the intervention (Fig. 1). All participants were third-year dental students who had not yet received phantom-head cavity preparation training and had no previous formal indirect vision training experience.

Participant flow diagram

The primary outcome was the change in total CADEP score. Secondary outcomes included cavity preparation performance scores, while item-level CADEP analyses were considered exploratory.

Participants and inclusion criteria

The study population consisted of third-year dental students at Bursa Uludag University Faculty of Dentistry. Inclusion criteria were as follows:

Students lacking regular attendance in the courses and who did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded (n = 3).

Indirect Vision Training (IVT) and cavity preparation procedures

The initial application of indirect vision training involves an extraoral line-tracing exercise where students use a mirrored drawing box (25 cm × 38 cm × 20 cm), a training booklet with The exercise sheet consisted of 13 tracing tasks, including basic geometric figures and occlusal fissure–mimicking patterns designed to simulate Class I cavity preparation morphology. Students were instructed to trace continuous lines within predefined double boundaries while relying exclusively on mirror vision. The completion time for each exercise was recorded in seconds. The modified box features side openings (15 cm × 13 cm) for hand access, a front-mounted mirror (25 cm × 15 cm), and a lid covered with black adhesive paper containing a viewing window (22 cm × 13 cm) (Fig. 2).

Indirect vision training protocol and representative exercises. (A) Standardized indirect vision training sheet used during the intervention (B) Representative student-completed exercise demonstrating tracing performance under indirect vision conditions (C) Mirror-box indirect vision exercise

In the second application, students perform an intraoral exercise on acrylic jaws fixed to the box, preparing standardized Class I occlusal cavities on tooth #16 (3 mm depth, buccolingual width ≤ 1/3 intertubercular distance) using a micromotor, angled handpiece, and #12 round diamond bur, while relying solely on mirror-reflected vision.

To assess the impact of indirect vision training on working posture, participants performed standardized Class I cavity preparations on teeth #16, and #46 (maxillary and mandibular right molars). Preparations were competed with an occlusal depth of 3 mm and a buccolingual width not exceeding one-third of the intertubercular distance, following the technique taught in the Restorative Dentistry course.

Exercises were conducted using phantom heads and artificial resin teeth in the preclinical phantom laboratory. Students adhered to ergonomic posture standards taught in the “Ergonomics in Dentistry” course. The indirect vision training was repeated once weekly for 16 weeks, with each session lasting one hour. The procedures were conducted under instructor supervision using standardized preclinical laboratory conditions. Cavity preparation performance was evaluated by comparing baseline and final assessment scores.

Posture evaluation tool: modified CADEP (CADEP)

Postures were assessed using a modified version of CADEP adapted from the method described by Wajngarten at al [16] This tool enabled direct observation of ergonomic compliance through photographs and included ten evaluation items related to both external factors (e.g., chair and light positioning) and the alignment of body segments (upper limbs, lower limbs, torso) Appropriate posture items were scored as 10 point, partially appropriate items as 5 points, and inappropriate items as 0 points. The total score was calculated by summing the ten items, with higher scores indicating better ergonomic compliance and a maximum possible score of 100.

Cavity assessment

Cavity preparations were evaluated by the supervising instructors (ZCC & GDA) according to seven predefined criteria, including outline form, bucco-lingual width, mesio-distal width, occlusal cavity depth, flatness and smoothness of the cavity floor, and inclination of the cavity walls. Each parameter was scored as either adequate (+ 10%) or inadequate (− 10%). The total percentage score obtained from these criteria represented the student’s operative performance score. Statistical analysis

For inferential analyses, participants were stratified according to study group (control vs. intervention). The Chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables between groups, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied to evaluate differences in continuous variables between two independent groups.

To assess repeated measurements, the variables “Tooth #16 Baseline”, “Tooth #16 Follow-up”, “Tooth #46 Baseline”, and “Tooth #46 Follow-up” were analyzed within a framework in which study group was specified as a between-subject factor and participant identification number was included as the subject-level variable. Within-group changes over time were initially evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. In addition, Poisson generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) were fitted to account for repeated observations and to evaluate the effects of time, group, and their interaction.

To further explore model estimates and identify differences between groups and time points, post-hoc analyses were conducted using model-based estimated marginal means and contrast procedures. Approximate R² values were calculated to quantify effect sizes. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 102 students were included in the study, comprising 35 students in the training group (34.3%) and 67 students in the control group (65.7%). Overall, 64 participants (62.7%) were female and 38 (37.3%) were male.

Discussion

This study evaluated dental students’ working postures before and after ergonomic training using a modified CADEP scoring system, focusing on parameters critical to preventing musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) in clinical practice. Our findings support the efficacy of structured ergonomic instruction in improving specific posture-related variables, aligning with recent literature that emphasizes the need for early ergonomic interventions in dental education.

There are studies conducted among dental students and professionals reporting that women tend to exhibit higher pain scores [18–21]. For instance, Felemban et al. [18] reported a 91.2% prevalence of musculoskeletal pain among dental students, with neck pain in 69.2%—more common in females (OR = 2.98, 95% CI: 1.3–6.7). In our study, the finding that post-training scores were significantly lower in female participants compared to males supports this observation. The fact that female participants demonstrated lower postural scores may explain their higher pain levels.

One of the most notable improvements was observed in lumbar support during operation, where significant enhancement was noted post-training for both tooth #16 (p < 0.001) and, to a lesser extent, tooth #46 (p = 0.062). Similarly, operator stool use and feet positioning on the floor improved significantly post-training for tooth #16 (p = 0.018 and p = 0.029, respectively). These outcomes reflect a shift toward more ergonomically sound working habits following indirect vision and posture education.

In their systematic review, Danylak et al. (2023) [22] highlighted the absence of uniform ergonomic training protocols in dental curricula and emphasized the critical role of early education in mitigating MSD risk. They also reported that sustained improvements in posture are rarely measured over time, a gap that our before-and-after design seeks to address, albeit in a short-term framework. Although we did not use motion capture or real-time sensor-based feedback as some studies in the review recommended, our application of structured observational tools like CADEP aligns with the validated methods described in several high-quality studies reviewed.

Interestingly, parameters such as the working and supporting hand positions, and the distance between operator’s eyes and the patient’s mouth, did not show statistically significant changes (p > 0.05). These findings suggest that certain ergonomic habits may be more resistant to change or may require more individualized or prolonged interventions to correct. This is consistent with Danylak et al.‘s observation that even when ergonomic principles are introduced, ingrained habits and clinical constraints may limit immediate improvements across all postural domains.

The distinction between arches was also highlighted in a 2020 study by Kamal et al., [2] which found that posture scores were significantly worse when students worked on the posterior mandibular arch (TP2) compared to the maxillary arch (TP1), particularly when magnification was not used. In our findings, students generally showed more favorable ergonomic outcomes during procedures on the maxillary arch (#16) than on the mandibular arch (#46), mirroring Kamal et al.‘s observations.

One likely explanation is the visual access and working distance challenges inherent in mandibular posterior regions. Achieving a proper working posture without compromising field of view often leads to increased neck flexion, trunk rotation, and poor limb positioning, especially in the absence of aids like magnifying loupes. Kamal et al. [2] reported significantly better posture when magnification was used, especially for mandibular work, suggesting that such equipment might compensate for the ergonomic strain specific to mandibular regions—a factor not included in our current study but highly relevant for future interventions.

The findings of our study closely align with the results reported by Garcia et al. [23] who demonstrated that a structured indirect vision training program significantly improved specific ergonomic posture parameters among dental students during preclinical procedures. In our study, significant improvements were observed particularly in parameters that relate to lumbar support (p < 0.001 for #16), operator stool use (p = 0.018 for #16), feet position on the floor (p = 0.029 for #16 and p = 0.02 for #46), and dental light positioning (p = 0.018 for #16 and p = 0.02 for #46). These findings mirror those of Garcia et al., who reported statistically significant gains in three core CADEP items after training: angle of the spine (p < 0.001), operator–mannequin distance (p < 0.001), and working arm position (p = 0.008). Danylak et al. [22] underscored the importance of feedback mechanisms to reinforce correct posture, which may explain why purely instructional interventions without real-time feedback may be insufficient for altering certain spatial or visual access behaviors.

However, our data showed limited improvement in fine-motor or visually dependent ergonomic parameters, such as distance between operator’s eyes and patient’s mouth and working/supported hand positions, with all p-values > 0.05. Similarly, Garcia et al. [23] noted that only a subset of CADEP items showed significant change, specifically those associated with visual field accessibility and posture-related visibility. Items more associated with habitual or deeply ingrained positioning did not significantly improve in either study, suggesting that behavioral postural habits may be more resistant to change than posture influenced by visibility or equipment setup.

Another parallel is seen in the training method’s pedagogical design. Garcia et al. [23] implemented a scaffolding approach beginning with simple two-dimensional mirror-based line tracing and progressing to complex intraoral cavity preparation tasks. While our study did not employ such a scaffolded program, the positive outcomes in gross postural corrections (lumbar support, stool use, and lighting) suggest that even non-tiered ergonomic interventions can be effective when clearly structured and practiced repetitively, especially in conditions that mimic clinical environments [24].

Garcia et al. [23]also highlighted that ergonomic parameters involving operator-mannequin distance and spinal alignment were significantly affected by visual field optimization, supporting our finding that improvements in dental light positioning and foot support—both related to positioning and comfort—are effective ergonomic markers of progress.

Our findings align with Bozkurt et al. [25], who identified the back, neck, and lower back as the most affected regions by work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WRMSDs) in dentists, underscoring the importance of early ergonomic intervention. The significant improvements we observed in lumbar support and operator stool use suggest that targeted training can effectively address these high-risk areas before poor postural habits become established.

Our study found a significant improvement in operator stool use following ergonomic training, particularly during maxillary arch procedures (p = 0.018 for #16), suggesting enhanced awareness of seated posture. This aligns with Dylla and Forrest [26] emphasis on the foundational role of operator positioning, where establishing a stable, aligned sitting posture is essential before adjusting the patient or equipment—highlighting that proper stool use is a primary strategy in maintaining neutral spinal alignment and minimizing musculoskeletal stress in dental professionals.

While a 16-year prospective study [27] found no association between baseline neck muscle strength and future neck disability (β < 0.1 for all variables; mean NDI after 16 years: 3.3–19.6), a cross-sectional study of 199 dental students revealed that 69% reported musculoskeletal pain, with the most affected areas being the hand/elbow (23%) and head (19%). These contrasting findings highlight that although neck strength and mobility may not predict long-term neck disability in a general population, ergonomic strain in clinical environments can lead to a high prevalence of short-term musculoskeletal symptoms in dental students [19, 20, 28].

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that a structured 16-week indirect vision training program significantly improved both ergonomic posture and Class I cavity preparation performance in preclinical dental students. The greatest improvements were observed in lumbar support, operator stool use, feet positioning, and dental light positioning, whereas hand positioning and operator–patient working distance showed limited change, suggesting that these components may require longer training periods or individualized feedback.

A novel aspect of this study is the use of a customized indirect vision training sheet incorporating occlusal fissure–mimicking exercises specifically designed to simulate the morphology encountered during Class I cavity preparation.

Limitations

Our study was limited by the short follow-up period, use of observational scoring without technological adjuncts, and evaluation within a preclinical setting. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal tracking of postural retention, integration of wearable sensors, and possibly video-based posture feedback to enhance the precision and sustainability of ergonomic improvement. Another limitation is that independent blinded assessors were not available, which may have introduced assessment bias despite the use of predefined scoring criteria. Although standardized scoring criteria were used throughout the study, the absence of reliability testing may have influenced the consistency of outcome assessment. Moreover, participants were not informed of the exact assessment timing, which may have minimized but not eliminated the potential Hawthorne effect.
