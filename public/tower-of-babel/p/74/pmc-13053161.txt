Effectiveness and Cost-Utility of Anti-Adhesion Agents in Intra-Abdominal Surgery Patients
Authors: Yoon Suk Lee, Seeun Park, Minseo Park, U-Syn Ha, Sung Jong Lee, Tae-Jin Lee, Euichul Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdhesive intestinal obstruction (AIO) is a common and critical complication of intra-abdominal surgery, often requiring preventative measures such as using anti-adhesion agents (AAA). However, despite their prevalence and significance, a notable lack of long-term and large-scale clinical evidence exists supporting the effectiveness and cost-utility of these agents. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness and cost-utility of applying AAA in patients undergoing intra-abdominal surgery.MethodsThis is a retrospective observational study utilizing the Korean National Health Insurance Database in combination with the data from the Clinical Data Warehouse collected from six university-affiliated hospitals in Korea. The study included patients who underwent intra-abdominal surgeries between April and December 2016, with 2-year follow-up. The effectiveness of AAA was assessed by the incidence rate of AIO, and cost-utility was evaluated through calculating quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) and direct costs. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to confirm the consistency and robustness of the study findings.ResultsA total of 2,831 patients were included in the study. Among them, 1,365 (47.2%) received AAA while 1,466 (52.8%) did not. The group that received AAA exhibited a significantly lower incidence of AIO, with a rate of 12.4% compared to 18.5% in the non-user group. Utility scores were higher in the user group, averaging 1.992 QALYs versus 1.986 in the non-user group. Direct costs were significantly lower in the user group, totaling 3,794 USD, which is 1,330 USD less than those incurred by the non-user group. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses reinforced these findings, demonstrating consistent reductions in AIO incidence, increased QALYs, and decreased costs observed across various subgroups, particularly among older patients and those without chronic conditions.ConclusionThese findings highlight the clinical effectiveness and cost-utility of AAA in patients undergoing intra-abdominal surgery. By significantly reducing the incidence of AIO and lowering healthcare costs, these agents provide empirical evidence supporting their broader adoption in clinical practice. This study addresses critical gaps in the understanding of postoperative management and offers valuable insights that could inform future healthcare policies.

INTRODUCTION

Adhesive intestinal obstruction (AIO) is a frequent yet critical postoperative complication following intra-abdominal surgery, manifesting through symptoms such as abdominal discomfort, nausea, vomiting, abdominal distension, and constipation.12 These symptoms often necessitate multiple treatment approaches, including pharmacological management, hospitalization, and surgical intervention, leading to increased healthcare expenditure and financial burdens.34 Consequently, anti-adhesion agents (AAA) are widely used to prevent AIO and reduce the risk of recurrence. However, the evidence regarding their effectiveness and cost-utility remains heterogeneous.

Previous research suggests that the postoperative effectiveness of AAA varies based on the type of surgery, the specific agent utilized, and patient and disease characteristics. Furthermore, the level of supporting evidence for these outcomes remains limited, highlighting a dearth of long-term and large-scale clinical evidence. One study evaluated the incidence rate of AIO among a total of 181 patients in Italy (91 in the treatment group and 90 in the control group) to assess the effectiveness of an agent (Icodextrin 4% Solution) after abdominal surgery, and there were no statistically significant differences found between the two groups.5 Another study, which evaluated the incidence of small bowel obstruction to evaluate the effectiveness of using an agent (Seprafilm®) following colorectal surgery in 167 patients, revealed a significant reduction in the incidence of small bowel obstruction in the treatment group (7.1%) compared to control group (11.4%).6 A systematic review and meta-analysis of 28 trials pertaining to AAA in abdominal surgery found conflicting results: some agents reduced the incidence of AIO, while others showed no significant difference between groups.7 Additionally, the methodology sections in some of the studies were described ambiguously, resulting in a low to moderate level of evidence. Moreover, the majority of studies were financially supported by commercial entities, which may raise concerns about potential biases toward positive findings. Research on the effectiveness of AAA in Korea has yielded mixed results, with the incidence rate of AIO either decreasing or showing no significant difference.8910

Moreover, the utilization of AAA after surgery incurs additional costs of the agents, yet comprehensive evaluations of their cost-utility remain insufficiently explored. In particular, there is a specific need for solid evidence concerning the cost-utility of AAA, especially in light of recent discussions about their potential inclusion in national health insurance coverage. The National Health Insurance system assesses the reimbursement eligibility of medical services based on their medical effectiveness and the financial sustainability of the system. Although the medical effectiveness of AAA is considered acknowledged, consistent and robust evidence supporting their cost-effectiveness remains insufficient. Currently, partial reimbursement for AAA, initiated in April 2016, has been accompanied by systematic efforts to evaluate their cost-utility. To address the research gap requiring scientific evidence and to meet pressing societal needs, this study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness and cost-utility of AAA in patients underwent intra-abdominal surgery.

METHODS

Study population

The study population consisted of patients with no history of abdominal surgery who underwent intra-abdominal surgery during the preceding two years. Intra-abdominal surgeries included open surgeries, namely gastrectomy, colectomy, proctectomy, ileectomy, pancreatectomy, splenectomy, and prostatectomy, while laparoscopic surgeries were excluded. The study utilized a combined database integrating data from the National Health Insurance Database (NHID) and the Clinical Data Warehouse (CDW) of a six-university hospital network affiliated with The Catholic University of Korea (Fig. 1). These hospitals include one tertiary and five secondary-level institutions, located across multiple regions, which enhances the representativeness and generalizability of the study population. All surgical procedures were performed by faculty surgeons, ensuring consistent standards of clinical practice across institutions.

From the NHID, 11,037 cases meeting the inclusion criteria were extracted for the period between April 1 and December 31, 2016. Similarly, 10,613 cases were retrieved from the CDW during the same timeframe. These two datasets were merged using patient-specific identifiers, including date of birth, sex, codes for intra-abdominal surgeries, admission dates, and healthcare facility details. The concordance rate for the clinical information between these datasets was 91.10%.

Among merged cases, those falling into the following categories were excluded: surgeries covered by the Diagnostic-Related Groups (DRG) Payment System (n = 4,832), an observation period of less than two years after surgery (n = 183), patients who underwent more than two intra-abdominal surgeries during the observation period (n = 1,184), and other cases (n = 297). The other cases included those in which prior intra-abdominal surgeries could be inferred, such as surgeries for AIO, partial colectomies due to bowel obstructions, hernia repairs, pelvic adhesiolysis procedures, and repeated cesarean sections. Furthermore, cases were excluded if patients received treatment in departments other than surgery or urology. During the creation of the integrated datasets, cases lacking date information were excluded, resulting in the exclusion of monthly recurring claims (NHID: 8,581 cases and CDW: 9,051), as well as split claims (N = 309).

In conclusion, a total of 2,831 patients who underwent intra-abdominal surgery were included in the final analysis. Among them, 1,365 patients had AAA used, while the remaining 1,466 did not. All patients were followed for two years post-surgery to assess the incidence of AIO. This follow-up period was determined based on evidence indicating that the majority of cases occur within two years of surgery,1112 maximizing the evaluation of AAA effectiveness by excluding the possibility of AIO occurring long after surgery.

Measures

Incidence of adhesive intestinal obstruction

The incidence of AIO was operationally defined with reference to prior research and consideration of the data availability from the processed databases. The operational definition was as follows: 1) reported symptoms, 2) history of non-surgical treatments, 3) surgical interventions, and 4) diagnostic conclusions of AIO. Specifically, 1) the presence of at least three of the following symptoms after the resumption of oral intake following surgery: nausea or vomiting, cramping abdominal pain, abdominal distension, or indigestion1314; 2) non-surgical treatments identified through specific procedure codes related to the management of AIO: fasting, intravenous therapy, parenteral nutrition, nutritional supplementation, or nasogastric/nasoenteral tube insertion; 3) surgical interventions: diagnostic laparotomy, small bowel resection, adhesiolysis for intestinal obstruction, general adhesiolysis, and pelvic adhesiolysis; and 4) the presence of diagnostic codes related to adhesions: those for adhesive ileus, ileus, postoperative ileus, peritonitis, pelvic adhesiolysis, and intra-abdominal adhesions.

Cost

The cost of intra-abdominal surgery was calculated based on direct costs, including those for the surgery itself and for the subsequent management. These costs encompassed both medical (i.e., physician consultation, hospitalization, medication, injection, anesthesia, therapy, surgery, laboratory tests, imaging, and special medical equipment usage fees) and non-medical expenditures (i.e., transportation and caregiving costs).15 Medical expenses were computed using reimbursement data from the NHID, while costs associated with non-reimbursable AAA were derived from the CDW. The expenses related to transportation and caregiving were estimated based on data from the Korean Medical Panel and the Korean Caregiver Association. All costs were measured in 2016 exchange rates and converted as follows: 1,160 KW to 1 USD, using the International Monetary Fund’s official exchange rate.

Utility score: quality-adjusted life year (QALYs)

To evaluate cost-utility, QALYs were calculated based on the methodology established by Colonna et al.16 Their study employed a Markov Model to simulate clinical pathways and postoperative health outcomes following intra-abdominal surgery, categorizing patient statuses into ‘stable,’ ‘medical treatment,’ ‘surgery,’ and ‘death.’ Health-related quality of life, measured in QALYs, was quantified for each of these scenarios. A score of 1 was assigned to represent a state of optimal health, and a score of 0 was assigned to represent death. Scores for various postoperative conditions were calculated using these as reference points, reflecting the impact of different circumstances and complications (Table 1).

Statistical analysis

To evaluate the effectiveness and cost-utility of AAA, incidence of AIO, the direct costs, and utility scores were calculated and compared between the group that utilized AAA and the non-user group using χ2 test, t-test. For the cost-utility analysis, the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) was calculated, representing the additional cost required to achieve one unit of effect. A lower ICER value indicates a more cost-effective alternative. The ICER is calculated by listing the non-dominated alternatives in order of increasing effectiveness and determining the cost-effectiveness ratios of these alternatives to identify the optimal choice.17 It is computed by dividing the incremental cost (ΔCost) by the incremental effectiveness (ΔEffectiveness).

For subgroup analyses, participants were stratified by sex, age groups, the 11 chronic conditions, designated by the Ministry of Health and Welfare (i.e., mental and behavioral disorders, nervous system diseases, hypertensive diseases, liver diseases, diabetes, other chronic obstructive pulmonary diseases, cerebrovascular diseases, intracranial injuries, thyroid disorders, heart diseases, and epilepsy), and the history of malignant tumor. Subsequently, the effectiveness and cost-utility of AAA were analyzed through comparisons between users and non-users within these subgroups.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted by applying varying utility scores and AAA costs to evaluate and confirm the robustness of the research results. In this process, utility scores provided by Behman et al. were used as transformation values, and a 20% and 40% increase in the price of the AAA was applied.18 All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide 7.15 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Ethics statement

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of The Catholic University of Korea and the requirement for informed consent was waived (approval No. KC21WESB0248).

RESULTS

Among the 2,831 study participants, 47.2% (n = 1,365) received AAA. When stratified by participant characteristics, females had a significantly higher usage rate (53.3%) than males (43.7%). Age-related differences were also observed, with the highest usage rate in participants aged 20–39 (56.3%) and the lowest in those aged 10–19 (16.7%), the latter being significantly lower than in other age groups. Notably, income quintiles did not yield any statistically significant differences in the rates of AAA usage. With respect to disease attributes, individuals without chronic conditions or malignant tumor exhibited a significantly heightened propensity for having AAA (Table 2).

When analyzing by age groups, among participants in the 40-59 and 60 or over age groups, the use of AAA demonstrated statistically significant improvements across all three indicators: a lower incidence of AIO, higher QALYs, and reduced direct costs. In the 20–39 age group, AAA use was associated with a lower incidence of AIO (0% vs. 8.6%). Among the 0–19 and 20–39 age groups, AAA use showed a tendency toward cost-utility, though the results were not statistically significant.

Among participants with chronic conditions, the utilization of AAA was associated only with a reduction in direct costs (4,102 USD vs. 6,138 USD). While other indicators were not statistically significant, a trend toward cost-utility was observed in this subgroup. Similar patterns were noted among participants without malignant tumors, where AAA use was linked to a significant reduction in direct costs (3,044 USD vs. 3,553 USD).

In the subgroup analysis based on the presence of malignant tumors, AAA use was associated with lower AIO incidence rates, higher QALYs, and reduced direct costs. However, most of these differences were not statistically significant. Nonetheless, a tendency toward cost-effectiveness was noted.

DISCUSSION

This study found that the utilization of AAA in intra-abdominal surgery is significantly associated with reduced post-operative AIO incidence rates, increased QALYs, and lowered direct costs. Subgroup analyses consistently indicated that AAA were either cost-effective or exhibited a tendency toward cost-utility across all subgroups. Sensitivity analyses, incorporating variations in utility scores and the price of AAA, further reaffirmed their cost-utility superiority.

This research supports previous research, suggesting that AAA use may be associated with reduced AIO incidence and favorable cost-utility outcomes.5131920212223 Krielen et al.23 reported similar results in colorectal surgeries, with AAA use linked to lower adhesion rates (8.6% vs. 6.2%) and cost-effective in open surgeries. The relatively higher AIO incidence in our study may reflect the inclusion of only open surgeries and the use of claims-based definitions, which may capture a broader range of clinical events. Nonetheless, both studies show a consistent pattern of reduced adhesion-related complications with AAA use.

While our findings are consistent with previous studies, conflicting evidence exists. For example, Catena et al.,5 found no significant benefit in preventing adhesive small bowel obstruction following AAA use in colorectal surgeries. This discrepancy may reflect differences in study design, surgical scope, and outcome definitions. Unlike their single-center study of a specific product, our analysis included multiple AAA types across diverse open intra-abdominal procedures in a multi-institutional setting. Furthermore, our definition of AIO encompasses a broader spectrum of conditions than the adhesive small bowel obstruction category used in their study, which may account for some of the observed differences.

In terms of economic outcomes, the findings of this study align with previous research, demonstrating that the use of AAA in open abdominal surgery reduces direct healthcare costs compared to non-use ($4,376 vs. $4,428).23 Another study further supports this, estimating potential savings of $328 to $680 with AAA implementation.24 Taken together, these findings substantiate the growing body of evidence supporting the clinical and economic benefits of AAA.

To further contextualize the findings, subgroup analyses were conducted. Notably, greater benefits of AAA use were observed among females and older patients. While causal mechanisms cannot be established, several explanations are plausible. Older adults may face higher baseline risk of adhesion-related complications due to reduced physiologic reserve, and females may have greater adhesion susceptibility related to gynecologic history. Additionally, surgeons may be more likely to use AAA in patients perceived as high risk. These patterns may suggest clinically meaningful heterogeneity in AAA effectiveness, though unmeasured confounding cannot be ruled out.

This study possesses several distinctive strengths. Notably, it includes a large cohort of 2,831 patients undergoing diverse intra-abdominal surgeries, enhancing the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, this study is unique in its utilization of not only clinical data sourced from medical institutions but also the integration of NHID. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to link these data sources to evaluate the effectiveness and cost-utility of AAA. In addition, the results of this study provide evidence for clinical implications. This can serve as a solid foundation for clinical decision-making regarding the use of AAA, ultimately contributing to the reduction of postoperative complications, care costs, and the promotion of patient health. From a policy perspective, since April 2016, AAA have been subject to temporary reimbursement, and it has now been several years since this policy was implemented. The cost-effectiveness evaluation results derived from this study are expected to serve as foundational data for future decisions regarding the transition of AAA to general reimbursement. The findings are anticipated to objectively confirm the cost-effectiveness of AAA, thereby providing essential data for policy decisions related to health insurance coverage of these agents. This research is particularly timely and relevant, addressing current healthcare challenges and contributing valuable insights for both clinical practice and healthcare policy. Ultimately, this is expected to reduce the financial burden of medical expenses on the public and contribute to the establishment of a medical safety net that ensures appropriate medical care.

Despite the strengths of this study, several limitations should be noted. First, obstetrics and gynecology (OBGY) surgeries were excluded due to their inclusion in the DRG reimbursement system, which is known to reduce unnecessary medical services, treatment intensity, and hospital stay.2526 In this context, providers may be hesitant to prescribe ancillary services, such as AAA. Since 2002, most OBGY surgeries have been covered by the DRG, with a notable decline in related medical expenditures.27 Their exclusion was necessary to ensure an unbiased sample, although they account for a substantial portion of intra-abdominal surgeries.

Second, there is heterogeneity in AAA use. These agents vary in composition, material properties, and dosing protocols. Future research should examine the effectiveness and cost-utility of AAA according to specific types, components, and administration methods to better capture this variation.

Third, AAA use was not randomized, and the underlying rationale for its application could not be determined from the available data. As AAA use largely depends on physician discretion, some baseline differences between groups likely reflect real-world practice rather than systematic bias. Nonetheless, all patients underwent major open intra-abdominal surgeries with broadly comparable indications, which may mitigate concerns about case complexity.

Fourth, this study did not account for the heterogeneity of intra-abdominal surgical procedures. Variations in surgical type and extent may influence both the likelihood of AAA use and the risk of postoperative complications such as AIO. This procedural variability represents a potential source of confounding and warrants stratified analyses in future research.

Fifth, although the Markov model is a framework that is broadly applicable across healthcare systems, the model parameters used in this study were not derived from Korean populations. As such, the contextual relevance of the cost-utility results may be partly compromised.

Finally, while the current study concentrated solely on patients undergoing intra-abdominal surgery, future endeavors should address the assessment of the cost-utility of AAA in other surgical contexts where their usage is prevalent, such as in musculoskeletal and vascular surgeries.

In conclusion, this study suggests that the use of AAA in intra-abdominal surgery is associated with lower incidence of AIO, higher utility scores, and reduced medical costs. These associations were consistent across subgroups and sensitivity analyses. AAA may thus be considered a potentially cost-effective strategy for mitigating the burden of AIO.
