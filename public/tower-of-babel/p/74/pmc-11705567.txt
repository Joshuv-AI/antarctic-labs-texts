Fertility journey of a patient with McCune-Albright syndrome associated with bilateral ovarian involvement
Authors: Joanna J. Kim, Stephanie Dufour, Sara Awad, Bryden Magee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

Introduction

McCune-Albright syndrome (MAS) is a rare sporadic disorder, arising from somatic gain-of-function mutation in the GNAS1 gene which leads to mosaic, often unilateral distribution of affected cells with an overactivation of the adenyl cyclase pathway (1, 2). It is characterized by precocious puberty, polyostotic fibrous dysplasia, café au lait spots, and hyperfunctioning endocrinopathies such as hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, hypercortisolism and excess growth hormone (GH) and/or prolactin secretion (1, 2). McCune-Albright syndrome remains largely a clinical diagnosis and a biopsy to confirm the mutation is not necessary or required to make the diagnosis (3). In the ovaries, this syndrome can cause autonomous ovarian activation through unregulated peripheral estrogen production and subsequent gonadotropin suppression (4). In childhood, this can result in precocious puberty and therefore the management of MAS has largely focused on the pediatric population. However, MAS also affects adults as they can develop other gynecologic disorders including infertility, abnormal uterine bleeding, and recurrent ovarian cysts (5).

In the last two decades, there has been growing interest in the management of infertility in this population (5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11). Infertile patients with MAS often have anovulatory cycles from unregulated excessive estrogen production from functional ovarian cysts and therefore the goal of therapy is to restore ovulation through surgical management of the affected ovary (ovarian cystectomy or oophorectomy) or medical therapy. Other causes that could contribute to anovulatory cycles are coexistent hyperfunctioning endocrinopathies including GH excess, hyperprolactinemia, or hypercortisolism, all of which suppress pituitary gonadotrophins, which prevents predictable ovarian follicle development and ovulation. More recently, patients with MAS conceiving through in vitro fertilization (IVF) have been reported as well (5, 6).

We present a case of a 33-year-old woman, with known MAS and prior bilateral ovarian cysts. She also had MAS-associated endocrine hyperfunctioning, specifically GH excess, which led to irregular menstrual cycles. Through the use of lanreotide to reduce elevated insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) levels and restore the hypothalamic pituitary ovarian axis, she resumed ovulation and predictable monthly menstrual cycles. Therefore, this unique case adds to the current literature on MAS and infertility by describing a case of bilateral MAS ovarian involvement as well as MAS-associated excess GH. Excess GH can inhibit gonadotropin hormone-releasing hormone (GnRH), preventing endogenous gonadotropin release, which inhibits ovulation and causes infertility (12, 13, 14). Unfortunately, she did not conceive spontaneously and underwent one cycle of IVF and two subsequent frozen embryo transfers (FET). She achieved a pregnancy with her second FET but unfortunately ended in an incomplete miscarriage.

Case report

A 33-year-old cis-female was diagnosed with MAS at 6 months of age when she presented with café au lait spots, polyostotic fibrous dysplasia, precocious puberty, and recurrent functional ovarian cysts. In infancy, she underwent multiple laparoscopic bilateral ovarian cystectomies for persistent functional ovarian cysts despite GnRH agonist treatment for attempted suppression. Calvarial and skull base polyostotic fibrous dysplasia was demonstrated on initial imaging at the time of diagnosis. She was referred for 4 years of primary infertility and failed ovulation induction with oral agents.

On examination, she was of short stature, measuring 147cm (4’10”) tall, with a body mass index of 18kg/m2. Her pituitary panel to screen for hyperfunctioning endocrinopathies related to MAS revealed an elevated IGF-1 508 ng/mL (reference range [RR], 86–243 ng/mL), and a mildly elevated prolactin at 29.6 ng/mL (RR, <25 ng/mL). We performed a GH suppression test using 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, and her GH did not suppress <1ug/L following glucose load, confirming autonomous excessive GH secretion. Magnetic resonance imaging of the sella region with microadenoma protocol was performed to rule out a functional pituitary tumor. It showed persistent skull base and calvarium fibrous dysplasia as well as narrowing of the sella turcica and slight leftwards displacement of the pituitary stalk without pituitary lesion. Her thyroid and parathyroid biochemical tests were normal, ruling out hyperthyroidism and hyperparathyroidism, respectively. Lastly, hypercortisolism was ruled out with appropriate cortisol suppression <50 nmol/L following a 1-mg dexamethasone suppression test. As a result, the excess GH secretion was determined to be due to dysplastic bone caused by the MAS (15).

Initial infertility investigations demonstrated normal ovarian reserve (antral follicle count, 14; antimüllerian hormone, 16.5pmol/L or 2.31ng/mL) (RR, >15 pmol/L or >2.1 ng/mL), bilateral tubal patency, normal uterine cavity, and normal partner semen analysis. She had irregular menstrual cycle length ranging from 30 to 90 days.

A previous trial of ovulation induction using an aromatase inhibitor, letrozole (5 mg), per os daily from day 3 to day 7 of the menstrual cycle was unsuccessful; she developed functional cysts but did not ovulate and her progesterone (P4) remained low. In collaboration with her endocrinologist, a trial of lanreotide (Somatuline), which is a long-acting somatostatin analogue, was administered given her elevated IGF-1 in an attempt to normalize her IGF-1 to age and gender-matched control to improve fertility success through spontaneous ovulation, but also improve pregnancy outcomes. She was started on 60 mg intramuscular injection monthly and was titrated ≤90 mg monthly, which reduced her IGF-1 levels by 50% to nearly normalized level of 319 ng/mL (RR, 86–243 ng/mL), and her menses became regular indicating the efficacy of treatment. Unfortunately, she experienced abdominal cramping on the higher dose, as a result, we reduced her dose to 60 mg monthly which was better tolerated. We were unable to titrate to maximum dose of lanreotide to normalize the IGF-1 given medication intolerance. She unfortunately did not conceive and ultimately, decided to proceed with IVF. We continued her on lanreotide given evidence of safety and improved pregnancy success and outcomes (14).

Given her normalized cycles, a natural cycle start antagonist protocol was selected. A cycle day 2 ultrasound was done to rule out any ovarian cysts and blood work demonstrated low baseline estradiol (E2, 157.45 pmol/L) and P4 (1.1 nmol/L) levels. Stimulation with recombinant follicular stimulating hormone (rFSH) 250 IU and recombinant luteinizing hormone (rLH) 150 IU was initiated on day 3 of menses. After 10 days of stimulation, she had developed 10 follicles > 15 mm and ovulation was triggered with recombinant human chorionic gonadotropin (rhCG) and GnRH agonist (dual trigger) on that day. There were 13 oocytes retrieved, 10 oocytes matured, and 7 fertilized with conventional IVF. Given that her P4 level on the day of the trigger was elevated at 10 nmol/L, a nonexpanded blastocyst was transferred. Unfortunately, β-human chorionic gonadotropin was negative 12 days after transfer. There were three blastocysts that were cryopreserved (2 day 5 B5-AA blasts and 1 day 6 B5-BB blast).

She elected to proceed with a FET. However, her cycles had become longer since reducing her dose of lanreotide from 90mg to 60mg monthly. In the cycle after her egg retrieval, she did not ovulate and was found to have a persistently high E2 that resolved with an rhCG trigger.

With menses, she had a cycle day 2 ultrasound demonstrating no residual functional cyst and corresponding low E2 and low P4 levels. The first attempt at FET was performed using a mild ovarian stimulated cycle with rFSH, however, it was canceled due to a thin endometrial lining of 4.7mm. She underwent a second attempt at FET using both rFSH and rLH this time, after the confirmation of no functional cyst with low E2 and P4 serum levels. She was started on rFSH 87.5 IU and rLH 75 IU on day 3. Gonadotropin hormone-releasing hormone antagonist was added on day 8 onwards to prevent spontaneous ovulation. On cycle day 11, 5 follicles reached maturity and the endometrial lining was sufficient at 9.4mm, therefore ovulation was triggered with rhCG. The P4 on the day of trigger was 5.7 nmol/L. Vaginal P4 was given for luteal support. Frozen embryo transfer of a good quality day 5 embryo (B5-AA) was done 7 days after trigger. Unfortunately, she did not conceive with this FET. A third cycle of FET was done using a lower dose of rFSH and rLH on day 3, and the addition of GnRH antagonist on day 8. She was triggered with rhCG on cycle day 10, with three mature follicles and an adequate endometrial lining of 7.1mm. The P4 on the day of trigger was 4.3 nmol/L. Vaginal P4 and rhCG 2,500 units q3d x 4 doses were given for luteal phase support. Frozen embryo transfer of a good quality day 5 embryo (B5-AB) was done 7 days after trigger. She had a positive but low β-human chorionic gonadotropin which continued to rise. An early obstetric ultrasound was done at 6 weeks gestation which showed no intrauterine pregnancy, but a gestational sac and yolk sac low in the cervix, already starting to miscarry. She did not have ongoing bleeding or cramping and ultimately, underwent a suction Dilatation & Curettage that was uncomplicated. Informed consent: The patient provided consent before the submission of the case report.

Discussion

McCune-Albright syndrome is associated with infertility mainly through autonomous ovarian activation that leads to gonadotropin suppression and ovulatory dysfunction (3). However, the co-development of hyperfunctioning endocrinopathies also contributes to reduced fertility and anovulation. As such, it is paramount to screen biochemically as targeted therapies are available for specific syndromes. The development of ovarian cysts (often unilateral) and subsequent unregulated estrogen production is related to the unilateral expression of the GNAS1 mutation. The fertility management of MAS is largely focused on restoring regular menstrual cycles and ovulation induction by removing the dysregulation of the hypothalamic pituitary ovarian axis caused by ovarian autonomous activation. This has been achieved in five cases (6, 8, 9, 10, 11) in the literature by removing the affected ovary – through unilateral oophorectomy or cystectomy, and spontaneous pregnancy was reported in two patients (9, 11).

In our case, our patient with MAS had a history of bilateral ovarian involvement requiring multiple bilateral ovarian cystectomies as an infant. She presented to our fertility clinic with primary infertility and oligoanovulation. Her fertility workup revealed a normal ovarian reserve, bilateral patent tubes, and no functional ovarian cysts. Therefore, there was no perceived benefit of surgical management with an oophorectomy in her case where surgery innately comes with associated risks and would diminish her ovarian reserve. Letrozole was given for ovulation induction; however, she formed functional ovarian cysts. In our patient, her irregular menstrual cycles were likely due to the excess GH and mild hyperprolactinemia, both of which are known to cause central hypogonadism and anovulation (12, 13, 14). In fact, through the near-normalization of IGF-1 levels using lanreotide, she began to have normal menstrual cycles and ovulated monthly.

There is debate in the literature over the use of octreotide/lanreotide in pregnancy due to the possible risks related to fetal growth restriction and therefore in general are recommended to be avoided in pregnancy (16, 17). However, as per the Endocrine Society guidelines from 2014, the use of medical therapy may be considered when there is concern of compressive symptoms such as tumor growth and there has been no fetal malformation reported from mothers who continued to take somatostatin receptor ligand during pregnancy (16). In addition, elevated IGF-1 levels can also be associated with other metabolic consequences including impaired glucose metabolism and insulin insensitivity (17), which then in turn can cause gestational diabetes, thus negative obstetric implications such as macrosomia, preeclampsia, and preterm delivery. Therefore, after consultation with her endocrinologist, the plan was to continue lanreotide throughout pregnancy given benefits outweigh the risks.

In the literature, there are reported cases of patients with MAS undergoing IVF. There are three successful cases of patients with MAS who conceived through IVF (5, 6, 8). One published case report describes a successful IVF cycle in a patient with MAS without previous surgical management of a unilateral ovarian cyst (5). She also had bilateral tubal obstruction and therefore the resumption of menses with a unilateral oophorectomy in her case was thought to be futile (5). Two other case reports describe women who underwent IVF and had successful pregnancies after failing to conceive spontaneously despite the resumption of regular menstrual cycles after unilateral oophorectomy or cystectomy (6, 8). This is in contrast to another study which describes a 29-year-old patient with MAS who underwent two unsuccessful cycles of IVF. They report that all retrieved oocytes had poor morphology, including granularity of cytoplasm and abnormalities of zona pellucida, and this may have played a role in her ability to get pregnant (7).

Our patient and her IVF cycle are described in detail in the above case report. She had good stimulation with the IVF cycle and an above-average number of good quality blastocysts. She has now had one good quality and two best quality embryos transferred and has achieved one pregnancy resulting in early miscarriage. It is difficult to know if this relates to the underlying hormonal milieu of the endometrium or a factor of reduced egg/embryo quality due to MAS, as postulated by others (7).

Agopiantz et al. (7) have hypothesized different possible etiologies of infertility faced by patients with MAS. An interesting theory is the possibility that oocyte quality may be adversely affected by GNAS1 mutation in granulosa cells. The authors were the first to confirm a case of GNAS1 mutation present in corona radiata cells surrounding oocytes and found that those oocytes had poor morphology including granularity of the cytoplasm (7). The poor quality of the oocytes was also reflected in the low fertilization rate and low blastocyst development: one poor quality blastocyst that was frozen (Day 5 B4-BC) and two days two embryos, of which one was freshly transferred and one was frozen.

When we reflect on our current case, there were 10 mature oocytes (from a total of 13 retrieved oocytes) and the fertilization rate with conventional IVF was 70%, with a blastulation rate of 71%. These fertilization and blastulation rates are well within the expected range for IVF (18, 19). An early blastocyst was transferred fresh and two best quality day 5 embryos were frozen (B5-AA x 2), with an additional good quality embryo frozen on day 6 (B5-BB). The results suggest that the oocytes retrieved from our patients were better quality compared with the ones described by Agopiantz et al., (7) as they were able to complete meiosis II and had a relatively high rate of blastocyst formation. However, similar to the subjective morphology assessment of oocytes described in the previous study, our embryologists rated the oocytes retrieved from our patient as “fair” with respect to the oocyte morphology, including components such as pronucleus, cytoplasm, zona pellucida, perivitelline space, and polar body. Therefore, it seems plausible that the poorer morphology of the retrieved oocytes may be as a result of a GNAS1 mutation present in the granulosa cells and may negatively affect the quality of the blastocysts. This may explain why she has not been able to have a successful pregnancy despite one fresh and two FETs.

It is common for patients with MAS not to have genetic testing done as part of their diagnosis or management as it is a clinical diagnosis and the detection of the GNAS1 mutation does not change their management (3). The few fertility clinics that have treated patients with MAS with IVF do not routinely perform genetic testing of the granulosa cells for the GNAS1 mutation (5, 6), except for one case report which was done for academic purposes (7). If there is a true influence of GNAS1 mutation of granulosa cells on the oocyte quality and morphology with clinical significance in pregnancy outcomes of IVF cycles, this may be an area where genetic testing for the GNAS1 mutation in patients with MAS will be clinically meaningful.

In conclusion, this patient with MAS with bilateral ovarian involvement and GH excess was able to resume ovulatory cycles through medical management using lanreotide. Although she did not achieve pregnancy with ovulation induction, it did allow for a predictable response to controlled ovarian hyperstimulation for IVF. Before each cycle of stimulation (for IVF or FET), care was taken to ensure low estrogen and lack of ovarian cysts on ultrasound. It is interesting that one FET attempt was canceled for a thin lining when stimulation using rFSH alone.

The pregnancy achieved with her last FET attempt suggests that endometrial receptivity was achieved, but it is hard to determine the etiology of the early loss. Ongoing collaboration with endocrinology has been and continues to be an important component of this patient’s management, as patients with MAS are vulnerable to both autonomous ovarian cyst formation resulting in subsequent hypothalamic suppression, but also central endocrine hyperfunctioning (as evidenced in this case by her elevated IGF-1) that can also lead to ovulatory dysfunction. It is difficult to know if this patient’s lack of ongoing pregnancy remains related to her MAS or is independent of it. At this time, she has one more remaining embryo cryopreserved, however, the transfer of the final embryo has been delayed as she is undergoing medical treatment and it is unclear when she will be ready for the final embryo transfer. When the time comes, we plan to proceed with her final embryo transfer using a similar FET protocol after re-evaluating both hormonal and uterine cavity optimization.
