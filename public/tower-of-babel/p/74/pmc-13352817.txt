Evaluating the impacts of piperonyl butoxide-treated ceiling nets on malaria risk among high-risk populations with limited access to conventional LLINs in Homa Bay Country, Kenya: study protocol for a household randomized control trial
Authors: Yura K. Ko, Wataru Kagaya, Bernard N. Kanoi, James Kongere, Jesse Gitaka, Noboru Minakawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionProgress in reducing the global malaria burden has stalled since 2015, with increases in malaria incidence and mortality reported in many African countries after 2020. In 2021, the WHO issued a conditional recommendation for house screening, including windows, ceilings, doors, and eave spaces, as a complementary intervention. However, evidence gaps remain, particularly regarding its effectiveness in combination with or as an alternative to long-lasting insecticide-treated nets (LLINs). This trial aims to evaluate the piperonyl butoxide-pyrethroid (PBO)-ceiling nets, a novel house screening tool covering ceilings, among high-risk households in Kenya with poor use of LLINs.MethodsThis trial includes both superiority and non-inferiority comparisons. Superiority comparisons will evaluate whether the combination of PBO-ceiling nets and PBO-LLINs provides greater protection than either intervention alone. Non-inferiority comparisons will assess whether PBO-ceiling nets alone are not inferior to PBO-LLINs alone in reducing malaria infection prevalence and entomological indicators. In Homa Bay County, Kenya, 500 households will be randomized into three arms: (1) receiving PBO-ceiling nets, (2) receiving PBO-LLINs at a ratio of one net per two household members, and (3) receiving both PBO-ceiling nets and PBO-LLINs. The primary outcome is the prevalence of Plasmodium infections by PCR among all age groups at 12 months post-intervention. Secondary outcomes include entomological indicators, such as the density of primary malaria vectors sampled using CDC light traps.DiscussionThis trial addresses key evidence gaps related to housing modifications for malaria prevention by evaluating the effectiveness of PBO-ceiling nets among high-risk populations with limited access to conventional LLINs. Although household-level randomization may limit the ability to capture potential spillover effects and introduce challenges such as open-label and observer biases, several measures have been incorporated to mitigate these risks, including frequent adherence reminders and blinding of laboratory and data analysis staff. Despite these limitations, the study is expected to generate important epidemiological and entomological data that will contribute to the evidence base for novel vector control strategies in malaria-endemic regions.Trial registrationPACTR202506499755138. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

The progress in reducing the global malaria burden has been stalled since 2015, and increases in malaria incidence and mortality have been reported in many African countries after 2020 [1]. Current levels of global malaria incidence, mortality, and financial resources fall far behind the targets set by the World Health Organization’s (WHO) Global Technical Strategy for Malaria 2016–2030 [2]. A major obstacle to malaria control is the persistent transmission in sub-Saharan Africa, where 95% of global malaria mortality occurs. Without controlling malaria in this region, global malaria eradication cannot be achieved.

To effectively reduce transmission in this region, it is essential to recognize that the tools that enabled major gains in the early 2000 s, including rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), indoor residual spraying (IRS), and long-lasting insecticide-treated nets (LLINs), are increasingly constrained in their effectiveness, in part due to stagnating global funding for malaria control [3]. Although LLINs remain the most widely implemented vector control tool in sub-Saharan Africa, their effectiveness has been increasingly challenged in real-world settings. Several studies have reported inconsistent LLIN use and the repurposing of nets for activities such as fishing or protecting crops and poultry [4–6]. Despite multiple rounds of mass net distribution over more than a decade, malaria prevalence has remained persistently high in our study sites in Homa Bay County, Kenya, suggesting that LLIN-based approaches alone are insufficient to interrupt transmission. These limitations highlight the need for updated intervention tools and more adaptive delivery strategies that can extend protection to individuals who remain exposed outside the coverage of current approaches.

One potential approach is house screening. In 2021, the WHO issued a conditional recommendation [7] for screening of windows, ceilings, doors, and/or eave spaces, based on a systematic review that provided low- to moderate-certainty evidence of its protective effect against malaria [8]. The updated version of this review identified one randomized controlled trial and six cluster-randomized controlled trials (CRTs) assessing various house screening interventions in sub-Saharan Africa [9]. However, the results across these trials were mixed, highlighting that evidence remains limited and that additional high-quality studies are required to determine the effectiveness of specific screening approaches under routine programmatic conditions.

In Kenya, two recent CRTs have evaluated a novel form of house screening known as ceiling nets, which use a dual-active ingredient LLIN material containing pyrethroid and piperonyl butoxide (PBO) (PBO-ceiling net). One CRT was conducted on Mfangano Island in Lake Victoria [10], and another is ongoing in Ndhiwa Sub-County, Homa Bay County [11]. Preliminary analyses from these trials have shown reductions in indoor mosquito densities and malaria risk when PBO-ceiling nets were used alongside standard malaria control measures, including LLINs; however, these findings have not yet been published. Importantly, neither study assessed whether the protective effect of ceiling nets differs when used with versus without LLINs, despite evidence from other screening interventions suggesting that residents of screened houses may be less likely to sleep under bed nets [8]. Moreover, both CRTs were conducted in populations with access to LLINs and therefore did not evaluate the potential utility of ceiling nets among households with insufficient access to or inconsistent use of LLINs. These evidence gaps are particularly relevant in the Kenyan context. Although the most recent mass net distribution campaign in November 2023 aimed to achieve universal coverage with LLINs [12], some households continue to lack sufficient LLINs or do not use them even when available [13]. This underscores the need for additional approaches that can protect individuals who remain exposed outside the coverage of conventional interventions.

To address these two issues, this trial will recruit households that either do not own LLINs or own only one LLIN despite the recent mass net distribution. We aim to evaluate the impact of PBO-ceiling nets both with and without LLIN use, by comparing the following groups: (1) the combination use of PBO-ceiling nets and PBO-LLINs, (2) PBO-ceiling nets alone, and (3) PBO-LLINs alone. This will allow us to assess: (i) the additional benefit of combining PBO-ceiling nets with PBO-LLINs, and (ii) whether ceiling nets could serve as a viable alternative to PBO-LLINs in populations that do not regularly use conventional PBO-LLINs.

Objectives {7}

The primary objective is to assess the protective efficacy of the combined use of the PBO-ceiling nets and PBO-LLINs in reducing Plasmodium infection prevalence by PCR compared to PBO-LLINs alone across all age groups over a 12-month follow-up period, as a superiority comparison.

As a sub-analysis, infection prevalence and vector density in each treatment arm will also be compared by diagnostic methods (RDT and PCR) and across four time points (6, 12, 18 and 24 months post-intervention).

The 12-month timepoint was selected as the primary objective to ensure comparability with our previously conducted CRTs. The 18- and 24-month timepoints were included as sub-analyses to evaluate the long-term persistence of protective efficacy, which has not been sufficiently examined in prior studies.

Trial design {8}

This study is an open-label, household-randomized controlled trial. The randomization unit is the household. A total of 500 households will be assigned to one of three groups: (1) receiving PBO-ceiling nets (225 households), (2) receiving PBO-LLINs at a ratio of one per two household members (225 households), and (3) receiving both PBO-ceiling nets and PBO-LLINs (50 households). A baseline survey will be conducted to determine pre-intervention Plasmodium prevalence and Anopheles density, as well as to collect demographic and socioeconomic data for covariate-constrained randomization of households. The post-intervention follow-up period will last 24 months. Cross-sectional surveys will be conducted at 6, 12, 18 and 24 months post-intervention, targeting all age groups from each household to estimate overall Plasmodium prevalence. Additionally, indoor resting Anopheles mosquitoes will be collected from randomly selected one house structure from each household (a total of 500 house structures) at the same time points.

This trial includes both superiority and non-inferiority comparisons, as defined in the study objectives. Superiority comparisons will be made between the combination intervention and each single intervention arm, while non-inferiority comparisons will assess whether PBO-ceiling nets alone are not inferior to PBO-LLINs alone in reducing malaria infection prevalence and entomological indicators.

Methods: participants, interventions and outcomes

Study setting {9}

The study area includes Gambe East, Gambe North, Gambe Central, and Lambwe West Sub-locations in Homabay County in western Kenya (Fig. 1). According to the integrated community health system (ICHS), which is regularly updated by community health promoters (CHPs), the area holds 5398 households and 10,593 house structures, with a total population of 37,041 residents in 2024. The total land area is approximately 146 km2. Most houses are constructed with a stick framework plastered with a mixture of mud and cow dung, topped with a corrugated iron roof. The majority of residents belong to the Luo ethnic group. Dholuo is the main language spoken, although most residents are fluent in English and Kiswahili. The main income sources are fishing, traditional small-scale farming, and cattle breeding.Fig. 1Study area in Homa Bay County, western Kenya, located near Lake Victoria. The species of malaria vectors recorded from the areas adjacent to the study area were Anopheles arabiensis, Anopheles gambiae s.s., and Anopheles funestus s.s. When the former two species are grouped as A. gambiae s.l., a previous study in the adjacent area reported that nearly 90% of A. gambiae s.l. was A. arabiensis [7]. Indoor residual spray was not implemented in recent years. Bed nets had been distributed at health facilities, mainly to protect infants and pregnant women, but chemoprophylaxis was uncommon. The common treatment was artemisinin-based combination therapy provided at local health facilities. Rainfall in the study area follows a bimodal distribution. The long rainy season may start in early March and extend to early June, and the short rainy season may last from October to early December. However, the periods of the rainy seasons vary from year to year.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

PBO-LLINs will be distributed to the households in the PBO-LLINs alone arm, while PBO-ceiling nets and PBO-LLINs will be installed in all dwelling units in the combination arm. PBO-ceiling nets will be installed in all dwelling units in the PBO-ceiling nets alone arm. PBO-LLINs will be distributed at a ratio of one net per two people, and participants will be instructed to use them consistently throughout the study period. Any type of LLINs currently in use will be replaced with new PBO-LLINs with the consent of the residents. All PBO-LLINs and PBO-ceiling nets will be provided free of charge. The detailed comparison of PBO-ceiling nets and PBO-LLINs is shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Intervention description {11a}

The ceiling net targets the gap between the ceiling and walls of residential structures, thus providing broader coverage than screening limited to walls or eaves. The detailed installation procedure has been described in the previous study protocol [10]. In brief, the ceiling net consists of a rectangular sheet of Olyset®Plus net (Sumitomo Chemical Co., Tokyo, Japan) with loops sewn along its diagonal seams. These loops are tied to the roof beams, and the edges of the net are secured to the walls using staples (Fig. 2). Since ceiling nets can be installed without tailoring to fit specific windows or openings, the labor requirements are minimal. Installation can typically be completed within 30 min by a team of three to four people. The ceiling net installation team will receive training from experienced local research assistants who were involved in previous RCTs [10, 11]. All costs related to the installation will be covered by the research team. PBO-LLINs and PBO-ceiling nets will each be provided and installed once at baseline. Both types of nets will remain in use throughout the study period, and households will be encouraged to maintain and continue using them as installed.Fig. 2Example of an installed PBO-ceiling net inside a household

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

If participants request removal of the ceiling net, it will be taken down either by the study team or by household members. Participants who migrate between study arms or move out of the study area will be withdrawn from study follow-up.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

Ceiling nets are semi-permanently installed; thus the risk of non-adherence is minimal. To encourage adherence among households allocated to the PBO-LLIN only arm, they will be offered PBO-ceiling nets at the end of the trial.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

There are no specific restrictions on concomitant care during the trial. All participants, regardless of study arm, will continue to have access to standard medical care.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

At the conclusion of the trial, households allocated to the control arm will be provided with both PBO-ceiling nets and PBO-LLINs.

Based on historical data collected from the Lake Victoria region in Kenya [11], we assumed a parasite prevalence of 40% in the comparison arms (PBO-ceiling net alone or PBO-LLINs alone), and k of 0.545 (corresponding to an approximate intra-cluster correlation coefficient [ICC] of 0.20). This assumption is supported by unpublished data from two previous trials conducted in the same epidemiological setting [10, 11], where a similar reduction was observed within 12 months after intervention. Under these assumptions, a total of 38 households, with five individuals per household, are required to achieve 80% power to detect the expected reduction.

In field trials, values of k are often ≤0.25 and seldom exceed 0.5 for many health outcomes [15]. As the assumed value (k = 0.545) lies at the upper end of this range, sensitivity analyses were conducted using more typical values (k = 0.25–0.5) and more conservative effect sizes (30–50% relative reduction), examining the required number of households per arm (Supplementary Fig. 1). The figure shows that the sample size of 50 households per arm provides adequate power under a range of plausible scenarios. In particular, even under a more conservative assumption of a 40% relative reduction, 80% power can still be achieved if the between-household variation is moderate (approximately k ≤ 0.35). Similarly, scenarios with a 45% reduction are also compatible with the planned sample size under a broader range of k values, indicating that the design is reasonably robust to deviations from the primary assumption.

To assess whether the PBO-ceiling net alone is not inferior to PBO-LLINS alone in the parasite prevalence, the required sample size per arm is 201 households, with five individuals per household, assuming a non-inferiority margin of 10% defined on the absolute scale. This margin was selected to represent a difference that would be considered epidemiologically meaningful in terms of parasite prevalence, while remaining feasible for the current study design. Smaller margins would have required substantially larger sample sizes and were not feasible within the scope of the study. The selected margin therefore reflects a balance between epidemiological relevance and study feasibility.

Based on these calculations, the target number of households was set as follows, assuming around 10% loss to follow-up: (1) 50 households for the PBO-ceiling net + PBO-LLINs arm, (2) 225 households for the PBO-ceiling net alone arm, and (3) 225 households for the PBO-LLINs alone arm.

Recruitment {15}

To ensure that at least 500 households meeting the predefined inclusion criteria could be recruited, the study area was gradually expanded during the planning phase to include Gambe East, Gambe North, Gambe Central, and Lambwe West sub-locations in Homa Bay County. Based on the preliminary survey, a total of 501 households meeting the inclusion criteria were identified. These households will be randomly assigned to one of the three intervention arms as follows: 50 households will receive both PBO-ceiling nets and PBO-LLINs, 225 households will receive PBO-ceiling nets alone, and 225 households will receive PBO-LLINs alone.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

Household identification numbers will be provided to KKY, who will not be present in the field during community enrolment. Simple randomization will be conducted to allocate households across the three study arms. Randomization of all study households will be performed simultaneously.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

At the beginning of the baseline survey, all individuals and households will be assigned unique identification numbers. All subsequent study procedures will use only these anonymized IDs. This trial will not involve continuous recruitment; all households will be enrolled at study initiation and randomized simultaneously.

Implementation {16c}

After the baseline survey, covariate-constrained randomization will be used to allocate participating households into the three study arms. The following factors will be constrained: baseline malaria infection prevalence by RDT, socioeconomic status (SES), population size, and vector densities. Local study assistants will perform participant enrollment.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

This is an open-label trial. Due to the visible nature of the PBO-ceiling nets, neither trial participants nor study staff involved in field activities can be blinded to group allocation. However, laboratory and office-based personnel (e.g., laboratory technicians and data analysts) will remain blinded to both the identity and intervention assignment of participants. All biological specimens and analytical datasets will be labelled solely with unique numeric study identifiers, and personal information will be removed prior to laboratory and analyses. Statistical analyses will therefore be conducted in a blinded manner to minimize potential observer bias.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

There is no circumstance that they need to be unblinded.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

Baseline survey

A baseline survey in the study area will be conducted to obtain data to assure the balance of the household allocation and obtain basic demographic data. The baseline survey includes a questionnaire and malaria testing, and an entomological survey. In the questionnaire, information on LLIN ownership will be collected as part of the eligibility assessment. This will document the initial status of net ownership and usage before intervention distribution. Pre-intervention Plasmodium prevalence will be determined through RDT for all household members. Baseline Anopheles density will be assessed by indoor mosquito collections using CDC light traps placed in randomly selected house structures in all households.

Cross-sectional malariometric surveys

Malaria prevalence across all age groups will be assessed through cross-sectional surveys conducted at 6, 12, 18, and 24 months post-installation. Community surveys will be carried out through household visits. Plasmodium infection status will be determined using two diagnostic methods: RDT and PCR. For on-site diagnosis, a finger-prick blood sample will be collected and tested using the Bioline Malaria Ag P.f/Pan RDT (Abbott Diagnostics Korea Inc., Republic of Korea). Participants who test positive will receive artemether-lumefantrine treatment according to the dosing guidelines of the Kenyan Ministry of Health, after reviewing their recent treatment history. For molecular diagnosis, two blood samples (70 µL each) will be collected using 75-mm heparinized micro-hematocrit capillary tubes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and spotted onto Whatman ET31 Chr filter paper (Whatman International, Maidstone, UK). The dried blood spots will be air-dried at ambient temperature, stored individually in zip-sealed plastic bags at −20 °C, and subsequently analyzed for Plasmodium infection by PCR [16].

Entomological surveys

Indoor host-seeking mosquitoes will be collected from all household structures selected for the epidemiological survey in each study arm using CDC light traps at 6, 12, 18, and 24 months after installation. When a household includes multiple structures, one structure will be randomly selected to represent the household. At each time point, mosquito collection will be conducted for one night per household. Collected specimens will be preserved in 96% ethanol and transported in cool boxes with ice. Mosquitoes will be examined for sex by microscopy and identified to species using both microscopy and PCR. Sporozoite infection status will be determined by PCR, and the entomological inoculation rate (EIR) will be estimated accordingly.

Monitoring of intervention coverage, condition, and insecticidal retention

To monitor the condition of both PBO-LLINs and PBO-ceiling nets, as well as their coverage, damage, and continued use, household visits will be conducted during each cross-sectional survey. During these visits, field teams will visually inspect nets for physical integrity and record data on net presence, hanging status, damage classification, and reported usage through structured questionnaires. Any necessary repairs or re-installations identified during monitoring will be supported by the research team to ensure consistent intervention coverage over the 24-month follow-up period.

In addition, chemical analyses will be conducted to assess the residual insecticidal content of both permethrin and PBO on the netting materials. Samples of PBO-ceiling net fabric will be collected from a subset of households at 6, 12, and 24 months post-intervention for chemical analysis to estimate the remaining concentrations of active ingredients.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}

All surveys will be conducted through household visits. CHPs will schedule appointments with eligible participants prior to each visit to confirm their availability. CHPs will receive comprehensive training and detailed instructions on all field procedures and will be closely supervised by the research team throughout the study period. Regular feedback will be collected from CHPs to address any concerns raised by participants, and any field-related issues will be discussed and resolved through ongoing communication.

Data management {19}

All data from the baseline and cross-sectional surveys will be captured using Microsoft Excel on electronic tablets. The data will be transferred to a secure computer located in the Nagasaki University office in Home Bay County, which is housed in a locked room. Access to the data and database management will be restricted to authorized study investigators and collaborators. Statistical analyses will be conducted using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; version 4.5.2) with analysis scripts archived to ensure reproducibility. Following validation of the uploaded data, all locally stored data on the tablets will be permanently deleted to minimize the risk of unauthorized access.

Confidentiality {27}

To ensure confidentiality, each participant will be assigned a unique study identifier. All collected data will be labelled with this identifier and stored separately from the key containing personal information (such as name, date of birth, household GPS location, and phone number). All publications will present aggregated data only, and no personally identifiable information will be disclosed.

Plans for collection, laboratory evaluation and storage of biological specimens for genetic or molecular analysis in this trial/future use {33}

Anonymized blood samples from study participants will be stored and analysed for Plasmodium infections by PCR at our laboratory in Homa Bay. Microscopic examination of adult mosquito specimens will be performed at the field laboratory in Mbita, while PCR analyses will be conducted at the laboratories in Homa Bay and MKU. Laboratory data will be initially recorded in Microsoft Excel and subsequently imported into the study database. No human genetic analyses are planned for this study. However, any remaining biological samples will be stored indefinitely for potential future studies, unless participants decline this option during the informed consent process. Participants will be provided with the research team’s contact information and may withdraw from the current or any future studies at any time without penalty or prejudice.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

The intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis is the primary analysis approach for both the primary and secondary objectives. The per-protocol (PP) analysis is included as a supplementary analysis for the primary and secondary objectives.

The primary analysis assumes independence between households conditional on the specified model structure and included covariates. The prevalence of malaria infection will be analyzed using a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a logit link, including a random intercept for household to account for clustering. Individual-level random effects will additionally be included in the model to account for repeated sampling of the same individuals across survey rounds. Covariates including age, sex, housing structure, malaria vaccination history, SES will be included as fixed effects. To account for multiple pairwise superiority comparisons, a Bonferroni correction will be applied to control the family-wise error rate. Differences in vector density between arms will be evaluated using GLMM with negative binomial distribution, including a random intercept for household to account for clustering and adjusting for the above-mentioned covariates. EIR will be evaluated using Gaussian regression.

Background characteristics collected during the baseline survey — including age, sex, number of participants, household size, number of LLINs per household, housing structure (walls, roof, and floor), malaria vaccination history, and socioeconomic status — will be summarized by study arm. A composite socioeconomic status index will be generated using principal component analysis (PCA) based on household-level variables adopted from the 2020 Kenya Malaria Indicator Survey (KMIS), following the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) Program’s standard procedure [17] (Supplementary list 1). The first principal component will be used as the SES index, with weights derived directly from the PCA results. A weighted score will be calculated for each household and used to categorize households into five quintiles.

Interim analyses {21b}

An interim analysis will be conducted using data from the 12-month cross-sectional survey. A significant disadvantage will be defined as a statistically significant adverse effect (p < 0.05) in Plasmodium infection prevalence in any of the ceiling-net intervention arms compared with the PBO-LLIN arm. If such a disadvantage is observed, the study steering committee will consider early termination of the affected arm for ethical reasons. However, if a significant benefit is observed in the ceiling net intervention arms, the study will not be stopped, as continued follow-up is necessary to assess the long-term effects beyond 1 year after the intervention. As this interim analysis is intended to detect potential disadvantage of the intervention arms, and does not involve formal efficacy testing, no adjustment to the overall type I error rate or sample size was made.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g. subgroup analyses) {20b}

Subgroup analyses will be conducted to evaluate potential effect heterogeneity across key demographic and contextual factors, including age group (children under 5 years, 5–15 years, and 16 years or older), sex, socioeconomic status, and other relevant household or environmental characteristics such as housing materials. Effect modification will be assessed by including interaction terms between the intervention and subgroup variables in the primary regression models. Results will be presented as subgroup-specific effect estimates with corresponding confidence intervals. These analyses are pre-specified and will be considered exploratory. No formal adjustment for multiple testing will be applied, and findings will be interpreted with caution.

In addition, exploratory spatial analyses will be conducted to minimize or account for the potential influence of spillover effects on the estimated intervention effects. Spillover refers to indirect effects of an intervention on nearby households that did not receive the same intervention, for example through local changes in vector density or malaria transmission [18]. To adjust for this possibility, measures of household proximity to households assigned to other study arms will be derived and included as covariates in the regression models. Where appropriate, distance-based variables, such as the distance to the nearest household in a different study arm or the local density of households assigned to each intervention arm within a defined radius, will be examined [19]. Sensitivity analyses excluding households located in close proximity to households assigned to other intervention arms will also be conducted to evaluate the robustness of the primary findings.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

The extent and patterns of missing data will be assessed after completion of data collection. If necessary, simple hot-deck imputation methods will be applied when the proportion of missing data for a covariate is less than 5%, and appropriate multiple imputation methods will be used when the missing proportion is 5% or greater. If a substantial proportion of subjects have missing values for a given covariate (whether missing at random or missing completely at random), that covariate may be excluded from the analysis model.

Plans to give access to the full protocol, participant level-data and statistical code {31c}

The full protocol, statistical analysis plan, anonymized individual-level data, and statistical code will be made available upon reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Oversight and monitoring

Composition of the coordinating centre and trial steering committee {5d}

The research team, comprising investigators from Kenya and Japan, as well as CHPs and laboratory technicians, established a communication group using mobile applications such as WhatsApp to facilitate experience sharing and coordinate field activities. A trial steering committee, consisting of study investigators and independent experts, will convene after each study survey to review progress and provide oversight.

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role and reporting structure {21a}

We will establish a Data and Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) for this trial. The DSMB will consist of one independent statistician and one local clinician who is familiar with malaria case management in Homa Bay County. The DSMB will review accumulating safety data, monitor any potential risks associated with the intervention, and provide independent recommendations to the study investigators regarding continuation, modification, or termination of the trial. The DSMB will meet on an ad hoc basis if serious adverse events are reported. The committee will report its recommendations to the principal investigators, who will in turn communicate with the local health authorities and ethical review committees as required.

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

Potential adverse events such as skin irritation, allergic reactions, or other minor symptoms possibly associated with PBO-treated nets will be systematically monitored during each cross-sectional household survey using a standardized questionnaire. All unanticipated problems will be reported to the research team and the Homa Bay County Ministry of Health (MOH) through CHPs. In the event of a study-related serious adverse event, the study team will immediately notify the DSMB and convene a joint review with the MOH and Homa Bay County Teaching and Referral Hospital representatives to assess the case and determine appropriate actions, including reporting to the Kenya and Japan regulatory authorities as required.

Although the risks associated with the ceiling nets are expected to be minimal and comparable to standard LLINs, we will take precautionary measures to monitor possible environmental or ecological concerns. During household surveys, we will collect information on net washing and disposal practices, including whether nets are discarded in open environments or water sources. This will allow the study team to identify any potentially unsafe practices, and appropriate guidance will be provided to households if needed. Any unexpected concerns raised by CHPs or community members will be documented and reviewed by the DSMB.

To monitor potential downstream effects, including the emergence of insecticide resistance, mosquitoes collected during the entomological surveys will be used for susceptibility testing and/or molecular assays, where feasible, to detect markers of pyrethroid and PBO resistance. The entomological survey will therefore serve not only to assess mosquito density and species composition but also to evaluate whether prolonged exposure to PBO-ceiling nets influences local resistance patterns.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {23}

No formal trial audit is anticipated. Study investigators meet monthly to review trial conduct to ensure that all surveys and investigations are conducted according to the study protocol.

Plans for communicating important protocol amendments to relevant parties (e.g., trial participants, ethical committees) {25}

Decisions on important trial amendments must be made through a formal procedure and will be approved by institutional review boards (IRB) at MKU and Nagasaki University. The protocol in the clinical trials registry will also be updated accordingly.

Dissemination plans {31a}

Study results will be shared with study participants, local communities, the Homa Bay County government, and the Kenya National Malaria Control Program (NMCP). In addition, the findings will be presented at international conferences, stakeholder meetings, and workshops to inform the development of malaria control strategies in other malaria-endemic countries. Suggestions and feedback from participants will also be considered to guide future improvements of the intervention.

The study results and anonymized data will be disseminated through reports to the sponsor, followed by peer-reviewed, open-access publications targeted at both scientific and policy-making audiences. All anonymized datasets will be made available to stakeholders upon reasonable request. At the end of the project, a dissemination meeting will be organized with the local community to present the findings and address any questions raised.

Discussion

The WHO has emphasized the need for high-quality research on the impact of interventions broadly categorized as housing modifications, including house screening, to inform future recommendations for malaria prevention and control [7]. While several ongoing trials are expected to contribute valuable evidence to this area [10, 11], important knowledge gaps remain. A recent trial reported that school-aged children living in houses with insecticide-treated screening of eaves and windows had lower malaria prevalence following the long rains. However, the trial was inconclusive due to low statistical power resulting from household refusals to participate [20]. Further research is needed to evaluate the effectiveness of housing modification interventions across diverse epidemiological settings where vector species may differ, and among different populations at risk. Additional evidence is also required to better understand contextual factors such as acceptability, feasibility, resource requirements, equity, and community preferences. This study aims to contribute to addressing these gaps by evaluating the impact of PBO-ceiling nets as a novel intervention among high-risk populations with limited access to conventional LLINs.

This trial has some limitations. First, randomization will be conducted at the household level rather than at the village or community level. This makes it harder to capture spillover effects, which refer to the indirect benefits of an intervention that influence disease transmission within a population [18]. Household-level randomization also limits the ability to establish buffer zones between intervention arms, further complicating the elimination of spillover effects. However, since the target population—households lacking access to LLINs—is scattered throughout the community, the likelihood of substantial spillover effects is minimized, as a certain threshold of intervention coverage is typically required to generate such effects on malaria transmission [18]. In addition, exploratory spatial analyses will be conducted by incorporating proximity-based measures as covariates in the regression models, and sensitivity analyses excluding households located close to households assigned to other intervention arms will be considered to assess the robustness of the main findings. Second, due to the visible nature of the ceiling nets, the possibility of open-label and observer biases cannot be excluded. Furthermore, participants assigned to the PBO-ceiling net + PBO-LLIN arm may potentially reduce their use of LLINs, perceiving the ceiling nets as sufficient protection. To minimize this risk, CHPs will regularly remind participants of the importance of consistently using PBO-LLINs throughout the study period. To further reduce observer bias, laboratory personnel and data analysts will remain blinded to group allocation. Third, if the true intervention effect is smaller than assumed 50% relative reduction, or if between-household variability exceeds the assumed level (k = 0.545), the study can be underpowered to detect a statistically significant difference. Although sensitivity analyses suggest that the planned sample size is robust across a range of plausible scenarios, this potential limitation should be considered when interpreting the findings. Fourth, the external validity of the findings should be considered. This trial will be conducted in a high-transmission setting with documented pyrethroid resistance, and the findings may therefore be most generalizable to similar settings. The results may not apply to areas with high LLIN adherence, where the incremental benefit of the intervention could differ. In addition, while the 12-month endpoint is appropriate for assessing the expected peak effect, the sustainability of the intervention effect beyond 12 months should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, because randomization is performed at the household level, the findings mainly reflect household-level effectiveness and may not directly inform community-wide deployment strategies. Despite these limitations, this trial will provide important data on the effectiveness of this intervention across both epidemiological and entomological outcomes in settings where conventional vector control tools have limited reach.

Trial status

The baseline survey began to list households without possessing LLINs or with only one LLIN on May 1, 2025 and ended on August 25, 2025. Based on the list, we will recruit 500 households, and the interventions are scheduled to start in September 2025. The current protocol is version 6.0 as of April 29, 2026.
