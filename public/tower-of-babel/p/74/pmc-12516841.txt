The relationship between perceived quality physical education and 7-day physical activity among secondary school students in China: the mediating role of exercise self-efficacy
Authors: Ling Qin, Walter King Yan Ho, Selina Khoo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization has highlighted that quality physical education (QPE) can lead to a more active lifestyle for adolescents. However, previous studies have not explored the relationship between students’ perceptions of QPE and physical activity (PA). According to Bandura’s social cognitive theory, self-efficacy plays a crucial role in influencing behaviour, including PA engagement. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the relationship between students’ QPE perception and 7-day PA among Chinese secondary school students, with a focus on analysing the potential mediating role of exercise self-efficacy (ESE) between these two factors.MethodsThis study employed a cross-sectional design. Adolescents (N = 2,349) from 10 secondary schools in China participated in this study through providing self-reports. The dependent variables included students’ perceptions of QPE measured across 10 dimensions. ESE was considered as the mediator, reflecting psychological functioning. 7-day PA served as the outcome variable. All multiple regression models were adjusted for covariates including sex, age, ethnic background, and school location.ResultsSignificant differences were observed across sex, ethnicity, and school location in the ten dimensions of QPE perception, ESE, and 7-day PA. All 10 QPE dimensions were positively associated with PA (r = 0.285 to 0.476, p < 0.001), with the strongest associations observed for family and community sports resources. Mediation analyses showed that ESE mediated the association between each QPE dimension and PA: the standardized indirect effects ranged from β = 0.095 to 0.158 and exceeded the corresponding direct effects in every model; the proportion mediated ranged from 52.20% to 80.12%.ConclusionsTeachers and policymakers should design activities that cater to female students’ interests and needs, enhance their self-efficacy, and encourage their active participation in PA. Ensuring equitable access to resources and opportunities for physical education and PA is also crucial. Interventions promoting PA should adopt a collaborative approach that integrates the efforts of families, schools, and communities. Moreover, rather than focusing solely on increasing PA, interventions should prioritise strengthening ESE for long-term success. Future studies should focus on longitudinal designs and expand the sample size to enhance the generalisability of the findings.Keywords: Quality physical education, Physical activity, Exercise self-efficacy, Middle school, Students, China

Introduction

Physical activity (PA) plays a critical role in fostering the physical, psychological, emotional, and cognitive development of children and adolescents, contributing to their overall health and well-being [1–3]. Despite these well-documented benefits, global data indicate that only 27–33% of children and adolescents meet the recommended 60 min of moderate to vigorous daily PA [4]. A 2022 report from China indicated that PA levels among adolescents remain low [5]. Insufficient PA among children and adolescents is also a leading cause of overweight and obesity in China [6]. Additionally, insufficient PA in children and adolescents increases the risk of cardiovascular diseases and chronic conditions such as diabetes [7].

In response, quality physical education (QPE) programmes have been proposed to improve students’ PA and foster healthier lifestyles [8, 9]. Studies have highlighted the benefits of QPE in enhancing PA levels. Masurier and Corbin identified 10 reasons for implementing QPE in schools, underscoring its potential to increase participation in PA [10]. Similarly, Ball et al. contended that a well-implemented QPE programme could significantly benefit students by increasing their moderate to vigorous physical activity (MVPA) levels [11]. Moreover, QPE programmes are designed to cultivate positive attitudes toward PA by promoting enjoyment of movement and development of transferable physical skills [12]. However. literature leaves some clear gaps. First, few studies have examined how students’ perceptions of QPE influence their PA levels, or the dimensions of QPE most relevant to PA outcomes. Second, students’ perceptions of QPE are rarely measured systematically. Students’ perceptions of their learning environment, teaching methods, and curriculum significantly affect their academic performance, engagement, and overall satisfaction with their educational journey [13–15]. Evaluating students’ perceptions of QPE allows for a reflective analysis of the strengths and weaknesses of previous PE programmes. Third, existing research on school-based interventions, including PE and extracurricular PA programmes, presents mixed findings regarding their effectiveness in increasing students’ PA levels. For example, Roclife et al. found that school provision of PE and PA had a positive impact on adolescent PA behaviours [16]. Conversely, Reis et al. found that after PE intervention implementation, no significant increases were observed in PA intensities and sedentary behaviours [17]. These conflicting results highlight the importance of examining the mechanisms through which QPE dimensions enhance students’ PA. Furthermore, although studies emphasise the effectiveness of school-based interventions [18, 19], they often overlook changes in overall PA levels maintained over longer durations. To address this gap, the present study investigates students’ PA over a 7-day period.

China offers a distinct context in which to examine these relationships. The country’s centralised policy framework shapes a relatively uniform educational system [20] in which curricular guidelines, teaching methodologies, and resource allocations are closely regulated at the national level. Such uniformity may reduce regional disparities and ensures that PE programmes and instructional practices are consistent across provinces and schools. Simultaneously, the increasing emphasis on academic success within Chinese culture often leads schools, teachers, and parents to prioritise academic achievement over PA [20], potentially influencing students’ experiences in PE and their subsequent participation in PA. Furthermore, evidence suggests that Chinese adolescents, especially those in secondary schools, have low PA levels [5]. These conditions make China an especially compelling setting for examining the ways in which dimensions of QPE perceptions shape the 7-day PA and underscore the need to understand how standardised policies translate into real-world student experiences in PE and PA behaviours.

To understand how QPE perception influences PA, it is important to examine psychological mechanisms. Bandura’s social cognitive theory provides a useful framework for behavioural change, including PA [21]. According to this theory, human behaviour results from the dynamic interaction of personal factors, environmental influences, and behaviour, which is known as triadic reciprocal determinism [22]. Exercise self-efficacy (ESE), a key concept within this framework, refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to succeed in challenging PA situations [23], drawing from the broader concept of self-efficacy in social cognitive theory [24]. ESE is domain and task specific, with confidence varying by activity type, intensity, setting and by the salience of particular barriers [25]. It is gradational and probabilistic, expressed on a continuum of certainty in which higher levels predict a greater likelihood of initiation, stronger effort investment and greater persistence [26]. It is contextual and dynamic, fluctuating with recent experiences, social cues and available opportunities, and it is sensitive to features of the instructional climate [27]. In addition, ESE has a self‑regulatory function by supporting goal setting, action and coping planning, attentional control and recovery after lapses [28]. Adolescents with higher ESE are more likely to engage in PA and maintain regular exercise habits [29–31]. In the context of PE, supportive learning environments can enhance students’ self-efficacy, promoting PA [9]. However, the role of ESE in linking QPE perception and PA levels remains unclear. Moreover, previous studies indicate that demographic factors, such as age, sex, and ethnic background, can influence students’ PA levels [32–34]. For instance, senior secondary school students, female students, and minors often report lower MVPA within and outside school settings [35, 36]. This pattern may reflect lower ESE and more negative perceptions of PE among these groups. However, gaps remain in the literature regarding how demographic differences shape students’ perceptions of QPE and ESE.

This study aimed to clarify how students’ perceptions of QPE bolster their 7-day PA, and whether ESE serves as a critical mechanism in this process. This study proposes three hypotheses.

Methods

Participants

This study employed a cross-sectional design. A total of 2,349 secondary school students from China participated in this study. All participants met the following inclusion criteria: (a) they were enrolled in a junior or senior secondary school, (b) they were given parental or guardian consent, (c) they had no diagnosed physical or mental health conditions, and (d) they possessed an adequate ability to read and understand Chinese.

The sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1.9.2. For correlation analysis, a t-test was conducted to determine the necessary sample size. Considering a two-tailed test with a medium sample size of 0.3, a significance level of 0.05, and a power (1 − β) of 0.95, the required sample size was calculated to be 134. An F-test was employed for the multiple regression analysis. Given a medium effect size of 0.15, a significance level of 0.05, a power (1 − β) of 0.95, and 6 independent variables, the required sample size was calculated to be 146.

A total of 53 school principals were invited to participate, and 10 mixed-sex school principals (five junior secondary and five senior secondary schools) agreed. Data were then collected. In total, 3,000 students received written informed consent forms in sealed envelopes to share with their parents or guardians. Of these, 2,349 students (78.3%) returned signed consent forms and agreed to participate. The final sample size exceeded the minimum required for power analyses. Measures

Perception of quality physical education

To assess the perception of QPE, the students completed the 48-item QPE Perception Instrument for Secondary School Students [37]. This instrument comprises four subscales measuring QPE perceptions at the student, family, school, and community levels, spanning 10 dimensions. The items were scored on a 7-point Likert scale (from 1 = completely disagree to 7 = completely agree).

The subscale for the student level comprises two dimensions: student development (StuD) and engagement and experiences in PE (SEEPE). The factor structure of the instrument demonstrated strong model fit, with χ2 = 174.18, df = 60, CFI = 0.942, TLI = 0.925, SRMR = 0.049, and RMSEA = 0.077 (90% CI: 0.06 to 0.09). The family subscale consists of two dimensions: parents’ involvement in PA and home-based sports resources (PIPAHBSR) and parents’ attitude toward PE (PAPE). The factor structure of the instrument demonstrated good model fit, with χ2 = 174.18, df = 60, CFI = 0.942, TLI = 0.925, SRMR = 0.049, and RMSEA = 0.077 (90% CI: 0.06 to 0.09). The school subscale comprised five dimensions: sports facilities and equipment in the school (SFES), PE curriculum (PEC), PE teacher (PET), family-school-community cooperation in PE (COFSCPE), and school-based extracurricular PA programmes (SBEPAP). The factor structure of the instrument demonstrated acceptable model fit, with χ2 = 377.95, df = 160, CFI = 0.928, TLI = 0.914, SRMR = 0.050, and RMSEA = 0.065 (90% CI: 0.05 to 0.07). The community subscale comprises one dimension: community-based sports resources (CBSR). The factor structure of the instrument demonstrated good model fit, with χ2 = 6.76, df = 2, CFI = 0.997, TLI = 0.993, SRMR = 0.013, and RMSEA = 0.047 (90% CI: 0.000 to 0.10). Moreover, the test-retest analysis indicated that all 10 dimensions exceeded 0.70, demonstrating statistically significant reliability.

Exercise self-efficacy

Bandura developed the original Exercise Self-Efficacy Scale (ESES) [38]. Modified versions of the ESES, including a 5-point Likert scale (from 1 = not at all confident to 5 = extremely confident), were used by Kim [39], Sabo et al. [40], and Yao et al. [41], with the revised scale showing strong reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.910, test-retest reliability = 0.86) [39]. Yao et al. [41] validated the Chinese version of the ESES with a sample of 1,573 primary and secondary school students, and reported excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.964) and a strong model fit (CFI = 0.918, TLI = 0.905, SRMR = 0.043, RMSEA = 0.066). These findings confirmed the construct validity of the ESES for Chinese children and adolescents. For our study, we used the Chinese version of the ESES to examine students’ ESE.

7-day physical activity

The Physical Activity Questionnaire for Adolescents (PAQ-A) was developed to assess general PA levels among adolescents, typically in junior and senior secondary schools, over a 7-day period [42]. The PAQ-A has been translated and validated in various languages including Amharic [43], Dutch [44], Malay [45], Thai [46], Indonesian [47], and Polish [48]. The Chinese version, translated by Li et al. [49], demonstrated good reliability and validity, with internal consistency coefficients of 0.82 and 0.85 and a test-retest reliability of 0.81. This version has been widely used in studies on PA and physical education in China, particularly among secondary school students [50–55]. In this study, we used the Chinese version of the PAQ-A to assess PA levels among secondary students.

Individual demographic

Evidence indicates that older students, female students, students from ethnic minority backgrounds, and students attending rural schools frequently demonstrate often exhibit lower engagement in PA [32–34]. Therefore, in our multivariate models, we accounted for potential confounding variables. Specifically, we included the following individual demographic factors: age (ordinal variable), sex (male or female), ethnic background (Han Chinese or Minority), and school location (urban or rural). By selecting these covariates, we aimed to control for factors that could independently affect the independent variable (perception of QPE), dependent variable (PA level), or mediator (ESE).

Procedure

Ethical approval was obtained from the first author’s institution. Secondary school principals were approached through face-to-face meetings and informed of the study. Permission to access students was secured during such discussions. Written informed consent was obtained from parents, and assent was obtained from students who agreed to participate. Students were informed that their participation was entirely voluntary and that they could withdraw without penalty. The students were informed of the study’s objective to assess their PE experiences, self-efficacy, and PA levels, emphasising that the survey did not seek ‘right’ or ‘wrong’ answers. The survey was completed in a quiet classroom setting without teachers and was supervised by the first author to encourage honest responses. Anonymity was emphasised, and students were assured that their responses would remain confidential and inaccessible to their teachers, ensuring privacy and confidentiality. Most students took 25–35 min to complete the questionnaire.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27) and R, version 4.0.4 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical analysis of mediation was conducted using SPSS PROCESS version 3.4 with model 4 [56].

Descriptive analysis was conducted to identify the participants’ characteristic distribution. Frequencies and percentages were used to describe categorical variables, and statistical means and standard deviations were used to measure continuous variables.

The association between correlation analyses was calculated for the 10 dimensions of QPE perceptions, ESE, and 7-day PA. The internal consistency reliability of the QPE sub-dimensions, ESES, and 7-day PA was estimated with McDonald’s Omega (cutoff value > 0.70) [57]. The significance of differences across the 10 dimensions of QPE perceptions, ESE, and PA among various demographic groups was assessed using a t-test. To account for multiplicity across the group comparisons, we controlled the false discovery rate (FDR) using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (q = 0.05) [58]. We report FDR-adjusted p-values (p-FDR). In addition, we quantified the magnitude of between-group differences using Cohen’s d with 95% confidence intervals, computed from the pooled standard deviation for unequal group sizes [59].

Hayes [56] describe a regression-based procedure for testing mediation using three equations. The hypothesised mediation model is shown in Fig. 2. First, a linear logistic regression model was used to test the total effect of the independent variable perception of QPE on the dependent variable 7-day PA (Path c). Second, a linear regression model was fitted to test the association between the independent variable perception of QPE and the mediator variable ESE (Path a). Third, a linear regression model with 7-day PA as the dependent variable and perception of QPE and ESE as covariates was fitted to test the independent effects of the mediator (Path b) and the independent variable (Path c’, direct effect) on the dependent variable. An attenuation of the direct effect compared to the total effect indicates evidence of mediation. The indirect, or mediated, effect of the exposure on the outcome was calculated as the difference between the total and direct effects (c-c’). To verify the reliability of the mediating effect, a bootstrapping method was employed to assess the significance of both the direct and indirect effects within the mediation model. This method involved 5,000 random samples to compute the estimated 95% confidence interval (CI) values. A mediating effect was confirmed if the 95% CI for the indirect effect did not include a zero.

Results

A total of 2,349 secondary school students aged 12–17 years participated in the study. The mean age was 15.18 years (SD = 1.58). A slightly higher proportion of the patients were female. Most participants were Han Chinese, reflecting the national demographic profile [60]. Over half of the schools were located in rural areas. Prior to the substantive analyses, we assessed common method bias using a single-factor confirmatory factor analysis, constraining all items to load on one latent factor [61]. The single-factor model fit was poor (χ2/df = 9.442, RMSEA = 0.064, NFI = 0.839, AGFI = 0.684, CFI = 0.853), indicating that a single common factor could not account for the data and that common method bias was unlikely to be a serious concern in this study. For the Durbin-Watson statistic, values near 2 indicate no first-order autocorrelation [62]. In our analysis, the Durbin-Watson statistic was from 1.496 to 1.727 relative to this near-2 benchmark, there was no evidence of first-order autocorrelation. These diagnostics suggest that residual normality and independence were reasonably satisfied. We screened for potentially influential observations using studentized residuals, flagging cases with ∣t∣ >3 [63]. In our data, the maximum absolute studentized residual was 1, well below this threshold, indicating no residual outliers.

Discussion

We investigated the associations between each dimension of the perception of QPE and PA levels, with ESE included as a mediating variable while controlling for demographic factors as covariates. Results showed significant differences across most QPE perception dimensions and in ESE and 7-day PA. These differences were particularly pronounced across sex, ethnicity, and school location, providing partial support for Hypothesis (1). Our findings showed that the 10 dimensions of QPE perception had a positive impact on PA level, supporting Hypothesis (2). Furthermore, there was a statistically significant indirect association between the 10 QPE perception dimensions and 7-day PA via ESE, which accounted for a substantial proportion of the total association, thereby supporting Hypothesis (3).

Sex differences were clear in our study. Except for the SFES and SBEPAP dimensions, female students scored lower than male students on most QPE dimensions, ESE, and 7-day PA. The direction of these differences is consistent with evidence from other countries. Araujo et al. [64] used data from 146 countries and territories and found that adolescent male students had a higher prevalence of meeting the recommended 60 min of MVPA per day for at least one day per week, with the largest differences being observed among the oldest adolescents. Rocliffe et al. [16] found that the overall typical school provisions for PE, PA, and sports had a significantly greater impact on males in comparison to their female counterparts. These converging findings indicate broad cross-national consistency in the direction of sex differences. These results indicate that sexual differences may arise due to cultural sexual norms, which increase the pressure to reduce societal sex inequality [65]. This evolving social context could lead to lower PA participation among female students, reduced ESE, and poorer PE experiences [35, 66]. Therefore, it is crucial to develop and implement PE strategies that promote PA among girls in schools.

Regarding age, most dimensions of QPE perception notably showed no significant differences between age groups, suggesting that China’s PE programme is highly standardised and adheres to the national educational framework. The Ministry of Education provides clear guidelines for PE at all school levels [67, 68], ensuring consistency in teaching methods and content across junior and senior schools. Students from different age groups were likely to share similar perceptions of PE, particularly regarding opportunities for participation.

Significant differences were observed between the age groups across SEEPE and 7-day PA. Older students scored higher than younger students. Conversely, older students reported significantly lower 7-day PA, which could be explained by the PE structure of Chinese senior secondary schools [67]. In these schools, PE courses are organised around distinct sports disciplines, allowing students to select specialised options that align with their individual interests and skill levels. This autonomy promotes greater participation and fosters more positive PE experiences. Moreover, engagement in activities is driven by the need for autonomy, competence, and relatedness [69]. Older students typically have more developed sports skills, which foster greater autonomy and competence in their learning, enabling them to independently access and participate in extracurricular sports programmes. These factors likely contributed to the higher SEEPE scores. Conversely, as academic workload increases, time becomes scarce. In examination-centred contexts, senior secondary students and their families prioritise study. These priorities reinforce a shift away from weekly PA, which typically declines with age. These mechanisms are not unique to China. Similar patterns are evident in the United States and Australia [70, 71]. PE encourages participation aligned with students’ own interests and self-selected activities, signalling greater autonomy at older grades. Population surveillance shows an age-related decline in PA in counties [72].

Students from minority groups and those attending rural schools reported lower ESE and PA levels. This pattern was also evident in the dimensions of QPE perception. Students in rural areas face resource-related challenges, including inadequate sports facilities [73], a lack of qualified PE teachers [74], and limited access to extracurricular programmes [73]. Similarly, minority students, most of whom come from rural areas [60], also encounter these challenges. By contrast, urban students benefit from greater opportunities to engage in high-quality PE and PA, often through specialised sports programmes [74, 75]. The lack of resources in rural areas negatively impacts the QPE experience of these students, hindering their ESE and contributing to lower PA levels compared with their urban counterparts. These findings highlight the need for further research to understand how geographic factors influence QPE, ESE, and PA levels.

In this study, we found positive correlations in all 10 dimensions of QPE perception and 7-day PA. In the subsequent multiple regression analysis, these dimensions emerged as significant positive predictors of PA. These results indicate that students with more positive PE experiences are more likely to engage in PA. This finding is similar to that of previous research, which suggests that when students perceive PE as supportive, well-structured, and enjoyable, they are more likely to participate in and sustain PA during and outside school hours [76, 77]. Bronfenbrenner [78] argued that various environmental systems, such as family, school, and community, influence individual behaviours, including PA. Bandura [21] emphasised the role of social environments in self-efficacy, arguing that supportive contexts can enhance individuals’ confidence in engaging in behaviours such as PA.

Consistent with the findings of Moeller et al. [79], we also found that school-based PA interventions had a small but statistically significant positive effect on students’ engagement in PA, such as PEC (r = 0.395, p < 0.01) and SBEPAP (r = 0.285, p < 0.01). Moreover, among the 10 dimensions of QPE perception, COFSCPE, PIPAHBSR, and CBSR demonstrated the strongest correlations with PA (r = 0.476, p < 0.01; r = 0.462, p < 0.01; r = 0.439, p < 0.01, respectively). In the subsequent multiple regression analysis, similar trends were observed across these dimensions. These results indicate that school-based PA intervention programmes have limited success in increasing PA intensity [17] and a minimal impact on increasing the time spent in MVPA [17]. Comte et al. [80] found no significant differences in achieving PA guidelines on weekends between adolescents enrolled in PE and those who were not. Although school-based PA programmes can effectively increase MVPA during PE classes, their influence on PA participation during leisure time remains inconsistent [81, 82]. Second, schools are not the only institutions in which PA should be promoted. Neighbourhood settings, including parks, playgrounds, and other green spaces, along with parental support and permission, play a significant role in the overall engagement of PA adolescents and may help increase their MVPA levels [83, 84]. Therefore, interventions should engage family, school, and community members as key sources of support to promote PA promotion [85]. Multi-component interventions involving the entire school community appear to be the most effective [82]. This aligns with the QPE framework of the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization [9], which emphasises the importance of involving multiple stakeholders in creating inclusive and supportive environments that foster active living among students.

We observed an indirect association between the 10 dimensions of QPE perception and 7-day PA via ESE, consistent with mediation. The 10 dimensions of QPE perception were positively associated with PA through higher ESE. Bandura’s [21] social cognitive theory highlights the importance of a supportive and positive learning environment for fostering self-efficacy. Factors such as positive feedback, role modelling, and accessible resources significantly boosted students’ confidence in their ability to learn and perform in PE [86]. A positive perception of the learning environment promotes the development of self-efficacy. Moreover, Bandura’s [21] social cognitive theory posits that an individual’s self-efficacy and beliefs about their ability to complete a task have a significant impact on their performance and resulting outcomes. These beliefs shape how individuals approach tasks and influence their effort and persistence. In PA behaviours, students with higher self-efficacy are more likely to confidently engage in regular PA, overcome challenges, and persist in PA despite obstacles, thereby increasing their overall PA levels [87]. A positive learning environment not only contributes directly to increased self-efficacy in PE, but also amplifies the positive impact on PE gains, thus creating a cascading effect. Thus, the findings of this research emphasise the importance of creating a QPE environment that not only promotes PA levels but also fosters self-efficacy and confidence in students’ ability to learn and excel in the field of PE.

Another key finding is that, when ESE was modelled as a mediator, the indirect associations of the 10 QPE perception dimensions with 7-day PA were stronger than the corresponding direct associations. This pattern underscores the role of ESE as an associative pathway linking QPE perceptions to sustained PA behaviours. While QPE perceptions were related to PA levels, the ESE-related indirect association appeared more prominent than the direct association, suggesting a proximal associative pathway for long-term engagement. Existing research often emphasises the direct impact of PE on PA. For example, students tend to have higher PA levels on days when they attend PE classes [88, 89]. However, an increasing body of research suggests that PE, as currently implemented, may not significantly improve overall PA levels [17, 90]. One possible explanation for this is that traditional PE programmes frequently fail to bolster students’ ESE. Specifically, a lack of improvement in self-efficacy appears to be a key factor preventing many students from remaining active outside school. For instance, some students do not participate in PA during PE classes or lack the confidence to continue exercising after the class ends [91, 92]. Consequently, revisiting the PE structure to foster not only physical skill development, but also greater confidence in engaging in PA beyond the classroom is essential. By addressing these factors, PE can contribute more effectively to long-term increases in adolescents’ overall PA levels.

Implications

This study has important implications for both practice and public health. First, we found significant differences in sex, minority status, and school location regarding the 10 dimensions of QPE perception, ESE, and 7-day PA. These findings highlight the need for targeted approaches to address disparities and ensure equitable access to PE and PA among all students. Teachers and policymakers should design activities that address the interests and needs of females, with an emphasis on enhancing female students’ self-efficacy and encouraging their active participation in PA. For example, implementing sex-segregated PE classes or offering sports programmes that specifically focus on activities traditionally favoured by females, such as dance or yoga. Public health policies must address the unique challenges faced by minority and rural students to ensure equitable access to resources and opportunities for PE and PA. For example, priorities include facility and equipment grants for rural schools, transport support and safe-route programmes, protected minimum PE minutes, incentives to recruit and retain qualified PE teachers, and school–community partnerships to expand after-school PA. Second, this study found that older students, who have the autonomy to choose their PE courses based on their interests, reported higher levels of the SEEPE dimension. This suggests that providing students with greater choices and flexibility in their PE curricula could enhance their PA motivation and involvement. Third, correlation and multiple regression analyses indicated that family and community sports resources have a stronger influence on students’ overall PA than traditional school-based PA programmes. This finding suggests that interventions promoting PA should involve a collaborative approach that integrates the efforts of families, schools, and communities. By fostering shared responsibility for supporting PA, such a model may lead to more sustainable and effective outcomes by encouraging students to engage in regular PA. Finally, the results revealed that the indirect associations in the mediation model were stronger than the direct associations across all 10 dimensions of QPE perception. This pattern indicates that ESE functions as a salient associative pathway linking QPE perceptions with PA. Rather than solely concentrating on physical skills, efforts should prioritise strengthening self-efficacy to achieve long-term outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the relationship between QPE perception and 7‑day PA while assessing the indirect association via ESE. This study addresses the existing evidence gaps and provides several key strengths. First, it highlights the importance of ESE as a correlate of overall PA, offering new insights into how QPE perceptions are related to behaviour through ESE. Second, this study employed a robust methodological approach, utilising both correlation and multiple regression analyses to explore complex relationships. Additionally, the large sample size of secondary school students from diverse backgrounds enhanced the generalisability of the findings. Finally, by considering the multiple dimensions of QPE perception, this study provides a comprehensive understanding of the factors that contribute to students’ overall PA engagement.

However, this study has some limitations First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference among QPE perceptions, ESE, and 7-day PA. Temporal precedence cannot be established. Longitudinal cohorts and intervention trials are needed to test causality and track change. Future work should design QPE interventions in which PE teachers explicitly operationalise the teacher behaviours identified by Ahmadi et al. [93] to enhance students’ autonomous motivation, engagement, and learning. Intervention studies should examine whether these practices strengthen ESE and increase PA level. Second, the self-report format may have introduced social desirability bias and recall limitations [94]. Students may have overstated favourable perceptions of QPE and their own efficacy to align with perceived expectations, and may have overreported PA due to idealised self-presentation. Future research should adopt objective measurements and multi-informant designs, for example accelerometers or heart-rate sensors, with teacher-reported measures of lesson quality and student engagement or structured classroom observations. Third, only 10 of the 53 invited schools participated (19% response rate), introducing risk of selection and non-response bias, and characteristics of non-responding schools and students were unavailable. Moreover, the sample comprised only secondary school students in China. These limitations collectively restrict the generalisability of the findings to other populations and settings. Future research should recruit schools and students from culturally diverse settings to broaden the evidence base and enhance external validity. Fourth, socioeconomic variables (family income, parental education) and students’ prior sport participation and athletic specialisation were not measured. These omissions may introduce residual confounding [95]. Their omission may bias the estimated associations. Additionally, this study focused primarily on the mediating role of ESE, and other potential mediating factors, such as social support or motivation, were not explored. Future research should include these variables as parallel and theory-driven serial mediators. Use bias-corrected bootstrapping and a longitudinal follow-up to strengthen temporal ordering.

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the relationship between QPE perception, ESE, and 7-day PA among secondary school students. Significant differences were observed in the 10 dimensions of QPE perception, ESE, and 7-day PA among students based on sex, ethnic background, and school location. ESE plays a crucial mediating role in translating QPE perceptions into increased PA, highlighting the importance of focusing on self-efficacy enhancement in PE programmes. These findings offer important implications for educational practices and public health, suggesting the need for targeted interventions that improve self-efficacy while considering the unique needs of different student populations. Future research should explore longitudinal designs, incorporate objective PA measures, and investigate additional mediating factors to further understand the mechanisms that influence students’ physical activity behaviours.
