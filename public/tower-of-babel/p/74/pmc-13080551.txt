2:1 Atrioventricular Block After Upgrade From DDD to CRT-D
Authors: Jingfang Cui, Jie Li, Yongjia Li, Yanzhuo Ma, Shuying Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA high burden of right ventricular pacing predisposes patients to pacing-induced cardiomyopathy. Cardiac resynchronization therapy with defibrillator (CRT-D) effectively addresses this by delivering biventricular pacing, and a high rate of such pacing is essential for optimal clinical response.Case SummaryWe present a patient with high-grade atrioventricular block who developed 2:1 ventricular pacing after upgrade to CRT-D given pacing-induced cardiomyopathy. History of Presentation

The patient was a 63-year-old man who experienced chest tightness during physical activity in 2017, which resolved after several minutes of rest. He was diagnosed with coronary heart disease, second-degree atrioventricular block (AVB), and complete right bundle branch block at another hospital, and he underwent percutaneous coronary intervention and dual-chamber pacemaker implantation (right sided). Postoperative echocardiography showed a 31-mm left atrium, a 52-mm left ventricle, and an ejection fraction (EF) of 60.78%, while electrocardiography indicated right ventricular pacing rhythm with a QRS duration of 200 ms (Figure 1A). In 2021, the patient began experiencing shortness of breath accompanied by lower limb edema. He was hospitalized multiple times at local hospitals and was diagnosed with heart failure. As his condition responded poorly to optimized medical therapy, he was referred to our hospital for further evaluation. Repeat echocardiography revealed a 43-mm left atrium, a 76-mm left ventricle, and an EF of 33%. An electrocardiogram showed atrioventricular sequential pacing with a QRS duration of 236 ms (Figure 1B). Pacemaker interrogation indicated a ventricular pacing proportion of 100%. Subsequent coronary angiography showed no significant vascular stenosis. Considering the clinical presentation, the patient's cardiac dysfunction was attributed to pacing-induced cardiomyopathy (PICM), and an upgrade to cardiac resynchronization therapy with defibrillator (CRT-D) was recommended.

Interventions

The patient underwent successful CRT-D implantation at our center. For the implantation procedure, the right axillary vein was punctured, and a coronary sinus guiding sheath (straight sheath) was introduced. Coronary venography revealed no suitable lateral or posterolateral veins. A Sion PTCA guidewire (Asahi Intecc) was advanced to the distal anterior lateral vein, passed through collateral circulation into the middle cardiac vein, and returned to the coronary sinus. Guided by a sheath-within-sheath system, a left ventricular quadripolar pacing lead was delivered to the distal anterior lateral vein. Testing parameters showed pacing thresholds of 0.5 V (pulse width: 0.48 ms) for the 4 electrode; impedance of 589 Ω; the distal electrode located in the midventricle (right anterior oblique view), with the other 3 electrodes positioned at the ventricular base. Next, a defibrillation lead was implanted at the right ventricular apex. Testing parameters included a pacing threshold of 0.5 V (pulse width: 0.48 ms), impedance of 475 Ω, sensing of 15 mV, and high-voltage impedance of 74 Ω (Figure 2).The existing right atrial lead tested with a pacing threshold of 0.8 V (pulse width: 0.48 ms), impedance of 513 Ω, and sensing of 3.1 mV. The original ventricular lead was capped and buried in the pocket. A CRT-D device (Medtronic DTBC2QQ CRT-D, serial number: BLX617913S) was implanted. Electrocardiography showed pacing rhythm in VAT mode (Figure 1C).

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient underwent regular follow-up after CRT-D implantation, demonstrating 100% biventricular pacing capture. Echocardiography showed improved EF compared with preimplantation values (Figure 3), along with significant improvement in symptoms.

At 22 months postimplantation, the patient presented to our hospital with nocturnal precordial discomfort and pronounced palpitations. Physical examination revealed cardiac arrhythmia. Ambulatory electrocardiography detected frequent premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) and intermittent 2:1 AVB (Figure 4). Echocardiography showed an EF of 62%, indicating no significant change from previous measurements. Chest x-ray confirmed stable lead positions without notable displacement (Figure 5). Device interrogation demonstrated normal parameters for the right atrial, right ventricular, and left ventricular leads. Intracardiac electrogram (Figure 6) revealed T-wave oversensing. The observed ventricular 2:1 pacing pattern was consequently attributed to T-wave oversensing leading to post-ventricular atrial refractory period (PVARP) automatic extension. Combined with relatively rapid sinus rhythm, this resulted in subsequent P waves falling within the PVARP and being misclassified as atrial refractory events. After adjustment of ventricular sensing sensitivity (from 0.3 to 1.2 mV) failed to resolve ventricular nontracking (Figure 7), pharmacological optimization was implemented by increasing metoprolol tartrate dosage and adding mexiletine. Follow-up evaluation 2 weeks later showed reduced PVC frequency and complete resolution of ventricular nontracking (Figure 8). Subsequent regular follow-up visits confirmed absence of AVB recurrence and maintenance of cardiac function within normal parameters.

Discussion

Right ventricular pacing can lead to ventricular electromechanical dyssynchrony, triggering cardiac dilation and cardiac dysfunction, thereby resulting in the development of PICM. The most common definition of PICM is an EF of <50% or a decline in EF of ≥10% after pacemaker implantation. The incidence of PICM ranges between 5.9% and 39.0%, and the long-term prognosis for these patients is poorer compared to patients without PICM.1,2 A meta-analysis identified risk factors for PICM, including male sex, history of myocardial infarction, chronic kidney disease, history of atrial fibrillation, low left ventricular ejection fraction, prolonged intrinsic QRS duration, high percentage of right ventricular pacing, and prolonged paced QRS duration.3 When patients have multiple risk factors, it is necessary to closely follow up the cardiac function, screen for PICM in time, and provide interventions, so as to reduce the mortality and readmission rate of heart failure.

CRT-D has become a critical therapeutic approach for patients with cardiac insufficiency. For those who have previously received conventional pacemakers and progressed to symptomatic heart failure, with EF ≤35% and right ventricular pacing proportion >40, upgrading to CRT-D is recommended by guidelines; this intervention helps improve clinical outcomes.4 However, a low biventricular pacing percentage after CRT-D implantation is a risk factor for worsening heart failure and may increase readmission rates and mortality in these patients. As illustrated in this case, 2:1 AVB can lead to bradycardia, reduce biventricular pacing percentage, and cause symptoms such as precordial discomfort. If not addressed promptly, it may exacerbate cardiac dysfunction. But why does ventricular 2:1 tracking occur? The following aspects should be considered: 1) atrial undersensing: Failure to consistently and accurately detect P waves may result in intermittent ventricular pacing; 2) upper tracking rate limit: When the atrial rate is too high, ventricular pacing may exhibit Wenckebach phenomenon or even 2:1 conduction; 3) ventricular lead malfunction: Ineffective capture of the ventricular myocardium may lead to pacing failure; 4) device malfunction: Battery depletion, for example, may cause decreased pacing output and ventricular noncapture; 5) special pacemaker functions: For example, Medtronic's MVP mode, which promotes intrinsic atrioventricular conduction, may lead to prolonged atrioventricular intervals and occasional dropped ventricular beats; and 6) ventricular oversensing: Oversensing of T waves or double counting of QRS may be misinterpreted as PVCs, causing subsequent P waves to fall within the PVARP and resulting in ventricular nontracking.

Based on pacemaker interrogation, chest x-ray, intracardiac electrograms, and other auxiliary examinations, we identified that the ventricular 2:1 tracking in this case was caused by T-wave oversensing, which occurs in approximately 8% to 14% of patients.5 Possible explanations include: 1) intrinsic changes in T waves: a) elevated T-wave to R-wave amplitude ratio due to factors such as electrolyte imbalances or myocardial diseases; b) when ventricular resynchronization is achieved through CRT, calcium-channel reverse remodeling may occur, modifying cardiac repolarization and consequently changing T-wave morphology; c) electrotonic modulation: restoration of normal ventricular activation sequence may cause transient reversible deep T-wave inversion through electrotonic modulation, though this phenomenon typically lasts only from several days to weeks; and d) pharmacological factors, such as sacubitril/valsartan induced ventricular reverse remodeling affecting ventricular repolarization, or diuretic-induced electrolyte disturbances leading to T-wave changes. Another explanation is: 2) CRT-related factors: a) cardiac reverse remodeling induced by CRT may lead to changes in lead position and b) excessively high ventricular sensing sensitivity.5, 6, 7

Based on our literature review regarding the management of such issues, we have identified the following primary approaches.8, 9, 10 1) Adjust CRT-D parameters: Reduce ventricular sensing sensitivity, extend the ventricular refractory period so that T waves fall within this period and cannot be detected by the ventricle, shorten PVARP to allow P waves falling after the PVARP to be sensed by the atrium, modify the left ventricular–right ventricular pacing sequence or switch to left ventricular–only pacing to alter ventricular repolarization, thereby changing T-wave morphology and eliminating oversensing; 2) treat the underlying disease; 3) change electrode polarity, such as switching the ventricular sensing electrode to an integrated bipolar sensing; 4) install a right ventricular pacing/sensing lead; or 5) administer antiarrhythmic drugs to reduce the intrinsic heart rate, allowing P waves to fall after the PVARP, which has an effect similar to shortening the PVARP.

In the present case, the patient experienced ventricular nontracking 22 months after CRT-D upgrade. Chest x-ray showed reduced cardiac silhouette size compared with previous imaging, with no identifiable precipitating factors such as electrolyte disturbances or cardiomyopathy. This phenomenon was therefore attributed to changes in ventricular lead position after cardiac reverse remodeling, combined with excessively high ventricular sensing sensitivity in the CRT-D device. We initially adjusted ventricular sensing sensitivity, but follow-up Holter monitoring still demonstrated persistent 2:1 AVB. Considering this patient's comorbid condition of PVCs, shortening the PVARP could have risked inducing, so we opted for pharmacological management. The metoprolol tartrate dosage was increased from 12.5 mg to 25 mg twice daily, and mexiletine 150 mg 3 times daily was added. Two weeks later, repeat Holter monitoring confirmed restoration of 1:1 ventricular tracking. Device interrogation showed no T-wave oversensing events on intracardiac electrograms. During subsequent regular follow-up visits, the patient reported no palpitations or sensation of skipped beats, and no further AVB episodes were detected on Holter monitoring, with cardiac function remaining normal.

Conclusions

We present a case of T-wave oversensing causing 2:1 ventricular pacing in a patient after CRT-D implantation, which was successfully managed with antiarrhythmic drugs to restore atrioventricular sequential pacing. This case demonstrates that when T-wave oversensing cannot be resolved through device parameter adjustments or treatment of underlying conditions, antiarrhythmic medication can serve as an effective alternative means to re-establish proper atrioventricular synchronous pacing.
