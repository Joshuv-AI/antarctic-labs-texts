Mitral reoperations, reimagined
Authors: Paola Keese Montanhesi, Arnar Geirsson, Daniel JP Burns, Gianluigi Bisleri
Published: 2026

Redo minimally invasive mitral valve surgery.

Central Message

Minimally invasive surgery has redefined mitral reoperations, improving perioperative outcomes and long-term survival. Multicenter data support right-chest access as a safe and effective redo strategy.

Cardiac surgeons have long been dedicated to advancing minimally invasive approaches for mitral valve surgery. The motivation is straightforward: to deliver safer operations, faster recovery, and durable outcomes. What follows reflects our collective experience and interpretation of the evolving evidence supporting the role of minimally invasive approaches in the setting of reoperations for mitral valve surgery.

Minimally Invasive Mitral Valve Surgery

Mitral valve surgery has come a long way. What began as right thoracotomy under direct vision1, 2, 3, 4 has evolved into fully endoscopic and, in many centers, robotic-assisted procedures.5, 6, 7 This transformation came from years of persistence and refinement. Each step, from direct vision to video-assisted to robotics, has been directed toward a major goal: reducing surgical trauma without compromising exposure, precision, or safety.

Minimally invasive mitral surgery has now become a primary strategy for repair and replacement in many institutions worldwide. The combination of better instrumentation, high-definition imaging, safer perfusion strategies, and Enhanced Recovery After Surgery protocols8, 9, 10 made it reproducible and safe in a wide spectrum of patients.11, 12, 13 Variations in access expanded the possibilities even further, providing equivalent exposure with superior cosmetic results in carefully selected patients.14,15

Why has minimally invasive surgery gained this ground? Because it delivers what patients and surgeons most value. In experienced programs, outcomes of right minithoracotomy match those achieved through sternotomy or, in some cases, even surpass them.11, 12, 13,16 Avoiding sternotomy can reduce pain, blood loss, ventilation times, and wound complications, allowing shorter hospital stays and faster recovery. Importantly, these advantages do not compromise safety or durability of the operation,11, 12, 13 and are associated with improved patient satisfaction17 and aesthetic results. And yet, despite the progress achieved in minimally invasive cardiac surgery, one frontier has remained less explored: the reoperative setting.

Mitral Valve Reoperations

Redo mitral valve surgery has a reputation for being high risk and technically challenging.18 Reoperations independently predict greater short- and long-term mortality after cardiac surgery, reflecting the baseline vulnerability of this population.19 However, the evolution toward a minimally invasive approach represents a meaningful technical improvement for surgeons routinely involved in redo cases. Such strategy brings the logic and advantages of minimally invasive surgery to a population that historically faced more significant operative hazards.

A redo procedure through right minithoracotomy protects structures at risk during sternal re-entry, especially patent grafts, the right ventricle, and great vessels, which often adhere to the posterior sternum. The lateral route minimizes dissection around cardiac structures and optimizes exposure of the mitral valve, even in the presence of dense adhesions: less trauma, better exposure, lower risk.20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25,E1, E2, E3, E4 Today, redo mini-mitral operations can be performed endoscopically and, in experienced centers, also with robotic assistance.E5,E6

Patient Selection

Patients requiring a redo mitral procedure represent a heterogeneous population, and not all are suitable candidates for a lateral approach. The same considerations that guide patient selection for minimally invasive mitral valve surgery in primary cases apply to reoperations.E7,E8 Absolute contraindications include severe chest-wall deformity, more than mild aortic regurgitation, and severe aortic and peripheral vascular disease, which limit safe cannulation and perfusion.E7,E8

Other factors historically viewed as contraindications are more nuanced and may be interpreted in the context of institutional volume and surgeon expertise. In our experience, for example, extensive mitral annular calcification alone does not preclude a minimally invasive approach. Similarly, pulmonary hypertension, particularly when associated with different degrees of right ventricular dysfunction, increases procedural risk and requires individualized assessment given the potential need for postoperative mechanical support.

Preoperative computed tomographic angiography of the aortoiliac and femoral vessels guides peripheral cannulation and endoaortic balloon positioning, whenever used, reducing the potential risk of vascular injury and perioperative cerebrovascular complications. Lung adhesions, however, remain unpredictable even with preoperative imaging. They must be approached carefully - and typically before heparin administration - to ensure safe access to the pericardium, especially in patients with prior right chest entry.E7, E8, E9, E10, E11

Intraoperative Considerations

Peripheral cannulation for cardiopulmonary bypass can be established through percutaneous access or surgical exposure of the femoral vessels, depending on surgeon preference and patient anatomy.E12, E13, E14, E15 Regardless of approach, transesophageal echocardiography is essential to guide wire advancement and confirm cannula position. In patients with peripheral artery disease or unfavorable iliofemoral anatomy, arterial inflow can alternatively be achieved through the right or less commonly left axillary artery, exposed via a subclavicular incision, to ensure antegrade systemic perfusion while minimizing the risk of complications.

Every mitral operation relies on effective myocardial protection. Surgeons may use direct crossclamping, endoaortic balloon occlusion, or hypothermic ventricular fibrillation. In reoperations, avoiding external aortic clamping limits aorta mobilization and dissection from surrounding adhesions, minimizing risks of inadvertent aortic or pulmonary artery injury. Therefore, there has been an increased interest in techniques that allow for cardiac arrest without external manipulation of the ascending aorta, such as endo-aortic balloon occlusion and ventricular fibrillation. Studies suggest that early morbidity and mortality are similar between approaches.E16,E17

The endoaortic balloon provides a controlled, endovascular occlusion of the ascending aorta, as well as antegrade cardioplegia delivery and root venting through a single device.E18, E19, E20 Technical success relies on preoperative selection and intraprocedural monitoring.E10 Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography, bilateral arterial lines and root pressure monitoring are key to ensure adequate positioning and detect migration or inadequate perfusion. Several studies in mitral valve surgery support the safety of this approach.E18, E19, E20, E21, E22 It should be noted that potential limitations are related to unsuitable peripheral arterial anatomy (vessel size, diffuse atherosclerotic disease, presence of aneurysmal disease or fibro-muscular dysplasia), as well as concerns about significant additional cost.E22

In our experience, ventricular fibrillation has become primary option in redo settings. This approach can be significantly simplified by having a temporary pacing wire percutaneously inserted through the right internal jugular vein by the anesthesia team immediately after induction and before patient's prepping and draping. It allows for rapid pacing (around 200 bpm) and induced ventricular fibrillatory arrest, combined with systemic cooling to approximately 30 °C. This strategy is particularly effective to avoid dissection of the right ventricle, which would otherwise be required for placement of epicardial pacing wires.E23

One potential challenge with this approach is ensuring adequate air removal before restoring ventricular function. Deairing is achieved using a combination of continuous carbon dioxide insufflation of the chest cavity, Trendelenburg positioning, and left atrial venting, which may be maintained until transesophageal echocardiography confirms clearance of intracardiac air.E24 We typically position a drop suction catheter through the mitral valve to facilitate left ventricular deairing; however, in the presence of a mechanical mitral prosthesis, the vent is kept in the left atrium rather than advanced across the valve. When an endoaortic balloon is used, additional aortic root venting is achieved through the device.E25 Once air removal is complete, the vent is removed and the left atrium, closed.

Safety and Outcomes

The safety of minimally invasive mitral reoperations has been previously debated, largely due to concerns regarding the potential need for conversion to sternotomy. In contemporary series, conversions have been reported mostly related to aortic or pulmonary artery injury, as well as severe pulmonary adhesions leading to significant lung damage.E26

In our program, these risks have been mitigated through careful preoperative planning. For example, femoral vessels are exposed first to allow rapid institution of cardiopulmonary bypass if required, but without systemic heparinization (which is deferred until the pericardium is clearly identified and any potential lung adhesions are dissected free). Consistent with our experience, high-volume centers performing minimally invasive redo mitral valve surgery reported very low conversion rates, including series with no conversions during extended follow-up.21,E26

Growing evidence supports right minithoracotomy as a safe and effective strategy for reoperative mitral valve surgery in a large majority of patients. Multiple recent series have shown that minimally invasive reoperations achieve comparable or even lower perioperative mortality compared with conventional resternotomy.E27, E28, E29, E30, E31 In addition, major complications including low cardiac output syndrome, early stroke, and new-onset renal failure were less frequent in the minithoracotomy group.E28 Patients also experienced less postoperative bleeding, fewer transfusions, shorter ventilation time, fewer wound complications, shorter intensive care and hospital stays, and greater overall satisfaction.23,24,E27, E28, E29, E30, E31 Of note, there was no relevant prolongation of bypass or crossclamp times with the right chest access.23,E30

Data from the Netherlands Heart RegistrationE31 showed that patients undergoing minimally invasive mitral valve surgery after previous cardiac surgery achieved early mortality comparable with repeat sternotomy, after adjustment for baseline differences. In matched analyses, the minithoracotomy was associated with lower pulmonary complications, similar stroke, renal failure and reoperation rates and shorter intensive care unit and hospital stays, supporting its safety and potential early advantages.

Such results reflect progression along the learning curve, with improved technical proficiency and team coordination positively impacting clinical outcomes. Meta-analyses have documented the impact of growing experience in redo mini-mitrals, with reductions in 30-day mortality, hospital stay, and new-onset renal failure, without increases in stroke or other major adverse events.E32, E33, E34 The largest data-setsE33, E34 are mainly influenced by the same series: the large study by Svensson and colleaguesE35 (2469 patients) provides most of the statistical weight and defines the baseline risk, with a neutral mortality signal; and the contemporary descriptive cohort reported by Kwon and colleaguesE28 drives mortality and morbidity benefits in the minimally invasive group.

In 380 isolated redo mitral operations after prior sternotomy from 2002 to 2018, Kwon and colleagues reported lower early mortality (4.3% vs 11.0%) and morbidity after right minithoracotomy versus sternotomy, including low cardiac output syndrome (5.6% vs 12.4%), early stroke (6.8% vs 14.2%), new-onset dialysis (6.2% vs 17.0%), prolonged ventilation (15.4% vs 33.0%), and reexploration for bleeding (7.4% vs 10.1%). The study also described shorter intensive care unit (2 vs 3 days) and hospital stays (8 vs 14 days) in the minimally invasive group.E28

As a result, both meta-analysis report reductions in early mortality (odds ratio, 0.59; 95% CI 0.39-0.90; P = .01E33 and risk ratio, 0.54; 95% CI, 0.37-0.80; P < .001E34), with lenght of hospital stay (mean difference, −3.71, 95% CI, −4.92 to −2.49; P < .001E33; and mean difference, −4.23; 95% CI, −5.77 to −2.68; P < .001)E34 and new-onset dialysis (odds ratio, 0.38; 95% CI, 0.22-0.65; P < .01E33 and risk ratio, 0.34; 95% CI, 0.19-0.61; P < .001E34) similarly favouring minithoracotomy.

Beyond early outcomes, a recent large-scale propensity-matched analysis suggests that redo mini-mitral surgery may be beneficial long-term.E36 After matching, 10-year survival was significantly higher in patients who underwent minithoracotomy than sternotomy (60% ± 5% vs 42% ± 5%, P = .0006). The survival advantage was greatest in the first 6 months and remained evident throughout follow-up.E36

However, despite increasing data, the minimally invasive approach remains markedly underused, with reported adoption ranging from approximately 10% to 70% across centers,18,E31, E37 including our own. This variability reflects differences in surgeon experience and institutional adoption, and highlights an opportunity for practice evolution in the field of minimally invasive mitral valve surgery.

Limitations

The perspective presented here reflects the experience of large-volume and established minimally invasive mitral valve programs and should not be generalized. Although most of the technical principles are broadly applicable, outcomes remain closely related to patient selection and institutional expertise. Therefore, further data are necessary to ensure that such encouraging findings can be generalized.

Moreover, the available evidence is predominantly observational, since randomized trials are unlikely to be feasible due to patient heterogeneity and the operator-dependent nature of the minimally invasive approach. Finally, this discussion is limited to isolated mitral valve reoperations, and the addition of concomitant procedures (besides concomitant surgical ablation) introduces complexity that may influence both risk and feasibility.

Conclusions

Redo mitral valve surgery performed through right minithoracotomy, once considered aspirational, has matured into a reproducible, evidence-based strategy. The approach preserves the fundamental benefits of minimal access surgery while offering excellent visualization and exposure in complex reoperative anatomy.

Multicenter experience demonstrates that the minimally invasive approach can deliver superior perioperative outcomes and a potential long-term survival advantage to selected patients. The consistency of these results across large-volume centers suggests that minimally invasive redo mitral surgery is no longer a niche approach, but a reproducible option when performed by experienced teams.

Surgical outcomes depend on careful patient selection, preoperative imaging and planning, team coordination, and appropriate perfusion and myocardial protection. When these elements are present, endoscopic and robotic approaches can safely extend the benefits of minimally invasive surgery to patients undergoing mitral valve reoperations.
