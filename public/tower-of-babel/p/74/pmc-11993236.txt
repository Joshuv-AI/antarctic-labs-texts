The Enhancement of Potato (Solanum Tuberosum L. Cv. Odyssey) Resistance to Bacterial Soft Rot Disease Through Transformation of the Glyphosate-Resistant Gene from Dickeya Dadanti
Authors: Hossein Pasalari, Amir Roeintan, Sezai Ercisli, Danial Kahrizi, Chamran Hemmati
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Objective: An efficient protocol was developed via the Agrobacterium-mediated transformation method with the plasmid, p485, harboring the aroA gene from the bacterial species Dickeya dadantii, to improve resistance to potato bacterial soft rot disease. The study aimed to investigate the relationship between glyphosate application and the enhancement of potatoes’ resistance to two bacterial pathogens affecting the plants.Materials and Methods: An optimal concentration of 1.8 mg.L-1 of glyphosate was applied to transgenic potato varieties. The leaves of the Odyssey cultivar demonstrated resistance to two pathogenic strains, Pectobacterium atrosepticum 21A and D. dadantii ENA49. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and reverse transcription-PCR (RT-PCR) validation demonstrated the successful integration and heterologous expression of the aroA gene in the potato genome. Additionally, the transcriptional analysis revealed the expression of pathogenesis-related genes and genes associated with the potato defence response.Results: The study revealed a significant increase in the expression of pathogenesis-related genes (PR-2, PR-3, and PR-5) and defence response genes (HSR-203j and HIN1 in transgenic potato leaves after glyphosate treatment and subsequent exposure to pathogenic bacterial infection, with a particular emphasis on the upregulation of HSR-203j. A comparative analysis assessed the average expression levels of these genes in both experimental and control samples. In contrast, minimal changes in gene expression were observed in plants infected with bacteria but not treated with glyphosate.Conclusion: The study suggests that glyphosate treatment in potatoes can enhance systemic acquired resistance to bacterial pathogens by upregulating pathogenesis-related and defence response genes. This approach shows potential for addressing bacterial diseases in potatoes, including soft bacterial rot.

1. Background

The cultivation of potatoes, the fourth most widely grown crop globally, is vulnerable to various diseases. Notably, Pectobacterium atrosepticum and Dickeya dadantii are two bacterial pathogens that pose signi-ficant risks to potato production ( 1 - 3 ).

The soft rot in potatoes is commonly associated with bacteria such as Pectobacterium and Dickeya species. These pathogens are known for their production of various extracellular enzymes, including pectolytic, cellulolytic, and proteolytic enzymes, which play crucial roles in their pathogenicity ( 4 , 5 ). Bacterial pathogens frequently infect potato storage organs, buds, stems, and petiole tissues, leading to detrimental effects on the plant. While disease control measures can help reduce the spread, genetic engineering plays a crucial role in enhancing and breeding potato crops due to their nutritional significance ( 6 ).

Glyphosate is a widely used herbicide that inhibits the activity of 5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate (EPSP) synthase, an enzyme in plant plastids which is crucial for the shikimate pathway. The shikimate pathway is responsible for the production of aromatic amino acids in bacteria, fungi, and plants. The aroA gene, recognized for conferring glyphosate resistance, also enhances resistance to biotic stresses caused by fungal and bacterial pathogens ( 9 ). The aroA gene encodes the 5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate synthase (EPSPS) enzyme. Glyphosate competitively inhibits EPSPS by mimicking phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP), forming a stable non-covalent ternary complex with the enzyme and shikimate-3-phosphate (S3P) ( 10 , 11 ). Previous research has shown that glyphosate competes with phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) at the enzyme active site, as supported by several studies ( 12 , 13 ). Furthermore, glyphosate accumulation in plant and fungal tissues can enhance resistance to fungal pathogens, but may also have toxic effects on microorganisms ( 14 ). When crops face biotic and abiotic stresses, plants activate defence mechanisms to respond effectively. These mechanisms include physical barriers, metabolites, and proteins that hinder the growth of invading microorganisms ( 15 , 16 ). Plants can either localize their responses to pathogenic infections to the infected organ or extend them systemically throughout the entire plant. Systemic Acquired Resistance (SAR) and Induced Systemic Resistance (ISR) are crucial defence mechanisms in plants. SAR involves the production of pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins at the site of infection, serving as a crucial signal transduction pathway for plant defence ( 17 - 19 ). The induction and accumulation of pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins in plants play a crucial role in enhancing defence mechanisms against adverse conditions. The accumulation of PR proteins helps protect the plant from further infections, with their presence observed in locally infected, adjacent, and even distant uninfected tissues. Plant responses to infection or stress involve the induction of genes encoding protective proteins ( 20 ). The PR genes encode defensive proteins expressed in plants in response to stress factors and pathogen infections. The accumulation of PR proteins is linked to the plant’s resistance to pathogens and the development of systemic acquired resistance ( 21 , 22 ). The induction of PR proteins has been observed in numerous plant species spanning diverse families, highlighting the broad involvement of these proteins in enhancing adaptability to both biotic and abiotic stressors. PR proteins have been categorized into 17 specific families across different plant species ( 22 , 23 ). The PR proteins are defined as proteins encoded by the genetic code of the host plant that is specifically activated in response to pathological or related circumstances. The term “associated circumstances” encompasses conditions that may not be pathogenic ( 20 ).

2. Objective

This study aimed to explore the relationship between glyphosate treatments and the enhancement of potato resistance against two bacterial pathogens. This research contributes to our understanding of how glyphosate treatments may impact plant defence mechanisms against these bacterial pathogens. We utilized the aroA gene from D. dadantii in a genetic construct to transform potato plants via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. It aimed to investigate the relationship between the accu-mulation of pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins and the acquisition of resistance following glyphosate treatment of infected leaves of transgenic potatoes. The focus was on enhancing potato resistance to bacterial soft rot disease by incorporating the glyphosate-resistant gene from D. dadantii.

3. Material and Methods

The study was divided into two distinct phases, which are explained in detail below.

3.1. Part 1: Transformation of Potato by Agrobacterium-Mediated Transformation Method

3.1.1. Plant Materials and Bacterial Strains

The potato plant cultivar Odyssey was propagated under in vitro conditions using Murashige and Skoog ( 24 ) medium (MS), which consisted of 20 g.L-1 sucrose and 8 g.L-1 Danagel (Sigma, USA). The pH of the MS medium was adjusted to 5. 7 using either 0. 1 N HCl or KOH before the addition of Danagel and subsequent autoclaving at 120 °C for 20 minutes. Growth regulators were indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), naphthalene acetic acid (NAA), 6-benzylaminopurine (BAP, Sigma, St Louis MO USA) and gibberellic acid 3 (GA3).

The growth chamber utilized a light source emitting a photoperiod of 16 hours, with an intensity of 100 µE m−2 s−1 provided by white fluorescent light. The temperature within the chamber was maintained at 22 °C (±1 °C) for the cultivation of potato plantlets.

In the process of genetic transformation, utilization was made of Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain LBA4404, containing the plasmid (p485), which housed the aroA gene as depicted in Figure 1. This material was generously supplied by Pasalari and their colleagues (Department of Molecular Biology, BSU, Belarus).

Schematic representation of the T-DNA region of plasmid pZH485 containing the aroA gene

3.1.2. Transformation and Plant Regeneration

The intermodal explants were obtained from plantlets that were four weeks old and had a size ranging from 4-6 mm. These explants were cut longitudinally at their midsection. The growth conditions of bacteria and their inoculation with the studied potatoes were applied based on the protocol of Bijan et al ( 28 ). The media applied in the transformation experiment are also shown in Table 1. Following a three-day co-cultivation period, the explants were washed using a liquid medium composed of MS salts, MS vitamins, 3% sucrose, and a pH level of 5. In this study, the growth medium utilized was fortified with a concentration of 1 g.L-1 cefotaxime (Claforan, manufactured by Roussel-Uclaf in France). The explants were immersed in this solution for 30 minutes. Following the dry blotting process, the explants were subsequently transferred onto a callus induction medium (CIM). When the calli were sufficiently developed, they were subcultured onto a medium called Shoot Induction Medium (SIM), which consisted of 25 mg. L-1 kanamycin and 150mg.L-1 vimentin, an auxin-lacking CIM medium. Green shoots were carefully excised, reaching a length of 1–2 cm, and subsequently transferred to large test tubes (150×25 mm) containing a selective Root Inducing Medium (RIM). The roots were acquired after one week of cultivation. Only plantlets exhibiting optimal root development were selected for transfer to the glasshouse facility for subsequent molecular analysis.

3.1.3. Treatment by Herbicide Glyphosate and Inoculation with the Bacteria, P. atrosepticum 21A and D. dadantii ENA49

In the investigation of resistance induction, the bacterial strains utilized for inoculation included P. atrosepticum strain 21A and D. dadantii strain ENA49. To examine the impact of glyphosate on the induction of resistance, the transgenic potato Odyssey cultivar was subjected to glyphosate treatment at the preferable concentration of 1. 8 mg.L-1. Colonies of the P. atrosepticum A21 and D. dadantii ENA49 strains were cultivated individually in an LB medium containing 50 mg.L-1 of kanamycin. The cultures were permitted to grow overnight at a temperature of 28 ºC with consistent shaking at 200 rpm until reaching the mid-log phase. Subsequently, they were diluted to a concentration of 1:100 in a sterile autoclaved saline solution. The bacterial strains P. atrosepticum A21 and D. dadantii ENA49 were each cultivated individually in LB medium supplemented with a concentration of 50 mg.L-1 of kanamycin. The cultures were subjected to overnight incubation at 28 ºC, with continuous agitation at 200 rpm until they attained the mid-log growth phase. Subsequently, the cultures were diluted to a ratio of 1:100 in a sterile autoclaved saline solution (NaCl solution). This culture was subsequently used for co-cultivation. For treatment by glyphosate, the potato leaves were sprayed with glyphosate in three replicates at a concentration of 1.8 mg.L-1 and three days after spraying, inoculated with the bacteria Pa 21A and Dd ENA49. To accomplish this, 10 ml of the bacterial suspension (approximately 2 ×107 CFU.mL-1) was applied to the plant surface, which had been injured with a scalpel. For the control, leaf samples of transformed potatoes were infected with the target bacteria without glyphosate treatment.

3.1.4. Features of the Plant Expression Cassette

The genetic transformation was performed using Agrobacterium tumefaciens harboring plasmid 485, provided by Pasalari and colleagues from the Department of Molecular Biology at BSU, Belarus. This plasmid contains the not II selectable marker gene under transcriptional control of the nos promoter and nos terminator and the CTP reporter gene transcribed by the 35S promoter and nos terminator between the T-DNA borders. A schematic representation of the T-DNA region of p485, which includes the aroA gene, is provided in Figure 1, as presented by Nikolaychik and Colleagues (Department of Molecular Biology, BSU, Belarus.

3.2. Part 2: Molecular Analysis of Transgenic Potato

3.2.1. Experimental Materials

Three days following the application of herbicides or the introduction of bacteria, plant leaves from both genetically modified and non-genetically modified potatoes were collected, freeze-dried, and finely ground to facilitate the extraction of genomic DNA ( 6 ). PCR amplification was employed for the initial molecular verification. The DNA fragment encompassing the aroA gene was subjected to polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification exploiting specified primers (Table 2). The PCR procedure was executed according to the following protocol: an initial denaturation step at 94 ºC for 4 minutes followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 ºC, lasting 30 seconds each, followed by an annealing step at a temperature of 55 °C, lasting 1 minute. A subsequent extension phase was then conducted at 72 ºC for 90 seconds. Finally, a final extension was performed at a temperature of 72 ºC, lasting 60 minutes. The PCR products were subjected to electrophoresis using a 1% (w/v) agarose gel for separation.

3.2.2. Expression Analysis

Reverse transcriptase RT-PCR was employed to examine mRNAs. To conduct molecular analysis, total RNA was obtained from leaves that had been inoculated by bacteria and subjected to both glyphosate treatment and an untreated control for three days. Total RNA extraction was made using the NucleoSpin RNA Plant kit (Cat. No: RN 7713C; Macherey-Nagel Co., Germany). The first-strand cDNA was synthesized by employing 0. 5 µg of RNA, the oligo (dT) 18 primer, and the first-strand cDNA synthesis kit (Catalog Number: K1611; Fermentas, Canada). This particular kit involved the utilization of M-MuLV reverse transcriptase. PCR amplification was accomplished in the first-strand cDNA template in conjunction with primers specifically designed for aroA, as well as potato pathogenesis-related and defence response genes (Table 2). The relative value of mRNA copies was calculated and determined by the formula N (mRNA) = 2 (∆Ct - min (∆Ct)) ( 25 ).

3.3. Statistical Analysis

The experiment for the analysis of the gene expression was factorial with three replications based on a completely randomized design. The findings in this study are presented in mean ± standard deviation.

The study demonstrated that transgenic potato leaves treated with glyphosate and infected with pathogenic bacteria showed increased expression of pathogenesis-related genes, including PR-2, PR-3, and PR-5, compared to the control group. Notably, the PR-2 gene showed significant upregulation, suggesting its potential role in plant defence mechanisms against bacterial pathogens. Additionally, defence response genes, particularly HSR-203j and HIN1, exhibited significant expression levels, with HSR-203j showing particularly elevated levels. The PR-2 gene expression in the leaves subjected to inoculation with D. dadantii and P. atrosepticum infections ex-hibited fold changes of 1. 5 and 2.9, respectively. The gene HSR-203j exhibited a 2.5-fold and 2.4-fold upregulation in response to infection by D. dadantii and P. atrosepticum, respectively.

5. Discussion

The potato is a pivotal crop in agriculture owing to its considerable nutritional significance. Unfortunately, due to the high level of infection with agricultural pests and diseases, including bacteria and fungi, the amount of their production per hectare of agricultural land has decreased. This shows the necessity of using new methods by genetic engineers for the improvement and breeding of this crop.

Numerous methodologies have been effectively employed to achieve genetic transformation in a di-verse range of plants by utilizing leaf discs, potato tuber discs, and intermodal explants. The objective of employing these protocols is to generate transgenic plants that exhibit resistance against both biotic and abiotic stresses. ( 27 ). The efficiency of traditional methods for potato breeding and transgenic plant production for commercial applications is significantly hindered by limitations, notably the infrequency of transformation and the occurrence of soma clonal variation at extremely high rates, primarily attributed to alterations in the ploidy levels. The majority of the established procedures for potato transformation between internodes involved the utilization of intact intermodal regions ( 28 ). Comparable outcomes were acquired in instances where leaf explants were employed for transformation; however, the corresponding data has not been presented. Working with intermodal segments proved to be a more con-venient approach compared to leaf explants due to the former’s higher tolerance towards injuries sustained during various manipulation procedures. Furthermore, it was observed that the variation exhibited a lesser magnitude when sampling the internodes of the plant rather than the leaves (data not presented). Consequently, the necessary explants were derived from the internodes for further experimentation and analysis.

This particular commercial potato variety had a significant increase in transformation efficiency (data not shown), resulting in transgenic plants that displayed increased resistance to both bacterial diseases and herbicides. The foliar of transgenic plants was resistant to glyphosate treatment at a dose of 1.8 g.L-1. The nontransformed control plants exhibited heightened sensitivity to glyphosate concentrations exceeding 1.8 g.L-1, as evidenced by the onset of chlorosis within 3-4 days after herbicide application. After one week, the leaves of all nontransformed plants treated with glyphosate displayed chlorosis and subsequently underwent rapid wilting. Conversely, all kanamycin-resistant plants displayed no such symptoms, retaining their green coloration and remaining firmly rooted even when exposed to a glyphosate dosage of 1.8 g.L-1. All the transformed potatoes were chlorotic and died at a very high glyphosate concentration of 3.6 g.L-1 (data not shown). The findings of this study provide evidence of successful integration and expression of the aroA gene in transgenic potatoes. However, it is imperative to conduct additional research to effectively assess the herbicide resistance of transgenic potatoes under field conditions. Potatoes are susceptible to infection by various bacterial pathogens, especially P. atrosepticum and D. dadantii. Consequently, these infections result in significant reductions in both potato crop yields and overall quality. ( 29 ). Potato bacterial soft rot is considered one of the potatoes’ most common bacterial diseases. Utilizing technologies that can use the properties of herbicides or pesticides to improve the antipathogenic properties of plants will be useful in plant biotechnology. Plants can exhibit diverse reactions to various forms of stressors. Induced resistance can be observed either locally, in the vicinity of the initial infection or systemically, in locations distant from the original site of infection. The plant reacts to the initial stimulus by initiating a signal transduction pathway, which involves both intra and intercellular signals. This pathway ultimately leads to the activation of defence mechanisms, primarily through the transcription and expression of novel genes. Different defence pathways in plants have evolved in reaction to pathogens. These defence mechanisms can be stimulated and activated by some microorganisms or chemicals. Identifying molecular mechanisms of plant resistance to pathogens can help us to increase their resistance to plant pathogens ( 5 ).

One defence pathway, known as systemic acquired resistance (SAR), was found to be effective in inducing resistance to pathogenic bacteria through the upregulation of pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins and defence response genes when plants were treated with glyphosate. The research findings indicated a positive correlation between the plant’s ability to resist pathogenic organisms and the buildup of pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins. Moreover, this accumulation of PR proteins was found to be closely linked to the establishment of systemic acquired resistance in plants ( 21 ). This study has demonstrated that transgenic potato leaves, upon treatment with glyphosate, followed by inoculation with pathogenic bacteria, exhibited a notable upregulation of genes associated with pathogenesis, namely PR-2, PR-3, and PR-5. Additionally, defence response genes, particularly HSR-203j, showed a considerable increase in expression levels, further emphasizing the potential role of HSR-203j in the defence response of these transgenic potato leaves. The expression levels of the PR-2 gene in leaves that were inoculated with D. dadantii and P. atrosepticum were 1.5 and 2.9 times higher than the expression levels of the PR-3 gene. Additionally, the PR-5 gene showed expression levels that were 1.7 and 1.7 times higher than the PR-3 gene. Similarly, the expression levels of the HSR-203J gene were 2.5 and 2.4 times higher, and the HIN1 gene showed expression levels that were 1.7 and 1.7 times higher, with D. dadantii and P. atrosepticum infection, respectively. Plant growth and development are often challenged by several abiotic and biotic stresses ( 30 ). However, the bacterial infection in the plants that were not subjected to glyphosate treatment did not exhibit any noteworthy alteration in the expression of these genes. The results from symptomatological studies reflecting the antibacterial impact of glyphosate application (Fig. 4), together with the findings obtained from the molecular analyses, are consistent with the findings of other researchers ( 21 , 22 , 26 , 31 ). Furthermore, notable associations were identified between the expression levels of pathogenesis-related (PR) genes and defence response genes in potato leaves, indicating their potential role in the resistance of potatoes to bacterial soft rot. This suggests a complex interplay between gene expression patterns and disease resistance mechanisms in potatoes.

6. Conclusion

In plant genetic engineering, the study of the effects of glyphosate in terms of herbicidal, fungicidal and antibacterial properties has been used to produce weed-resistant plants and cure some fungal and bacterial diseases of plants. The findings suggest that glyphosate treatment in plants could effectively control weeds in agricultural settings while also potentially triggering systemic acquired resistance to pathogenic bacteria. This response may involve the expression of pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins and defence response genes, highlighting a potential dual benefit of glyphosate application in crop management.
