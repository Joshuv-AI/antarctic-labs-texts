Hierarchical Classification of Factors Associated With Noninvasive Prenatal Testing Failures and Its Impact on Pregnancy Outcomes
Authors: Jieqiong Xie, Yu Jiang, Yulin Zhou, Dandan Jin, Xingxiu Lu, Yunsheng Ge
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo conduct a hierarchical classification analysis of the nonreportable results of noninvasive prenatal testing in an attempt to reduce failure rates and provide pregnant women with accurate information to alleviate their anxiety.MethodsIn this study, 30,039 singleton pregnancies who underwent noninvasive prenatal testing in a single center from May 2019 to April 2022 were collected, and 811 samples with initial noninvasive prenatal testing failure were retrospectively analyzed. Grouping was based on the reasons for initial test failure; tracking the noninvasive prenatal testing results and prenatal diagnosis results (if any) of the “z-scores in the gray area” group and analyzing the possible influencing factors of the “low fetal fraction” group in the pre-experimental and experimental period by using one-way analysis of variance, Mann-Whitney U test, and χ2 test; and tracking the pregnancy outcomes of the test failures samples to analyze the risk of perinatal complications and adverse pregnancy outcomes of the different types of test failures.ResultsNone of the samples' initial inconclusive results because of z-scores in the gray area were found to have chromosomal aneuploidy. However, pregnancy complications (P = 0.018) and a high likelihood of adverse pregnancy outcomes (P = 0.048) may still occur. Maternal gestational age (P < 0.001), body mass index (P < 0.001), library concentration (P < 0.001), and fetal gender (P < 0.001) were considered to be the associated factors for the initial low fetal fraction results. This may be associated with pregnancy complications (P < 0.001) and a high likelihood of adverse pregnancy outcomes (P = 0.034). The body mass index (P = 0.015) and time between draws (P = 0.001) were associated with the second test’s success. The incidence of low fetal fraction samples was more frequent with blood collection tubes of the G type than with the K type (P < 0.001).ConclusionInitial inconclusive results because of z-scores in the gray area did not imply an increased risk of aneuploidy, but vigilance is needed for an increased risk of pregnancy complications and adverse pregnancy outcomes. Because of the low fetal fraction, the initial absence of results may be related to the assay method, as well as the effect of blood collection tubes and the need to be alert to the risk of pregnancy complications and adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Introduction

In 1997, Lo et al1 discovered cell-free fetal DNA (cffDNA) in the plasma of pregnant women, opening a new era of noninvasive prenatal testing (NIPT). This testing is currently used worldwide for the detection of common fetal trisomy 21 (T21), trisomy 18 (T18), and trisomy 13 (T13). Because of its noninvasiveness, accuracy, and safety, NIPT is well integrated into the current clinical system of obstetric inspection and is highly recommended by physicians for pregnant women.2 In terms of the test's performance metrics, attention has been paid to its sensitivity and specificity and less to its failure rates.3 However, testing at times returns no initial results, and approximately 0.8%4 to 12.2%5 of NIPTs fail. Not only does this decrease the test's actual sensitivity, but it can also delay reporting, which can affect patient satisfaction and limit options for prenatal diagnosis, especially at later gestational ages.

Generally, three main reasons are responsible for an uninterpretable report: logistical failure, technical problems, and inherent factors.3,6 The former includes improper specimen collection, transport, and storage of samples, which are the main causes of hemolysis, coagulation, and degradation of free DNA. Technical issues include failed DNA extraction, amplification, or sequencing, such as insufficient library concentration, guanine-cytosine (GC) bias, and z-scores in the gray area. GC bias is a common issue for applications using current massively parallel sequencing platforms and can be introduced either by sample preparation or sequencing procedures.7 The z-score in the gray area refers to z-scores with an absolute value between 1.96 and 3.00. As true negatives and true positives are not fully distinguishable at this point, no interpretable report can be given, but they can be used to flag potential negatives or positives. The latter refers to maternal or fetal factors, including but not limited to vanishing twins, maternal malignancy, fetal fraction (FF), and placenta or fetal mosaicism. The FF is the most critical determinant of the success of NIPT analysis.8 Cell-free DNA consists of maternal fragments9 and fetal fragments.1 The amount of cffDNA relative to the total amount of cell-free DNA, referred to as the FF, is a fundamental parameter that affects NIPT performance. The discrimination of chromosomal abnormalities improves with increasing FF10; therefore, a low FF may lead to false-negative NIPT results.11 For this reason, the minimum FF required for a valid result uses a fixed cutoff of 4%.12

Currently, there is little research on the NIPT failure rate and even less on the initial test failure. This study aims to systematically investigate the causes of NIPT failure by categorizing cases based on different types of test failures and conducting a hierarchical analysis of the results from each repeated test. This includes assessing prenatal diagnosis (if available) and pregnancy outcomes in cases where the initial NIPT results were inconclusive. For samples with an initial indication of low FF, we compared these with a control group to explore differences between obtaining interpretable and noninterpretable results upon secondary testing. Additionally, we analyzed the impact of different blood collection tubes on the incidence of low FF. The goal of this study is to provide insights for better managing pregnant women who do not achieve effective initial NIPT results and to offer a foundation for genetic counseling.

Materials and methods

Study population

This was a retrospective study of 30,039 singleton pregnant women enrolled in NIPT at Women and Children's Hospital, School of Medicine, Xiamen University between May 2019 and April 2022. The exclusion criteria included multiple pregnancies, a gestational age less than 12 weeks, a maternal body mass index (BMI) of more than 40 kg/m2, one spouse having a definite chromosomal abnormality, allogeneic blood transfusion, transplantation, and allogeneic cell therapy within a year.

Sample acquisition, sequencing, and grouping

Five milliliters (mL) of peripheral blood was collected and stored in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid anticoagulant tubes (K-tube, Kangwei Biological Technology Co, Ltd, Jiangsu, China; G-tube, Boomingshing Biological Medical, Shenzhen, China). The blood samples were then centrifuged at 1600g for 10 minutes, followed by centrifugation at 16,000g for another 10 minutes, both at 2°C to 8°C. The plasma supernatant was collected in 2 mL nucleic acid–free centrifuge tubes. Samples for coagulation, severe hemolysis, and lipemia were considered unqualified and required the pregnant women to be resampled with 5 mL of peripheral blood and re-entered into the test from the two-step centrifugation method. The plasma supernatant was subjected to DNA extraction, library establishment, and PCR, among others, according to the instructions of the kit. Finally, the quality of the library concentration was tested, and samples whose concentration was below the instructions were regarded as unqualified and those samples with qualified library concentration were entered into the sequencing process. After sequencing, the quality control (QC) was conducted by instrument analysis. Unqualified samples would undergo a second test, and if still unqualified, they would proceed to the final stage of retesting and resampling.

There were two kinds of results for qualified samples, one with interpretable results and the other with uninterpretable results. The samples with uninterpretable results needed to enter the secondary test. The steps to enter the second test varied according to the reasons for failure. According to the system prompt, the reason for failure was divided into three cases, respectively, z-scores in the gray area, low FF, and GC bias and cffDNA <8 μmol/L. The z-scores in the gray area needed to be reconstructed, and if it still prompted the gray area, resampling and setting up reholes were required to enter the last test. For samples with low FF, pregnant women were required to resample after an interval of 1 to 2 weeks for secondary testing; when GC bias and cffDNA were <8 μmol/L, only the library needed to be reconstructed for the second test. The reagents used for DNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing were obtained from Beijing Genomics Institute (BGI)—Wuhan (Wuhan, China). The sequencing platform and analysis software were BGI sequencing-500 (BGISEQ-500) (Shenzhen Genomics Biomedical Engineering Co, Ltd, Shenzhen, China) and Halos-NIFTY (BGI-Shenzhen, Shenzhen, China), respectively. The BGISEQ-500 calculates the FFs for male fetuses based on the Y chromosome NIPT method13 and for female fetuses based on FF-QuantSC.14 All steps were performed following the operating specifications of BGI.

To explore the performance of NIPT for the samples whose z-scores fell in the gray area in the initial test, the data were divided into four groups, namely, the z-scores of T13, T18, and T21 in the gray area and data fluctuation, which refers to the z-scores of two or more chromosomes in the gray area or the z-scores in the gray area combined with other abnormalities, and tracked on a case-by-case basis with respect to each NIPT result, prenatal diagnostic outcome (if any), and pregnancy outcome. For the low-FF group, we first analyzed the differences between them and the control group. Second, we analyzed the differences between obtaining interpretable results (effective group) and obtaining uninterpretable results (ineffective group) in the secondary test. Finally, the difference in the incidence of low FF between the use of the K-type blood collection tubes and the G-type blood collection tubes was analyzed after repeatedly confirming that there were no changes in reagents, protocols, and inclusion criteria while blood collection tubes were the only variable. For the QC failure group, failed samples at the experimental process QC point were analyzed, and their NIPT results were followed up until the pregnancy outcome. The control group in this study consisted of 100 randomly selected pregnant women with low-risk initial test results.

Data collection

Basic clinical information, such as maternal age, height, weight, and gestational age, was obtained from the prenatal diagnosis application form. The library concentrations, FFs, and test results were obtained from Halos-NIFTY. The participants' medication use and pregnancy outcomes during NIPT testing were collected through medical record system queries combined with telephone data collection. Those participants who refused to be interviewed or were unreachable were considered lost to follow-up.

Validation of abnormal and inconclusive NIPT results

Genetic counseling was provided to patients with abnormal or inconclusive NIPT results, and then amniocentesis was carried out after obtaining their informed consent. Karyotype analysis and copy number variation sequencing (CNV-seq) were performed for validation according to routine standard operating procedures.

Statistical analysis

The median and interquartile range (IQR) were used to describe all continuous data. Parametric tests between the low-FF case group and control group as well as between the effective group and ineffective group in the secondary results of the “low FF” were pioneered by using the one-way analysis of variance. Mann-Whitney U test was used when the data did not satisfy the assumption of normal distribution or homoscedasticity. In the comparison of the prevalence of abnormalities in perinatal women and infants, z-scores in the gray area group, low-FF group, and QC failure groups were compared with the control group separately using the χ2 test. The χ2 test was used for comparison of fetal gender rates. All statistical analyses were run in SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY), and P < 0.05 was set as a statistically significant difference.

Ethical approval

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Women and Children's Hospital, School of Medicine, Xiamen University (KY-2023-026-K01), and all participants provided their informed consent.

Results

NIPT outcome classification and statistics

The procedures and outcomes for all samples are shown in Figure 1. A total of 30,039 initial samples, of which 22 (0.07%, 22/30,039) unqualified samples met the criteria after resampling, participated in the NIPT, together with another 30,017 (99.93%, 30,017/30,039) qualified samples. Thereafter, 29,972 (99.78%, 29,972/30,039) met the quality assessment metrics relative to cDNA library concentration, and 67 (0.22%, 67/30,039) were unqualified, all of which passed the metric after library reconstruction. The first test showed that 181 (0.60%, 181/30,039) samples were QC failures, and all qualified after library reconstruction, except for the multiple chromosomal anomalies (MCA) case. Of the 29,858 (99.4%, 298,58/30,039) samples that passed QC in the initial test, interpretable results were obtained in 29,228 (97.30%, 29,228/30,039), and uninterpretable results were obtained in 630 (2.10%, 630/30,039).

Of the 521 (82.70%, 521/630) cases with z-scores of T13/T18/T21 in the gray area, 130 (24.95%, 130/521) failed the second assay and were resampled, with the final retesting process set up. However, 5 (0.96%, 5/521) still failed to obtain results in the end. Sixty-two cases (9.84%, 62/630) were resampled for low FF failure, half of which obtained interpretable results after the second assay, whereas the remaining half were still judged as low FF and considered test failures. Forty-seven (7.46%, 47/630) cases had results indicative of GC bias and cffDNA <8%, and all obtained interpretable results by library reconstruction. In summary, the NIPT initial test failure rate and the final test failure rate were 2.7% (811/30,039) and 0.12% (37/30,039), respectively. The total success rate of repeat testing was 95.44% (774/811), specifically 50% (31/62) for low FF, 99.04% (516/521) for the z-scores of T13/T18/T21 in the gray area, and 100% (47/47) for GC bias and cffDNA <8%; 99.45% (180/181) for QC failed. This caused a 3.80% wastage rate because additional replicate tests were required for samples that failed to obtain interpretable results in the initial NIPT.

Classification and outcomes of the initial z-scores of T13/T18/T21 in the gray area

A z-score in the gray area is the most common reason for no results for the initial test, accounting for 82.70% (521/630). In this study, the z-score of T18 in the gray area was the most common, followed by T21, T13, and data fluctuation. In the second test (Fig. 2), 75.05% (391/521) of the cases obtained interpretable results, of which 3.32% (13/391) had abnormal NIPT results, including 7 cases with high risk of T13/T18/T21 and 6 cases with other abnormalities. The remaining 24.95% (130/521) with no results were asked to resample and participated in the last NIPT test, of which 3.08% (4/130) were detected as having a high risk of T13/T18/T21 and no other abnormalities were detected. Ultimately, a total of 22 cases were recommended for prenatal diagnosis, including 11 cases with high risk of T13/T18/T21, 6 cases with other abnormalities, and 5 cases with no results (Table 1). Among them, 3 chose other screening methods, 2 were lost to follow-up, and 2 withdrew from the study; only 15 cases received prenatal diagnosis without abnormal findings. Only 9 cases underwent CNV-seq, 33.33% (3/9) showed CNVs less than 300 kb, which were of unclear clinical significance, whereas the remaining 6 cases did not show any abnormalities (Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/MFM/A56). For these samples that hover in the gray area multiple times, even if the final test results are positive, the z-scores are generally between 3 and 5. The positive predictive value (PPV) of a z-score in this interval is low regardless of whether the initial test yields a gray area result or the initial test indicates a high-risk result (Supplementary Table 2, http://links.lww.com/MFM/A56). Therefore, this means that the initial inconclusive results because of the gray area do not necessarily mean an increased risk of aneuploidy.

Risk factors and outcomes of an initial low FF

Characteristics of an initial low FF

Sixty-two pregnant women who received no results because of low FFs after the first test were classified as the case group. A total of 100 pregnant women who obtained reports of low risk after the initial test were randomly selected as the control group. Comparisons of the characteristics of the two groups are presented in Table 2. Fetal fraction showed a positive correlation with maternal gestational age and a negative correlation with BMI, and it was uncorrelated with maternal age. Notably, the fetal male-to-female ratio in the case group was significantly different (P < 0.001) from that of the control group. In addition, the library concentration was generally higher in the case group.

Characteristics and outcomes of a low FF in the second test

After the initial test, the low-FF samples were recollected and retested. Maternal and fetal factors, as well as laboratory processes, were compared to determine the key to the success of repeated tests (Table 3). However, no differences were found between the two groups in terms of maternal age, gestational age at two times of sampling, or initial FF, but there were significant differences in BMI and time between draws. It is inconceivable that the FFs of the ineffective group increased slightly or even negatively after the second blood draw. The final NIPT results of all effective group members were low risk, so amniocentesis was not performed. The follow-up of the effective group revealed 1 case of intrauterine stillbirth (cause unknown), 2 cases lost to follow-up, and 28 cases of normal pregnancies with 22 male and 6 female infants. In the ineffective group, only 9 cases underwent amniocentesis, as shown in Supplementary Table 3 (http://links.lww.com/MFM/A56). Karyotype analysis showed that 1 case had a karyotype of 45, x [10]/46, xy [41] and 8 cases had normal karyotypes. The CNV-seq results showed 1 case with 2.32 Mb deletion of Yq11.223q11.23, 3 cases with no abnormality, and 4 cases where CNV of unclear clinical significance was evident. Follow-up revealed 1 case of miscarriage (gestational hypertension), and the rest were all normal pregnancies with 23 male and 5 female infants. However, in the second test, there was no significant difference in the fetal male-to-female ratio between the two groups (P = 0.739). None of the cases followed in both groups had taken any anticoagulant drugs, such as low-molecular-weight heparin, during blood drawing and redrawing.

The impact of types of blood collection tubes on the FF

The blood collection tube used before May 2020 was a K-tube group, followed by a G-tube group so the influence of these two types of tubes on the detection of FF was analyzed. The test failure rate of K-tube group was 0.038% (3/7812) in the initial test and remained unchanged in the second test after resampling, whereas it was 0.265% (59/22,227) in G-tube group (Fig. 3A) and 0.126% (28/22,227) after the second test (Fig. 3B). The test failure rates of the two groups differed significantly in both initial and secondary attempts (P < 0.001, P < 0.01). Therefore, as part of the experiment, the blood collection tube can also affect the detection of FF.

Characteristics and outcomes of QC failure

Owing to the nonstandard operation involved, 22 samples failed to meet the pretest QC, including 6 coagulated blood, 13 severe hemolysis, and 3 lipid blood samples that were rejected by the laboratory and requested to be resampled. For the extraction or amplification errors of the assay, 67 (0.22%, 67/30,039) samples had cDNA library concentrations that did not meet the in-test QC and were asked to rebuild the libraries. The cases that did not meet the posttest QC, namely, reference number, GC, unique reads, and MCA, were 36, 97, 47, and 1, respectively. After the second test, they all obtained low-risk results, except for MCA, which failed all four tests. This case was recommended for amniocentesis and complete physical examination; no abnormalities were found, except for gallstones, and no additional abnormalities were found at the 1-year follow-up. In addition, 47 pregnant women obtained NIPT results with GC bias and cffDNA <8%, which were considered unreliable results requiring library reconstruction for validation. A total of 1.06% (317/30,039) of the samples required resampling or library reconstruction because of QC failures, and 99.68% (316/317) were eventually able to meet QC requirements and obtain results, all of which reported a “low-risk.”

Pregnancy outcomes in cases of initial NIPT inconclusive results

The pregnancy outcomes of 811 initial NIPT nonresult cases were tracked through the medical record system and telephone follow-up and then compared with normal controls. We followed up by telephone and excluded cases with unclear descriptions, resulting in an effective follow-up rate of 67.82%. We grouped them according to the reason for initial failure. The number of follow-ups in each group is shown in Table 4 and Table 5. The differences in terms of perinatal complications of perinatal women and perinatal abnormal infants in the z-scores in the gray area group and the low-FF group were statistically significant compared to the normal controls. This shows that the z-scores in the gray area and low FF of the NIPT indicate an increased risk of pregnancy complications and abnormal conditions in perinatal infants. It is also interesting to note that the gender composition of the infants exhibits significant bias in both the z-scores in the gray area group and the low-FF group.

Discussion

The NIPT exhibits excellent sensitivity and specificity, but the test failure rate is not negligible. Compared with the 0.8% to 12.2% test failure outlined by Yaron,12 our study showed lower initial (2.7%) and final failure rates (0.12%). The success rate of repeated tests has been reported to be approximately 79%; however, our study showed a success rate of 95.44%, which is higher than previously reported.15 About 75.05% of the samples with the initial result of the z-score in the gray area could obtain interpretable results by library reconstruction, and 96.15% of the remaining samples could obtain interpretable results by resampling, with a final failure rate of 0.96%. It follows that a second run of NIPT is effective in reclassifying most of the gray area samples and providing reliable results, which helps to reduce the number of invasive tests or no calls.15 Notably, no significant abnormalities were found after prenatal diagnosis in the patients with NIPT-positive results, suggesting that the absence of interpretable results in the NIPT because of the gray area does not imply an increased risk of aneuploidy. However, these results differ from those of previous studies, perhaps because of different sequencing platforms and possible differences in technical approaches.16 The CNV-seq results of some NIPT false-positive samples detected CNV from other chromosomes; therefore, we speculate that the detection may be misclassified because of NIPT depth limitations. Confined placental mosaicism is also an important cause of false positives and may be related to low z-scores,17 but generally, the performance of PPV increases with the z-score.18 In addition, a large amount of data are needed to validate whether the final positive results of initial gray samples tested by multiple testing are all 3 ≤ z < 5, whether such reports add unnecessary prenatal diagnoses, and how they are handled clinically. However, at least the present study showed that repeating the test, although prolonging the waiting time of pregnant women, need not be too much of a concern.

The current consensus is that FF in the first trimester is too low to provide reliable and interpretable results; therefore, premature NIPT is not recommended for pregnant women. This study found that an eligible FF was strongly dependent on gestational age and BMI, which is consistent with the findings of Lu et al.19,20 However, although all samples were obtained at the recommended gestational age, a portion still received low FF results. Moreover, we found that the FF increased rapidly or even showed negative growth over a short period in several cases. The data for this study were obtained from the BGISEQ-500 platform, which calculates the FFs of male fetuses based on the Y-chromosome NIPT method and the FFs of female fetuses using FF-QuantSC. Although the Y chromosome–based method is the gold standard for detecting male FF, the fact that many of the long gestational weeks in our study had undetectable FFs and that 80% of the fetuses were males illustrates the need to find complementary methods for the low detection of male FFs by this method.21 Previous studies have suggested that aneuploidy factors11 and maternal drug exposure22 may be associated with low FFs. We excluded the effects of low-molecular heparin by following up with the patients and still found two cases of sex chromosome abnormalities, which also indicates the inaccuracy of the Y chromosome–based method estimates for Y chromosome–containing abnormalities. Therefore, the possibility of karyotype abnormalities in NIPT no-call cases should not be ignored when encountering cases that are inconclusive because of low FFs,3,23,24 especially sex chromosome aneuploidy, which have higher no-call rates than trisomy.8

Usually, the FF does not increase by more than 1/1000 per week before the 20th week of pregnancy,25 so a sample redraw after 2 weeks will not have a significant impact. Therefore, if no result is caused by a low FF, short-term redrawing is not recommended for pregnant women, but diagnostic testing is.26 Therefore, the three cases in K-tube group with low FFs in this study could not achieve an eligible concentration by short-term redrawing. Nevertheless, the situation in G-tube group was different. We speculate that some specific components of the G-tube interfere with FF detection or are less effective in preserving fetal DNA. The reasons for the failure of FF detection have not been fully elucidated. The change of blood collection tubes in this study resulted in a significant increase in samples with low FFs, suggesting that the effect of blood collection tubes on the detection of FF should not be overlooked. A previous study suggested that resampling is not recommended because it delays diagnostic testing and may compromise the reproductive options of the pregnant woman.27 However, resampling must be considered not only from a biological perspective but also in terms of different platforms and technical factors. In our study, the success rate of repeated NIPT in low-FF cases was 50%, similar to that in previous studies,14,28 confirming the value of repeat testing.

Quality control failures can be introduced into laboratory processing and sequencing processes. Pre-experimental QC should be noted; for example, lipid blood should be rejected because high triglyceride levels are positively correlated with leukocyte count, increasing maternal DNA background and thus decreasing fetal DNA concentration.29,30 The QC indicators in the experiments, such as unique reads, indicated low effective data volume, probably because of insufficient spiked samples. The most common problem that leads to QC failures is related to GC content, such as GC bias, GC isolation, and reference numbers (severe GC isolation). These can be resolved with resampling and library reconstruction, and the results are essentially low risk. This is no problem with the sample itself and thus does not predict an increased risk of aneuploidy. Grömminger et al.31 reported that the injection of low-molecular-weight heparin increased the proportion of small DNA containing higher GC in plasma. Therefore, the reoccurrence of this problem after repeating the test also required consideration of the factors involved before the experiment. The only patient with MCA in this study was diagnosed with gallbladder stones, but MCA is still of high concern because of the high proportion of malignancy diagnoses when multiple chromosomal aberrations are detected by NIPT.32 The basic version of NIPT was used in this study, and the detection range was mainly for chromosome 13, 18, and 21 aneuploidies. Although mentioned previously, the gray area does not imply an increased risk of aneuploidy within the NIPT detection range, nor does it mean that the fetus is not at risk of any chromosomal abnormality. We also found fetal malformations, such as polydactyly and congenital heart disease, during the follow-up. As in previous studies,19,33 there was an increase in pregnancy complications and adverse pregnancy outcomes in cases with inconclusive NIPT, so attention should be given to maternal and fetal abnormalities in these cases.

Although this study identified and summarized the associated factors and outcomes of initial NIPT nonresult samples, there were several limitations. There is much discussion of NIPT failure samples in current studies, but the results cannot be generalized to all laboratories because of differences in testing platforms, inclusion populations, and interpretation of the results. The present study was a cohort of 30,039 cases from a single tertiary center using the BGI 500 platform, which is one of the mainstream instruments for NIPT testing, especially in China, and may serve only as a reference for this platform. Next, we did not seek confirmation of whether the false positives in the gray samples that ultimately yielded a high-risk report were related to confined placental mosaicism; adding this may be more complete. Finally, as the current study was retrospective, the medication use of patients with low FFs during the testing period was obtained from clinical charts and follow-up visits, and memory bias and omissions may have occurred. In recent years, studies have been conducted to address the problems in NIPT and expand its application. Budis et al.34 proposed a supplementary algorithm for z-scores to reduce the number of gray samples, Bo et al.35 developed a magnetic nanoplatform for fetal DNA fragment enrichment, and Li et al.36 summarized the MTOP5Zscores rule for identifying patients with maternal tumors. More sophisticated features of NIPT are being developed. Clinicians focusing on issues that affect test failure rates, avoiding pretest disadvantages, and providing optimal posttest counseling and clinical management also suggest that NIPT will work better.

Conclusion

Unreportable results were categorized and analyzed to provide accurate information to pregnant women and reduce anxiety during pregnancy. Initial inconclusive results because of the gray area did not imply an increased risk of aneuploidy, especially because the PPV of such results was very low when the gray area was measured multiple times, but the final z-scores were between 3 and 5. The initial inconclusive results because of low FFs may be related to assay methodology, which can involve fetal sex-bias, and to the effects of blood collection tubes. In addition, a low FF needs to be alerted of the risk of aneuploidy. Finally, the gray area and a low FF, both of which result in inconclusive NIPT, require vigilance for an increased risk of pregnancy complications and adverse pregnancy outcomes. This study is from a single tertiary center, and the management and optimization of NIPT failures need to be driven by a consensus of studies from multiple independent centers.
