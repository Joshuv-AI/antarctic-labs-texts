Relapse of HIV-associated multicentric Castleman disease temporally associated with acute coronavirus disease 2019: a case report
Authors: Hiroki Yamamoto, Kazuaki Fukushima, Yota Aizawa, Kohei Ukai, Shota Kodaira, Yukari Nishikawa, Shuhei Tada, Seowoong Jung, Masaru Tanaka, Taiichiro Kobayashi, Ryoko Sekiya, Keishiro Yajima, Atsushi Ajisawa, Ryu Gomikawa, Tsunekazu Hishima, Akifumi Imamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHuman immunodeficiency virus-associated multicentric Castleman disease (HIV-MCD), a human herpesvirus 8 (HHV-8)-driven lymphoproliferative disorder affecting individuals infected by HIV, typically exhibits a relapsing-remitting course and can be fatal. The incidence of HIV-MCD is on the rise while that of Kaposi’s sarcoma, another HHV-8-associated disease, has been declining. Although an increasing number of studies have reported relapses of human herpesvirus-associated diseases and HIV-negative MCD following coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), to the best of our knowledge there are no reports of HIV-MCD relapses.Case presentationA 75-year-old male patient with well-controlled HIV infection and pre-existing HIV-MCD experienced a relapse of HIV-MCD temporally associated with infection by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). He had a two-month history of persistent cough and received the diagnosis of mild COVID-19 based on positivity for SARS-CoV-2 on a polymerase chain reaction test and characteristic findings on computed tomography. Although the COVID-19 resolved with remdesivir therapy, the patient subsequently experienced a fever, generalized lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, and thrombocytopenia. A lymph node biopsy confirmed a MCD relapse. The patient improved markedly after rituximab therapy.ConclusionThis case is the first to report a temporal association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and HIV-MCD relapse. While a direct causal relationship cannot be established, the clinical course is consistent with the hypothesis that SARS-CoV-2 infection may have contributed to HHV-8 reactivation and MCD relapse. Further case series and mechanistic studies are needed to evaluate this potential association.

Background

Castleman disease, a lymphoproliferative disorder, is categorized as either unicentric Castleman disease (UCD) or multicentric Castleman disease (MCD). The latter is further classified into three clinicopathological entities: POEMS-associated MCD, idiopathic MCD, and human herpesvirus-8 (HHV-8)-associated MCD [1].

Human immunodeficiency virus-associated MCD (HIV-MCD) is a rare but serious complication in people living with HIV (PLWH), in whom HHV-8 plays a pivotal, pathogenic role [2]. HHV-8-infected plasmablasts within affected lymph nodes expand polyclonally and promote a cytokine storm dominated by interleukin-6 (IL-6) and other proinflammatory mediators, which comprise the central mechanism driving the disease activity [3]. Historically, HIV-MCD carried a dismal prognosis, with the median overall survival being only 12–14 months [4, 5]. However, the introduction of rituximab (RTX) has transformed the outcomes, improving 5-year survival from roughly 33% to > 90% [6].

Until June 2025, there have been 750 million cases of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) resulting in more than seven million deaths worldwide [7, 8]. Accumulating evidence indicates that COVID-19 can influence the course of other conditions, including malignancies and chronic kidney disease [9].

Similar concerns apply to human herpesvirus (HHV) infections and MCD. Recent studies have suggested that SARS-CoV-2, the causative pathogen in COVID-19, may trigger reactivation of latent HHVs, including HHV-8, and several studies have reported disease relapses occurring in HIV-negative patients with MCD following COVID-19. SARS-CoV-2 infection has been linked to immune dysregulation that may predispose immunocompromised patients to opportunistic viral reactivation; however, no prior reports have described HIV-MCD relapse occurring after COVID-19.

Case report

A 75-year-old male patient with a 20-year history of HIV infection presented with a persistent cough of two months’ duration and an unintentional weight loss of 10 kg over the preceding month. He initially sought care at another hospital, where chest computed tomography (CT) revealed findings consistent with pneumonia, and oral amoxicillin/clavulanate was prescribed. As further evaluation was deemed necessary, he was referred to our hospital, where he regularly received care, five days later. At our hospital, SARS-CoV-2 was detected in a nasopharyngeal swab specimen by a polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test with cycle threshold [Ct] 34.5 for the N gene and 31.7 for the E gene. At that time, molnupiravir was prescribed in the outpatient setting. These high Ct values likely indicate low viral load, consistent with a resolving or late-phase infection given the prolonged symptom history prior to presentation to our hospital. The patient’s HIV was well-controlled with lamivudine/abacavir plus dolutegravir; the viral load was undetectable, and the CD4-positive T-lymphocyte count was 295/μL. Ten years earlier, an analysis of a cervical lymph node biopsy specimen had led to the diagnosis of HIV-MCD. Complete remission was achieved with rituximab (RTX) therapy. Relapses that had occurred five and two years prior to the current presentation had both responded to additional RTX therapy.

On admission, vital signs were as follows: temperature 36.6 °C, blood pressure 103/62 mmHg, heart rate 72 bpm, respiratory rate 12 breaths/min, and SpO2 96% on room air. A physical examination found no abnormal breath sounds, cervical lymphadenopathy or cutaneous lesions indicative of Kaposi’s sarcoma (KS). The initial laboratory studies demonstrated mild anemia (hemoglobin: 90 g/L; reference range, 137–168 g/L) and slightly elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) at 2.8 mg/L (reference range, 0.0–1.4 mg/L). The leukocyte and platelet counts were normal. Electrolytes, including sodium, fell within the reference range, but serum albumin was somewhat low (31 g/L; reference range, 41–51 g/L). In contrast, hypergammaglobulinemia, elevated ferritin, and increased soluble interleukin-2 receptors (sIL-2 R) suggested systemic inflammation (Table 1). A course of remdesivir (RDV), with a 200 mg loading dose on day 1 followed by 100 mg/day, and empirical ceftriaxone 1 g once daily was begun on day 1. Remdesivir was administered in accordance with Japanese national guidelines at the time, which recommended treatment for high-risk patients (age ≧65 years, immunocompromised) regardless of oxygen requirement. Although more than 7 days had elapsed since symptom onset, remdesivir was used because prolonged symptoms attributable to COVID-19 were suspected and the patient was considered to have severe immunocompromise. RDV was discontinued on day 5 following clinical improvement. During this treatment, the patient remained afebrile, and his cough improved. Another PCR test on day 6 was negative for SARS-CoV-2. Ceftriaxone administration was halted on day 7, but a fever of 38.2 °C developed, delaying his discharge.

CT performed on day 8 demonstrated resolution of the pulmonary infiltrates but found newly enlarged cervical, supraclavicular, mediastinal, and inguinal lymph nodes and splenomegaly (Fig. 2). An HIV-MCD relapse was suspected, prompting a cervical lymph node biopsy on day 9. By day 12, laboratory findings demonstrated persistent anemia and worsening thrombocytopenia (platelets: 41 × 109/L; reference range, 158–348 × 109/L), markedly elevated CRP (108.3 mg/L), hyponatremia (Na: 130 mmol/L; reference range, 138–145 mmol/L), and hypoalbuminemia (albumin: 24 g/L) (Table 2). Based on these findings, the deterioration of the patient’s health was attributed to an HIV-MCD relapse rather than a COVID-19 recurrence, and rituximab 375 mg/m2 (total dose 560 mg) was administered once weekly for four weeks beginning on day 13. Fluconazole 200 mg/day was also begun for suspected oral candidiasis. A PCR test for SARS-CoV-2 on day 15 was negative. On day 20, black hairy tongue accompanied by pain developed. Although oral candidiasis was initially suspected and treated with fluconazole, the symptoms progressed and did not respond to antifungal therapy; therefore, a clinical diagnosis of herpes simplex virus (HSV) stomatitis was made, and valacyclovir 2,000 mg/day was initiated. Packed red blood cells and platelet concentrates were transfused as needed. The hematological indices and inflammatory markers gradually improved, and the clinical signs, including fever and lymphadenopathy, resolved. The patient was discharged home on day 36.

Histopathology of the cervical lymph node revealed follicular hyperplasia with an atrophic germinal center and increased immunoblasts or plasmablasts in the mantle zone, as well as marked vascular proliferation in the interfollicular areas. Immunohistochemical findings were positive for HHV-8 latency-associated nuclear antigen 1 (LANA1) (Fig. 3). Discussion and conclusion

Of the HHV-8-associated diseases in PLWH, KS, an AIDS-defining illness, is the most common whereas MCD is still relatively rare. While the incidence of KS began declining with the spread of antiretroviral therapy (ART), studies are now reporting a rise in the MCD incidence. For example, one study reported an incidence of 2.3 per 10,000 person-years in the pre-ART era (1983–1986), which increased to 2.8 per 10,000 in the early ART era (1997–2001) and to 8.3 per 10,000 in the late ART era (2002–2007) [10].

HHV-8 is implicated not only in KS and MCD but also in certain lymphomas. These conditions can overlap or occur sequentially during the clinical course. Although MCD is benign, it may act as a precursor to lymphoma, especially in PLWH with long-term ART or other forms of suppressive antiviral therapy [11]. HHV-8-associated malignancies include primary effusion lymphoma (PEL), HHV-8-positive diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, not otherwise specified (HHV-8+ DLBCL, NOS), and HHV-8–positive germinotropic lymphoproliferative disorder (HHV-8+ germinotropic LPD) [12, 13]. The incidence of non-Hodgkin lymphoma is estimated to be 15 times higher in patients with HIV-MCD than in PLWH without MCD [14].

Patients with MCD are generally older than those with UCD, with the median age at onset in the former being the fifth to sixth decade of life [15]. Although some studies have denied any sex difference [15], a Japan-wide survey of 342 MCD patients receiving tocilizumab reported a male-to-female ratio of approximately 6:4 [16]. In PLWH, risk factors of HHV-8-associated MCD include a nadir CD4-positive T-lymphocyte count > 200/μL, age > 33 years, no previous ART exposure, and non-Caucasian ethnicity [10].

HIV-MCD typically follows a relapsing-remitting course presenting severe, nonspecific, inflammatory symptoms [17]. Without treatment, it can lead to fatal, multiorgan failure, infection or progression to lymphoma [18]. Common symptoms include fever, lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, respiratory symptoms, and edema [4]. Laboratory findings during the active disease phase often reveal cytopenia, hyponatremia, hypoalbuminemia, elevated CRP, and HHV-8 viremia [17]. Some patients also exhibit hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH)-like features, such as hyperferritinemia and pancytopenia. Serum or plasma IL-6 and IL-10 typically increase during active inflammation and normalize after treatment [19, 20]. The HHV-8 viral load also increases during relapses and decreases during remission.

There is no consensus on the diagnostic criteria for HIV-MCD. However, the ANRS 117 CastlemaB trial in France proposed criteria that are widely used today to define disease flares [21]. A definitive diagnosis requires histopathological findings of characteristic features, such as plasmablasts in the mantle zone of lymphoid follicles staining positive for the HHV-8 LANA1 [22].

Our patient had a fever and elevated CRP, but the only clear, MCD-related symptoms were superficial lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly. Oral lesions were observed, but xerostomia was absent. The diagnosis of HIV-MCD relapse was established based on: (1) histopathological findings characteristic of HIV-MCD (plasmablast infiltration in the mantle zone and LANA-1 positivity); (2) markedly elevated serum IL-6 (48.4 pg/mL); (3) high HHV-8 viral load (1.7 × 106 copies/106 cells); and (4) clinical response to rituximab. Although the patient did not fully meet all ANRS 117 CastlemaB major criteria for a ‘flare’ (only two MCD-related symptoms were observed), the combination of histological, virological, and biochemical evidence was considered sufficient for diagnosis.

Serum IL-6 was measured on day 12 at the time of MCD relapse suspicion with a level of 48.4 pg/mL. This measurement was obtained 13 days after the initial COVID-19 diagnosis, when SARS-CoV-2 PCR had already become negative, reducing the likelihood that this elevation was attributable to acute COVID-19-associated cytokine release.

The elevated HHV-8 viral load observed at the time of relapse is also consistent with HHV-8 reactivation. However, in the absence of pre-COVID-19 baseline HHV-8 viral load data, a causal relationship between SARS-CoV-2 infection and HHV-8 reactivation cannot be established. SARS-CoV-2-induced HHV-8 reactivation is therefore presented here explicitly as a hypothesis.

Several alternative explanations for the relapse must be acknowledged. HIV-MCD has a well-recognized relapsing-remitting natural history, and this patient had experienced two prior relapses (approximately 5 and 2 years before the current episode), indicating an intrinsic propensity for recurrence independent of external triggers. Advanced age (75 years) may also have contributed through reduced immune surveillance. In addition, fever and cytopenia in HIV-infected individuals have a broad differential diagnosis. Bacterial superinfection, which can present with systemic inflammation, was considered but was not supported by microbiological investigations, including sputum culture, acid-fast bacilli testing, and multiplex PCR. Drug-related adverse events were also unlikely given the temporal pattern and the presence of progressive lymphadenopathy. Furthermore, immune dysregulation independent of SARS-CoV-2 infection, including non-specific inflammatory stimuli, could theoretically have contributed to HHV-8 reactivation. Taken together, although these alternative explanations cannot be completely excluded, the combination of histopathological, virological, and clinical findings supports the diagnosis of HIV-MCD relapse in this case.

We emphasize that the available clinical data support just a temporal association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and HIV-MCD relapse, rather than a causal relationship. On the other hand, several reports have described HHV reactivation following COVID-19. Le Balc’h et al. reported HSV-1 and cytomegalovirus (CMV) reactivations in 36 patients with severe COVID-19, which indicated a higher reactivation rate than reported in previous cohorts of critically ill patients [23]. Liu et al. also found disease progression in four of 25 HIV-negative patients with MCD following SARS-CoV-2 infection. This inflammatory response is characterized by a cytokine storm involving interleukin-1β, interleukin-2, interleukin-6, interleukin-8, interferon-γ, and tumor necrosis factor-α, many of which overlap with cytokines implicated in the pathogenesis of multicentric Castleman disease. The authors reported that clinical deterioration may occur even after apparent recovery from COVID-19, suggesting that post-infectious immune activation may contribute to disease exacerbation [24]. Similarly, Orta et al. reported that HLH, another hyperinflammatory syndrome, may develop in the context of viral infections in patients with HIV-associated Kaposi sarcoma, highlighting that virus-induced immune dysregulation and cytokine activation can drive disease exacerbation across HHV-8–associated conditions [25]. To the best of our knowledge, however, no previous reports have described a temporal association between COVID-19 and HIV-MCD relapse.

Nearly all HIV-MCD cases are HHV-8 positive, compared with only 40–50% of HIV-negative MCD cases [26]. Like other HHVs, HHV-8 alternates between the latent and lytic phase [27], and MCD is distinguished by robust lytic replication and marked expression of viral lytic antigens [28]. Viral homolog of interleukin-6 (vIL-6), which shares 25% sequence homology with human IL-6, drives the pathogenesis by activating the Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) pathway by binding to IL-6 receptors and promoting vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) production [29]. vIL-6 can also activate the IL-6 signaling pathway independently through direct binding to gp130 without requiring the IL-6 receptor alpha chain. This property may allow vIL-6 to amplify cytokine-mediated pathology in a manner distinct from human IL-6.

Evidence from South Africa, where HHV-8 seroprevalence exceeds 50%, demonstrated that SARS-CoV-2 seropositivity was associated with a sharp rise in HHV-8 viremia from 0 to 9.4% to 69.2% in non-hospitalized, HIV-infected patients with a CD4-positive T-lymphocyte count < 350/μL [30]. A high SARS-CoV-2 antibody titer was found in patients with detectable HHV-8 viremia and no history of COVID-19 vaccination, which suggested viral reactivation. A previous case report also described a KS recurrence following hospitalization for COVID-19 in a female patient with a history of KS. Electron microscopy of a tissue sample from the patient revealed colocalization of SARS-CoV-2 and HHV-8 in the same cells [31]. SARS-CoV-2 spikes and nucleocapsid proteins have been shown to promote HHV-8 lytic reactivation, and KS tissue overexpresses angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2), the entry receptor for SARS-CoV-2 [32]. In addition to these direct effects, SARS-CoV-2 infection is characterized by systemic inflammation and immune dysregulation, which may further facilitate herpesvirus reactivation. Proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 can activate signaling pathways including STAT3 and NF-κB, both of which are implicated in the regulation of HHV-8 latency and reactivation. Furthermore, disruption of host immune surveillance, including impaired interferon responses and lymphocyte dysfunction, may weaken the control of latent infection. At the molecular level, these inflammatory and immune signals can influence the balance between LANA–mediated latency and replication and transcription activator (RTA)–driven lytic activation, thereby promoting viral reactivation [27].

Several limitations of this report warrant acknowledgment. First, the patient’s COVID-19 vaccination history could not be reliably ascertained from the available medical records; whether prior vaccination may have modified immune responses relevant to HHV-8 reactivation therefore remains unknown. Second, SARS-CoV-2 was not evaluated within the lymph node lesions by PCR or immunohistochemistry, which limits interpretation of the potential direct role of the virus in triggering MCD relapse. These limitations highlight the need for further studies with careful virological and immunological evaluation.

This case is the first to report a temporal association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and HIV-MCD relapse. While a direct causal relationship cannot be established, the clinical course is compatible with the hypothesis that SARS-CoV-2 infection may have contributed to HHV-8 reactivation and MCD relapse. Alternative explanations, including the natural relapsing-remitting course of HIV-MCD and patient-specific factors, must be considered. Further case series and mechanistic studies are needed to evaluate this potential association.
