ESC guidelines 2025 on cardiovascular disease and pregnancy: considerations and future perspectives
Authors: Eloisa Arbustini, Fausta Beneventi, Antonio Bozzani, Barbara Cattadori, Maria Paola Delmonte, Alessandro Di Toro, Lorenzo Giuliani, Maurizia Grasso, Carlo Pellegrini, Francesca Perotti, Roberto Rordorf, Alexandra Smirnova, Mario Urtis, Alessandra Serio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPregnancy in women with cardiovascular disease (CVD) represents a distinct clinical condition, as the physiological adaptations of pregnancy may interact with underlying cardiac pathology in variable and sometimes unpredictable ways. Consequently, maternal and foetal risk cannot be assumed a priori and requires structured, patient- and disease-specific assessment. The new ESC guidelines on cardiovascular disease and pregnancy incorporate updated evidence following the 2018 version. The contents span from models of multidisciplinary care (the Pregnancy Heart Team, PHT), pre-pregnancy counselling, and pregnancy-related risk assessment, to novel recommendations that are both shared by all pregnancies in women with CVD and specific to each distinct disease. Due to limited prospective or randomized studies largely prevented by ethical reasons, most recommendations are based on evidence level C. This document provides a concise synthesis of innovations in shared management strategies and disease-specific pathways in pregnancy of women with known CVD. It also acknowledges that not all clinical scenarios can be fully captured by guideline-based recommendations. Beyond guidelines, and primarily because approximately 50% of recommendations are Level C evidence, there is considerable decision-making margin for cardiovascular specialists to adhere recommendations. For rare unpredictable complications/events the limited available evidence does not allow recommendations to be made. Looking to the future, the expectation is that AI, which is already generating data and models of care in pregnancy, can help support widespread access to PHT, both in hospitals equipped with the necessary requirements and remotely to all healthcare facilities serving pregnant women with CVDs.Keywords: Pregnancy, Cardiovascular Disease, Guidelines, Risk Assessment, mWHO 2 0

Introduction

The 2025 ESC Guidelines on pregnancy and cardiovascular disease (CVD), endorsed by the European Society of Gynecology (ESG), replace the 2018 edition and integrate the substantial evidence generated over the past seven years, including data from international disease registries.1-3 These guidelines are presented as a single, comprehensive document addressing pregnancy across the full spectrum of CVDs, reflecting three key considerations. First, organizational pathways, management models, and therapeutic indications and contraindications are largely shared across different CVDs during pregnancy. Second, maternal cardiovascular risk stratification has evolved and is now anchored to the modified World Health Organization 2.0 (mWHO 2.0) classification, incorporating evidence from the CARPREG II study published in 20183 (Figure 1). Third, despite the heterogeneity of cardiovascular conditions and the need for disease-specific recommendations, optimal care consistently depends on multidisciplinary expertise. This has led to the formalization of expert teams, conceptualized in the new guidelines as the Pregnancy Heart Team (PHT).

Accordingly, the guidelines provide both shared recommendations applicable across multiple CVDs and disease-specific guidance aligned with existing ESC documents for individual conditions, including cardiomyopathies,4 heart failure,5 arrhythmias,6,7 genetic and non-genetic aortopathies and arteriopathies,8 pulmonary arterial hypertension,9,10 pulmonary embolism,11 congenital heart disease,12 valvular heart disease,13 ischaemic heart disease,14,15 hypertension,16 cardio-oncology,17 and long-term cardiovascular consequences of adverse pregnancy outcomes.1 Overall, the document introduces 33 new and 10 revised recommendations, some upgraded or downgraded in class or level of evidence. Given the ethical and practical challenges of conducting randomized trials in pregnant populations, only one of the 207 recommendations reaches Class IA, and only 16 are Class IB. The majority are based on expert consensus (Level C evidence), and only nine recommendations are classified as Class III (not recommended) (Table 1).

This review summarizes the main innovations in shared management strategies and disease-specific pathways, while acknowledging that guideline-based recommendations cannot encompass all clinical scenarios.

The right to maternity

Reproductive choices of women with CVD must be fully respected, provided that decisions are informed and autonomously made. Maternity is a fundamental human right recognized in European law, notably in Article 33 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union and reinforced by EU Directive 92/85/EEC, building on the principles of the European Social Charter. These frameworks protect women from discrimination, promote maternal health throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period, and support the reconciliation of family and professional life. Once reproductive decisions are acknowledged as conscious and autonomous, cardiovascular specialists within the PHT have the responsibility to accompany and protect women with CVD throughout their reproductive journey. This includes providing comprehensive and comprehensible information, verifying patient understanding, and delivering individualized, committed care at every stage.

While certain severe conditions may contraindicate pregnancy, many women with CVD can achieve successful outcomes with appropriate planning and monitoring. A paradigmatic example is the series by van den Bersselaar et al., reporting 121 pregnancies in 43 women with vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome (vEDS) without pregnancy-related deaths, arterial dissections, or uterine ruptures.18 These data support the upgrade of recommendations regarding pregnancy in vascular EDS from Class IIIC (2018) to Class IC in the new guidelines (Table 5, Section E, ref. 1). Similarly, the persistence of many autosomal dominant CVDs across generations demonstrates that numerous pregnancies in affected women, often unaware of their diagnosis, have been uneventful.

Common recommendations for all pregnancies, irrespective of the specific CVD

a) The Pregnancy Heart Team (PHT)

In the 2025 guidelines, the PHT—anticipated in the 2018 document—assumes a clearly defined and structured role. The PHT is responsible for cardiovascular risk assessment and management using the updated mWHO 2.0 classification, incorporating CARPREG II evidence.2 Its guiding principle is comprehensive care across the entire reproductive continuum, from preconception counselling to postpartum follow-up, in women with both acquired and genetic CVD. The guidelines introduce two Class I, Level C recommendations mandating PHT involvement for women in mWHO 2.0 high-risk categories (II–III, III, and IV).

The guidelines strongly recommend a formal multidisciplinary PHT, including expertise in cardiovascular medicine (medical, interventional, and surgical), obstetrics and gynaecology, anaesthesiology, intensive care, cardiovascular imaging, pharmacology, and genetics).1 Syndromic or multisystem diseases require additional specialty input, as well as psychological and logistical support (Figure 1). Formal PHT establishment may be challenging in some healthcare settings. In practice, cardiologists often coordinate multidisciplinary pathways even in the absence of an institutionally defined team. This model has been applied for over two decades in our centre for inherited syndromic and non-syndromic cardiovascular diseases, and progressively expanded to disease-tailored multispecialty collaborations (Figure 2).

b) Imaging During Pregnancy

Ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are the safest and preferred imaging modalities during pregnancy. Ultrasound remains first-line for foetal assessment, while MRI is indicated when ultrasound is insufficient or cross-sectional imaging is required. Imaging involving ionizing radiation (X-ray, CT, PET/SPECT) should be avoided whenever possible and reserved for situations in which it is clinically indispensable. Guidance from international regulatory bodies confirms that diagnostic radiological procedures are generally safe when medically justified and performed using optimized protocols. These include careful risk–benefit assessment, dose minimization, selective shielding, and thorough patient counselling. The overarching principle is adherence to ALARA (‘As Low As Reasonably Achievable’), ideally maintaining foetal exposure below 50 mGy (Class IC).19 In life-threatening scenarios, such as suspected aortic dissection or pulmonary embolism, imaging must not be withheld, as timely diagnosis is critical for maternal and foetal survival.

c) What Women Should Know About Pregnancy and CVD

Structured, multidisciplinary counselling is central to care for all women with CVD considering pregnancy. Counselling should address gynaecological and obstetric history, cardiovascular diagnosis, pharmacological therapy, maternal and foetal risks, and the anticipated intensity of monitoring. Beyond risk communication, counselling must ensure genuine understanding to support autonomous decision-making. These principles are particularly relevant for genetic CVDs, where rapid advances necessitate precise phenotypic and genotypic characterization.1

d) What Women Should Know About Their Specific CVD

The guidelines provide disease-specific recommendations covering cardiomyopathies, heart failure, arrhythmias, prior peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM), aortopathies and arteriopathies, pulmonary hypertension, congenital heart disease, pulmonary embolism, valvular heart disease, coronary artery disease, hypertension, cardio-oncology, heart transplantation,20,21 and long-term cardiovascular risk after pregnancy. Although many recommendations already exist within disease-specific ESC guidelines,4-17 their integration into a single document facilitates comparison, harmonization, and clinical implementation. This approach improves understanding of indications and contraindications, timing, and modalities of medical therapy, interventional procedures, and cardiovascular surgery across different disease entities.

e) The Genetic Pathway in CVD and Pregnancy

Known genetic disease

Most genetic CVDs are autosomal dominant; when the mother is affected, counselling addresses both maternal and foetal risk. Management includes optimizing maternal safety, potentially through preventive interventions before pregnancy. Autosomal recessive and X-linked recessive conditions primarily raise foetal risk, as parents are usually asymptomatic carriers; females carriers of X-linked recessive defects may develop mild and late phenotypes. Vice versa, in the very rare X-linked dominant disorders, both maternal and reproductive risks are involved. In maternally inherited mitochondrial diseases counselling is complex due to variable phenotype expressivity (heteroplasmy), making both maternal and foetal risks difficult to predict.

Reproductive options include prenatal diagnosis (chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis, with foetal loss rates of 0.1–0.2%) and non-invasive prenatal testing for common aneuploidies (Trisomy 21, 18, 13).22 Preimplantation Genetic Diagnosis (PGD) is performed during in Vitro Fertilization to screen embryos before transfer. It carries risks related to assisted reproduction (e.g. multiple pregnancies, hormonal treatments); guidelines recommend single-embryo transfer for women with CVD.

Unrecognized genetic disease

Pregnancy can contribute to unmasking an existing genetic CVD that is currently asymptomatic and unrecognized. In such cases, cardiovascular specialists may be the first to suspect a genetic aetiology, often in the context of unexpected complications. This can be particularly difficult to suspect when complications known to be associated with particular genetic diseases occur in women with prior uneventful successful pregnancies, or in women with prior complicated pregnancies whose causes went undetected or under-investigated, and therefore unrecognized. In these cases, essential elements are missing to ensure that the pregnancy path fully aligns with clinical guideline recommendations. When a woman with undiagnosed genetic CVD develops pregnancy complications, management requires urgent PHT involvement (onsite or remote), comprehensive risk assessment, monitoring (ECGs, imaging, biomarkers), medication review, individualized planning, including early delivery if necessary, and referral to tertiary centres when feasible.

New disease- and condition-specific recommendations

Recommended (class/level of evidence)

In cardiomyopathies, vaginal delivery is generally preferred; caesarean section is reserved for obstetric indications, severe heart failure (EF <30% or NYHA III–IV), uncontrolled arrhythmias, severe left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO) (≥50 mmHg) in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), or vitamin K antagonist use shortly before or during labour (IC). Counselling is recommended regarding recurrence risk in DCM and EF deterioration during pregnancy (IC), and pre-pregnancy PHT counselling is advised in HCM, symptomatic LV dysfunction (EF <50%), or LVOTO ≥50 mmHg (IC). In PPCM, genetic counselling/testing is advised (IIaC), and HF therapy should continue for ≥12 months after complete LV recovery (IIaC). In cardiogenic shock guidelines recommend inotropes/vasopressors (levosimendan, dobutamine, milrinone) (IC). For heart transplantation, the recommendations include delayed pregnancy post-transplant (IC) and close immunosuppressant monitoring at scheduled intervals (IC).

For primary arrhythmias, beta-blockers (nadolol or propranolol) are recommended for LQTS during pregnancy and lactation (IB), with particular attention to LQT2 in the postpartum period (IB). Flecainide plus beta-blockers is recommended in CPVT with prior cardiac events (IC). In supraventricular arrhythmias, low-molecular-weight heparin is indicated in high-risk AF (IC), and flecainide may be added for rhythm control (IIaC); the use of direct oral anticoagulants is contraindicated in pregnancy. For patients with VT, beta-blockers should be used as first-line therapy. Conversely, the use of amiodarone is not recommended in pregnancy and should be limited to women with refractory or life-threatening arrhythmias.

Catheter ablation can be considered during pregnancy in cases of drug-resistant SVT and VT, but the procedure should be performed by experienced operators and in centres equipped with non-fluoroscopic mapping techniques (IIaC). During cardiac arrest, left uterine displacement (IC), supradiaphragmatic i.v. access (IC), and unrestricted drug use (IC) are recommended.

Aortopathies, congenital heart diseases, and pulmonary arterial hypertension require specialized pre-pregnancy or pregnancy counselling by a multidisciplinary or PHT team (IC recommendations), especially in high-risk conditions such as Fontan circulation or PAH.

For venous thromboembolism, immediate diagnostic evaluation is mandatory (IB). For native valve disease, surgery is recommended when there is high maternal mortality risk and other treatments have failed (IIaC). For prosthetic valves disease structured anticoagulation plans are recommended before or immediately upon pregnancy recognition for women with mechanical heart valves (IC).

For coronary artery disease, life-threatening causes must be excluded in pregnant women with chest pain (IC); dual antiplatelet therapy duration follows non-pregnant standards with individualized risk assessment IC), and statin continuation may be considered in established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (IIbC). Hypertension targets BP <140/90 mmHg (IB), with recommended i.v. or oral agents for severe hypertension (IC). Adverse pregnancy outcomes should be documented with long-term cardiovascular risk assessment and counselling (IB).

Finally, joint management by the PHT and cardio-oncology team is required for cancer patients receiving cardiotoxic therapy (IC).

Not recommended (class IIIC)

New guidelines do not recommend myosin inhibitors in HCM, prophylactic temporary pacing during delivery in asymptomatic congenital atrioventricular block, and use of ACE-Is, ARBs, ARNIs, MRAs, ivabradine, or SGLT2 inhibitors during pregnancy due to foetal risk.

Considerations beyond guidelines

Decision-making beyond recommendations

In personalized medicine, Class IIa and IIb recommendations may be difficult to apply, particularly in unplanned pregnancies, emergencies, or settings when the door-to-decision may be very narrow, and PHT may not be readily accessible. Tailoring decisions for individual patients requires knowledge, expertise, and skill, and adherence to recommendations can be conflicting. As emphasized in the Preamble of the document,1 guidelines do not override individual professional responsibility; thus, the role and responsibility of the specialist’s personal clinical judgment in each different circumstance is critical.

When evidence is insufficient to make recommendations

Rare but critical scenarios may require immediate decisions with limited evidence including acute complications such as amniotic fluid embolism,23,24 postpartum haemorrhage and acute obstetric coagulopathy,25 uterine and arterial rupture in undiagnosed vascular Ehlers Danlos,26 pneumothorax during pregnancy or labour,27 ineffective spinal analgesia for caesarean section in women with unknown dural ectasia, e.g. Marfan Syndrome and Loeys-Dietz Syndrome,28 spontaneous coronary dissections in previously healthy women,29 intracranial heamorrage.23,25 These clinical scenarios are rare but possible. Door-to-decision can be time-sensitive and may require rapid actions for which it may be necessary to balance the time for urgent multidisciplinary assessment when possible.23

Additional recommendations from novel ESC guidelines for the management of myocarditis and pericarditis

Attention to pregnancy was recently given by the new guidelines on myocarditis and pericarditis that incorporate six pregnancy-related recommendations: pre-conception counselling in recurrent pericarditis or myocarditis (class IC); nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs during pregnancy, anti-inflammatory therapy during lactation, and corticosteroids during pregnancy and breastfeeding in pericarditis (all class IIaC); colchicine and anakinra (both class IIbC) in pericarditis.30

Can AI support pregnancy heart teams?

AI models are increasingly applied to the analysis of clinical and instrumental data in pregnant women with CVD,31 with the potential to enhance risk stratification, support imaging interpretation, and refine prognostic assessment of maternal and foetal outcomes. Most existing AI tools operate in isolation, addressing single data sources (e.g. ECG, blood pressure, or foetal monitoring) or narrowly defined clinical scenarios. An illustrative example in maternal care is the AI-guided screening strategy using a digital stethoscope and 12-lead ECG to detect pregnancy-related left ventricular systolic dysfunction in Nigeria.32 To date, no unified AI system is capable of dynamically integrating heterogeneous clinical information with modified WHO (mWHO) 2.0 risk classes and translating this knowledge into individualized care pathways. Such a platform should adaptively guide the selection, timing, and frequency of diagnostic investigations throughout pregnancy, while balancing maternal cardiovascular risk, foetal safety, and delivery planning. In addition, an AI-assisted platform could provide real-time decision support by calculating risk according to CARPREG II criteria and assigning mWHO 2.0 risk classes. Current guidelines emphasize that ‘not every hospital needs to establish a dedicated pregnancy heart team (PHT), but each hospital should establish communication and collaboration with nearby expert teams, optimizing emergency and elective referral pathways within a shared-care model’.1 In this context, a unified AI platform capable of integrating multidimensional data, guiding investigations, and supporting remote decision-making could substantially strengthen and extend PHT functionality, particularly in settings lacking dedicated multidisciplinary teams.

Conclusions

While we are reassured by the support of these new ESC guidelines for all elective situations involving pregnancy in women with known CVD, many questions remain in rare but possible emergencies, in situations lacking strong evidence to support recommendations, and women with previously undiagnosed CVDs. In these contexts, multidisciplinary collaboration, specialist judgment, and emerging technological support remain essential to optimize maternal and foetal outcomes.
