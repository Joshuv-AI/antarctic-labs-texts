Microplastic contamination in drinking water sources and distribution networks of a Western City in Iran
Authors: Sara Namdarian, Bahram Kamarehie, Mohammad Amin Karami, Reza Fouladi-Fard, Abbas Norouzian Baghani, Faramarz Azimi, Fatemeh Piroozfar, Mostafa Rezaali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMicroplastic contamination has become a widespread environmental issue, prompting extensive research into its potential health and ecological impacts. This study investigates the occurrence, characteristics, and seasonal dynamics of microplastics in the drinking water supply system of Khorramabad, Iran, and evaluates associated exposure risks.MethodsWater samples were collected from 55 strategic locations—including wells, springs, and inlets/outlets of treatment plants—across the city’s distribution network during the wet (April, May) and dry (August, September) seasons of 2023. Microplastics were detected and quantified using density separation techniques, with µ-Raman spectroscopy employed to determine their polymer composition.ResultsThe results revealed an average microplastic concentration of 2.06 MPs/L, with white fibers ranging from 250 to 5000 μm being the most prevalent morphology. The dominant polymer types identified included polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP), and polystyrene (PS). Seasonal analysis revealed a statistically significant increase in microplastic abundance during the wet seasons (P < 0.001), indicating that rainfall is a primary factor driving groundwater contamination. Spatial variability across sampling points was not statistically significant (P = 0.25). Based on the risk assessment, the drinking water in Khorramabad was classified as low-risk. However, despite the minimal risk posed by these polymers, their potential health impacts should not be overlooked.ConclusionsThis study highlights the need for improved monitoring, optimized treatment processes, and public awareness campaigns to mitigate microplastic pollution in urban water systems. This study is notably the first to investigate microplastics in drinking water within the western region of the country.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28034-4.Keywords: Seasonal variation, Microplastic, Exposure risk, Water distribution network

Background

Plastic production has increased more than 200-fold over the past six decades, and projections exceeding 100 million tons annually by 2025 have turned plastic pollution into a global environmental crisis [1, 2]. Plastics’ durability and low density facilitate their persistence and widespread dispersal across ecosystems, including freshwater systems, oceans, soils, and even remote Arctic environments [3–9]. Microplastics are also transported to various ecosystems through water currents, streams, and atmospheric sediment [10]. Environmental factors degrade plastics into smaller particles, with those ranging from 1 to 5,000 μm in size being termed microplastics [11, 12]. Microplastics are classified into primary and secondary categories based on their origin. Primary microplastics are intentionally manufactured, occurring in products such as personal care items, industrial plastic pellets, and synthetic textile fibers. Conversely, secondary microplastics result from the fragmentation and degradation of larger plastic materials. This process typically occurs when larger plastics are exposed to environmental conditions such as air, ultraviolet radiation, and wind-induced abrasion [13, 14]. Microplastics exhibit a variety of forms; the most prevalent among them in the environment are fibers, granules, and fragments [15]. Regarding chemical composition, the prevalent microplastic types include polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP), polyvinyl chloride (PVC), polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polyoxymethylene (POM), polystyrene (PS), and polyamide (PA) [16] .

Microplastics pose extensive ecological and health risks in both freshwater and marine environments [13]. Current research indicates that microplastic particles may induce oxidative stress, cytotoxicity, metabolic alterations, immune system dysfunction, neurotoxicity, cellular mutations and even cancer in humans [16, 17]. Sedimentation of microplastics in aquatic and terrestrial soils enhances the likelihood of incidental ingestion by marine and land animals, facilitating entry into food webs [18]. Studies on marine organisms demonstrate that these particles are ingested and bioaccumulate in tissues, potentially disrupting systemic homeostasis and metabolic functions, which can result in organism mortality [19, 20]. Similarly, the transfer of soil-borne microplastics through the food chain can pose health risks to both animal and human populations [21]. Microplastics possess high adsorption capacity, which enables them to adsorb other environmental pollutants such as heavy metals, endocrine-disrupting chemicals, and persistent organic pollutants. These substances induce a range of adverse effects in living organisms, including congenital malformations, cognitive developmental disorders, reproductive complications, and cancer [14, 19, 22].

Microplastics can cause abrasion, internal injuries and bleeding, as well as intestinal obstruction. They can also serve as vectors for persistent organic pollutants and heavy metals, facilitating their bioaccumulation and enhancing toxicity [13, 23]. Upon ingestion, microplastics can directly engage with the intestinal epithelium and encounter the gut’s local immune system, potentially leading to inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). IBD, encompassing Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, affects over 6.8 million individuals globally, with projections indicating that cases in China will surpass 1.5 million by 2025. Beyond their inflammatory potential, certain microplastics, such as polystyrene, demonstrate significant cytotoxicity even at minimal concentrations. Specifically, polystyrene microplastics have been shown to increase apoptotic cell populations and diminish cellular viability. By dysregulating the expression of genes crucial for inflammation and oxidative stress, microplastics collectively represent a substantial risk to gastrointestinal health [17]. Microplastics can act as carriers for other toxic contaminants, including heavy metals and PAHs, facilitating their transfer into living tissues [24]. Heavy metals disrupt the aquatic oxygen balance, leading to cytogenetic abnormalities and developmental issues in fish [25]. PAHs, on the other hand, exert detrimental effects on human health and the environment due to their significant teratogenic, carcinogenic, mutagenic, and toxic properties. Consequently, the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has classified these compounds as high-priority pollutants [26–28].

The presence of microplastics of varying dimensions across surface water, groundwater, and wastewater bodies has generated apprehension regarding the potential contamination of potable water sources. Erkes-Medrano first identified microplastics in municipal tap water in 2019 [29]. Subsequently, Kosuth, Mason [30] and Mason, Welch [31] also reported the presence of microplastics in drinking water sources, with concentrations of 5.45 MP/L and 325 MP/L, respectively. Some of these studies garnered significant attention within the scientific community and the media, bringing the issue of human exposure to microplastics through drinking water to the forefront of public health agendas worldwide. To date, studies investigating microplastic contamination in drinking water remain limited. The World Health Organization and United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) have prioritised the provision of safe, clean and affordable drinking water as part of sustainable development, underscoring the need for further research [32, 33]. In a study conducted by Esfandiary et al. in Shiraz, one of Iran’s arid southern cities, the average microplastic concentration in water samples from ten wells was estimated at 0.48 MP/L [34]. Currently, no established baseline exists for microplastic levels within Iran’s tap water infrastructure. Given the limited research on microplastics in Iran, particularly concerning drinking water, and the absence of comprehensive data on microplastic concentrations in Khorramabad city’s water sources and distribution network, this study sought to determine microplastic levels in the city’s drinking water sources and distribution system across both wet and dry seasons, alongside evaluating the potential human exposure risk.

Methods

Study area

Khorramabad (33°28′06′′N, 48°20′25′′E; elevation: 1,147.8 m ASL), the capital of Lorestan Province, in the west of Iran. lies within a tectonically active karstic terrain characterized by Cretaceous limestone aquifers. The city’s water supply relies on 23 alluvial wells (depth: 35–120 m) and three perennial karstic springs (Gerdab Sangi, Golestan, and Motahari), which collectively yield ~ 12,000 m³/day [35–38]. Climatic seasonality a Mediterranean regime with mean annual precipitation of 480 mm creates distinct wet (April-May) and dry (August-September) hydrological phases [38].

Sampling points

In this study, a total of 44 samples were collected from various water sources, 62 from the distribution network, and four from the inlet and outlet points of the Khorramabad city treatment plant (Table 1) The water sources included three springs and nineteen wells, which constitute Khorramabad’s primary drinking water supply. To determine the sampling locations within the drinking water distribution network, Geographic Information System (GIS) software (version 10.5) was employed. A total of 31 sampling points were selected at approximately one-kilometer intervals across the city, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Sampling protocol

Samples were collected from various points using one-liter, metallic containers labeled with the date and location of sampling. Following collection, the samples were transported to the Laboratory of the School of Public Health [39]. Sampling was carried out during two distinct periods in 2023: in the spring (April and May) representing the wet season and in the summer and fall (August and September) representing the dry season. Typically, sampling was conducted weekly, with three to four samples collected per week. Prior to use, the sampling containers were rinsed twice with water sourced from the same location to eliminate potential interfering substances [30].

Microplastics extraction

First, 0.45 μm cellulose nitrate paper filters (47 mm in diameter, Whatman, England) were affixed to a Büchner funnel, which was then placed on a 1000 ml Erlenmeyer flask. The water samples were thoroughly filtered through the filters under vacuum. Following filtration, the filters were carefully transferred onto glass plates. To digest the organic matter in the samples, 5–10 ml of 35% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) was added to each glass plate. The plates were subsequently covered with aluminum foil and incubated at room temperature under a fume hood for 7 to 10 days. Afterwards, each sample was rinsed with 20 ml of distilled water to remove residual salt crystals, ensuring they did not remain on the filters. Subsequently, the filters were transferred onto clean glass plates and kept in a cupboard at room temperature for approximately one hour to allow them to dry. To isolate microplastics using the density separation method, the filters were carefully scraped with a metal spatula, and their contents were transferred into 50 ml Erlenmeyer flasks. Subsequently, 9 mL of zinc chloride (ZnCl₂) solution (density 1.8 g/cm³, Merck, Germany) was added to each flask. The flasks were then covered with aluminum foil and shaken for 10 min at 500 rpm using a laboratory shaker. After shaking, the flasks were left at room temperature for 24 h to facilitate the separation process. After 24 h, the contents of the Erlenmeyer flasks were transferred to Falcon tubes and subjected to centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 3 min. Subsequently, the supernatant was filtered under vacuum through 0.45 μm cellulose nitrate paper filters (47 mm in diameter, Whatman, England) The filters were then rinsed with 20 ml of distilled water to remove any residual salt crystals and ensure they did not remain on the filters. Following this, the filters were transferred to glass plates and stored in a cupboard at room temperature for approximately one hour to allow them to dry [10, 12, 40–42].

Detection and identification of microplastics

The number of microplastics on each filter was identified and quantified using a stereo microscope (40× magnification), based on their physical properties such as thickness, gloss, hardness, and response to a hot needle (a 250-micron stainless steel probe). Results were expressed in MP/L. Microplastics were further categorized by shape (fibers, flakes, and films), size (using a 230 μm metal probe), color (black, white, red, green, yellow, and blue), and length of the long axis (less than 100 μm; 100–250 μm; 250–500 μm; 500–1,000 μm; and 1,000–5,000 μm). It should be noted that the detection limit in this study was 50 μm. A subset, representing 9% of the most prevalent microplastics in terms of shape, color, and size, was analyzed for chemical structure using micro-Raman spectroscopy. Raman spectroscopy has garnered considerable attention owing to its capacity to integrate a multitude of characteristics. µ -Raman spectroscopy offers a level of precision and versatility that enables the detection of organic compounds as well as plastic materials. Additionally, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to examine the morphology of the microplastics [42–44].

Quality assurance and quality control

Reliability of extraction methods

To verify the method, type and accurately identify microplastics, recovery experiments were conducted using pristine water and polymer samples. Irregularly shaped blue polypropylene particles measuring 600 μm and black polypropylene particles measuring 300 μm were employed to mimic the microplastic content within the samples. Consequently, twenty microplastic particles of each size were introduced into two separate 400 mL clean water samples. Following thorough mixing, all subsequent procedures were performed on the samples from the outset, culminating in the calculation of the extraction efficiency for the blue polypropylene microplastics [42]. To ensure the reliability of the method for detecting and identifying microplastics, recovery experiments were conducted using clean water samples spiked with polymer particles. Specifically, 20 microplastics (300 and 600 μm in equal proportions) were added to two 400 mL clean water samples; following mixing, the full procedural steps were applied, and polypropylene microplastic extraction efficiency was ultimately calculated by examining dried filters. Secondary pollution control

To prevent secondary contamination from airborne microplastics, specific measures were implemented throughout all stages of sample preparation and microscopic analysis within the laboratory. These measures included wearing a laboratory gown, closing doors and windows, turning off the air conditioning system (during summer), conducting procedures under a laminar flow hood, Washing glassware three times with deionized water [45], cleaning surfaces with 70% ethanol, filtering solutions and distilled water through cellulose filter paper (47 mm, Whatman, England), utilizing glass and stainless steel tools and containers, thoroughly washing all containers and tools with distilled water, sealing container lids, and storing them in cabinets. Additionally, work tools were wrapped in aluminum foil for storage. Furthermore, laboratory traffic and movement were minimized during experimental procedures and the washing of containers [44, 46].

Human exposure to microplastics

Human exposure to microplastic particles via various pathways namely ingestion and inhalation may pose risks to human health. The pervasive use of plastics and their extensive presence in the environment have led to the proliferation of microplastics within the food chain, consequently increasing human exposure. In this study, the Estimated Daily Intake (EDI) of microplastics was calculated (Mp.kg/day) using Eq. (1) [47].

In this equation, C denotes the average surface area of microplastics (MP/g), IR represents water intake (g/day), and body weight (kg) (BW). Based on previous studies, a maximum daily water intake of 2 L for adults (assumed 70 kg average weight) and 1 L for children (assumed 30 kg average weight) was considered [48]. These values were utilized to calculate the EDI.

Statistical analysis

Following data collection on microplastic abundance, size, and type, descriptive statistical analysis was performed, including the calculation of means and standard deviations. Prior to inferential statistical testing, assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances across groups were assessed using appropriate tests, such as the F-test. Based on the normality of the data and the research objectives, suitable parametric tests (e.g., analysis of variance, ANOVA, to compare microplastic counts and sizes across different water sources) or non-parametric alternatives (e.g., Kruskal-Wallis test for examining differences among various water sources) were employed. When significant differences were identified, post hoc analyses such as Tukey’s test was applied for pairwise comparisons. To illustrate the distribution of microplastics across different water sources, box plots were generated. Additionally, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was employed to investigate the relationships between microplastic measurements and environmental variables, such as seasonal variations. All statistical analyses were conducted using Excel and R (Version 4.6.0). For spatial visualization and mapping, GIS Version 10.5 software was employed.

Microplastic abundance in distribution networks vs. treatment plants

Microplastic abundance was assessed at 31 points across the distribution network, and at both the inlet and outlet of the Khorramabad City treatment plant, during both wet and dry seasons. Results indicated a higher mean microplastic concentration within the treatment plant (2.46 MP/L) compared to the distribution network (1.46 MP/L), as presented in Fig. 4. Additionally, the majority of these particles both within the network and in the treatment plant were white fibers measuring approximately 1000 μm in length.

a: Abundance of microplastics in wet and dry seasons; b: Relationship between the abundance of microplastics in the distribution network and water treatment plants

A study by Ziajahromi, Neale [6], the concentration of microplastics in a water treatment plant was found to be 1.54 MP/L. Similarly, Magni, Binelli [49] reported a microplastic concentration of 2.5 MP/L in a water treatment plant. Conversely, a 2023 study by Yang, Xu [51] in China observed microplastic levels ranging from 1.74 to 20.88 MP/L within a water distribution network. The findings of these studies are consistent with those of the current research. It appears that the concentration of microplastics was higher within the treatment plant compared to the distribution network. In a study by Paredes et al. (2019) conducted in Riobamba, Ecuador, on microplastics in the city’s distribution system, only 12 out of 62 collected samples contained microplastics. Given the absence of standardized parameters for acceptable microplastic levels in drinking water, it can be inferred that the microplastic concentrations in the analyzed samples are relatively low. Consequently, the authors posited that the infrequent detection of microplastics in distribution networks could be attributed to water hardness, which facilitates the formation of a protective layer within polyvinyl chloride pipes, thereby mitigating their wear and tear [52]. Given that the water in Khorramabad city, according to study by Ahmadi Khalaji, Tahmasbi [53], is of the calcium to magnesium bicarbonate type (with temporary hardness), this explanation appears plausible for the city’s drinking water.

Morphological composition and sources of microplastics

The findings of this study indicated that the microplastics detected in the collected samples predominantly consisted of fibers, fragments, and films morphologies. Analysis revealed that fibers constituted the most prevalent form, accounting for the highest percentages observed across different sources. Specifically, fibers had an average occurrence of 2.45 MP/L in the wet season and 1.44 MP/L in the dry season when examining sources. Similarly, within the distribution network, fibers exhibited average counts of 3.77 MP/L during the wet season and 1.86 MP/L during the dry season. Fibers dominated the morphological composition (97% of particles), consistent with global studies reporting 69–98.3% fiber prevalence in drinking water [33, 34] (Fig. 3d). As illustrated in Table 1; Fig. 3d, fibrous microplastics constituted the predominant morphological form, accounting for 97% of identified particles. This aligns with global studies, including Kasuth et al. (2018), who reported 98.3% fiber prevalence in drinking water samples (n = 539 particles), and Shruti et al. (2020), where 69% of microplastics were fiber-shaped. The dominance of fibers in Khorramabad’s groundwater is can be related urban wastewater effluent discharge, atmospheric deposition, and routine domestic activities such as laundry and carpet cleaning [6, 42, 55]. Additionally, single-use plastics, including water bottles, carrier bags, straws, plastic sheets, and packaging, gradually degrade over time due to weathering and temperature fluctuations [56].

Fibrous microplastics infiltrate groundwater through multiple pathways: [1] vertical migration via soil pores and fissures, exacerbated by agricultural practices (e.g., mulching, fertilizer application); [2] surface runoff during flooding events; and [3] hyporheic exchange, where elevated river stages facilitate microplastic transport from surface water to aquifers [34]. Additionally, airborne fibers, driven by microdust proliferation and atmospheric deposition, represent a significant contamination route, particularly in urban areas [21, 42]. Urban environments are primary sources of synthetic fibers, while industrial regions predominantly release spherical and polyhedral particles [57].

The ubiquity of microplastics in freshwater systems documented in sediments, estuaries, and coastal zones [58] highlights their pervasive environmental distribution [4, 9]. Predominance of fibers underscores the critical need to address fiber pollution sources, particularly inadequate wastewater treatment processes and urban synthetic material usage, to mitigate groundwater contamination.

Size distribution of microplastics

The results of this study demonstrated a considerable presence of microplastics during both the wet and dry seasons, with concentrations reaching approximately 5 MP/L within the size range of 1500 μm. Specifically, during the wet season, the particle count was about 5 MP/L for particles approximately 1500 μm in size, while in the dry season, the concentration decreased to a particle volume of 2.5 MP/L within the smaller size range of 100 μm.

As shown in Table 1; Fig. 3a, microplastic sizes in Khorramabad’s groundwater predominantly ranged between 250 and 500 μm, with a notable concentration near 500 μm. This aligns with global observations: Ghanbari, Fataei [55] reported 87% of microplastics in the Qara Su River fell within 100–5,000 μm, while Shruti, Pérez-Guevara [54] documented 75% of particles < 1,000 μm in drinking water, of which 25% were within the 500 μm range. Similarly, Ranjdoost, Abbasi [10] identified 71.6% of snow microplastics in the 250–500 μm range.

In this study, particles < 250 μm or > 5,000 μm were undetected, attributed to two factors: [1] municipal treatment processes effectively remove larger particles (> 5 mm), and [2] environmental degradation mechanisms (e.g., UV radiation, mechanical abrasion) fragment plastics into smaller sizes [6, 59]. Consequently, 250–1,500 μm emerged as the dominant size range.

Seasonally, wet-season samples exhibited larger microplastics (e.g., 500 μm peaks) compared to the dry season. This is likely due to increased surface water infiltration into springs during rainfall, elevating turbidity and facilitating microplastic transport into groundwater. Conversely, no such trend was observed in dry conditions, underscoring rainfall’s role in modulating microplastic size distribution.

Color characteristics of microplastics

The microplastic particles found in the analyzed samples displayed a variety of colors, such as white, black, blue, red, yellow, and green. Among these, white was the most predominant color, accounting for the highest percentages observed across different contexts. Specifically, in the sources, the average count of white microplastics was 3.18 MP/L during the wet season and 1.71 MP/L during the dry season. Similarly, within the distribution network, the average was 2.79 MP/L in the wet season and 2.28 MP/L in the dry season.

Based on this study and as illustrated in Table 1 and (Fig. 3b), the predominant color of microplastics detected in both the wet and dry seasons was observed to be white. Considering that most microplastics produced by human activity are black or white [55], this result aligns with expected patterns of color prevalence. In a study by Shruti et al. [54], 69% of microplastics were white. Many colored particles may undergo discoloration during or after entering surface waters, due to processes such as degradation or bleaching. This phenomenon can be attributed to chemical and physical interactions within the aquatic environment, which significantly impact water quality and aquatic ecosystems. Additionally, most plastic bags used daily are white. Since a majority of the observed microplastics are fibers delicate in nature they are more susceptible to chemical reactions that can result in a white coloration [60]. The white coloration of microplastics is primarily determined by polymer type and environmental factors.

Polymer composition of microplastics

The findings of this study revealed that, in groundwater samples collected during both the wet and dry seasons, Polyethylene was the most prevalent polymer, comprising 50% of the total polymers detected. Analysis of Khorramabad city’s drinking water with a µ-Raman sensor revealed that polyethylene was the predominant microplastic polymer, constituting 50% of the total. This was followed by polypropylene and polystyrene, which represented 30% and 20% respectively.

Polyethylene, polypropylene, and polystyrene formed the polymers of microplastics, as shown in (Fig. 3-c). Polyethylene was dominant with 50% of microplastics, followed by polypropylene and polyester with 30% and 20%, respectively. In a 2020 study by Tang et al. [61] in China and a 2019 study by Keolmans et al. in the Netherlands [33], the highest levels of polymers detected in drinking water were polypropylene and polyethylene. Global demand for plastics has increased dramatically, leading to more orders in various markets. Among them, polyethylene and polypropylene are known as the main consumer polymers. Studies indicate that polyethylene and polypropylene, with densities below 1 g/cm3, are expected to float in aquatic ecosystems rather than sink. On the other hand, polyesters with densities between 1.04 g/cm3 are denser than both fresh and seawater. This difference in density helps explain the lower abundance of polyester in water samples [36]. The presence of microplastics in tap water may be attributed to the use of polypropylene and polyethylene in the construction of drinking water distribution pipes. In 2020, polyethylene and polypropylene accounted for 29.8 and 19.4% of total polymers produced globally, respectively. Also, in Iran, these two polymers are the most consumed polymers. These statistics clearly show that these two types of polymers have the largest share among other polymers. Polypropylene also has a special place in various industries. Its applications include food packaging, chocolate coating, the production of plastic bottle caps, and use in the manufacture of automotive parts. These uses demonstrate the diversity and importance of this polymer in everyday life and industry [22]. On the other hand, based on an inquiry from the Khorramabad water and sewage organization, 35% of the length of the distribution network is made of polyethylene. Therefore, the increased percentage of polyethylene may be due to this [33] .

Seasonal variability in microplastic abundance

This study investigated microplastic abundance across wet and dry seasons using the Wilcoxon test, revealing a statistically significant difference (P < 0.001). The wet season exhibited a higher average concentration of 2.81 particles/L compared to 1.31 MP/L in the dry season (Fig. 4). These findings align with a 2020 study in China by Xia, Rao [62], which reported increased microplastic abundance after rainfall (29.6 MP/L) versus before rainfall (7.4 MP/L). Similarly, Kankanige and Babel [63] carried out an investigation into water quality across rainy and non-rainy seasons. Their findings indicated that water samples during the rainy season were approximately 3% more contaminated than during the dry season. These results underscore the significant influence of seasonal rainfall on water quality. While rainfall is generally viewed as beneficial for drinking water supply and agricultural productivity, these studies demonstrate that during rainy periods, pollutants from soil and air can more readily enter water sources. These findings further suggest that rainfall influences microplastic concentrations in lake surface waters. Microplastic levels in surface waters can fluctuate considerably over time, particularly in freshwater environments, and are strongly influenced by precipitation events [63]. During rainy seasons, increased water flow and runoff enhance the likelihood of microplastics entering water bodies and soil, thereby raising their abundance in these environments [64]. A study in Anzali Wetland, found substantial microplastic contamination in the sediment, with concentrations ranging from 14 to 282 MP/100 g of dry sediment. This contamination appears to be influenced by heavy rainfall and the resulting runoff [64]. During the dry season, characterized by less precipitation and higher temperatures, environmental changes may influence microplastic accumulation. While microplastics are still present, their concentrations are generally lower than in the wet season [65].

Microplastic removal efficiency in the treatment plant

The abundance of microplastics was significantly higher in the treatment plant influent (7 MP/L) than in the effluent (2 MP/L) Which indicates a 67–78% removal efficiency of microplastics from inlet to outlet of the water house selection. The elevated influent concentrations align with Tabatabaei, Mafigholami [66], who attributed this to atmospheric deposition of airborne microplastics, particularly during precipitation events. Seasonal variability was pronounced, with wet-season influent levels (7 MP/L) exceeding dry-season values (2 MP/L), likely due to increased runoff-driven contamination. The plant’s removal efficiency (78% in cold seasons; 67% in dry seasons) aligns with global studies, such as Pivokonsky, Cermakova [67], which reported 70–83% efficiency in Czech wastewater facilities. This performance underscores the role of sedimentation and filtration processes in reducing microplastic loads. However, the persistence of particles in effluent highlights the need for advanced tertiary treatments to address finer particles (< 250 μm), which were not fully captured in this system.

Spatial distribution of microplastics in Khorramabad City

Spatial zoning analysis using GIS software version 10.5 categorized Khorramabad into three distinct zones (Zones 1–3; Fig. 5). Statistical evaluation via the Shapiro-Wilk and Kruskal-Wallis tests revealed no significant differences in microplastic abundance across zones during wet (p = 0.72) or dry (p = 0.82) seasons. Seasonally, average concentrations doubled from 1 MP/L (dry season) to 2 MP/L (wet season), reflecting rainfall-driven contamination.

Different areas of Khorramabad City based on geographic system zoning

Figure 6 illustrates the spatial heterogeneity of microplastics, with localized hotspots (> 5 MP/L) marked in red, predominantly in urbanized and runoff-prone areas. These hotspots are likely associated with areas of high human activity and inadequate drainage infrastructure, underscoring the significant role of anthropogenic factors in microplastic distribution. Accordingly, more prolonged and longitudinal investigations may be warranted. Further research in this domain will contribute to identifying which pollution source exerts a greater influence on the contamination of aquatic resources.

Morphological analysis of microplastics

Considering the role of SEM in improving the accuracy, verification, and objectivity of results, this method was used to evaluate the morphology of microplastics [6, 22]. Six particles, representing prevalent shapes and colors (e.g., white/transparent, black, red, and yellow fibers), were selected from groundwater samples, mounted on double-sided copper tape, and analyzed (Fig. 7). SEM images revealed smooth fiber surfaces with negligible weathering (e.g., cracks, scratches, or biofilm adhesion), indicating recent environmental introduction and limited exposure to degradation processes [68].

The absence of surface weathering suggests these microplastics (Fig. 7) have a lower capacity for adsorbing and transporting co-pollutants (e.g., heavy metals, organic contaminants) compared to aged particles with roughened surfaces [68]. This aligns with studies linking particle degradation to enhanced pollutant mobility in aquatic systems [59, 69]. The pristine morphology further supports the hypothesis that microplastics in Khorramabad’s groundwater originate from recent anthropogenic sources, such as urban runoff or inadequately treated wastewater, rather than long-term environmental accumulation.

Human exposure and health risk assessment

Based on an average groundwater microplastic concentration of 2.06 MP/L, the Estimated EDI for Khorramabad residents were calculated. Assuming daily water consumption of 2 L for adults and 1 L for children, the EDI was estimated to be 0.06 Mp.kg/day for adults and 0.07 Mp.kg/day for children. This indicates a relatively higher microplastic intake in younger populations when normalized to body weight [47]. These values align with global studies: 0.3–0.8 Mp.kg/day via egg consumption in China, Japan, and Bangladesh [39]. 0.16–0.48 Mp.kg/day (adults) and 0.21–0.64 Mp.kg/day (children) via beef/lamb consumption in Iran [70]. 6.5 Mp.kg/day via salt intake in Lebanon, highlighting variable contamination across food matrices [71]. 0.64 Mp.kg/day via canned fish in Iran [72].

The elevated EDI in Lebanon [70] likely stems from high microplastic contamination in unrefined salt, underscoring source-dependent exposure risks. While Khorramabad’s EDI is moderate, chronic exposure raises concerns due to microplastics’ potential to adsorb toxins, induce oxidative stress, and translocate across biological barriers [17, 47]. Children’s higher vulnerability emphasizes the need for targeted monitoring.

Polymer-Specific Hazards: PS exhibited the highest individual hazard (HI = 6), linked to its chemical additives and potential for leaching toxins [58]. PE (HI = 5.5) and PP (HI = 0.3) posed lower risks, consistent with their inert chemical structures and widespread use in food-grade materials [33, 69].

Cumulative Risk: The cumulative HI (11.8) exceeded thresholds for individual polymers, emphasizing the need to evaluate combined effects of mixed microplastic exposure.

Contextual Considerations: While current HI levels suggest low immediate risk, chronic exposure to even “low hazard” polymers may synergize with other pollutants (e.g., heavy metals, pathogens), amplifying long-term health impacts [17, 47]. Notably, in an expansive area such as the city of Khorramabad, assessment based solely on a single indicator is not feasible. As further research in the field of microplastics progresses, the potential for identifying lower concentrations continues to emerge, thereby necessitating more reliable technologies to establish standardized thresholds for microplastic pollution [50].

Conclusion

The study This study reported an average microplastic concentration of 2.06 particles per liter in the drinking water of Khorramabad. Out of a total of 110 samples collected, 88 contained microplastics, predominantly white fibers measuring between 1000 and 1500 micrometers in length. Spectroscopic analyses confirmed the presence of polymers such as polyethylene, polypropylene, and polystyrene in the samples. The findings may suggest that rainfall could play a significant role in influencing the abundance of microplastics in groundwater sources, including wells and springs. The water treatment plant exhibited an average microplastic concentration of 2.46 particles per liter, while the distribution network showed 1.46 particles per liter. Furthermore, it is likely that the statistically significant difference observed in microplastic prevalence between the wet and dry seasons (P < 0.001) may be attributed to the effect of rainfall. The mean abundance in the wet season was 2.81 particles per liter, which appeared to be approximately 1.30 particles per liter higher than that in the dry season. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images revealed fibers lacking signs of weathering, which could indicate a relatively young age of these microplastics. Additionally, the analysis indicated that there appears to be no statistically significant difference in microplastic abundance across different urban areas (P = 0.25). The observed variations may be attributed to various factors, including water storage practices, incomplete filtration, inadequate maintenance, human activities, and geographical location.
