A clinical scoring system to predict tuberculous lymphadenitis in settings with high disease burden
Authors: Ermias Teklehaimanot Yefter, Asefa Adimasu Taddese, Getahun Mengistu Tessema, Miteku Andualem Limenih, Alemante Tafese Beyna, Addisu Minaye Dejen, Destaye Shiferaw Alemu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Limited diagnostic service availability challenges the timely diagnosis and treatment of tuberculous lymphadenitis, the most common extrapulmonary tuberculosis in endemic areas. To address this, our study aimed to develop a clinical scoring rule for estimating the likelihood of tuberculous lymphadenitis, designed as a simple and easy-to-apply classification tool to facilitate timely diagnosis. We used a cross-sectional study to collect data from 1364 patients with lymphadenopathy. A prediction model was developed by incorporating predictors from multivariable logistic regression analysis. Optimal cutoff point was determined using Youden’s Index. Performance was evaluated using discrimination and calibration. Internal validation and clinical utility were assessed using bootstrapping and decision curve analysis, respectively. A total of 790 patients (57.9%, 95% CI 55.3%–60.5%) were diagnosed with tuberculous lymphadenitis. Sex, duration, size, location, and consistency of lymphadenopathy, presence of pain, sinus tract, systemic symptoms, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate were predictors incorporated to develop the scoring rule. With a discrimination power of 96.9% (95% CI 96.1%, 97.8%), the clinical scoring rule using these predictors showed a sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of 93.7%, 89.7%, and 92%, respectively. It demonstrated internal validity with near-zero optimism coefficients for various performance metrics. Using easily accessible clinical parameters, we developed a scoring rule that reliably predicts tuberculous lymphadenitis. Besides its use in clinical settings for making timely clinical decisions, the scoring rule is a valuable candidate for community-level screening, facilitating early case detection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20322-0.

Introduction

Tuberculous lymphadenitis (TBLN) is a mycobacterial infection of lymph nodes1,2. Lymph nodes (LN) are small, bean-shaped lymphoid tissues that are part of the body’s immune system. The human body harbors hundreds of LNs located near junctions of the major lymphatic and blood vessels, most prominently in the neck, axilla, groin, thorax and abdomen. Lymph nodes are not normally palpable and they become recognizable when they become enlarged or painful. Lymphadenopathy (LAP) is a clinical term given to such LNs that have abnormal growth, consistence and/or pain1–3. There are several potential causes of LAP, ranging from nonspecific mild reactions to specific infectious, autoimmune, lymphoproliferative and neoplastic diseases. One of the most notable infectious processes that cause a LAP is TBLN1,2,4. In Ethiopia, the vast majority of clinically significant LAPs are the result of TBLN with an estimated range of 44.7–68.5% among patients with LAPs5–9.

As a mycobacterial infection, TBLN is the most common form of extra-pulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB) in endemic areas10,12–16. Ethiopia is one of the 30 countries with the highest overall tuberculosis (TB) burden, which includes both pulmonary and extra-pulmonary cases, including TBLN17,18. According to studies conducted in central and northern parts of Ethiopia, the proportion of TBLN is estimated to range from 33.3 to 48.9% of the total reported EPTB cases7,19,20.

The main presenting symptom of TBLN is slowly developing painless LAP which may progress to different stages2,4. It may initially present with well-defined, firm LAP, which can become fixed and matted. Later, it may be soft, and fluctuant with abscess formation and eventually leading to pus discharging sinus tract formation. Associated with this, patients may manifest with systemic symptoms including fever, night sweating, and weight loss. However, the clinical profile of TBLN varies widely, with diverse presentations influenced by geographic area, age group, and the presence of preexisting risks and comorbidities1,4,7,11,21–24.

The definitive diagnosis of TBLN requires microbiological, pathological, and/or molecular methods18–20,25. In routine clinical practice, the diagnosis of TBLN in many endemic regions, including Ethiopia, relies on pathological methods, primarily fine-needle aspiration cytopathology (FNAC), which continues to be an effective technique6,26–28. The effectiveness and diagnostic performance can be further enhanced by incorporating microbiological and molecular tests like Gene-Xpert utilizing the samples collected for conventional FNAC. Unfortunately, our setting suffers from limited availability and coverage of pathology diagnostic services due to a severe shortage of skilled professionals and a scarcity of resources29,30. In Ethiopia, most pathology diagnostic services are centralized in the capital or major regional cities, making them inaccessible to the majority of patients. Consequently, clinical practice often involves referring patients to these facilities, usually after an empirical trial of broad-spectrum antibiotics. This approach, exacerbated by an inadequate referral system, leads to delayed diagnosis and treatment, resulting in increased mortality, morbidity, healthcare costs, and antimicrobial resistance31,32. This adds further strain on patients and the healthcare system and contradicts one of the WHO END TB initiative’s main strategies, which is to ensure early diagnosis and proper treatment for all TB cases25. In this regard, it would be of great clinical and public health importance to develop easy-to-implement methods, like a simplified clinical scoring rule, to identify cases of TBLN. Studies on developing such scoring systems for various EPTB forms, including TBLN, have shown promising yet highly variable performance, often limited to specific populations23,33–37. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and validate a clinical scoring rule to estimate the probability of TBLN in patients with LAP. This rule, based on readily accessible clinical parameters, is intended to serve as an easy-to-use alternative tool to identify TBLN cases right at the point of care, thereby aiding early diagnosis.

Methods

Study design, period, and setting

The study utilized an institution-based cross-sectional design that involved medical record review of patients presenting with clinically significant lymphadenopathy between September 2021 and August 2024. It was conducted at the University of Gondar comprehensive specialized hospital (UoGCSH) located in Gondar town, Northwest Ethiopia. This tertiary-level teaching and referral hospital serves up to 250,000 people annually and acts as a referral center for more than five million people in its catchment area. It has a range of specialty and subspecialty units and departments that provide various in-patient and out-patient medical services. Patients who present with LAP customarily undergo FNAC for diagnosis as part of regular clinical practice and diagnostic work-up. The hospital has a fairly equipped anatomic pathology department which performs various pathology diagnostic procedures and tests. In its cytopathology unit, specimen processing and microscopic slide preparation routinely involve air drying and Wright stain staining, following the standard protocol.

Population, inclusion criteria, and exclusion criteria

All patients who presented with clinically significant LAP at UoGCSH were the source population, whereas all patients who presented with clinically significant LAP between September 15, 2021, and August 15, 2024, at UoGCSH were the study population. Patients who presented with clinically significant LAP during this study period at UoGCSH and who had a cytopathology diagnosis by FNAC were included in the study. All patients, regardless of age group, were included in the study. Patients with clinically significant LAP who had equivocal or non-definitive diagnoses on FNAC reports were excluded. To prevent under- or overestimation of parameters, patients with a preexisting baseline diagnosis for whom FNAC was performed as follow-up or treatment monitoring were also excluded. Additionally, patients with LAP involving readily inaccessible visceral body compartments were excluded to prevent imprecise data and estimates.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

In order to ensure precise prediction and minimize over-fitting, the minimum sample size required for the study was calculated using the method originally proposed by Riley et al.38 and van Smeden et al.39,40. The method requires specifying the overall outcome risk in the source population, the number of candidate predictor parameters, and the anticipated model performance in terms of overall model fit (R2). It is recommended to have a conservative choice of R2CS. If there is no previous given value regarding similar model performance, it is advised to consider R2CS as 15% of the max(R2cs) (i.e., R2cs = 0.15* max(R2cs). For this study, the proportion of TBLN among patients with clinically significant LAP in the study area was found to be 0.685,8. Defining the outcome proportion with smaller event as 0.32 (I.e. 1–0.68), max(R2cs) was calculated to be 0.7146. Then, the anticipated model performance R2CS was found to be 0.11 (0.15*0.7146 = 0.11). As a result, based on the above formula, using 17 candidate parameters (p = 17), anticipated model performance R2CS of 0.11, and the recommended value of shrinkage factor (S = 0.9), the minimum sample size required for the study was determined to be 1305. By considering a 10% contingency for missing FNAC reports and archives, a total of 1436 patients with 460 events (i.e., non-TBLN cases) were needed for this study.

Study participants were selected using a simple random sampling technique after the number and lists of FNAC performed on patients with LAP within the stated study period were identified from department of pathology archive. A unique FNAC report number and the respective medical registration number (MRM) from the procedure requisition form were used to identify and review the records of these selected study participants. Variables of the study

The outcome variable was TBLN status of patients with LAP (TBLN/Non-TBLN). Cytopathological diagnosis made by a senior expert anatomic pathologist was used to determine the status. The candidate predictor variables includes Socio-demographic variables (Age, sex, and place of residency), Clinical variables (Duration of LAP, anatomic location of LAP, size of LAP, number of LAP, discharging sinus, weight loss, loss of appetite, fever, night sweating, cough, contact history with TB case), and laboratory variables (Erythrocytes sedimentation rate(ESR), HIV status, and serum hemoglobin level (Hb)). These candidate variables were considered for inclusion in the study based on a comprehensive review of the existing literature on TBLN and expert opinions from senior physicians in clinical practice and experienced clinical researchers.

Operational definitions

Clinically significant lymphadenopathy—Lymphadenopathy was considered clinically significant for this study if the affected lymph-node size is ≥ 1 cm at its largest diameter or lasted for ≥ 2 weeks or if it is generalized affecting multiple anatomic sites1,2.

Tuberculous lymphadenitis—A cytopathological diagnosis given by an expert senior pathologist using FNAC was used to define tuberculous lymphadenitis.

Data collection procedures and quality assurance

A structured data extraction tool was prepared after consulting experienced researchers and expertise from clinical practice and incorporating their input, as well as analyzing several prior studies of a similar nature. The tool was then organized according to the study’s objectives, and a digital data collection template was deployed after transforming the proposed tool using KoBoToolbox41. The data collection was conducted by two trained general practitioners. It primarily involved reviewing patients’ medical records and FNAC reports from the pathology department archive. The collected data was checked for its completeness and consistency on daily basis during the data collection period.

Data management and statistical analysis

Data digitally collected using KoBoCollect was exported to R software (version 4.1.3) for further processing and analysis. Missing values for the following eight candidate predictors ranged from 5.7 to 28.4%: contact history with TB patient (21.6%), chronic cough (28.4%), ESR (12.5%), hemoglobin (23.2%), HIV status (6.4%), painless LAP (12.4%), size of the LAP (5.7%) and systemic symptoms (11.3%). Number of lymph nodes involved (69.8%) and loss of appetite (78.5%) were excluded from the analysis since they were absent in a significant number of the study participants. Patterns of missingness were assessed for each missing value by examining distribution of predictors and distribution pattern of missing value with respect to the incomplete cases as well as association with missingness. Predictors correlated with missingness and the existence of association was identified. Assuming missing at random (MAR), the multiple imputation (MI) technique was employed to manage missing data using the multivariate imputation by chained equations (‘mice’) package. The number of imputations was determined by considering the fraction of missing information (FMI), the width of the 95% confidence interval, the P-value and their relation42. To correctly account for the variability between imputations and the uncertainty due to missing data, the combined pooled estimates were computed from the imputed datasets. Continuous variables were dichotomized using MedCalc statistical software version 22.026 that determined cutoff point for dichotomization by using an optimal criterion after evaluating the prediction performance and standard error of the predictor based on the method by DeLong et al.43.

Predictor’s selection, theoretical design, model development, and internal validation

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) analysis, which is a variant of penalized estimation, was used for predictor’s selection. Predictors with a coefficient of zero were excluded from the model. Predictors with non-zero coefficients were included in the multivariable logistic regression analysis for a diagnostic prediction model development. The domain was patients with clinically significant LAP at UoGCSH. The theoretical design was forwarded as follow\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{Probability}}\;{\text{ of}}\;{\text{ having }}\;{\text{TBLN}}\;{\text{ at}}\;{\text{ presentation }}\; = \;{\text{ f}}\; \, \left( {{\text{predictor}}\;{\text{ variables}}} \right)$$\end{document}

Pr (Y) = f (x), where Y = 1, means having TBLN at presentation, and Y = 0, means not having TBLN at presentation (non-TBLN).

Pr(Y = 1) = f (β0 + β1 age + β2 sex + β3 residence + β4 Duration of LAP + β5 anatomic location of LN + β6 LN size + β7 LN number + β8 LN consistency + β9 Discharging sinus + β10 Fever + β11 night sweating + β12 weight loss + β13 chronic cough + β14 contact history + β15 ESR + β16 HIV status + β17 Hb).

After checking all the necessary assumptions, predictors selected from LASSO were considered for inclusion to multivariable binary logistic regression analysis. Predictors with p value ≥ 0.15 were reduced one by one from the full model to get a reduced model containing predictors that have statistically significant association with TBLN (p < 0.05). Quantified as beta coefficients with a 95% confidence interval, these predictors were utilized to build a prediction model to estimate the probability of TBLN in patients with LAP, which was then transformed into a simplified clinical scoring rule. The score values of each predictor were determined by defining the weights as the quotient of the corresponding estimated coefficient divided by the smallest coefficient, then rounded to the nearest integer. This gave a simplified clinical score that would be easy to implement in the clinical setting. The Youden Index method was used to determine optimal cutoff point for classification. The performance of the model was assessed through its discrimination, calibration as well as prediction density plot and Brier score. Internal validity of the model was assessed using the bootstrapping technique, whereby randomly generated bootstrap samples (boot number, B = 10,000) with replacement were used to evaluate optimism and bias-corrected predictive performance. In order to simultaneously determine and compare multiple model performance indices on the bootstrap samples along with the bias corrected estimates, the “validate” and “val.pro” functions from ‘rms’ package in R software were used. Its clinical utility was evaluated using the decision curve analysis (DCA). Transparent reporting of a multivariable prediction model for individual prognosis or diagnosis (TRIPOD) guideline was used to report the conduct and findings of the study.

Results

Socio-demographic, clinical, and laboratory profiles of participants

A total of 1364 patients with clinically significant LAP were included in the study. The age of participants ranged from 1 to 75 years, with a mean of 26.8 years (SD = 14.1). Nearly three-fourths (72.4%) of the participants were over the age of 15. Males made up a slight majority at 52.1%, with a male-to-female ratio of 1.1:1. Additionally, significant portions (58.1%) of the participants were from rural areas. The median duration of presentation of patients with clinically significant LAP was 4 months (range 0.25–24 months). The vast majority (86.4%) of patients had a painless LAP and nearly half of the patients (49.5%) had no systemic constitutional symptoms like fever or night sweating. More than half (56.9%) of patients had discrete LAP with mean size of 2.5 cm (SD = 0.94) and the neck region (cervical and supraclavicular LNs) were involved in the majority of patients (67.8%). Patients had a mean ESR of 49.3 mm/hr (SD = 27.2), with values ranging from 3 to 113 mm/hr. Of the 1364 patients who came with clinically significant LAP, 112 (8.2%) were HIV positive. Detailed clinical and laboratory profiles of participants are provided in the supplemental files (Supplementary Table 1).

Prevalence of tuberculous lymphadenitis

From a total of 1364 patients with clinically significant LAP, cytopathologic diagnosis of TBLN was made in 790 (57.9% (95% CI 55.3%–60.5%)). Nearly two-thirds of patients with TBLN had denied contact history with TB patient (63.9%) and had no chronic cough (65.3%). In nine out of ten TBLN patients, the size of LAP was ≥ 2.5 cm, the duration was ≥ 2 months, and the LAP was painless. The neck region (cervical & supraclavicular LNs) was involved in more than three-quarters (78.4%) of TBLN cases (Supplementary Table 1).

Prediction model performance

Based on the beta coefficients of predictors, the discriminatory power of the original prediction model was found to be 0.971 (95% CI 0.963, 0.979) indicating an excellent discriminatory power. Hosmer–Lemeshow test for model calibration yielded a p value of 0.585, indicating that the model was well calibrated and there was no significant difference between the observed and predicted probabilities of TBLN (Supplementary Fig. 1).

As it is seen from the prediction density plot, the model yielded higher predictive probabilities for the positive cases (TBLN cases), and lower probabilities for the negative cases (non-TBLN cases) with minimal overlap between the noticeable density distributions of predicted probabilities, indicating the model performed well in differentiating patients with LAP as TBLN and non-TBLN (Supplementary Fig. 2). Additionally, the Brier score and the Nagelkerke R Square (R2) of the developed prediction model were 0.0589 and 0.82, respectively, indicating good overall model performance.

As it is shown in Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3, the optimism corrected slope and intercept were approximately similar with the original index and there is no evidence to suggest that the optimism corrected calibration line deviates significantly from the ideal 45-degree line with slope 1 (p value 0.959). With an optimism coefficient of 0.0016, the bias corrected model after bootstrap validation was found to have AUROC of 0.9694 (95% CI 0.9615, 0.9775), indicating an excellent discrimination power. Moreover, it was also approximately similar with the original index of model with an optimism coefficient of 0.0016 (Table 2, and Supplementary Fig. 3). Clinical scoring rule: application and performance

The numerical score values of the predictor variables were weighed against their respective coefficients which involved dividing the corresponding coefficients from the reduced model by the smallest coefficient and rounding to the nearest integer. Accordingly, the total score ranges between 0 and 12 with high score indicating high probability of being TBLN (Table 1). The AUROC using the simplified clinical scoring rule was 0.9690 (95% CI 0.961 to 0.978), which is close to the model performance using original beta coefficients. Discussion

This study has developed and internally validated a prediction model using simple clinical parameters to estimate the probability of TBLN among patients with clinically significant LAP. The model is based on nine predictors (Sex, Duration of LAP, size of LAP, consistency of LAP, sinus tract, painless LAP, location of LAP, systemic symptoms, and ESR). Eight of the nine predictors used in the model are clinical features that can be identified easily by conventional history taking and physical examination. The remaining one predictor used in the model is ESR which is also a simple laboratory test available in any health care facility. Without employing advanced laboratory or imaging procedures, this study examined the effectiveness of a prediction model that can be applied as an alternative diagnostic tool in resource limited settings with high TB burden. As evident from the DCA, making clinical decisions using the developed clinical scoring rule has a higher clinical net benefit compared to other alternative strategies, regardless of the predicted threshold probabilities.

The present study findings showed that the proportion of TBLN among patients with clinically significant LAP was 57.9% (95% CI 55.3%–60.5%). This finding is in line with the findings from other TB endemic sub-Saharan and Asian nations, whereby 44.7% to 69.5% of clinically significant LAPs were reported to be caused by TBLN1,5–11. In this study, we developed a prediction model for TBLN using nine clinical parameters as predictors, which have also been reported in previous studies as either occurring more frequently or being associated with TBLN1,5–11,21. On the other hand, in contrast to several studies conducted on pulmonary TB, the current study showed that nearly two-thirds of patients with TBLN denied contact history with TB patients (63.9%) and had no chronic cough (65.3%). Similar findings were reported from a meta-analysis done in sub-Saharan African countries including 12 nations which reported contact history and chronic cough in about 33% and 32% of TBLN respectively22. In contrast, our findings were higher than the report from Bangladesh (contact history of 26.2%), India (chronic cough 23.4%), central Ethiopia (contact history of 21% and chronic cough of 17%) and northern Ethiopia (contact history of 24.5% and chronic cough of 20.8%)4,21,26. In conclusion, in contrast to several prediction model developed for pulmonary TB that uniformly includes contact history with TB patients as a strong predictor, the result from the current study on TBLN as well as other aforementioned literatures failed to show the importance of contact history as a factor for TBLN44–46. As a result, unlike pulmonary TB, this might signify looking for non-airborne transmission dynamics for TBLN, particularly foodborne routes. In this regard, the potential role of strains like Mycobacterium bovis (bovine TB), often transmitted through ingestion of unpasteurized milk products, in TBLN pathogenesis should be explored. In line with the findings of previous studies in Ethiopia, the HIV positivity rate of patients with TBLN in the present study was very low (8.9%) when it is compared to the overall African estimate (52%)21–23,37. Similar discrepancies between Ethiopia and other sub-Saharan African countries have also been also noted in systematic review and meta-analysis22. In the present study, HIV status as a predictor was among the variables initially excluded by LASSO regression for predictor selection. While HIV is a major global driver and critical factor for TB progression, our findings suggest that HIV positivity may not be a dominant factor in TBLN in Ethiopia relative to other regions. This highlights the need for further studies on other factors (such as etiologic, host, and environmental factors) and how they complexly interact to influence TBLN in Ethiopia, beyond HIV status.

With an AUROC of 0.969 (95% CI 0.096, 0.98%), the developed scoring rule in this study has classified the study participants in to clinically relevant categories and was found to have sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV, and NPV of 93.7%, 89.7%, 92.0%, 92.6% and 91.2% respectively. These findings are approximately similar with the finding reported by Khalil EAG et al. from Sudan which also developed a scoring algorithm for TBLN and showed AUROC of 0.964 (95% CI 0.949–0.980) as well as sensitivity and specificity of 91% and 90% respectively23. The predictors used by Khalil EAG et al. were ESR, nocturnal fever, discharging sinuses and Mantoux skin test reading. The present study has incorporated six more clinical predictors and excluded Mantoux skin test as it was difficult to collect data on it with retrospective medical record review of study participant. The AUROC of the model from the current study was approximately similar with the original index after internal validation and the optimism coefficient was near zero. On the contrary, the scoring algorithm developed by Khalil EAG et al. was not calibrated and internally validated.

Based on nine simple and easily accessible clinical parameters as predictors, the present study has generated a probability clinical scoring rule that can identify patients with TBLN with a sensitivity of 93.7%, specificity of 89.7%, and accuracy of 92.0%. These figures do meet the WHO standard for a community-based TB screening test (90% sensitivity and 70% specificity)47. As a result, in addition to its potential use as an alternative diagnostic approach in clinical settings, this scoring rule has the potential to be used as a screening test at the community level for the early detection of TBLN cases in resource-limited areas with a high disease burden.

The developed scoring rule had the highest net benefit across the full range of threshold probabilities, as it is shown in the DCA. As a result, making clinical decisions using the scoring has a higher clinical benefit than the alternative other strategies regardless of the predicted threshold probabilities. This will avoid the unnecessary inadvertent broad-spectrum antibiotic use that is routinely given to patients with LAP; thereby contributing positively to the fight against antimicrobial resistance. Moreover, by triaging those with higher probability of TBLN for further investigation, it will help to minimize the diagnostic delay and reduce the time and economic cost for patients as well as for the overall health system.

The current study has several strengths and advantages. By adhering to expert recommendations in clinical prediction modeling, the sample size computation ensured an appropriate sample size, and the model was constructed on a sufficient number of events per predictor variable. The model is well-calibrated and demonstrated high performance after internal validation, showing that it is less sample-dependent and can predict TBLN in independent patient sets with similar accuracy. The predictors used in the model are inexpensive and easily measurable basic clinical parameters that do not require sophisticated or advanced technologies. Moreover, it is a convenient and easy to compute point score system that can be easily implemented in clinical settings for rapid assessment and stratification of patients for timely clinical decision.

The study’s findings should be interpreted with the following limitations in mind. As data was collected by retrospective review of medical records, some predictors which were highlighted in previous studies, like loss of appetite, significant weight loss, and Mantoux skin test were often missed and not examined in the current study. Additionally, pathological method (FNAC) was used to determine TBLN status, and we didn’t include microbiological or molecular methods as they weren’t performed in the routine clinical practice in the study area. Even though the diagnostic yields of these methods are arguable, one can incorporate them and use a composite reference standard in future studies. Finally, the study’s single-site nature and lack of external validation should be also considered in the interpretation of the results of the study.

Conclusion and recommendation

The present study developed a clinical scoring rule that provides high accuracy in estimating the probability of TBLN in patients with LAP by using nine inexpensive and easily measurable predictors: sex, duration of LAP, anatomic location of LAP, size of LAP, consistency of LAP, presence of a sinus tract, painless LAP, systemic symptoms, and ESR. Being well-calibrated and exhibiting excellent discrimination, the model was internally valid and offered superior net benefit over other potential strategies. It has effectively classified patients as TBLN and non-TBLN with high sensitivity and specificity. Consequently, besides its applicability in clinical settings as an alternative diagnostic approach, the developed scoring rule is a promising candidate for community-level screening method to facilitate early TBLN detection in resource-limited areas with a high disease burden. While the developed clinical score is internally valid, convenient, and easy to use, further evidence from similar studies and, crucially, external validation in independent populations and settings are recommended before its wide implementation.
