Direct and opportunity costs of comprehensive abortion care for women living in humanitarian settings in the Amhara Region, Ethiopia
Authors: Tewodros Seyoum, Abdella Amano Abdo, Nakachew Sewnet Amare, Marie Klingberg Allvin, Elisa M. Maffioli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProviding comprehensive abortion care (CAC), including induced abortion and post-abortion care (PAC) to manage complications, poses a financial challenge, particularly in humanitarian settings. Unsafe abortions and the costs of PAC impose a significant health and economic burden on women and society. This study aimed to assess the direct and opportunity costs of CAC for women who experienced CAC in humanitarian settings.MethodsA stratified sampling approach was initially used to select 31 public health facilities across seven administrative zones in the conflict-affected northern Amhara region of Ethiopia. Structured surveys were administered to 1,642 women aged 13–49 years who received care for induced abortions and treatment of incomplete abortions between September 2023 and March 2024. Descriptive statistics were used to analyze direct costs associated with attempts for pregnancy termination at home, visits and stay at the health facility, needs and care after the surgical procedure, and re-visits. The opportunity costs of time for women and their caregivers were measured using gender-specific daily wages for low-skilled workers.ResultsThe total direct cost for women undergoing all stages of CAC, including attempts for pregnancy termination, transportation, cost of food, drugs, and diagnostic tests, and additional costs for recovery and readmission, and caregiver expenses, was ETB 7,416.8 (US$134.16). Direct costs limited to the facility visit and post-procedure period were 3,273.4 (US$ 59.21). Opportunity costs were substantial, totaling ETB 2,024 ($36.61) of household income lost. Incomplete abortions (ETB 8,540.8, US$ 154.5) were costlier than induced abortions (ETB 7,282.3, US$ 131.7) and women treated in general hospitals incurred the highest total costs (ETB 11,038.0, US$ 199.7), while costs were the lowest at primary hospitals (ETB 7,635.8, US$ 138.1).ConclusionWomen in humanitarian settings in the Amhara Region of Ethiopia face significant financial burdens when seeking CAC, despite its availability through public services. The burden is pronounced for those receiving treatment for incomplete abortions. These findings highlight the urgent need for policy measures to reduce financial barriers and ensure equitable access to safe, affordable abortion care, especially for populations in humanitarian settings.Clinical trial numbernot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25170-1.

Introduction

Armed conflict is a major public health problem that places immense strain on health systems worldwide [1]. In humanitarian settings, such crises have profoundly disrupted sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR), leading to increased risks of maternal morbidity and mortality, unsafe abortion, and unmet need for contraception [2]. The scale of the humanitarian crisis is escalating, with an estimated 305 million people worldwide currently in need of humanitarian assistance to address their SRHR needs. Women and adolescent girls are disproportionately affected [3].

Ethiopia has reduced abortion-related mortality from 34% to 5% over the past 15 years [4–6]. The government has taken measures including legalizing abortion [7] and expanding access to comprehensive abortion care (CAC) [8]. The CAC services include safe induced abortion using recommended methods and post-abortion care (PAC) for managing complications from incomplete abortion [9]. However, ongoing conflict in Northern Ethiopia has severely disrupted service delivery, particularly for internally displaced women. In the last five years, around 4.5 million Ethiopians have been displaced, nearly half a million in the Amhara Region [10]. Displacement, insecurity, and economic strain have reduced access to contraceptives and contributed to unintended pregnancy. Limited access to safe abortion drives many women to choose unsafe methods, leading to high PAC needs [11, 12]. Treating unsafe abortion complications is costly, requiring skilled personnel, surgery, expensive drugs, supplies, and prolonged hospital stay [13].

To reduce maternal and child mortality, Ethiopia introduced healthcare reforms in 2005 [6] and free health insurance in 2008 [7], guaranteeing free access to maternal and child services, including abortion care in public health facilities. Yet, shortages of supplies and drugs often push patients to private facilities, where they incur out-of-pocket expenditures [14]. Some public facilities also charge user fees due to a lack of reimbursement and struggle to cover operational costs [8, 13]. In addition, women incur non-medical and opportunistic costs, such as transportation, food, lodging, and wage losses, for themselves and caregivers during the hospital stay [15]. This further creates burdens for conflict-affected women and adolescent girls.

Providing CAC services, including managing complications, is also financially challenging for governments. Treating unsafe abortion complications in Sub-Saharan Africa costs an estimated US$ 68 76 million annually, varying by treatment methods, which often include outdated curettage, dilatation, or manual vacuum aspiration (MVA) [16]. While legalization in some countries increases access, it can also lead to higher overall health expenditures and individual costs. The economic impact of unsafe abortion on individuals is poorly understood, with limited evidence for policymakers in Sub-Saharan African countries [5, 17–19]. In Ethiopia, no comprehensive estimate exists of the costs of CAC for women and their households living in humanitarian settings. To our knowledge, the only national study, based on 2008 data, estimated PAC health system direct costs at US$ 6.5–8.9 million annually, averaging US$ 36.2 per client across five complication types [20]. We lack more recent and comprehensive evidence on the individual direct and opportunity costs of CAC in humanitarian settings in Ethiopia. Therefore, this study estimates direct and opportunity costs of CAC for women and caregivers in conflict-affected areas of the Amhara region, Ethiopia, disaggregated by abortion type and facility level.

Methods

Study design and setting.

A descriptive, facility-based study was conducted in 31 public health facilities located in humanitarian settings within the Amhara Region of Ethiopia. Ethiopia is the second most populous country in Africa, with a population of 126.5 million as of 2022 [21]. The government is divided into ten regional states, including the Amhara National Regional State, which is organized into 13 administrative zones. The study region is the second most populous region in Ethiopia, with an estimated population size of 22,876,991 [22].

The Ethiopian healthcare system is organized into three tiers: primary, secondary, and tertiary levels of care. The primary level encompasses primary healthcare units, including health posts, health centers, and primary hospitals. At the secondary level, services are delivered by general hospitals, whereas specialized hospitals provide tertiary care [23]. CAC services are available across all three levels of the healthcare system. Within the Amhara Region, there are 82 hospitals, 861 health centers, and 3,565 health posts serving both the host community and IDPs [24]. The number of Internally Displaced People (IDPs), refugees, returnees, and affected populations in the Amhara Region due to the northern Ethiopian conflict totals 542,300 people across 15 woredas (districts), corresponding to half of the total IDPs in Northern Ethiopia (1.1 million) [25].

The study was conducted in seven administrative zones of the Amhara Region: North Shewa, Oromia Special Zone, North Wollo, South Wollo, Waghimra, North Gondar, and South Gondar. Over the past five years, numerous humanitarian organizations, including the Ethiopian Midwives Association, have deployed more than 500 midwives to address the SRHR needs, including CAC, of displaced and host communities, with considerable financial and technical support from the United Nations Population Fund and the Ethiopian Ministry of Health (MOH) [26].

Study Population.

This study included women and adolescent girls who received CAC services at selected health facilities serving both host communities and IDPs over a seven-month data collection period, from September 21, 2023, to March 30, 2024. Eligible participants were those who sought CAC services for safely induced abortion, management of incomplete abortion, or post-abortion care during the study period. Women and girls who presented with complete abortions and did not receive any component of CAC services at the study facilities were excluded from the analysis.

Health Facility Selection.

A stratified sampling approach was initially employed to obtain a representative sample of public health facilities at different levels of care across the seven conflict-affected zones in northern Ethiopia. All three referral hospitals in the study area were purposively included. From the 46 primary and general hospitals in the selected administrative zones, 14 (30%) were randomly chosen, comprising seven general hospitals and seven primary hospitals. In addition, 14 of the 70 health centers (20%) were randomly selected. In total, this process yielded a sample of 31 public health facilities providing CAC services. In collaboration with the Ethiopian Ministry of Health (MOH) and the Amhara Regional Health Office, the selected facilities were subsequently assessed in terms of security conditions and client flow. Four facilities were found to be inaccessible due to active conflict and were replaced with more accessible alternatives (Ataye Hospital was replaced by Woldya Hospital, Ataye Health Center by Woldya Gonder Ber Health Center, Tefera Hailu Memorial Hospital by Dabat Hospital, and Seqota Health Center by Dabat Health Center). Over the seven-month data collection period, these facilities served an average of 60 women each for CAC services, amounting to approximately 1,860 clients.

Sample Selection.

The sample size for this study was determined based on the primary objective of a larger person-centered CAC project (Amare NS, Seyoum T, Tariku A, Mattison C, Klingberg Allvin M. Person-centered comprehensive abortion care provision and associated factors in northern Ethiopia: a cross-sectional study in a humanitarian setting, underview), yielding a final required sample size of 1,508 participants. All eligible participants (women and adolescent girls aged 13–49 years receiving CAC services) were consecutively enrolled over a seven-month data collection period. A total of 1,667 women and girls accessed health facilities during this period. However, 25 of them arrived with complete abortions and did not receive CAC services. Therefore, the actual study participants who received CAC at the 31 selected health facilities were 1,642, which comprises our final sample for analysis. 29.3% of our sample defined themselves as internally displaced women or girls (Amare NS, Seyoum T, Tariku A, Mattison C, Klingberg Allvin M. Person-centered comprehensive abortion care provision and associated factors in northern Ethiopia: a cross-sectional study in a humanitarian setting, underview).

Measurement.

Initially, a comprehensive costing assessment tool was developed based on previous studies done elsewhere [19, 27]. Data were collected using both closed-ended and open-ended questionnaires that covered several domains. The first domain included socio-demographic variables such as age, marital status, urban or rural residence, educational attainment, and occupation of the woman and her partner. Other domains included reproductive health-related characteristics such as the history of pregnancies and abortions, access to CAC services, women’s experiences and perceived quality of CAC, and assessments of immediate post-abortion contraceptive uptake. The rest of the survey was used to gather direct and opportunity costs incurred by the women from home to the facility level, including costs incurred by women who self-reported abortion attempts before reaching the health facility, costs of visits and stay at the health facility, costs after the procedure, and costs related to complications and readmissions.

More specifically, the tool was structured to gather the following inputs to calculate direct and opportunity costs: (i) before visiting a health facility: experiences with attempting unsafe pregnancy termination and consumption of substances, insertion of objects, and drugs, as well as inability of carrying out normal activities due to the pregnancy termination; (ii) at health facility: distance and time traveled and costs of transportation to one or multiple health facilities, days of stay at the health facility for women and caregivers, expenses on food, drugs, diagnostic tests or other fees, and inability to work during those days for both individuals; (iii) After the procedure: inability to work due to the recovery time for the woman, as well as for the caregiver taking care of her, and medical or other costs such as sanitary products; (iv) Revisits due to complications post abortion: whether the woman experienced complications and had to return to the health facility, transportation costs, duration of stay at the health facility, expenses on food, drugs, diagnostic tests or other fees, and inability to work during those days for her and her caregiver. For the opportunity cost of time, we use daily wages for low-skilled workers estimated by the International Labor Organization at ETB 2,382.4 (US$ 43.1) for men and ETB 1,584.6 (US$ 28.7) for women. All estimated costs in Ethiopian Birr (ETB) were converted to US dollars for reporting. The exchange rate of the Ethiopian Birr to the US dollar was based on the rate recorded at the beginning of the data collection period, from September 21, 2023, to March 30, 2024 (US$ 1 = ETB 55.28 ETB on September 21, 2023).1 The survey tool is in the Supplementary Material.

Data collection.

The data collection tool was initially prepared in English and then translated into the local languages, followed by a back-translation into English to ensure consistency. The pilot test was conducted in a health facility in the North Wollo zone that was not included in the final list of study facilities. The choice of North Wollo was primarily due to security and logistical considerations, as it was one of the few accessible conflict-affected zones at the time of preparation. To avoid contamination, data from the pre-test facility were excluded from the sample for analysis. The data collection took place from September 21, 2023, to March 30, 2024, using Kobo Toolbox, an intuitive and powerful software for collecting, analyzing, monitoring, and managing survey data. Twenty midwife data collectors, all of whom held a bachelor’s degree, seven zonal supervisors, and one regional coordinator, received ten days of training on approaching women and conducting face-to-face interviews with participants. Consistency and quality of survey responses were maintained by assigning data collectors to study facilities that matched their local language fluency, such as Amharic or Afaan Oromo. Women aged 13–49 who received care for induced or incomplete abortions at study facilities were invited for interviews after providing written consent. Field assistants conducted interviews, with data quality monitored daily by central and regional teams. Continuous feedback ensured consistency and corrections during data collection.

Ethical Considerations.

The ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the University of Gondar. The support letters were obtained from the Amhara Public Health Institute, Zonal, and Woreda health offices. The study was conducted under the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research involving human subjects. The IRB granted us to take informed consent from participants aged 13–49 years. Written informed consent was obtained after presenting the study’s aims and objectives to all participants, including emancipated minors, in accordance with the national research ethics guideline. These emancipated minors, typically under the age of 18 but working, earning their own living, married, or parenting, were eligible to provide consent, as approved by the IRB [28]. Special ethical consideration was given to emancipated minors, with additional safeguards such as confidentiality, post-abortion contraceptive counseling, and private settings during interviews, ensuring their protection throughout the study.

Data analysis.

The data were analyzed using Stata 18. The analysis aimed to: [1] provide descriptive statistics for the demographic characteristics of the women, and [2] examine the direct costs and opportunity costs involved in CAC. We also explore the costs by type of abortion (incomplete abortion or induced abortion), and by type of health facility (health centers, primary, general, and referral hospitals). Means and standard deviations (SD) were calculated to estimate each item in direct and opportunity costs. Both conditional means - the average of those who paid anything for the specific category - and unconditional means, the average of those who paid anything, including those who paid nothing for a particular category, were calculated. Descriptive statistics were computed as percentages for categorical variables.

Results

Sample characteristics.

Demographic characteristics of the women (N = 1,642) who participated in the survey are shown in Table 1. The majority (65.9%) presented with incomplete abortion, while 34.1% sought induced abortion. Medical abortion (MA) (40.8%) was the most common method, followed by MVA (37.8%) and both MA and MVA (21.3%). The overall post-abortion contraceptive uptake in the sample was 27.8% (Amare NS, Seyoum T, Tariku A, Mattison C, Klingberg Allvin M. Person-centered comprehensive abortion care provision and associated factors in northern Ethiopia: a cross-sectional study in a humanitarian setting, underview).

On average, the women were 27.5 years old (ranging from 13 to 48 years old). 79.0% of women were married, and 71.3% lived in an urban area. The majority (61.1%) was Orthodox, while 38.8% was Muslim. In terms of education, 38.8% of women did not have any formal education, 26.4% had primary education, and 14.3% had secondary education. Almost half of the women were housewives (46.7%), while the rest had a variety of occupations, including being a farmer (11.4%) or a student (8.95%), having a government (8.7%) or daily (8.6%) job, merchant (6.0%) or other (7.2%) and in private employment (0.6%) (Table 1).

Direct costs.

The direct costs for women and their caregivers, from their experience at home to the revisit of health facilities for complications, are reported in Table 2. In the sample, 3.2% of women self-reported having attempted to terminate the pregnancy at home by swallowing drugs (53.8%), inserting objects (28.8%), or consuming substances (13.5%). The majority of those who attempted (76.1%) paid by themselves, about ETB 1,624.1 (US$ 29.37). The average woman in the sample reported that the first health facility she visited was about 8.3 km away, but 44.9% were also transferred to another facility further away. In total, they spent ETB 195.4 (US$ 3.53) on transportation. At the health facilities, women spent ETB 297.4 (US$ 5.37) on food, ETB 405.7 on drugs (US$ 7.33), ETB 268.4 (US$ 4.85) on diagnostic tests, and ETB 108.0 (US$1.95) on other fees. Only 5.5% of them were covered by health insurance, so in practice, most women paid out of pocket to cover their costs associated with CAC. Additionally, 82.0% were accompanied by a caregiver, in most cases the husband or partner (67.0%), who spent 2.1 days with the woman at the health facilities. His stay was estimated to cost an additional ETB 621.9 (US$ 11.25). For those experiencing surgical procedure, 26.8% of women were also taken care of by someone in the household, who was paid in a few instances (3.4%) for a total of ETB 1,193.3 (US$ 21.58). 67.9% also spent on additional items, like sanitary products, for a total ETB 183.3 (US$ 3.31).

More than 8% of the women in the sample self-reported that they had a complication, and among those, only 34.6% had to go back to the health facility for a revisit. Women spent about ETB 206.1 (US$ 3.72) for transportation. They also spent on food (ETB 291.8, US$ 5.27), drugs (ETB 373.0, US$ 6.74), diagnostic test (ETB 166.2, US$ 3.0) and other fees (ETB 146.8, US$ 2.65). For the 65.2% of women who had a caregiver, an additional ETB 1,335.4 (US$ 24.15) was spent on their stay at the health facility. These costs were not significantly different from what women had to spend on their first visits. Thus, for women and their caregivers experiencing all these stages of CAC, the direct costs could total a maximum of ETB 7,416.8 (US$ 134.16). However, note that the majority of women self-reported to not have attempted a pregnancy termination at home (Panel A) and to not have experienced any complications, thus did not need to revisit (Panel D). Accounting only for the direct costs for the visit to the health facility (Panel B) and after the procedure (Panel C), the total would be less than half, at ETB 3,273.4 (US$ 59.21).

Opportunity costs

Table 3 outlines the opportunity costs for women and their caregivers, from their experience at home to revisiting health facilities for complications. Before the first visit to the health facility, 40.4% of women self-reported that they were unable to carry out normal activities for about 3.5 days. This resulted in an estimated income loss of ETB 279.2 (US$ 5.05) if those women were working at a low-skill wage. Women visiting health facilities spent about 1.8 days, thus being unable to work and losing ETB 142.6 (US$ 2.57). Similarly, 72.2% of the caregivers who accompanied the women were unable to work for about 4.2, adding loss of ETB 499.3 (US$ 9.03). Women who underwent a surgical procedure (MVA) were also unable to work for about 10.2 days, resulting in a total loss of ETB 808.5 (US$ 14.62). Finally, women spent an additional 1.2 days at the health facilities when complications arose, totaling ETB 91.8 (US$1.66) for days lost to work. Caregivers who accompanied women for a revisit for complications after the procedure also lost 1.7 days of work at ETB 202.7 (US$ 3.66). Thus, for women and their caregivers experiencing all these stages of CAC, the opportunity costs could total a maximum of ETB 2,024.1 ($36.61).

Direct and opportunity costs of women admitted for CAC at health facilities, by type of abortion and health care facility.

As noted in Table 2, 3.2% of women self-reported having attempted pregnancy termination before a visit to a healthcare facility. Therefore, we aim to better understand the costs encountered by women when accessing the health care service, by isolating primarily the costs associated with visits and re-visits to the health facilities. Table 4 categorized the direct costs as medical (drugs and diagnostic tests) and non-medical (transportation, food, other fees, and stay of caregivers) to highlight which costs are paid by the women rather than covered by the health system. It is noteworthy that about 27% of all direct costs encountered at the health facility are for medical supplies (ETB 1,213.3, US$ 21.94). We also identify other direct costs (ETB 3,000.7, US$ 54.28), which are costs encountered by women if they attempted pregnancy termination before visiting health facilities and at home after the surgical operation. Opportunity costs relate to the inability to work for both women and caregivers, with the majority (86.0%) associated with visits and re-visits to the health facility (ETB 1,744.9, US$ 31.56).

Discussion

This study estimated both the direct and opportunity costs incurred by women and their caregivers when seeking CAC services in the humanitarian context of Ethiopia’s Amhara Region. Our findings reveal that, despite Ethiopia’s policy that maternal and abortion services should be free, women continue to face substantial financial burdens when accessing care. On average, women paid considerable out-of-pocket costs, with a notable share spent on purchasing medical supplies, likely due to stockouts in public facilities. Costs were higher for women treated for incomplete abortion compared to those who underwent induced abortion. Non-medical costs were also particularly pronounced at general hospitals, while the opportunity costs of time for women and their caregivers during an initial visit were higher at referral hospitals, probably attributable to longer waiting times and more complex case management. We also observed significant non-medical expenditures at general hospitals and higher opportunity costs at referral hospitals, which are likely attributable to longer waiting times and more complex case management.

Firstly, costs remain significant despite legal exemptions [7]. We found that women incurred an average direct cost of ETB7,416.8 (US$ 134.16) when receiving the full spectrum of care for incomplete and induced abortion. Notably,27% of this cost (ETB 1,219.3; US$ 22.05) was spent on medical supplies, likely due to drug stockouts in public facilities that compelled women to purchase essential items from private providers at higher prices [29]. The direct costs for women in our study are higher than those reported earlier in Ethiopian (US$36.2) [20] and Rwandan (US$93) studies [5]. This discrepancy may be linked to disruptions in public health services, such as limited transportation and inconsistent drug availability, stemming from the ongoing conflict in the study area. Additionally, rising national inflation may be associated with increased prices for medications and other consumables, further elevating costs. Our results, however, align more closely with findings from Uganda (average annual post-abortion care cost per client: US$ 131.0) [30] and Colombia (median cost per client ranging from US$ 44 to US$ 141 for abortion complications, and US$ 189–213 for legal abortions at higher-level facilities) [27]. Like our findings, the latter study highlighted high out-of-pocket costs driven by medication stockouts, which forced women to seek care from private providers or pay for supplies themselves. A key distinction from previous studies is that earlier research, often based on interviews with healthcare providers, focused on estimating system-level costs. Our analysis is more recent and uniquely captures patient-level financial burden, providing a clear picture of the economic impact on women themselves. ‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬.

Secondly, insurance coverage was found to be very low, with only 5.5% of women reporting having insurance. This reflects ongoing implementation challenges with Ethiopia’s community-based health insurance scheme, particularly in rural or conflict-affected areas, resulting in high out-of-pocket costs. The low uptake of insurance increases financial vulnerability among women and undermines the goal of achieving universal health coverage. These findings align with previous analyses in Ethiopia, which highlight persistent challenges in financing “exempted” services such as maternal and abortion care, primarily due to facility-level budget shortfalls and weak reimbursement systems [13]. Expanding community-based insurance coverage and reducing logistical barriers could alleviate much of the financial burden associated with abortion care.

Thirdly, we also observed that abortion care imposes substantial opportunity costs, creating a hidden layer of financial burden. Women and their caregivers often lose income and productivity due to time away from work. These costs include foregone wages, time spent traveling, and time taken from household or caregiving responsibilities. Unlike direct medical expenses, such costs have rarely been examined in previous studies, which have largely focused on health system or patient out-of-pocket expenditures. In our study, women and caregivers each lost an average of 3–4 days of income-generating activities, amounting to ETB 2,024 (US$ 36.6). In comparison, modeled estimates from Uganda suggest higher opportunity costs, with patients losing an average of US$ 62 in productivity [31]. These differences may reflect cross-country variations in income levels, labor markets, and broader economic conditions. Importantly, our estimates are derived from women’s self-reported experiences rather than modeled projections, offering a more grounded perspective on the lived realities of abortion care. Productivity losses due to recovery time and repeated facility visits place significant pressure on household financial resources. Such costs can create substantial barriers to timely and equitable access to abortion care, particularly for low-income women. In many cases, these financial strains exacerbate existing health and economic inequalities, pushing families into debt or deeper poverty. The burdens are likely even more severe for women and girls in humanitarian settings, where pre-existing social and economic vulnerabilities are already acute.

Fourthly, we found that incomplete abortions were costlier than induced abortions. Women treated for incomplete abortion incurred an average of ETB 8,540.8 (US$ 154.5) compared to ETB 7,282.3 (US$ 131.7) for induced abortion. This finding is consistent with studies from Zambia [32], Burkina Faso [33], and Malawi [17]. The cost differential is likely explained by the more intensive clinical care required for incomplete abortions, including surgical interventions, longer hospital stays, and management of complications. These results highlight the broader economic implications of delayed or suboptimal abortion care, particularly in contexts where access to timely and comprehensive services is limited. They reinforce global evidence that unsafe abortions and treatment delays significantly increase both health system costs and household financial burdens. Expanding timely access to safe abortion services could therefore help prevent complications and reduce the associated economic strain.

Finally, costs vary by type of health facility. Women treated in general hospitals incurred the highest total costs (ETB 11,038.0, US$ 199.7), while costs were lowest at primary hospitals (ETB 7,635.8, US$ 138.1). However, this variation in total costs did not follow a linear pattern aligned with the hierarchical structure of the health system. A closer examination revealed significant differences in cost components. At general hospitals, non-medical direct costs were particularly high, underscoring the geographic and logistical barriers women encounter when seeking care. Similarly, higher opportunity costs at referral hospitals appeared to result from greater time losses for both clients and caregivers, likely driven by longer waiting times, more complex case management, and higher acuity of care that requires active caregiver involvement. These findings align with evidence from Colombia [27] and Malawi [17], which suggests that decentralizing CAC services to primary-level facilities can reduce both financial and access-related burdens. In Ethiopia, scaling up CAC services at health centers and primary hospitals, where costs are lower and services are more accessible, could help alleviate these burdens and promote more equitable access to care.

The findings have policy implications. First, the implementation gap in Ethiopia’s policy for free maternal and abortion services must be addressed. Facility-level shortages of drugs and supplies, inadequate insurance reimbursement mechanisms, and informal charges undermine the policy’s intended purpose. Second, tailored interventions are needed to subsidize opportunity costs for women and their caregivers, compensating for productivity loss through wage support. Third, stronger integration of CAC services at lower-level facilities could reduce the need for costly referrals and ease the financial burden on women and their households. Finally, our study results highlight the need to strengthen post-abortion family planning services, ensuring women have timely access to a full range of contraceptive options to prevent repeat unintended pregnancies and reduce future economic strain.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study.

This study has several limitations. First, our study is facility-based, which means it includes only women who sought care and may therefore differ socio-demographically from the general population. In addition, the study relies on self-reported data, which may be prone to recall bias, particularly concerning cost estimates, duration of activities such as travel time, days spent at the facility, and expenditures during the stay. Second, we were unable to capture non-monetary costs, including emotional distress, psychosocial burden, or long-term health consequences, which are important components of the overall cost burden of abortion care. Third, the standardized wage estimates from ILO may not accurately reflect actual foregone earnings in conflict-affected settings, where many women are housewives, students, or unpaid workers; as a result, our opportunity cost estimates may be biased upward, thus this assumption should be kept in mind when interpreting the results. Fourth, the facility selection was purposive, restricted to sites with higher case volumes in conflict-affected areas. As such, the findings may be subject to selection bias and may not fully represent lower-volume facilities or those in less-affected settings, thus affecting the interpretability of the results. Similarly, the study was conducted in conflict-affected areas of the Amhara Region, the findings may not be generalizable to more stable regions or other humanitarian contexts with different health system dynamics. Fourth, although our study examined women in humanitarian settings as a whole, financial burdens may be particularly significant for subgroups such as displaced women, adolescents, and housewives without independent income. Future research should include equity-focused cost analyses to identify which groups are most affected. Lastly, this study did not assess supply-side costs, which would provide a fuller picture of the economic burden of CAC from a health systems perspective.

Despite these limitations, this study has notable strengths. It is the first to provide a comprehensive cost assessment of CAC from the perspective of women and caregivers in a humanitarian setting in Ethiopia. The large sample size of 1,642 respondents across 31 health facilities enhances the reliability and representativeness of the findings. The detailed costing approach, which encompasses both direct and opportunity costs, provides critical insights into the multifaceted financial barriers faced by displaced women. Additionally, by disaggregating costs by type of abortion care and facility level, the study highlights key areas where policy interventions could reduce financial hardship and improve access to equitable care. More research is needed to identify cost-effective and context-appropriate strategies to reduce the financial burden of abortion care in humanitarian settings, including evaluating long-term impacts of delayed or inadequate access on women’s health and livelihoods. Future studies should also incorporate provider-side costs and explore how to sustainably deliver CAC services along with post-abortion family planning at lower-level facilities in conflict-affected regions, where health system disruptions and economic vulnerability are most severe.

Conclusion

Women in humanitarian settings of the Amhara Region continue to face substantial financial and opportunity costs when seeking CAC, despite the existence of a free-care policy. The burden is particularly pronounced for women with incomplete abortions and those treated at higher-level hospitals. To reduce these inequities, policy efforts should prioritize strengthening drug supply chains, expanding health insurance coverage, decentralizing CAC services to lower-level facilities, and addressing opportunity costs through targeted subsidies.
