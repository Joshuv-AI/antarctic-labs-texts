Testing of significant changes in big-five personality factors over time in the presence and absence of memory impairment and life-related stress
Authors: Katsuya Oi, Cleothia Frazier
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study tests whether memory impairment and perceived increases in stress due to life changes independently contribute to similar personality changes, such as increased neuroticism and decreased conscientiousness. Longitudinal data from 12,912 participants aged 50+ from the Health and Retirement Study (2006–2020) were analyzed using Latent Growth Curve Models (LGCMs). Six LGCMs were simultaneously estimated to examine how changes in personality across three data points spanning 8 years are predicted by both the classification of memory impairment (MI) statuses assessed via the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status and estimated increases in life stress. Decreases were observed in all Big Five personality factors (B = − 0.240/− 0.510, SE = 0.085/0.089) over the 8-year period. No significant differences in personality changes were found between MI cases and non-MI cases. Increased life stress was significantly associated with a rise in neuroticism (B = 0.587, SE = 0.094) and reductions in conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and openness (B ranges from − 0.223 to − 0.497). The findings emphasize adaptive/developmental shifts in personality in response to life stress rather than neuropathological ones related to memory impairment. Clinical assessment of personality changes in mid-to-late life should first consider psychological maladaptation to situational threats and challenges.

Introduction

A person’s alignments with the Big Five personality factors—neuroticism, openness, extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness—are increasingly recognized as predictors of the future likelihood of cognitive impairment and other symptoms of age-related neurological disorders1–3. Early studies linked late-life cognition to socio-economic conditions, socialization, and mobility4,5. For instance, conscientious individuals, who are often motivated and goal-oriented, tend to achieve greater academic and occupational success, which can enhance their cognitive development6–8. Greater reserve of cognitive health developed in earlier life, in turn, facilitates resilience against impairment in later life9. Conversely, high levels of neuroticism can be sources of constant emotional disruptions in social, private, and professional settings, limiting one’s opportunity to develop and maintain cognitive abilities in mid-to-late life10,11.

Older adults who are more conscientious and less neurotic consistently show better cognitive patterns, particularly in memory. Not only do they perform better on memory tests compared to the population average, but they also exhibit superior retention of new information12,13 and are less likely to develop memory impairment14,15. Instead of personality formation in earlier life, recent research examines shifts in personality in later life as potential clinical signs of neuropathological conditions2,3. Regardless of individual personality alignments that previously developed as adults, a heightened risk of neuropathological conditions such as Alzheimer's disease is known to coincide with decreased conscientiousness and increased neuroticism in mid-to-late life16. However, the exact nature of the link between these personality changes and memory impairment, a common preclinical symptom of many age-related neurodegenerative disorders, remains contested1,2,17–19.

To shed new light on the neuropathological origins of personality changes in mid-to-late life, this study examines and contrasts changes in the Big Five Factors among individuals aged 50 and older, between those with and without memory impairment. If certain personality changes are etiologically linked to memory impairment, one can expect characteristic changes in the Big Five factors, such as increased neuroticism and decreased conscientiousness, to be more pronounced in the presence of memory impairment. To augment this study’s insight, we introduce upticks in stress that mid-to-late life adults perceive due to changes in their life circumstances (i.e., life stress) as naturally occurring stimuli that can non-pathologically induce similar personality changes20–22. We then confirm if any personality changes indicative of neuropathologic conditions can be attributed to the presence of memory impairment, regardless of life stress changes that are observed in the same period. In addition to providing new evidence that further implicates the neuropathological nature of certain personality changes, this study aims to evaluate the salience of life stress in mid-to-late life personality development, newly within the context of memory impairment.

Personality changes in later life

Some prior studies have attempted to unearth neuropathological changes in personality by correlating the Big Five factors to the traces of Tau proteins in the brain, a marker of Alzheimer’s disease17,19. In parallel, others linked the Big Five factors with physiological characteristics of the brain, such as lower volumes of prefrontal and medial temporal regions and larger dorsolateral prefrontal cortices23,24. These studies consistently hint at increased neuroticism and decreased conscientiousness as prominently characteristic of impeding/existing neuropathological conditions. At the same time, prior studies by large do not consider other sources of personality changes that older individuals may undergo non-pathologically.

In a separate body of personality research, alignment shifts in the Big Five factors during mid-to-late life are understood as psychological adaptation and adjustments to changes in self and one’s surroundings due to age, referred to as adaptive personality development25,26. In this framework, the preservation of less neurotic and more conscientious/agreeable dispositions into mid-to-late life is considered beneficial for cognitive health27. A reversal of positive personality development can occur, particularly when constraints are perceived to become so great that one finds themselves in a constant state of feeling vulnerable, exhausted, and frustrated25. These sentiments typically accompany many age-related changes, often instilling a diminished sense of self-relevance among older adults20. This study extends the adaptive perspective on personality changes by testing whether increased life stress and memory impairment independently contribute to similar personality changes that involve increased neuroticism and decreased conscientiousness.

Life stress framework of personality changes

Life stress refers to ongoing situations of any losses, which can concern not only the self but also interpersonal relationships, work, and overall housing/financial situations. In motivating life stress as a predictor of personality changes, this study conceptualizes it as a proxy for a negative evaluative assessment of situational threats and challenges, rather than psycho-neural changes in the brain that likely follow prolonged exposure to psychological stress28. To be sure, both mechanisms of changes are plausibly represented in self-reports of life stress. However, from the developmental perspective, a reversal of positive personality development, as seen in increased neuroticism and decreased conscientiousness and agreeableness, is distinctly social cognitive rather than neuropathological, stemming primarily from the persistent perception that overall life situations are becoming more challenging/threatening. We argue that it is this affective perception of the appraised reality that can deal a devastating blow to one’s self-relevance to the world and often prompt a maladaptation of the self29,30. Compelling evidence in support of this view comes from the most recent meta-analysis of existing evidence linking stress and personality change, reporting that increased neuroticism and decreased conscientiousness/agreeableness concern a subjectively negative appraisal of stress rather than stress responses observed in the body21.

Existing personality research readily provides theoretical predictions as to how increased life stress may induce maladaptive/reversal shifts away from positive personality dispositions. When life stress is appraised to become more threatening/challenging than one can cope, negativity in one's worldview amplifies, destabilizing emotional control and potentially increasing neuroticism22,31,32. Coping with perceived losses of stability and emotional/financial resources leads to impulsive actions to compensate, compromising the ability to plan effectively (i.e., becoming less conscientious)21,33. Such challenges and threats may also lead to social disengagement, withdrawal, and interpersonal abrasiveness (i.e., becoming less agreeable and extraverted)34–36. It is uncertain how openness in a person shifts in the face of looming threats and challenges to self—they might cause individuals to narrow their conscious calculus of choices or, alternatively, engage in new high-risk behaviors such as substance dependence, as a coping mechanism37.

Empirically, previous research has not explicitly examined how life stress affects personality changes across all Big Five factors in mid-to-late life individuals with varying memory impairment statuses. However, this evidence is crucial for synthesizing existing knowledge of personality changes in both neuropathological and adaptive contexts13,33 especially in the context of socio-cognitive aging38. The inclusion of life stress is substantively warranted when assessing the neurological alteration of the Big Five personality factors in the presence of memory impairment. The subjective assessment of life stress, defined here as situational threats and challenges in life, can prompt a reversal of positive personality development, and its characteristic changes in personality overlap with known neurological signs of personality changes, including increased neuroticism and decreased conscientiousness.

The analytic advantage of including life stress in this study is to novelly isolate personality changes associated with memory impairment from a growing perception that life is becoming more stressful to manage. Increased life stress thus represents naturally occurring and non-pathological stimuli of similar personality changes that become increasingly common with age. This study does not attempt to assert, however, whether personality changes precede or result from increased life stress or memory impairment. Those who struggle with memory or life situations may be forced to adapt. Conversely, it is also plausible that changes in personality create new challenges with memory and life27,29.

Hypotheses

Without being attributed to life stress or memory impairment, a reversal of positive personality development is broadly considered part of aging because threats and challenges to the self can stem from any perceived loss39. In partial support of this view that sees aging as net psychological losses rather than gains, the most recent study reports decreases in all Five-Factors over time among individuals aged 50 and above without any signs of memory impairment2. As such, the first analytic goal of this study is to replicate these findings by confirming the following hypothesis.H1: In the period free of memory impairment, all Five-Factors are self-rated as significantly decreased over time.

H1: In the period free of memory impairment, all Five-Factors are self-rated as significantly decreased over time.

With these expected changes in personality as reference points of personality changes in the absence of memory impairment, we then examine how personality changes diverge in the presence of memory impairment, as specified in the second hypothesis.H2(a-b): For those who were initially memory-impaired or developed over the period, (a) conscientiousness decreases, and (b) neuroticism increases more greatly over time.

H2(a-b): For those who were initially memory-impaired or developed over the period, (a) conscientiousness decreases, and (b) neuroticism increases more greatly over time.

We then explored the final set of hypotheses specifying that H2 holds true regardless of life stress, although increased life stress itself coincides with similar personality changes.H3: H2(a-b) holds regardless of life stress at baseline and its changes over time.H4(a-b): Increased life stress is associated with at least one of the following changes in personality over the same period: (a) increased neuroticism, and (b) decreased openness, agreeableness, conscientiousness, and extraversion.

H3: H2(a-b) holds regardless of life stress at baseline and its changes over time.

H4(a-b): Increased life stress is associated with at least one of the following changes in personality over the same period: (a) increased neuroticism, and (b) decreased openness, agreeableness, conscientiousness, and extraversion.

Data and methods

The ethics declarations

The data used in this study are publicly available from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) at the University of Michigan (see Data Availability Statement). The Northern Arizona University Institutional Review Board (IRB) reviewed methods of reporting findings based on these data in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The IRB granted a waiver of ethical parameters and approval, including additional informed consent for the HRS study participants for this study, under project number 1628997-1.

Data description

The Health and Retirement Study (HRS) has been collecting biennial data since 1992, establishing itself as the U.S.'s longest-running longitudinal study. The use of data it gathers data from a nationally representative sample of individuals aged 50 and older living in non-institutional settings. In 2006, approximately half of the 15,000 HRS participants were randomly selected to complete the Psychosocial and Lifestyle questionnaire, which periodically assessed their life stress and alignments with the Big-Five personality factors40. This group completed the questionnaire every four years, corresponding to every two HRS waves (2006, 2010, 2014, and 2018). The other half started in 2008 and continued every four years (2008, 2012, 2016, and 2020). Aligning baseline years (2006/2008) for these panel members was essential for examining changes over a fixed period and ensuring comparability with previous research on intra-individual personality changes2,13. Among the psychological panel, the final sample of 12,921 individuals were included in this study as they meet the following criteria: having been available for MI assessment at least twice during the period, not missing any information on control variables (see below) and having their personality and life stress at least once.

The data were reorganized to reflect successive HRS years from which personality and life stress measurements were repeatedly taken post-baseline, denoted as ith year points (i0, i4, i8), excluding the 12th-year data point due to limited availability to ensure non-missing rates above 40% at any given observation of each construct. This reorganization allowed tracking of life stress and personality factors up to three times for each participant at four-year intervals, regardless of their starting HRS year: 2006 denoted as i0, in 2010 as i4, and then in 2014 as i8. For instance, consider a participant whose personality and life stress were first assessed in 2006 (i0) and last in 2014 (i8).

During the observation period (i0 to i8), participants took the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status-modified (TICS-m) in each HRS Wave. We used individual scores of 12–27 to identify respondents as non-memory impaired (non-MI) and scores below 12 as memory impaired (MI). This classification of individual TICS scores aligns with the Langa-Weir method, which derives three statuses: “Normal” (12–27), “Cognitively Impaired but not Demented” (7–11), and “Demented” (0–6)41,42. Following the convention of previous studies43,44, we used the 12-point cut-off to identify those with a wide range of clinical risks implied by the TICS-m scores between 0 and 11, and those without any indication of memory impairment by scoring 12 or above on the TICS-m.

To address both random and non-random missing data patterns over i0 through i8 years, the Full-Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) method in Mplus version 8.8 was used. The FIML method addressed missing observations by using placeholders and longitudinal survey weights eliminating the need to impute missing observations45. This approach is capable of accounting for missing non-random conditions by adjusting estimates rather than predicting missing observations, with all variables included in the model and also with so-called auxiliary variables46. Variables are defined as auxiliary when used not to predict estimates but rather to adjust them for potentially systemic missingness. This study did not use any auxiliary variables in the final analysis, as conclusions drawn from the full sample were consistent with those from a listwise deletion sample for life stress and personality (See Table S3). This indicates that missing data is likely at random between the life stress and personality of those who remained in the study for the entire period and that of those who did not, conditioned on the other variables included in the analyses.

Personality traits

The HRS conducted an extensive item analysis for each set of items designed to measure each Big-Five factor40. For this study, we quantified each of the five dimensions by averaging responses within each set. Subsequent item analysis shows that agreeableness (a = 0.79) and openness (a = 0.79) are most reliable, while conscientiousness (a = 0.70) and neuroticism (a = 0.70) have the lowest level of internal consistency40. On a scale of one to four, the personality index created for each trait quantifies how strongly respondents are aligned with the trait. On the original scale of 1 to 4, higher (positive) values indicate a stronger tendency (e.g., more agreeable, more neurotic); lower (negative) scores indicate a weaker tendency.

The use of repeated assessments makes individual MI statuses prone to practice effects towards false negatives (i.e., taking the TICS-m multiple times tends to improve their scores)47. To mitigate this type of practice effect, we created the fourth category to isolate cases that show an improvement above the threshold at any point during the observation from the rest.

The TICS-m scores range from 0 to 27 and represent the sum of scores from three separate tasks: recalling words (out of 20), subtracting a number (out of 5), and counting a number backwards (out of 2). The immediate and delayed recall tasks involved asking respondents to first recall a set of 10 English words read to them by the interviewers (immediate recall) and then to recall the same set after a five-minute interval with unrelated questions. The number of correctly recalled words out of 20 was counted as their sub-score. By design, different sets of words were read out at each time of assessment to mitigate practice effects of remembering the list from the previous assessment. The subtracting task requires participants to subtract 7 from 100 and repeat the process five times. The scoring is based on the number of correct results out of the five trials, with possible scores ranging from 0 to 5. The third task involves counting backward from 20 as fast as possible, and the performance is rated on a scale of 0, 1, or 2. A score of 1 is given when the participant successfully completes the second trial, a score of 2 is assigned for successful completion of both trials, and 0 otherwise.

Life stress

In this study, life stress refers to ongoing stressful situations that broadly involve inter-personal relationships, work, and overall housing/financial situations. In the same psycho-social module that assess their personality, HRS respondents were also asked to indicate if they were experiencing a set of ongoing and current stressful situations that lasted for 12 months or more. They could respond with “no” (coded as 0, indicating it didn't happen) or rate the level of distress for each situation on a 3-point scale. A rating of “1” meant they experienced the situation but found it not upsetting, while a rating of “2” indicated that it was somewhat upsetting, and a rating of “3” meant it was very upsetting. The six situations that the respondents were asked to rate included: alcohol or drug use by a family member, work, finances, housing situations, close relationships, and caregiving. Following the conventional use of these measures35,43, we calculated the unweighted sum of the ratings for each participant so that higher index values mean greater life stress.

Controls

In establishing the relevance of life stress to personality changes rather than explaining it, control variables are limited to demographic variables that were used in prior studies of HRS data2, including age at baseline, years of formal schooling (centered at 12 years), race/ethnicity (black, Hispanic, and white respondents), and sex (female and male).

The time function f(i) dictates how the latent slope numerically translates to i-year specific changes. Conventionally it is assumed to be linear so that the slope represents a numeric change value per year after baseline, but this assumption has been questioned in the context of this study 14. Instead, we used a "free" form for the time function f(i), implementable via Structural Equation Modeling, not Hierarchical Linear Modeling often used in prior research 48. A “free” time function f(i) consists of coefficients representing estimated proportions of net change at each year-mark point (0 at i0 and 1 at i8), with β1j quantifying the net change over 8 years. Whether assumed as linear or estimated as free, β1j of the Big-Five factors in this study is comparable to those from the most recent and relevant past study of HRS data, which reported the net change of each personality factor over 10 years 2.

Testing of the hypotheses

To test the first two hypotheses, Xj in Eqs. 2 and 3 are extended to include MI statuses (“MI”, “non-MI to MI”, “MI to non-MI”, with “non-MI” as the reference). Per the first hypothesis (H1), the constant coefficients, γ10(N, O, E, A, C), are expected to be negative for all Big-Five factors. With the MI statuses included as covariates predicting the latent intercept (γ0memory(N, O, E, A, C)) and slope (γ1memory(N, O, E, A, C)), these constant coefficients reference average estimates of change in each respective factor for those who were classified as “non-MI” status, indicating the extent of personality change over the MI-free period of 8 years. The second hypothesis (H2a-b) specifies γ1memory(N) to be positive (increased neuroticism) and γ1memory(C) to be negative (decreased conscientiousness) for the “MI” and/or “non-MI to MI” groups compared to the “non-MI” group.

Discussion

Recent research has increasingly focused on intra-individual personality changes that accompany Alzheimer's disease and dementia in the pre-diagnostic/progressive stage38,49. While ongoing studies are primarily dedicated to understanding the physiological mechanisms linking personality changes to cognitive impairment, our work contributes perspectives on adaptive development, a relatively understudied facet of mid-to-late life changes in the Big-Five factors20,21,50.

Grounded in existing research2, this study’s support for H1 confirms significant decreases in all Big-Five factors over time for those whose memory is not impaired. Prior studies reported relatively minor changes in personality over time without memory or any cognitive impairment2,14,51,52. Unstandardized estimates of changes, although not reported in tables, corroborate those findings. For example, using similar data sources and designs, changes in each factor (on the scale of 1–4) among the cognitively healthy in a past study2 and ours were estimated as follows, respectively: extraversion (b = − 0.10, SE = 0.02 vs. b = − 0.065, SE = 0.01); agreeableness (b = − 0.11, SE = 0.02 vs. b = − 0.035, SE = 0.011); conscientiousness (b = − 0.12, SE = 0.02 vs. b = − 0.084, SE = 0.01); neuroticism (b = 0.04, SE = 0.02 vs. 0.072, SE = 0.011); and openness (b = − 0.06, SE = 0.02 vs. b = − 0.096, SE = 0.011)2. It is worth noting that in both studies, the extent of these changes was consistently small (e.g., − 0.1 out of 4), implying a relatively high level of stability for all Big-Five factors, with time accounting for a substantial portion of the relatively small variability of changes experienced by the participants.

To our surprise, we found no support for significant changes in personality associated with MI statuses, with or without life stress considered (H2a-b and H3 not supported). Increased neuroticism (0.064) and decreased conscientiousness (− 0.040) at baseline for the “non-MI to MI” group imply that those personality characteristics are likely precursors of neuropathological symptoms such as memory decline3,15. At the same time, MI classification by TICS-m assessment may be limited in predicting personality changes attributed to underlying neurodegenerative conditions. Auxiliary analysis (not shown) that did not isolate the “MI to non-MI” group from the rest found significantly increased neuroticism and decreased conscientiousness for the MI group. Future research is urged to better discern false negative biases from true cases of improvement into non-MI status.

By confirming Hypothesis 4a-b, our study reveals a strong link between changes in life stress and self-reported personality shifts. We found stress-related associations across all Big-Five personality factors, ranging from moderate (e.g., a − 0.223 correlation with agreeableness) to strong (e.g., a 0.587 correlation with neuroticism). These findings extend previous research on adaptive personality development in response to stress20,21,33 in the context of memory decline, newly demonstrating profound impacts of perceived threats and challenges in life that can reverse positive personality development regardless of memory impairment. Increased neuroticism and decreased conscientiousness are often seen in the early stages of dementia and Alzheimer’s disease3,53, but need to be assessed with attention to one’s personality and life situations. Otherwise, rated changes in personality by a person can be misconstrued as a neuropathological symptom when, in fact, they are indicative of an internal tumultuous tension between self and life2,3.

Extending the existing framework, this study explores how life stress can lead individuals to reassess and shift their self-perceptions of personality by projecting changes in their circumstances directly onto themselves35. For instance, work-related challenges could prompt individuals to reconsider their level of agreeableness with others, and new financial difficulties might lead to a reassessment of their conscientiousness or their foresight33. This process does not imply that stress directly leads to personality shifts. Instead, the perception of personality changes, whether due to natural variation or adaptive responses to stress, can profoundly impact relationships, employment, and other critical aspects of life54,55.

Increased life stress may trigger neurological and psychological responses that weaken emotional regulation and interpersonal skills, suggesting a feedback loop, where stress exacerbates the erosion of interpersonal traits, which in turn, can increase perceived stress. In scenarios where pathological conditions such as Alzheimer's disease or dementia prompt changes in personality, these alterations become particularly noticeable to observers in interpersonal situations that likely involve them. This heightened awareness among observers could be due to their direct involvement in the stressful interpersonal situations that these personality shifts exacerbate, such as increased neuroticism and decreased extraversion52,53. Consequently, observers, rather than the individuals themselves, may become more alert to these changes, recognizing them as potential indicators of underlying pathological conditions.

This observation could explain why changes in these specific traits are often more emphasized by both observers and patients of Alzheimer’s disease in prior research52,53, while this study did not find any significant change in personality attributable to MI statuses. It suggests that the noticeable impact of personality shifts in interpersonal contexts, prompted not by neuropathological conditions like Alzheimer's disease or dementia but by psychological maladaptation, may still alert those around the affected individuals to a clinical phenomenon.

Interpersonal traits such as agreeableness, conscientiousness, openness, and extraversion were found to be positively correlated (Supplemental Table S1), consistent with prior research utilizing varied analytical approaches56. Neuroticism, however, was less associated with these other traits, which supports the notion of unique etiological connections between cognitive functions and neuroticism, unlike with other personality dimensions57. This distinction underscores that life stress impacts memory and personality independently, with a notable and consistent effect on openness, conscientiousness, and agreeableness, which were estimated as not changed for those without memory impairment when experiencing no increase in stress (γ10 not significant in Table 4).

Interpreting these results requires mindfulness of several limitations. The focus of the TICS-m on episodic and working memory narrows the scope of our understanding of personality changes, potentially excluding other cognitive aspects impacted by dementia or Alzheimer's disease. The paucity of data points for tracking personality and stress, alongside limited statistical power, challenges our ability to distinguish between normal aging processes and those unique to the oldest-old population, who are at a heightened risk for cognitive impairments. Furthermore, the methodology employed in this study, which consolidates various stress factors into a single comprehensive index, deserves consideration.

While the Big-Five personality framework shows potential as a tool for screening cognitive impairments, these findings urge caution in using personality shifts as standalone diagnostic markers. Life events that induce stress, even those not considered pathological, can prompt adaptive changes in personality that resemble dementia or Alzheimer's symptoms, thereby complicating diagnostic clarity. This emphasizes the importance of adopting holistic assessment strategies that integrate psychological and biological considerations to accurately evaluate cognitive health.
