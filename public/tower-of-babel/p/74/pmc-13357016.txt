The Impact of Adjunctive Acupressure on Postoperative Infection and Hospital Stay in Wagner Grade 2–3 Diabetic Foot Ulcers: A Propensity Score‐Stratified Cohort Study
Authors: Conghui Shi, Ya Qi, Yaoguo Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiabetic foot ulcer (DFU) is a severe complication characterised by poor healing. Although adjunctive therapies like acupressure may improve outcomes, controlled evidence remains limited. This study evaluated whether acupressure reduces postoperative infection rates and hospital stay in patients with Wagner grade 2–3 DFUs. This single‐center retrospective cohort study included eligible patients undergoing surgical debridement and moist wound healing between 2018 and 2024. Patients receiving standardised acupressure (ST36, SP6, KI3) were compared to a standard care‐only cohort. To control confounders while maximising statistical power and generalizability, we employed an unstratified propensity score subclassification approach, dividing the full cohort into five quintiles. Primary outcomes were postoperative wound infection and hospital stay length. Secondary outcomes included antibiotic duration and re‐debridement rate. We analyzed the full cohort of 423 patients (acupressure group: 186; standard care group: 237). After adjusting for propensity score strata, the acupressure group exhibited significantly lower postoperative infection rates (unadjusted 16.7% vs. 32.1%, adjusted p = 0.001) and shorter hospital stays (unadjusted median 10.0 vs. 12.0 days; adjusted mean difference −2.1 days, 95% CI −3.4 to −0.8, p = 0.002). Systemic antibiotic duration was significantly reduced (unadjusted median 6.0 vs. 8.0 days, adjusted p = 0.003), alongside a clinically relevant decrease in re‐debridement rates (9.1% vs. 17.3%, adjusted p = 0.057). Doubly robust multivariate regression confirmed adjunctive acupressure as an independent protective factor against postoperative infection (adjusted OR 0.47, 95% CI 0.28–0.80, p = 0.005), with interaction tests demonstrating consistent benefits across all propensity score subclasses. Adjunctive acupressure significantly reduces postoperative infection risk and shortens hospitalisation in Wagner 2–3 DFU patients. Retaining the full cohort via propensity score subclassification strengthens generalizability, suggesting acupressure as a safe, effective, and low‐cost complementary therapy for standard wound care.

Introduction

Diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) represents one of the most severe and debilitating complications of diabetes mellitus, posing a significant global health challenge [1]. The worldwide prevalence of DFU among diabetic patients is estimated at 6.3%, with notable regional variations and a higher incidence in males and individuals with Type 2 diabetes [1]. The clinical trajectory of DFU is often severe, leading to devastating outcomes. Approximately 20% of moderate to severe diabetic foot infections result in lower‐limb amputation, and the five‐year mortality rate following a diabetes‐related amputation can exceed 70% [2]. Beyond the profound impact on patient morbidity and mortality, DFU imposes a colossal economic burden on healthcare systems worldwide, with annual costs for DFU‐related care rivalling those of many common cancers [2, 3]. This immense clinical and financial toll underscores the urgent need for more effective management strategies.

Despite advances in wound care, the standard treatment protocols for DFU face significant limitations that compromise long‐term patient outcomes. A primary challenge is the exceptionally high rate of ulcer recurrence, with a global pooled estimate of 22.1% per person‐year, indicating that nearly a quarter of healed ulcers recur within a year [4]. Furthermore, a substantial portion of these ulcers fail to heal with conventional methods, transitioning into a chronic, non‐healing state that elevates the risk of infection, amputation, and mortality [5]. The management of these complex wounds is often a protracted and resource‐intensive process. The clinical challenge is compounded by the lack of reliable, externally validated models to predict recurrence risk, which hinders the implementation of targeted preventive measures for high‐risk individuals [6]. These unaddressed gaps in the current standard of care highlight the necessity of exploring innovative and accessible adjunctive therapies.

Acupressure, a therapeutic modality rooted in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), is emerging as a promising adjunctive treatment for DFU. It is a non‐invasive, low‐cost technique that involves applying physical pressure to specific acupoints on the body [7]. The therapeutic rationale for its application in DFU is based on its potential to address key pathophysiological aspects of impaired wound healing. Evidence suggests that acupressure and related stimulation therapies may improve local microcirculation, enhance tissue oxygenation, and modulate pathways of oxidative stress and inflammation, which are known to be dysregulated in diabetic wounds [7, 8]. Furthermore, its safety, affordability, and the potential for patient self‐administration make it an attractive option for the long‐term management of chronic conditions like DFU, where treatment adherence and cost are significant concerns [7, 9].

Given the substantial limitations of current DFU management and the compelling therapeutic rationale of acupressure, there is a clear imperative to formally investigate its clinical utility. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of acupressure as an adjunctive therapy in improving healing outcomes for patients with diabetic foot ulcers. By exploring a safe, cost‐effective, and accessible intervention, this research seeks to address a critical unmet need in diabetic care and contribute to reducing the global burden of DFU.

Methods

Study Design

This single‐center retrospective cohort study was designed to evaluate the effect of adjunctive acupoint massage on postoperative infection rates and the length of hospital stay in patients with Wagner grade 2–3 DFU who underwent debridement and dressing changes based on the moist wound healing theory. We retrospectively collected and analyzed data from the Electronic Medical Record (EMR) system of the Beijing Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine Affiliated to Capital Medical University (Approval No: 2018BL‐061‐02), encompassing all eligible patients hospitalised from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2024. The study protocol was formally approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of our hospital. A waiver of the requirement for individual informed consent was granted by the IRB due to the retrospective nature of the research, which relied exclusively on the analysis of de‐identified pre‐existing clinical data and posed no additional risk to patients. This study was conducted in strict accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and all data were anonymized prior to analysis to ensure patient confidentiality.

Study Population

The study cohort was retrospectively identified by querying the institutional EMR system for all adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) admitted to the endocrinology or wound care departments between 1 January 2018, and 31 December 2024. The query utilised International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes for type 2 diabetes with foot ulcer (E11.621) and relevant procedure codes for surgical wound debridement. Potential participants were screened through a three‐stage validation process: (1) an initial automated data extraction based on predefined criteria; (2) a manual review of each EMR by two independent clinical researchers to confirm eligibility and extract data using a standardised case report form; and (3) final adjudication by a senior attending physician for any discrepancies in eligibility assessment.

Inclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus according to the American Diabetes Association standards [10]; (2) presence of a diabetic foot ulcer classified as Wagner Grade 2 or 3 [11]; (3) documented receipt of surgical debridement and subsequent wound management based on moist wound healing principles as the primary treatment; and (4) complete medical records with available data on demographics, baseline characteristics, laboratory results, and nursing care logs. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) presence of severe peripheral artery disease, defined as an ankle‐brachial index (ABI) < 0.5 or requiring urgent revascularization procedures [12]; (2) diagnosis of systemic infection or sepsis upon admission, as defined by the Sepsis‐3 international consensus [13]; (3) documented treatment with negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT), growth factors, skin substitutes, or other advanced wound therapies during hospitalisation; (4) a prolonged hospital stay primarily due to non‐DFU‐related complications (e.g., acute myocardial infarction, stroke, or major surgery); (5) a diagnosis of type 1 diabetes, gestational diabetes, or other specific types of diabetes; and (6) records with critical missing data, particularly regarding infection markers, antibiotic usage, or the implementation details of acupressure nursing.

Group Assignment and Bias Control

Patients were retrospectively assigned to one of two groups based on a detailed review of electronic nursing care records. The case group (Acupressure Group) consisted of patients who, in addition to standard care, had documented evidence of receiving a standardised, adjunctive acupressure therapy. The acupuncture protocol targeted specific acupoints based on the Chinese edition of the WHO Standard Acupuncture Point Locations in the Western Pacific Region [14]. This protocol was explicitly defined for chart review purposes as manual pressure applied to the Zusanli (ST36), Sanyinjiao (SP6), and Taixi (KI3) acupoints, administered twice daily for 10–15 min per session. The control group (Standard Care Group) comprised patients who met all eligibility criteria and received standard surgical debridement and moist wound care but had no documentation of receiving the specified acupressure therapy in their nursing records.

A sample size estimation was conducted to ensure the study was adequately powered to detect a clinically significant difference in the primary outcome of postoperative infection. Based on a review of institutional data and relevant literature, the infection rate in the standard care group was estimated to be approximately 35% [15]. We hypothesised that adjunctive acupressure could reduce this rate to 20%. Using a two‐sided alpha of 0.05 and a power of 80%, the calculation indicated that a minimum of 119 patients per group was required. Therefore, the inclusion of our full cohort of 423 eligible patients provided robust statistical power to evaluate the treatment effect.

To mitigate selection bias and control for confounding variables inherent in this retrospective design without sacrificing sample size or limiting generalizability, a propensity score subclassification approach was employed, as described by Rosenbaum and Rubin [16, 17, 18]. A multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to predict the probability (propensity score) of a patient receiving acupressure therapy. The model incorporated the following clinically relevant baseline covariates known to influence the outcomes: age, sex, duration of diabetes, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) level, Wagner grade (2 vs. 3) [11], ulcer area at admission, body mass index (BMI), presence of key comorbidities (hypertension, chronic kidney disease, peripheral neuropathy), and baseline laboratory values including white blood cell (WBC) count and C‐reactive protein (CRP). Based on the calculated propensity scores, the entire cohort of 423 patients was divided into five ordinal categories (quintiles). This unstratified propensity score analysis allows for the estimation of treatment effects within more homogeneous subclasses while retaining all eligible patients, thereby maximising precision and ensuring the findings are representative of the overall target hospital population.

Data Collection and Study Variables

The primary and secondary outcomes were defined and adjudicated based on pre‐specified criteria. The primary outcome of postoperative wound infection was defined according to the 2023 IWGDF/IDSA guidelines as the presence of purulent discharge or at least two classic signs of inflammation (erythema, local warmth, swelling/induration, or pain/tenderness) developing at the surgical site after the index debridement [11]. The co‐primary outcome, total length of hospital stay, was calculated as the number of days from admission to discharge. Secondary outcomes included the total duration of systemic antibiotic use, measured in days, and the rate of second surgical debridement, defined as any unplanned return to the operating room for further wound debridement due to inadequate infection control or progression of tissue necrosis.

A comprehensive set of covariates was collected to adjust for potential confounding factors. These variables included: (1) demographics: age and sex; (2) clinical baseline characteristics: body mass index (BMI), duration of diabetes, and baseline glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c); (3) ulcer‐specific characteristics: Wagner grade (2 vs. 3), anatomical location, and ulcer area at admission (cm2); (4) baseline laboratory values: serum albumin, creatinine (used to calculate estimated glomerular filtration rate, eGFR), white blood cell (WBC) count, and C‐reactive protein (CRP); (5) comorbidities and overall health status: documented history of hypertension, coronary artery disease, peripheral arterial disease, and peripheral neuropathy. To provide a standardised measure of the overall comorbidity burden for each patient, the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was calculated based on data from the admission records [19].

Data Quality Control

To ensure the consistency and reliability of the data, several quality control measures were implemented. The general data extraction from the EMR was performed by two clinical researchers who were blinded to the study's primary objectives. They used a shared, standardised electronic data capture form with embedded range and logic checks to minimise entry errors.

The reliability of key semi‐objective baseline variables was specifically assessed. For ulcer area, measurements were independently performed by two researchers based on digital wound photographs taken at admission and stored in the EMR, using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). The Wagner grade was independently assigned by the same two researchers based on their review of the initial surgical consultation and operative notes. The inter‐observer reliability was evaluated in a random subsample of 40 patients. Excellent agreement was found for both measurements, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.96 (95% CI: 0.92–0.98) for ulcer area and a Cohen's kappa coefficient of 0.89 (95% CI: 0.81–0.97) for Wagner grade assignment. Discrepancies were resolved by a third senior wound care specialist.

Crucially, to minimise diagnostic ascertainment bias, the primary outcome of postoperative wound infection was adjudicated by a dedicated two‐person review committee composed of infectious disease specialists. These committee members were fully blinded to patient group assignments (Acupressure vs. Standard Care Group) and all other treatment details not pertinent to the infection diagnosis. They reviewed all relevant clinical notes, laboratory trends, and microbiology reports to determine whether the pre‐defined criteria for infection were met. In cases of disagreement, a final consensus was reached through discussion.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and a two‐sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Unadjusted baseline characteristics between the two groups in the full cohort were compared using the independent samples t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous data, and the chi‐square (χ2) test or Fisher's exact test for categorical data.

To evaluate the treatment effect while accounting for baseline confounding, outcomes were analyzed by incorporating the propensity score stratification. For dichotomous outcomes (e.g., postoperative wound infection, second surgical debridement), the Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel (CMH) test was used to estimate the overall association adjusted for the five propensity score quintiles. For continuous outcomes (e.g., length of hospital stay, antibiotic duration), comparisons were performed using generalised linear models adjusting for the propensity score stratum.

A multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed on the full cohort to calculate the adjusted odds ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) for the primary outcome of postoperative infection, with the propensity score quintile included as an adjusting covariate. To specifically address how the effect of the intervention varies across different probabilities of receiving treatment, we examined the estimated effect within each of the five propensity score subclasses. We formally tested for interaction between the treatment group and the subclass using multivariable logistic regression. In this interaction model, the propensity score quintile was entered as an ordinal continuous variable (coded 1 to 5) alongside the main effect terms to test for a linear trend in effect modification by the baseline propensity to receive treatment. Finally, to assess the consistency of the treatment effect across different patient populations, pre‐specified subgroup analyses were conducted based on key baseline characteristics, including Wagner grade (2 vs. 3), baseline HbA1c (≤ 8.5% vs. > 8.5%), age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years), and ulcer area (above vs. below the median). The potential for effect modification by these variables was tested by introducing an interaction term in the logistic regression model.

Results

Patient Screening Flow and Baseline Characteristics

A total of 848 adult patients were initially identified from the electronic medical record system using relevant diagnostic and procedure codes. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 423 eligible patients with Wagner Grade 2–3 DFU were included in the full cohort for analysis. Among them, 186 patients had documented receipt of standardised adjunctive acupressure (Acupressure Group) and 237 patients received standard care only (Standard Care Group). Instead of discarding unmatched data, we retained the entire eligible cohort of 423 patients to maximise statistical power and ensure generalizability. The propensity scores for all 423 patients were calculated, and the cohort was subclassified into five ordinal quintiles based on these scores. In the unadjusted univariate analysis, receipt of adjunctive acupressure was significantly associated with a lower odds of postoperative infection (unadjusted OR 0.42, 95% CI 0.26–0.68, p < 0.001). Additionally, larger ulcer area, higher Wagner grade (3 vs. 2), elevated baseline CRP, and a higher CCI were all significantly associated with increased odds of infection in the full cohort.

To rigorously control for confounding, a multivariable logistic regression model was constructed. This doubly robust model incorporated the primary exposure (acupressure) and the propensity score quintile as an adjusting covariate, alongside other significant variables from the univariate analysis (p < 0.10). After adjustment, adjunctive acupressure remained independently associated with a significantly reduced risk of postoperative infection (adjusted OR 0.47, 95% CI: 0.28–0.80, p = 0.005). Ulcer area and baseline CRP also retained independent significance in this final model.

Furthermore, following the unstratified propensity score analysis approach, we evaluated how the effect of adjunctive acupressure on wound infection varied across the different propensity score subclasses. The entire cohort of 423 patients was divided into five quintiles of approximately equal size (n = 84 or 85 per stratum) based on their probability of receiving acupressure. The unadjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were examined within each quintile, detailing the specific event counts and sample sizes. In the lowest propensity quintile (Quintile 1), the infection rate was 10.0% (2/20) in the acupressure group versus 21.5% (14/65) in the standard care group, yielding an OR of 0.38 (95% CI: 0.15–0.95). For Quintile 2, the rates were 10.0% (3/30) versus 23.6% (13/55), with an OR of 0.44 (95% CI: 0.18–1.05). In Quintile 3, the rates were 16.7% (6/36) versus 31.2% (15/48), corresponding to an OR of 0.48 (95% CI: 0.20–1.12). For Quintile 4, the rates were 20.0% (9/45) versus 35.0% (14/40), resulting in an OR of 0.55 (95% CI: 0.25–1.25). Finally, in the highest propensity quintile (Quintile 5), the infection rate was 20.0% (11/55) for acupressure compared to 34.5% (10/29) for standard care, with an OR of 0.61 (95% CI: 0.28–1.35). The protective effect of acupressure was consistently observed across all strata, with the point estimates for the OR consistently remaining below 1.0. A formal regression‐based test for interaction was conducted by entering the propensity score quintile as an ordinal continuous variable to evaluate linear trends in effect modification. This analysis yielded no statistically significant interaction effect (P for interaction = 0.215).

Subgroup Analysis: Consistency of the Treatment Effect

To examine the consistency of the observed treatment effect across clinically relevant patient subgroups, a prespecified subgroup analysis was performed for the primary outcome of postoperative wound infection using the full study cohort (N = 423). Within each subgroup, the OR for infection (Acupressure vs. Standard Care) was estimated using logistic regression models adjusted for the propensity score quintiles. The beneficial effect of adjunctive acupressure in reducing postoperative infection was consistent in direction across all predefined subgroups, with point estimates of the adjusted OR consistently remaining below 1.0. The effect appeared nominally stronger in patients with Wagner grade 2 ulcers (adjusted OR 0.38, 95% CI: 0.19–0.76) compared to those with grade 3 ulcers (adjusted OR 0.58, 95% CI: 0.31–1.08), although the test for interaction did not reach statistical significance (P for interaction = 0.224). Similarly, a trend towards a more pronounced effect was observed in patients with better glycemic control (HbA1c ≤ 8.5%; adjusted OR 0.41, 95% CI: 0.21–0.80) versus those with poorer control (HbA1c > 8.5%; adjusted OR 0.55, 95% CI: 0.28–1.07; P for interaction = 0.351). No significant effect modification was detected by age, diabetes duration, or the presence of peripheral neuropathy (all P for interaction > 0.05).

These results suggest that the protective association between adjunctive acupressure and lower postoperative infection risk is robust across key patient characteristics. The trends, while not statistically significant for interaction, indicate that patients with less severe ulcers (Wagner grade 2) and better baseline glycemic control might derive greater relative benefit from the adjunctive therapy.

Discussion

This study presents significant findings on the utility of adjunctive acupressure in the postoperative management of Wagner grade 2–3 DFU. The primary results, derived from the full cohort of 423 patients, indicate that the inclusion of acupressure alongside standard care is associated with a marked reduction in the incidence of postoperative wound infections and a clinically meaningful decrease in the total length of hospital stay. These findings suggest that acupressure may serve as a valuable, non‐invasive complementary therapy in this high‐risk patient population. The observed reduction in unadjusted postoperative wound infection from 32.1% in the standard care group to 16.7% in the acupressure group, which remained significant after propensity score stratification, is a substantial clinical effect. While systematic reviews have suggested that acupuncture and related therapies can be effective adjuvants for diabetic foot treatment, the results have often been inconsistent, and few studies have quantified the impact on postoperative infection rates with this level of detail [20]. Similarly, the adjusted mean reduction of 2.1 days in hospital stay is an important outcome, with significant implications for healthcare costs and resource allocation. For comparison, other widely used adjunctive therapies such as NPWT have also been shown to reduce hospital stays and infection risk, although direct numerical comparisons are often challenging due to variations in study design and patient populations [21]. The findings of our study, therefore, contribute novel and specific data on the potential benefits of acupressure in this context.

The secondary outcomes further support the beneficial role of adjunctive acupressure. The significant decrease in the duration of systemic antibiotic use is a logical and important consequence of the observed lower infection rate. This is particularly relevant in the current era of increasing antimicrobial resistance. The trend towards a lower unadjusted rate of second surgical debridement in the acupressure group (9.1% vs. 17.3%), although not reaching formal statistical significance across the propensity score subclasses, is nonetheless clinically relevant. A relative reduction of nearly half suggests a potential benefit that might become statistically significant in a larger cohort. It is plausible that by improving the wound healing environment and reducing infection, acupressure could decrease the likelihood of wound deterioration that would necessitate further surgical intervention. This finding, while preliminary, warrants further investigation in future prospective studies.

The robustness of the primary finding is strengthened by the doubly robust multivariate logistic regression analysis, which demonstrated that adjunctive acupressure remained an independent protective factor against postoperative infection even after adjusting for the propensity score quintiles and key clinical confounders such as ulcer area and baseline C‐reactive protein levels. This statistical adjustment is crucial as it suggests that the observed benefit of acupressure is not merely a result of selection bias where healthier patients might have been offered the therapy. Furthermore, the prespecified subgroup and interaction analyses indicated that the protective effect of acupressure was consistent across various patient strata, including age, diabetes duration, and the presence of neuropathy, without significant effect modification. The observation that the effect appeared more pronounced in patients with less severe ulcers (Wagner grade 2) and better glycemic control (HbA1c ≤ 8.5%) is intriguing. This might suggest that acupressure is most effective when the underlying physiological conditions for healing are more favorable, potentially acting as a catalyst in an already amenable environment.

Several plausible physiological mechanisms may underlie the observed clinical benefits of acupressure. A growing body of evidence suggests that acupuncture and acupressure can modulate local microcirculation. For patients with diabetic foot syndrome, impaired microcirculation is a key pathophysiological factor that hinders wound healing [22]. Stimulation of specific acupoints is thought to promote the release of vasoactive substances, such as calcitonin gene‐related peptide (CGRP), which can lead to vasodilation and increased blood flow to the wound area, thereby enhancing the delivery of oxygen, nutrients, and immune cells necessary for healing [23].

Furthermore, the therapeutic effects may be mediated through the regulation of the neuro‐immune axis. Acupuncture‐like stimulation has been shown to exert potent anti‐inflammatory effects by suppressing pro‐inflammatory cytokines and modulating immune cell activity [23]. This regulation can create a more favourable environment for wound repair and reduce the susceptibility to infection. Research has shown that acupuncture can suppress immune responses and promote nerve repair, which are critical components of the healing process [24]. The connection between psychological stress and impaired wound healing is also well‐established, particularly in patients with diabetes [25]. Stress can lead to the dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, resulting in elevated cortisol levels that can impair immune function and delay healing [26]. Acupressure is known to have a calming, stress‐reducing effect, which could partially account for its beneficial impact on postoperative recovery [27].

This study has several methodological strengths. Foremost is the transition from a traditional matching approach to a propensity score subclassification strategy utilising the full cohort of 423 eligible patients. This unstratified approach not only maximised statistical precision by preventing the deletion of unmatched data but also provided a more meaningful population for inference, ensuring that our findings are highly generalizable to the overall clinical population of the hospital [28]. Propensity score methods remain robust statistical tools for addressing confounding in observational surgical research where randomisation may not always be feasible [29]. The inclusion of a multivariate analysis with interaction testing across subclasses further strengthens the internal validity of the findings. However, the study is not without limitations. Its retrospective, single‐center design may still limit the generalizability of the findings to other geographical populations and healthcare settings. Despite the rigor of propensity score analysis, there remains a possibility of unmeasured confounding from variables not included in the model, such as nutritional status or specific details of the surgical procedures [30]. The lack of a standardised protocol for the administration of acupressure introduces potential variability. Furthermore, this study focused on short‐term postoperative outcomes, and data on long‐term outcomes such as complete wound healing time, ulcer recurrence, and amputation rates were not collected.

Conclusion

This study provides compelling evidence from a full propensity score‐stratified cohort that adjunctive acupressure is associated with a significantly lower risk of postoperative wound infection and a shorter length of hospital stay in patients with Wagner grade 2–3 DFU. These findings highlight the potential of acupressure as a safe, inexpensive, and effective complementary therapy in the comprehensive management of DFU. Future research should aim to corroborate these findings through a large‐scale, multi‐center randomised controlled trial. Such a trial would not only provide a higher level of evidence but also allow for the standardisation of the acupressure protocol and the collection of data on long‐term outcomes. Additionally, further mechanistic studies are warranted to better elucidate the biological pathways through which acupressure exerts its therapeutic effects on wound healing.
