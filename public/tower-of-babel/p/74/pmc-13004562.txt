A cross-sectional clinical analysis on diabetic foot ulcer and associated risk management
Authors: Mohamed Arsath Shamsudeen, Subash C. B. Gopinath, Mohamad Zahin Hafiz Zulkiple
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) are a serious complication of diabetes mellitus, leading to significant morbidity and an increased risk of amputation. This study aimed to determine the clinical profile and associated risk factors of DFU patients at a tertiary care center in Chennai, India.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A cross-sectional study of 189 DFU patients was conducted from September 2018 to August 2019. Data on patient demographics, diabetes history, and risk factors were collected and analyzed.RESULTS:The mean age of the patients was 62 ± 11 years, with 67.7% being male. Uncontrolled diabetes was observed in 88.9% of patients and 85.2% had peripheral neuropathy. Other significant risk factors included a history of trauma (67.7%), smoking (51.3%), and low socioeconomic status (64%). DFUs were more common in elderly males with uncontrolled diabetes. Peripheral neuropathy, trauma, and smoking were identified as major risk factors.CONCLUSIONS:Early detection of peripheral neuropathy and patient education on foot care are crucial for prevention.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic condition. with a rapidly increasing prevalence across the various regions of the world. It is characterized by long-term elevated blood glucose (sugar) levels. Type 1 diabetes, an autoimmune disease, is marked by the body’s immune system attacking and destroying the pancreatic beta cells that produce insulin. The more prevalent form, known as type 2 diabetes, is frequently associated with obesity and a sedentary lifestyle. In type 2 diabetes, the body either fails to produce enough insulin to maintain normal blood sugar levels or becomes resistant to the effects of the insulin produced.[1234] One of the most significant and concerning complications of long-standing diabetes is diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs).[56789] These ulcers result from peripheral neuropathy, vascular insufficiency, and altered biomechanics and are often complicated by infection.[1011] Proper management and treatment of DFUs are essential to avoid further complications, such as infection or amputation. Diabetes-related lower-extremity complications are a leading cause of global disability, disproportionately affecting middle-to older-aged populations and resulting in significant reductions in quality of life. It is estimated that up to 50% of people with diabetes have neuropathy, and up to 34% will develop a foot ulcer in their lifetime, with many leading to infections and amputations, further contributing to the global burden of disability.[1213] DFUs represent a major public health concern due to their high morbidity and potential for lower extremity amputation. They significantly impact patients’ quality of life and pose a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems. With the rising prevalence of diabetes, particularly in urban areas, the incidence of DFUs is a growing concern.[214] Despite recognizing various risk factors for DFUs, such as poor glycemic control, peripheral neuropathy, and peripheral vascular disease, these factors can vary across different populations and geographic regions. In addition, socioeconomic factors and healthcare access may play crucial roles in the development and progression of DFUs, particularly in developing countries. Previous studies have reported varying incidence rates and risk factor profiles for DFUs in different parts of India. However, there is a lack of data from tertiary care centers in South India, where the prevalence of diabetes is notably high. Understanding the clinical profile of patients with DFUs and identifying significant risk factors in this population are crucial for developing targeted prevention strategies and improving patient outcomes.[15161718] This study aims to determine the clinical profile of patients presenting with DFUs at a tertiary care center in Chennai, Tamil Nadu, India and to identify and analyze risk factors associated with DFUs in this population. In addition, the study will examine the relationship between socioeconomic status, duration of diabetes, and the occurrence of DFUs and discuss the role of patient education in foot care as a preventive measure against DFUs. By exploring the specific characteristics and risk factors of DFUs in this population, this research aims to contribute to the development of more effective prevention and management strategies, thereby reducing the burden of DFUs.

Materials and Methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital in Chennai, Tamil Nadu, India, from September 2018 to August 2019. A total of 189 patients with DFUs were included in the study. Demographic and clinical data were collected using a structured questionnaire and through the review of medical records. Data were analyzed using the SPSS software version 29.0.1.0 [MAHSA UNIVERSITY, Bandar Saujana Putra, Selangor, Malaysia]. Descriptive statistics were employed to analyze the sociodemographic and clinical profiles of the study population. Continuous variables are described as mean ± standard deviation, whereas categorical variables are described as frequencies and percentages. The association between risk factors and the presence of DFUs was analyzed using Chi-square tests for the categorical variables and Student’s t-test for the continuous variables. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and Discussion

DFUs are a chronic complication of diabetes mellitus that develop due to uncontrolled diabetes. Without appropriate treatment, DFUs can lead to extended periods of hospitalization, resulting in significant financial burdens for healthcare centers.[19202122] In severe cases, amputation may be necessary. Notably, diabetes mellitus accounts for 70% of lower limb amputations, which is a significant concern.[9] This high percentage suggests that there is potential for reduction through the implementation of effective preventive strategies. DFUs primarily develop due to sensory, motor, and autonomic neuropathy, which lead to loss of protective sensation, foot deformities, and skin changes, respectively, often resulting in callus formation [Supplementary Tables 1 and 2].[11] These changes, combined with minor trauma from repetitive impact, constant low pressure from poorly fitting shoes, or high pressure from sharp objects, can cause tissue damage and ultimately lead to deep ulcers involving subcutaneous tissue.[11] The findings of this study on DFU risk factors align with previous research, such as the study by (NO_PRINTED_FORM) in Vellore, India.[23] Both studies identified peripheral neuropathy as a significant risk factor. Additionally, the need for insulin therapy, indicating potentially more severe diabetes or poorer glycemic control, was also identified as a risk factor in both studies. The study included 189 patients with DFUs, with a mean age of 62 ± 11 years. There was a significant gender difference, with males comprising 67.7% (n = 128) of the study population compared to 32.3% (n = 61) females [Figure 1]. The majority of patients (68.8%, n = 130) were over 60 years old. Regarding the duration of diabetes, 45.5% (n = 87) had been diagnosed for more than 8 years, while 35.9% (n = 68) had diabetes for 4–8 years [Figures 2 and 3]. Notably, a high proportion of patients (88.9%, n = 168) had uncontrolled diabetes, with only 11.1% (n = 21) showing good glycemic control. Peripheral neuropathy was prevalent in 85.2% (n = 161) of the patients. A history of trauma was reported by 67.7% (n = 128) of the patients. Smoking was a common risk factor, with 51.3% (n = 97) of patients being smokers [Figure 4]. Hypertension was present in 26.4% (n = 50) of the cases. Socioeconomic analysis revealed that 64% (n = 121) of the patients came from a low socioeconomic background, compared to 36% (n = 68) from a higher socioeconomic status. Both socioeconomic classes can be further categorized based on the presence or absence of infection.

Management of diabetic foot ulcers

The management of DFUs involves a comprehensive approach that includes strict glycemic control, infection management, and wound care to prevent amputations.[24] Glycemic control directly influences wound healing and reduces the risk of infections and amputations. Studies have shown that maintaining an HbA1c level below 7% can significantly enhance wound healing outcomes and decrease the likelihood of severe complications.[25] In addition, continuous glucose monitoring provides more precise control over blood glucose levels, which is crucial for optimizing wound healing in patients with DFUs.[25] Infection control is equally important. Appropriate antibiotic therapy should be initiated promptly to manage and contain infections, thereby preventing progression to severe complications such as osteomyelitis or sepsis.[20252627] Wound management involves regular debridement and the use of appropriate dressings to maintain a clean and conducive healing environment. Debridement helps remove necrotic tissue that can harbor bacteria and increases the rate of the healing process, thereby promoting faster and more efficient wound healing.[25] Preventing recurrent ulcers and amputations requires not only managing current wounds but also implementing preventive measures. Therapeutic footwear and offloading practices are essential to reduce pressure on the affected areas and prevent new ulcers from forming.[25] Lifestyle modifications, such as adhering to a diabetic diet, engaging in regular exercise, and smoking cessation, also play a critical role in managing diabetes and reducing the risk of DFUs.[212225]

Foot care and approaches

Proper foot care is crucial for individuals diagnosed with diabetes mellitus [Figure 5]. Daily inspection of the feet, especially after prolonged walking, helps identify early signs of ulcers or injuries that might otherwise go unnoticed due to diabetic neuropathy.[28] It is essential to avoid walking barefoot at all times to prevent accidental cuts and infections. Before wearing shoes, checking for any foreign objects inside them can prevent injuries that may lead to ulcers. Wearing socks with shoes and choosing well-fitting footwear are also vital measures. Shoes should not be worn for more than an hour when new, to allow the feet to adjust, and it is advisable to change shoes two to three times a day to reduce constant pressure on any one part of the foot. Daily washing and thorough drying of the feet help maintain skin integrity, and applying moisturizing oil or cream prevents dryness that can lead to cracking and subsequent infections. In addition, cutting toenails straight across minimizes the risk of ingrown toenails, which can become infected and exacerbate foot issues.[29] Preventing DFUs also involves broader lifestyle modifications and adherence to specific clinical practice guidelines as well as consensus guidelines [Figure 6]. Strict glycemic control is fundamental in preventing DFUs and their recurrence.[29] By maintaining blood glucose levels within the target range, patients can significantly reduce the risk of developing DFUs. Foot care education and regular follow-up with healthcare providers play an essential role in the early detection and management of foot problems. Properly fitted therapeutic footwear has been shown to prevent the recurrence of plantar ulcers, especially in patients with a history of foot ulcers.[29] A summary of the approach to care for DFUs is shown in Figure 6.

Conclusions

Diabetes mellitus frequently results in DFUs. Peripheral neuropathy, or nerve damage, and peripheral arterial disease, or inadequate blood circulation, contributes to their development. Uncontrolled diabetes was prevalent in 88.9% of patients with DFUs, highlighting the importance of strict glycemic control in preventing amputation. Peripheral neuropathy was observed in 85.2% of patients, underscoring the need for regular neurological examinations for diabetic patients undergoing follow-up at healthcare centers. This study also identified potential socio-cultural and occupational factors influencing DFU development; however, more evidence and research are needed to support these findings. A history of trauma (67.7%) and smoking (51.3%) were common among patients, emphasizing the importance of patient education on foot care and smoking cessation programs. Further studies are needed to explore the relationships between DFU risk factors and DFU development in this population. By addressing these factors comprehensively, the incidence of DFUs can be reduced, thus improving the outcomes for diabetic patients in Chennai and similar populations across India. Effective management of DFUs involves strict glucose control, appropriate antibiotics for infection control, and wound debridement to prevent amputations.
