Dual targeting of GPX4 and TXNRD1 triggers eradication of AML cells through induction of apoptosis and ferroptosis
Authors: Cécile Favreau, Maxence Bourgoin, Coline Savy, Thomas Botton, Sarah Bailly, Florence Granger, Catherine Birck, Marwa Zerhouni, Emeline Kerreneur, Adele Rivault, Alban Vigroux, Jade Dussart-Gautheret, Marie-Laure Arcangeli, Arnaud Poterszman, Thomas Cluzeau, Stephane Rocchi, Arnaud Jacquel, Rachid Benhida, Patrick Auberger, Anthony R. Martin, Guillaume Robert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) are hematological disorders associated with bone marrow failure and abnormal hematopoietic cell growth, often progressing to acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Current treatments for AML and high-risk MDS are limited in efficacy, highlighting the need for new therapies. Recent studies show ferroptosis induction, alone or with standard chemotherapy, as a promising strategy for treating MDS/AML cells. Here, we report two novel compounds, HA344 and #231, that target both ferroptosis and apoptosis pathways to effectively eradicate MDS/AML cell lines and patient-derived bone-marrow blasts. RNASeq analysis reveals oxidative stress and apoptosis as key pathways activated by these compounds in different AML cell lines. In cellulo click-chemistry experiments coupled to mass spectrometry analysis identified glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and thioredoxin reductase 1 (TXNRD1) as primary targets of both compounds, inhibiting GPX4 and TXNRD1 in the micromolar range. Mass spectrometry analysis confirms that HA344 and #231 covalently bind GPX4; with however a higher affinity for selenium-containing GPX4 (GPX4-Se) than for sulfur-containing GPX4 (GPX4-S). These findings design HA344 and #231 as potential therapeutic options for MDS/AML treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40164-026-00766-4.

Background

MyeloDysplastic Syndromes (MDS) consist of a heterogeneous spectrum of myeloid clonal hematological disorders that appear frequently in elderly patients with a median age at diagnosis of about 70 [1]. In about 30% of cases, MDS evolve to acute myeloid leukemia (AML) a more aggressive disease [2, 3]. Due to the significant toxicity associated with chemotherapy previously used in elderly patients with high-risk MDS or AML, Azacitidine, either alone or in combination with Venetoclax, has now supplanted the anthracycline/Ara-C regimen [4]. Although these agents can achieve a sustained remission in about 70% of cases and even a cure in some patients, almost all patients relapse with a progressively more aggressive disease. In this context, identifying new molecules able to eradicate MDS and AML cells remains a major challenge to improve patient care. To be efficient as potential future therapies these molecules should (1) affect preferentially preleukemic (MDS) or leukemic cells (AML), sparing normal hematopoietic cells and (2) be able to inhibit cell growth or/and induce cell death.

Different forms of cell death have been characterized so far, including apoptosis, necroptosis, autophagic cell death and more recently ferroptosis [5–10]. Apoptosis, a programmed and selective form of cell death, can occur via the extrinsic pathway triggered by death receptors such as Fas or the intrinsic pathway, regulated by Bcl2 family proteins [11]. Both pathways converge on caspase activation, leading to substrate cleavage and the execution of apoptosis. Accordingly, the reactivation of apoptosis in MDS/AML cells using BH3 mimetics, such as Venetoclax, has significantly improved treatment outcomes for elderly patients [12].

Ferroptosis is a recently characterized form of cell death induced by a wide range of agents including Erastin, an inhibitor of the glutamate/cystine antiporter or RSL3, which targets GPX4 [13]. The glutamate/cystine antiporter is composed of a light chain (SLC7A11) and an heavy chain (SLC3A2) and is the main transporter of cystine into the cell [14]. Key features of ferroptosis include lipid peroxide accumulation, heightened reactive oxygen species (ROS), oxidative stress (OS), and mitochondrial shrinkage. Ferroptosis differs from other modes of cell death in that it is impaired by lipid antioxidants such as ferrostatin-1 and a-tocopherol and iron chelators such as deferoxamine and ciclopirox [15]. Of note, pharmacological inhibition of the cystine/glutamate antiporter, reduction of GSH levels, or iron overload are all conditions triggering cellular ferroptosis.

Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) is a member of the GPX protein family and a major actor in the regulation of ferroptosis. GPX4 carries a highly reactive selenocysteine (Se) residue in its active site that is crucial for enzymatic activity. GPX4 catalytic site is composed of a tetrad of amino acids: Sect. 46, Gln81, Trp136 and Asn147. Replacement of Sect. 46 by a cysteine or mutation of any of the 3 other amino acids results in an abrogation or a diminution of GPX4 enzymatic activity. GPX4 plays a key role in the regulation of ferroptosis by catalyzing the reduction of lipid hydroperoxides into their alcohol counterparts, thus preventing the propagation of oxidative damage and ensuring cell survival by maintaining the integrity of cell membranes. In addition, inactivation or overexpression of the Gpx4 gene resulted in induction or reduction of ferroptosis, respectively [16]. Thus ferroptosis can be defined by iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation particularly when GPX4 is inhibited [15, 17, 18]. Finally, increasing pieces of evidence have accumulated to imply GPX4 as an important determinant of myeloid cell homeostasis, differentiation and likely of leukemic transformation, overexpression of GPX4 being associated with a poor prognosis, notably in patients suffering AML [19].

Thioredoxin reductase 1 (TXNRD1), another selenoprotein is a key antioxidant enzyme also involved in the defense against OS through its ability to tune the dithiol/disulfide balance [20]. Of note, TXNRD1 knock down has been reported to trigger ferroptosis following GSH depletion [21] but also apoptosis [22]. TXNRD1 expression is upregulated in different types of cancers [23], including chronic myeloid leukemia cells, in which the occurrence of ferroptosis requires the coordinated regulation of GPX4 and TNXRD1 [24] and also in AML [20].

The NRF2 signaling pathway plays a cardinal role in antioxidant response through the transcriptional regulation of antioxidant genes such as SLC7A11, GPX4, Glutamate–Cysteine Ligase Catalytic subunit (GCLC), Glutamate-Cysteine Ligase Modifier subunit (GCLM), NAD(P)H dehydrogenase Quinone 1 (NQO1), Heme Oxygenase 1 (HMOX1), TXNRD1 and Thioredoxin (TXN) among others [25]. Several of these genes such as GPX4, TXNRD1, SLC7A11, GCLC and GCLM are key players in the regulation of ferroptosis. Importantly, upregulation of NRF2 expression is a frequent hallmark of different types of cancers, including leukemia and is most of the time associated with a pejorative prognosis [26]. In this context, targeting NRF2 has been proposed as a promising strategy to kill cancer cells and overcome resistance notably in AML [27].

Many cancer cell lines exhibit dysregulated cell death pathways, contributing to drug resistance. Restoring specific cell death mechanisms, such as apoptosis and/or ferroptosis, offers a promising therapeutic strategy for overcoming resistance and effectively treating various cancers. In line with this strategy, we described here a new pharmacological approach with two related small covalent inhibitors to eradicate MDS and AML cells through the concomitant activation of apoptosis and ferroptosis. Both compounds exhibit an original mechanism of action by directly targeting and degrading GPX4 and TXNRD1 two, key enzymes involved in the protection of leukemic cells from apoptosis and ferroptosis.

Results

HA344 and #231 induce cell death of AML cell lines and efficiently kills CD34+ cells from AML patients

We previously described that HA344 (Fig. S1a) was highly effective on human melanoma cell lines [28]. To evaluate the efficacy of HA344 and #231, a nucleoside analog of HA344 (Fig. S1b) we performed NCI-60 screening of the 60 most common cell lines representative of cancers from all tissues (Fig. S1c). Compound #231 proved particularly effective in inhibiting growth of different leukemic cell lines in the 10− 7-10− 5M range, while most of solid cancer cell lines were 10-fold less sensitive to the drug. To further evaluate the efficiency of HA344 and #231 on leukemic cells, we performed dose response curves for these two compounds on 5 AML cell lines representative of the pathology and harboring different types of chromosomal translocation and/or mutations: HL-60 (CDKN2A, NRAS, TP53), OCI-AML3 (NPM1, DNMT3a), MV4-11 (FLT3-ITD, MLL-AF4), NB4 (PML-RARa), and MOLM14 (FLT3-ITD, MLL-AF9). Using DAPI staining (Fig. 1a) and XTT assay (Fig. 1b) we observed that HA344 and #231 compounds killed AML cells very efficiently. Nevertheless, both compounds were slightly more efficient on MOLM-14 and MV4-11 AML cells, that harbor FLT3-ITD mutations. The LD50 values for HA344 and #231 in different AML cell line ranged from 0.4 to 1.3 µM (Fig. 1c).

To further investigate the anti-leukemic effect of our molecules, 25 individual medullary biopsies patients diagnosed with MDS/AML were treated with increasing concentrations of HA344 or #231 for 48 h (Table S1). Among them, 12 patients had intermediary gene abnormalities and 15 had adverse genetic abnormalities. Cell death induced by HA344 and #231 was evaluated on hematopoietic cells (CD45+), as well as blast cells (CD45+/CD34+) using Dapi staining, as illustrated in Supplemental Fig. 1d. HA344 and #231 triggered a dose-dependent increase in cell death in bone marrow samples from AML patients. Of greater interest was the fact that blast cells displayed a statistically significant increase in their sensitivity to both molecules when compared to non-blast cells (Fig. 1d). This effect was observable in patients with both intermediate and adverse genetic abnormalities. Importantly, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) and to a lesser extent CD34 + cord blood cells from healthy donors were less sensitive to cell death induction after a 48 h treatment (Fig. 1e and f).

HA344 and #231 induce transcriptional modifications in the apoptotic and ROS-dependent pathways, contributing to the elimination of malignant myeloid cells

Using principal component analysis (PCA), we first illustrated the sample distances for control and treatment conditions based on each cell line and compound (Fig. 2a).

HA344 and #231 induce significantly more gene modifications in HL60 cells compared to OCI-AML3. Furthermore, treatment with compound #231 has a much stronger effect than HA344 on gene expression in both cell lines. These gene regulations are further visualized in the volcano plot (Fig. 2b). Of note, the extent of gene regulation was substantially greater in HL-60 cells, where HA344 and #231 influenced the regulation of 7 332 and 17 447 genes, respectively, contrasting with OCI-AML3, where they affected the regulation of 2 960 genes and 3 423 genes, respectively (Fig. 2b). Venn diagrams show that most of the genes upregulated by HA344 are also upregulated by compound #231 (94% in HL60 (6085/6454), 86% in OCI-AML3 (1637/1904)), and similarly for downregulated genes (78% in HL60 (688/878), 69% in OCI-AML3 (727/1056)) (Fig. 2C). This suggests that beside regulating a common set of genes with HA344, #231 also exerts additional regulatory effects, indicative of a broader spectrum of action. Using the Gene Ontology classification system, we identified apoptotic death process and response to oxidative stress as the most prominently regulated pathways among the 17 most regulated pathways on a total panel of 9403 (Fig. 3a). Further analysis of the GSEA results established that the NRF2 pathway was also highly enriched in HL60 and OCI-AML3 treated cell lines. The normalized enrichment scores (NES) for HL60 cells treated with HA344 and #231 were 1.55 and 1.29, while in OCI-AML3 cells, the scores were 1.51 and 1.71, respectively, demonstrating that a significant induction of NRF2-dependent genes occurs upon treatment with HA344 and #231 (Fig. 3b). We highlight four upregulated common genes in our volcano plots and venn diagrams that are associated with NRF2-dependent reactive oxygen induction (EGR1, HMOX1, SQSTM1, SLC2A6) and one downregulated gene directly linked to apoptotic myeloid cell death (BCL2) (Fig. 2b and c). The downregulation of BCL2 found in all conditions, likely explain the apoptotic component of the cell death observed in our model upon HA344 and #231 treatment. All together these data are compatible with a major effect of our compounds on the regulation of the apoptotic death process and the response to oxidative stress.

Induction of cell death by HA344 and #231 is inhibited by apoptosis and ferroptosis inhibitors

In view of the results obtained in the RNA-seq analysis, HL-60 and OCI-AML3 cell lines were subjected to pretreatment with the pan-caspase inhibitor Q-VD-OPh (qVD), followed by treatment with HA344 or #231 at their LD50 for 24–48 h (Fig. 4a). HA344 and #231-induced cell death was significantly reduced in the presence of qVD, a pan-caspase inhibitor, both in HL-60 and OCI-AML3 cells at 48 h (Fig. 4a). To further support the involvement of apoptosis in the cell death mechanism induced by our compounds, we carried out caspase activity assays. Both HA344 and #231 induced a robust activation of executioner caspases 3 and 7 (Fig. 4b, left panel) as well as initiator caspase 9 (Fig. 4b, right panel) after 24 h of treatment. These data confirm the involvement of caspases in the cell death process induced by the two compounds. To strengthen our findings, HL-60 cells were exposed to HA344 or #231 at concentrations equivalent to 1 or 2 times the LD50 for 24 h (Fig. S2a). Western blot analysis confirmed the cleavage of procaspase 3 and PARP. Indeed, we observed that at the higher concentration of both compounds (2x LD50) the zymogen of caspase 3 completely disappeared. These findings provide robust evidence supporting the implication of caspase and apoptosis in HA344 and #231 effects. To further demonstrate the involvement of mitochondria in HA344- and #231-induced caspase-dependent cell death. we performed a kinetic of mitochondrial membrane permeabilization (MMP) (Fig. S2b), mitochondrial superoxide production (Fig. S2c), and SMAC release into the cytosol (Fig. S2d). Both compounds induced a robust increase in MMP (Fig. S2b), a concomitant rise in mitochondrial ROS (Fig. S2c) and SMAC release in HL-60 and OCI-AML3 cells (Fig. S2d), all hallmarks of intrinsic apoptosis. In line with the results of our RNA-seq analysis, we next used a large-scale pharmacological approach to inhibit various cell death processes or ROS generation. For the sake of simplicity, we only depicted the results with the two most active inhibitors i.e. qVD (Fig. 4a) and ciclopirox, an iron chelator. Ciclopirox was found to significantly inhibit the antileukemic effects of HA344 and #231 at 24 h in both cell lines but failed to prevent cell death at 48 h (Fig. 4c). Our data also indicate that qVD is more effective at 48 h (Fig. 4a), while the effect of ciclopirox is higher at 24 h (Fig. 4c), suggesting that both apoptosis and ferroptosis contribute to the effects of our compounds, with ferroptosis preceding apoptosis. We confirmed that HA344- and #231-induced cell death is impaired by Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) or Liproxstatin-1 (Lip-1), two well-characterized lipid peroxidation inhibitors capable of blocking ferroptosis (Fig. S3a). In addition, deferoxamine, an iron chelator and known ferroptosis inhibitor, also prevented cell death induced by both compounds (Fig. S3b). Ferroptosis is a recently recognized form of regulated cell death triggered by lipid peroxidation of membrane proteins and characterized by iron dependency. The involvement of ferroptosis in addition to apoptosis in HL-60 and OCI-AML3 cells was confirmed by the increase in lipid peroxidation at 6–24 h of HA344 and #231 treatment (Fig. 4d). Moreover, increasing the concentrations of both compounds clearly triggers a dose-dependent augmentation of lipid peroxidation (Fig. S3c).

Having established the role of apoptosis in HA344 and #231 effects in myeloid leukemia cell lines, we next exposed BM-derived blastic and non-blastic cells from AML patients to qVD, followed by a 24 h treatment with HA344 or #231. qVD was shown to mitigate HA344 and #231 mediated cell death, mainly within blast cells population (Fig. 4e), thereby validating the importance of apoptosis induction by our compounds in patient blasts. Additionally, we evaluated lipid peroxidation levels in patient samples following a 24 h treatment with increasing concentrations of HA344 or #231 (Fig. 4f). Like with AML cell lines, we detected a significant increase in lipid peroxidation in cells from AML patients, thus confirming the involvement of ferroptosis in the mechanism of action of our compounds in patient’s cells. Although it is not among the 17 most regulated pathways (Fig. 3a), GSEA analysis showed that the ferroptosis pathway was significantly negatively enriched in HL60 cells upon treatment with HA344 and #231 (Fig. S3d). In OCI-AML3 cells, although both compounds modulated genes involved in the oxidative stress response, ferroptosis enrichment was not significant (Fig. S3d).

HA344 and #231 trigger ROS production, NRF2 relocation and overexpression of genes containing ARE elements

Considering the effect of our compounds on lipid peroxidation we analyzed their impact on ROS production using the DCFDA probe. Treatment of AML cell lines with HA344 and #231 triggered ROS production starting at 30 min and peaking at 75 min (Fig. 5a). However, ROS production was less robust in OCI-AML3 cells compared to HL-60 cells (Fig. 5b). These findings are in line with the lower sensitivity of OCI-AML3 cells to both drugs (Fig. 1b). As GSEA analysis previously established a significant induction of the NRF2 signaling pathway under HA344 and #231 treatment (Fig. 3b), AML cell lines were treated for 10 h with HA344 and #231 and cytoplasmic and nuclear extracts were prepared and subjected to Western Blot analysis. Both compounds were found to trigger the nuclear accumulation of the transcription factor NRF2 (Fig. 5c). As accumulation of NRF2 in the nucleus is associated with increased gene expression related to the antioxidant response, we performed RT-qPCR analysis of well characterized antioxidant genes NRF2, NADPH quinone dehydrogenase 1 (NQO1), heme oxygenase 1 (HMOX1), thioredoxin (TXN), Thioredoxin reductase 1 (TXNRD1), TXNRD2, glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (GCLG) and glutamate-cysteine ligase modifier subunit (GCLM) in AML cell lines treated with HA344 and #231 (Fig. 5d-e and Fig. S4a-b). In HL60 cells, HA344 and #231 drastically increased the expression of HMOX1 mRNA (400 to 600-fold) at 6 h while expression of NRF2, NQO1, TXNRD1 and GCLM increased more moderately (2 to 4 times) (Fig. 5d-e). We observed highly similar results in OCI-AML3 AML cells (Fig. S4a-b). All together, these observations clearly indicate that HA344 and #231 trigger NRF2 nuclear relocation leading to increased expression of ARE responsive genes and well-characterized ferroptosis genes. A list of additional genes linked to the NRF2 signaling pathway is provided in (Fig. S4c).

To assess potential additive or synergistic effects between our compounds and current therapies used in AML, HL60 and OCI-AML3 cells were co-treated for 24 and 48 h with HA344, #231, and Venetoclax, either alone or in combination (Fig. S5a-b). Combined treatments produced effects equivalent to the theoretical cumulative effect, indicating an additive rather than synergistic effect of our compounds and Venetoclax on the induction of AML cell death.

We reanalyzed our patient cohort by stratifying samples into MDS, AML and AML carrying FLT3 mutations. Interestingly, blast cells from patients harboring FLT3 mutations seem more sensitive to ferroptosis induction (Fig. S5c). This observation supports our initial findings in cell lines (Fig. 1a and b) and is consistent with the study by Sabatier et al. [29] showing that AML cells carrying FLT3 mutations are more sensitive to ferroptotic cell death.

Identification of GPX4 as a substrate for HA344 and #231 compounds in AML cell lines

Identification of cellular targets remains a challenge for the development of molecules with high anticancer potential. The development of click-chemistry over the last 10 years has greatly improved the discovery of protein targets [30]. To identify the cellular targets of HA344 and #231 in AML cell lines, we generated the active probes VG41 and VG40, along with their counterparts VG43 and VG60, which do not contain the ethyl propiolate active motif highlighted in red in Fig. 6a. We first checked that increasing concentrations of VG43 and VG60 failed to induce cell death in three different AML cell lines, namely HL60, OCI-AML3 and MV4-11 after 48 h of incubation, conversely to VG41 and VG40 (Fig. S6a). We next incubated these three AML cell lines with 2 µM of VG41, VG40 or VG43 and VG60 as negative controls. Following a click reaction with a biotin label, several washing and enrichment steps, eluates were submitted to mono-dimensional gel electrophoresis, staining and finally subjected to mass spectrometry analysis (Please see Fig. S6b for the complete protocol).

To confirm a direct interaction of our compounds with GPX4, we performed thermal shift assays in the HL60 and OCI-AML3 cell lines (Fig. 7c). One hour after HA344 or #231 addition, a significant GPX4 shift was observed compared to the untreated condition. The modification of GPX4 migration likely reflected a post-translational modification or a major GPX4 conformational change induced by HA344 or #231. HA344 and #231 induced a drastic reduction in the melting temperature, which decreased from 62 °C (under control conditions) to 56 °C and 54 °C for HA344 and #231 respectively in HL60 cells (Fig. 7c, left panel). In OCI-AML3 cells, the impact of HA344 and #231 compounds was even higher, resulting in a decrease from 62 °C to 50 °C and 54 °C respectively (Fig. 7c, right panel). FIN56 was previously reported as a degrader of GPX4 [32]. As expected, we demonstrated that a 1-hour incubation with FIN56 reduces the melting temperature of GPX4 from 62 °C to 54 °C in HL-60 cells and from 62 °C to 50 °C in OCI-AML3 cells (Fig. 7C), confirming its degradation. Quantification of the western blot bands confirmed the lower stability of GPX4 when bound to HA344 or #231 (Fig. 7c lower panels). We then treated HL60 and OCI-AML3 cell lines for 48 h with increasing concentrations of HA344 and #231. Both compounds induced a dose-dependent reduction in GPX activity as assessed by the decrease in GSH level (Fig. 7d).

All together our findings provide compelling evidence that both HA344 and #231 affect GPX4 stability (Fig. 7c), leading to a drastic inhibition of expression and enzymatic activity, and to the concomitant collapse of the GSH pool (Fig. 7d). The recombinant GPX4 protein was synthesized following the procedure outlined by Borchert et al. [33], featuring a selenocysteine in the active site. With this protocol, we can expect around 50% of the GPX4 protein that contained a selenocysteine. The effects of HA344 and #231 compounds on the recombinant GPX4 protein were evaluated using nano differential scanning fluorimetry. The GPX4 negative control exhibited a melting temperature (Tm) of 47.96 ± 0.07 °C. Upon treatment with 100 µM of HA344 or #231, GPX4 displayed a significant shift in Tm from 47.93 °C/47.90 °C to 40.60 °C/39.61 °C, respectively (Fig. 7e, upper and lower panel). Ultimately, we assessed the binding ability of our compounds to the recombinant GPX4 protein through mass spectrometry. For the recombinant GPX4 protein alone, we obtained a mixture consisting of two forms, with and without selenium, according to a 40/60% ratio, which is close to the expected 50/50% ratio. After incubation for different times with the compounds, it was observed that HA344 bound to the GPX4-Se form but not to GPX4-S after 120 min, while #231 bound 100% of GPX4-Se within 30 min, and 40% of GPX4-S after 120 min. These data indicate that HA344 and #231 both bound GPX4-Se, but that #231 exhibited a significantly higher reactivity for the selenoenzyme (Fig. 7f). Because of the strong affinity of our compounds for GPX4-Se, we carried out a click-chemistry experiment to investigate their binding to another selenocysteine-containing protein, namely TXNRD1. We confirm that HA344 and #231 can effectively bind to TXNRD1 in all three AML cell lines (Fig. 8b), a finding corroborated by MS analysis (data not shown). Furthermore, both HA344 and #231 were found to inhibit TXNRD1 activity in a dose-dependent manner as efficiently as Tri-1 a well-characterized inhibitor of TXNRD1 [34] (Fig. 8c). We further observed that the melting temperature (Tm) of TXNRD1 decreased from 64 °C under control conditions to 58 °C in the presence of HA344 and #231, indicating a marked destabilization of TXNRD1 (Fig. 8d). By contrast, FIN56, despite its strong activity against GPX4, failed to affect the Tm of TXNRD1. Additionally, unlike GPX4, the expression of TXNRD1 remains relatively constant across the various cell lines (Fig. 8e). Having confirmed the ability of HA344 and #231 to directly bind GPX4 (Fig. 6c) and TXNRD1 (Fig. 8b), to alter their melting temperature (Tm) (Figs. 7c and 8d), and to inhibit their enzymatic activity (Figs. 7d and 8c), we further show using western blot analysis that increasing concentrations of HA344 or #231 induced the degradation of both GPX4 and TXNRD1 (Fig. 8f). To further investigate the mechanism of action of our compounds, we genetically or pharmacologically inhibited GPX4 and TXNRD1 using siRNA and specific inhibitors. Interestingly, GPX4 knockdown (Fig. 9a) strongly induces lipid peroxidation, a hallmark of ferroptosis, along with moderate mitochondrial ROS (superoxide) and mitochondrial depolarization (Fig. 9b). The GPX4 inhibitor FIN-56 replicates the effect of GPX4 silencing effect (Fig. 9c). By contrast, TXNRD1 knockdown (Fig. 9a) fails to induce lipid peroxidation but triggers robust mitochondrial ROS and permeabilization, leading to apoptosis (Fig. 9b), an effect reproduced by Tri-1, a TXNRD1 inhibitor (Fig. 9c). Simultaneous knockdown of GPX4 and TXNRD1 using siRNAs, or their pharmacological inhibition using FIN-56 and Tri-1, induces a robust increase in lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial depolarization (Fig. 9b and c), demonstrating that GPX4 inhibition contributes to the ferroptotic component of cell death, whereas TXNRD1 inhibition contributes the apoptotic one. In addition, the covalent dual inhibitors HA344 and #231, which target both GPX4 (Fig. 7d) and TXNRD1 (Fig. 8c), recapitulate this dual induction of ferroptosis and apoptosis, thereby reinforcing the therapeutic potential of these compounds.

Discussion

In the present study, we used a structure-activity analysis to identify small molecule inhibitors bearing a nucleoside and non-nucleoside core structure and exhibiting a potent anti-leukemic effect on different hematopoietic cell lines. Two lead compounds, HA344 and #231 showed very strong efficacy not only on a panel of AML cell lines but also in various bone marrow samples from AML patients, within the micromolar concentration range. HA344 and #231-mediated leukemic cell death was dependent on both apoptosis and ferroptosis, since both compounds activated caspase 3, 7 and 9 and induced lipid peroxidation whatever the AML cell lines tested. Additional studies, using a pharmacological approach confirmed the dependence of HA344 and #231 effect on apoptosis and ferroptosis. RNAseq analysis performed on different AML cell lines identified OS and apoptosis pathways as major determinants of the cell death programs triggered by our compounds. We also confirmed that both compounds induced nuclear relocation of NRF2 and the subsequent upregulation of ARE responsive genes as a ROS-mediated defense mechanism [35].

Using click-chemistry in cellulo coupled to mass spectrometry we discovered among the putative HA344 and #231 targets some selenoproteins and more particularly GPX4 and TXNRD1. We next performed nanoDSF in vitro and thermal shift assay in cellulo and demonstrated that HA344 and #231 covalently bind to the selenocysteine (Se) present in GPX4 and TXNRD1 active site. This interaction resulted in the destabilization of these proteins and their rapid degradation by an unknown mechanism. Attempts to identify the mechanism of GPX4 and TXNRD1 were unsuccessful since neither caspase nor proteasome inhibitors were found to affect their degradation. Very recent results in the literature have highlighted that ferroptosis inducers, including ML-162 and RSL3, originally believed to directly target GPX4, also inhibited TXNRD1 and that part of the observed effects of these drugs could be mediated by TXNRD1 inhibition [34]. Interestingly, we found that HA344 and #231 not only bind GPX4 and TNXRD1 but also trigger their degradation. During the screening process to identify FDA approval small molecules that target GPX4, lusutrombopag (a thrombopoietin agonist), Navitoclax and Venetoclax (two BH3-mimetics) were also discovered as potential GPX4 inhibitors [19, 36]. Whether these drugs frequently used in the clinic to treat AML patients inhibit GPX4 and/or TXNRD1 activity and whether they induce ferroptosis is currently unknown. Finally, as Venetoclax is one of the leading treatments for elderly patients suffering AML, we tested our compounds in combination with this drug in different AML cell lines. HA344 and #231 were found to exert additive anti-leukemic effects in combination with Venetoclax, reinforcing the interest of both compounds for the treatment of AML. Consistently with the findings of Sabatier et al. [29], we showed that compounds inducing ferroptosis are highly effective against cells from FLT3-mutated AML patients, who account for approximately 30% of all AML cases, highlighting this high-risk subgroup as a particularly promising therapeutic target for HA344 and #231.

Under physiological circumstances, the generation of ROS constitutes a significant threat to cellular integrity. ROS can act on proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and DNA, causing oxidative damage. Faced to these deleterious effects of ROS, the cell must promptly respond by initiating an antioxidant program aimed at inhibiting the various components responsible for ROS production and mitigating their impact on cellular function. The central player in the response to OS is the transcription factor NRF2 [35]. During ROS production, NRF2 translocates from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, where it activates the transcription of genes harboring antioxidant response elements (AREs) in their promoter regions. ARE responsive genes assume crucial roles in safeguarding the cell through the production and restoration of GSH and TXN, regeneration of NADPH, management of heme and iron metabolism, and detoxification of ROS. Here, we showed that HA344 and #231 triggers the nuclear translocation of NRF2 and the transcription of a panel of antioxidant genes as a ROS-mediated defense mechanism. Among the upregulated genes induced by HA344 and #231, several including HMOX1, TXNRD1, GCLC, GCLM are known inhibitors of ferroptosis.

While our two compounds induced apoptosis and ferroptosis in all the leukemic cell lines tested, there are, however, some slight differences in their respective effects in different AML cell lines. Experiments using a mixture of recombinant GPX4-Se and GPX4-S protein allow to characterize the direct binding of both compounds to the enzyme. They highlighted that HA344 is less reactive to the enzyme but with a higher selectivity for GPX4-Se, while #231 is more reactive to the enzyme but with less selectivity for GPX4-Se as coupling to GPX4-S was also detected and showed an increase with incubation time.

As TXNRD1 was also significatively enriched among the potential targets of our compounds, we performed click-chemistry experiments on several AML cell lines and confirmed a direct binding of HA344 and #231 on TXNRD1. We also checked that both compounds were also able to efficiently inhibit TXNRD1 activity in AML.

The mechanism of action of HA344 and #231 involves rapid covalent binding to the selenocysteine residue within the active sites of the selenoenzymes GPX4 and TXNRD1, followed by their degradation. Our study paves the way for the development of covalent inhibitors targeting both GPXs and TXNRDs selenoenzymes as a promising strategy for the treatment of myeloid malignancies.

Leukemias have evolved multiple strategies to escape therapeutic pressure. While resistance to apoptosis is a well-established hallmark of these malignancies, growing evidence indicates that dysregulation of ferroptosis leads to chemotherapy resistance and treatment failure [37]. Accordingly, pharmacological inhibition of ferroptosis has recently emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy in MDS/AML [19, 29, 38–41]. To survive to increased ROS production, leukemic cells upregulate detoxification enzyme expression mainly through NRF2 induction, thus promoting survival and likely transformation. Among the detoxification enzymes whose expression is increased in AML cells, GPX4 and TXNRD1 represent novel and promising therapeutic targets [19]. Numerous direct or indirect inhibitors of these enzymes have been developed such as FIN56, ML210, RSL3 or APR246. Of note, in the present study we described two covalent small molecule inhibitors, that can concomitantly inhibit GPX4 and TXDNR1 to trigger ferroptosis and apoptosis. Importantly, we found that HA344 and #231 induce the degradation of both enzymes, a property that may have important therapeutic implications. Indeed, HA344 and #231 ability to induce both apoptotic and ferroptotic cell death presents several advantages compared to other chemotherapeutic agents that target a single mode of cell death (i.e. apoptosis or ferroptosis). By triggering multiple cell death pathways HA344 and #231 offer a broader ability to kill tumoral cells which are often resistant to several forms of cell death. In this line, Chatterjee et al. identified the natural product sunshinamide, a cyclodispeptide as a dual activator of apoptosis and ferroptosis through the targeting of both GPX4 and TNXRD1 in breast cancer [22]. Compared to sunshinamide, our two compounds have the significant advantage of inducing the degradation of both GPX4 and TXNRD1.

Our findings may have an impact on AML patient management in the future. Indeed, compelling studies have highlighted an increase of intracellular ROS both in cell lines and in leukemic cells derived from patients’ samples [42–46]. Keeping an elevated intracellular ROS level within myeloid cells represents an important threat due to their high toxicity, requiring the establishment of a set of detoxification mechanisms to support their continued growth and viability. The use of compounds capable of selectively interfering with these detoxification processes will certainly help in eliminating leukemia cells while minimizing the impact on healthy cells.

Conclusions

We describe here two new compounds capable of inducing both apoptosis and ferroptosis in different AML cell lines and CD34 + cells from AML patients. These compounds, which exhibit high affinity for the selenoproteins GPX4 and TXNRD1 and induce their degradation, hold great promise in the context of AML, where high GPX4 and TXNRD1 expression is associated with a pejorative prognosis. Thus, HA344 and #231 might represent new weapons in the therapeutic armamentarium of antileukemic drugs, either individually or in combination with currently available treatments.
