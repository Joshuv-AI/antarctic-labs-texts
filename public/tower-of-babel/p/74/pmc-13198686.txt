Does Radioactivity From Nuclear Power Plants Raise the Risk of Cancer? Harvard School of Public Health: Yes. Biology: No
Authors: Gregory Meyerson, Bill Sacks, James S. Welsh
Published: 2026

A recently published epidemiological study by researchers at the Harvard School of Public Health 1 purports to show that living near a nuclear power plant (NPP) confers a measurably greater risk of cancer due, it would appear, to airborne radionuclides. But they decline to propose any evidence-based mechanism for the increased cancer incidence they claim to observe. In particular, they provide no information about which radionuclides are emitted from each of the twelve included NPPs, their quantities over time, their degree of radioactivity, the energy emitted per decay event, or what doses or dose rates people are subjected to and by what routes – through inhalation, ingestion, or merely having radionuclides deposited on one’s skin or clothing. They also provide no information about the biological half-lives of internalized radionuclides or staying power of externally deposited materials, and no information about the wind patterns that carry these radionuclides and disperse them over varying distances from their source. In fact, they provide no evidence that there were any leaked radionuclides at all. And while they do not directly blame radiation for their observations, they do insinuate this.

The authors also fail to mention the existence or role of biological protective responses to radiation exposure, ruling them out by implication. Furthermore, rather than searching for confounders that could explain the elevated cancer incidence, such as socioeconomic status, diet, smoking, or the placement of NPPs at locations previously contaminated with chemical waste, the authors merely “hypothesize” that such proven causative factors are incidental and irrelevant and that low dose radiation exposure is the etiology. Absent any mechanistic information or evidence of radiation in the vicinity, their assumption that proximity to NPPs raises the risk of cancer makes this the type of study that was satirized in a 2004 paper showing a statistical correlation between the stork population around Berlin, Germany, and the birth rate for out-of-hospital deliveries. 2

There is a veritable epidemic of studies that invoke radiation as the cause of cancer while ignoring exposure levels, confounding influences, and most importantly, biological processes – prominently including the fact that our internal oxygen metabolism (aerobic respiration) damages far more DNA than does normally encountered radiation exposure, 3 and that damaged cells are regularly repaired or removed. If this repair did not occur, we would all die or develop cancer at a young age.

Even right outside the walls of an NPP, any detectable radiation is a tiny fraction of natural background radiation (NBR). NBR averages just under 10 μGy per day in the US. Daily dose at the plant gate is less than 0.01 μGy, and at 50 miles is around 0.0003 μGy – four to five orders of magnitude lower than the average NBR. 4 Yet by failing to measure or reveal doses or dose rates, the authors leave it to be inferred that any or all radiation doses or dose rates are carcinogenic.

Radiobiologically, the Bayesian prior probability that minuscule leakage of radionuclides from an NPP increases cancer incidence approaches zero, rendering their data irrelevant. Moreover, the authors withhold most of their data, incomprehensibly due to claimed confidentiality concerns. This prevents empirical scrutiny of their prior (and posterior) hypotheses that leakage of radionuclides from NPPs might even possibly be the cause of the observed cancers. When we turn to the portion of data that is presented, the authors erroneously assume that the baseline cancer incidence, from which they derive their relative risk (RR), is the same everywhere. It is not, and their assumption is clearly wrong.

Additionally, in their graphs (Figure 3 of Alwadi et al. 1 ), relative risk smoothly declines with distance from NPPs, with smoothly diminishing confidence intervals (CIs). Because the authors omit any scatter plots of actual data, they in effect demand that the reader simply trust their idealized graphs – graphs undermined by the fact that US cancer incidence by county shows significant variation, often exceeding that near NPPs.

A similar study in Spain demonstrated that when the real data is shown, not an idealized graph, there is no pattern of higher cancer mortality rates with proximity. And there is no monotonically diminishing incidence with distance from the NPP (distances averaged over all NPPs). 5 Rather, risks fluctuate non-monotonically (likely due to incidental confounders). For example, the point estimates for lung cancer RR are1.049 (higher than the average attributable risk in the Harvard study) at 0-13.3 km, 0.827 at 13.4-18.9 km, and 1.034 at 19-23.1 km. It is important to note that these trend numbers are overall averages for distance from nuclear power plants taken in the aggregate. From the same study, the “All cancers” numbers also undercut the proximity claim. At the same respective distance ranges, the RRs are 0.961, 1.030, and 1.021.

Possibly confounding the conclusions of Alwadi et al. 1 are the documented variations in cancer incidence with relation to socioeconomic status. For example, breast cancer incidence is somewhat higher in affluent communities, while the incidence of other cancers and clinical outcomes tends to be worse in lower-income regions.6,7 There are mixed reports of the income levels near NPPs with respect to poverty versus affluence. This raises the question as to whether elevated cancer rates ostensibly observed in regions nearer to NPPs are related to income rather than to radiation exposure.

With regard to additional possible confounders, an important but often overlooked study showed that the incidence of leukemia and Hodgkin lymphoma was elevated not only near active NPPs but also near potential sites, where NPPs were considered, planned, and even approved but never actually constructed. 8

Actual and planned placements of NPPs are often proximate to waterways that host erstwhile or current industrial or fossil fuel power plant sites, contaminated by toxic chemical waste. A similar conclusion was drawn by the authors of another paper showing that leukemia rates near NPP sites were already slightly higher than in control areas prior to the building of the NPPs, and that the differences actually declined slightly after NPP construction and onset of operation. 9

A fact sheet issued by the National Cancer Institute dated 19 April 2011 points out that cancer incidence and/or mortality in many counties near NPPs show lower rates than in control counties, while just as many show higher. 10 Obviously, much depends on the choice of control county. Even if summing over these many counties were to show an overall higher result near NPPs, this would be by chance. Judicious cherry-picking of comparison locations can appear to demonstrate either result, depending on desired outcomes, a statistical problem that can only be overcome by including reference to biological processes. 11

Given their preconceptions, Alwadi et al. 1 estimate that between the years 2000 and 2018, over 20,000 cancers developed in Massachusetts from NPPs – 3 to 4% of all the incident cancers in that 19-year interval. However, only two tiny regions of the state, near the Seabrook and Pilgrim NPPs, are anywhere near an NPP. Of the only two still operating NPPs, Seabrook (in New Hampshire) is more than 3 km from Massachusetts in a sparsely populated region, and Millstone (in southern Connecticut) is almost 80 km from Massachusetts. Their figures show little change beyond about 5 km from an NPP. Their attribution of 20,000-plus cancers over 19 years to radiation vastly exceeds the roughly 4,000 thyroid cancers that UNSCEAR estimated could be attributed to the Chernobyl accident over the subsequent 25 years, which, in just ten days, emitted 3 million times the radioactivity of a normally operating 1 GW NPP. The Harvard authors’ conjecture of more than 20,000 cancers also far exceeds the figure estimated to be attributable to the Fukushima accident, where the emitted radioactivity was hundreds of thousands of times greater than that of a functional 1-GW NPP. But without the listing of isotope composition of these purported emissions, their respective energies of decay, and their respective contributions to the overall radiation exposures, no conclusion can be reached as to the doses or dose rates received at different distances from NPPs. And again, the authors provide no proof whatsoever that any radionuclides leaked at all. In the absence of any supporting data, it may be reasonable to assume that the NRC figures are accurate (i.e., that the dose rate at the plant gate is less than 0.01 μGy/day, and at 50 miles is around 0.0003 μGy/day). 4

If trace (phantom?) radionuclides could produce 20,000 cancers over 19 years in Massachusetts, then, according to the logic of these authors, natural background radiation should have produced three to five orders of magnitude more (20 million to 2 billion cancers!) over the same period in that state. Put another way, the authors would have to explain why significant cancer risk arises from exposure to roughly 0.1% to 0.001% of the population’s total natural radiation exposure while neglecting the other 99.9% to 99.999% – not to mention the additional average exposure from nuclear medicine and radiological diagnostic studies, which is roughly comparable to average NBR in the US.

Finally, the fly ash from coal-driven power plants contains two orders of magnitude greater concentrations of radionuclides, aside from many other toxins, than the leakage of radionuclides from NPPs. Yet the authors ignore this comparison as well, instead focusing their research on targets that effectively promote radiophobia and nucleophobia, whether intentionally or not.

We have argued elsewhere that the official view of radioprotection in the United States, based on the hypothesized linear no-threshold (LNT) model of dose response, entails the denial of the well-established reality of DNA repair mechanisms.12,13 Despite its lack of validity, LNT use for regulatory purposes is often justified on grounds of the precautionary principle, even as its proponents acknowledge that the effect of low-dose and low-dose-rate radiation on cancer and mortality is so small that it could only be detected by infeasibly vast sample sizes.

Researchers who purport to find that minuscule radiation exposures raise cancer incidence or mortality are obligated to identify errors in studies with opposed conclusions – studies that, if valid, render their own work invalid. But instead of critiquing studies that come to conclusions incompatible with their own, researchers like the present Harvard authors generally act as though incompatible studies are not worth discussing, even though they may cite them for the appearance of completeness. They basically prefer to ignore rather than to refute, thereby keeping doubt alive.

We contend that this study 1 is an exercise in numerology, lacking any consideration of physics, biology, or the effects of radiation on humans. Even if their numerology turned out to be valid for the geographical areas they chose to examine – though they prohibit confirmation by concealing their data – their assumption that cancer incidence is attributable to leaked NPP radionuclides is baseless.

The imprimatur of Harvard should not be allowed to influence the credulity of peer reviewers and editors, leading to the publication of such unscientific studies in science journals. Furthermore, Harvard’s vaunted motto “Veritas” imposes a particular responsibility on its researchers to seek truth rather than promulgate misleading innuendo, a responsibility that applies no less to all other researchers but one that is particularly vital for a school of public health.
