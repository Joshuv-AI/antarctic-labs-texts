A retrospective study of the safety and efficacy of peritoneal dialysis catheter placement under combined local infiltration anesthesia and monitored anesthesia care
Authors: Li Fang, Jianghua Shen, Huhai Zhang, Ling Zhang, Xiaoling Zheng, Hongwen Zhao, Jun Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveGiven the lack of global consensus on anesthesia selection for peritoneal dialysis catheter (PDC) placement via open surgery, this study investigates the safety and efficacy of combining local infiltration anesthesia (LIA) with monitored anesthesia care (MAC) in patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD).MethodsThis retrospective analysis of ESRD patients who underwent open surgical placement of PDC in the Department of Nephrology, the First Affiliated Hospital of the Army Medical University from 1 August 2020 to 31 May 2022. Patients were categorized into two groups based on anesthesia method: LIA group and LIA + MA group. MA was defined as preoperative sedation (0.2–0.7 µg/kg/h) with dexmedetomidine injection (Huidrican trade name, DEX), and intraoperative analgesia with dexrazoxane injection (Garonin trade name, DEZ) as appropriate according to the patients’ pain scores. We compared the general clinical data of the two groups of patients, the changes in blood pressure and heart rate during the whole operation, the intraoperative and postoperative pain, the total duration of the operation and the immediate postoperative complications.ResultsThe study included 123 patients (59 in the LIA + MAC group and 64 in the LIA group). The LIA + MA group exhibited lower pain scores measured by Visual Analogue Scale(VAS) during surgery (skin incision, subcutaneous adipose tissue dissection, anterior fascia, muscle traction, posterior fascia, peritoneum, and catheterization) compared to the LIA group(p<0.05). In terms of surgical incisions, to intraoperative pain scores (VRS), the LIA + MA group showed higher score level I and lower score level II compared to the LIA group (p = 0.002, 0.004, respectively). The LIA + MA group experienced lower postoperative resting pain (NRS) and VAS than the LIA group (p = 0.001,0.003, respectively). The surgical duration for the LIA + MA group was shorter than that of the LIA group (p<0.001). Preoperative systolic and diastolic blood pressures (SBP and DBP) were higher in the LIA + MA group compared to the LIA group (p<0.001,<0.001, respectively). Postoperative heart rate and DBP were lower in the LIA + MA group (p<0.001, 0.004, respectively). The LIA + MA group exhibited greater changes in heart rate, SBP, and DBP during and after surgery compared to the LIA group (p = 0.009, <0.001,<0.001, respectively). In terms of immediate postoperative complications, the proportion of patients requiring analgesics within 24 h post-surgery was significantly lower in the LIA + MA group (p = 0.031).ConclusionOpen surgery for PDC placement under LIA + MAC is both safe and effective.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-024-02751-y.

Introduction

Peritoneal dialysis (PD) is a widely utilized renal replacement therapy for patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD). It offers significant advantages, including better preservation of residual renal function, improved quality of life, and fewer cardiovascular complications compared to hemodialysis. Despite these benefits, the success of PD largely depends on the effective placement of the peritoneal dialysis catheter (PDC), which allows for the exchange of dialysate within the peritoneal cavity.

The method of PDC placement has evolved over time, with open surgery being one of the most common techniques. This procedure requires careful consideration of anesthesia, as ESRD patients often present with multiple comorbidities that can complicate anesthetic management. Anesthesia for PDC placement aims to provide adequate analgesia and sedation while minimizing hemodynamic instability and postoperative complications.

Globally, approximately 11% of patients opt for peritoneal dialysis (PD) [1]. PD offers benefits such as reduced economic and time burdens and lesser cardiac load [2, 3]. Internationally, the most prevalent method for peritoneal dialysis catheters (PDCs) insertion is open surgery [4]. Selecting an appropriate anesthesia is crucial for successful PDC placement. Currently, local infiltration anesthesia (LIA), transverse abdominal plane block (TAPB), subarachnoid, or general anesthesia (GA) are the commonly used methods [5–7]. Yet, a global consensus on the optimal anesthesia for PDC placement is lacking. While GA provides adequate analgesia and ease of operation, it poses a high risk for end-stage renal disease (ESRD) patients with a physical condition score of ≥ 3 as per the American Anesthesiology Society [8]. In contrast, TAPB is relatively safe but often insufficient in analgesia [5] and has a higher risk of necessitating a switch to GA [6, 9, 10]. Strong opioids for analgesia/sedation can lead to blood pressure reduction, respiratory depression, addiction, and potential lethality, making them suboptimal for ESRD patients [11]. Notably, although dexmedetomidine (Huidrican trade name, DEX) might cause respiratory depression [12], at doses of 0.2-0.7ug/kg/h, it is less likely to do so and can provide cooperative or semi-conscious sedative hypnotic effects, aiding in relieving tension and pain [13, 14]. However, it cannot fully replace other analgesics. Dezocine (Garonin trade name, DEZ), with its significant analgesic effect manifesting 15 min post-intravenous administration, poses a lower risk of respiratory depression and addiction [15]. The synergistic effects of DEX combined with analgesics can act as a secondary rescue analgesic. The safety and effectiveness of LIA combined with monitored anesthesia (MA) in open surgical PDC placement is under-researched. This study examines the efficacy of LIA combined with MA in open surgical catheterization.

Methods

This study received approval from the First Affiliated Hospital Ethics Committee of the Army Medical College (Ethics Approval No. (B) KY2022139). Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects, a legal surrogate, parents, or legal guardians for minor subjects. We conducted a retrospective study, collecting clinical data from 123 ESRD patients who underwent open PDC insertion surgery in the renal department of the First Affiliated Hospital of the Army Medical University between August 2020 and May 2022. Patients were allocated into two groups: the LIA group (n = 64) and the LIA + MA group (n = 59).

Inclusion criteria

ESRD patients aged 18 to 65 years, undergoing intraperitoneal placement of a double polypropylene cuff Tenckhoff catheter via open surgery. The patients had no prior surgery or a prior PD catheter.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with uncontrolled acute heart failure, respiratory failure, body mass index (BMI) > 30 kg/m2 or < 18.5 kg/m2, skin lesions at the puncture site, chronic pain, exposure to anesthetics or dependency on opioids or NSAIDs within the past year, and impairment in real pain perception, abstract understanding, visual acuity, or writing ability due to cerebrovascular or mental disorders.

We recorded preoperative clinical data and general information (gender, age, body mass index(BMI), hemoglobin (Hb), blood albumin (Alb), blood creatinine (Cr), glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), underlying diseases, presence of hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, etc.). The type and dose of preoperative and intraoperative medication to DEX (ug), DEZ (mg), ropivacaine (mg), lidocaine (mg), after treatment, if the vital signs or pain cannot be controlled, it will be transferred to GA, and the salvage dose of DEZ during the operation is 2 ~ 5 mg. The use of analgesics, nausea, vomiting, dizziness and other adverse reactions within 24 h after surgery was recorded, the rescue analgesic within 24 h after surgery was Siweipu, and if it was ineffective, intramuscular tramadol injection 100 mg was used, and the catheter position was evaluated by abdominal plain film 1 day after surgery, and 500 ml of intraperitoneal perfusion was started each time, indwelling for 2 h, 2 times a day, and the patient gradually increased the dose of peritoneal dialysis after adaptation. There are very few cases of infiltration. Immediate complications (bleaching, leakage, bleeding, etc.) within 1 week after surgery. Systolic blood pressure(SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), heart rate, and operation time in and out of the operating room (preoperative and postoperative) were recorded. The doctor scores the patient according to the degree of pain during and after the operation; Patient satisfaction (within 24 h after surgery), physician satisfaction (scoreable after surgery).

Definitions

Visual Analogue Scale(VAS) [16]: Patients rated their physical pain on a scale from 0 (no pain) to 10 (most severe pain), with no pain (0), mild pain (1–2), moderate pain (3–4), pain (5–6), severe pain (7–8), and unbearable pain (9–10). The VAS score was performed during the separation of tissue and from 12 h to 24 h after the operation.

Verbal Rating Scale (VRS): Grade 0: No pain; Grade I (mild): pain is tolerable, life is normal, and sleep is not disturbed; Grade II (moderate): The pain is obvious, intolerable, requires analgesics, and disturbs sleep. Grade III (severe): The pain is severe, unbearable, requires analgesic drugs, and severe sleep disturbance may be accompanied by autonomic nervous disorder or passive posture. VRS score was performed for intraoperative incision and tunnel pain.

Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) [17]: This scale has a total score range from 0 to 10, where 0 indicates no pain; 1–3 denotes mild pain (pain that does not disrupt sleep); 4–6 represents moderate pain; 7–9 signifies severe pain (pain that prevents sleep or causes awakening); and 10 equates to excruciating pain. Resting pain was scored by NRS within 12 h after surgery.

Anesthesia and surgical procedure

All patients were fasted from water for at least 8 h before surgery, and all patients underwent open abdominal surgery to place full-length PDC, and the surgery and LIA were performed by the same experienced nephrology physician, and the MA was completed by the same senior anesthesiologist. All patients were given low-flow oxygen with nasal cannula, and blood pressure, heart rate, respiration, and oxygen saturation were measured in the supine position during the operation, and patients were intermittently asked whether they had pain and nausea. In the LIA + MA group, intravenous infusion of DEX 0.2 ~ 0.7 µg/kg/h before surgery was used to maintain the patient’s alertness/sedation (0AA/S) score of > 3 points.

In the LIA group and the LIA + MA group, diluted with 1% ropivacaine 10 ml + 2% lidocaine 10 ml + normal saline 10 ml, 15 ml of layer-by-layer LIA was extracted, and a vertical incision of about 4 ~ 6 cm was made on the left side of the umbilicus and pubic symphysis at 9 ~ 13 cm, and the subcutaneous fat was separated to reach the anterior and posterior sheath of the rectus abdominis muscle, and a 5 − 0 absorbable suture was used on the posterior sheath for purse suture, the posterior sheath was lifted, the posterior sheath was cut with tissue scissors, and the posterior sheath and peritoneum were separated. The opening of about 1 cm long was cut on the peritoneum, the PDC was slowly inserted into the rectal depression through the abdominal wall, the function and position of the catheter were tested by perfusion and flow out with 250 ml of normal saline, the peritoneal purse suture was ligated after satisfaction, the PDC cuff was anchored between the posterior rectus abdominis sheath and the anterior sheath with absorbable thread, and the DEX was discontinued after the catheter was successfully placed. Place the PDC using a tunnel needle after subcutaneous LIA(the remaining 15 ml) in the pre-set catheter exit area. The dose of DEZ or LIA was increased according to the patient’s intraoperative pain level. During the operation, the nephrology department and anesthesiologist jointly decide whether to transfer to GA according to the patient’s pain level.

Statistics

Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism statistical software, version 8.0. Results

General information

The study included 123 patients (78 males and 45 females), divided into the LIA group (n = 64) and the LIA + MA group (n = 59). The participants had a range of underlying diseases, including chronic glomerulonephritis (18 cases), diabetic nephropathy (5 cases), gouty nephropathy (1 case), membranous nephropathy (3 cases), monoclonal gamma globulin nephropathy (1 case), hepatitis B-related nephritis (1 case), rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis (4 cases), subacute tubulointerstitial nephritis (5 cases), and unknown etiology (85 cases). Thirty patients had concurrent cardiovascular disease and 5 had pulmonary disease. No significant differences were found between the two groups in terms of gender, BMI, biochemical indicators and preoperative complications. Detailed data are available in Table 1.

Effect of surgery on patient hemodynamics

The preoperative systolic and diastolic blood pressures were higher in the LIA + MA group compared to the LIA group (p<0.001 for both). Postoperatively, both the heart rate and diastolic blood pressure in the LIA + MA group were lower than those in the LIA group (p<0.001, 0.004, respectively). The variations in heart rate, systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure observed pre-and post-surgery were more pronounced in the LIA + MA group compared to the LIA group (p = 0.009, <0.001, <0.001, respectively). Immediate postoperative complications

In terms of immediate postoperative complications, the percentage of patients requiring analgesics within the first 24 h post-surgery was significantly lower in the LIA + MA group (p = 0.031).Postoperatively, the LIA group experienced 5 cases (7.813%) of catheter migration, 1 case (1.56%) of leakage, and 1 case (1.56%) of tunnel bleeding. In the LIA + MA group, there were 3 cases (5.08%) of catheter migration, 1 case (1.56%) of leakage, and 2 cases (3.39%) of nausea and vomiting. However, no differences were noted in postoperative nausea, vomiting, and other immediate complications within one week of surgery, as detailed in Table 5. This indicates that LIA + MA may still have analgesic effect within 24 h after surgery, and other complications may still occur.

Discussion

Throughout the various stages of the surgery, including skin incision, subcutaneous fat peeling, anterior sheath, muscle tension, posterior sheath, and PDC placement through the tension of the peritoneum, the pain score assessed by the VAS was consistently lower in the LIA + MA group. This suggests that DEX may offer effective analgesic properties for abdominal organs, possibly due to its dual action of sedation and analgesia, akin to “natural sleep.” [18] DEX’s extensive application in laparoscopic, cataract, and dental surgeries supports this finding [19, 20]. An analysis of research results revealed no significant difference in intraoperative and postoperative pain associated with PDC placement via subcutaneous tunnel. This could be linked to the administration of local anesthetics in both groups before the procedure. Notably, the LIA + MA group experienced considerably less pain during and after the surgery in terms of the surgical incision. Furthermore, there was a marked reduction in post-surgical painkiller usage. The study also indicated that LIA + MA was associated with minimal serious side effects, limited to nausea and vomiting, and was deemed safe for surgical procedures. Both patients and nephrologists expressed greater satisfaction with the anesthesia quality in the LIA + MA group, echoing the findings of Jiang et al [21].

Due to the frequent incidence of hypertension and heart failure in patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD), effective sedation/pain management during surgery is crucial for maintaining stable hemodynamics. Anesthesia drugs commonly cause bradycardia and hypotension in a dose-dependent manner. In this study, administering 0.2–0.7 ug/kg/h of dexmedetomidine (DEX) led to statistically significant changes in heart rate, systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure pre- and post-surgery in both groups. This effect might stem from DEX’s α-receptor-mediated vagal nerve influence and vasodilation [22], underscoring DEX’s vital role in stabilizing hemodynamics. To minimize the use of strong opioids like fentanyl and morphine [23, 24], notorious for their abuse and addiction issues globally [25], DEX was chosen for sedation/pain relief to prolong perioperative pain control and reduce post-anesthesia recovery duration. The DEX dose was adjusted based on the patient’s pain score. For intraoperative salvage analgesic treatment, the LIA group received ropivacaine + lidocaine in 50/64 (78.12%) cases, while the LIA + MA group used DEZ in 40/59 (67.80%) cases at dosages of 2 mg-5 mg. All patients successfully underwent PDC implantation.

In this research, there was no significant difference in immediate complications within one week post-surgery between the two patient groups undergoing PDC placement. Although the Seldinger technique can effectively create a pathway for PD patients, its lack of direct visual access to the peritoneum, even under ultrasound guidance, poses risks of mechanical complications and intra-abdominal organ damage [26], with a high incidence of leakage. A study by Japanese scholar Fukunaga et al. [27] reported a 5.1% postoperative catheter leakage rate following PDC placement. Similarly, Henderson et al [28] observed a 10% leakage rate. In our study, the leakage rate was below 2%, possibly owing to the use of absorbable sutures for the closure of the peritoneal sheath and peritoneum, which resulted in fewer openings and less damage. While TAPB offers effective nerve blockade, its need for pre-surgical ultrasound guidance by a sonographer and time-consuming nature restrict its use in PDC placement. A key benefit of LIA + MA is the reduction in operation time. The average operation duration was 64.36 ± 2.195 min, compared to 51 to 259 min for TAPB under ultrasound [9, 29], attributed to the coordination required between sonographers and anesthesiologists. Our findings indicate that LIA + MA not only saves time but also provides effective analgesia, yielding favorable clinical and surgical outcomes with fewer postoperative complications, thus offering a novel approach to PDC placement.

Our study has several potential limitations. First, it was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size. Second, in this study, considering that DEX may cause respiratory depression side effects, and considering that DEX is mainly composed of sedation and supplemented by analgesia, the dose of DEX was not increased or decreased according to the pain degree of patients, but DEZ analgesia was administered. Third, the study focused only on immediate postoperative complications, without long-term follow-up.

Conclusion

The LIA + MAC group had significantly lower intraoperative pain scores and shorter operation durations. They also experienced less postoperative resting and activity-related pain. Hemodynamic was a greater effect in the LIA + MAC group, with fewer immediate postoperative complications and lower analgesic requirements. Open surgery for PDC placement under LIA + MA is safety and efficacy.
