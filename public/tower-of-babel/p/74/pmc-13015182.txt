Social cues from neighboring patches influence patch dynamics in a spatially structured population of a colonial bird
Authors: Killian A Gregory, Charlotte Francesiaz, Jean‐Yves Barnagaud, Pierre‐André Crochet, Jean‐Dominique Lebreton, Aurélien Besnard, Julien Papaïx
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe dynamics of spatially structured populations (SSPs) depend on dispersal movements between habitat patches. Individual‐level studies show that dispersal can be informed by social cues about habitat quality, such as breeding success or the number of conspecifics in a patch. However, informed dispersal has rarely been considered in studies investigating SSP dynamics. In particular, little is known about how patch dynamics are influenced by characteristics of neighboring patches, and the spatial scale of this influence. Here, we used occupancy and growth rate models to investigate the effect of breeding success and numbers in all patches of an SSP of black‐headed gulls (Chroicocephalus ridibundus) on patch colonization, persistence and growth rates. The spatial scale of influence of neighboring patches was estimated using a distance decay function weighting the effect of patch covariates. Our results showed a strong influence of breeding success and breeding numbers, both in the focal patch and in neighboring patches, on patch dynamics. Persistence was higher for patches occupied by large and successful colonies, suggesting high attractiveness of such colonies, although their growth rate was reduced, probably due to increased costs of group‐living. Patch colonization rates were higher within a few kilometers from patches occupied by large and successful colonies, in accordance with an attraction of dispersers to areas showing favorable breeding conditions. The breeding success of neighboring patches from the entire SSP reduced patch persistence, but only for patches that failed to produce chicks, and negatively affected the growth rate of persisting colonies, consistent with individuals moving away towards higher quality habitats. These results advocate for the use of social information in black‐headed gull dispersal decisions. However, we were unable to distinguish between the use of breeding success (public information) and breeding numbers (conspecific attraction), which were highly correlated. Our findings emphasize the need to consider neighboring patches as dynamic components of the landscape that actively shape dispersal decisions, with consequences on SSP dynamics.Keywords: black‐headed gull, Chroicocephalus ridibundus, colonies, demography, informed dispersal, Larids, patch occupancy, social information

INTRODUCTION

Spatially structured populations (SSPs) consist of population units occupying discrete habitat patches connected by dispersal movements (Bowne & Bowers, 2004; Clobert et al., 2012; Hanski, 1998). Theoretical and empirical evidence shows that dispersal between patches is not random in heterogeneous and temporally variable environments (Bowler & Benton, 2005). Dispersal can be informed by a variety of cues and signals that allow individuals to select patches of better quality than by chance, as long as habitat quality exhibits some degree of temporal predictability (Bocedi et al., 2012; Clobert et al., 2009; Reed et al., 1999). In addition to considering environmental cues and their own individual condition, a number of species rely on social information to guide their emigration and settlement decisions (Danchin et al., 2004). Social information, such as the number or breeding success of conspecifics or heterospecifics (Boulinier et al., 2008; Buxton et al., 2020; Danchin et al., 2004; Doligez et al., 2004), can be collected at different spatial scales, from neighbors on the same patch (Bled et al., 2011; Parejo et al., 2006) to distant patches, depending on the mobility of the species (Kralj et al., 2023; Oro et al., 2021).

A small number of studies have examined how individuals integrate information from different spatial scales in dispersal decisions. For breeding dispersal more specifically, an individual's breeding success is often the main determinant of whether or not to disperse (Brown et al., 2000; Hénaux et al., 2007). Yet, dispersal decisions based on individual breeding success can be overridden by the success of conspecifics within the same patch (Boulinier & Danchin, 1997; Danchin et al., 1998), which provides more integrative and reliable information on site quality. As demonstrated in black‐legged kittiwakes (Rissa tridactyla), dispersal costs are likely to favor sequential dispersal decisions, in which local information is first used to decide whether and where to disperse before information from larger spatial scales is considered (Acker et al., 2017). In some species, however, no clear relationship between dispersal and distance between patches has been identified (Fernández‐Chacón et al., 2013; Spendelow et al., 1995), suggesting that individuals may alternatively be informed about all patches and decide to disperse to the best patch in a best‐of‐n strategy (Ponchon et al., 2021). Therefore, information from neighboring patches can influence dispersal decisions in multiple ways, but the spatial scale at which it is considered by individuals is not well understood.

As dispersal strongly shapes the colonization, extinction and the demographics of patches within SSPs (Bowne & Bowers, 2004; Hanski, 1998), information from neighboring patches can be expected to play a significant role in SSP dynamics. Historically, dispersal in SSP dynamics has mainly been studied in the context of metapopulation theory, which assumes uninformed dispersal mechanisms driven by the size of source patches, inter‐patch distances and habitat permeability, all of which dictate how patches influence each other (Clobert et al., 2012; Moilanen & Nieminen, 2002). However, informed dispersal results in different dynamics within SSPs compared to random dispersal (Greene, 2003; Ponchon et al., 2015; Smith & Peacock, 1990), and it is likely that dispersal decision rules shape how patches influence each other's dynamics. At a small scale, for instance, Bled et al. (2011) showed that conspecific density and breeding success of neighbors contribute to nesting site persistence within a colony of black‐legged kittiwakes. More generally, agent‐based models suggest that informed dispersal relying on cues from neighboring patches increases the occupancy rate of high‐quality patches, although it reduces the overall occupancy rate of patches in the SSP compared to random dispersal (Delgado et al., 2014; Ponchon et al., 2015; Schmidt, 2017). Yet, despite behavioral studies showing the widespread occurrence of informed dispersal (Clobert et al., 2009; Reed et al., 1999) and some of its consequences on SSP dynamics having been theorized (e.g., Ponchon et al., 2015; Schmidt, 2004), informed dispersal remains often overlooked when investigating SSP dynamics in empirical systems. As a result, how patches interact through information transfer and how such interactions influence patch dynamics at the scale of the SSP remains unclear (Lister, 2014; Ponchon et al., 2021).

Colonial species that aggregate on clearly delimited breeding patches offer several advantages for the study of informed dispersal and its consequences on SSP dynamics. Dispersal movements between breeding patches appear to be quite frequent in many colonial species and can be the main driver of colonization and extinction events, as individuals often leave their patch during the nonbreeding season and face a new choice of breeding site when they return each year (e.g., Oro & Ruxton, 2001; Serrano & Tella, 2003). Additionally, seminal work on informed dispersal in colonial species demonstrated the use of conspecific numbers or breeding success in dispersal decisions, with observable effects on the functioning of the SSP (Danchin et al., 1998; Shields et al., 1988). Colonial waterbirds, in particular, form SSPs on discrete habitat patches with various degrees of philopatry and, as long‐lived species, offer multiple opportunities to investigate dispersal movements (Coulson, 2016).

Here, we investigate how the information carried by neighboring patches in an SSP affects patch dynamics, and at what spatial scale. We focused on a population of black‐headed gulls (Chroicocephalus ridibundus) in central France, where individuals breed in colonies on a network of human‐managed freshwater ponds. The high dispersal dynamics between ponds mean that this SSP is closer to being a patchy population than a metapopulation (Harrison, 1991). Building on tools used to investigate the spatial scale of structuring of SSPs (Barnagaud et al., 2015; Papaïx et al., 2021), we examined whether, and at what distance, the breeding success and the size of neighboring colonies affect (1) colony persistence and patch colonization rate and (2) the growth rates of persistent patches. Assuming that social information influences dispersal decisions, large colonies and colonies that succeeded in breeding the previous year should experience enhanced patch fidelity (Boulinier et al., 2008; Danchin et al., 1998; Serrano & Tella, 2003) and attractiveness (Danchin et al., 1998; Oro & Ruxton, 2001). In fact, previous work on the same population showed higher extinction rates for colonies that failed to produce chicks (Lebreton, 1987) and higher dispersal towards large colonies (Grosbois et al., 2003; Péron, Lebreton, & Crochet, 2010b). We therefore first expected to find increased persistence for patches occupied by large or successful colonies. We further expected neighboring patches to have an additional effect on patch dynamics, with different outcomes depending on their distance from the focal patch. Neighboring patches occupied by large or successful colonies may serve as a cue that the nearby area offers favorable breeding conditions and thus attract individuals to surrounding patches, thereby increasing patch colonization and persistence at short distances. On the contrary, distant neighboring patches occupied by successful or large colonies may draw individuals away from patches occupied by small or unsuccessful colonies, further reducing their persistence. The increased attractiveness of patches occupied by large and successful colonies could have two opposite consequences on patch growth rates. An increased dispersal rate towards attractive areas could lead either to an increase in breeding numbers if resources are not limiting, or to a decrease in breeding numbers if competition gets the upper hand.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study species and study area

The black‐headed gull is a medium‐sized colonial bird species that breeds in coastal and continental wetlands, mostly from western Europe to eastern Asia. Populations are mainly migratory and shift southwards during the nonbreeding season, most of them spending the winter near the coasts, lakes or large rivers of southern Europe and Asia down to the coast of central Africa. Between March and July, during their breeding season, they gather in colonies of tens to thousands of breeding pairs, usually nesting on islets or vegetation on the edge of inland freshwater ponds, lakes, rivers, marshes, or coastal lagoons (del Hoyo et al., 1996).

The Forez plain is an agricultural lowland of about 750 km2 in central France, crossed from south to north by the Loire River. More than 350 human‐managed ponds, each measuring over 1000 m2 and featuring emerging vegetation belts and occasional islets, offer nesting sites for black‐headed gulls. The ponds are either isolated or clustered in plates of 2–30 ponds with less than 50 m between two adjacent ponds (Figure 1). Ponds are generally considered to be stable from 1 year to another, although they can be occasionally drained for one entire breeding season as part of their management, in order to promote the mineralization of sludge, limit eutrophication, and prevent the development of cyanobacteria. Drained ponds are never occupied by black‐headed gulls.

The plain is crossed from south to north by the Loire River. Main cities and roads have been represented as notable landmarks.

The population of black‐headed gulls breeding on the Forez plain numbers several thousand individuals and has been monitored since 1976 (Lebreton, 1981). More than 6000 breeding pairs occupied about 25 ponds at the beginning of the monitoring program. After a period of expansion in the 1980s–1990s, their numbers have steadily declined to 1500 breeding pairs spread over less than 10 ponds in 2019. Historical records from the monitoring program indicate large variations in pond drainage frequency, with a mean interval of 6.3 ± 6.9 years between successive drainings of the same pond. An average of 1.1 occupied ponds were drained each year over the study period (unpublished data).

Data collection

Drained ponds and ponds occupied by colonies have been identified each year between April and early May from 1976 to 2019, first by ground surveys and then from the early 1990s by airborne surveys. All identified colonies were then visited on the ground to check that birds were building nests or incubating and to estimate the number of breeding pairs. At the end of the breeding season, around mid‐June, the breeding success of the colonies was assessed from various sources (observation from the shore or from a floating blind and chick‐banding visits to the pond). Breeding output for the colony was recorded as “failure” when no chicks fledged for the entire colony or as “success” when at least one fledgling was detected on the pond (Lebreton, 1981).

Ninety‐five ponds have been occupied at least once by a colony over the study period (Figure 1). We recorded 129 breeding failures and 469 breeding successes in 775 colony‐years. Breeding success could not be assessed for 177 colony‐years due to difficult access to ponds.

Relationship between colony breeding success and number of breeding pairs

We fitted a binomial generalized mixed model for the probability of a colony to be successful as a function of the log‐transformed number of breeding pairs, while accounting for pond identity through a random intercept. A positive correlation was highlighted (see Results ). However, both breeding success and breeding numbers are likely to provide complementary information on patch dynamics with potentially different effects, which could appear despite their correlation if there is sufficient variation in breeding numbers within each category of breeding success, as seems to be the case in our data. Therefore, we decided to investigate both in separate models to avoid redundancy in covariates and compare the results.

Patch dynamics models

We defined a patch as a pond that hosted a colony at least once over the study period. Our aim was to quantify the effect of patch breeding success and breeding numbers (i.e., colony size) in year t−1, referred to as patch covariates, on patch dynamics between years t−1 and t.

We built two models: (1) a patch occupancy model related patch covariates with patch persistence and colonization probabilities (Hanski, 1994), and (2) a patch growth model related patch covariates with interannual growth rates of persistent patches (Kéry & Royle, 2016). Each of the two models was replicated twice to investigate either the effect of patch breeding success or the effect of patch breeding numbers. No observation error was modeled for breeding numbers, as the lack of repeated counts each year did not allow the estimation of an additional variance parameter. We assumed that the draining of a pond would have no effect other than causing the disappearance of the colony previously occupying it and preventing a new colony from establishing during the same year. Therefore, we accounted for pond draining by setting persistence and colonization probabilities on a pond to zero in years it was drained.

We assumed that the dynamics of focal patch i depended on its own patch covariate in year t−1. We considered that the covariates of all neighboring patches j∈1:J in year t−1 could also influence the dynamics of focal patch i, but we assumed the strength of this influence to depend on the Euclidean distance between the focal patch i and neighboring patch j. The covariates of neighboring patches were therefore weighted using a Gaussian‐shaped distance decay function with scale parameter σ, which indicates the width of the function and was estimated as a proxy for the spatial scale of influence of neighboring patches. This function is equivalent to a Gaussian kernel, which is standard for describing the diffusion of dispersers from neighboring patches with no directionality (Clobert et al., 2012), except that we used it to model the influence of neighboring patches via patch covariates rather than via a number of dispersers. The Gaussian shape further ensures that closer patches always have a stronger influence than more distant patches, while maintaining low variability in the influence of a neighboring patch over short distances. This is consistent with the assumption that most movements occur near the breeding site, and with the observation that gulls favor familiar sites in their dispersal decisions (Péron, Lebreton, & Crochet, 2010b).

Model implementation

The occupancy models included all ponds occupied at least once by a colony. The growth rate models, however, excluded the colony of La Ronze, which was by far the colony with the highest number of breeding pairs over most of the study period (between 11% and 77% of the breeding numbers in the SSP) and the highest stability (it persisted over the entire monitoring period but 1 year; Appendix S3: Figure S2). As a result, its growth dynamics differed strongly from those of the other colonies. We also removed one outlying interannual growth rate for a colony growing from one to 90 breeding pairs, resulting in an unusual growth rate of 90 that drove the results of the models (Appendix S2).

The models were implemented in a Bayesian framework using JAGS (Plummer, 2003) and the jagsUI package (Kellner, 2024) in R 4.2.0 (R Core Team, 2022). We used non‐informative priors for all parameters (Appendix S1). All models were run until convergence of MCMC chains, using a Gelman‐Rubin convergence diagnostic Rhat of less than 1.1 as a sign of convergence, confirmed by visual inspection of the trace plots (Gelman & Rubin, 1992).

RESULTS

Description of the dynamics of the study system

Patches exhibited a high turnover rate characterized by 220 colonization and 234 extinction events between 1976 and 2019, over 90% of which were independent from the draining of ponds. Colonization events led to a median settlement of 60 breeding pairs (interquartile range: 22; 140). Colonies that went extinct hosted a median of 42 breeding pairs in the year before extinction (15; 121). Persisting colonies exhibited a median growth rate of 1.00 (0.61; 1.33), and persisted for a median of 2 years (1; 4) on a pond. The median distance between two colonies in a given year was 14,283 m (7450; 18,099). The logarithm of breeding numbers on a patch was positively correlated with colony breeding success (coefficient = 1.08, SE = 0.12, Z = 8.890, p < 0.001; Appendix S3: Figure S1).

The breeding success in neighboring patches negatively affected persistence probability if the focal patch was occupied by a colony that failed to breed, but not if it was occupied by a colony that also bred successfully (Figure 2C). DISCUSSION

As expected from informed dispersal theory (Reed et al., 1999), the extinction, colonization, and demographic dynamics of black‐headed gulls colonies in the Forez plain were affected by their own size and breeding success in the previous year, but also by the characteristics of neighboring patches. Patches occupied by large or successful colonies in the previous year were more likely to persist, but their growth rate was reduced if they persisted. This reduction was even stronger in the presence of neighboring patches occupied by large or successful colonies anywhere on the plain in the previous year. Patches occupied by colonies that failed to produce chicks were sensitive to the breeding success of neighboring patches: if another patch succeeded somewhere on the plain, the likelihood of persistence for the unsuccessful patch diminished. New colonies were more likely to establish within a few kilometers of patches occupied by a large or successful colony. Due to the strong positive correlation between patch breeding success and breeding numbers in the same year, their respective influence as social information remained however indistinguishable.

Intrinsic patch dynamics: Demographic processes or information‐based fidelity/attraction

We found higher persistence probabilities for patches occupied by large and successful colonies, consistent with our predictions and previous findings (Lebreton, 1987). Two nonexclusive mechanisms could be involved: (1) Increased persistence may stem from intrinsic demographic processes, since large and successful colonies produce more juveniles and are less sensitive to stochastic events (Reed & Hobbs, 2004). However, given the moderate natal fidelity (50%–60%) and the age at recruitment of the species (2–5 years; Lebreton, 1987, Péron, Crochet, et al., 2010), survival and reproduction likely play a minor role in explaining the median colony persistence of 2 years that we observed here. (2) Instead, the increased persistence of patches occupied by large and successful colonies is more likely driven by increased patch fidelity or attractiveness, which finds support in theoretical models and some other species (Barbraud et al., 2003; Schmidt, 2004). The attractiveness of patches occupied by large and successful colonies aligns with the well‐documented use of social information in emigration and immigration decisions by colonial species (Boulinier et al., 2008; Danchin et al., 1998; Fernández‐Chacón et al., 2013; Serrano & Tella, 2003). Beyond serving as a simple cue to locate a breeding site, that is, a form of local enhancement, breeding numbers most likely provide a cue about the benefits of settling on a particular site (Valone, 2007). Larger breeding numbers indeed enhance group‐living benefits by providing protection from predators or information about resources and habitat (Birkhead, 1977; Ward & Zahavi, 1973), fostering conspecific attraction if social costs like disease transmission or competition remain low (Buxton et al., 2020; Kildaw et al., 2005; Stamps, 1988). Similarly, high breeding success may attract individuals, as it signals good reproductive prospects (Danchin et al., 1998).

The increase in patch attractiveness could have led to either an increase or a decrease in the growth rate of persistent patches, depending on the balance between the costs and benefits of colonial life. Here, growth rates were negatively correlated with patch breeding numbers and breeding success in the previous year, suggesting density‐dependent regulation. The high number of colonizers arriving on unoccupied patches and the median interannual growth rate of 1.00 for persisting patches suggest that colonies were close to their carrying capacity, leaving even less room for growth in patches occupied by large and successful colonies (Appendix S3: Figure S3). Large breeding numbers and breeding success are generally associated with improved growth rates in colonial species (Brown et al., 2000; Parejo et al., 2006). However, the positive effect of colony size on growth rates can disappear in the largest colonies (Serrano et al., 2004). Negative density‐dependence in colonies arises as costs like competition and environmental risks intensify at high densities (Clode, 1993; Danchin & Wagner, 1997). In particular, resources appear to be limited in our study area, with a finite number of good quality nesting sites on ponds (Grosbois et al., 2003) and declining foraging resources on the plain, as suggested by the decline in population size since the 1990s (Péron, Crochet, et al., 2010). A proper assessment of resources available on each patch would provide more definitive insight into the potential for colonies to grow.

It is worth noting that the correlation between breeding success and patch growth rates may have been further biased by our coarse assessment of breeding success. Only a few individuals succeeding to raise fledglings led us to consider a patch as successful, while there may be a majority of unsuccessful individuals leaving the patch and contributing to reduce its growth rate. Therefore, our coarse assessment of colony breeding success may have limited our statistical power by lowering the sample size in the “breeding failure” category and by introducing some heterogeneity in the “breeding success” category. However, the fact that we detected differences between these two categories suggests that this binary variable still captured some of the cues used in dispersal decisions and was worth investigating. Nevertheless, future work would benefit from a more precise definition of breeding success, which could also reveal the specific cues used by individuals.

Short‐distance effect of neighboring patches

The hypothesis of social information use in dispersal decisions led us to predict distance‐dependent effects of neighboring patches on patch persistence and colonization probabilities. Indeed, characteristics from neighboring patches could contribute to drawing individuals towards or away from a given (focal) patch. At short distances, we expected “nearby” neighboring patches occupied by large and successful colonies to positively influence patch occupancy. Dispersers could indeed be attracted to nearby patches offering similar environmental conditions but with less competition (Kildaw et al., 2005), or where they may benefit from the proximity of the neighboring colonies, even when patch quality is lower, forming aggregation patterns similar to those observed in some territorial species (Nocera et al., 2009). In accordance with this hypothesis, we found that colonization probabilities for unoccupied ponds increased within a few kilometers of patches occupied by large or successful colonies. This finding extends previous conclusions on nesting site dynamics to colony dynamics (Bled et al., 2011). The spatial scale at which such an attraction can be expected should depend on a balance between the costs of dispersal, the social benefits of aggregation, and the spatial scale of autocorrelation in habitat quality, which determines the distance at which the information conveyed by a patch remains reliable (Fletcher and Sieving, 2010). In our system, the ponds and nearby fields where the gulls forage are managed by a number of local stakeholders, which can make environmental conditions change over a few kilometers and likely explain the short distance of attraction, while the costs of dispersal for our species at this spatial scale are likely to be negligible. Colonization near patches occupied by large and successful colonies fosters colony clustering, which is susceptible to scale up both the benefits and costs of colonial life. This phenomenon may also contribute to lowering the information barrier that limits colonization of empty patches when dispersal decisions are based on social cues (Forbes & Kaiser, 1994).

Long‐distance effect of neighboring patches

In contrast to their “short distance” effect on patch dynamics, more “distant” neighboring patches occupied by large and successful colonies may negatively affect patch occupancy. Indeed, distant neighboring patches conveying information about good breeding conditions can favor the relocation of individuals in their own vicinity (Fernández‐Chacón et al., 2013). Our results support again the use of informed dispersal, as patches that failed to produce chicks were indeed less likely to persist when patches occupied by successful colonies were present anywhere in the SSP. In this instance, the neighborhood considered in dispersal decisions included patches from all over the plain, with no effect of the distance to the focal patch. This suggests individuals left or avoided unsuccessful colonies when good breeding conditions could be expected elsewhere, causing the sudden colony extinctions observed in the SSP (Lebreton, 1987). Given the small size of the plain (~40 km), it is not surprising that gulls use information from all breeding sites. Some gulls equipped with GPS tags have been observed making daily trips anywhere on the plain, including visits to other ponds up to 30 km away from their current breeding site (unpublished results). This behavior could correspond to prospecting movements, whereby individuals assess the quality of some distant breeding sites. However, using information about all breeding patches may concentrate individuals in the same patches and increase local competition (Ponchon et al., 2021). In such a situation, the overflow of dispersers onto nearby patches likely contributes to the high colonization rate we observed for empty patches near attractive colonies, and to the previously reported queuing for breeding in large colonies (Péron, Lebreton, & Crochet, 2010b).

The interaction between breeding success in the focal patch and the effect of breeding success in neighboring patches on persistence further suggests that individuals compared the success in their patch to the success in all neighboring patches. This is in line with previous findings that emigration decisions are primarily driven by personal or local success rather than the performance of neighbors (Acker et al., 2017; Brown et al., 2000; Hénaux et al., 2007). Patch dynamics thus seem to mirror directly informed dispersal decisions unraveled at the individual level in colonial species.

Similarly, as they likely draw individuals away, we expected distant patches occupied by large and successful colonies to reduce local patch growth rates, which our results confirmed. However, the negative effect of distant neighboring patches on growth rates was only detected for patches occupied by successful colonies. While this result seems counterintuitive under an informed dispersal scenario where individuals compare breeding success across patches, it is consistent with the hypothesis of increased costs in successful patches, which could also drive emigration decisions. Individuals may indeed leave areas with high breeding success to avoid future competition (Doligez et al., 1999). Yet, it is difficult to conclude on a different effect of distant neighboring patches on the growth rates of successful and unsuccessful patches. Unsuccessful patches were more likely to go extinct than successful patches, especially in the presence of successful neighboring patches. Therefore, colonies on unsuccessful patches may not have had the opportunity to decline as colonies on successful patches did, resulting in a lower number of unsuccessful persistent patches compared to successful persistent patches in our dataset. This may have resulted in a lack of statistical power to detect a significant effect of neighboring patches on the growth rates of unsuccessful patches.

Is dispersal informed by breeding numbers or breeding success?

We have shown that social information is involved in SSP dynamics, but whether the observed patterns are generated by conspecific attraction (breeding numbers) and/or public information (breeding success) remains unclear. Although previous work showed that large colonies have a higher chance of producing fledglings compared to small colonies (Péron, Lebreton, & Crochet, 2010c), sufficient variance in the combinations of breeding success and breeding numbers could have enabled us to distinguish the effects of these two patch covariates on patch occupancy and growth rates. However, because they were still strongly correlated in our data, their effects remained indistinguishable. Either or both could inform dispersal decisions. Breeding success and colony size are correlated to various degrees in colonial species (Brown & Bomberger Brown, 1996), which often complicates studies on the use of public information in dispersal decisions (Doligez et al., 2004; Oro & Ruxton, 2001). Studies considering both public information and conspecific attraction generally highlight the latter as the strongest driver (Doligez et al., 2004; Fernández‐Chacón et al., 2013; Oro & Ruxton, 2001).

CONCLUSION AND PERSPECTIVES

Our study provides multiple lines of evidence for the role of informed dispersal in patch occupancy and growth dynamics. The distance‐dependent influence of neighboring patches indicates that black‐headed gulls do not select their breeding patch at random, but make an informed decision. Our results extend previous findings on the effect of breeding success and numbers on nesting site persistence to understand patch dynamics within SSPs (Bled et al., 2011) and provide empirical confirmation of the predicted asymmetry between stable high‐quality patches and the turnover of low‐quality patches due to informed dispersal (Barbraud et al., 2003; Ponchon et al., 2015). It would be interesting to test these models on other systems to verify the generality of our results in different ecological contexts, particularly when the frequency of dispersal is lower and movements are more restricted.

Fully understanding the link between colony dynamics and the use of social information would require monitoring variations in intrinsic patch quality, which can influence patterns of conspecific attraction or act as additional factors directly influencing colony persistence or growth capacity in interaction with social cues (e.g., Betts et al., 2008; Buxton et al., 2017; Pärt et al., 2011). Although pond vegetation affects nest availability and thus patch carrying capacity, and has changed since the beginning of the monitoring programme (Francesiaz et al., 2017), changes from one year to another are expected to be slow, ensuring that this aspect of habitat quality remains predictable between successive years. However, the importance of the immediate environment of ponds in foraging remains unclear (see Prévot‐Julliard & Lebreton, 1999), as does the presence of predators on colony persistence, which effects have been demonstrated in other colonial species (e.g., Payo‐Payo et al., 2018). Finally, differences in colony composition could also explain part of the observed dynamics. Experienced individuals that rely on their own assessment of habitat quality may exhibit high philopatry and explain high persistence of high‐quality patches, while younger individuals with low experience could rely more on conspecific attraction and form unstable small aggregations in low‐quality patches, or be more involved in colonization processes (Nocera et al., 2009; Ward et al., 2010).

The mobility of black‐headed gulls at a regional scale allowed us to model the influence of all neighboring patches as a function of inter‐patch distance only. However, the spatial structure and connectivity within the SSP also determine patch dynamics (Fortuna et al., 2006). Future developments could take into account patch density, which may be particularly appropriate when social dynamics are structured across multiple patches. For species where dispersal between all patches is not possible, landscape connectivity metrics could also be implemented instead of Euclidean distance to model the influence of neighboring patches. By uncovering patterns that emerge from dispersal decisions, population‐level studies like ours complement individual‐level studies on informed dispersal. A promising avenue would be to integrate both perspectives by combining capture‐mark‐recapture or GPS data with patch dynamics, for example, by using multisite integrated population modeling. Such a multilevel perspective would be particularly relevant to investigate population patterns that emerge from collective dispersal, which we were unable to account for. Our results highlight the need to consider neighboring patches as dynamic landscape components actively shaping dispersal decisions. Understanding dispersal mechanisms and decision rules is thus crucial for a comprehensive view of SSP dynamics.
