Season of birth in Puerto Rico: Rethinking changes in patterns using 90 years of births occurring under US occupation
Authors: Alexis R Santos-Lozada, Wilmarí De Jesús Álvarez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article examines birth seasonality in Puerto Rico and explores whether patterns shift during periods of economic transformation. We compiled monthly birth counts for approximately 5.64 million births occurring from 1935 to 2024. Data sources include reports to U.S. agencies (1935–1966), a prior publication (1967–1971), and the Puerto Rico Vital Statistics System (1972–2024). To assess the temporal concentration, we calculated a seasonal index of births and modeled 5-year patterns with LOESS curves. We identified four distinct seasonal patterns. From 1943 to 1949, births peaked in April – May. Between 1950 and 1954, this peak declined as a new one emerged from August to October. Beginning in 1955, a third pattern appeared, with fewer births early in the year and a peak in the second half. This pattern remained stable until about 2010. After 2010, we observe (1) a narrowing of early-year minimums, (2) increased within-period variability, and (3) a decline in December, though (4) the August to November peak persists. These changes align with economic transitions that occurred under US occupation, bringing forth the need to reexamine the hypothesis of cultural or media influence in future studies. Given observed patterns, future research should explore the role that economic shifts may have in shaping birth seasonality in Puerto Rico.

Introduction

Seasonal birth patterns have long been a topic of interest to biodemographers, social biologists, human ecologists, and researchers in other disciplines (Condon and Scaglion 1982; Warren and Tyler 1979). In Puerto Rico, studies on this matter are scarce and date back to the 1980s, with the notable absence of emerging lines of inquiry and evaluation of hypotheses on these matters. Recently, an emerging line of work has started to (re)visit how colonization and post-colonial dynamics affect individual and long-term prospects (Marein 2020). This topic is not new, demographic studies have long attempted to figure out how the regime changes influence fertility or birth dynamics (Billingsley 2010; Doepke 2004). Puerto Rico is not the exception, with two studies conducted over 40 years ago having approached this issue (Cowgill 1964; Vazquez-Calzada and Rivera Acevedo 1982). Further, during the period analyzed in these studies, Puerto Rico was undergoing a transformation from a mainly agricultural economy to an industrialized one (Irizarry Mora 2002) and contextualization of these processes and the possible influence of economic or employment structures and transformations has not been incorporated in these discussions.

To illustrate the transition for a mainly agricultural economy to an industrialized one, we show employment structure for Puerto Rico between 1950 and 2020 in Figure 1. An examination of the employment structure across 70 years shows a notable shift in employment; from a mainly agricultural economy in 1950 to one of higher concentrations in manufacturing and service. Most recently, since 2000, we note a reduction in total employment, lower number and percentages of manufacturing jobs and higher percentages in employment in commerce and service. We believe that the discussion about seasonality in births should incorporate the element of economic transformations to better contextualize shifts in seasonal birth patterns.

The transformations in the economic structures could lead to changes in seasonality through many mechanisms. First, there is the issue of seasonality in work present in agricultural economies. In Puerto Rico, the sugar harvest season – locally known as “la zafra” - extended from January to May (McEniry et al. 2008). This was a period of increased work activity and often meant the absence of the agricultural worker from the house for extended periods due to seasonal migration to live closer to the sugar plantations and the main hubs of economic activity (Steward 1953). Employment figures compiled by McEniry et al. (2008) show substantial reductions in plantation employment in the second half of the year. With the change in the economic structure, due to industrialization process that started in the 1940s, there would lower likelihood of experiencing unemployment during the “slack” or “lean” seasons and the absence of the agricultural worker from home. Such seasonality is not present in the industrial activities established in Puerto Rico. Concerning changes in seasonality of births in Puerto Rico, within the biodemography and economic demography literature, the there are two competing hypotheses. However, examination of these hypotheses remains an area in need of exploration and expansion.

As mentioned before, researchers have put forward two possible hypotheses for the shift in birth seasonality observed in Puerto Rico in the first half of the 20th century. The first explanation argues that “greater contact with things American” (Cowgill 1964) is the mechanism that led to this change. The second explanation, though less developed in scholarly work, hints at the possible influence of economic structure in birth seasonality (Vazquez-Calzada and Rivera Acevedo 1982). If the first hypothesis is true, then we would not expect any disruption in seasonal patterns as there has not been any regime change since 1898. On the other hand, if the second explanation is true, we would expect some changes in seasonality as the economy of Puerto Rico transforms or experiences major shifts. In this study, we provide evidence that supports the second hypothesis. We show that changes in birth seasonality emerge in periods of economic transformations. Further support comes from the fact that absent major shifts in economic structures or in period of relative economic stability, the seasonal birth patterns remain relatively consistent.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

The Puerto Rico Vital Statistics System (PRVSS) registers information for every birth that occurs in Puerto Rico since 1931. In the earlier decades of the 1900s, the Government of Puerto Rico implemented a series of administrative measures and laws to assure that every birth that occurred in Puerto Rico was recorded in a timely manner. In 1931, the Government of Puerto Rico centralized the vital statistics registration and established/enforced regulations to limit the instances and length in which a recent born remained unregistered. Back then, the government established a $1 penalty if the registration occurred past 10 days after the birth (Janer 1958). This penalty is equivalent to $20 in 2025 when inflation is considered. To contextualize the value of a dollar back then, we turn to data on wages in the 1930s. Analysis of waves for this period indicate that for a man – the majority of workers – working in the sugar cane fields, the average wages were approximately 60 cents per week (Hanson 1937). Simply said, the late fee was not a trivial amount for the working-class population. With the establishment of PRVSS, birth registration improved markedly within a year of birth (Janer 1945; Vazquez 1968). Thus, we are confident that the data counts included in our study reflect the birth dynamics observed in Puerto Rico.

While the PRVSS has collected data for more than 90 years, no single dataset exists that contains information on monthly birth counts for Puerto Rico. Our first task was to compile our analytic dataset. To accomplish this, we accessed data from multiple sources. Data for the 1935–1948 period are from three reports produced by local or federal agencies concerning demographic and economic dynamics in Puerto Rico. These reports include: The Annual Report of the Governor of Puerto Rico, The Report by the Commissioner of Health, the Annual Book on Statistics of Puerto Rico. These reports were accessed through the Hathi Trust (www.hathitrust.org). Our second source is the annual report series titled Vital Statistics of the United States (1949–1966). We accessed these reports through the historical repository hosted by the National Center for Health Statistics (www.cdc.gov/nchs/). Data for the 1967–1971 period come from a study published in the Proceedings of the Social Statistics Section of the American Statistical Association (Bangdiwala 1973). Data for the 1972–2010 period are from the Annual Vital Statistics Report published by the Puerto Rico Department of Health available through the Puerto Rico Institute of Statistics (www.estadistica.pr.) and the Department of Demography at the University of Puerto Rico – Medical Science Campus (demografia.rcm.upr.edu). Data for the 2011–2024 period were obtained from the PRVSS through official petitions by the study authors. We also compiled monthly birth counts for 1919–1921 as data were available in the report series that we consulted to build our dataset. In sum, we have compiled a time-series that captures 5,796,146 births registered in Puerto Rico between 1935 and 2024, and for selected months preceding the establishment of the PRVSS.

Measures

Birth Counts and the Seasonal Index of Births (SIB)

The birth counts are the total births registered each specific month/year as reported in official tabulations. Using these birth counts, we calculate the Seasonal Index of Births (SIB). The SIB was calculated for each month by year; we divided the average number of births per day for each month by the average number of births per day of the year and the product is multiplied by 100 (Vazquez-Calzada and Rivera Acevedo 1982; Vlachadis et al. 2024). For leap years, we divided the average number of births by 366 days, otherwise the division is done by 365 days. If all months had an SIB of 100, this would mean the births are equally distributed across months (no seasonality). An SIB above 100 indicates that the observed births exceeded the annual average, and vice versa. The SIB is a well-established measure used in the study of birth seasonality in many disciplines.

Periods

Our dataset includes information for 90 years. To be able to detect and identify differences in seasonality, we model seasonal birth patterns in 5-year periods. Thus, seasonal birth patterns are modeled for 18 five-year periods. We also produced estimates for seasonality using data collected in the years preceding 1935 to illustrate patterns in the earlier years of the 1900s in Puerto Rico. However, we note these years preceded the establishment of the PRVSS, and past work has concluded published counts do not have full coverage. Statistical Analyses

In Table 1, we present descriptive statistics for the period of analysis. To visualize the underlying seasonality in births for each the 18 different time periods included in our analysis, we fit a series of LOESS curves – short for LOcally Estimated Scatterplot Smoothing – which allows for the visualization of patterns underlying data of interest (see Figure 2). The LOESS curve is a non-parametric technique that fits a series of low-degree polynomial regression models to the observations and plots it as a smooth curve (Cleveland, Devlin, and Grosse 1988; Cleveland and Devlin 1988). By displaying these curves, we were able to identify (1) the existence and magnitude of the period minimum (valley) and maximum (peak) and (2) changes in these patterns between 1935–2024.

Results

We present a basic description of our data and by period in Table 1. Our data includes information for about 5.3 million births recorded in Puerto Rico between 1935 and 2024. Between 1935 and 1939, recorded births added up to 347,985 and increased until 1950–1954. Since then, the number of births per period has reduced with the most recent period having 96,018 recorded births. The number of births recorded between 2020 and 2024 was represents 28% of the number of births recorded in between 1935 and 1939. The average number of births per month was 5,800 in 1935–1939, this indicator increased to 6,812 births per month in 1950–1954 and has also reduced since then. In 2020–2024, the most recent period, the average number of births per month was 1,600 births. The average births per month in the last period represents 27.5% of the average calculated for 1935–1939. Concerning the minimums and maximums, the lowest valley observed was 78.7 which corresponds to May 1966, and the highest peak was 128.8 which corresponds to September 2021.

In Figure 2, we present the birth seasonality for Puerto Rico for years for which data are available by period. In the first panel, we present birth counts for 1919–1921, and we confirm the existence of the peak in the earlier part of the year, even before major legislation on vital registration was implemented. In the next two panels, we present the patterns for 1935–1939 and 1940–1944. These patterns also show a peak in the earlier half of the year, in line with the patterns observed in the previous panel. The next two panels show data for 1945–1949 and 1950–1954, we observe a pattern that differs from the one observed in previous periods. In 1945–1949, there is no seasonal birth pattern. This is exemplified by the flat line. This points toward a disruption in seasonal birth patterns. For 1950–1954, we notice the presence of two peaks. The first is in the early months (similar to previous periods) and a newer peak in the later months of the year (previously unobserved).

The next panel is that for 1955–1959, we note a shift in birth seasonality with lower births observed in the early months of the year and a peak observed in the second half of the year. This pattern is also observed for all the periods between 1960 and 1979. Since 1980, the seasonal pattern of births is characterized by lower births in the first half of the year and a peak in September and October, with higher births also being observed in November and December. This pattern remained relatively stable until 2009. We observe an erosion of the higher levels in December and a narrowing of the minimums observed in the first months of the year. By 2015–2019, we note the following patterns: (1) slightly higher births in January, (2) narrow difference for the first 6 months of the year, (3) a slightly lower peak in the last quarter of the year, and (4) a reduction in the indicator for December (no longer as high as before). In the last panel, we show the pattern for 2020–2024. The seasonal birth pattern modeled for this period is in line with the one observed between 2015–2019.

Discussion

This study explores birth seasonality in Puerto Rico, with a focus on examining patterns in periods of economic transformations following the approval of economic reforms that promoted industrialization in 1947 (Dietz 1982). With industrialization also came the integration of women into the labor force in higher numbers, mainly in the needle and tobacco industry (Silvestrini de Pacheco 1979). Our analysis of birth seasonality adds 44 years to past studies conducted on this matter (Bangdiwala 1973; Cowgill 1964; Vazquez-Calzada and Rivera Acevedo 1982) and provides an overview of the economic transformation that occurred in Puerto Rico during the study period (see Figure 1). We observe a shift in seasonality as the economy transitioned from mainly agricultural economy to an industrial one. These patterns remained consistent until the 2000s, when the expiration of a series of tax-breaks to foreign companies led to the closure of manufacturing enclaves in Puerto Rico (Caraballo-Cueto and Lara 2017). During the 1990s, Puerto Rico experienced a series of exogenous shocks that contributed to the economic instability including the onset of a process of deindustrialization (Caraballo-Cueto and Lara 2017; Romero-Ramirez 2023; Santos-Lozada et al. 2020). The main shock being the Congressionally enacted phasing out of the tax-breaks to foreign corporations due to the US joining the World Trade Organization. These tax-breaks were the main component of Puerto Rico’s economic growth strategy, used to attract companies between 1976–2006.

Our descriptive findings suggest that seasonality in births is related to major economic shifts in Puerto Rico. The competing hypothesis, that this was a pattern drive by contact with the US, can be rejected for a variety of reasons. First, the US had been occupying Puerto Rico since 1898. If this was true, then we would have already observed a major shift by 1935. This did not occur. It is possible that the diffusion of ideas and contact with “things American” was also limited due to language differences. Attempts to make English the primary language of instruction in Puerto Rico were unsuccessful (Angrist, Chin, and Godoy 2008). To date, Spanish remains the principal language in Puerto Rico. Second, during most of the second half of the 1900s, the US allowed and encouraged the implementation of fertility control programs and forced sterilization (Gutierrez and Fuentes 2010; Warren et al. 1986). Despite this, the seasonal patterns remained consistent during this period.

Finally, even if diffusion of “things American” occurred, it was in a limited way. A content-analysis of prime-time news broadcasted in 1982 in Puerto Rico found that: (1) less than 15% of news stories broadcasted in Puerto Rican television and radio originated in the US and (2) ~65% originating in Puerto Rico, (3) ~12% originating in Europe/Latin America and (4) and the rest coming from other regions of the world (Canino and Huston 1986). While the structure of news programs was similar, the penetration of US stories into local discussion was limited. Thus, even after 84 years of US occupation, most of the news broadcasted in Puerto Rico did not deal with issues related to the US. We argue that the claim of increase contact with “things American” as the driver of the changes in seasonal birth patterns warrants examination. It is highly likely that the “North American pattern” that Cowgill refers to is simply a result of the influence of economic structures in biosocial processes. Future examinations of these competing hypotheses would do well to employ formal tests such as time-series break analyses, sectoral correlations, and cross-national studies/comparisons to examine the causal mechanisms underlying the patterns described in our study.

Will the ongoing economic crisis impact birth seasonality? We note that there are two ongoing processes concerning birth levels in Puerto Rico. First, there seems to be an ongoing process of disruption in birth seasonality in Puerto Rico. This pattern is characterized by higher variability in the seasonal index of births. This is evidenced by the wider confidence intervals for the seasonal curves in the most recent period. Note, such disruptions are rare before 2010. The second process is the downward trajectory in births which has been observed in Puerto Rico since the end of World War II (Warren 1987) and has continues in modern-day Puerto Rico (Caraballo-Cueto 2020). Past research conducted in contexts of economic crises have found that fertility dynamics are disrupted during such periods (Puig-Barrachina et al. 2020; Tragaki and Bagavos 2019). We believe this is occurring in Puerto Rico, but it is an issue that merits further evaluation. However, such pursuits are beyond the scope of this study.

The emergence of this new pattern occurs in a period characterized by economic instability. Clear signs or contributors to this instability are as follows: 1. the default on public debt due to the ongoing economic crisis (2016), 2. Hurricanes Irma and Maria and their aftermath (2017–2018), 3. the first ever removal of a sitting Governor due to a public uprising (2019), 4. seismic activity affecting the Southern region of Puerto Rico (late 2019 and early 2020), 5. the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic (2020-present), and Hurricane Fiona (2022) echoes of all this uncertainty are still felt today (Santos-Lozada and Mariola Rivera-Reyes 2023; Santos-Lozada et al. 2020). Additional studies on the issue of fertility and birth patterns in modern-day Puerto Rico are needed to illustrate and contextualize the dynamics of the 21st century.

Conclusion

Our findings revisit the discussion on what factors influence birth seasonality. We find that changes or disruptions in birth seasonality in Puerto Rico occur after major shifts in employment trends and/or economic structures. Future research should continue monitoring seasonality in births to be able to detect changes and determine the mechanisms that underlie them. Such examinations would do well to incorporate causal methods, to examine the competing hypotheses present in the academic literature. This is particularly relevant within the context of Puerto Rico, where economic fluctuations are highly related to the economic conditions of the United States. The close relationship between both economies is the result of the modern-day colonial arrangement between these countries. From a public health perspective, understanding whether and how economic structures and/or shifts influence birth patterns around the world can help countries prepare better for the needs of the population.
