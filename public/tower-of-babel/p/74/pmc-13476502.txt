Bowel Urgency in Ulcerative Colitis Is Common and Associated With Disability, With Mediation From Psychosocial Factors
Authors: Chung Sang Tse, Melissa G. Hunt, Lily A. Brown, Sara N. Horst, Samir A. Shah, Sumona Saha, Megan K. Lutz, Sasha Taleban, Hannah WF. Goodrich, S. Alandra Weaver, Raymond K. Cross, James D. Lewis, Crohn’s and Colitis Foundation’s Clinical Research Alliance’s ADEPT study team
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsBowel urgency is a distressing and disruptive symptom in adults with ulcerative colitis (UC). We hypothesized that bowel urgency is associated with disease-related disability and influenced by psychosocial factors.MethodsHierarchical multiple linear regression assessed baseline disease symptoms (severity of bowel urgency, stool frequency, and rectal bleeding) and psychosocial factors’ association with disability, using cross-sectional data from the ADEPT (Addressing Disability Effectively with Psychosocial Telemedicine) trial.ResultsAmong 241 adults with UC, 48% (116/241) had moderate-to-severe bowel urgency, and 75% (89/119) of participants without diarrhea or rectal bleeding experienced bowel urgency. Bowel urgency was strongly correlated with disability (ρ = 0.597; P < .0001), with mediation from negative illness perceptions, lower self-efficacy, and more health-related social needs (all P < .001). Gastrointestinal symptoms explained 40% of the variance in disability, and psychosocial factors accounted for an additional 19% (both P < .001).ConclusionBowel urgency occurs in 75% of patients with UC without diarrhea or rectal bleeding and is strongly associated with disability. Illness perceptions, self-efficacy, and health-related social needs mediate bowel urgency and disability.

Introduction

Bowel urgency is a distressing and disruptive symptom in adults with ulcerative colitis (UC).1 Disability reflects functional impairments and participation restrictions in life activities, including work, education, physical activities, and social interactions.2 Absence of disability is a long-term treatment target for patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).3 We hypothesized that bowel urgency is associated with disease-related disability and influenced by psychosocial factors.

We conducted correlational and regression analyses to determine the degree to which bowel urgency and psychosocial factors contribute to disability, independent of diarrhea and rectal bleeding,3 using prospectively collected data from ADEPT (Addressing Disability Effectively With Psychosocial Telemedicine), a randomized controlled trial that demonstrated the efficacy of cognitive behavioral therapy to reduce disability among patients with IBD.4

Methods

Adults aged ≥18 years with UC from 5 US centers were assessed for bowel urgency (11-point Urgency Numeric Rating Scale5), IBD-related disability (14-item IBD Disability Index),6 illness perceptions (Brief Illness Perceptions Questionnaire),7 self-efficacy (13-item IBD Self-efficacy Scale),8 disease knowledge (Crohn's and Colitis Knowledge Score),9 and health-related social determinants of health, including housing instability, food insecurity, and transportation barriers (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services Health-Related Social Needs screening tool).10 Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s beliefs about their agency to affect their health and disease outcomes.8

Independent-samples t-tests, Spearman’s ρ, and linear regression were used to evaluate associations among patient demographics, gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms, and disability.

Mediation analyses of psychosocial factors between bowel urgency and disability were performed using separate linear regression models for each psychosocial factor, with stool frequency and rectal bleeding adjusted for in all models.11 Psychosocial factors whose models with P < .05 on the F-test for ΔR2 were entered into a hierarchical multivariable linear regression analysis with 3 blocks were conducted to assess the relative contributions of GI symptoms (block 2) and psychosocial factors (block 3) to disability (the criterion), with patient demographics (block 1) tested as potential confounders. Disease symptoms were entered in 2 steps to delineate the contributions to disability from the cardinal UC symptoms (block 2a: stool frequency and rectal bleeding) and bowel urgency (block 2b). Unstandardized β with standard error and standardized β coefficient (standardized to 1 unit of standard deviation of the predictor variable) were reported at each step of the regression. In a sensitivity analysis, the order of blocks 2 and 3 was reversed. Variance inflation factors were all <3.0, indicating no significant multicollinearity among predictor variables.

Bonferroni correction was used to adjust for multiple comparisons. Nominal P values are reported. Statistical significance was set to P < .05. Data were analyzed using JMP, version 18.2.2 (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, Cary, NC); hierarchical regression analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 31 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

The protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia, PA). All study participants provided informed consent. All authors had access to the study data and approved the final manuscript.

Results

Bowel urgency was common, with nearly half (48.1%, 116/244) of the adults with UC experiencing moderate-to-severe bowel urgency (Supplementary Table 1). A majority (74.8% (89/199) of patients without diarrhea or rectal bleeding continued to have bowel urgency, with 22% (26/199) having moderate-to-severe urgency.

Bowel urgency was strongly correlated with disability (ρ = 0.597), negative illness perceptions (ρ = 0.591), more health-related social needs (ρ = 0.277), and lower self-efficacy (ρ = −0.500) (all ρs < 0.0001) (Supplementary Table 2). Even after adjusting for stool frequency and rectal bleeding, the association between bowel urgency and disability remained significant (β = 3.3; P < .001). Illness perceptions, self-efficacy, and health-related social needs partially mediated the association between bowel urgency and disability (attenuated β = 2.0, 2.5, and 3.0, respectively; all P < .001). Notably, IBD-related knowledge was not associated with bowel urgency or disability (all ps > 0.5).

In a hierarchical multiple linear regression (Table 1), patient demographics were not associated with disability (model 1: P = .371). GI symptoms accounted for 40% of the variance in disability: 28% from stool frequency and rectal bleeding (model 2a: ΔR2 = 0.276; P < .001) and 12% from bowel urgency (model 2b: ΔR2 = 0.122; P < .001). Hierarchical Regression Analyses for IBD-Related Disability, Disease Symptoms, and Psychosocial Factors

A hierarchical multiple linear regression was performed to examine the associations of disease symptoms and psychosocial factors with IBD-related disability. Model 1 includes demographic variables. Model 2 adds disease symptom variables (a: stool frequency and rectal bleeding; b: bowel urgency). Model 3 adds psychosocial variables. Statistical significance in models 2a, 2b, and 3 requires P < .0083, P < .0071, and P < .0005, respectively.

In the final model, psychosocial factors explained an additional 19% of variance (model 3: ΔR2 = 0.192, P < .001), indicating that psychosocial factors contribute significantly to disability beyond GI symptoms. The associations between GI symptoms and disability were no longer statistically significant after accounting for psychosocial factors, reflecting mediation.

When reversing the order of blocks 2 and 3, GI symptoms explained only 5.6% of the variance in disability after accounting for psychological factors (ΔR2 = 0.056, P = .013), signaling a weaker influence on disability from GI symptoms than psychosocial factors.

Discussion

Bowel urgency is common (48%), even in the absence of diarrhea or rectal bleeding (75%). Bowel urgency has a substantial impact on disability, though its impact on people’s lives is influenced by psychosocial factors (self-efficacy, illness perceptions, and health-related social needs). In a previous study, bowel urgency was not associated with fecal calprotectin, suggesting that objective inflammation may not be the sole driver of urgency.12 The unpredictability and severity of bowel urgency can elicit significant anxiety, embarrassment, and fear of fecal seepage and incontinence and detrimentally impact patients’ work, education, social interactions, and physical activities1 Psychosocial interventions can change maladaptive social-cognitive thoughts and reduce avoidance behaviors. Cognitive behavioral therapy delivered via telehealth can also reduce disability, even without altering the severity of GI symptoms.4

Strengths of this study include the use of prospectively collected data from a multicenter, randomized controlled trial, with well-validated measures of bowel urgency, disability, and psychosocial constructs, to conduct a broad biopsychosocial assessment of disability. The hierarchical linear regression provided potential mechanistic pathways linking GI symptoms and psychosocial factors to disability, while delineating the unique contributions of bowel urgency to disability apart from diarrhea and rectal bleeding. However, the use of cross-sectional baseline trial data precludes drawing causal inferences or establishing temporal relationships.

Conclusion

Psychotherapists who treat patients with IBD should be aware of the link between fecal urgency and IBD-related disability, especially as fecal urgency may underlie or exacerbate social anxiety and behavioral avoidance. Clinicians should routinely ask patients about bowel urgency in addition to rectal bleeding and the number of bowel movements. Further research into scalable and effective interventions that target the multifactorial etiologies of IBD-related disability is imperative to advance IBD care.3
