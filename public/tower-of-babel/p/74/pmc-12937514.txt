The impact of U.S. foreign aid reduction on global health
Authors: Tomomi Tezuka, Naomi Ito, Kenzo Takahashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA reduction in U.S. foreign aid under the “America First” policy of President Donald Trump, who took office in 2025, has significantly impacted global health. As the world’s largest provider of foreign aid, the U.S. has frozen development aid to evaluate its alignment with national interests. This has led to the termination of numerous international health programs, including those addressing malaria, HIV, tuberculosis, and polio, and has caused funding shortages for non-profit and international organizations like GAVI and the World Bank. Projections indicate dire consequences. According to USAID, a potential 18 million additional malaria cases and 166,000 deaths could occur annually. Paralytic polio cases are expected to increase by 200,000 per year, and new tuberculosis cases could rise by 10.7 million by 2030. Recent studies estimate that new HIV infections and between 770,000 and 2.93 million HIV-related deaths from 2025 to 2030. This crisis presents an opportunity for the global community to rethink its approach to aid. Other forms of financing, such as private sector investment, CSR activities, and innovative mechanisms like the Global Fund, could fill the gap left by reduced ODA. The article also stresses the importance of strengthening governance in recipient countries, promoting self-reliance, and fostering international collaboration through shared data platforms and multilateral programs. Ultimately, the document argues that providing foreign aid is not just a moral obligation, but is also in the national security and economic interest of donor countries, including the United States.Keywords: Global health, Foreign aid, Trump administration, U.S.A.

Providing support to low- and middle-income countries is not merely a moral imperative for high-income nations; it is also fundamentally driven by their own national security and economic interests [1]. Since 1960, the United States has consistently been the world’s leading provider of foreign aid and investment [2]. Our analysis demonstrates that the U.S. has maintained a pivotal leadership role in global health initiatives, particularly since the early 2000s, by spearheading efforts in infectious disease control—most notably through the President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR)—and providing humanitarian assistance during conflicts and disasters. In 2023, the U.S. Official Development Assistance (ODA) budget was 1.7 times that of Germany (the second-largest donor) and 2.4 times that of the European Union (EU) institutions. Overall, the U.S. contribution accounted for 29% of the total ODA among all Development Assistance Committee (DAC) member countries. [3]

President Donald Trump, who assumed office on January 20, 2025, has implemented an ‘America First’ policy framework, prioritizing the strengthening of national defense and the reduction of the long-standing fiscal deficit. Under this administration, foreign development aid has been largely suspended—with certain exceptions—to facilitate a comprehensive evaluation of its alignment with U.S. national interests [4].The budget for the Department of State, Foreign Operations, and Related Programs (SFOPS), which accounts for approximately 1.2% of the federal budget, decreased by 10.8% compared to 2024. The suspension of foreign assistance by the United States—the world’s preeminent donor—led to the abrupt termination of numerous bilateral global health programs. This disruption spanned critical initiatives in malaria, maternal and child health, and nutrition, as well as essential infectious disease control efforts targeting HIV, tuberculosis, and polio. Furthermore, the funding gap has extended to non-profit organizations, foreign governments, and major multilateral health institutions, including Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance, the African Development Fund, and the World Bank, all of which are now confronting severe budgetary shortfalls. USAID has about 6200 projects [6], of which 770 are related to global health. About 80% of them, or 615 projects, were forced to close [7]. The Office of Management and Budget (OMB) reported that although planned budget cuts for PEPFAR were avoided, only $2.9 billion of the $6 billion appropriated budget has been released [8].

The administration’s reductions in global health assistance pose a grave risk of escalating preventable mortality worldwide. Empirical modeling suggests the following concerning outcomes:

In light of these developments, this discussion seeks to frame the current crisis not merely as an adversity, but as a pivotal opportunity for a critical reappraisal of foreign aid strategies and an examination of the most effective modalities of assistance required in the current era.

The ODA budget of DAC countries had been increasing annually, rising from $81.1 billion in 2000. However, after peaking at $223.45 billion in 2023, the ODA budget is now projected to decrease [11, 12]. In fact, the ODA budget for DAC countries collectively decreased by 7.1% in fiscal year 2024 compared to 2023. This reduction was reported by 22 out of 32 member countries, including Germany, the United Kingdom, Japan, Switzerland, and Ireland [12].

As the preeminent global donor and economic powerhouse, the United States has historically exercised international leadership through integrated counterterrorism and trade policies. The economic development and stability of recipient nations have, in turn, stimulated bilateral trade and mitigated security risks, yielding substantial dividends for the U.S. [13] economy. However, the current moratorium on foreign assistance jeopardizes this leadership. The potential economic repercussions—stemming from regional instability, extremist threats, forced migration, and the transborder spread of infectious diseases—represent a profound fiscal risk. This vulnerability was underscored during the COVID-19 pandemic, when the U.S. economy contracted sharply and unemployment reached a historic peak of 14.7% [14]. Consequently, foreign aid and technical cooperation in global health are not merely philanthropic; they are fundamental to global security and economic prosperity, constituting a core national interest for the United States. Thus, in a context where global health security is intrinsically linked to U.S. national security, an isolationist ‘America First’ strategy appears logically incoherent and may inadvertently catalyze heightened international security risks.

In September 2025, the United States once again announced the America First Global Health Strategy, built upon three core pillars: 1. SAFER—protecting Americans from infectious diseases; 2. STRONGER—strategic bilateral relationships; and 3. MORE PROSPEROUS—protecting the economy and U.S. innovation [15]. Marco Rubio, the 72nd Secretary of State, stated that the U.S. will do so by prioritizing trade over aid, opportunity over dependency, and investment over assistance [16]. As developed countries, led by the United States, reduce their budgets for foreign assistance—a trend predicted to further threaten global public health and human security—a critical question arises: how can nations effectively pursue their own national interests under these evolving conditions?

Historically, global health has confronted numerous challenges. We have supposedly learned lessons and aimed for improved global governance following global pandemics, such as the Spanish Flu and HIV/AIDS, and multilateral conflicts, including World War II and the Vietnam War. Despite this history, why are so many lives still being lost in the face of the COVID-19 pandemic and the Israel–Palestine conflict? The reasons can be attributed to several factors: an overconfidence in the progress of science, medicine, and public health, which has led to the neglect of sustained, long-term planning and the prioritization of curative care over preventative health. Furthermore, the proliferation of misinformation in the digital age has fostered confusion and deep-seated distrust of expert knowledge. Additionally, it can be argued that decision-makers often profit from short-term solutions at the expense of addressing the root causes of inequity and food insecurity. Evidence suggests that the roots of global health challenges and recurrent institutional failures lie in a fundamental imbalance between the collective interests of the international community and state-centric national agendas, exacerbated by a deficiency in inclusive dialogue. In essence, both the resurgence of pandemics and the repeated inadequacies of humanitarian responses can be traced to a persistent neglect of historical precedents and a prioritization of immediate political gains over long-term, systemic solutions.

Since the DAC established the definition of ODA in 1969, the global landscape has undergone profound demographic and epidemiological transitions. These shifts are characterized by significant progress in poverty alleviation, health outcomes, and educational attainment, bolstered by unprecedented technological innovation. The instrumental role of ODA in driving these advancements is indisputable. For example, substantial global investments in HIV/AIDS control in Africa have been transformative, yet the risk of epidemic resurgence remains a persistent threat.

Nevertheless, the emergence of complex global crises—including protracted armed conflicts, environmental degradation, and novel pathogens—now extends beyond traditional developmental contexts. In response to these evolving dynamics, ODA must undergo a substantive paradigm shift. The objectives, quality standards, delivery mechanisms, and evaluative frameworks of development assistance require fundamental transformation to remain effective in addressing contemporary global realities.

Given these dynamics, World Bank statistics indicate that foreign direct investment and private international remittances have expanded rapidly since the 1990s—particularly in Asia—and now surpass ODA in volume [17]. There is significant potential for entities less vulnerable to geopolitical volatility—such as corporate social responsibility (CSR) initiatives, impact investing (II), and private philanthropic organizations—alongside innovative financing mechanisms like international solidarity taxes and the Global Fund, to mitigate the deficits caused by ODA contractions.

In the wake of substantial U.S. ODA reductions under the Trump administration, non-state actors must assume a more prominent role. CSR and II provide specialized expertise, advanced technology, and market-driven solutions that foster self-sufficiency and strategic shared value. While private philanthropies offer flexible, targeted funding for high-priority areas, these private sector contributions cannot fully compensate for the immense, stabilizing, and sustained financial commitment provided by governmental ODA. This persistent funding gap represents a critical threat to global public health and humanitarian stability, necessitating a restoration of public funding to avert catastrophic mortality.

Furthermore, advancements in digital transformation (DX) and medical technology can significantly enhance healthcare accessibility. These innovations facilitate expert diagnosis and treatment in resource-limited settings or regions where healthcare infrastructure has been decimated by conflict or disaster. The acceleration of pharmaceutical and medical device R&D further strengthens interventions against specific disease burdens. Ultimately, integrating these technologies—particularly in optimizing supply chain management—is expected to form the cornerstone of future global health initiatives.

Nevertheless, significant challenges persist regarding heterogeneous definitions, impact evaluation methodologies, and a lack of informational transparency. [17] Concerns have also been raised concerning the adverse effects of multifaceted and unregulated capital flows, including issues of funding sustainability, excessive profit-making at the expense of social impact, and insufficient investment in the institutional capacity-building of recipient nations. To enhance transparency and efficiency while acknowledging the diversity of actors, international organizations should spearhead the establishment of a ‘Common Data Platform’ to track assistance status and localized needs. Furthermore, these organizations must play a central role in developing standardized guidelines and certification systems to ensure accountability. To optimize limited fiscal resources, donor nations must critically reassess historical aid frameworks, enhance inter-donor information sharing, and pivot toward strategies that foster long-term self-reliance in recipient countries. Concurrently, recipient nations must develop context-specific pathways to sustainability, utilizing external assistance as a catalyst for domestic growth. An integrated approach—combining public health, infrastructure, and economic development—is indispensable for constructing social systems resilient to disasters and pandemics.

Strengthening governance at the municipal and community levels is equally vital. By institutionalizing the decentralization of authority and fostering citizen-participatory decision-making, nations can achieve more rapid and precise responses during public health emergencies. Furthermore, the exchange of regional expertise through South-South and triangular cooperation can bridge socio-cultural gaps, thereby enhancing the sustainability of interventions. Conversely, recipient countries must diversify revenue streams and strengthen institutional capacity. This necessitates comprehensive fiscal reviews, tax reforms, and robust anti-corruption measures to bolster domestic resource mobilization for healthcare and infrastructure. While various initiatives are underway globally, persistent barriers—such as resource constraints, ambiguous accountability, and resistance from vested interests—remain. However, as these obstacles closely align with the challenges identified in Table 2, the requisite solutions become increasingly evident.

Ultimately, the current reduction in U.S. foreign assistance represents an unavoidable opportunity for the international community to transcend traditional aid models and evolve toward a more resilient, sustainable, and collaborative global health ecosystem.
