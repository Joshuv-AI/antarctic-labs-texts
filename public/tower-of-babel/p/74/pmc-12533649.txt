Temporal Changes in Epicardial Adipose Tissue and Left Atrial Substrate After Atrial Fibrillation Ablation: Implications for Procedural Success
Authors: Ryuta Watanabe, Katsuaki Yokoyama, Koichi Nagashima, Moyuru Hirata, Shu Hirata, Yuji Wakamatsu, Masanaru Sawada, Sayaka Kurokawa, Atsushi Ikeda, Kenta Murotani, Naoya Matsumoto, Yasuo Okumura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGanglionated plexi (GPs) within epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) are involved in the initiation and maintenance of atrial fibrillation (AF), yet the effects of catheter ablation on EAT volume and GP activity remain unclear.MethodsA prospective 2‐part study was conducted. Study 1 included 120 patients undergoing initial AF ablation, with pre‐ and 1‐year postablation computed tomography used to assess changes in EAT and left atrial wall characteristics. Body composition, biomarkers, and echocardiographic variables were also evaluated. Study 2 included 20 patients undergoing redo ablation for recurrent AF, in whom vagal responses elicited by high‐frequency stimulation in 5 major left atrial GP areas were compared.ResultsIn study 1, significant reductions in total EAT (median, 116 [interquartile range (IQR), 86–153] to 103 [IQR, 71–138] mL; P<0.001) and left atrial EAT (median, 31 [IQR, 21–44] to 28 [IQR, 18–38] mL; P<0.001) were observed in patients without AF recurrence. In contrast, AF recurrence was associated with increases in total EAT (median, 112 [IQR, 77–161] to 133 [IQR, 85–171] mL; P<0.001), left atrial EAT (median, 30 [IQR, 19–45] to 35 [IQR, 25–56] mL; P<0.001), and decreased EAT computed tomography values. Multivariable analysis showed that AF recurrence was significantly associated with changes in EAT volume. In study 2, GP responses reemerged during redo ablation, particularly in the inferior right GP, alongside increases in EAT volume.ConclusionsPostablation maintenance of sinus rhythm was associated with a reduction in EAT volume. AF recurrence was associated with EAT expansion, compositional changes, and GP reactivation. Further investigation is needed to clarify clinical implications of these findings.Keywords: ablation, atrial fibrillation, epicardial adipose tissueSubject Categories: Atrial Fibrillation

Cardiac function is regulated by complex interplay between the extrinsic and intrinsic autonomic nervous systems. Within the intrinsic cardiac autonomic nervous system lie the ganglionated plexi (GPs), clusters of autonomic nerves that independently influence atrial myocardium and play a crucial role in the onset and maintenance of atrial fibrillation (AF). 1 , 2 Notably, these GPs are located primarily within the epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) surrounding the atria. EAT is not just a passive fat depot 3 , 4 , 5 ; it actively secretes proinflammatory cytokines. 6 Due to its close proximity to the heart and shared blood supply with the coronary arteries, EAT is believed to induce electrical and structural remodeling of the atria, thereby contributing to the development and maintenance of AF. Several studies have even identified EAT volume as a predictor of AF, and previous investigations have shown that EAT can infiltrate the atrial myocardium, potentially promoting fibrosis. 4 , 7 , 8 Thus, GP ablation has been developed as a targeted approach to disrupt AF mechanisms and is now used as an adjunct to extensive encircling pulmonary vein isolation (PVI). 9 The effectiveness of GP ablation in modifying autonomic nerve activity is well documented, but it remains unclear how GPs themselves are altered in association with AF recurrence.

Considering the roles of EAT and GPs in AF, we hypothesized that maintaining sinus rhythm by means of AF ablation could have beneficial effects on both EAT and GPs. However, temporal changes in EAT and abnormal accumulation of fat cells within the atrial myocardium (fatty infiltration) following catheter ablation have not been fully explored. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the effects of sinus rhythm after catheter ablation on the volume and quality of EAT, as well as on left atrial (LA) tissue characteristics, especially fatty infiltration of the atrial myocardium, using pre‐ and 1‐year postprocedural computed tomography (CT) images. We were especially interested in the association between postablation change in these variables and AF recurrence. Additionally, in cases of AF recurrence requiring redo ablation, we assessed vagal responses elicited intraprocedurally by high‐frequency stimulation (HFS) to identify changes in GP behavior associated with the recurrence.

Methods

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design

Our investigation comprised 2 prospective studies. Study 1 involved 120 patients who underwent their first catheter ablation for nonvalvular AF and in whom we investigated postprocedural changes in EAT and LA tissue characteristics. The 120 study patients were derived from a total of 160 consecutive enrollees who met the inclusion criteria: age >20 years, catheter ablation as initial treatment for AF, and provision of informed consent for participation in the study, which included CT imaging before and 1 year after the procedure. None of the enrollees had severe valvular disease, and none were on hemodialysis. Ultimately, 36 patients were excluded because CT imaging could not be performed during follow‐up. Additionally, 1 patient who died, 2 under treatment for cancer, and 1 who became pregnant were excluded, leaving 120 patients (91 men, 29 women; mean age, 66±11 years) to be included in the final analysis. Study 2 involved 20 consecutive patients who had AF recurrence (13 men, 7 women; mean age, 68±7 years) and, having provided informed consent, were examined for changes in response to HFS after the initial PVI and GP ablation and before redo ablation procedures. Patients enrolled in study 2 were an entirely independent cohort and were not derived from study 1, even if AF recurrence occurred after the initial procedure. In study 1, 70 of the 120 patients underwent PVI plus GP ablation, and 10 of these experienced AF recurrence. However, these 10 recurrence cases were not included in study 2. Study 1 took place at 2 hospitals, Nihon University Itabashi Hospital and Nihon University Hospital, between July 2021 and December 2022; study 2 took place exclusively at Nihon University Hospital between December 2021 and April 2023. Approval for both studies was granted by Nihon University Hospital Research Ethics Committee (20210502), and all subjects provided written informed consent before participation. In study 1, follow‐up assessments of GP activity were not performed after the initial ablation procedure. Separately, study 2 was designed to evaluate chronic GP activity in patients with AF recurrence after initial PVI plus GP ablation, by performing reassessments during redo ablation procedures. Chronic evaluation of GP activity in nonrecurrence cases was not performed, as invasive reassessment in asymptomatic patients is ethically difficult.

Multidetector CT and EAT Measurements

Three‐dimensional spiral CT was performed in all cases with a 320‐row detector, dynamic volume CT scanner (Aquilion ONE, Toshiba Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan; 0.35‐second gantry rotation time, 120 kV, and 350–450 mA) within 1 week before PVI (both noncontrast and contrast CT) and 1 year after ablation (noncontrast CT). Gated studies were performed under an ECG‐triggered scanning protocol. To minimize motion artifacts, patients with a heart rate >80 beats per minute were given a β blocker. On a workstation (ZIO STATION2; Amin Co, Ltd, Tokyo, Japan), whole‐heart EAT was detected by assigning Hounsfield units (HU) ranging from −50 to −200 to fat, and the whole‐heart EAT (total EAT) volume was semiautomatically reconstructed from contiguous 0.5‐mm axial image slices from the bifurcation of the pulmonary artery to the diaphragm. 4

Thereafter, the volume of EAT surrounding the LA was manually segmented from the total EAT; it was obtained by deleting the EAT volume from the left ventricular side anterior to the mitral annulus and the right atrial side anterior to the right superior PV and then from the lower side of the coronary sinus from the total EAT, leaving the EAT surrounding the LA (Figure 1A). CT values were automatically determined for the total EAT and LA EAT. Baseline LA wall thickness was measured at the roof and posterior wall on coronal plane contrast‐enhanced CT images. Measurements were acquired by electronic calipers at 3 sites per location and averaged. While there remains no established method for assessing fatty infiltration of the atrial myocardium, we determined and averaged CT values of the LA roof and posterior wall at 3 sites corresponding to sites where LA wall thickness was measured before ablation, assuming these values to some degree reflect fatty infiltration. One year after ablation, CT values were determined at the same 3 sites and averaged (Figure 1B).

Assessment of Body Composition

Patients' body composition was assessed before and 1 year after ablation by means of a body composition analyzer (MC‐780A‐N; TANITA, Tokyo, Japan). Measurements included patients' body weight, body mass index (BMI), body fat mass, and body fat percentage.

Biomarker Assessment

Cardiac biomarkers NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide), total cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and triglycerides were measured in blood samples obtained from a femoral vein before ablation in the electrophysiology laboratory and from a peripheral vein 1 year after ablation.

Echocardiographic Assessment

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed in all patients within 1 week before ablation and then 1 year after ablation. Patients' left ventricular ejection fraction was assessed in the parasternal long‐axis view by the Teichholz method. Maximum LA volume was calculated by the prolate ellipsoid method. LV filling pressure was calculated by dividing early diastolic mitral inflow velocity by early diastolic mitral annular velocity.

Ablation Preparation

Before ablation, all study patients received oral anticoagulation therapy for at least 1 month. Transesophageal echocardiography or intracardiac echocardiography was performed within 48 hours before the index procedure to exclude LA thrombus. Procedures were done under deep sedation achieved with fentanyl plus dexmedetomidine, midazolam, or propofol. After vascular access was obtained, single transseptal puncture was performed under intracardiac echocardiography guidance, and intravenous heparin was administered to maintain an activated clotting time of >300 seconds. After 2 long sheaths (a steerable Agilis NxT sheath and an SL0 sheath; Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, Chicago, IL) were inserted into the LA via the transseptal puncture, the 3‐dimensional geometry of the LA and 4 PVs was reconstructed by means of the CARTO3 mapping system (Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA) or the EnSite Precision system (Abbott). Bipolar signals were acquired with the PENTARAY NAV (Biosense Webster) or Advisor HD Grid catheter (Abbott).

Ablation Method

PVI was performed in the study 1 patients as previously reported. 10 Point‐by‐point ablation was performed with a contact force–sensing irrigated tip catheter (Thermocool Smart Touch; Biosense Webster or TactiCath; Abbott). Radiofrequency energy was delivered at a maximum power output of 30 to 45 W and a target contact force of 10 to 20 g with an ablation index of >450 with the upper temperature limit set to 43 °C at a saline irrigation rate of 17 to 30 mL/min, or a lesion index of 4.5 to 5.5 with the temperature limit set to 43 °C at a saline irrigation rate of 25 mL/min. The end point of PVI was elimination or dissociation of all pulmonary vein potentials and complete entrance block. If a pulmonary vein remained electrically connected, additional touch‐up ablation lesions were created until PVI was achieved. Linear ablation of the cavotricuspid valve isthmus was performed in patients in whom common atrial flutter was documented clinically. Seventy study 1 patients were treated at Nihon University Hospital, and before PVI, these patients underwent GP ablation according to the procedure described below. The remaining 50 study 1 patients were treated at Nihon University Itabashi Hospital, and these patients underwent PVI but not GP ablation.

Both GP ablation and PVI had been performed in the study 2 patients, and these patients experienced AF recurrence before enrollment in the study. The GP ablation had been performed at sites within EAT regions containing the 5 major GPs (Marshall tract, superior left [SL], inferior left [IL], anterior right [AR], and inferior right [IR] GPs) where HFS elicited a positive response 11 (Figure 2A). After GP ablation, HFS was repeated to confirm a negative response at each site. Subsequently, PVI was performed by the same procedure used in study 1. In the redo session, HFS was performed in each GP area stimulated during the initial session (Figure 2B). GP reablation was performed if HFS elicited a positive response. Additional treatment (ie, re‐PVI, non‐PV foci ablation, or linear ablation) was performed if deemed appropriate.

Postablation Follow‐Up

Antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs) were resumed after ablation but then typically stopped after a 3‐month blanking period. In some cases, the AADs were continued beyond this point on the physician's recommendation or patient's preference even in the absence of AF recurrence. All patients underwent routine follow‐up examination at our outpatient clinic at 2 weeks and then 1 month after ablation and at 1‐ to 3‐month intervals thereafter for at least 6 months. Twenty‐four–hour Holter monitoring was performed 3 to 6 months and 12 months after ablation. An electrocardiographic event recorder was used if patients reported any cardiac symptoms. Any AF episode lasting >30 seconds and documented on a standard ECG, event recorder, or 24‐hour Holter monitor was considered an AF recurrence.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean±SD or median (interquartile range [IQR]) values, as appropriate. Student t test or the Mann–Whitney U test was used to analyze differences in continuous variables, depending on whether the values were normally distributed, and the χ2 test was used to analyze differences in dichotomous variables unless the expected value in any cell was <5, in which case the Fisher exact test was used. These tests were performed to compare variables between AF recurrence and nonrecurrence groups. Change in values was analyzed by paired t test or Wilcoxon signed‐rank test for continuous variables, and McNemar's test for paired categorical variables, as appropriate. Because EAT volumes and NT‐proBNP and triglyceride levels were skewed, values were transformed by taking the natural logarithm (Ln) to facilitate further statistical analysis. Relations between change in Ln total EAT volume and Ln LA EAT volume, and various factors, including patients' clinical characteristics, changes in body composition, echocardiographic variables, biomarkers, and AF recurrence, were assessed on the basis of the Pearson correlation coefficient. Relations between the changes in total EAT volume and LA EAT volume and changes in NT‐proBNP and triglyceride levels were assessed on the basis of the Spearman correlation coefficient. Multivariable regression analyses were then performed separately to identify factors associated with changes in Ln total EAT volume and changes in Ln LA EAT volume from baseline to 1 year after ablation, including significant variables from the univariate analyses as well as age, sex, and CHADS2 score. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed with JMP 16.0 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Of the 160 patients initially enrolled in study 1, 120 patients were included in the final analysis after excluding 40 patients who could not undergo follow‐up CT or were otherwise ineligible. Among these 120 patients who underwent PVI (age, 66±11 years; paroxysmal AF, n=69), 18 had recurrence within 1 year after the procedure. Clinical characteristics of the total study 1 patients and of those who had recurrence and those who did not are shown in Table 1. The prevalence of heart failure was significantly greater in the recurrence group than in the nonrecurrence group (5/18 [28%] versus 10/102 [10%]; P=0.034). Body weight tended to be greater in the recurrence group than in the nonrecurrence group. Other preablation characteristics, including total EAT and LA EAT volumes, total EAT and LA EAT CT values, LA roof/wall thickness, LA roof/wall CT value, and NT‐proBNP, hemoglobin A1c, and lipid levels, were not shown to be significant predictors of recurrence. Postblanking period use of AADs is also shown in Table 1. There were no significant differences in AAD use between the AF recurrence and nonrecurrence groups (class I: 1/18 [6%] versus 2/102 [2%]; P=0.39; bepridil: 6/18 [33%] versus 31/102 [30%]; P=0.79).

CT‐Based Evaluation of EAT and the LA Wall

CT‐based evaluation of EAT and the LA wall in the nonrecurrence and recurrence groups is shown in Figure 3. Total EAT volume decreased significantly from 116 (IQR, 86–153) to 103 (IQR, 71–138) mL (P<0.001) and LA EAT volume from 31 (IQR, 21–44) to 28 (IQR, 18–38) mL (P<0.001) in the nonrecurrence group, whereas total EAT volume increased significantly from 112 (IQR, 77–161) to 133 (IQR, 85–171) mL (P<0.001) and LA EAT volume from 30 (IQR, 19–45) to 35 (IQR, 25–56) mL (P<0.001) in the recurrence group (Figure 3A and 3B). No significant change in CT values for total EAT (from −91±3 to −91±4 HU; P=0.96) or LA EAT (from −89±4 to −89±4 HU; P=0.34) occurred in the nonrecurrence group, whereas significant increases in CT values of total EAT (from −90±3 to −93±4 HU; P=0.002) and LA EAT (from −88±3 to −91±5 HU; P=0.004) occurred in the recurrence group (Figure 3C and 3D). No significant change in LA CT values occurred in either group at the LA roof (nonrecurrence group: from 24±10 to 23±9 HU; P=0.29; recurrence group: from 26±9 to 26±10 HU; P=0.89) or the LA posterior wall (nonrecurrence group: from 32±11 to 29±10 HU; P=0.10; recurrence group: from 34±9 to 32±10 HU; P=0.75) (Figure 3E and 3F).

Echocardiographic Variables

LA volume decreased (from 57±21 to 46±16 mL; P<0.0001), left ventricular ejection fraction increased (from 65.1±7.6 to 67.4±6.2%; P=0.002), and the E/e′ ratio did not significantly change (from 10.0±3.8 to 9.6±4.0; P=0.16) in the nonrecurrence group. LA volume decreased (from 66±30 to 53±26 mL; P=0.005) in the recurrence group, but no significant change in left ventricular ejection fraction (from 65.1±8.1 to 65.1±6.3%; P=0.99) or the E/e' ratio (from 10.8±5.0 to 11.9±7.7; P=0.28) was observed in this group.

Changes in Body Composition

In the nonrecurrence group, body weight and BMI decreased significantly (body weight: from 64.7±11.9 to 63.7±12.2 kg; P=0.015; BMI: from 24±3 to 23±4 kg/m2; P=0.036), without a significant change in body fat percentage (from 23.1±7.7 to 24.6±8.0%; P=0.22). In the recurrence group, no significant change was observed in body weight (from 70.2±11.7 to 68.9±12.6 kg; P=0.14), BMI (from 24±3 to 23±3 kg/m2; P=0.17), or body fat percentage (from 25.5±6.6 to 24.2±6.7%; P=0.55).

Changes in Biomarkers

NT‐proBNP decreased significantly over time in the nonrecurrence group (from 253 [IQR, 88–812] to 80 [IQR, 44–1879] pg/mL; P<0.001) but showed no significant change in the recurrence group (from 358 [IQR, 100–551] to 98 [IQR, 50–425] pg/mL; P=0.10). Hemoglobin A1c decreased significantly in both groups (nonrecurrence group: from 6.1±0.6 to 6.0±0.6%; P=0.004; recurrence group: from 5.9±0.4 to 5.7±0.3%; P=0.044). Furthermore, the change in hemoglobin A1c did not significantly differ between the nonrecurrence and recurrence groups (−0.1±0.2% versus −0.1±0.3%; P=0.75). High‐density lipoprotein cholesterol increased significantly (from 61±16 to 64±20 mg/dL; P=0.020) in the nonrecurrence group, and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol decreased significantly (from 109±29 to 101±25 mg/dL; P=0.001), without a significant change in total cholesterol (from 196±36 to 194±36 mg/dL; P=0.36) or triglycerides (from 110 (IQR, 76–139) to 104 (IQR, 80–156) mg/dL; P=0.93). No significant change occurred in total cholesterol (from 191±39 to 189±41 mg/dL; P=0.81), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (from 56±14 to 54±13 mg/dL; P=0.44), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (from 110±32 to 110±36 mg/dL; P=0.97), or triglycerides (from 134 (IQR, 84–193) to 116 (IQR, 87–138) mg/dL; P=0.75) in the nonrecurrence group.

Relations Between Changes in EAT and Other Study Variables

Changes in EAT volume correlated significantly with change in body weight after the procedure (change in Ln total EAT: r=0.26, P=0.006; change in Ln LA EAT: r=0.22, P=0.021) (Figure 4A and 4B). Moreover, changes in EAT volume were significantly associated with AF recurrence after ablation (change in Ln total EAT: −0.12±0.18 versus 0.15±0.14; P<0.001; change in Ln LA EAT: −0.16±0.20 versus 0.18±0.16; P<0.001) (Figure 4C and 4D).

In study 2, the 20 patients (mean age, 68±7 years; 13 with paroxysmal AF) who had AF recurrence following PVI and GP ablation underwent redo ablation procedures at 3±2 years after the initial ablation. Discussion

Main Findings

The main study findings were as follows:

1. EAT volume decreased over the 1‐year period following catheter ablation in patients who did not have AF recurrence, but EAT volume increased in patients who did have recurrence.

2. Temporal changes in LA CT values, values indicative of fatty infiltration of the atrial myocardium, did not differ significantly between the nonrecurrence and recurrence groups.

3. In cases of AF recurrence following catheter ablation, the HFS‐positive response rates increased. Notably, some sites that were shown to be HFS negative after successful GP ablation at the time of the initial session had become HFS positive by the time of the redo session. Additionally, some sites that were originally HFS negative at the time of the initial session had become HFS positive.

EAT Volume and Quality After Ablation

LA reverse remodeling after AF ablation is well documented. Our investigation confirmed significant reductions in LA volume and E/e' after ablation, particularly in patients who did not have recurrence. Notably, we documented significant reductions in the total and LA EAT volumes over 1 year after catheter ablation in patients who did not have AF recurrence, whereas we documented significant increases in those who did have recurrence. Given that EAT is closely linked to both occurrence and recurrence of AF, our data provide new insight into the potential mechanisms driving these associations and the outcomes of ablation. We previously documented a postablation decrease in EAT thickness via echocardiography, 12 but spatial distribution of EAT around the heart and LA have made it unclear whether the total EAT volume decreases.

While data regarding the effects of ablation on EAT volume and quality remain limited, several mechanisms can be postulated to explain the observed reduction. Due to its insulating nature, EAT has been shown to resist radiofrequency energy, inhibiting its effect on target myocardial tissues. However, radiofrequency ablation may modulate autonomic GPs, affecting clinical outcomes. Notably, a reduction in EAT volume following hot balloon ablation has been reported, and the investigators attributed the reduction partly to direct injury to the EAT. 13 Another factor is recovery from the systemic inflammatory condition by sinus rhythm maintenance after ablation. Indeed, postablation maintenance of sinus rhythm decreases levels of systemic inflammation markers like high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein and interleukin‐6, 14 decreases that may contribute to the reduction in EAT. Weight loss is also a potential factor in reducing EAT and systemic inflammation. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 As shown above, a slight reduction in body weight, BMI, and low‐density lipoprotein level occurred after ablation, particularly among our patients who did not have AF recurrence. However, after adjustment for these variables, AF recurrence was found to be the factor most strongly associated with change in EAT, suggesting that the effect of other variables is limited. In line with this, the degree of hemoglobin A1c reduction did not differ significantly between the recurrence and nonrecurrence groups, further suggesting that glycemic improvement was not a major determinant of EAT dynamics or AF recurrence in this cohort. Additionally, continuation of AADs beyond the blanking period did not differ significantly between the recurrence and nonrecurrence groups (Table 1), suggesting that postablation AAD use was unlikely to have influenced the observed differences in EAT or AF recurrence. A third mechanism may involve changes in the local function of EAT. EAT can initially serve as a heat source for the myocardium during unfavorable hemodynamic conditions in AF through brown fat–like thermogenic activity. 19 Over time, sustained hemodynamic instability can shift EAT's role from thermogenesis to energy storage, resembling white fat activity. 19 This shift could explain the significant decrease in EAT volume as well as CT values observed in our AF recurrence group, indicative of a change in EAT characteristics. Conversely, with the restoration of sinus rhythm, the thermogenic role of EAT may no longer be required, leading to the reduction in EAT.

In contrast, temporal changes in LA CT values reflecting LA substrates did not differ significantly between our nonrecurrence and recurrence groups. Histologic analysis of LA appendage samples taken during cardiovascular surgery from patients with AF has shown fibrosis and inflammatory markers in peri‐LA EAT to be linked to atrial myocardial fibrosis, providing a substrate for the AF. 20 Other studies have shown that obesity is linked to atrial electrostructural remodeling, resulting in LA enlargement, conduction abnormalities, and fibrosis, which increase AF. 8 , 18 , 21 Our previous study in an obese canine model and rapid atrial pacing model further indicated a link between vulnerability to AF and elevated LA pressure and EAT/fatty infiltration, highlighting the role of EAT/fatty infiltration in AF pathogenesis. 7 In the study reported herein, even with sinus rhythm maintenance, CT values in the LA wall remained unchanged after ablation. A different physiologic condition, weight loss, has been linked in an obese ovine model to structural and electrophysiological reverse remodeling and a reduced propensity toward AF, but there was only a nonsignificant reduction in epicardial fatty infiltration of the posterior LA (P=0.34). 18 Together, our findings and current evidence suggest that persistent LA wall substrates, such as fatty infiltration and fibrosis, remain unresolved after ablation. These substrate abnormalities may continue to pose a risk for postablation AF recurrence.

To further explore whether procedural differences influenced EAT dynamics, we conducted subgroup analyses comparing patients who underwent PVI alone and those who underwent PVI with GP ablation. The recurrence rates were similar between groups, and the relationship between changes in EAT volume and AF recurrence remained significant in both subgroups. Specifically, changes in both total and LA EAT volumes were significantly correlated with AF recurrence in each group, suggesting that the impact of EAT remodeling on recurrence was consistent regardless of GP ablation. These findings reinforce the robustness of the association between EAT dynamics and arrhythmia outcomes and support the generalizability of our observations across ablation strategies.

GP Activity

Despite GP ablation eliminating the vagal response, we found it to be restored and even enhanced in association with AF recurrence, particularly in the IR GP area. There are several reasons for the change in GP activity in the recurrence group. The first is hyper‐reinnervation of the autonomic nervous system after GP ablation. A previously reported canine study showed that reinnervation of the autonomic nervous system occurs after GP ablation. 22 That study compared epicardial GP ablation groups with a sham ablation group and showed the density of both sympathetic and parasympathetic nerves in the atrium to be higher in the epicardial GP group examined at 8 weeks than in the sham operation group (also examined at 8 weeks), explaining the increased GP activity in the IR GP region. The second reason is the interplay among AF, increased EAT, and GP activation. We showed previously that patients with AF exhibit heightened GP responses compared with those of patients without AF. 23 Our study 1 patients in whom sinus rhythm was maintained after ablation showed a reduction in EAT, suggesting that the increase in EAT observed with AF promotes GP activation. This potential crosstalk between increased EAT and GP activity could be 1 of the key mechanisms underlying AF recurrence. However, we should consider several methodological drawbacks. The first is insufficient detection of GP sites. There are 2 GP ablation methods. One is selective GP ablation, as used in our study, and the other is anatomic GP ablation based on the anatomic location of the GPs. Previous reports have suggested that anatomic GP ablation leads to better outcomes than those seen with selective GP ablation. 9 GPs are widely distributed within the major 5 GP areas, and thus it is challenging to adequately detect a sufficient number of GPs before and after ablation. Another drawback is incomplete GP denervation by ablation. GPs are located in EAT, 2 and the location and amount of EAT may serve as an insulating cushion to targeted epicardial sites, minimizing the efficiency of thermal‐based ablation strategies.

Limitations

Our study was subject to several limitations. First, chronic evaluation of GP activity in nonrecurrence cases was not performed, because invasive reassessment in asymptomatic patients is ethically difficult. Second, GP evaluation may have been insufficient, although thorough reassessment of HFS‐elicited responses at the same sites as in the initial session likely minimized this limitation. Third, the clinical significance of CT values for EAT and LA remains uncertain. However, our observation of decreased EAT CT values in cases of recurrence is inversely supported by the fact that increased HU have been associated with reduced fat accumulation following significant weight loss in patients with obesity. 16 Fourth, the limited sample size in study 2 may limit both the generalizability and the statistical power of our findings, particularly for subgroup analyses. However, it should be noted that among 70 patients who underwent PVI plus GP ablation in study 1, only 10 (14%) experienced AF recurrence. Thus, assembling 20 patients who experienced AF recurrence after initial PVI plus GP ablation and underwent redo procedures required follow‐up of more than 140 initial ablation cases. Given the relatively low recurrence rate and the fact that not all patients with recurrence undergo redo ablation, the study 2 cohort represents a carefully selected and well‐followed patient population, although the findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Fifth, the thin LA wall may not have been accurately evaluated due to CT resolution constraints. The CT images used in this study were acquired at a slice thickness of 0.5 mm, limiting the resolution at which LA wall and EAT thicknesses and CT values were evaluated. In particular, subtle structural changes in the atrial myocardium after ablation may not have been detectable because of the inherent thinness of the LA wall and the limited spatial resolution of CT imaging. Sixth, although 40 patients were excluded from the initial cohort of study 1 due to missing follow‐up CT or other reasons, we compared their baseline characteristics with those of the included patients. There were no significant differences in clinical, echocardiographic, or imaging parameters between the 2 groups (Table S1), suggesting that selection bias due to these exclusions is unlikely. Finally, it remains unclear whether EAT expansion is a cause or consequence of GP reactivation or AF recurrence, and further studies are needed to elucidate these interrelationships.

Conclusions

Successful catheter ablation for AF is associated with a reduction in EAT volume without significant change in EAT or LA wall CT values when sinus rhythm is maintained. AF recurrence is, however, linked to an increase in EAT volume, a slight decrease in EAT CT values, and reactivation of GP responses. Further investigation is needed to clarify whether these changes are a cause or consequence of AF recurrence and to determine their clinical implications.
