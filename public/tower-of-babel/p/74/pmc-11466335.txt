Eustachian Valve Endocarditis: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Danna Reyes, Gamal Musleh, Sherif Elkattawy, Muhammad Noori, Jesus Romero, Ana L. Romero, Omar Elkattawy, Omran Ahmed, Gabriel Zaverucha, Razan Shamoon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Infective endocarditis (IE) is a well-known condition, but certain manifestations, such as Eustachian valve endocarditis (EVE), remain rare. This report presents a case of EVE in a 66-year-old male with no apparent risk factors. EVE is a form of right-sided endocarditis, often associated with intravenous drug use, yet it can occur in patients without traditional risk factors.The patient presented with a constellation of symptoms, including lightheadedness, dyspnea, generalized weakness, and fever. Diagnostic workup revealed multiple complications, including liver abscesses, pulmonary nodules, and spinal osteomyelitis.EVE was confirmed through transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), highlighting multiple Eustachian valve vegetations. Blood cultures identified Bacteroides fragilis as the causative agent. The patient received appropriate antimicrobial therapy, underwent drainage of liver abscesses, and was started on therapeutic anticoagulation for thrombophlebitis. A favorable clinical response was observed, and the patient was discharged for continued outpatient follow-up.This case underscores the importance of considering atypical forms of endocarditis, such as EVE, even in patients lacking traditional risk factors. The diagnostic utility of TEE and the management of complications, including liver abscesses and thrombophlebitis, are discussed. Awareness of such presentations is essential for timely diagnosis and effective treatment.

Introduction

Infective endocarditis is a well-known disease in general, but certain involvements in the disease are still rare; this is the case of eustachian valve endocarditis (EVE), with less than 40 cases reported in almost 4 decades. The Eustachian valve (EV) is an embryologic remnant of the sinus venosus, located at the junction of the inferior vena cava (IVC) and right atrium (RA) that directs oxygenated blood from the IVC through the foramen ovale to the left atrium (LA) during fetal circulation. In adults, EV is non-functional and can rarely become a focus of infection.1,2 EVE is a right-sided endocarditis; therefore, the biggest risk factor is also intravenous drug use, but other causes must be taken into consideration.3 Here, we describe our recent experience with EVE presenting with no apparent risk factor at first sight at our academic medical center.

Case presentation

A 66-year-old male with no known past medical history and no prior history of IV drug abuse, presented to the emergency department with light-headedness associated with dyspnea, generalized weakness, anorexia, chills, subjected fever, and back pain for the past several weeks. On presentation, vitals were blood pressure 147/87 mmHg, pulse 158 bpm, temperature 99.8 F°, respiratory rate 38/minute, and SpO2 of 100% at room air. On physical examination, the patient appeared cachectic in mild respiratory distress and was awake, oriented to person, place, and time, cardiovascular exam revealed regular rate and rhythm, normal S1 and S2, no murmurs or gallops, JVD +7 with positive Juglar-hepatic reflex and unremarkable lung exam. The abdomen was soft, none distended, none tender with a reducible superior ventral hernia, and no overlying erythema or warmth. ECG: sinus tachycardia with no ST or T wave changes.

Laboratory data significant for WBC of 20,000 with bandemia of 19%, low hemoglobin of 7.5, Alkaline Phosphatase 243, Glutamic Oxalate-GOT (AST) 81, Glutamic Pyruvic Transaminase (ALT) 58, Lactic acid of 16.9, anion gap of 27 and ABG suggestive of mixed respiratory alkalosis and metabolic acidosis. Chest X-ray revealed mild ground glass haziness, CTA of the chest did not show Pulmonary embolism however, revealed multiple pulmonary nodules ranging from 2 to 9 mm in size, and CT abdomen and pelvis revealed multiple liver masses. Blood Cultures were withdrawn, the patient received 3 L bolus of IV fluids as well as 1 dose of vancomycin, started on Zosyn, and the patient was admitted to the ICU for high anion gap mixed respiratory acidosis and metabolic alkalosis management.

During the hospital stay, blood cultures were positive for Bacteroides Fragilis. Percutaneous needle CT-guided biopsy was performed as part of the workup for liver masses which revealed thick purulent fluid confirming the diagnosis of liver abscesses and abscesses were drained. TTE was performed, concerned for aortic valve vegetation therefore, further TEE was recommended to confirm the presence of vegetation. TEE revealed multiple Eustachian valve vegetation with a small PFO and ejection fraction of 45–50% and no aortic valve vegetation was found (Fig. 1). MRI of the spine showed Lumbosacral Spine osteomyelitis and Diskitis at L2-L3 with Paravertebral Abscess and right small psoas muscle abscess. Flagyl antibiotic was initiated. Fluid culture from liver abscess also grew Bacteroides Fragilis. Repeated blood cultures after initiating the antibiotic were negative.

The patient continued to improve on medications. Repeat CT chest and abdomen revealed resolving pulmonary nodules as well as liver abscess; however, thrombophlebitis of the middle hepatic vein was seen therefore, the patient was started on therapeutic anticoagulation. The patient’s symptoms continued to improve and was discharged home with oral Flagyl antibiotic for a total of 6 weeks duration therapy. The patient was scheduled for cardiology, and infectious disease follow-up and was advised to repeat CT of the lumbosacral spine toward the end of the therapy course.

Discussion

Infective endocarditis (IE) is characterized as an infection of the endocardium, most frequently involving the heart valves.4 The incidence rates of infective endocarditis (IE) in the general population have remained stable, estimated at 5–7 cases per 100,000 person-years in the United States.4 Nevertheless, the ongoing opioid epidemic has caused a noticeable shift in the demographics of IE. Recent studies have uncovered a significant increase, ranging from 2 to 12 times, in hospitalizations related to injection drug use-associated IE (IDU-IE). 4 Consequently, the incidence of IE in the IDU population now stands at approximately 150–200 cases per 100,000 individuals, which is roughly 40 times higher than the incidence of IE in the general population.4

Over time, the risk factors linked with infective endocarditis (IE) have changed. The past five decades have witnessed significant shifts in healthcare delivery, which have had noticeable impacts on the clinical manifestations of IE.5 These changes encompass the utilization of intracardiac devices, and prosthetic valves, the prevalence of hemodialysis, and the increasing elderly population.5 Additionally, modifications in antibiotic usage have resulted in shifts in infection patterns and bacterial resistance.5

Despite substantial advancements in therapeutic approaches, infective endocarditis (IE) still carries a significant burden of mortality, with in-hospital mortality rates ranging from 14% to 22% and a striking long-term mortality rate of up to 51% at 10 years.6 Furthermore, IE is associated with significant morbidity, including prolonged hospitalization, reduced quality of life, and an increased risk of recurrent infections.6

Right-sided valvular endocarditis, particularly involving the tricuspid valve, is a relatively uncommon occurrence, accounting for only about 5–10% of all IE cases.3 The primary population at risk for right-sided valvular endocarditis is intravenous drug users, representing the largest proportion of cases. Additionally, other groups at increased risk include individuals with rheumatic heart disease, those with indwelling catheters, recipients of pacemakers or defibrillators, those with compromised immune systems, and individuals with congenital heart disease.3

While there has been an increase in the occurrence of right-sided infective endocarditis due to the global rise in intravenous drug use and the growing use of implantable cardiac electronic devices and central venous catheters, it’s important to note that right-sided infective endocarditis continues to be less common than its left-sided counterpart.6

Eustachian valve endocarditis (EVE) represents an unusual manifestation of right-sided endocarditis, where patients display clinical signs and symptoms resembling those of tricuspid valve (TV) endocarditis. Since its initial description in 1986, there have been relatively few documented cases of EVE in the medical literature.1–7

Eustachian valve endocarditis (EVE) typically occurs in isolation, without concurrent involvement of other heart valves. However, when other valves are affected in EVE cases, the tricuspid valve (TV) is the most affected. There have also been documented instances of vegetation involving the pulmonary and mitral valves.6

The Eustachian valve is a remnant from embryonic development found at the junction of the inferior vena cava (IVC) and the inferior portion of the right atrium (RA), present in approximately 25% of individuals.1 During fetal development, this valve serves the purpose of directing oxygenated blood toward the foramen ovale, diverting it away from the right atrium. However, once the foramen ovale closes after birth, the Eustachian valve typically holds no pathological significance. In adults, it persists without causing any symptoms, and patients usually do not require any specific medical management.8

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is considered the most sensitive diagnostic tool for detecting right-sided endocarditis and Eustachian valve endocarditis (EV). It is recommended, especially when a transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) yields normal results in a patient displaying typical signs of right-sided endocarditis.7

During echocardiographic examinations, the Eustachian valve can appear in various forms. It may be observed as a delicate, thin flap originating from the opening of the inferior vena cava (IVC), or it may be absent. In some cases, it may be present as a rigid and elongated structure located in the lower part of the right atrium. Alternatively, it might manifest as a membranous, undulating, and echogenic mass within the right atrial cavity.9

Eustachian valve endocarditis (EVE) generally follows a benign clinical course and can be effectively treated with a 4–6-week course of culture-directed antibiotic therapy, starting when cultures first test negative. Surgical intervention is reserved for cases where vegetation does not resolve with antimicrobial treatment or when vegetation leads to hemodynamic compromise.1

In this instance, we present a case of endocarditis caused by Bacteroides fragilis, accompanied by an asymptomatic thrombophlebitis of the middle hepatic vein, which is frequently linked to the presence of multiple risk factors. In this patient, it was associated with a gastrointestinal infection, however, it is important to note that other risk factors for this condition encompass neoplasia, cirrhosis, myeloproliferative disorders, thrombophilia, pregnancy, or liver transplantation.10

Endocarditis resulting from anaerobic bacteria is a relatively uncommon occurrence, and instances of endocarditis specifically caused by B. fragilis are rarely documented. Patients with such infections should undergo a thorough evaluation to assess potential complications associated with this anaerobic infection and receive timely treatment.
