Profiling biological effects of microbiome metabolites via machine learning
Authors: Hong A. Chung, Zachary Fralish, Tiffany Tu, Daniel Reker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHuman microbiome-derived metabolites are key mediators of host physiology. However, their biological effects remain largely uncharacterized due to limitations of current low-throughput and untargeted experimental approaches that are time intensive and costly. This has hindered the systematic biological characterization of microbiome metabolites. To address this gap and accelerate the identification of biological effects of microbiome metabolites, we developed and experimentally validated a machine learning platform trained on publicly available drug development data to rapidly predict a wide array of chemical and biological properties of microbiome metabolites. Prospective experimental validation confirmed the accuracy of our models and uncovered previously unknown effects of several metabolites. For example, we identified previously unknown interleukin 8 secretion stimulation by the metabolites spermine and spermidine, which have been regarded anti-inflammatory thus far. Our findings demonstrate the potential power of machine learning to accelerate the functional annotations of microbiome-derived metabolites, paving the way for biomarker and therapeutic discovery.

Introduction

The human microbiome produces thousands of metabolites that can influence host health and disease.1,2,3 For example, microbiome-derived tryptophan has been associated with modulation of the liver’s inflammatory responses,4 and microbiome-derived inosine has been found to exhibit immunomodulatory effects that can support immunotherapeutic responses in mice.5 Conversely, microbiome-derived trimethylamine N-oxide is under scrutiny for its potential role in cardiovascular disease.6 Such microbiome-host interactions are considered highly medically relevant because these associations could ultimately lead to the discovery of important biomarkers and serve as informed starting points for the development of novel therapeutic interventions. However, only a minute fraction of microbiome metabolites has been characterized for their biological effects.7 This is primarily driven by the cost and complexity of current experimental approaches, which often rely on correlative analyses of metagenomic or metabolomic data. While these studies can include large sample sizes, they typically provide limited mechanistic or causal insight and remain time and resource intensive to perform.8 Despite the growing catalog of microbiome-derived metabolites, their biological and pharmacological effects remain poorly characterized. There is currently no systematic, scalable framework for predicting complex properties such as absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) or immunomodulatory potential across the diverse chemical space of microbiome metabolites.

Recent advances in machine learning offer a path to bypass these constraints, as substantial progress has been made in using machine learning for natural product-based drug discovery.9,10 Bespoke computational tools can now anticipate the potential biological effects of metabolites from various organisms including microbes and plants. This is achieved using increasingly accurate algorithms that process large datasets to associate the chemical structures of metabolites with those of molecules whose biological effects are known, enabling the prediction of similar bioactivity. These tools allow drug hunters in industry and academia to triage their experimental testing, thereby saving valuable resources compared with alternative approaches such as high-throughput screening of natural product libraries or affinity-guided purification approaches.9

Here, we propose that similar computational approaches could accelerate the identification of biological effects of microbiome metabolites (Figure 1). By building on publicly available data repositories of millions of small molecules with known biological effects and properties, we trained machine learning models to predict a wide range of biological properties for microbiome metabolites including potential intestinal absorption, blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration, hepatotoxicity, mutagenicity, cardiotoxicity, and activation of interleukin 8 (IL-8) secretion. We selected IL-8 as a representative marker of immune activation because it is a well-characterized proinflammatory cytokine that plays a central role in host-microbiome interactions. As a key mediator of neutrophil recruitment and early innate immune activation, IL-8 provides a sensitive and quantifiable measure of metabolite-induced immune responses that can be robustly assessed in vitro, making it both biologically relevant and experimentally tractable for this study. General workflow of the present study

A database of human microbiome metabolites was semi-automatically curated by mining data from five different public databases to extract chemical structures (SMILES) and microbial origin of the metabolites. The physicochemical properties of these metabolites were calculated using cheminformatics tools (RDKit). More complex biological properties were predicted using machine learning models (KNIME data analytics platform) trained on data for drug-like molecules (TDCommons or PubChem). We used external datasets and conducted prospective in vitro experiments to validate our models.

In this study, we define “microbiome metabolites” as compounds that can be biosynthesized or chemically modified by microbial metabolism. We base our knowledge on the origin and the modification of metabolites on source annotations from established, peer-reviewed public databases11,12,13,14,15 (Data S1). We acknowledge that source annotations vary in quality and completeness across databases and may not fully capture biosynthesis origins. As such, this classification serves as an operational framework rather than a strict biological definition. To ensure rigor, we performed manual review of biosynthetic context of any top candidate identified through model predictions and considered for downstream applications to confirm its potential microbial origin (Data S2). Additionally, we did not exclude metabolites that are also produced by other organisms, including the human host, because their production by the microbiome might alter host exposure and their local concentrations in physiological conditions.16

To assess the utility of our models (Data S3), we evaluated the performance of our models on external test data and conducted prospective in vitro experiments in-house. While performance varied across different tasks, we consistently found strong performance of our models, with an average accuracy of 81.7% on external validation sets and an average accuracy of 92.86% in prospective experiments. While these exact statistics need to be interpreted with caution given the relatively small sample size, we believe these trends are promising for the potential of our model to accurately guide experimental validations. In addition, our experiments identified previously unknown properties of specific microbiome metabolites, such as the IL-8 stimulatory potential of spermine and spermidine.

Taken together, this study aims to establish a systematic framework that uses machine learning to accelerate the functional annotation of microbiome-derived metabolites. Specifically, we developed and validated predictive models to triage and prioritize metabolites for targeted experimental testing, thereby shifting microbiome metabolite research from explorative and resource-intensive screening toward hypothesis-driven validation studies. The key innovation of this work lies in combining machine learning predictions with experimental validation to confirm biological effects and demonstrate real-world applicability. This integrative computational and experimental strategy enables rapid, large-scale assessment of microbiome metabolites and provides a general framework for identifying bioactive microbial compounds with potential relevance as biomarkers or future therapeutics.

Results

Microbiome metabolites have “drug-like” properties

To ensure that machine learning models trained on drug-like molecules could be applicable to microbiome metabolites, the metabolites need to share chemical features with the drug-like molecules in the training data to allow algorithms to recognize learned molecular patterns and make accurate predictions for metabolites.9 As a first simple assessment of whether microbiome metabolites share similarities with drug-like molecules, we generated a t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) as a dimensionality reduction to visually assess the neighborhood relationships in chemical structure spaces occupied by 2,645 microbiome metabolites and 1,855 FDA-approved drugs (Figure 2A). We observed a notable overlap between the metabolites and approved drugs in chemical space, suggesting that several metabolites share molecular patterns with drug-like molecules. This implies that models trained on drug-like structures might be applicable to microbiome metabolites. This finding also hints at many metabolites having chemical structures that could enable them to modulate biological pathways in hosts similarly to approved medicines, as they could be considered “drug-like.” Notwithstanding this notable overlap, we did observe that there were also regions in chemical space that were exclusively occupied by metabolites or exclusively occupied by drugs, suggesting that certain types of drugs or metabolites are chemically distinct from the other groups. Beyond chemical structure similarity, we also aimed to assess whether microbiome metabolites and drug-like structures share similar basic physicochemical properties. We analyzed the distribution of six different molecular properties (Figure 2B), including the logarithm of the molecule’s partition coefficient (logP), molecular weight, number of hydrogen-bond donors, number of hydrogen-bond acceptors, number of rotatable bonds, and the molecular topological polar surface area (TPSA). We observed similar property distributions between drugs and microbiome metabolites for all these properties (Figure 2B). Additionally, for further contextualization, we calculated these molecular properties for metabolites from the Pseudomonas aeruginosa Metabolome Database (PAMDB)17 and found that these metabolites appear to exhibit a distinct property profile (Figure 2B). It is important to note that P. aeruginosa represents a single, opportunistic pathogen and is not intended to reflect the broader diversity of microbial species.18 Rather, it serves as an illustrative contrast to highlight how metabolites from a well-characterized pathogen may occupy distinct regions of chemical space. The observed differences could reflect unique metabolic features or reporting biases specific to P. aeruginosa, rather than general trends between pathogens and commensal microbiota (Figure 2B). Although we cannot discount the influence of reporting biases on these observed differences, this analysis continues to highlight a considerable similarity between many microbiome metabolites and drug-like compounds that can potentially be exploited by machine learning algorithms trained on drug-like structures to predict metabolite activities.

The first four of the investigated chemical properties (logP, molecular weight, number of hydrogen bond donors, and number of hydrogen bond acceptors) are also commonly used to determine whether molecules are compliant with Lipinski’s “Rule of Five.” Originally, Lipinski’s “Rule of Five” had been developed to serve as a simple heuristic guideline for gauging the oral bioavailability of a synthetic small molecule compound.19 While no longer considered an accurate filter due to the recognition of important exceptions, these rules remain utilized as indicative benchmarks for properties crucial to a molecule’s bioavailability and their “drug-likeness.”20 However, it is important to recognize that many bioactive natural and microbiome-derived metabolites exert physiological effects despite falling outside these physicochemical boundaries.9 We found that 43.55% of the microbiome metabolites meet all four criteria stipulated by Lipinski’s “Rules of Five,” and 14.98% have only one violation. This indicates that more than a half (58.54%) of the microbiome metabolites are compliant with Lipinski’s “Rules of Five” by not violating more than one of the rules. In contrast, only 15.83% of the P. aeruginosa metabolites met all four criteria, and 6.65% were found to have only one violation, indicating that metabolites produced by other microbes might be less “drug-like” compared with human microbiome metabolites.

Overall, this analysis suggests that microbiome metabolites share similar molecular patterns and properties with drug-like structures, which indicates that machine learning models trained on drug-like structures may be well suited for guided elucidation of the properties and biological effects of microbiome metabolites.

Machine learning model development for ADMET property prediction

Because our chemical similarity assessment and physicochemical property comparisons indicated that many microbiome metabolites might be sufficiently similar to approved drugs and thereby could be processed by molecular machine learning algorithms,21 we next aimed to predict biological properties of the microbiome metabolites with machine learning. For this, we first focused on various ADMET properties of microbiome metabolites. First, to identify the most promising predictive machine learning models for each of the properties of interest, we systematically tested five different models for each property in retrospective cross-validation experiments. We explored different predictive architectures ranging from classical machine learning models such as random forest (RF) to neural networks such as multilayer perceptron (MLP). For the ADMET properties of interest, the XGBoost Tree (XGBT) model consistently outperformed all other tested models, followed by either RF or support vector machine (SVM), when evaluating the model’s predictive ability using the Cohen’s κ metric (Figure S1). Cohen’s κ measures how well a classifier predicts the correct class of test set datapoints compared to agreement by random chance contextualizing classifier performance, especially in situations where the data are highly imbalanced. The XGBT model, which applies gradient boosting to combine multiple weak decision trees, offers strong predictive accuracy and generalization by sequentially minimizing residual errors. Its ability to handle heterogeneous molecular descriptors and complex, non-linear feature interactions made it apparently particularly suitable for predicting diverse ADMET properties.

For example, the XGBT model achieved a Cohen’s κ of 0.698 for predicting BBB penetration, which was significantly higher (p ≤ 0.0001) than that of other models. Its performance was comparable (p > 0.05) to the RF model in terms of ROC-AUC (XGBT: 0.925; RF: 0.915) and accuracy (XGBT: 0.894; RF: 0.889). Across all endpoints, the XGBT model either performed significantly better than alternative models (p ≤ 0.0001) in two tasks or demonstrated statistically indistinguishable results compraed to one other well-performing model in the remaining three, reinforcing its selection as the primary model for prospective prediction tasks. Based on this high retrospective performance of the XGBT model, we hypothesized that these XGBT models could be used to predict ADMET properties of the microbiome metabolites, and we set out to analyze predictive trends and validate individual predictions.

To better understand which molecular characteristics drive model performance, we analyzed the feature importance of the XGBT models for each ADMET endpoint (Figures S1B–S1G). TPSA and lipophilicity-related descriptors (e.g., SlogP, the Wildman–Crippen LogP as implemented in RDKit) consistently ranked among the most important features for human intestinal absorption (HIA) and BBB permeability, in line with the established pharmacokinetic principles that lower polarity and moderate lipophilicity facilitate membrane diffusion. For toxicity-related endpoints such as drug-induced liver injury (DILI), mutagenicity, and cardiotoxicity, hydrogen bonding and aromatic substructure descriptors were among the key predictors, reflecting potential structure-toxicity relationships. These results indicate that the models captured chemically interpretable patterns consistent with empirical pharmacological knowledge. Improved interpretability of such models could, in the future, help prioritize descriptors or chemical motifs most relevant to metabolite safety and pharmacokinetics.

Machine learning identifies ADMET trends of microbiome metabolites

We applied the top performing model, XGBT, to predict the modeled ADMET properties of our microbial metabolites (Figure 3B). We first predicted HIA from a training dataset containing 578 tested small molecules annotated for their ability to permeate across the human intestine via passive diffusion, a criterion crucial for designing orally administered drugs that must bypass the intestinal barrier to reach their intended targets. Similarly, microbiome metabolites that can cross the intestinal barrier may be able to enter systemic circulation and thereby impact host health in tissues beyond the gastrointestinal tract. In this study, intestinal absorption predictions were applied to all microbiome metabolites to broadly assess their potential for systemic exposure, independent of their site of microbial production. While intestinal absorption is particularly relevant for gut-derived metabolites, we applied our predictions to all microbiome metabolites to capture a broader perspective on systemic exposure potential. This includes metabolites that may reach the bloodstream or other organs through non-intestinal routes, such as microbial production in extraintestinal sites or host co-metabolism. Several reports have already highlighted instances of microbial metabolites being absorbed across the intestinal barrier,23,24 but it is not yet understood at a larger scale how many and which metabolites might possess the ability to cross this important physiological barrier. Accurate experimental characterization of the intestinal absorption of metabolites requires complex ex vivo or in vivo experiments and availability of sufficient metabolites for testing, prohibiting the large-scale systematic characterization of the intestinal absorption of microbiome metabolites. Instead, we used our machine learning model to rapidly predict the intestinal absorption propensity of microbiome metabolites. While these predictions might not always be correct, they can prioritize metabolites for further testing and reveal larger overall trends. Our model estimated that about half (50.63%) of microbial metabolites from our database are likely to be absorbed into systemic circulation. Conversely, a substantial proportion (49.37%) of microbiome metabolites were predicted to have poor intestinal permeability, suggesting limited potential for systemic distribution and a higher likelihood of exerting localized effects near their site of origin (Figure 3B). Interestingly, a larger set of the FDA-approved drugs (83.83%) are predicted to be able to permeate across the intestinal membrane, possibly hinting at rational drug design steering this class of molecules toward being more intestinally permeable.

Beyond crossing systemic circulation through intestinal absorption, another important biological barrier is the BBB. The BBB plays a vital role in protecting the brain from xenobiotics and regulating the passage of substances between the bloodstream and the central nervous system. Some microbiome metabolites have been reported to permeate the BBB and impact regulatory mechanisms in the central nervous system,25 thereby potentially playing a central role in mediating the “gut-brain axis.” The training dataset employed for BBB penetration comprises 1,962 chemical compounds categorized into binary classes based on their ability to permeate the BBB.26,27 Based on our predictions, the metabolites demonstrate only slightly lower probabilities of penetrating the brain (59.88%) compared with FDA-approved drugs (66.9%), highlighting the need for further exploration of the brain-targeting effects of the microbiome metabolites. One example of such a metabolite that has been studied before is trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO), a microbiome metabolite that has been reported to cross the BBB and has been associated with various neurodegenerative diseases including Alzheimer disease and vascular dementia.28 TMAO was predicted to be BBB penetrable by our machine learning model, providing another supporting example for the notion that our model can correctly predict the behavior of selected metabolites.

Beyond the ability of metabolites to cross biological barriers, potential toxicities of the microbiome metabolites were also predicted. For example, the liver is a pivotal organ responsible for regulating xenobiotic metabolism and excretion processes.29 This also makes the liver vulnerable to damage caused by such xenobiotics. For example, approximately 52.83% of all available drugs have been reported to have caused at least one case of liver injury in patients.30 This is reflected in our prediction results for the hepatoxic potential of FDA-approved drugs, where 47.33% of the drugs were predicted to be liver toxic by our machine learning models when using a training dataset of 475 compounds tested for their hepatoxicity.31 Interestingly, our model predicted that the majority of microbial metabolites (74.2%) are safe to the liver. This potentially hints at the symbiotic relationship between the microbiome and the host that will steer molecular evolution away from creating microbiome metabolites that could be harmful to their hosts. Additionally, to assess how intestinal absorption relates to other key pharmacokinetic features, we evaluated the overlap of metabolites with positive HIA predictions and those predicted as BBB permeable or liver safe. We found that most metabolites predicted to be absorbed by the intestine were also predicted to cross the BBB and to not cause liver damage, indicating potentially coordinated physicochemical properties of metabolites that enable both absorption across various barriers and reduced risk of liver damage induced by these absorbable metabolites (Figure S2A).

Another toxicity we aimed to assess was mutagenicity. To investigate this, we used a training set comprising 7,253 compounds with known mutagenicity propensities.32 Our machine learning model predicts that the vast majority (95.01%) of microbiome metabolites are unlikely to cause mutagenesis. This result may be somewhat expected, as molecular mutagenicity is commonly assessed using microbial assays such as the Ames test, which commonly relies on assessing mutagenicity in microbes such as Salmonella or E. coli. Since microbiome metabolites are produced by microbes, it is plausible that they have evolved to avoid producing compounds that could damage their own genetic material. For comparison, we also performed mutagenicity prediction on metabolites from the opportunistic pathogen P. aeruginosa, which was analyzed earlier for its chemical properties (Figure 2B). Consistent with the microbiome metabolite results, 97.1% of P. aeruginosa metabolites were predicted to be non-mutagenic (Figure S2D), further supporting the idea that even pathogenic bacteria are unlikely to produce large numbers of mutagenic compounds. Nonetheless, the small fraction of metabolites predicted to be mutagenic may still exert significant biological effects and warrants further investigation.

Finally, we predicted the expected cardiotoxicity risks of microbiome metabolites by assessing their ability to inhibit the cardiac human ether-à-go-go related gene (hERG)-encoded Kv11.1 potassium channel by relying on publicly available data for Kv11.1 inhibition with 644 entries.33 The majority of microbiome metabolites were predicted to pose a low risk of hERG inhibition, with 61.85% classified as non-inhibitors of hERG. Similar to the liver toxicity predictions, this could potentially hint at the ability of the microbiome to largely avoid causing damage to their hosts by preventing the creation of cardiotoxic metabolites.

To further contextualize these global ADMET trends, we next examined genus-level patterns among major gut microbiome taxa (Figures S2B–S2D and Table S4). We selected seven representative genera—Clostridium, Ruminococcus, Lactobacillus, Enterococcus, Bacteroides, Prevotella, and Escherichia—because they are among the most abundant bacterial genera in the human microbiome and are also well represented in our microbiome metabolite database. Analysis of shared metabolites across these genera revealed extensive overlap, with the majority of metabolites (600 compounds) shared among all seven genera (Figure S2B).

We then performed ADMET property predictions for each genus individually. The predicted distributions across all five ADMET endpoints (HIA, BBB penetration, liver safety, mutagenicity, and cardiotoxicity) were largely consistent with those observed for the larger microbiome metabolite dataset, with only minor genus-specific variations (Table S4). As a representative example, HIA prediction results are shown in Figure S2C, where all genera exhibited similar proportions of predicted intestinally absorbable metabolites (HIA = 1). These consistent trends suggest that core microbiome taxa produce chemically similar metabolites with comparable pharmacokinetic and safety profiles. Some specific variations on the level of specific metabolites exclusive to certain groups of species indicate an opportunity for studying specific metabolites that are potentially uniquely expressed in certain microbiomes and their unique biological effects.

Taken together, these predictions suggested that many microbiome metabolites can be generally considered safe based on outcomes in three major toxicity assays, but several metabolites were predicted to potentially exert some toxicities that might warrant further attention. Additionally, predictions of biological barrier behavior indicate distinct groups of metabolites, including metabolites likely to be restricted to the gastrointestinal tract and those with potential to enter systemic circulation, and even cross the brain. It is important to note that these trends are based on computational predictions and that additional validation experiments are necessary to strengthen our confidence in our models and to identify which metabolites indeed exert the predicted properties. Therefore, we set out to perform additional validations and experiments based on these predictions.

Validating barrier-penetration predictions using external datasets

While our machine learning models revealed some predictive trends among microbiome metabolites, it is crucial to further validate the models to ensure that their predictions are accurate. Although our retrospective evaluations suggested that the models have acceptable performance (Figure 3A), it is possible that these evaluations overestimate performance by testing models on data that, at least in part, follow the same underlying distributions as the training data. Instead, evaluations on external data are necessary to assess whether the machine learning models can generalize and make predictions for microbiome metabolites.

For example, by identifying the overlap between metabolites predicted to exhibit a certain property with metabolites that are already known to exhibit this property, we can test the ability of our models to correctly classify positive samples and thereby estimate the model’s sensitivity. Although we cannot easily validate whether a model can correctly identify negative samples (specificity) because the absence of a literature report does not necessarily indicate the lack of a property for this metabolite, this, overall, still allows us to estimate the ability of our models to correctly predict true metabolite properties.

Here, we employed the GUTSY Atlas plasma metabolite dataset34 as an external validation set for our model that classifies the propensity of molecules to undergo HIA. The GUTSY Atlas provides an experimentally validated list of metabolites that have been previously detected in human plasma and whose plasma concentration has been shown to correlate with distinct gut microbiome compositions. We hypothesized that a set of microbiome-produced metabolites that can be detected in both the human intestine and human plasma and whose concentration varies with different microbiome compositions are likely to be absorbed through the intestine. Therefore, we matched the metabolites in our database with those detected in the intestine as indicated in the Microbial Metabolites Database (MiMeDB)14 and then further cross-referenced these metabolites with the GUTSY online atlas of metabolites detected in plasma. This cross-referencing resulted in an overlapping set of 50 microbiome-derived metabolites that can be detected both in the intestine and in plasma.

To evaluate the enrichment of intestinally absorbed compounds within this biologically validated set, we compared the fraction of predicted positives between the full microbiome metabolite dataset and the GUTSY subset. Across all microbiome metabolites, 50.6% were predicted to be intestinally absorbed, whereas 86% of the GUTSY metabolites were predicted to be absorbed, corresponding to an odds ratio of 6.0 (Fisher’s exact test, p = 2 × 10−4; Figure 4A). This enrichment indicates that the model selectively identifies metabolites with experimental evidence of intestinal absorption, rather than uniformly assigning positive predictions. In addition, we identified another external validation set for our BBB penetration model, a dataset of metabolites detected in human brains from the National Institute on Aging Genetics of Alzheimer’s Disease Data Storage Site (NIAGADS: NG00131.v2).35 Similar to the GUTSY plasma database validation, our hypothesis is that microbial metabolites are likely to be able to penetrate the BBB if they have been reported to be detectable in the brain. Accordingly, we created a validation set by cross-referencing our microbiome metabolite database with brain metabolites from NIAGADS, resulting in a set of 151 microbiome-derived metabolites that can be found in both datasets. Among these, 77.33% were predicted to be BBB penetrant (Figure S3B), compared with 59.9% across all microbiome metabolites, corresponding to an odds ratio of 2.3 (Fisher’s exact test, p = 5 × 10−4; Figure 4A). This enrichment demonstrates that the model preferentially identifies compounds with biological evidence of BBB penetration. Collectively, the observed enrichment in correctly predicted positives provides strong independent support for the models’ biological relevance and generalizability.

In spite of these promising results, it is important to acknowledge some limitations in this analysis. Both external datasets include only metabolites that have been experimentally detected in plasma or brain, respectively, and, therefore, contain only positive-class examples for the modeled property. As such, the reported external validation values represent the proportion of known positives correctly identified by the model, rather than full two-class accuracy including true negatives. Because negative examples are not defined in these external datasets, additional metrics such as precision, specificity, balanced accuracy, or Cohen’s κ cannot be computed. Furthermore, some metabolites in our database have been reported to be produced not only by microbes but also by their hosts. Similarly, some metabolites may not directly cross various biological barriers but instead have primary forms that penetrate and are subsequently metabolized into a specific downstream metabolite in situ. In such cases, we are unable to distinguish whether a metabolite is present in a certain tissue because it has trafficked to this tissue from the intestine or because of other mechanisms such as host metabolism. However, the inclusion of such metabolites in our analysis is intentional, as our goal was to capture both microbiome-derived and host-microbiome co-metabolites that may differ in concentration or function due to microbial metabolism. It is also important to note the apparent similarity between the achieved recall values (86% for intestinal absorption and 77% for BBB penetration) and the proportion of positive samples in the respective training datasets (Table S1 and Figure S1A). While these similarities could indicate a model that relies on priors in the training data to make predictions, our models showed strong retrospective performance and produced distinct predictive trends across the larger microbiome metabolite dataset (Figure 3B). Furthermore, when applied to FDA-approved drugs, the same models produced different class distributions compared with microbiome metabolites, underscoring the robustness and domain sensitivity of the predictive framework. Therefore, we believe it is unlikely that the high enrichment is simply caused by training data priors, but it is difficult to fully refute this. Taken together, while external datasets offer an economic path to validate predictive models, these inherent limitations might bias, at least, some of the results. Instead, prospective experiments are a more explicit way to assess model performance and to characterize the properties of specific metabolites. Therefore, we next set out to conduct prospective validation experiments for several of our metabolites.

In vitro validation of drug-induced liver injury predictions

A more rigorous way to evaluate a model, other than using external test data, is prospective experiments because they enable the validation on novel, unknown data in real-world settings, instead of necessitating reliance on historical data. We set out to validate the predictions of DILI for the metabolites through our in-house in vitro cell culture experiments. Utilizing human hepatocyte carcinoma HepG2 cells, a simple but widely employed model for hepatotoxicity assessment,36 we evaluated whether a set of metabolites that are confidently predicted to be hepatotoxic could cause cytotoxicity and whether metabolites predicted to be safe would have no effect on HepG2 cellular viability. We curated a list of 12 candidate metabolites (Figures S3C and S3D; Table S2) based on our machine learning model prediction for prospective experimental evaluation using high predictive confidence, while ensuring commercial availability, and sufficient aqueous solubility of the metabolites to enable experimental testing. We purchased seven metabolites predicted to be liver safe and five metabolites predicted to be liver toxic by our model for testing. Specifically, we prioritized metabolites with predicted probabilities of being liver safe either well above 0.9 or below 0.15 to enable evaluation of both potentially safe and unsafe compounds. One metabolite, indoxyl β-D-glucoside, with a predicted probability of 0.49, was included as a borderline case to further probe the model’s performance.

All of these selected metabolites were then tested for their cytotoxicity on HepG2 cells at three different molecular concentrations (N = 3, n = 3). Cells were treated for 24 hours, and we assessed cell viability using the MTT assay. Remarkably, all six metabolites predicted to be toxic demonstrated dose-dependent liver cell toxicity (Figures 4C and S3C; Table S2), exhibiting a dose-dependent pattern similar to the well-known liver-toxic drug isavuconazole, which was used as a positive control in our experiments (Figure 4B). Conversely, none of the metabolites predicted to be safe showed any statistically significant cytotoxicity at any of the tested concentrations (Figures 4D and S3D; Table S2).

For some of our predicted liver-toxic metabolites, studies have provided evidence of hepatotoxicity. For example, phloretin was shown to be liver toxic in rats in recent research.37 Although we did not find direct evidence of liver toxicity for 2′-deoxyguanosine in the literature, the oxidatively modified form, 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine, is known to exhibit liver toxicity.38,39 In contrast, to the best of our knowledge, levomefolic acid and xanthosine have no prior reported evidence of associated liver toxicity but showed dose-dependent cytotoxicity in our assays, which is in full agreement with our predictions. Although we were able to test only 12 metabolites in a simple in vitro model of liver cell toxicity, the lack of false predictions in this small test set suggests that our model is robust for further characterization and the analysis of trends among metabolites (Figure 4E). The novel associations such as the in vitro effects of levomefolic acid and xanthosine on HepG2 viability add to the body of work on these metabolites and warrant further research.

Predicting IL-8 stimulatory effects of metabolites

Having studied and further validated our machine learning models’ predictive ability of ADMET properties of metabolites, we next aimed to address an immunological question using our platform. It is known that certain microbial metabolites can influence the host immune system to provide important health effects, such as microbiome-derived inosine’s potential role to support the response of mice to immunotherapy.5 However, given the complexity of the immune system and the microbiome, our understanding of the interplay between microbiome metabolites and the immune system continues to be sparse.

For a case study to assess the potential of machine learning to support the identification of immunomodulatory microbiome metabolites, we built a machine learning model to predict whether a small molecule could induce IL-8 secretion, using a training set comprising 1,321 data points sourced from the publicly available PubChem repository. The training set includes binary labels denoting small molecules to be active (stimulating IL-8 secretion) and inactive (not stimulating IL-8 secretion).

Similar to our previous model selection strategy, we trained and evaluated multiple machine learning model architectures on these data. We found that RF and XGBT outperformed (p < 0.001) all other model architectures on these data in cross-validation experiments in terms of the ROC-AUC and Cohen’s κ metrics (Figure 4F and Data S3), while RF consistently had the highest absolute value for these metrics across all models. RF, an ensemble of decision trees that averages predictions across multiple bootstrapped samples, offers strong predictive stability, interpretable feature importance, and robustness to noise and class imbalance, which made it particularly suitable for this dataset. We further examined the interpretability of the IL-8 secretion model to identify molecular features that most strongly influenced predictions (Figure S1G). Feature importance was calculated as weighted importance across all trees in the RF ensemble. The most informative variables were primarily Morgan fingerprint bits, which represent distinct substructural motifs, rather than global physicochemical properties, suggesting that localized chemical fragments predominantly drive IL-8 secretion predictions. This interpretability analysis supports that the model captured chemically meaningful relationships and may help guide future studies aiming to design or identify microbiome-derived metabolites with targeted immunomodulatory effects.

Subsequently, we applied this RF model to our microbiome metabolite database to predict new associations for further testing. To validate these predictions, we selected three positively predicted metabolites as well as four negatively predicted metabolites for further in vitro experiments (Figures 4H and 4I; Table S3). We assessed IL-8 stimulation by treating human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) with each of the seven metabolites at a concentration of 100 μM for 24 hours (N = 1, n = 3; Figure 4H). Experimental results demonstrated that, out of seven tested metabolites, two were true positives (spermidine and spermine), four true negative, and only one was false positive (indoxyl β-D-glucoside). Both true positive metabolites are well known for their anti-inflammatory properties,40,41 but the model correctly predicted that they could stimulate IL-8 secretion in vitro with high confidence (probability for spermidine = 0.76, probability for spermine = 0.64; Figure 4G and Table S3). None of the negatively predicted metabolites induced IL-8 secretion, indicating overall good sensitivity of the model across this limited small test set.

We subsequently tested spermine and spermidine at three concentrations to explore potential concentration-dependent effects on IL-8 release (N = 2, n ≥ 2; Figure 4I). While the mean IL-8 levels tended to increase with concentration, the variance between donor samples was high, likely reflecting biological variability in primary PBMC responses. As a result, a clear statistical dose-response or EC50 could not be established from the current dataset. To further contextualize the IL-8 secretion stimulation by spermine and spermidine, we tested these metabolites for their potential to stimulate the secretion of other cytokines, including IL-1α, IL-6, IL-12p70, and TNF-α (Figure S3E). No significant stimulation was observed for any of the tested cytokines, indicating a striking selectivity of spermine’s and spermidine’s immunostimulatory potential.

Taken together, this case study highlights the potential of machine learning to facilitate study of the interactions between the microbiome and the immune system. Additionally, the previously unknown, selective IL-8 release stimulation property of the two well-studied anti-inflammatory molecules, spermine and spermidine, warrants further attention, with potentially important implications for their further development as biomarkers or supplements.

Discussion

The microbiome is increasingly being recognized as a crucial component of host physiology, impacting various processes associated with human health and disease. In particular, microbiome metabolites are receiving increasing attention due to their potential role in mediating these interactions. However, only a small subset of microbiome metabolites has been functionally characterized to date. Since the study of the biological effects of metabolites is laborious and expensive, we here propose, prototyped, and evaluated machine learning models that can triage experiments to accelerate the identification of biological effects of microbiome metabolites.

Specifically, we developed several machine learning models capable of predicting a wide range of properties of human microbiome metabolites, ranging from ADMET properties such as intestinal absorption and hepatoxicity to immunological properties exemplified by IL-8 secretion stimulation. The models were validated through a series of external datasets and prospective in-house experiments, demonstrating strong predictive performance and robustness. Notably, the DILI prediction model showed perfect concordance in the prospective test set, while the IL-8 secretion model reached 85.71%, resulting in an overall accuracy of 92.86% across prospective validations. Although these validations were performed on small prospective datasets, these results suggest that machine learning algorithms are well suited to anticipate microbiome metabolite properties, although occasional false predictions may still occur. Furthermore, our work has already led to the discovery of several previously unknown properties of multiple microbiome metabolites, including the selective stimulation of IL-8 secretion through spermine and spermidine.

Experimental results demonstrated encouraging predictive performance across a small test set, with two of the three predicted IL-8-stimulating metabolites (spermidine and spermine) confirmed experimentally. These two metabolites are structurally related polyamines that can interconvert via spermine oxidase (SMO) and, therefore, may not represent fully independent validation points. Given the limited sample size (n = 7) and the moderate imbalance of the training dataset (31.54% positive), these results should be interpreted as qualitative support for the model’s predictive capability, rather than quantitative evidence of specific levels of accuracy. Nevertheless, the correct identification of two known IL-8-stimulating metabolites highlights the model’s ability to capture meaningful relationships despite the inherent complexity of cytokine response data. We recognize that the small number of test compounds limits the statistical power of this validation and that additional IL-8-active metabolites will be needed in future work to strengthen these findings.

While distinguishing properties of metabolites derived from pathogenic and commensal microbes would be very valuable, such analysis is currently limited by the lack of standardized microbial source annotations across public metabolite databases. Information on microbial origin, including whether a metabolite is produced by pathogenic or commensal species, is not universally or consistently defined among data sources. In this study, microbial origins were aggregated from multiple databases that vary in annotation depth and curation criteria, and, therefore, the genus-level mappings used here should be interpreted as operational rather than definitive biosynthetic assignments. Similarly, detailed annotations on the anatomical or environmental location of metabolite production were not uniformly available, which limited our ability to analyze tissue- or site-specific patterns. Expanding and harmonizing these microbial source and location annotations will be crucial for enabling more holistic comparisons and facilitating future analyses focused on specific microbial taxa, tissues, or communities. Incorporating such information represents an important future direction that will enhance the biological interpretability of microbiome metabolite datasets.

While the experimental validations in this study confirmed several predicted metabolite activities, they were performed in a limited number of cell types and under in vitro conditions. As such, these findings may not fully represent the complexity of in vivo physiological contexts. Future work will extend the validation to additional cell types, organ systems, and animal models to confirm the observed effects and better assess their translational relevance. To further contextualize our experimental framework, it is important to note that the tested concentrations were selected to cover a broad range of metabolite dosages because it is not always known what true physiological concentrations of metabolites are and they can often vary widely. For example, while circulating concentrations of most microbiome metabolites are generally low, local hepatic exposure may be higher, particularly under conditions affecting gut barrier integrity that could allow greater transfer of metabolites from the gut to the circulation. Accordingly, our broad in vitro testing range (10 μM–1 mM) was chosen to attempt to encompass concentrations that may approximate both physiological and pathophysiological exposure levels relevant for assessing liver safety and cytokine secretion.42,43,44

The quality of predictive platforms such as the one developed here depends on the quality of the machine learning models, training data for specific properties, and the coverage of the microbiome metabolite database. With increasing focus of the research community on artificial intelligence, high-throughput experimental platforms for large data generation, and microbiome metabolomics, we expect the utility and quality of such machine learning-driven predictive platforms to continuously improve. Furthermore, many of the datasets used in machine learning models are derived from in vitro experiments, which might not always correlate with in vivo outcomes of metabolites. Nevertheless, the prioritization of metabolites for in vitro and subsequent in vivo experiments could constitute a rapid alternative to untargeted screening experiments.

In the future, we expect the machine learning-assisted functional annotation of microbiome metabolites to facilitate the identification of biomarkers, which can aid in patient diagnostics and prognostics as well as triage patient populations to better serve patients with distinct microbiome composition. By linking metabolite chemical structures to predicted biological properties such as intestinal absorption, immune modulation, and toxicity, our models can highlight metabolites that are both biologically active and likely to circulate systemically or appear in accessible biofluids. These predicted bioactive metabolites represent promising biomarker candidates because their abundance or detection in plasma, stool, or urine may reflect microbial activity, barrier integrity, or host-microbiome interactions associated with disease states. Furthermore, if there is a specific interest in a disease area, this framework can be applied to rapidly predict which microbiome metabolites are most likely to exhibit phenotypic effects relevant to that condition. This targeted, prediction-driven screening strategy allows researchers to narrow down large metabolite libraries to the most plausible disease-relevant candidates for experimental and clinical validation. Ultimately, we hope that our microbiome’s symbiotic molecular arsenal can be used to derive novel therapies and adjuvants for treatment and prevention of diseases.

Limitations of the study

This study provides a systematic framework for predicting and validating biological properties of microbiome-derived metabolites; however, several limitations remain that warrant consideration. Prospective experimental validation was performed on a small set of metabolites, and some validated compounds, such as spermine and spermidine, are structurally related and may not represent fully independent validation points. Class imbalance in the training data, the limited number of experimentally confirmed IL-8-active metabolites, and reliance on in vitro-derived training datasets constrain quantitative interpretation of the predictive accuracy. Experimental validation was conducted in a limited number of cell types under in vitro conditions, and test concentrations were selected to span a broad range due to uncertainty in physiological exposure levels that can vary by tissue, anatomical location, and disease context. In addition, analyses of microbial origin and site of metabolite production were limited by inconsistent and incomplete annotations across public databases, restricting conclusions regarding metabolite provenance and tissue specificity.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

All cells were cultured at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. HepG2 cells were cultured using DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. Two primary human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were used. The first sample (PBMC060822AH) was obtained from a male Caucasian (46 years old). The second sample was obtained from ATCC (PCS-800-011, Lot 8032322), from a female Caucasian (72 years old). PBMCs were maintained using RMPI-1640 based Lymph-1 lymphocyte medium (Labscoop Lymph-1) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. Cell lines were not independently authenticated and were not tested for mycoplasma contamination in this study. Sex-specific analyses were not performed and the study was not powered to detect sex-based differences.

Method details

Curation of the microbiome metabolite database

We curated a database of microbiome metabolites from five different publicly available sources, with a focus on selecting compounds of microbiome origin or human–microbiome co-metabolites that are chemically modified by commensal microbes. The databases are: Human Metabolome Database (HMDB),11 Virtual Metabolic Human (VMH),12 KNApsack Comprehensive Species-Metabolite Relationship Database (KNApSAcK),13 Microbial Metabolites Database (MiMeDB),14 and the Metabolomics Data Explorer.15 From HMDB and VMH, we included metabolites whose source annotation is “bacteria.” Because both databases contain metabolites detected in humans, this filter identifies compounds that are of microbial origin but observed in the human system, thereby capturing microbiome-derived or microbiome-modified metabolites. In KNApSAcK, we filtered for metabolites reported to originate from both human and bacterial sources. From MiMeDB, we selected metabolites categorized as either “primary” (originating solely from microbial biosynthetic pathways) or “co-metabolites” (produced or modified by both human and microbiome metabolic processes). All metabolites obtained from the Metabolomics Data Explorer were included based on their detection in in vitro culture of commensal microbes. Each metabolite data entry was annotated with the microbial strains that are known to produce this metabolite along with additional identifiers such as their chemical name, CAS number, InChI key, PubChem CID, tissue location, and biofluid location, if this information was available in any of the data sources. Data that contained missing values and duplicates were removed. The final database captures the chemical structures (stored as canonical Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System; SMILES) of 2,645 metabolites.

Datasets for machine learning model training

The dataset of FDA-approved drugs was sourced from a review paper,45 originally derived from DrugBank 2020.46 Training datasets for ADMET property prediction were obtained from the Therapeutics Data Commons (TDCommons).22 Additionally, the training set for IL-8 secretion was obtained from PubChem (Assay IDs 651758, 327254, 598346)47,48,49 while removing compounds labeled as “Inconclusive” and “Inhibitor” and labeling “Active” compounds as the positive class and “Inactive” compounds as negative class for IL-8 secretion. For all the datasets, data entries containing missing values were removed and duplicates were retained only when they were consistently annotated according to their labels. No rebalancing techniques were applied to preserve real-world class distributions.

Analysis of “drug-like” properties

Chemical properties (LogP, molecular weight, hydrogen bond donors, hydrogen bond acceptors, topological surface area, rotatable bonds) were calculated using RDKit nodes (Version 2023.09.2) in KNIME (Version 5.2.3.). The t-SNE plot was generated using Morgan fingerprints (1024 bit, radius of 2) and RDKit property descriptors. Kernel density estimate (KDE) plots were generated using the Seaborn library in Python.

Designing and cross-validation of machine learning models

All the classification machine learning models were developed in the KNIME data analysis software (KNIME 5.2.3). Five machine learning models were tested for each property prediction: random forest (RF), XGBoost linear, XGBoost tree, multi-layer perceptron (MLP), and Support Vector Machine (SVM). All models were implemented using default parameters unless otherwise specified. The XGBoost Tree model used a binary logistic objective (softprob) with 100 boosting rounds, maximum tree depth of 6, learning rate (eta) of 0.3, and L1/L2 regularization (alpha = 1, lambda = 1). The XGBoost Linear model used the same objective and regularization settings with 100 boosting rounds. The RF model was trained using 100 trees and the Information Gain Ratio as the split criterion with a fixed random seed (1646190278764). The SVM model (LIBSVM Learner) used a C-SVC classifier with a linear kernel, cost parameter of 1.0, epsilon of 0.001, and probability estimates enabled. The MLP model (RProp Learner) employed three hidden layers with 10 neurons per layer, trained for 100 iterations with a random seed of −30,629,736. Molecules were described with Morgan fingerprints (1024 bit, radius of 2) and RDKit descriptors. All descriptors were normalized to a range between 0 and 1 using min–max scaling. The minimum and maximum values were determined from the training data, and the same transformation was subsequently applied to the test data to avoid data leakage. Models were evaluated through stratified 5 X 5-fold cross-validation evaluations for model selection. Performance was quantified using the metrics ROC-AUC, accuracy, balanced accuracy, Cohen’s κ, recall, precision, and F-1.

Cytotoxicity assay of HepG2 cells

HepG2 cells were plated in a 96-well plate at a density of 300,000 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight at 37°C in 5% CO2. The following day, the cells were treated with metabolites or positive control cytotoxic drug at three different testing concentrations (1 mM, 100 μM, 10 μM) in culture medium, followed by a 24-hour incubation period. After incubation, MTT solution was added to each well and incubated for 4 hours at 37°C in 5% CO2. The medium was then removed, and 100 μL of DMSO was added to each well. After shaking the plate at 400 rpm for 10 minutes at room temperature, the absorbance at 570 nm was measured using a plate reader (Tecan Infinite M Plex).

Cytokine stimulation assay

Human PBMCs were plated in a 96-well plate at a density of 150,000 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight at 37°C in 5% CO2. The following day, cells were treated with metabolites at different concentrations (1 mM, 100 μM, 10 μM). 1 μg/mL LPS (Invivogen tlrl-eblps) and 1 μg/mL PGN-EK (Invivogen tlrl-pgnek) served as positive controls. All treatments were dissolved in culture medium. Cells were incubated with the treatments for a 24-hour period. After 24 hours, the media was removed and stored at -80°C until analysis. The IL-1α, IL-6, IL-8, IL-12p70, and TNFα levels were measured using a Milliplex analyte kit (EMD Millipore). Cytokine stimulation experiments were performed in collaboration with the UNC CGIBD Advanced Analytics core facility and ZenBio Inc. in fee-for-service agreements. (5-PA: 5-Phenylvaleric Acid, IBDG: Indoxyl β-D-Glucoside, 3,4-DHA: 3,4-Dihydroxyhydocinnamic Acid, 3-HT: 3-Hydroxytramine, HA: Hippuric Acid).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of enrichment

To evaluate enrichment of predicted positives in external validation datasets, we constructed 2×2 contingency tables comparing the number of predicted positive and negative metabolites in each external set (GUTSY or NIAGADS) relative to the full microbiome metabolite background. Odds ratios (OR) were calculated as OR=(a×d)/(b×c), and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using the log-transformed standard error method. Statistical significance was determined using Fisher’s exact test (two-tailed) in GraphPad Prism.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed with the GraphPad Prism software (Prism 10.0.3). For cytotoxicity assays, treatment groups were compared with the vehicle-only control (media without metabolite) using an unpaired two-tailed t-test. For cytokine stimulation assays, treatment groups were compared with the negative control (media only) using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test to account for multiple comparisons. Machine learning performance metrics were compared across models using one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s or Tukey’s post-hoc tests. These parametric tests were chosen because ANOVA and t-tests are robust to moderate deviations from normality and are appropriate for cross-validation metrics and biological assay data that approximate normal distributions. For biological assays, N represents independent biological replicates and n represents technical replicates within each biological replicate. Statistical analyses for cytotoxicity assays were performed using N as the unit of analysis. For machine learning analyses using 5 × 5 cross-validation, replicates refer to the five repeated cross-validation runs. Statistical significance was defined as p ≤ 0.05 unless otherwise specified. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Error bars denote standard deviation across biological replicates in all figures. Exact statistical tests, sample sizes (N and n), and details for each experiment are provided in the corresponding figure legends.
