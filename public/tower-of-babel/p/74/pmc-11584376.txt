Gender differences in the management and outcomes of acute coronary syndrome in indians: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Deep Dutta, Kunal Mahajan, Lokesh Verma, Gunjan Gupta, Meha Sharma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGender differences in acute coronary syndrome (ACS) outcomes have been noted in global data, which however did not analyse Indian data. No prior systematic review and meta-analysis (SRM) has addressed this important aspect of gender bias in Indian women with ACS. Hence this SRM aimed to address this knowledge gap.MethodsElectronic databases were searched for studies in ACS comparing cardiovascular disease presentation, treatment received and outcomes in women and men from India. Primary outcomes were to evaluate gender-differences in 30-day death and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). Secondary outcomes were to evaluate gender-differences in presentation, management and mortality. The SRM is registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023477286).ResultsFrom initially screened 3753 articles, data from 9 studies (61,185 patients) were analysed. Women with ACS had higher prevalence of diabetes [Odds ratio (OR) 1.65(95%CI:1.33–2.04); p < 0.001; I2 = 95 %] and hypertension [OR2.06(95%CI:1.88–2.25); p < 0.001; I2 = 42 %]. Smoking was significantly lower in women [OR 0.05(95%CI:0.03–0.07); p < 0.001; I2 = 87 %]. Non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) was significantly higher in women [OR 1.92(95%CI:1.66–2.21); p < 0.001; I2 = 0 %]. Diagnostic angiography [OR 0.64(95%CI:0.56–0.74); p < 0.001; I2 = 46 %] and percutaneous coronary interventions [OR0.71(95%CI:0.55–0.92); p = 0.01; I2 = 92 %] were significantly lower in women. Women had significantly higher 30-day mortality [Hazard ratio (HR)2.26(95%CI:2.01–2.55); p < 0.001; I2 = 6 %], 1-year mortality [HR2.41(95%CI:1.89–3.07); p < 0.001; I2 = 53 %], in-hospital death [HR1.88(95%CI:1.19–2.96); p = 0.007; I2 = 92 %], stroke [HR 1.84 (95%CI:1.34–2.52); p < 0.001; I2 = 0 %] and MACE outcomes [OR 2.05 (95%CI:1.78–2.35); p < 0.001]. Use of aspirin, clopidogrel, beta-blockers and nitrates were significantly lower in women.ConclusionOur study highlights worse outcomes in Indian women with ACS. Higher burden of diabetes and hypertension, decreased used of PCI and lesser aggressive pharmacotherapy may be some of the contributing factors.Keywords: Gender differences, Gender disparity, Meta-analysis, Acute coronary syndrome, Outcomes

Introduction

The diabesity pandemic, especially in the low/middle-income countries (LMICs), is driving the cardiovascular disease pandemic globally, with LMICs contributing to nearly 80 % of cardiovascular disease burden.1 Data coming from the so called “developed” or high-income countries have suggested that women with cardiovascular disease tend to be diagnosed late, less likely to receive optimal therapy (medical and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI)) as compared to men.2, 3, 4

Gender difference in acute coronary syndrome (ACS) clinical presentation was recognised as early as 2010. In a meta-analysis involving patients predominantly from the pre-PCI era, Shin et al noted that women were more likely than men to report back pain, palpitations, nausea/vomiting, and loss of appetite as presenting features of ACS.5 In a subsequent systematic review and meta-analysis (SRM) on the same topic published by Oosterhout et al (analysing data from 27 studies), the authors noted that as compared to men women with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) had higher odds of presenting with pain between shoulder blades (OR 2.15; 95 % CI: 1.95–2.37), nausea/vomiting (OR 1.64; 95 % CI: 1.48–1.82), shortness of breath (OR 1.34; 95 % CI: 1.21–1.48), lower odds of presenting with chest pain (OR 0.70; 95 % CI: 0.63–0.78) and diaphoresis (OR 0.84: 95 % CI, 0.76–0.94).6 In a SRM involving data from 37 observational studies and 21 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published in 2023, Lunova et al reported that as compared to men, women with ACS were more likely to experience delays in treatment (RCTs; odds ratio (OR) 1.43; 95 % CI: 1.12–1.82), less often treated invasively (RCTs; OR 0.87; 95 % CI, 0.83–0.9; observational studies (OSs); OR 0.66; 95 % CI: 0.63–0.68), higher crude in-hospital mortality (OSs; OR 1.56; 95 % CI, 1.53–1.59) and 30-day mortality (OSs; OR 1.71; 95 % CI, 1.22–2.4; RCTs; OR 2.74; 95 % CI, 2.48–3.02).7 However, SRMs published till now did not include any study published from India.

India, which also comes among the LMICs, unfortunately has become the diabetes and cardiovascular disease capital of the world.8,9 ACS is increasingly been reported in young Indian less than 40 years age, primarily driven by early onset of type-2 diabetes (T2DM) and metabolic syndrome.10,11 Several studies have been published from India looking into the gender differences and disparities in cardiovascular disease outcomes.12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 Literature review revealed that no SRM has holistically analysed till date the gender differences in people living with cardiovascular disease in India, focussing on the clinical presentation, diagnosis, treatment received and long-term clinical outcomes. Hence the aim of this SRM was to compare the gender differences in the management and outcomes of people living with ACS in India.

Methods

Methodology

The meta-analysis was done using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines having PROSPERO registration number of CRD42023477286. All studies published till 30th September 2023 were considered for this meta-analysis. Using PICOS criteria, studies having patients with ACS having data available comparing the cardiovascular disease presentation, disease profile, treatment received and clinical outcomes in women as compared to men were considered for this systematic review. Studies focussing on other forms of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) and not primarily on ACS in coronary artery disease were excluded. Studies focussing on other non-communicable disease like advanced kidney disease, liver disease, or other complications of diabetes were excluded. Small studies with less than 150 patients were excluded as they were more likely to suffer from recruitment and referral bias. Only studies published on patients from India were included. The primary outcomes were to evaluate the 30-day death and major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE; death from a cardiovascular cause, nonfatal stroke, or nonfatal myocardial infarction) rates in women as compared to men. The secondary outcomes of this study were to evaluate the gender differences in presentation, in-hospital medical management, need for PCI, in-hospital mortality, discharge medication management and beyond 30-day MACE outcomes and beyond 30-day mortality. We systematically searched Embase database, Web of science, Cochrane library, Medline (PubMed), clinicaltrials.gov, CNKI database, ctri.nic.in, and Google scholar as either key-words or MESH terms using a Boolean search strategy: (acute coronary syndrome [MeSH]) AND (gender [All Fields]) AND (female) [All Fields]).

Data extraction with regards to all the primary and secondary outcomes stated above was carried out independently by two authors. Multiple publications from the same group on the same cohort of patients were pooled together and considered as a single study for our meta-analysis. Details have been elaborated in previous meta-analysis published by our group.18,19

Continuous variable outcomes were presented as mean differences (MD). For dichotomous variables, outcomes were expressed as risk ratios (RR) with 95 % confidence intervals (CI) and as hazard ratios (HR) for adverse events. RevMan 5.4 was used for doing all the statistical analysis and generation of Forest plots in this meta-analysis. Random effect model has been used for analysis. The forest plot generated for all the different outcomes was used to assess the heterogeneity. We specifically used Chi2 test on N-1 degrees of freedom, with an alpha of 0.05 used for statistical significance and with the I2 test.20,21 The details of heterogeneity analysis have been elaborated elsewhere.18,19

Two authors utilized the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) to evaluate the risk of bias of the studies. This scale employs three different aspects to assess the potential bias which consists of: 1) participant selection (consisting of four criteria: representativeness of the exposed cohort, comparable derivation of exposed and non-exposed participants, proper ascertainment of exposure, and absence of the outcome of interest at the study's commencement), 2) comparability (based on the design of the analysis and assigning zero points if odds ratio or relative risk were calculated solely from raw participant numbers in the original reports), and 3) outcomes (evaluating the adequacy of outcome assessment, follow-up duration, and follow-up sufficiency). The highest possible score on the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale is 9, indicating the highest quality, whereas we categorized studies as low (0-3 points), moderate (4-6 points), or high (7-9 points) quality.22 In the event of any disagreements, a consensus was achieved through discussion.

Publication bias was assessed by plotting the Funnel Plot.23 The details of how the Funnel plots were plotted have been elaborated elsewhere.18,19 Significant publication bias was considered to be present if one or more study lies outside the Funnel plot. Since ethical approval was already taken for the all the RCTs which were included in this meta-analysis and no new patients were evaluated here, there was no need for separate ethical approval for this SRM.

Results

A total of 3753 articles were found after the initial search (Fig. 1). Two-hundred and twelve duplicates were removed. After screening of the titles, the search was reduced to 270 articles. Following further screening of the abstracts, the search was further reduced to 106 articles which were evaluated in detail for inclusion in this SRM (Supplementary Figure-1). Finally, we ended up with 17 articles, which evaluated gender differences in different aspects of CAD in Indians. Eight studies were excluded as they did not fulfil the inclusion and exclusion criteria.24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31 Nine out of 17 studies, which fulfilled all criteria were analysed.12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17,32, 33, 34 The details and characteristics of the patients in the 9 studies analysed in this SRM have been elaborated in supplementary table-1. Risk of bias assessment was done using the NOS, details of which have been elaborated in supplementary table 2. All studies were found to be of high quality (Supplementary Table-2).

Forest plots comparing the prevalence of different risk factors for acute coronary syndrome in women as compared to men (a) Diabetes; (b) Hypertension; (c) Smoking; (d) Previous history of myocardial infarction; (e) Previous history of stroke; (f) Previous history of percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass surgery.

Gender difference in clinical risk profile of patients with ACS

Data from 9 studies involving 61,185 people with ACS was analysed to find the gender difference in the clinical risk profile of patients with ACS. Women with ACS had a significantly higher prevalence of diabetes [OR 1.65 (95 % CI: 1.33–2.04); p < 0.001; I2 = 95 % (CH); figure-1a] and hypertension [OR 2.06 (95 % CI: 1.88–2.25); p < 0.001; I2 = 42 % mild heterogeneity (MiH); figure-1b]. Smoking was significantly lower in women as compared to men [OR 0.05 (95 % CI: 0.03–0.07); p < 0.001; I2 = 87 % (CH); figure-1c]. Previous history of myocardial infarction [OR 1.10 (95 % CI: 0.86–1.40); p = 0.47; I2 = 61 % (MH); figure-1d], stroke [OR 1.01 (95 % CI: 0.72–1.43); p = 0.94; I2 = 76 % (MH); figure-1e] and PCI/coronary artery bypass surgery (CABG) [OR 0.48 (95 % CI: 0.16–1.41); p = 0.18; I2 = 11 % (LH); figure-1f] was similar in women as compared to men.

Gender differences in presentation and diagnosis

Treatment delay as assessed by misinterpretation of initial symptoms [OR 1.36 (95 % CI: 0.95–1.95); p = 0.09; I2 = 80 % (CH); Fig. 2] and >6 hours from initial presentation to first medical contact [OR 1.19 (95 % CI: 0.92–1.53); p = 0.18; I2 = 88 % (CH); figure-2b] were similar in women as compared to men. ST elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) was significantly lower [OR 0.62 (95 % CI: 0.39–0.96); p = 0.03; I2 = 96 % (CH); figure-2c] and non-STEMI (NSTEMI)/unstable angina (UA) was significantly higher [OR 1.92 (95 % CI: 1.66–2.21); p < 0.001; I2 = 0 % (LH); figure-2d] in women as compared to men. Occurrence of Killip class>1 at the time of presentation [OR 1.27 (95 % CI: 0.85–1.89); p = 0.24; I2 = 97 % (CH); figure-2e] and serum creatinine >1.5 mg/dl at the time of presentation [OR 0.93 (95 % CI: 0.80–1.07); p = 0.29; I2 = 0 % (LH); figure-2f] was similar in women as compared to men.

Forest plot comparing the gender differences in (a) Misinterpretation of clinical symptoms of acute coronary syndrome; (b) More than 6 hour delay in seeking appropriate cardiac healthcare; (c) presenting as ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI); (d) non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI); (e) Killip Class>1; (f) Serum creatinine >1.5 mg/dl.

Baseline medical therapy

The use of aspirin [OR 0.92 (95 % CI: 0.72–1.17); p = 0.50; I2 = 83 % (CH); Fig. 3], clopidogrel [OR 0.97 (95 % CI: 0.77–1.21); p = 0.76; I2 = 76 % (MH)], statins [OR 0.95 (95 % CI: 0.78–1.17); p = 0.66; I2 = 73 % (MH); figure-3b] and calcium channel blockers [OR 1.58 (95 % CI: 0.82–3.05); p = 0.17; I2 = 93 % (CH)] were similar in women as compared to men. The use of beta-blockers [OR 1.08 (95 % CI: 1.02–1.14); p = 0.007; I2 = 0 % (LH); figure-3c] and ACEIs/ARBs [OR 1.23 (95 % CI: 1.12–1.35); p < 0.001; I2 = 0 % (LH); figure-3d] were significantly higher in women as compared to men.

Forest plot comparing the gender differences in medications being taken at baseline at the time of clinical presentation to cardiac care (a): Aspirin use; (b): Statin use; (c): Beta-blockers use; (d): Angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs)/Angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) use.

In-hospital therapy

PCI for ACS were significantly lower in women as compared to men [OR 0.71 (95 % CI: 0.55–0.92); p = 0.01; I2 = 92 % (CH); Fig. 4]. Even diagnostic coronary angiography without PCI was significantly lower in women as compared to men [OR 0.64 (95 % CI: 0.56–0.74); p < 0.001; I2 = 46 % (MH); n = 15,784]. The occurrence of thrombolysis in people with STEMI were similar in women as compared to men [OR 1.04 (95 % CI: 0.74–1.45); p = 0.83; I2 = 97 % (CH); figure-4b]. The occurrence of coronary artery bypass surgery (CABG) was similar in women as compared to men [OR 0.99 (95 % CI: 0.80–1.24); p = 0.95; I2 = 0 % (LH); n = 27,934].

Forest plot comparing the gender differences in-hospital therapeutics (a): Percutaneous coronary interventions; (b): Thrombolysis; (c): Aspirin use; (d): Clopidogrel use; (e): Statin use; (f): Beta-blocker use.

During in-hospital therapy, the use of aspirin [OR 0.76 (95 % CI: 0.64–0.90); p = 0.002; I2 = 36 % (LH); figure-4c] and clopidogrel [OR 0.83 (95 % CI: 0.75–0.92); p < 0.001; I2 = 0 % (LH); figure-4d] was significantly lower in women as compared to men. During in-hospital therapy, the use of statins [OR 1.08 (95 % CI: 0.85–1.38); p = 0.52; I2 = 28 % (LH); figure-4e], beta-blockers [OR 0.93 (95 % CI: 0.82–1.06); p = 0.26; I2 = 73 % (MH); figure-4f], ACEI/ARBs [OR 1.14 (95 % CI: 0.91–1.43); p = 0.24; I2 = 61 % (MH); n = 28,997] and heparin [OR 0.92 (95 % CI: 0.80–1.06); p = 0.25; I2 = 70 % (MH); n = 51,936] were similar in women as compared to men.

Treatment at the time of discharge

Women were significantly less likely to receive aspirin [OR 0.67 (95 % CI: 0.47–0.96); p = 0.03; I2 = 96 % (CH); Fig. 5], clopidogrel [OR 0.69 (95 % CI: 0.49–0.96); p = 0.03; I2 = 95 % (CH); figure-5b], beta-blockers [OR 0.81 (95 % CI: 0.70–0.92); p = 0.002; I2 = 83 % (CH); figure-5c] and nitrates [OR 0.84 (95 % CI: 0.79–0.89); p < 0.001; I2 = 0 % (LH); n = 27,886] at the time of discharge as compared to men. Also, women were less likely to receive statins at the time of discharge which approached statistical significance [OR 0.74 (95 % CI: 0.54–1.02); p = 0.07; I2 = 95 % (CH); figure-5d]. At the time of discharge, there was no difference in the prescription of ACEIs/ARBs [OR 0.90 (95 % CI: 0.73–1.10); p = 0.3; I2 = 94 % (CH); n = 47,559] in women as compared to men.

Forest plot comparing the gender differences in the treatment advised at the time of discharge from hospital (a): Aspirin; (b): Clopidogrel; (c): Beta-blocker; (d): Statin.

Impact of gender on post-ACS cardiovascular outcomes

Data from 5 studies involving 29,494 people with ACS was analysed to find out the impact of gender of 30-day mortality. Women had significantly higher 30-day mortality as compared to men [Hazard ratio (HR) 2.26 (95 % CI: 2.01–2.55); p < 0.001; I2 = 6 % low heterogeneity (LH); Fig. 6]. Data from 3 studies (7075 patients) was analysed to find out the impact of gender on 1-year mortality post ACS. Women had significantly higher 1-year mortality as compared to men [HR 2.41 (95 % CI: 1.89–3.07); p < 0.001; I2 = 53 % moderate heterogeneity (MH); figure-6b]. Risk for re-infarction [HR 0.67 (95 % CI: 0.07–6.38); p = 0.72; I2 = 78 % considerable heterogeneity (CH); n = 3440; figure-6c], stroke [HR 2.47 (95 % CI: 0.33–18.71); p = 0.38; I2 = 74 % (CH); n = 3440; figure-6d] and heart failure [HR 2.07 (95 % CI: 0.59–7.23); p = 0.26; I2 = 74 % (CH); figure-6e; n = 3440] at 1 year was similar between women and men.

Forest plots comparing the gender differences in different events post-acute coronary syndrome (ACS) (a): 30-day mortality; (b): 1-year mortality; (c): 1-year reinfarction (d): 1-year stroke; (e): 1-year heart failure.

Impact of gender on in-hospital event rates

The in-hospital death rates [HR 1.88 (95 % CI: 1.19–2.96); p = 0.007; I2 = 92 % (CH); n = 53,798; figure-6f], stroke rates [HR 1.84 (95 % CI: 1.34–2.52); p < 0.001; I2 = 0 % (LH); n = 50,163; figure-6g] and MACE outcomes [OR 2.05 (95 % CI: 1.78–2.35); p < 0.001; I2 = 0 % (LH); n = 21,995] were significantly higher in women as compared to men. The occurrence of heart failure/cardiogenic shock in hospital was higher in women as compared to men, which approached statistical significance [OR 1.32 (95 % CI: 0.97–1.79); p = 0.08; I2 = 78 % (MH); n = 50,163; figure-6h].

Funnel plots were plotted to rule out the presence of publication bias for some of the major outcomes of this systematic review. Publication bias was high risk for in-hospital mortality, in-hospital reinfarction, and in-hospital heart failure/cardiogenic shock (Supplementary Figure-2). Publication bias was low risk for in-hospital stroke (Supplementary Figure-2).

Discussion

Our analysis showed than Indian women with ACS has worse in hospital, short- and long-term cardiovascular outcomes as compared to Indian men. The in-hospital death rates, stroke rates and MACE were significantly higher in women as compared to men. The worse outcomes in women continued even after discharge from hospital. Indian women had significantly higher 30-day and 1-year mortality following an event of ACS, as compared to men. However, 1-year rates of re-infarction, stroke and heart failure were similar in women as compared to men. Hence the cause for this increased 30-day and 1-year mortality in women is not clear and may be due to a more severe coronary artery disease profile to start with. Indian women with ACS had a higher prevalence of diabetes and hypertension, 2 of the most common non-communicable disease worldwide associated with increased risks of ACS as well as worse outcomes. Also, the issue of sudden cardiac death, arrhythmias and non-cardiac causes also need to be looked into.

The cardiac disease profile on Indian women was different to that noted in men. Women more commonly had NSTEMI as compared to men who had STEMI at the time of presentation. No sex differences were noted with regards to misinterpretation of initial symptoms or treatment delay. Baseline mediations in women and men were similar with regards to use of anti-platelet medication and lipid lowering medications. However, women had a higher use of ACEIs/ARBs and beta blockers at baseline as compared to men. This may in part be explained by the higher prevalence of hypertension and diabetes in women as compared to men.

Women has significantly lower chances of undergoing diagnostic coronary angiography and/or therapeutic PCI as compared to men. This is especially concerning as women have a higher background risk profile (hypertension and diabetes) and worse in-hospital, 30-day and 1-year clinical outcomes. This meta-analysis highlights the need for greater vigilance in women with ACS with lower threshold for prompt PCI to improve short- and long-term outcomes in women. It is also concerning to note that the use of anti-platelet medications (aspirin and clopidogrel) as a part of in-hospital treatment was significantly lower in women as compared to men. The reason for this remains to be determined.

Women continued to have lower chances of receiving anti-platelet medications (aspirin and clopidogrel) at the time of discharge from the hospital, as compared to men. Women also had lower chances of receiving beta-blockers, nitrates and statins at the time of discharge as compared to men. This is concerning as all these medications (except nitrates) have been demonstrated to improve cardiovascular outcomes in ACS. Nitrates are primarily used in clinical practice for angina pain relief. Whether the lesser use of nitrates in Indian women with ACS at the time of discharge is due to lesser angina symptoms in women remains to be determined. Also, whether the lower prescription of beta-blockers and statins in women as compared to men contributes to the increased mortality and worse outcomes in women as compared to men remains to be determined.

To conclude, our meta-analysis highlights important sex differences in ACS presentation in Indian women and men. Indian women with ACS have higher background history of hypertension and diabetes, have increased mortality and worse outcomes as compared to men. Indian women are more likely to have NSTEMI, less likely to undergo diagnostic angiography as well as therapeutic PCI, less likely to receive anti-platelet medications and statins during treatment. Our study is a timely reminder to the Indian cardiologists to be more aggressive which managing a woman with ACS. There should be lower threshold for coronary interventions, and more aggressive with medical therapy. Societal factors (education, financial independence, patriarch factors) need to be looked into why Indian women have worse ACS outcomes. It may be said that a greater vigilance with lower threshold of therapeutic interventions and pharmacotherapy is warranted in Indian women with ACS to improve their clinical outcomes.
