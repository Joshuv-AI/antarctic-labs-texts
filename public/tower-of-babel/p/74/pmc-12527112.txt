Longitudinal effects of pregnancy on PTSD symptoms in black female participants: Moderation by sexual trauma and progesterone
Authors: Mariana Rocha, Rebecca Lipschutz, Meghna Ravi, Nicole R Nugent, Emma C Lathan, Shimarith Wallace, Merom Arthur, Sriya Karra, Rebecca Hinrichs, Jennifer S Stevens, Andrea Braden, Suchitra Chandrasekaran, Alicia K Smith, Abigail Powers, Vasiliki Michopoulos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBlack pregnant females experience disproportionately higher rates of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and associated health complications, which may be exacerbated by exposure to sexual trauma. Hormonal fluctuations during pregnancy, including progesterone, may also play a key role in modulating PTSD symptoms. The current study assessed how PTSD symptoms change over pregnancy and how sexual trauma and progesterone independently impact the trajectory of PTSD symptoms in a sample of under-resourced pregnant Black females. Participants (N = 283) completed up to three interviews assessing PTSD symptoms over pregnancy; the enrollment interview assessed pre-pregnancy trauma history. Progesterone concentrations were measured in a subset of participants (N = 91). Results from linear mixed models showed that PTSD symptoms decreased over the course of pregnancy, B= -0.34, p<.001, and that sexual trauma exposure was associated with higher initial PTSD symptoms, B = 12.11, p<.001, and a significant decrease in PTSD symptoms across pregnancy, B = -0.48, p = .002. Individuals with no sexual trauma exposure did not show significant change in PTSD symptoms across pregnancy, B =-0.14, p = .207. This pattern was observed for total PTSD symptoms and all symptom clusters. In the subset of participants with progesterone data, higher progesterone levels, relative to gestational age, was associated with a greater decrease in PTSD symptoms over pregnancy, B =-0.01, p = .049. The current findings indicate that individuals with a history of sexual trauma demonstrate higher levels of PTSD symptoms early in pregnancy and suggest that PTSD symptoms may decrease over pregnancy via increasing progesterone levels.Keywords: PTSD, Progesterone, Pregnancy, Sexual trauma

Introduction

Pregnant Black females have disproportionately high lifetime exposure to traumatic experiences, increasing their risk for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (Seng et al., 2011). This health disparity is especially pronounced in under-resourced pregnant Black females from urban areas (Powers et al., 2020) who are also at heightened risk of pregnancy-related morbidity and mortality (Callaghan, 2012). PTSD may be contributing to pregnancy-related morbidity and mortality in Black pregnant populations and their offspring, as PTSD during pregnancy increases risk for pre-term birth and low infant birth weight (Sanjuan et al., 2021) as well as preeclampsia and gestational diabetes (Shaw et al., 2017). PTSD during pregnancy is also associated with increased risk for psychopathology in offspring (Nielsen et al., 2019). Given the risks stemming from PTSD during pregnancy, a better understanding of how PTSD symptoms change through pregnancy in Black pregnant females may help reduce disparities in pregnancy-related health outcomes, as providers could tailor screening and intervention efforts to high-risk prenatal periods.

Current literature exploring how PTSD symptoms change over pregnancy is limited and results are equivocal. Findings from a cross-sectional study comparing PTSD symptoms in a majority Black and under resourced sample found that pregnant women, relative to non-pregnant women, had higher clinical and psychophysiological hyperarousal (Michopoulos et al., 2015). One study of ethnic- and income-diverse pregnant women found that PTSD symptoms generally decreased over pregnancy (Onoye et al., 2013), while another study of ethnic- and income-diverse pregnant women with a lifetime diagnosis of PTSD found that increases in PTSD symptoms during pregnancy were predicted by anxiety about labor or experiencing a traumatic event during pregnancy (Muzik et al., 2016).

The trajectory of PTSD symptoms over pregnancy may be influenced by having experienced sexual trauma, which has a unique and robust impact on PTSD development and severity. A recent meta-analysis on the effects of sexual trauma found that 41.49 % of individuals met criteria for probable PTSD 12-months following assault (Dworkin et al., 2023). Another meta-analysis found that over one-third (36 %) of individuals who experienced sexual trauma had a lifetime diagnosis of PTSD (Dworkin, 2020). A study involving a large, racially diverse, and transnational population suggests that sexual trauma, above other traumas, is associated with the highest PTSD burden (Liu et al., 2017). Pregnancy may be a particularly vulnerable period for individuals with a history of sexual trauma. Importantly, sexual trauma exposure before a pregnancy is associated with increased risk for PTSD during pregnancy (Wosu et al., 2015). Specific aspects of prenatal medical visits, such as vaginal examinations (Byrne et al., 2017) and lack of control over provider selection (Roller, 2011), are linked to PTSD symptoms of reexperiencing, intrusive thoughts, dissociation and avoidance. Understanding how sexual trauma impacts the trajectory of PTSD symptoms over pregnancy is crucial for identifying intervention points and improving maternal mental health outcomes.

Notably, pregnancy is accompanied by significant hormonal changes that may also influence the trajectory of PTSD symptoms over pregnancy (Ravi et al., 2019). Progesterone increases by two to three-fold over pregnancy (Stites and Siiteri, 1983) to prepare the endometrium for implantation and confer immunoprotective changes that are critical for the maintenance of pregnancy (Druckmann and Druckmann, 2005). Progesterone may play a critical role in perinatal mental health, as its direct metabolite, allopregnanolone, exhibits anxiolytic effects that are utilized as a treatment for postpartum depression (Bitran et al., 1995; Deligiannidis et al., 2021). Specific to PTSD, non-pregnant women with PTSD exhibit lower levels of allopregnanolone in plasma and cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) (Pineles et al., 2018; Rasmusson et al., 2006). Additionally, reductions in progesterone and allopregnanolone are associated with impaired fear extinction and extinction recall (Pineles et al., 2018). Investigating the role of progesterone in changes in PTSD symptoms across gestation may provide insights into the biological mechanisms driving PTSD-associated health disparities in pregnant females exposed to sexual trauma.

Thus, in the current study, we examined changes in PTSD symptoms through pregnancy in a prospective cohort of Black pregnant females with few economic resources. Based on prior findings (Muzik et al., 2016; Onoye et al., 2013), we hypothesized that PTSD symptoms would decrease over the course of pregnancy. Next, we assessed the impact of sexual trauma on initial PTSD symptoms and their trajectory over pregnancy. We hypothesized that experiencing sexual trauma would be associated with greater initial PTSD symptom severity and less decrease in PTSD symptoms over the course of pregnancy. Lastly, we assessed how progesterone levels influenced the trajectory of PTSD symptoms. We hypothesized that higher levels of progesterone would be associated with greater decreases in PTSD symptoms over the course of pregnancy.

Methods

Participants

Pregnant female participants (N = 294) were recruited and enrolled from the obstetrics and gynecological clinic at Grady Memorial Hospital, a publicly funded hospital primarily serving minoritized individuals with few economic resources in downtown Atlanta, Georgia. Participants were enrolled between 2018 and 2022. Trained interns approached potential participants in the obstetrics clinics regarding potential participation. During the COVID-19 pandemic, potential participants were identified via electronic medical records and invited to participate via telephone. Inclusion criteria were: self-identifying as Black or African American, endorsement of trauma exposure, 18–40 years of age, confirmed pregnancy (via prenatal care provider), ability to give informed consent, having a phone number by which they could be contacted, and the ability to speak and understand English. Exclusion criteria were: no endorsement of trauma exposure, active psychosis, hospitalization for mental health reasons within the past month, and acute impairment from drugs or alcohol to the degree that they could not provide informed consent. Written and verbal informed consent was obtained for all participants. All study procedures were approved by the Emory Institutional Review Board and the Grady Hospital Research Oversight Committee.

Study design

The current study was a prospective longitudinal cohort study. Participants were enrolled when they were 8–38 weeks pregnant (N = 294); 61 % of participants enrolled in the first timepoint (8–20 weeks), mean gestational age at enrollment, M(SD) = 20.17(7.03). After enrollment, 11 participants were excluded due to not endorsing any trauma exposure (N = 283). Participants were invited to complete assessments during pregnancy at 8–20 weeks (T1; n = 169), 21–28 weeks (T2; n = 147), and 28–38 weeks (T3; n = 133). At each assessment, participants completed a clinical interview either in person or virtually to assess trauma exposure and PTSD symptoms. Within the overall sample, 44 % of participants completed more than one clinical interview across pregnancy, (75 completed two clinical interviews, 49 completed three clinical interviews),and 160 (56 %) only completed one clinical interview. Participants were also invited to complete blood draws at a separate visit within each assessment window. A subsample of 91 participants completed at least one blood draw during pregnancy (n = 131 blood samples; 34 participants had more than one sample) between 10 and 38 weeks of pregnancy (Mean=28.9 weeks, Median=28.9, SD = 7.4). Participants were compensated $50 for completing these assessments.

Clinical interview measures

Demographic information was collected including measures of maternal age, self-identified race, education, income, and parity. The Traumatic Events Inventory (TEI), a 14-item measure of lifetime history of traumatic events, was administered during each participant’s initial visit (Mekawi et al., 2021). The TEI asks about frequency of sexual abuse and assault experience during childhood (ages 0–13 and 14–17 years of age) and adulthood (17 years and older). Lifetime exposure to sexual trauma at any age was coded as 0 (no exposure) and 1(sexual trauma exposure) for analyses. The PTSD Symptom Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) (Blevins et al., 2015), a 20-item self-report questionnaire based on the DSM-5 symptom criteria for PTSD (range= 0–80), was administered to assess overall and symptom subcluster PTSD scores at each visit. PTSD clusters include 1) re-experiencing, 2) avoidance, 3) negative alterations in cognition and mood, and 4) alterations in arousal/reactivity. The PCL-5 has previously been validated in a minoritized sample similar to the current sample (Mekawi et al., 2022). The Stressful Events Questionnaire II (SEQ-II), a 15-item self-report measure of stressful events (range= 0–15), was also administered to quantify recent stressors within a year of the initial interview date (Smith et al., 2011). The total number of stressors reported for the past year was used.

Blood sampling and progesterone quantification

Venous blood samples were collected in EDTA tubes by trained research staff using standard techniques. EDTA tubes were centrifuged at 4 degrees C and the plasma stored in 500ul aliquots and frozen at –80 degrees C until time of assay. Plasma progesterone concentrations were quantified with liquid chromatography-triple quadrupole tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) at the Emory National Primate Research Center Biomarkers as previously described using a QTRAP®6500 system (AB SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA) (Lalonde et al., 2021). Samples were assayed in duplicates and progesterone levels were determined from a standard curve using linear regression analysis. The lower limit of detection for progesterone was 0.05 ng/mL and the intra-run and inter-run precision was less than 10 %, all samples met these criteria. Progesterone samples were collected within the same assessment window as the clinical interview (average time between clinical interview and blood collection, M(SD) =2.38(1.69) weeks, range = 0–7 weeks). One sample was identified as an outlier and winsorized. Progesterone values were log transformed.

Data analyses

To assess changes in overall PTSD symptoms and PTSD symptom cluster scores over the course of pregnancy, we first conducted unconditional growth curve mixed models, then ran conditional growth curve mixed models to determine the effect of sexual trauma on initial PTSD symptoms and change in PTSD symptoms over pregnancy. In both unconditional and conditional models, time was defined by weeks’ gestation at the respective study visit (calculated using pregnancy due date in each participant’s medical record) with the model intercept set to conception. Conditional models also examined the effect of progesterone on PTSD symptoms over time, measured in weeks gestation. Progesterone was not able to be assessed longitudinally due to a smaller sample size (n = 91), with the majority of samples collected during later gestation (21–38 weeks). Due to expected changes in progesterone levels across pregnancy and to minimize confounding with time, log-transformed progesterone values were residualized on gestational age at blood collection prior to inclusion in growth curve models (Fox et al., 2015; García et al., 2020). Post hoc simple slopes analyses examined conditional associations at the mean and + - 1 SD. Maternal age, education, recent stressors, and parity (1 = no other children; 2 = has other children) were examined as potential covariates if they correlated with PTSD outcomes at p < .05. Only recent stressors and parity were correlated with PTSD outcomes and included as covariates in subsequent models (supplemental table 2). Growth curve models were con-ducted using linear mixed models in Jamovi(2.5) (The Jamovi Project, 2024), an R-based statistical interface (R Development Core Team, 2021). Alpha levels for analyses were set to p < .05.

Power analyses.

A priori power analyses were conducted using a series of MPlus Monte Carlo simulations with 1000 replications and three seeds to confirm model stability. To examine power to detect change in PTSD symptoms over the course of pregnancy and effects on the slope over pregnancy, simulations were repeated assuming a sample of participants retained throughout the study (n = 105) as well as a sample of participants completing at least one assessment (n = 340) using missingness based on prior work. Simulations in samples demonstrated that models were well powered (>.80) to detect even small effects of the mean slope growth factor (as small as.13) or change over time. When adding a predictor to the simulations described, models showed strong power (>.80) to detect small effects of cumulative trauma (.10–.12) on growth processes as well as adequate power (>.80) to detect small effects (.10–.12) of the relationship between PTSD symptoms and hormones. In line with best practices, posthoc power analyses were not conducted based on observed effect sizes (Dziak et al., 2020).

Results

Sample characteristics

Participants (N = 283) were on average 27.16 (5.51) years old, with 46 % of participants reporting a history of sexual trauma. Within each timepoint, 29.8 % met the cut-off for a probable PTSD diagnosis at 8–20 weeks (T1; M = 23.98, SD = 18.90), 27.9 % at 21–28 weeks (T2; M = 21.27, SD = 17.07) and 24.2 % at 28–38 weeks (T3; M = 19.79, SD = 17.38). Mean progesterone levels at T1 were 46.81 (SD = 15.89 n = 33), at T2 (M = 71.23, SD =22.22, n = 46) and at T3 (M = 135.60, SD =46.11, n = 54). Sexual trauma exposure was not associated with progesterone values at any timepoint (ps >.05; see supplemental Table 2). Participants that completed blood draws and were included in the progesterone subsample were demographically similar to the overall sample in terms of education, employment, income, age, level of trauma exposure, PTSD symptoms and gestational age at enrollment (p > .05 for t-test and chi-square analyses). Sexual trauma conditional growth curve models

To examine whether changes in total PTSD symptoms over the course of pregnancy varied by sexual trauma history, conditional models included the interaction between sexual trauma exposure (yes/no) and slope, parameterized by weeks’ gestation (see Table 2). Models also controlled for recent stressors in the past year and parity. The main effect of sexual trauma for PTSD symptoms was significant (B=12.11, p < .001), indicating that participants with a history of sexual trauma had higher levels of PTSD symptoms in early pregnancy. Sexual trauma significantly predicted the slope for PTSD symptoms (B= -0.48, p=.002), such that the trajectory of PTSD symptoms differed by trauma history. Post hoc and simple slopes analyses showed that for participants with lifetime sexual trauma exposure, PTSD symptoms decreased significantly across pregnancy, (B=-0.61, p <.001) (Fig. 1a). In contrast, pregnant females without a history of sexual trauma did not exhibit a significant change in PTSD symptoms across pregnancy (B= -0.14, p =.207), but demonstrated consistent lower symptom levels (Fig. 1a). As shown in Fig. 1a, individuals with sexual trauma exposure exhibit higher initial PTSD symptoms above clinical cutoff, M(SE) =32.99 (1.60), and a subsequent decrease in symptoms across pregnancy to scores below cutoff, M(SE) = 23.71 (1.63).

Progesterone conditional growth curve model

To examine if progesterone was associated with changes in PTSD symptoms over pregnancy, conditional models included the interaction between residual progesterone and weeks of gestation (See Table 3). Models controlled for recent stressors, parity, and sexual trauma exposure. Progesterone significantly moderated the trajectory of PTSD symptoms (B = -0.01, p = .049), such that higher than expected levels of progesterone (relative to gestational age) were associated with more negative slope in PTSD symptoms. Post-hoc simple slopes analyses showed that individuals with higher levels of progesterone (relative to gestational age) experienced steeper declines in PTSD symptoms over the course of pregnancy (ps< .01; Fig. 2). Individuals with lower levels of progesterone (relative to gestational age) did not show changes in PTSD symptoms over pregnancy (p = .700).

Discussion

In the current study, we examined changes in PTSD symptoms over the course of pregnancy and assessed the effects of sexual trauma and progesterone on PTSD symptoms across pregnancy in a high-risk sample of Black females. We found that total PTSD symptoms and all symptom clusters generally decreased across pregnancy in our sample. Individuals with lifetime sexual trauma had higher initial PTSD symptoms early on in pregnancy and showed a greater decrease in symptoms over the course of gestation. Additionally, higher levels of progesterone relative to gestational age, independent of sexual trauma, were related to a greater decrease in PTSD symptoms over pregnancy. These results suggest that later stages of pregnancy may exert protective effects on the mechanisms contributing to PTSD symptoms in females with sexual trauma histories.

Our results are consistent with previous studies showing higher initial PTSD symptoms, followed by a decrease in symptoms over the course of pregnancy (Muzik et al., 2016; Onoye et al., 2013). We extend these previous findings by showing that decreases in PTSD symptoms occur across all clusters of symptoms (re-experiencing, avoidance, negative cognitions and mood, and alterations in arousal/reactivity). Notably, both our study and that of Muzik et al. (2016), observe a pattern of high initial PTSD symptoms and decreasing PTSD across pregnancy for minoritized women who report high levels of trauma exposure and psychosocial risk factors, characteristics that are generally associated with higher risk for PTSD (Muzik et al., 2016). Altogether, later stages of pregnancy may have a protective effect on PTSD symptoms, especially for Black pregnant females, who are at increased risk for PTSD (Seng et al., 2011).

Our findings corroborate previous studies establishing that exposure to sexual trauma is associated with higher risk for PTSD (Dworkin, 2020) and highlight sexual trauma as a particularly potent risk factor for increased PTSD prevalence and symptom severity (Wosu et al., 2015). Exposure to sexual trauma contributes to dysregulation of the hypothalamic pituitary adrenal axis and increased inflammation, which can contribute to lifetime PTSD symptoms (Östlund et al., 2003; Ravi et al., 2021). Although experiences of sexual trauma across the lifespan may be associated with markers of increased glucocorticoid release (i.e. cortisol) (De Bellis et al., 2011), a meta-analysis found associations between PTSD and lower cortisol levels, particularly in studies of female survivors of sexual trauma (Meewisse et al., 2007). Sexual trauma may have distinct physiological effects compared to other types of trauma because survivors of sexual trauma face high levels of skepticism and victim-blaming (Rowland et al., 2022). This additional burden of social invalidation and stigma may further exacerbate PTSD symptoms and complicate recovery. Additionally, survivors of sexual trauma are at increased risk for revictimization (Walker et al., 2019), which may contribute to persistence and severity of PTSD symptoms over their lifetime. Our study shows that individuals with lifetime sexual trauma experience more severe PTSD symptoms at the start of pregnancy, but also demonstrate a decrease in PTSD symptoms as pregnancy progresses into later stages. It is possible that, over time, pregnancy has a protective effect that may result in greater reductions in PTSD symptoms in individuals with higher initial PTSD symptom burden.

The protective effects of later gestation on PTSD symptoms, especially for pregnant females with sexual trauma histories, may be driven by significant increases in progesterone levels over gestation (Soldin et al., 2005), which influence neurocircuitry involved in fear regulation and can contribute to changes in PTSD symptoms (Frye et al., 2025; Pang et al., 2013). Our findings that higher levels of progesterone, relative to gestational age, are related to greater decrease in PTSD symptoms align with previous research demonstrating the efficacy of progesterone’s metabolite, allopregnanolone in treating postpartum depression (Bitran et al., 1995; Deligiannidis et al., 2021). This mechanism may hold relevance for treating PTSD in pregnant females with sexual trauma histories, highlighting progesterone’s role in modulating PTSD symptom trajectories during pregnancy and extending existing evidence of progesterone’s influence on perinatal mental health.

The current sample of pregnant Black females with few economic resources demonstrated high rates of probable PTSD diagnosis (24.2–29.8 %) and sexual trauma histories (46.1 %), much higher than rates of PTSD in both perinatal and general samples of women (6–8 % and 4–5 % respectively) and sexual trauma prevalence worldwide for females (29 %; Li et al., 2023; Pereda et al., 2009). This suggests that the current sample of pregnant Black individuals with few economic resources are disproportionately impacted by sexual trauma and PTSD. Notably, the change in PTSD symptoms for females with sexual trauma exposure was clinically significant in this study, as average scores decreased from above to below clinical cutoff for probable PTSD diagnosis across pregnancy. As we did not assess PTSD symptoms prior to pregnancy, we do not know if participants had higher levels of PTSD prior to pregnancy that maintained into the first pregnancy assessment timepoint or if they showed an increase in symptoms during the first trimester. Growing evidence has shown that some aspects of pregnancy may increase vulnerability to PTSD symptoms, particularly for those who have experienced sexual trauma (Sanchez et al., 2017). In the context of pregnancy, sexual trauma may be a poignant stressor due to the physical or somatic changes, nature of physical exams by health care providers, or loss of autonomy or control during procedures, all of which, may trigger more re-experiencing or arousal symptoms of PTSD (Leeners et al., 2006) at the earlier stages of pregnancy. While exposure to these potentially distressing reminders may be associated with higher levels of PTSD symptoms at the beginning of pregnancy, repeated exposure may contribute to habituation and a decrease in symptoms by the end of pregnancy. Additionally, as our sample was primarily minoritized females with fewer economic resources, pregnancy may be associated with increased access to social services and resources. Gaining access to related services, such as supplemental nutrition programs (e.g. WIC, SNAP), can help offset the stress associated with food insecurity and reduce the likelihood of stress-related psychopathology (Munger et al., 2016).

Screening and prevention efforts for sexual trauma should therefore give special consideration to these minoritized and under-resourced communities, who face higher levels of PTSD and PTSD-associated maternal morbidities and mortalities (Rocha et al., 2024). Further, evidence of intergenerational transmission of stress suggests that maternal exposure to trauma and subsequent PTSD may have negative consequences for development of offspring in utero and postpartum (Buss et al., 2017; Van den Bergh et al., 2020). Preventing symptom exacerbation through screening and referral to appropriate resources may help ensure individuals at highest risk for adverse pregnancy and birth outcomes receive the full support and care they need during this vulnerable period, ultimately contributing to the reduction of Black pregnancy-related health disparities. Pregnant females typically have increased contact with the healthcare system, making it an ideal opportunity for both sexual trauma history and PTSD symptom screening. Together, these results support the importance of trauma-informed maternity care that includes screening for sexual trauma and PTSD, providing appropriate resources or referrals, and ensuring the prevention of re-traumatization or new trauma during pregnancy and childbirth (Lathan et al., 2023).

Limitations and future directions

Our study focused on Black pregnant females, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to the general prenatal population. However, Black pregnant females face disproportionate risks for trauma, PTSD, and associated pregnancy-related health complications (Rocha et al., 2024), making it imperative for studies to prioritize this population. A key limitation is that this study collected data on biological sex but not gender identity. Future research should examine how the experiences of gender-diverse and trans pregnant individuals differ to those of cisgender women, and how these differences impact PTSD symptom trajectories during pregnancy. PTSD symptoms were assessed using the PCL-5 rather than the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale DSM-5 (CAPS-5) (Weathers et al., 2001), the gold-standard in PTSD assessment. The PCL-5, however, has been shown to produce similar estimates of PTSD symptom change as compared to the CAPS-5 (Lee et al., 2022) and may be a more accessible measure for socioeconomically disadvantaged individuals of color without access to mental health care. Another limitation is the absence of pre-pregnancy PTSD assessments, which make it difficult to discern how initial levels of symptoms in pregnancy compare to preconception, highlighting the need for longitudinal research across preconception, pregnancy and postpartum periods. Importantly, this study did not collect data on sexual trauma during pregnancy and whether the pregnancy at hand resulted from sexual assault. In the United States one in 20 individuals experiences a pregnancy from sexual assault in their lifetime (D’Angelo et al., 2024) and 3–9 % of individuals experience abuse, including sexual abuse during pregnancy (Alhusen et al., 2015). Given these prevalence rates, it is important for future studies to consider these factors, as they may profoundly impact PTSD trajectories over pregnancy. While we examined the impact of sexual trauma, and sexual trauma is particularly salient during the perinatal period, the findings may not be specific to this form of trauma. Future work should examine the impact of overall trauma exposure or different types of trauma exposure on PTSD symptoms in pregnancy. A potential alternative explanation for the observed PTSD symptom reduction in participants with sexual trauma histories is regression to the mean, given their higher initial PTSD symptoms. However, the majority of participants with sexual trauma exposure in this sample had repeated exposures across multiple age timepoints, making it less likely that the observed symptom trajectories are due to a natural course of recovery and more likely that pregnancy-specific mechanisms may be contributing to this decrease. Another possibility is that pregnancy may lead to trauma-related distress in early pregnancy in participants with sexual trauma that resolves over the gestational period. This interpretation is supported by prior research showing a 5.33-fold increased risk of PTSD in early pregnancy among women with a history of childhood sexual abuse (Sanchez et al., 2017). Moreover, although study recruitment was conducted both in person and via telephone regardless of prenatal visit attendance, there may be some recruitment bias as participants were somewhat engaged in seeking care and individuals with higher levels of symptoms and life stress may not seek care. Finally, progesterone data was available for only a subset of participants, with most samples collected during late gestation, limiting statistical power to assess whether sexual trauma interacted with progesterone to influence initial PTSD symptoms and PTSD symptom trajectories over pregnancy.

Pregnancy is associated with significant neuroendocrine and immune changes that may contribute to a reduction in PTSD symptoms across pregnancy, not all of which we were able to assess. A growing body of research suggests that pregnancy is accompanied by attenuated physiological and psychological stress reactivity across gestation, with pregnant females exhibiting blunted cortisol reactivity and reporting lower subjective stress in the third trimester as compared to the first trimester (De Weerth and Buitelaar, 2005; Entringer et al., 2010). Assessing cortisol changes as a marker of stress physiology may be valuable in future studies, as reduction in stress reactivity may contribute to improvement of PTSD symptoms during later pregnancy. This consideration is especially relevant in the context of the present sample, as exposure to higher levels of trauma and chronic PTSD symptoms has been associated with lower cortisol levels during pregnancy (Swales et al., 2018). Beyond cortisol, it is also important to consider the impact of allopregnanolone, a metabolite of progesterone with demonstrated positive effects in mental health symptoms (Bitran et al., 1995; Deligiannidis et al., 2021), as well as estradiol (E2), which increases significantly over pregnancy and can impact the neurocircuitry underlying PTSD symptoms such as fear regulation (Ravi et al., 2019). Additionally, pregnancy instills a decrease in systemic inflammation, which may counteract PTSD-related increases in inflammatory cytokines (Ravi et al., 2022). Altogether, a more comprehensive understanding of the complex relationship between neuroendocrine steroid hormones, immune factors and stress reactivity during pregnancy is needed to understand the neurobiological and psychophysiological mechanisms underlying PTSD symptom changes in pregnant females.

Conclusion

The current results are novel in demonstrating decreases in both total and cluster-level PTSD symptoms across pregnancy, and in identifying how sexual trauma and progesterone influence this trajectory. The present study focused on under resourced Black pregnant females, a population that faces high levels of PTSD and associated pregnancy-related health outcomes. The current study supports and extends prior findings demonstrating a general decrease in PTSD symptoms over pregnancy, by showing that individuals who have experienced sexual trauma have higher initial PTSD symptoms and exhibit a decrease in symptoms over the course of pregnancy, while those without a history of sexual trauma do not see a decrease in PTSD symptoms and display lower initial PTSD symptoms. Furthermore, higher levels of progesterone, relative to gestational age, were associated with a greater decrease in PTSD symptoms, suggesting that pregnancy-related neuroendocrine changes may contribute to symptom improvement in this high-risk population. Future longitudinal studies should investigate risk factors and underlying biological mechanisms, such as hormonal pathways like progesterone metabolism, that may shape PTSD symptom trajectories across the perinatal period and impact fetal development.
