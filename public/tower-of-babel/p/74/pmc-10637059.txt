High‐dose rate endorectal brachytherapy for rectal cancer: A state‐of‐the‐art review
Authors: Tian Cheng, Ran Peng, Ang Qu, Hao Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractRectal cancer is a common malignancy that requires multidisciplinary treatment. By utilizing the dose–response relationship in rectal cancer radiotherapy, increasing the radiotherapy dose can improve clinical complete remission rates. High‐dose rate endorectal brachytherapy (HDREBT) is a novel technique that delivers high doses of radiotherapy directly to the tumor via an endorectal applicator, sparing the adjacent normal tissues from excessive radiation exposure. HDREBT includes contact X‐ray brachytherapy and high‐dose‐rate intracavitary brachytherapy. We introduce the latest developments in applicators and imaging techniques for HDREBT in rectal cancer and summarize the current evidence on the efficacy, safety, and feasibility of HDREBT as a neoadjuvant, definitive, or palliative treatment option for all stages of rectal cancer patients. We also discuss the potential advantages and challenges of HDREBT in achieving organ preservation and improving the quality of life of rectal cancer patients. HDREBT has shown promising results in achieving high complete response rates, enabling nonoperative management, improving organ preservation rates, and providing effective palliation in rectal cancer patients. More studies are needed to optimize its dose and fractionation schemes in different clinical scenarios.Keywords: brachytherapy, endorectal, high‐dose rate, rectal cancer, review

BACKGROUND

Rectal cancer is a common malignancy worldwide. 1 Radical surgery for low rectal cancer that preserves the anal sphincter is challenging and can reduce the patient's quality of life. Preoperative chemoradiotherapy (CRT), followed by total mesorectal excision (TME), is the preferred treatment modality for rectal adenocarcinoma. Neoadjuvant CRT is usually delivered with 50–50.4 Gy in 25–28 fractions, plus capecitabine or fluorouracil during treatment. Preoperative CRT can improve local control (LC) and overall survival (OS) while reducing the local recurrence rate. Pathological complete response (pCR) can be achieved in 5%–30% of rectal cancer patients who receive preoperative radiotherapy, and low rectal cancer patients with pCR may have the opportunity to delay surgery or avoid abdominoperineal excision through a “watch‐and‐wait” strategy to improve their quality of life. 2

There is a dose–response relationship in rectal cancer radiotherapy. 3 Increasing the dose can improve the clinical complete response rate, which may reduce the risk of local recurrence 4 and increase the possibility of sphincter preservation surgery for patients who would otherwise need a stoma. However, this usually results in higher doses to the surrounding tissues and an increased risk of side effects. high‐dose rate endorectal brachytherapy (HDREBT) has an advantage in dose escalation of radiation therapy. Compared with external beam radiotherapy (EBRT), HDREBT delivers a higher dose rate of radiation with a quicker dose fall‐off around the tumor target. This allows higher dose penetration and improved dose coverage, 5 while protecting the surrounding organs that may be damaged by radiation exposure (organs at risk, OARs), such as the small bowel, colon, bladder, femur, and perineum, and reducing toxic side effects. 6 HDREBT is performed by applicators, and the radiation dose acts directly to the tumor. A much higher radiation dose can be delivered, which can reduce the dependence on chemotherapy for radiosensitization. According to the different radiation energies, HDREBT can be divided into contact X‐ray brachytherapy (CXB), which uses low‐energy X‐rays, and high‐dose‐rate intracavitary brachytherapy (HDR‐ICBT), which uses high‐energy γ‐rays or electron beams.

High‐dose rate endorectal brachytherapy has been initially employed for palliative or adjuvant treatment of rectal malignancies. 7 Subsequent studies have reported its advantages in achieving pCR as a neoadjuvant monotherapy in the early stage 8 or as a boost to EBRT in locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC). 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 HDREBT is now widely applied across various stages of rectal cancer and has shown promising results in achieving high complete response rates. HDREBT also has the potential to enable nonoperative management and improve organ preservation rates in selected cases. In this review, we introduce the latest developments in applicators and imaging techniques for HDREBT in rectal cancer. We then summarize the current evidence on the efficacy, safety, and feasibility of HDREBT as a neoadjuvant, definitive, or palliative treatment option for rectal cancer patients. Lastly, we discuss the potential advantages and challenges of HDREBT in achieving organ preservation and improving quality of life for rectal cancer patients (Figure 1).

ADVANCES IN HDREBT: TECHNICAL ASPECTS

Brachytherapy applicators are essential tools for delivering endoluminal radiotherapy, enabling precise placement of radiation sources within the tumor site for targeted treatment, while minimizing damage to surrounding OARs. 18 A variety of applicator designs have been developed to accommodate different tumor types, locations, shapes, and dose rates (Figure 2). For example, Bellezzo et al. 19 designed the MAASTRO applicator for CXB, which is a cylindrical multichannel device with lateral shielding and angled edges to increase contact with the tumor. Poon et al. 20 designed a flexible applicator made of silicone rubber for HDR‐ICBT, which conforms to the anatomical curvature of the rectum, thus reaching cavities with deep‐seated tumors.

Some applicators incorporate dose shielding to further reduce the dose to OARs and enhance radiotherapy outcomes. Devic et al. 21 successfully reduced the dose to OARs by over 60% using tungsten shielding rods and employed a double‐balloon technique to improve dose conformity to the target. Unlike previous shielding designs, the tungsten shielding rods are located at the center of the applicator, with radiation source particles entering and exiting through surrounding channels. In prostate cancer high‐dose rate brachytherapy, applicators with hyperthermia functions have appeared, which include ThermoBrachy designed by Androulakis et al. 22 ThermoBrachy enables simultaneous interstitial hyperthermia and highly localized high‐dose rate brachytherapy, which can significantly enhance cell killing effects. It has been proven that the combination of regional hyperthermia and neoadjuvant CRT may lead to an increased pCR rate in LARC, 23 but there is no report on hyperthermia applicators for rectal cancer so far.

The steep dose distribution inherent to HDREBT in the vicinity of the tumor renders it susceptible to significant dose discrepancies resulting from minor positional errors. Consequently, accurate simulation and localization are of paramount importance for HDREBT. Among the imaging techniques commonly employed, the limited contrast afforded by computed tomography (CT) in soft tissue imaging impedes its capacity for precise delineation of rectal tumor target volumes. 24 Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), while capable of more accurately defining smaller tumor contours, necessitates substantial equipment investment and is constrained by the limited availability of MRI‐compatible applicators. 25 The integration of CT and MRI can mitigate their respective limitations; however, this requires more imaging and patient transfer, with an attendant risk of image misalignment. 26

A recent development in imaging techniques for HDREBT is ultra‐short echo time magnetic resonance (UTE MR), which combines the advantages of CT and MRI while avoiding their drawbacks. UTE MR can achieve clear images of both the applicator channels and radiation sources, as well as the rectal tumor target volumes and surrounding tissues. 27 It can also reduce the reconstruction errors and dose errors of conventional MRI by using efficient MRI distortion assessment correction algorithms and image alignment procedures. 28 Thus, UTE MR is an upgraded technology in MRI‐guided brachytherapy that improves the accuracy of rectal soft‐tissue observation.

CLINICAL APPLICATION OF HDREBT IN RECTAL CANCER

For early‐stage rectal cancer: organ preservation strategies

Over the past 20 years, the introduction of TME and neoadjuvant CRT has dramatically reduced the local recurrence rate of early‐stage rectal cancer from 25%–30% to less than 5% and increased the rate of organ preservation. 4 The substantial proportion (5%–30%) of patients who achieved pCR after neoadjuvant CRT has led to the emergence of the organ preservation strategy “watch‐and‐wait” management. 29 Habr‐Gama et al. 2 treated 265 patients with resectable adenocarcinoma of the distal rectum (0–7 cm from anal verge) with CRT, and 71 patients (26.8%) achieved complete clinical response (cCR). However, the local recurrence after the “watch‐and‐wait” strategy may raise some concerns. To address this issue, Habr‐Gama et al. 30 provided long‐term follow‐up data showing that all local recurrences were salvageable by transanal excision (pT1), and that there was no negative impact on the survival of cCR patients after CRT in a cohort of patients bound to have a permanent stoma.

TME surgery is the gold standard for early‐stage rectal cancer treatment, but for frail elderly patients (over 85 years old) their mortality rate at 6 months after radical resection of rectal cancer is 30%. 31 An analytical model by Smith et al. 32 demonstrated that using organ preservation methods for healthy 80‐year‐old elderly people can increase the survival rate by 10% after 1 year, therefore avoiding radical surgery can improve the early survival rate of elderly rectal cancer patients, and choosing endorectal brachytherapy that can control local disease provides a nonoperative management alternative for elderly patients who cannot tolerate surgery, patients who strongly wish to preserve the anus, and patients who are unwilling to have a stoma. 33

CXB combined with EBRT is becoming an effective organ preservation treatment strategy for early‐stage rectal cancer (Table 1). 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 Gerard et al. 34 conducted a recent randomized controlled trial that assigned 148 patients with early‐stage rectal adenocarcinoma to either a standard neoadjuvant CRT group or a CXB dose escalation group, with a median follow‐up of 38.2 months. The 3‐year organ preservation rate was 59% (95% confidence interval 48–72) in the standard neoadjuvant CRT group and 81% (72–91) in the CXB group, demonstrating that adding CXB to neoadjuvant CRT significantly improved the 3‐year organ preservation rate for early‐stage rectal cancer patients. Sun Myint et al. 36 reviewed the efficacy of CXB dose escalation for 83 patients who had ≤3 cm residual tumors after EBRT. Most patients received a total of 90 Gy of CXB in three fractions within 4 weeks. Of these 83 patients, 53 (63.8%) achieved cCR. This suggests that CXB boost can be used as an alternative to radical surgery for patients who have suspicious residual lesions after EBRT.

The strengths and weaknesses of the dose characteristics of CXB are very clear: Since the low‐energy X‐rays at 50 kV have limited tissue penetration, with doses at 1 cm being only 10% of the surface and at 2 cm only 1% of the surface, each therapeutic dose of CXB can only be applied to the surface of the tumor. However, because of this, CXB is able to deliver very large doses of radiation to a defined area of the tumor with little collateral damage to the surrounding tissue. 40 CXB therefore has the unique advantage of treating early tumors or residual tumors, and is able to increase the cCR rate through dose escalation, enabling a “watch‐and‐wait” strategy that avoids surgery. 41 CXB may be suitable for patients who are ineligible for radical surgery due to factors such as adverse medical conditions and advanced age, especially when the tumor is located at the anorectal junction edge.

For LARC: boost HDR‐ICBT

The standard treatment for LARC is CRT followed by surgery according to TME principles after 6–8 weeks. 42 For operable LARC patients, using HDR‐ICBT as a boost to the EBRT dose to achieve a better response rate is a widely used approach. Previous studies have shown that dose escalation with HDR‐ICBT results in higher complete response rates 10 , 12 , 14 , 15 and improved downstaging, 10 , 11 with no significant increase in toxicity or surgical complications 9 , 10 , 12 , 14 compared to neoadjuvant CRT alone, and that most of the adverse effects could be controlled medically (Table 2). 10 , 11 The T downstaging may be closely associated with improved sphincter retention, 10 , 16 , 17 which would significantly improve patients' quality of life.

There have been attempts to use HDR‐ICBT alone as a complete replacement for EBRT as preoperative neoadjuvant radiotherapy in patients with low depth of rectal mesenteric infiltration, and it was found that using HDR‐ICBT alone has achieved comparable pCR rates and superior T downstaging 43 , 44 to conventional EBRT with a favorable toxicity pattern. 45 In addition, neoadjuvant HDR‐ICBT performed preoperatively significantly reduced intraoperative bleeding compared with direct surgery. 46 However, one concern with treating rectal tumors with HDR‐ICBT rather than conventional EBRT is the lack of sterilization of the pelvic lymph nodes due to the rapidly decreasing dose of HDR‐ICBT, which covers only the rectal mesenteric lymph nodes and provides little to no coverage of the pelvic lymph nodes. It is therefore conceivable that HDR‐ICBT may lead to higher rates of lymph node metastasis and local recurrence. Careful patient selection with negative pelvic lymph nodes may be crucial for the successful implementation of HDR‐ICBT as a replacement for EBRT.

As previously discussed, older, frail patients with LARC rectal cancer often do not tolerate surgery. Enhanced‐dose radiotherapy with HDR‐ICBT is an attractive option for this frail patient population, and it has been demonstrated that this strategy can lead to a high overall response rate 47 and improved local progression‐free survival in patients with complete response (Table 3). Garant et al. 48 evaluated the efficacy of HDR‐ICBT as a boost after EBRT for 94 rectal cancer patients (cT1‐4, cN0/+) medically unfit for surgery. The cCR rate was 86.2%, which supports a strong dose–response relationship. The 3‐year LC rate was 67.1% and the local recurrence rate was 21.9%. Long‐term toxicity (≥grade 2) occurred in 18 patients (19.2%).

The low incidence of severe late toxicity observed in almost all studies confirms the safety of the EBRT in combination with HDR‐ICBT strategy in older and frail LARC patients. 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 However, Rijkmans et al. 52 , 53 reported a high rate of late toxicity, which they attributed to the use of anticoagulants by 65% of their patients. Anticoagulants might increase the risk of severe rectal bleeding, but another possible explanation for the high toxicity rate is that the study allowed the 100% isodose line to reach a depth of 2 cm, which is too deep and toxic. This highlights the importance of properly defining the clinical target volume and dose prescription depth for rectal HDR brachytherapy, as these factors can significantly affect the outcome and toxicity of this treatment modality. Overall, EBRT combined with HDR‐ICBT is feasible and well tolerated in older and frail LARC patients; however, attention must be paid to its interactions with drugs and dose optimization. This treatment strategy can be an integral part of a highly personalized and elective nonoperative management for patients who are not suitable for or refuse surgery.

For RRC: promising re‐irradiation and effective palliative care

Although neoadjuvant CRT followed by TME has improved the prognosis of LARC patients, 4%–8% of patients still face postoperative tumor recurrence. 54 In recurrent rectal cancer (RRC), just as in LARC, neoadjuvant CRT and radical surgery remain the key treatments. Neoadjuvant CRT could be used to downstage and downsize the tumor, resulting in a better chance of an R0 resection. However, the reirradiation of RRC patients is controversial, as it may cause greater damage to the already impaired normal tissues, or even endanger life. Dijkstra et al. 55 attempted to reirradiate 35 RRC patients with lower doses (30–30.4 Gy) and compared their toxicity and oncological outcomes with 25 RRC patients who received long‐course CRT. There were no significant differences in OS, disease‐free survival, and local recurrence‐free survival between the groups. No differences were observed in late toxicity. These studies indicate that it is safe and feasible to reirradiate RRC patients with moderate doses.

The dose of reirradiation is limited by the risk of potential normal tissue complications. 56 A reirradiation approach employing EBT would theoretically result in significantly less damage to previously irradiated normal tissues while potentially improving LC by permitting dose escalation to the tumor. HDR‐ICBT is ideally suited for these patients because of the rapid dose falloff away from the endorectal applicator (Table 4). 57 , 58 Chuong et al. 57 replaced EBRT with HDR‐ICBT (26 Gy/4 fr.) for pre‐surgical reirradiation of 10 rectal cancer patients who had received prior pelvic irradiation. The reported pCR rate was 28.6%, comparable to previously published EBRT reirradiation data. 59 , 60 Additionally, none of the HDR‐ICBT patients had grade 3 or above toxicity, whereas up to 35% grade 3 toxicity was observed in the EBRT series. 60 Balyasnikova et al. 61 suggested that RRC patients who received HDR‐ICBT without EBRT as neoadjuvant treatment could safely receive full‐dose CRT, which may be an advantage of using HDR‐ICBT alone as neoadjuvant treatment for LARC patients.

HDR‐ICBT also plays a palliative role for incurable patients, 7 who can benefit from local treatment to relieve pain and bleeding at the end stage of the disease and potentially achieve a turnaround. Corner et al. 51 showed that palliative HDR‐ICBT achieved high rates of complete response (>50%) for mucus secretion, diarrhea, and pain. Hoskin et al. 47 evaluated 22 advanced disease patients treated with palliative HDR‐ICBT, and 64% patients experienced relief of bleeding symptoms. The median survival was 6 months. Overall, recent research has shown that HDR‐ICBT is a promising reirradiation treatment and a suitable palliative treatment for RRC, with good LC and tolerable toxicity (Table 5).

CONCLUSIONS

The originality of this review lies in its integration of HDREBT applications in different clinical scenarios for rectal cancer treatment, including neoadjuvant, definitive, or palliative treatment. It also discusses the potential of HDREBT in achieving organ preservation and improving the quality of life of rectal cancer patients. These aspects are important issues and challenges in current rectal cancer treatment. In an era where nonoperative management is becoming increasingly important for patients, HDREBT may become an essential component of rectal cancer treatment.

However, HDREBT faces some challenges that need to be addressed before its widespread clinical implementation and acceptance. More studies are needed to compare HDREBT with other radiotherapy modalities and optimize its dose and fractionation schemes in different clinical scenarios. Furthermore, its safety and efficacy as a reirradiation modality for patients who have received prior pelvic irradiation require more evidence. Finally, more studies are required to evaluate the impact of HDREBT on patient‐reported outcomes and quality‐of‐life measures in rectal cancer patients. This review highlights the potential of HDREBT as a novel and versatile technique for rectal cancer treatment that may offer significant benefits for patients and clinicians alike.
