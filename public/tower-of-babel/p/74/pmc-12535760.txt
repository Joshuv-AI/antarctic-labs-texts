Adult NREM parasomnia and its health, social, and economic associations: a national matched-control study
Authors: Sine Arvedsen, Michael Ibsen, Rune Frandsen, Poul Jørgen Jennum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesThis study investigates the association between non-rapid eye movement parasomnias (NREMp) and health and socioeconomic outcomes in adults.MethodsWe estimated health and socioeconomic consequences of NREMp in the 7 and 4 years preceding diagnosis, respectively, and 2 years following diagnosis. The retrospective cohort included 159 Danish adults diagnosed with NREMp after age 18 years compared with 636 controls (matched for age, gender, marital status, and residential municipality at index year). Diagnoses, healthcare costs, and socioeconomic data were obtained from Danish nationwide health and administrative registers.ResultsPatients with NREMp had significantly more healthcare contacts and higher prevalence of comorbidity across several major disease groups: certain infectious and parasitic diseases; mental and behavioral disorders; diseases of the nervous system; diseases of the eye and adnexa; diseases of the digestive system; diseases of the respiratory system; diseases of the genitourinary system; and symptoms, signs, and abnormal clinical and laboratory findings, injury, poisoning, and external causes. Their healthcare costs were also higher than those of the matched controls. However, no significant differences in mortality were observed during the 2-year post-diagnosis follow-up. In addition, educational attainment and employment rates were comparable with the matched-control population.ConclusionAdults diagnosed with NREMp showed increased comorbidity and healthcare utilization in the years preceding diagnosis compared to matched controls, without corresponding differences in education or employment. Statement of SignificanceThis study provides novel insight into the health, social, and economic patterns in the 7 years preceding and 2 years following a diagnosis of non-rapid eye movement parasomnias (NREMp). The findings indicate that individuals later diagnosed with NREMp had a significantly higher prevalence of comorbidities across a broad range of disease categories, while no substantial differences were observed in mortality rates, education, employment, or income compared to controls. These results contribute to a better understanding of NREMp while the condition is linked to increased healthcare utilization and comorbid disease burden, it may not be associated with adverse outcomes in educational attainment or employment. Keywords: non-REM parasomnia, societal, health, socioeconomic, burden, comorbidity, mortality

Introduction

Non-rapid eye movement sleep parasomnias (NREMp) are disorders that arise from the NREM sleep state. They primarily occur during N3, the deepest stage of NREM sleep. The transition between sleep stages can also initiate the events especially when shifting from deep sleep to lighter stages or wakefulness. During this stage, brain regions are not synchronized, leaving the individual neither fully awake nor completely in deep sleep [1–3]. This sleep stage dissociation gives rise to a variety of complex motor and emotional behaviors, which the person has no or only weak memory of in the morning. The disorder is relatively common in childhood, with a lifetime prevalence of 18.5% during childhood [4], but for some individuals, NREMp persist into adulthood or first appear later in life.

In children, NREMp are linked to the immaturity of the central nervous system, which regulates sleep–wake cycles and sleep-phase interactions. These behaviors are usually benign and tend to resolve as the central nervous system matures. In adults, NREMp encompass similar behaviors such as sleepwalking, night terrors, and confusional arousals, along with additional conditions like sexsomnia and sleep-related eating disorders, which are rare in children [5].

The prevalence of NREMp in adults remains unclear due to the limited number of registry and population-based studies. The International Classification of Sleep Disorders estimates the prevalence of NREMp in adults to be between 1.5% and 4.2% [4, 6, 7]. Although generally considered benign in adults, there is growing awareness that NREM parasomnias can contribute to excessive daytime sleepiness, impaired mental health, and pose potential risks for harmful behaviors not only to the individual but also to those nearby [8, 9]. Proper identification of NREMp is crucial because its symptoms can overlap with other medical conditions, such as epilepsy, which require different treatments [10, 11].

The sleep disruption caused by NREMp, along with the associated stress and anxiety, can be severe and cause daytime sleepiness. This, in turn, affects cognitive functions such as attention, memory, and decision-making, which are crucial for academic and professional performance, potentially affecting education and employment opportunities [2, 8].

While the symptomatology and etiology of NREMp are well documented in the literature and we have some understanding of their frequently associated comorbidities, such as epilepsy, insomnia, obstructive sleep apnea, rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder, periodic limb movement disorder, and restless legs syndrome [5, 12–14], very little is known about health aspects preceding and following the diagnosis, and the associated socioeconomic burden of the disorder. In fact, we found no nationwide register or cohort studies addressing this issue. Consequently, this study aims to systematically evaluate the health and socioeconomic burden of NREMp in adults, viewed from both individual and societal perspectives.

Methods

Study design

This is an entirely register-based study that uses data from the extensive and well-organized Danish national registries. Data are collected from the Civil Registration System (CRS), the Danish National Patient Register (DNPR), the National Health Insurance Service, the Danish National Prescription Register (NPR), and various Statistics Denmark (DST) registers, which contain longitudinal sociodemographic information about, for instance, education, income, public transfers, employment status, immigration or emigration, and death. The registers use a unique personal identification number (PIN) that has been assigned to every citizen in Denmark since 1968. This PIN allows individual-level linkage across all registers, because it is used for registration in all health registers. It has been validated and is considered a vital tool in Danish epidemiological research.

The dates from which historical data are available vary among the Danish national registries. All data are available from 1995 to 2021, except for those concerning diagnosis-related group (DRG) healthcare tariffs, with which we calculate healthcare costs. These data are available from 2002 to 2020.

Study population

We identified all patients with NREMp in the DNPR with a hospital contact with a either a primary diagnosis—the main condition for which the patient was examined or treated—or secondary diagnosis—referring to additional conditions coexisting with the primary diagnosis—during the period from January 1, 2006 to December 31, 2018 (Figure 1). In Denmark, the diagnoses of NREMp (DG 47.5B) and REM parasomnia (DG 47.5A) rely on clinical history, interview, and video-PSG recording conducted in a hospital setting either during an overnight stay (inpatient) or at home while still being monitored by the hospital (outpatient).

Design of NREM sleep disorder study. Data capture period 1994-2021 (cost data available 2002-2020).

Patients were included the first time they had a hospital contact with a primary or secondary NREMp diagnosis (DG 47.5B, excluding REM parasomnia), registered in the DNPR (index date) during the period January 1, 2006 to December 31, 2018 (Figure 1). In the DNPR, ICD-10 diagnosis data are available from 1994, so we can be certain that all included patients with NREMp were NREMp-naive when they were included. Data were available until December 31, 2020, which thereby allows a minimum 2-year follow-up period after each patient’s index date.

For the comorbidity analysis, all patients were followed for a period of 7 years up to their index date. In the mortality analysis, patients were followed from their index date until death, emigration, or end of follow-up, whichever occurred first. For the cost and income analysis, patients were followed for a period from 4 years before until 2 years after their index date or until their death or emigration (all patients were eligible for follow-up 2 years after their index date). The choice of periods of 4 years before and 2 years after the index date were based on the availability of cost data, which are available for the period from January 1, 2002 to December 31, 2020.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with Parkinson’s disease have a higher incidence of sleepwalking. Due to the known negative prognosis associated with this condition, we excluded these patients from our study to prevent their inclusion skewing the analysis [15, 16].

In total, 169 patients were diagnosed with NREMp (G47.5) between 2006 and 2018. Of these, 10 patients younger than 18 years were excluded, ending with a total of 159 included patients.

Control group

Controls were randomly selected from the total Danish population, with the exclusion of patients diagnosed with REM parasomnia, Parkinson’s disease, and NREMp. This selection ensures an unbiased, representative sample. Controls were matched with cases (at a ratio of 4:1) by age, gender, marital status, and residential municipality at index year. They entered the study on the same calendar date as their case index date.

The SAS SURVEYSELECT procedure was used to select a probability-based random control group.

Study variables and outcomes definitions

Comorbidity: We identified comorbidity based on ICD-10 diagnoses registered in connection with a hospital contact (primary and secondary hospital diagnoses were included) 7 years before the patient’s index date. We did not include the ICD-10 code G47.5, which covers parasomnias, as a comorbidity.

The comorbidity reported is the accumulated comorbidity for each of the 21 WHO groups. This means that, for example, if a patient had a WHO 9 condition (Diseases of the circulatory system contact [I00-I99]) in year 7 before index, they would be regarded as being in that WHO comorbidity group for all subsequent years. The comorbidity at index was calculated based on all diagnostic contacts within the 7-year period before the index date. Comorbidities were grouped according to the 21 WHO categories [17].

Mortality: All-cause mortality. Patients were followed from the time of diagnosis (index date) until their death or emigration or censoring if they were still present at the end of the study period.

Health cost: Healthcare cost from the secondary sector was calculated from DNPR data on inpatient admissions, outpatient services, and psychiatric services and DRG rate tariffs.

Medication cost was calculated from NPR data on total sales of prescribed medicinal products in Denmark since 1995.

Primary health sector cost was calculated from Danish health insurance registry data on the consumption and cost of health services in primary practice within the Danish public health insurance system.

The healthcare cost analysis covers the 4 years before and the 2 years after the index date (index year + year 1). The cost related to the index hospital contact is included in the “after” cost (index year). The average yearly cost per patient was calculated for each year before and after the index date (year −4, year −3, year −2, year −1, index year, and year 1). The index year starts on the index date and includes costs related to the index NREMp hospital contact.

Income and public transfers: Income and public transfers were calculated from Danish Income Register data. The income analysis also covers the 4 years before and the 2 years after the index date (index year + year 1). The income data were of annual stocks following the calendar year, so the index year in the income analysis was the calendar year the patient was diagnosed with NREMp.

The annual average income per patient year was calculated for each year to measure the change in income over time. All costs and income were adjusted to 2020 prices.

Statistical methods: Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 TS Level 1 M5 (SAS, Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Categories with fewer than five observations were not reported. Sociodemographic, clinical, and other descriptive characteristics were summarized as: frequency and percentage for categorical variables (e.g. sex), and mean and standard deviation for continuous variables (e.g. age). The key sociodemographic characteristics included age, sex, partner/marital status, highest level of educational attainment, and employment status. Chi-square tests, t-tests, and Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon tests were used to examine differences for patients and matched controls as appropriate.

Conditional logistic regression models were used to analyze comorbidity conditions. Odds ratios, with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and probabilities (p), were estimated for each comorbidity group.

For the cost and income analysis, the mean cost was calculated, and a bootstrapped t-test used to examine differences in cost and income for patients versus their matched controls.

Mortality

No patients with NREMp died during the follow-up period, compared with seven deaths (0.9%) in the matched-control group. Therefore, there was no indication of increased mortality associated with NREMp; however, this observation should be interpreted with caution due to the low number of events and the limited sample size.

Education level and employment status

Comparison of the education levels and employment rate in the NREMp population and the matched controls indicated similar distributions of attainment, which suggests that NREMp did not affect individual education attainment or employment status at the time of diagnosis. Furthermore, incomes from employment among the NREMp population were comparable to those of their matched controls (Table 1; Figure 2a).

Health cost and income analysis

We analyzed the total health costs 4 years before (−4 to −1 year) and 2 years after (index year + 1 year) the diagnosis. The NREMp population incurred greater healthcare costs in years 4 and 3 before the year of diagnosis and about three times the cost in the year (−1 year) of diagnosis (Figure 2b). The total healthcare costs for patients with NREMp in the year of diagnosis were also significantly higher, with a mean of €8613 compared with €1704 for controls. However, a year after index, the total use of healthcare costs was similar in the patient and control population.

Patients with NREMp had similar home care costs, income, and public transfer costs as the matched controls in the year of diagnosis. The complete expenses are shown in Table 5. All types of public transfer were similar in the two populations (Figure 2c).

Discussion

Several studies have described the various types of NREMp and their associations with comorbidities and injury risk [5, 12, 18], but there are no comprehensive studies that quantify the health and socioeconomic burdens of NREMp. This study aimed to provide a detailed insight into the burden of NREMp by presenting an overview of the total welfare health, and costs incurred in the years preceding the diagnosis and during the 2 years post-diagnosis compared with matched controls.

We found that the NREMp population was associated with a higher frequency of diagnoses and healthcare contacts in the years up to the NREMp diagnosis. The greater comorbidity included 9 of the 21 WHO major disease groups, including neurological diseases, infectious and parasitic diseases, mental and behavioral disorders, diseases of the eye and adnexa, and genitourinary and digestive disorders. Notably, the NREMp individuals were associated with a significantly higher risk of injuries.

NREMp range from subclinical events only noticed by close family to complex and sometimes violent, potentially life-threatening behaviors. We do not know the severity of the NREMp in the cases included in this study, but we may assume that they were severe enough to have been diagnosed. Therefore, it is also worth noting that NREMp was not associated with any burdens in terms of employment status and education attainment. Furthermore, during the follow-up period the NREMp population did not exhibit a higher mortality rate compared to the matched controls.

Diseases of the nervous system

Diagnoses related to the nervous system became steadily more common in the years leading up to a diagnosis of NREMp, with a statistically significant rise observed from 5 years before the diagnosis. This association suggests that the central nervous system may be progressively affected during the development of NREMp. Alternatively, it may indicate that the symptoms increasingly affect individuals as external demands grow during adulthood, prompting them to seek healthcare services. To further explore diagnostic patterns, we listed specific ICD-10 diagnoses within the major categories where more than three cases were observed (Table 4). Notably, all diagnoses within the G00–G99 category fell under G40–G47, corresponding to episodic and paroxysmal disorders, including epilepsy. This is of particular interest, as NREMp and certain epilepsy subtypes can be difficult to distinguish in clinical settings [11, 13].

It is likely that some patients initially referred for epilepsy evaluation were ultimately diagnosed with NREMp.

Behavioral and mental disorders

Mental and behavioral disorders and diseases became statistically significant from year 3 before index. NREMp in adults is associated with psychiatric disorders and the NREMp symptoms occur in a higher proportion of patients with psychiatric condition (8.5%) than in the general population [5, 7, 19]. However, this is partly due to side effects from medication. This trend reflects the fact that patients with mental and behavioral disorders often experience sleep disturbances, but might also be because some medication for mental disorders causes NREMp as a side effect [12, 19–22]. Therefore, it would have been helpful to know about the medicines prescribed in the two groups.

Diseases of the eye and adnexa

Diseases of the eye and adnexa occurred more often from year 5 before index with an OR of 2.5 (95% CI: 1.32 to 4.88) than in the controls. This major disease group includes a variety of diseases of the eye and its surroundings, but there was no indication that any specific disease was more pronounced in the NREMp population than in the controls.

Injury, poisoning, and certain other consequences of external causes

Table 4 shows a higher incidence of injuries to the wrist, hand, and fingers, as well as to the knee, lower leg, ankle, and foot. Some of these injuries may have been accidental and potentially occurred during episodes of nocturnal physical activity related to NREMp; however, this remains speculative. Though, three thorough clinical studies found that 31%–54% of adult individuals who sleepwalked or experienced sleep terrors had injured themselves, suffering mild-to-severe injuries such as fractures, lacerations, bruises, bleeding, hematomas, concussions, and burns [8, 23, 24]. In addition, while it is known that individuals with sleep-related eating disorder may consume inedible items, there were no cases of poisoning in our dataset.

Symptoms, signs, and abnormal clinical and laboratory findings

This diverse group includes, for example, symptoms involving the digestive system and abdomen, and the urinary, circulatory, and respiratory systems, all of which can cause arousals and affect the depth and quality of sleep. However, we found no indication of any specific symptoms that were more pronounced in the NREMp population than in the matched controls.

Education and employment

We know almost nothing about how NREMp affects educational attainment and employment. Therefore, it is of particular note that the NREMp population in this study is largely comparable to its matched controls in terms of educational level, employment status, and income. The hypothesis that sleep disorders may be related to stress or shift work is pertinent [25–28]. However, our current data only include education levels and do not provide information about the types of positions, shift work, or the presence of stressful working conditions. These influences can have an effect irrespective of the level of education, from none to extensive, required to do the job, but we cannot examine this aspect using the data available here. Furthermore, the individuals in the present study were relatively young and likely not to have had many years in full-time employment, for which the requirements are different from those for people in education.

Total healthcare costs and total public transfers

Due to the higher prevalence of comorbidities within the NREMp population, total health costs were higher during the 4 years preceding diagnosis, peaking in the index year, than in the controls. Despite more frequent healthcare visits, this trend was not reflected in public transfers, either before or after diagnosis. With only 2 years of follow-up it remains unclear whether healthcare costs returned to prediagnosis levels, were similar to those of the control population, or if they diverged. Given that NREMp affects the quality of life, a 2-year follow-up may be an insufficient period to reliably assess the long-term impacts on work capability, income, and public transfer needs.

The study population comprised almost equal proportions of married or cohabiting individuals and single individuals (50.3% vs. 49.7%; Table 1). In comparison, 50.1% of Danish adults aged 25–29 years were married or cohabiting in 2020, whereas the percentage was lower (approximately 30%) for the 30- to 59-year age group [29, 30]. Given that the study population (mean [SD] age, 32 [11] years) is closer in age to the 25- to 30-year group, this suggests that living with NREMp does not significantly affect the rate of cohabitation or marriage. However, it is worth considering that individuals living alone may be unaware of the extent of their NREMp because they do not recall the occurrences the following morning. In addition, if they have experienced NREMp since childhood, they might not discuss the matter with their doctor when they are adults.

Limitations

A key limitation of the study is the relatively small number of individuals in the NREMp population, which may reduce the generalizability of the findings. These individuals may not be representative of the broader population with NREM parasomnias (NREMp). In addition, the mortality data—where seven deaths (0.9%) occurred among controls but none in the NREMp group—suggest the study may be underpowered to detect differences in rare outcomes.

The low number of individuals diagnosed with NREMp could be attributed to limited awareness of this disorder in adults, leading to underdiagnosis and an underestimation of its prevalence during the study period. Many adults with NREMp may not seek medical attention unless their symptoms are severe, dangerous, result in injury, or significantly impair daily functioning. Consequently, the individuals identified in the study likely represent more severe cases. On the other hand, growing awareness of sleep disorders may have contributed to a higher number of NREMp diagnoses in recent years, possibly capturing a broader range of severity. This trend is reflected in the number of patients excluded due to index years falling in 2019 (n = 42) and 2020 (n = 67), which far exceeds the five cases diagnosed in the early years of the study period (2006–2011), as shown in Table 2. The increase in diagnoses over time highlights the growing recognition of the disorder and supports the value of repeating the study in another decade. Another limitation is the short follow-up period for evaluating healthcare costs and income: only 2 years of data after diagnosis were available. This limited timeframe may not fully capture the long-term socioeconomic impact of NREMp. It remains unknown whether individuals with NREMp will continue to align with the general population in terms of labor market attachment and employment income over the longer term. Nevertheless, our findings indicate that healthcare costs are higher for these individuals both in the years leading up to and in the year of diagnosis.

Finally, the study did not include data on medication use. This would have provided important information, as some medications—such as non-benzodiazepine sedatives, benzodiazepines, antidepressants, beta-blockers, and mood stabilizers [5, 7]—are known to trigger or exacerbate NREMp symptoms. Such information could have clarified whether the NREMp observed in this cohort was secondary to pharmacological treatment for another condition or whether it was a primary, independent diagnosis.

Conclusion

Given the concerns about the burden of NREMp raised in the aforementioned studies, our present study suggests that, overall, there are reasons to be optimistic. Although the NREMp population had significantly more healthcare contacts and diagnoses in the years preceding the NREMp diagnosis, the condition was not associated with impaired employment status or level of educational attainment. Furthermore, in the period we followed the populations, there was no indication of higher mortality in the patients with NREMp than in the matched controls.

Future studies

Given the presumed number of symptomatic but undiagnosed individuals and the increased focus on NREMp, we anticipate a rise in the number of diagnosed cases. We recommend repeating this analysis within the next 10 years, as it is likely to involve a larger cohort of individuals with NREMp. This follow-up should include participants from the current study so that we can better understand the long-term burden of the disorder. We may hope that such an approach will improve our understanding of the impact of NREMp.
