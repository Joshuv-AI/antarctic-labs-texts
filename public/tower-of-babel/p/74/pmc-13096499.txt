Self-propelled platinum based magnetite Janus nanomotors prepared from PCL and PHEMA graft copolymer with physicochemical properties motility and peroxidase-like activity
Authors: Banafsheh Nikfar, Maryam Soleymani, Sakine Shirvalilou, Saba Nadi, Sepideh Khoee, Samideh Khoei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present research focused on the manufacturing and designing of a magnetic self-propelled theranostic Janus nanomotor for targeted cancer cell diagnosis and treatment. Platinum (Pt)-based magnetite Janus nanomotors were synthesized by the Pickering Janus emulsions method and used as carriers for the chemotherapy drug Doxorubicin (DOX). Nanomotors were functionalized with a folic acid (FA) ligand and two biocompatible and biodegradable polymers, polycaprolactone (PCL) and poly (2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate) (PHEMA), termed Ac/FA-PHEMA-g-PCL MNP. The hydrodynamic diameter, zeta potential, morphology, drug loading capacity and in-vitro release at pH 7.4 and 5.8, MR contrast agent potential, motility, and peroxidase-like activity of the synthesized nanomotors were analyzed. The findings showed their size of 105.275 ± 26.48 nm and zeta potential of -45.63 ± 0.80 mV and proved cellular internalization, the ability of motion and H2O2 catalyze, and diagnostic feature by MRI about them. The drug’s cumulative release from nanomotors was restricted. Only 37.26% and 49.90% of the encapsulated drug were released within 24 h at 37 °C in neutral and acidic pH levels, respectively. Under hyperthermia, the release also only reached 58.05%. Introduction

Intelligent delivery systems that can specifically target certain cells and controllable cargo release have shown growing applications in biomedicine1. An excellent example of these systems is nano-theranostics, which integrates therapeutic and diagnostic tools or imaging into a single agent using nanoparticles2,3. When a nano-platform is integrated with imaging capabilities, it can be applied to monitor the accumulation of drug-loaded nanoparticles in the tumor site and acquire the synergy of diagnosis and therapy to increase treatment accuracy or enhance the therapeutic impact via determining the extent of tumor progression before surgery, and evaluating treatment response afterward4. Conventional chemotherapeutic agents are distributed non-selectively throughout the body, leading to systemic toxicity. Furthermore, these agents often struggle to penetrate the depths of solid tumors. To address this issue, researchers are exploring chemotherapeutics approaches that selectively target tumor cells to overcome this issue5. These targeting approaches can be categorized into three main categories: passive, active, and physical targeting6.

Micro/nanomotors (MNMs) are excellent carriers that can encapsulate active compounds, including anticancer agents, targeting and imaging tools. They also demonstrate suitable tissue permeability within the tumor microenvironment, thanks to their potential for autonomous movement7,8. Chemically driven MNMs are synthetic power devices inspired by the bio-motors of microorganisms. They range in size from nano to micro scales and can harness energy from their surroundings to produce efficient movement9. The remarkable movement capabilities of MNMs, especially in micro-scale or enclosed environments, combined with their chemically active properties, hold significant promise for applications in nano-theranostics10–12. Moreover, chemically driven MNMs can respond to various stimuli, such as metabolite concentration13,14 and microenvironment temperature15,16, enabling them to release their cargo effectively.

In recent years, there has been significant interest in developing self-propelling, fuel-dependent synthetic MNMs that mimic bio-motor features using synthetic materials containing catalysts17. A recent review article examined the enhanced therapeutic efficacy and the corresponding implementation strategies of Janus MNMs across a diverse spectrum of diseases, notably including cancer, cardiovascular disorders, neurological diseases, microbial infections (bacterial/fungal), and chronic inflammatory conditions18. Their propulsion mechanisms are classified based on their energy source: externally driven (actuation via external fields) or chemically driven (self-propulsion utilizing ambient fuels)19. One particularly effective fuel for powering these chemically driven MNMs is hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). The mechanism involves the decomposition of H2O2 into water (H2O) and oxygen (O2); the release of oxygen bubbles provides the thrust needed to propel the motors20. This propulsion method is known as bubble propulsion, whereby fuel (H2O2) is decomposed by MNMs that are adorned with catalysts to generate micro-sized gas bubbles (O2) that escape from the motor surface, thus driving movement21. Notably, Ismagilov et al. developed a synthetic MNM powered by H2O2, wherein platinum (Pt) was used as a catalyst and was integrated on the motor’s surface to facilitate the breakdown of H2O2 and produce oxygen bubbles for autonomous movement22.

The Janus Pt/Fe₃O₄ architecture was specifically designed to synergistically integrate Pt-mediated propulsion with Fe₃O₄-enabled MRI imaging and magnetic guidance. Platinum’s superior H₂O₂ decomposition catalysis generates oxygen bubble propulsion essential for active tumor penetration23. Similarly, amphiphilic copolymers have been widely demonstrated for tumor-targeted DOX delivery24,25.

This study synthesized and characterized the nanotheranostics platform consisting of FA (folic acid)-conjugated Ac/FA-PHEMA-g-PCL MNP. Kefayat et al. demonstrated higher Fe uptake for FA-targeted nanoparticles in C6 glioma cells versus non-targeted controls26. On the other hand, DOX, an antitumor agent, generates free radicals, interferes with DNA topoisomerase II, damages the DNA of tumor cells, and inhibits tumor cell proliferation27. However, preclinical research has revealed several disadvantages associated with DOX, including poor water solubility, instability, and rapid clearance in vivo28,29. Therefore, utilizing a nanostructured format for DOX may be essential. Janus nanoparticles are composed of two chemically or physically distinct domains. Their unique broken symmetry and chemical anisotropy at the nanoscale allow them to exhibit molecule-like directionality. Due to their high interfacial activity30–33, Janus nanoparticles can be applied in biomedicine for various purposes, including biosensing34 and biomimicking35, as well as facilitating cell membrane penetration1,36. Consequently, this formulation of nanomotors presents a promising platform for simultaneous cancer diagnosis and treatment.

Materials and methods

Chemical materials

2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA), ε-caprolactone, acryloyl chloride, dimethylformamide (DMF), tin(II) 2-ethylhexanoate (Sn(Oct)2), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 4-dimethylamino pyridine (DMAP), N, N′-dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC), N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF), paraffin wax, and folic acid (FA) were prepared from Sigma Aldrich Co. Triethylamine, FeCl2. 4H2O, FeCl3. 6H2O, ammonia solution (25%), irgacure 2595, (3-aminopropyl) triethoxysilane (APTES), chloroform, cis-platin, 3,30,5,50-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB), potassium ferrocyanide (II) trihydrate, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), formaldehyde solution, trypsin, EDTA, hydrochloric acid (37%), butylated hydroxy toluene (BHT), nitric acid (65%) and ethanol were obtained from Merck Co. Fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin-streptomycin, as well as Ham’s F12, dulbecco’s modified eagle’s medium (DMEM) culture medias was purchased from Biowest and Gibco, respectively. Doxorubicin hydrochloride solution (DOX) was prepared from products of EBEWE Pharma Co. All solvents and compounds were utilized without further purification.

Synthesis of polymeric nanocarrier/nanomotor

Synthesis of poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL) as a macromonomer

Acrylate-terminated poly(ε-caprolactone) (AcPCL) was synthesized as a macromonomer following a modified version of the reported37. Briefly, 6 mL ε-caprolactone (5.4 mmol) and 5 mg BHT (0.02 mmol) as a radical inhibitor were dissolved in 12 mL DMF within a three-necked round-bottom flask. Subsequently, 1 mL 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (8 mmol) was added as an initiator, stirring the mixture. The nitrogen gas was purged to deplete the oxygen reaction mixture, and the temperature was gradually increased to 80 °C. A catalytic amount of Sn(Oct)2 was introduced to the reaction, and the temperature was adjusted to 120 °C. After approximately 24 h of stirring, the resulting product was precipitated in cooled deionized water and dried with a freeze-drier. The purified product was obtained by dissolving the dried polymer in a minimum amount of DMF and precipitating it in cooled deionized water, yielding an 89% yield.

Synthesis of poly (2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate)-graft- poly (ε-caprolactone) (PHEMA-g-PCL)

A solution containing a mixture of 0.5 g macromonomer (0.45 mmol) and 0.12 mL of 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (0.1 mmol) in 15 mL DMF was poured into a round-bottom flask, and 2 mg Irgacure 2595 as a photoinitiator was added to the reaction media. The mixture was exposed to UV radiation with a wavelength of 365 nm under magnetic stirring (450 rpm, room temperature) for 6 h. The resulting product was obtained in cooled deionized water and dried overnight in a freeze-drier. Purification was repeated, and the precipitated polymer was dried with a 74% yield.

Conjugation of folic acid to PHEMA-g-PCL (FA-PHEMA-g-PCL)

0.2 g folic acid (0.45 mmol) was dissolved in 4 mL DMSO; after that, 0.09 g DCC (0.43 mmol) and 0.5 g DMAP (0.4 mmol) were added and stirred to dissolve them completely. 1 g of the PHEMA-g-PCL was poured into the above mixture and stirred for 24 h at room temperature. After the time allowed for the reaction, the folic acid-conjugated polymer was obtained with precipitation of the solution in cooled deionized water and purified from unreacted materials by a repeated dissolution-precipitation process once. The product yield was 81%.

Acrylation of folic acid-conjugated PHEMA-g-PCL (Ac/FA- PHEMA-g-PCL)

To acrylate folic acid-conjugated PHEMA-g-PCL, in a three-necked round bottom flask, 2.5 g of this polymer was dissolved in 7 mL DMF containing 0.28 mL triethylamine (2 mmol) and stirred in an ice bath to maintain the temperature below 10 °C for 30 min. An inert atmosphere was established in the reaction media, followed by the dropwise addition of 0.16 mL acryloyl chloride (2 mmol), after which the mixture was stirred for 48 h. Afterward, the resulting product was precipitated in deionized water and dried using a freeze-dryer overnight, yielding a 76% yield of the acrylated polymer.

Synthesis of magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (MNP)

The co-precipitation method was utilized to prepare iron oxide nanoparticles37. In a three-necked round bottom flask equipped with nitrogen intel and a mechanical stirrer, 0.8 g of FeCl2. 4H2O (4 mmol) and 2.4 g of FeCl3. 6H2O (9 mmol) was dissolved in 25 mL of deionized water, and the temperature was adjusted to 85 °C. Subsequently, 10 mL of ammonia solution was injected dropwise into the reaction mixture under stirring. After two hours of stirring, the resulting magnetite nanoparticles were separated from the unreacted materials using an external magnetic field. The magnetite nanoparticles underwent a thorough purification process involving washing with deionized water three times and ethanol twice, resulting in a final product yield of 88%.

Modification of magnetite nanoparticles with APTES (AMNP)

The bare magnetite nanoparticles were surface modified with APTES as follows: 1.2 g of synthesized nanoparticles were homogenized in 40 mL ethanol using an ultrasonic probe (35 W power) for 5 min. Subsequently, 0.7 mL (3 mmol) APTES was poured into the vessel while the solution was magnetically stirred at 450 rpm. The reaction was allowed to proceed for approximately 24 h. After the specified period, the dispersed phase was magnetically separated and purified with ethanol three times38. The resulting amine-terminated magnetite nanoparticles were dried using a freeze-dryer, yielding 80%.

Anchoring Ac/FA-PHEMA-g-PCL on AMNP (Ac/FA-PHEMA-g-PCL MNP)

A Michael addition reaction was utilized to functionalize amine-terminated magnetite nanoparticles with synthesized graft copolymers. 0.2 g of AMNP was dispersed in 30 mL DMF using a probe sonicator for 5 min, followed by stirring. Subsequently, a solution containing 0.07 g of graft copolymer in 2 mL DMF was added to the homogenized dispersion, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 72 h at room temperature. Afterward, the reaction product was separated using a centrifuge at 4000 rpm, and the yield was determined to be 87%.

Preparation of Janus nanomotors (JNM)

The experiment involved dispersing 300 mg of Ac/FA-PHEMA-g-PCL MNP into 40 mL of deionized water using an ultrasonic probe. The resulting homogenous mixture was then mechanically stirred at 950 rpm and heated to 70 °C for 1.5 h. Following the principles of Pickering emulsions, 5 g of paraffin wax granules were gradually added to the mixture. The temperature was then raised to 78 °C, and stirring continued for two hours to facilitate the attachment of the nanoparticles to paraffin wax, as mentioned above. In the next step, 30 mL of cisplatin (50 mg/50 mL) was added and stirred for two hours. 5 mL of freshly prepared NaBH4 (0.02 g/mL) aqueous solution was then introduced into a reaction flask and stirred for 24 h. The Janus nanoparticles were subsequently collected magnetically. To remove the paraffin wax, 20 mL of warm chloroform was poured into a mixture and repeated several times39. The Janus nanoparticles were subjected to a 24-hour freeze-drying process, resulting in a 52% yield.

Preparation of DOX-loaded Janus nanoparticles (DOX@JNM)

Doxorubicin (DOX) was utilized as the model hydrophobic drug to evaluate its potential delivery, encapsulation efficiency, and drug loading. For this purpose, 100 mg of Janus nanoparticles were dispersed in deionized water, and the pH was adjusted to 7.5 by adding 0.1 M NaOH solution. Next, 10 mL of the doxorubicin hydrochloride solution (containing 10 mg DOX) was added dropwise to the suspension. The resulting mixture was stirred overnight, and the DOX-loaded Janus nanoparticles were then magnetically separated and dried using a freeze-dryer for 24 h.

Characterization and equipment

Physicochemical characterization of nanomotors

A Varian-INOVA 500 MHz spectrometer was utilized to record the 1H-NMR spectra. The number average molecular weight (Mn) of acrylate-terminated poly(ε-caprolactone) was calculated from the 1H-NMR data. FT-IR analysis was carried out using a Bruker EQUINOX 55 FT-IR spectrometer. Dispersing and homogenizing of the nanoparticles was performed using a SONOPULS ultrasonic homogenizer (20 kHz ± 500 Hz ultrasonic generator) model HF-GM 3200 (BANDELIN electronic GmbH & Co. KG). A titanium microtip MS-72 with a diameter of 3 mm served as a probe. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Zeta-sizer NANO-flex) was applied to determine the nanomotors’ hydrodynamic size and Zeta potential. Before measurement, the synthesized Janus nanomotors were homogenized in deionized water and diluted 20 times. In order to the stability evaluation in plasma, the principal polypeptides constituting the protein corona remained largely unchanged after both short (15 min) and prolonged incubation periods (up to 6 h), indicating that the resulting corona composition is kinetically stable, based on Fedeli et al. reports40. Consequently, an incubation time of 15 min was selected for investigations on protein corona stability.​ Thus, nanomotors were incubated with plasma at 37 °C for 15 min under continuous agitation. Following incubation, the suspensions were centrifuged at 18,000 RCF for 10 min at room temperature to pellet the nanoparticle–protein complexes, which were then separated from the supernatant plasma. The resulting pellet was resuspended in 500 µL of PBS and subjected to a second centrifugation step to isolate the biomolecular corona41, after which the final pellet was prepared for hydrodynamic size measurements.

The morphology of nanomotors was characterized with transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Zeiss LEO 906, Jena). Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) (TESCAN, MIRA III) was used to study surface topography. To accomplish the analysis, the nanoparticles were first dispersed in deionized water. A drop of this suspension was placed onto a carbon-coated grid and allowed to dry before analysis. A UV-visible spectrophotometer (Ultraospec 3000, Pharmacia Biotech) was applied to determine nanoparticle drug content. Janus nanomotors fabrication and the presence of folic acid in this structure were validated using a UV-visible light spectrophotometer on a JASCO V-750. A Zeiss light microscope was also used to confirm the synthesized folic acid-conjugated Janus nanomotors. Drug loading capacity (DLC) and encapsulation efficiency (EE) were calculated by measuring the amount of drug in 5 mg Janus nanomotors in the DMSO phase after magnetically separating the insoluble nanomotors from the dispersion. A UV-Vis spectrophotometer was used at the maximum absorption wavelength of DOX (480 nm) according to the standard calibration curve plotted with the absorbance against DOX concentration in DMSO. EE and DLC were calculated using Eqs. 1 and 2, respectively.

The in vitro DOX release behavior from Janus nanomotors was studied in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution at 37 °C and two different pH (7.4 and 5.8) and 43 °C at the pH value of 7.4 via diffusion technique. According to the literature, the DOX release is faster in acidic conditions42 and lowering the pH to 5.8 is sufficient to induce DOX release in a simulated tumor environment43.

Although the mean extracellular pH of solid tumors is commonly reported to be around 6.6–6.9, numerous in vivo measurements have demonstrated significant spatial heterogeneity, with certain tumor regions exhibiting pH values as low as approximately 6.0, particularly in hypoxic and poorly perfused areas. Consequently, pH 5.8 was chosen as an operationally convenient acidic condition to represent the more acidic fraction of the heterogeneous tumor microenvironment, as well as the endosomal/lysosomal compartments involved in nanoparticle trafficking44. Additionally, many studies on pH-responsive nanocarriers intentionally select pH values ranging from 5.0 to 6.0 (often around pH 5.5–5.8 in PBS or acetate buffer) for in vitro release assays to approximate the pH of endosomal/lysosomal compartments and the more acidic tumor niches, with pH 7.4 serving as the control for normal tissue and blood45. Notably, reviews on acidic tumor microenvironment (TME)-responsive nanomedicines typically describe the extracellular pH of tumors as approximately 6.5–7.0, yet they still utilize pH 5.5–6.0 as the “acidic trigger” in in vitro tests to ensure a clear pH-dependent release window46.

Chronoamperometry

Chronoamperometry was employed to determine the minimum concentrations of H₂O₂ capable of eliciting responses from the nanomotors. Measurements were conducted using a three-electrode system comprising an Ag/AgCl reference electrode, a glass working electrode modified with nanomotors, and a platinum wire auxiliary electrode13. A dispersion of Janus nanomotors in ethanol (1 mg/mL) was prepared, from which a droplet was deposited onto a glass electrode and air-dried for approximately 30 min. Chronoamperometry was conducted at 0.2 V for 100 s in 0.1 M KCl electrolyte containing varying concentrations of H₂O₂ with each measurement performed in triplicate under identical conditions.

MRI contrast enhancement capability of nanomotors

Magnetic resonance imaging was utilized to assess the diagnostic capabilities of nanoparticles in detecting Fe3O4 nanoparticles within the core of nanomotors. For this experiment, nanomotors containing an IC10 concentration and a control sample of distilled water were placed in a self-designed water phantom. T2-weighted images were obtained using a 3 T MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Prisma, Siemens) with a head receive coil, employing the following settings: TR/TE = 3000 ms/57 ms, field of view = 256 × 256 mm2, matrix size = 512 × 512 mm2, flip angle = 60, slice thickness = 3 mm.

Cell culture

Both cancer cells (C6 rat glioma) and normal cells (OLN-93 rat glial) were cultured in Ham’s F12 medium for the cancer cells and DMEM for the normal cells. The media were enriched with FBS (10%), penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 mg/mL). and the cultures were incubated at 37 °C in an environment containing 5% CO2 and 95% humidity.

Cytotoxicity of nanomotors

The preliminary cytotoxicity of three types of nanomotors was evaluated: nanomotors containing DOX and folic acid (N + D+FA), nanomotors with only DOX (N + D), nanomotors with only folic acid (N + FA), and free DOX. This evaluation used the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay, which involved C6 and OLN-93 cells. Both cell types were exposed to different concentrations of free DOX and nanomotors for 48 h. After incubation, the culture mediums were removed, and the cells were rinsed twice with PBS. Following this, the cells were dissolved using 200 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for 20 min. Absorbance was measured using an ELISA reader (Labsystems Multiskan MS) at a wavelength of 570 nm. Quantification of cellular uptake of iron by the ICP-OES method

The uptake of iron by cells was assessed in folate-receptor overexpressing C6 cancer cells and low-expressing OLN-93 normal cells using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES). Cells were grown in Petri dishes and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C before being treated with an IC10 concentration of nanomotors for 48 h at 37 °C. One untreated Petri dish for each cell type served as a negative control. After treatment, the cells were washed with PBS, collected, and counted. They were then digested in a 3:1 mixture of 37% hydrochloric acid (HCl) and 65% nitric acid (HNO3) on a heater stirrer at 140 °C for 2 h47. Following digestion, the samples were diluted with deionized water to a final volume of 3 mL. Finally, the concentration of iron in all samples was measured, and its content per cell was calculated. Iron deposition in the cells was analyzed using ICP-OES (Varian 730 ES).

Qualification of cellular uptake of iron by Prussian blue staining

The Prussian blue staining technique is employed for in-situ detection of iron deposits in cells and can be used to visualize IONPs. The staining procedure was performed on fixed, differentiated cells, following the method previously described by Miyoshi et al.48. In brief, the cells were seeded in Petri dishes for 24 h and then treated with an IC10 concentration of nanomotors for 48 h. Moreover, one cell culture petri dish for C6 and OLN-93 was considered a negative control without treatment. A solution of 2% (w/v) potassium ferricyanide and 2% (v/v) HCl in distilled water was prepared at room temperature. These two solutions were mixed in a 1:1 ratio and then applied to the fixed C6 and OLN-93 cells. The samples were incubated at room temperature for 20 min and then washed thrice with water. Images of the stained cells were captured using an Olympus CK2 microscope.

Peroxidase-like activity of nanomotors

The peroxidase-like behavior of the synthesized nanomotors was evaluated using TMB (3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine). This chromogenic substrate can be oxidized by the peroxidase enzyme in the presence of H2O2, resulting in a blue-color product with maximum absorbance at 370 nm and 650 nm49. Initially, a fixed concentration of H2O2 (400 µM) and TMB (200 µM) was allowed to react at room temperature (27 °C), and the peak absorbance at 650 nm was monitored over different time points. In the next step, the reaction conditions, including time and the concentrations of TMB and H2O2, were optimized to achieve the highest performance. Separate experiments assessed a range of H2O2 concentrations (100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 µM) and TMB concentrations (50, 100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 µM). Various concentrations of H2O2 were then added to a fixed concentration of TMB (200 µM) and incubated at room temperature for the optimal time determined earlier. After selecting the optimal concentration of H2O2, it was added to separate microtubes containing different concentrations of TMB. This experiment evaluated the peroxidase-like activity of nanomotors by determining the optimal time and concentration for TMB and H2O2. Additionally, to simulate the catalytic performance of the nanomotors in solid tumor situations, a similar test was conducted using the optimal concentration of TMB and the nanomotor, along with 5 × 10− 5 M of H2O2 at 37 °C, and compared to a temperature of 43 °C (simulating hyperthermia conditions).

Statistical analysis

Data were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Using Graph Pad Prism Software (version 9.0, San Diego, CA), a one-way ANOVA and t-test were employed to assess the significance of the difference between samples. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

As mentioned in the introduction section, the biomedical application of nanomotors has garnered significant attention due to its considerable potential. Nanomotors’ unique ability to overcome physiological barriers and achieve precise targeting offers substantial advantages that may profoundly influence future patient care50. A recent review article anticipates that nanomotors will assume a pivotal role in the treatment of a wide range of medical disorders, as emerging in vivo proof-of-concept studies have substantiated their distinctive functional capacities and versatility51.

The novelty of our work lies in the rational integration, materials design, and experimental demonstration of combined functions that are not collectively shown in prior Pt/Fe3O4 Janus systems. We present a twin-engine nanomotor constructed from Janus morphology formed by the amphiphilic block copolymer poly (2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate)-graft-poly(ε-caprolactone) (PHEMA-g-PCL). Superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (MNP) were synthesized via the co-precipitation method as the core of Janus nanomotors, rendering the particles responsive to an external magnetic field. The polymer mentioned above was chemically linked to the surface of MNPs, isotropically. In the third step, to incorporate chemical fuel responsiveness, we integrated platinum nanoparticles in half the surfaces (anisotropically) of Janus nanoparticles using a Pickering emulsion (Fig. 1, Step 1). Figure 1, Step 2 depicts a schematic of the internalization process of targeted nanomotors into a cell. The folic acid ligand binds to the folate receptor, leading to the invagination of the plasma membrane. Consequently, DOX is released into the intracellular environment, and the folate receptor is recycled back to the cell surface.

Synthesis and characterization of platinum-based polymeric Janus nanomotors

According to Figure S1 and S2, the poly (2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate)-graft-poly(ε-caprolactone) (PHEMA-g-PCL) was synthesized via a two-step reaction. First, ring-opening polymerization of ε-caprolactone was carried out in the presence of HEMA as initiator at 120 °C (Figure S1), followed by free radical polymerization of this macromer in the presence of pristine HEMA (Figure S2). Characterization of the polymers’ functional groups was conducted using FT-IR measurement. The analysis of AcPCL revealed specific peaks indicative of various functional groups (Fig. 2a). Peaks at 1725 and 1193 cm− 1 were ascribed to the stretching vibration frequency of C = O and C-O-C of steric groups, respectively. The stretching vibration peak of C-H groups was observed at 2945 cm− 1. Furthermore, the 3438 and 1638 cm− 1 peaks were attributed to the stretching vibration of hydroxyl and double bond end groups, respectively.

FT-IR spectrum of acrylate-terminated poly(ε-caprolactone) (AcPCL) (a), FT-IR spectrum of poly (2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate)-graft- poly (ε-caprolactone) (PHEMA-g-PCL) (b), 1H-NMR spectrum of the PHEMA-g-PCL in CDCl3 (c), and 1H-NMR spectrum of PHEMA-g-PCL in DMSO-d6 (d).

In the meantime, to analyze the chemical composition of acrylate-terminated poly(ε-caprolactone), 1H-NMR was employed. Figure 2c demonstrates the 1H-NMR spectrum of PCL. Multiple peaks at 1.39 (d, 2 H), 1.65 (e, 2 H), 2.31 (c, 2 H), and 4.06 (f, 2 H) ppm were attributed to methylene protons of the PCL chain. The protons of the terminal vinyl group were revealed as a two-proton signal in the region 5.60 (g, 1H) and 6.13 (h, 1H) ppm. Furthermore, the peaks at 1.95 (a, 3 H) and 4.35 (b, 2 H) ppm were attributed to methyl pendant groups and hydroxyethyl units, respectively. The mean number molecular weight (Mn) of acrylate-terminated PCL was measured from integrated peak areas of 4.06 (2 H) ppm related to the methylene groups in repeating units of PCL to the methyl pendant group in the initiator in the region of 5.60 (1H) ppm. The Mn of PCL obtained was about 1050 g/mol.

The graft copolymer of PHEMA-g-PCL was synthesized via free radical polymerization using acrylate-terminated PCL as a macromer and 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate monomer in the presence of Irgacure 2595 as a photoinitiator (Figure S2). FT-IR analysis verified the chemical composition of the graft copolymer. As shown in Fig. 2b, all characteristic peaks of PCL were present; however, the stretching vibration peak of a double bond at 1638 cm− 1 disappeared. Furthermore, the 1H-NMR measurement was used for more characterization, and the result was illustrated in Fig. 2d. Similar to the results obtained from FT-IR analysis, all the peaks related to PCL were observed except for the double bond peaks at 5.60 (1H) and 6.13 (1H) ppm.

Folic acid is one of the most common vitamin B groups in the human body, and it has high stability, small size, compatibility with many solvents, and so on. However, as a biological molecule, it has become widely used for cancer therapy because overexpressed folic acid receptors on the surface of many cancer cells target folic acid and mediate an endocytotic uptake. Folic acid receptors are overexpressed in various human carcinomas such as breast, ovary, kidney, lung, etc52. Therefore, the conjugation of folic acid and nanoparticles raised the folic acid receptor mediation for targeting the delivery of therapeutic agents. In this report, the folic acid-conjugated PHEMA-g-PCL was successfully prepared (Figure S3) and characterized by 1H-NMR analysis. As demonstrated in Fig. 3a, the coupling of folic acid to graft copolymer was verified with the revelation of the new signals at 6.63 (i, 2 H), 7.63 (h, 2 H), and 8.65 (g 2 H) ppm, which assigned to the aromatic protons of folic acid, and other characteristic peaks of folic acid were observed at 4.27 (k, 1H) ppm. The degree of folic acid substitution on the graft copolymer was calculated from the integrated ratio area of 6.63 (2 H) ppm related to folic acid to the area of 4.06 (2 H) associated with PCL, which was 10.3%.

The acrylated folic acid-conjugated PHEMA-g-PCL was accomplished via the reaction between terminal OH groups of the substituted graft copolymer and acryloyl chloride in the presence of triethylamine (Figure S4). Figure 3b illustrates the 1H-NMR spectrum of the acrylated graft copolymer. Along with all the characteristic peaks of the functionalized graft copolymer, which were characterized in previous steps, the three proton resonance signals in the region 5.80–6.42 ppm (m, n, and p, 1H) attributed to vinyl groups are evidence of a successful reaction.

Our strategy in this report was to prepare a Janus nanomotor based on magnetite nanoparticles. Once the functionalized PHEMA-g-PCL was obtained, the next step was synthesizing magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles. The co-precipitation method was used to synthesize the magnetite nanoparticles under a nitrogen atmosphere (Figure S5). FT-IR analysis was utilized to characterize naked MNP, as shown in Figure S6. The stretching vibration of -OH groups on the surface of nanoparticles appeared at 3385 cm− 1, and the peak of 583 cm− 1 was also ascribed to the stretching frequency of Fe-O groups. The AMNP was prepared from the surface modification of bare magnetic nanoparticles in the presence of APTES (Figure S7). Figure S8 indicates the FT-IR measurement of amine-functionalized magnetic nanoparticles (AMNP). The Si-O stretching frequency peaked at 1108 cm− 1, and the stretching vibration band of amine groups appeared at 3385 cm− 1. Furthermore, the absorption band at 585 cm− 1 was assigned to the Fe-O stretching vibration.

The Michael addition reaction was conducted to anchor the Ac/FA-PHEMA-g-PCL onto amine-terminated magnetite nanoparticles (Figure S9). Figure S10 illustrates the FT-IR analysis of the Ac/FA-PHEMA-g-PCL MNP. The characteristic peak at 1731 cm− 1 is attributed to the stretching vibration of carbonyl groups in the graft copolymer. Additionally, the stretching vibration frequency of the C-O groups have appeared at 1159 cm− 1. The, Fe-O stretching vibration frequency was observed at 583 cm− 1, which confirms the successful synthesis of polymer-coated magnetite nanoparticles.

We report the preparation of Janus nanoparticles with tunable engine power. For this purpose, the obtained Ac/FA@PHEMA-g-PCL MNPs were entrapped in wax microparticles through the Pickering emulsion technique and half of their surfaces were covered by paraffin wax. The free surface of magnetite nanoparticles was then treated with the platinum precursor. The reduction of Pt salts to Pt (0) leads to the formation of heterogeneous platinum nanoparticles (Figure S8), which allow for movement when placed in fuel media, such as hydrogen peroxide.

Platinum nanoparticles exhibit pronounced peroxidase-like activity, enabling them to function as continuous oxygen generators by catalyzing the decomposition of endogenous hydrogen peroxide. This catalytic mechanism drives the directed propulsion of nanomotors, facilitating enhanced active diffusion and expanding their search area within biological environments, which improves their capacity to locate and deliver therapeutic payloads to target cells. The superior search efficiency and targeted delivery exhibited by Pt-based nanomotors represents a substantial advantage over many existing nanocarriers, which typically rely on external energy sources or passive diffusion, thereby constraining there in vivo efficacy53.

Figure 4a displays the EDX pattern of Ac/FA@PHEMA-g-PCL MNPs, which helps us identify the elements in the material. The spectrum shows distinct peaks corresponding to iron, platinum, carbon, nitrogen and oxygen. The EDX analysis also provides information about the quantity of each element in the final Janus nanomotor. According to the EDX data, the composition consists of 11.3% carbon, 2.51% nitrogen, 21.04% iron, 3.48% platinum, and 61.67% oxygen. This detailed breakdown demonstrates the ratios of the Fe and Pt within the nanomotors. In addition to examining the surface, the SEM-mapping analysis (Fig. 4b and c) allows us to investigate the distribution of elements throughout the material. This deeper analysis enhances our understanding of how the mentioned elements are spread within the nanomotors, providing valuable information about the material’s uniformity and structural quality.

As Fig. 4d demonstrates, the synthesized Janus nanomotors have a uniform spherical structure with a size of 60 nm and two different light and dark gray phases. A light gray compact structure core seems to belong to the Fe3O4, and the dark gray domain is related to non-continuous and small-sized Pt nanoparticles.

The analysis of FA-conjugated Janus nanoparticles using fluorescence microscopy revealed a distinct green emission, indicating successful conjugation of folic acid to the graft copolymer. Additionally, the presence of a cap with varying contrast on one hemisphere of the nanoparticles is attributed to the deposition of platinum nanoparticles. In conjunction, UV-visible spectroscopy was employed to characterize the Janus nanomotors alongside the pristine amine-modified nanoparticles (AMNP). To evaluate the stability of the final Janus nanomotors formed, hydrodynamic size and zeta potential studies were carried out over four consecutive weeks after initial sample preparation (Fig. 5c). The results determined that the average hydrodynamic diameter of Janus nanomotors was almost 105.275 ± 26.48 nm over four weeks, and the nanomotors exhibited a surface charge range of −45.63 ± 0.80 mV. According to the results, the Janus nanomotors were observed to be stable. Stability of the Janus nanomotors was also tested on plasma that their mean average during four weeks resulted in 283.35 ± 21.10 nm. As expected, interaction between proteins and nanoparticles substantially augmented the hydrodynamic diameter of the nanoparticles. This enlargement in hydrodynamic size can be attributed to the protein diffusion layer adsorbed onto the nanoparticle surface54. Visual inspection of the thawed samples indicated that freezing storage durations of two weeks or longer resulted in pronounced alterations in the rheological properties of the fluid. Larger agglomerates were particularly observed, accompanied by a reduction in viscosity. In addition, an increase in particle size was associated with a concurrent rise in the PDI55.

After the Janus nanomotors were characterized, we monitored their drug delivery properties. The DOX-loaded Janus nanomotors were prepared, and the drug-loading capacity and encapsulation efficiency were 6.65% and 73.89%, respectively. The in-vitro drug release of Janus nanomotors was performed under acidic and neutral pH conditions, at 37 °C and 43 °C, and in the presence or absence of 0.1% H2O2, using a dialysis technique, and monitored over three weeks. The in-vitro release curves of DOX from nanoparticles are demonstrated in Fig. 5d. The UV-Vis absorbance measurements at 480 nm of released DOX from the nanomotors within the dialysis tube indicate that Janus nanomotors serve as an effective carrier for the entrapment of the anticancer drug DOX. As shown in Fig. 5d, only 37.26% of DOX was released from the Janus nanomotors after 24 h at pH 7.4 and 37 °C. In contrast, release percentages increased to approximately 49.90%, 58.05%, and 59.49% under pH 5.8 at 37 °C, pH 7.4 at 43 °C, and pH 5.8 at 43 °C, respectively. These findings underscore a higher drug release rate at pH 5.8 compared to pH 7.4 and at 43 °C compared to 37 °C, indicative of the drug’s solubility increasing by both lower pH and elevated temperature conditions. Research has indicated that both high temperatures and acidic pH levels can trigger drug release56.

Hervault et al. demonstrated that strong DOX-polymer matrix interactions—rather than temperature alone—limit drug release from magnetic nanocarriers, with only minimal DOX liberation at physiological pH/temperature. The Higuchi model confirmed diffusion-controlled kinetics through the dense polymer shell as the rate-limiting step57.

In addition, many DOX–magnetic systems show only moderate enhancement of release when increasing temperature from physiological to mild hyperthermia (37–41/43°C), because these temperatures are below the range that causes major structural rearrangements of the matrix58. On the other hand, hyperthermia‑triggered systems based on polymer‑coated magnetic nanorods also reached ~ 50% DOX release within several hours, and this plateau is due to the limited expansion of the coating under clinically acceptable temperature elevations59.

DOX continues to be a cornerstone chemotherapeutic in 2025 clinical guidelines, routinely used for different cancers, often in combination regimens. Recent studies demonstrate Pt-DOX nanoparticles achieve 8-fold enhanced efficacy against colon and breast cancer cells via improved targeting and reduced resistance, directly supporting our nanomotor design60. Also, a study by Norouzi et al. on the release of DOX from iron oxide nanoparticles demonstrated a burst release of approximately 42% at pH 7.4, alongside an accelerated initial release of up to around 64% at pH 4.5 during the first few hours61. Hervault et al. studied DOX-loaded magnetic nanocomposites consisting of an iron oxide core. They reported that the DOX release after 8 h at pH 7.4 and 25 °C was 13.6%, whereas at pH 5.7 and 50 °C, it was 57.8%57. Another study by Shen et al. on DOX-loaded superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) coated with PEGylated phospholipid demonstrated a 54% DOX release under physiological conditions (pH 7.4), during only two days62. Haji Ali et al. also reported that nearly 50% of the encapsulated DOX in the magnetic Janus nanoparticles was released over 168 h at pH 7.46, which aligns closely with our findings. Furthermore, the release profile was evaluated in the presence of 0.1% hydrogen peroxide, revealing an enhancement in drug release at 43 °C −62.10% at pH 7.4 versus 71.37% at pH 5.8 over 24 h. The introduction of H2O2 activates the catalyzing properties of platinum within the nanomotors, facilitating the reduction of hydrogen peroxide to oxygen and water. The generated oxygen bubbles create turbulence within the nanomotors matrix, thus accelerating the exit of the drug molecules and resulting in a faster release rate compared to scenarios devoid of H2O2.

Investigation of the motion behavior of the Janus nanomotors

Platinum is one of the best-known catalysts for various chemical reactions and plays an important role in other industries, such as electronics and medicine. In this project, because Pt is present as a catalyst in the synthesized Janus nanoparticles, the motion behavior of nanoparticles was monitored in different percentages of hydrogen peroxide fuel to produce oxygen bubbles during a redox (reduction-oxidation) reaction. The catalytic decomposition of H2O2 leads to self-propelling nanomotors. The motility of these nanomotors was first investigated without fuel (Video S1) and then in the presence of 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, and 2% w/v of H2O2 by optical microscopy (videos S2- S5), and the time-lapse images are demonstrated in Figure S9. The tracking trajectories of the nanomotors, as depicted in Fig. 6a, reveal a distinctly straight-line movement for approximately 5 s. We calculated their velocity to evaluate the impact of varying hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) concentrations on the nanomotors’ motility. Without H2O2, the nanoparticles displayed a mere dispersive Brownian motion, with a modest velocity of 0.69 μm/s. However, the synthesized Janus nanoparticles demonstrated significantly enhanced propulsion, with velocities recorded at 3.03 ± 0.12 μm/s, 5.88 ± 0.12 μm/s, 10.05 ± 0.31 μm/s, and 11.02 ± 0.24 μm/s for H2O2 concentrations of 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, and 2% w/v, respectively. This data indicates a linear relationship between the nanomotors’ velocities and the concentration of H2O2 used as fuel, underscoring the importance of fuel concentration in enhancing locomotion.

The particle tracking trajectories of Janus nanoparticles without and with different concentrations of H2O2 (a), distributions of mean square displacement values obtained for individual nanoparticles at the time scale Δt = 5 s (b), and the diffusion coefficients (c) for nanoparticle trajectories.

The mean-square displacements (MSDs) of the particles in the presence of various concentrations of H2O2 were calculated from particle tracking to gain further insight into nanomotors’ motion (Fig. 6b). It was found that MSD data increased exponentially as the hydrogen peroxide concentration increased. The diffusion coefficient of the tracked nanoparticles is presented in Fig. 6c. The Stokes-Einstein (SE) equation was applied to compute these values, considering the MSD function in different fuel concentrations. The diffusion coefficient of the nanoparticles was enhanced from 8.19 µm2/s for about 0.1% fuel concentration to 105.38 µm2/s for 2% H2O2 concentration.

Focusing more on the results, it can be found that increasing the fuel concentration from 1% to 2% caused the velocity data to be too close to each other. So, increasing the amount of fuel (more than 1%) does not significantly increase the movement speed of nanoparticles and keeps its speed relatively constant.

Notably, that 1% H₂O₂ (~ 290 mM) is substantially higher than physiological or even tumor-associated H₂O₂ levels (typically < 0.2 mM) and may induce cytotoxicity in cellular environments. However, we emphasize that motility assays were performed in buffer as part of the fundamental physicochemical characterization of catalytic propulsion—a standard approach in the nanomotor field to establish baseline performance63–65.

It is well-established that the interstitial environment of tumors tends to be more acidic than that of healthy tissues. To investigate the effect of acidic conditions on the movement of Janus nanoparticles, an experiment was conducted in which the nanoparticles were incubated in media with a pH value of 5.8 (Video S6- S9). The motility of these nanomotors was performed in the presence of 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, and 2% w/v of H2O2. Their movement was recorded using optical microscopy, revealing that the path traveled by nanomotors was enhanced compared to the normal condition. The tracking trajectories of the nanomotors are shown in Fig. 7a. Based on trajectory data, the estimated velocities of the nanomotors were 8.20 ± 0.24, 11.08 ± 0.11, 15.18 ± 0.21, and 16.46 ± 0.34 μm/s for 0.1, 0.2, 1, and 2% of hydrogen peroxide concentrations. These results reveal that the nanomotors moved faster than in the PBS medium. Figure 7b illustrates the MSD curve of the particles at various concentrations of H2O2. A decrease in pH resulted in an increase in MSD data compared to the PBS medium. Furthermore, as expected, the data increased with higher concentrations of H2O2. The results demonstrate that the diffusion coefficient of Janus nanomotors, such as motion velocity and MSD data, increases as the incident pH decreases and H2O2 concentration increases (Fig. 7c).

Nanomotor-cell interactions: biological testing

Diagnostic behavior by MRI

Based on MTT results (Fig. 9a and b), when treated with C6 tumor cells, the cytotoxic activity of DOX (IC50: 45 ± 0.1 µg/mL) was found to be significantly (P-value < 0.0001) lower than that of the DOX-loaded nanomotors (IC50: 4 ± 0.10 µg/mL). This may be due to the DOX-resistant nature of C6 tumor cells68. However, the cytotoxic activity of DOX and DOX-loaded nanomotor s in OLN-93 cells was significantly higher than in C6 cells (P-value < 0.0001). The cellular uptake of nanomotors in C6 cells was significantly higher than in OLN-93 cells (P-value < 0.001). This difference may be attributed to the presence of FA receptors on the surface of C6 cells69, facilitating the transport of nanoparticles into the cells through receptor-mediated endocytosis70.

For the in-vitro study, the IC10 concentration of nanomotors was chosen to eliminate 10% of the cells at 0.1 µg/mL concentration. Accordingly, a control sample of distilled water was placed in a self-designed water phantom. A T2-weighted MRI of the phantom confirmed the presence of a significant difference between the signal intensity of IC10 concentration of nanomotors and water, 698.15 ± 28.10, in contrast to 1043.97 ± 54.68 (Fig. 9c and d). Existing literature supports the theranostic effect of nanoparticles decorated with iron oxide2 and the superparamagnetic iron oxide core functions as an established T2-weighted MRI contrast agent (FDA-approved precedents like Feridex), enabling real-time imaging and magnetic navigation71. This feature reduces T2-weighted signals, darkening MR images. By loading a chemotherapy drug, such as DOX, it can effectively act as a negative T2-weighted MRI contrast agent for both diagnosis and cancer cell treatment simultaneously.

Cell uptake of nanomotors

The internalization of the nanomotors into cancer and normal cells was assessed using the ICP-OES and the Prussian blue staining analysis. To this end, we used C6 and OLN-93 cell lines, which are FA-receptor positive and negative, respectively. The quantitative ICP-OES data showed that the cellular uptake of Fe in C6 and OLN-93 cells was 423.52 ± 12.72 and 298.67 ± 8.97 pg/cell, respectively. Our results suggest an enhanced cellular uptake of targeted nanomotors into C6 cells compared to OLN-93 (Fig. 9e).

To determine the suitability of nanomotors with and without DOX for intravenous administration, the blood biocompatibility was evaluated at concentrations ranging from 0.015625 to 2.00 mg/mL (Fig. 9f). Deionized water and PBS were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. The results showed a concentration-dependent increase in hemolysis for the nanomotors. Nevertheless, the hemolysis percentages for all tested concentrations remained below the acceptable threshold of 5% for hemolytic activity72(Fig. 9g). These findings indicate that the nanomotors exhibit good blood compatibility and are therefore suitable candidates for intravenous delivery.

The internalization of nanoparticles in both cell lines, in the presence and absence of FA, was confirmed by qualitative images obtained from Prussian blue staining (Fig. 10). These images were taken with a 40X lens. Although non-Pt and non-Janus controls were not synthesized in this study, we emphasize that the Janus architecture provides unique synergy. However, extensive literature confirms Pt’s unique role in H₂O₂ catalysis for nanomotor propulsion, while Fe₃O₄ alone lacks such activity73. Our motility data (1% H₂O₂ propulsion) directly isolates this contribution, as non-catalytic particles remain static.

Fe3O4 (magnetite)74, and Pt (platinum)75,76 nanoparticles are recognized for their inherent peroxidase-like activity. For example, Gao et al.77 first demonstrated that Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles exhibit intrinsic peroxidase-like activity with measurable Km values for TMB and H₂O₂. In another study, similar findings were reported by Hasan et al.78, who showed that Pt decoration of Fe₃O₄ boosts peroxidase-like activity under physiologically relevant conditions. More importantly, Ma et al. directly compared Janus Fe₃O₄–Pt nanoparticles with Fe₃O₄-only and Pt-only controls and reported significantly enhanced TMB oxidation only in the integrated Janus system, confirming that the catalytic synergy arises from the Pt–Fe₃O₄ interface49. Thus, Fe3O4@Pt hybrid nanoparticles effectively combine the advantages of both precious metals and magnetic materials. This synergy results in superior catalytic performance compared to the individual metals alone79. Thus, we applied the synergistic effect of the inherent peroxidase-like activity of Ac/FA-PHEMA-g-PCL MNP Janus nanomotors, which effectively catalyzed the oxidation of TMB by hydrogen peroxide, resulting in a color change. To optimize conditions, we conducted tests at various time intervals, tested a range of concentrations of TMB and H2O2, and performed them in an appropriate pH of acetate buffer (pH ~ 4)80 and at room temperature. Since the literature has proven that the aberrant metabolism of cancer cells in solid tumors can produce endogenous acidic H2O2 (ranging from approximately 5 × 10− 5 to 10− 4 M)64, a minimum concentration of H2O2 to accurately evaluate the function of nanomotors in a simulated environment at 37 °C.

Limitations

Regarding kinetic parameter quantification, the Michaelis–Menten constants (Km, Vmₐₓ), as well as evaluation of cytotoxicity or oxidative damage associated with H₂O₂ exposure have not been addressed in the study and should be considered for future optimization of this platform. Furthermore, animal studies, along with investigation of tumor accumulation data, biodistribution, clearance, and protein corona profiling (e.g., via SDS-PAGE or LC–MS) are essential for subsequent research.

Taken together, our designed nanomotors appear to mitigate adverse biological interactions for the following reasons:

- Surface engineering for colloidal and biological stability: The nanomotor is functionalized with a graft copolymer combining hydrophilic poly(2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate) (PHEMA) and hydrophobic polycaprolactone (PCL). PHEMA provides a hydrated, biocompatible shell that reduces nonspecific protein adsorption—similar to PEG-based stealth coatings—while the FA ligand is conjugated to preserve active targeting. This amphiphilic architecture is known to modulate protein corona composition, favoring adsorption of dysopsonins (e.g., albumin) over opsonins, thereby potentially extending circulation time81.

Conclusions

This study focuses on the design, synthesis, and investigation of novel theranostic magnetite nanomotors aimed at delivering DOX and potentially being catalytically driven. For this purpose, magnetite nanoparticles were prepared using the coprecipitation technique and modified with APTES. In a separate process, poly (2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate)-graft-poly(ε-caprolactone) (PHEMA-g-PCL) was synthesized. This synthesis involved two steps: first, producing PCL macromer through ring-opening polymerization of ε-caprolactone in the presence of 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate as an initiator, followed by the free-radical polymerization of the PCL macromer in the presence of HEMA. Folic acid which serves as a ligand for targeting tumor cells that overexpress folate receptors, was covalently conjugated to the graft copolymer. Ultimately, the FA-PHEMA-g-PCL was functionalized with acryloyl chloride. AC/FA-PHEMA-g-PCL MNPs were obtained by Michael addition reaction between AMNP and AC/FA-PHEMA-g-PCL. Catalytically-driven Janus nanomotors were created by reducing the Pt salt on one hemisphere of AC/FA-PHEMA-g-PCL MNPs using a Pickering emulsion method. The motion behavior of Janus nanomotors was investigated at varying concentrations of hydrogen peroxide as a fuel under two different conditions (pH values at about 7.4 and 5.4). The results indicated that in both scenarios examined, increasing the fuel concentration resulted in faster motility of nanomotors. However, a decrease in pH at the same concentration of H2O2 led to even greater motility of the nanomotors. According to the findings, Ac/FA-PHEMA-g-PCL MNP Janus nanomotors exhibited an acceptable loading capacity. The platinum-based nanomotors exhibited sustained release of their drug payload, indicating significant therapeutic potential for efficient cancer treatment. Additionally, they demonstrated exceptional enzyme-mimetic activity and autonomous propulsion, enabling directed movement through hydrogen peroxide gradients in microenvironments such as tumor sites. This capability confers a distinct advantage over other nanomotor types, as it facilitates targeted drug delivery and deep penetration into solid tumors, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy.
