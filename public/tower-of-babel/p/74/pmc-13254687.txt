Enlarged perivascular spaces in subcortical white matter are linked to amyloid–tau depositions and cognitive decline: Data from a memory clinic in Thailand
Authors: Kittithatch Booncharoen, Poosanu Thanapornsangsuth, Thanakit Pongpitakmetha, Yuthachai Sarutikriangkri, Yuttachai Likitjaroen, Sekh Thanprasertsuk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONEnlarged perivascular spaces (EPVSs) have been implicated in Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathogenesis, but their relationships with amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau pathology remain unclear.METHODSWe conducted a cross‐sectional study of 51 patients with amnestic mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia from a memory clinic in Thailand. EPVS severity in centrum semiovale (CSO) and basal ganglia (BG) was rated on T1‐weighted magnetic resonance imaging. Aβ burden and tau deposition were assessed using positron emission tomography imaging. Cognitive change was measured using longitudinal Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores.RESULTSHigher CSO‐EPVS severity was associated with greater Aβ and tau burden, particularly when CSO‐EPVS exceeded BG‐EPVS severity. No significant associations were observed for BG‐EPVS. Greater CSO‐EPVS severity was also linked to greater decline in follow‐up MoCA scores.DISCUSSIONCSO‐EPVS may reflect region‐specific processes linked to AD pathology, while their association with cognitive decline may be mediated by underlying amyloid and tau burden.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, amyloid beta, enlarged perivascular space, positron emission tomography, tau

BACKGROUND

Enlarged perivascular spaces (EPVSs) are fluid‐filled structures surrounding small vessels in the brain parenchyma, readily identifiable on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Once regarded as incidental findings, EPVSs have accumulated increasing attention for their potential role in neurodegenerative diseases, particularly Alzheimer's disease (AD). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 The biological significance of EPVSs has been linked to impaired interstitial fluid drainage, vascular dysfunction, and neuroinflammation, all of which are hypothesized to contribute to the accumulation of pathological proteins such as amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau.

Despite these associations, the relationship between EPVSs and AD pathology remains incompletely understood. Previous studies have yielded mixed results regarding the link between EPVS severity and Aβ burden, and few studies have investigated tau pathology in this context. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Moreover, the implications of EPVS distribution across brain regions, such as the centrum semiovale (CSO) and basal ganglia (BG), have been largely unexplored in relation to AD pathology.

This study aims to investigate the association between EPVS topographic distribution and the burden of Aβ and tau pathology in memory clinic patients with amnestic mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or mild dementia. Using [18F]‐florbetaben positron emission tomography (PET) for Aβ, and PI‐2620 PET for tau, quantifications, we hypothesize that EPVS severity and distribution patterns may correlate with the burden of AD pathological markers, helping explain potential mechanisms underlying EPVSs in AD pathogenesis. We also aim to investigate whether EPVS severity and distribution patterns are associated with cognitive changes over a 2‐year follow‐up period.

METHODS

Participants

Participants with amnestic MCI and mild dementia were consecutively recruited from all new patients presenting to the Memory Clinic, King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital, Bangkok, Thailand, between October 2021 and August 2022, who provided informed consent, and no additional sampling strategy was applied. MCI was defined as objective cognitive impairment (test scores ≤ −1.5 standard deviation below age‐ and education‐adjusted norms) with a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) of 0 or 0.5. Mild dementia was defined by the same cognitive test criteria as MCI but with a CDR of 1. Clinical assessment was performed by neurologists specialized in neurocognitive disorders, accompanied by neurocognitive assessment batteries administered by clinical neuropsychologists. Brain MRI was performed as part of the routine diagnostic workup at the first memory clinic visit.

Patients were excluded if they had cognitive impairment attributable to an established cause, including substantial neurological disorders (e.g., epilepsy, encephalitis, and brain tumor), severe systemic disease or organ failure (e.g., advanced liver cirrhosis, stage 4–5 chronic kidney disease), uncontrolled psychiatric disorders, or major structural abnormalities on neuroimaging. Predefined MRI exclusion criteria included: (1) white matter hyperintensities with a Fazekas score ≥ 2, (2) large territorial infarction, (3) lacunar infarctions in locations potentially contributing to cognitive or behavioral symptoms, and (4) microbleeds consistent with probable cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) according to the Boston criteria version 2.0 (i.e., ≥ 2 strictly lobar microbleeds or other supportive hemorrhagic markers). 9 Cerebral microbleeds were assessed using susceptibility‐weighted imaging sequences within the routine MRI protocol. PET imaging was subsequently performed in all recruited participants.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The use of human subjects in this study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of the Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University, Thailand (IRB NO. 221/64). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All methods in this study were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1975.

PET imaging procedures

All participants underwent Aβ PET imaging conducted at the National Cyclotron and PET Centre at Chulabhorn Hospital, Bangkok, using [18F]‐florbetaben (Neuraceq) PET/computed tomography, adhering to standardized protocols. 10 Assessment of Aβ burden was determined using the Centiloid scale (CL). The pipeline analysis conducted at Chulabhorn Hospital demonstrated a robust correlation with previously published CL data, exhibiting a strong coefficient of determination (R 2 = 0.9972). 11 , 12

Tau PET imaging was conducted at same center as Aβ PET, using [18F]PI‐2620. This was carried out on a simultaneous PET/MRI 3T scanner manufactured by Siemens Healthcare GmbH, with an injected activity averaging 198.96 ± 24.17 MBq (range: 148–263.81 MBq). The processing of [18F]PI‐2620 images into the Montreal Neurological Institute space was achieved through the automatic processing function within the PNEURO pipeline (PMOD V4.101, PMOD Technologies Ltd.). Regional tau PET values were determined by calculating the ratio of activity in combined areas associated with tau stages III to IV, including parahippocampal, fusiform, amygdala, middle, and inferior temporal cortices, to the activity in the reference region (inferior cerebellum).

All PET image analyses were performed by nuclear medicine specialists blinded to MRI‐based EPVS assessments and clinical data.

Assessment of EPVS

Severity of EPVSs was evaluated on magnetization‐prepared rapid gradient echo T1 structural MRI images as previously described, using a 4‐point semiquantitative scale, with scores classified as high degree (degree > 2) or low degree (degree ≤ 2) in the CSO (CSO‐EPVS) and in the BG region (BG‐EPVS). 13 Briefly, CSO‐EPVS were graded as degree 1 if < 10 EPVSs were present in total CSO white matter; degree 2 for > 10 EPVSs in total CSO white matter but < 10 in the slice with the most EPVSs; degree 3 for between 10 and 20 EPVSs in the slice with the most EPVSs; and degree 4 for > 20 EPVS in the slice with the most EPVSs. BG‐EPVS severity was categorized as follows: degree 1 if < 5 EPVSs were observed in the BG region; degree 2 for 5 to 10 EPVSs; degree 3 for > 10 EPVSs but still countable; and degree 4 for an extensive number of EPVSs resulting in a cribriform change in BG. All T1‐weighted images were acquired with a slice thickness of 1.0 mm and no interslice gap. Additionally, all identified EPVSs were visually cross‐validated on corresponding T2‐weighted images to confirm cerebrospinal fluid–like signal characteristics.

To ensure the reliability of EPVS severity assessments, each patient's EPVS severity was independently rated twice by the same trained assessor blinded to clinical information and PET imaging results, with a 2‐week interval between the two assessments to minimize recall bias. During each session, the assessor completed the grading for all 51 patients within a single day to maintain consistency in evaluation conditions. The intra‐rater agreements, determined using Cohen kappa, were very good for both CSO‐EPVS (κ = 0.89 [95% confidence interval (CI) 0.79–0.99]) and BG‐EPVS (κ = 0.87 [95% CI 0.74–0.99]). In cases in which discrepancies were observed between the first and second ratings, the EPVS severity was re‐evaluated on the day after the second assessment. For this re‐evaluation, the assessor was made aware of the results from both prior assessments to reconcile the discordant ratings and ensure the most accurate final grading.

In addition to rating EPVS severity in BG and CSO, we classified EPVS severity comparatively between the regions as follows: BG greater than CSO (BG > CSO), BG less than CSO (BG < CSO), or similar severity (BG = CSO) to explored regional predominance patterns of EPVS burden. 14 This categorization was intended to provide a relative measure of regional predominance of EPVSs, rather than a direct comparison between the regions. Given that EPVSs in the BG have been more consistently associated with hypertensive arteriopathy, while EPVSs in the CSO have been linked to AD and CAA, 3 , 4 , 15 , 16 this approach may help to partially disentangle hypertensive arteriopathy from AD‐related mechanisms underlying EPVS burden.

Follow‐up cognitive assessment

Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores with memory index scores (MISs) were reassessed ≈ 2 years after baseline for most participants. 17 The change in MoCA and MoCA‐MIS was calculated as the difference between follow‐up and baseline scores, with negative values indicating cognitive decline and positive values suggesting improvement. Participants who were lost to follow‐up or did not undergo repeat MoCA testing were excluded from related analyses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 29 (IBM Corp.). Univariate analyses were used to assess differences in demographic data, clinical characteristics, Aβ CL, and tau standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) across EPVS categories. To examine independent associations between EPVS severity and Aβ CL and medial temporal tau SUVR, multivariable regression models were constructed adjusting for age, sex, and baseline MoCA score. For analyses of the change in MoCA score between baseline and follow‐up, regression models were adjusted for age and sex but not baseline MoCA score, as baseline MoCA score is mathematically incorporated into the outcome measure. To further explore whether the association between EPVS and cognitive decline was independent of underlying AD pathology, an additional exploratory model was constructed adjusting for Aβ CL and tau SUVR in addition to age and sex. Additionally, to account for potential confounding effects of small vessel disease risk factors, a subgroup analysis was performed excluding patients with a history of hypertension or diabetes. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

Fifty‐one participants with amnestic MCI and mild dementia were enrolled. The mean age was 70.7 ± 8.5 years, with 15 (29.4%) males and 36 (70.6%) females. The median (interquartile range) MoCA score at baseline and on follow‐up visit were 20 (15, 24) and 20 (14, 23), respectively. With the median follow‐up duration of 729 (715, 757) days, there were 10 missing follow‐up MoCA assessments: six transferred follow‐up care to another hospital or had relocated, two were lost to follow‐up, and two were unable to undergo testing due medical illnesses unrelated to cognitive impairment. The mean change in MoCA score was –2.5 ± 4.1. The median MoCA‐MIS at baseline and on follow‐up visit were 5 (3, 9), and 4 (2, 9), respectively, while the mean change in MoCA‐MIS was −1.1 ± 3.5.

The median Aβ CL and temporal lobe tau SUVR were 51.80 (15.13, 80.27) and 1.24 (1.07, 1.58), respectively. In our study population, Aβ CL and tau SUVR were moderately correlated (ρ = 0.62, p value < 0.001). Association between EPVS severity and AD pathology

For univariate analyses, patients with a high degree of CSO‐EPVS demonstrated a higher Aβ CL (p value = 0.005) compared to those with low CSO‐EPVS severity, while tau SUVR was not different (p value = 0.06) between the two groups. However, multivariate analyses adjusting for age, sex, and baseline MoCA score demonstrated higher values of both Aβ CL (B = 3.06 [95% CI 1.47–4.65], p value < 0.001) and tau SUVR (B = 1.56 [95% CI 0.06–0.25], p value = 0.001) in patients with a high degree of CSO‐EPVS (Table 1). On the other hand, there were no differences of Aβ CL and tau SUVR between patients with high and low degree BG‐EPVS. Subgroup analysis of patients without hypertension or diabetes

In the subgroup of patients without a history of hypertension or diabetes (n = 30), those with a high degree of CSO‐EPVS (n = 12) exhibited significantly higher Aβ CL (B = 3.12 [95% CI 0.91–5.33], p value = 0.008) and tau SUVR (B = 0.15 [95% CI 0.02–0.28], p value = 0.025) compared to those with low CSO‐EPVS severity (n = 18; Table 2). However, no significant differences in Aβ CL or tau SUVR were observed between patients with high and low degrees of BG‐EPVS. In this subgroup, there was a trend toward differences in Aβ CL (B = 1.19 [95% CI −0.06–2.44], p value = 0.062) and tau SUVR (B = 0.07 [95% CI 0–0.14], p value = 0.05) among the three EPVS categories (BG > CSO, BG = CSO, BG < CSO; Table 2), though these did not reach statistical significance.

Association between EPVS severity and change in MoCA score over 2 years

No significant difference was found in the change in MoCA scores between the low and high BG‐EPVS severity groups (Table 1, Figure 2A). However, in a univariate analysis, patients with severe CSO‐EPVS showed a significantly greater decline in MoCA scores over 2 years compared to those with lower CSO‐EPVS severity (mean change: −4.3 ± 4.6 vs. −1.4 ± 3.4, p value = 0.043; Table 1, Figure 2B). A multivariate analysis adjusting for age and sex further confirmed this significant difference in MoCA score decline between the low and high CSO‐EPVS groups (B = −2.98 [95% CI −5.68–2.77], p value = 0.032; Table 1). In contrast, changes in MoCA‐MIS over 2 years showed no significant differences between low and high severity groups for either BG‐EPVS or CSO‐EPVS (Table 1).

In an additional model adjusting for Aβ CL and tau SUVR in addition to age and sex, the association between CSO‐EPVS severity and change in MoCA score was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (B = −1.36 [95% CI −4.11–1.39], p value = 0.32).

Association between EPVS topographic distribution (BG > CSO, BG = CSO, and BG < CSO groups) and change in MoCA score over 2 years

There was a trend toward a difference in MoCA score changes over 2 years among the three EPVS categories (B = −1.55 [95% CI −3.11–0.01], p value = 0.051; Table 1, Figure 2C). DISCUSSION

Our findings demonstrate significant associations between EPVS severity, particularly in the CSO (CSO‐EPVS), and higher burdens of Aβ and tau pathology. Multivariate analyses revealed that patients with a high degree of CSO‐EPVS exhibited significantly elevated Aβ CL and temporal lobe tau SUVR compared to those with lower CSO‐EPVS severity. In contrast, EPVS severity in the BG was not associated with Aβ or tau burdens, suggesting a region‐specific relationship. These results suggest that CSO‐EPVS may reflect processes related to AD pathogenesis.

The results align with prior studies suggesting a relationship between CSO‐EPVS and burdens of Aβ and tau pathology, supporting the hypothesis that subcortical white matter changes are integral to AD pathogenesis. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 These are also consistent with the prior finding that Aβ and tau in AD prefer to deposit in subcortical white matter than in deep gray nuclei. 18 , 19 In addition, along with Braak and Braak staging, Aβ and tau tend to deposit in deep gray structure later in the disease course compared to the cerebral hemisphere. 20 , 21 The result also supports the hypothesis that the pathogenesis of BG‐EPVS may be related to cerebral small vessel disease rather than proteinopathies. 22

However, several studies did not demonstrate associations between EPVS severity and Aβ accumulation measured by PET scan or cerebrospinal fluid analysis. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Differences in study populations may account for this inconsistency. However, our study exclusively included cognitively impaired patients with substantial correlation between Aβ CL and tau SUVR; other studies often included cognitively unimpaired individuals or those with vascular cognitive impairment, potentially diluting associations between EPVS and AD pathology.

An additional explanation for the observed association between CSO‐EPVS and Aβ burden is the potential contribution of CAA. CSO‐EPVS are recognized as an imaging feature associated with CAA, and are incorporated as a supportive marker in Boston criteria version 2.0. Even without overt hemorrhagic markers such as lobar microbleeds or cortical superficial siderosis, early or subclinical CAA pathology may still be present, especially in patients with underlying AD. 9 Thus, whether the association between CSO‑EPVS severity and amyloid burden is explained by AD, CAA, or both remains uncertain. Nevertheless, our findings support at least a partial relationship between CSO‐EPVS and AD pathology, as CSO‐EPVS severity was associated not only with global amyloid burden but also with temporal lobe tau burden measured by PET. However, the contribution of early or subclinical CAA cannot be entirely excluded, and warrants further study using detailed hemorrhagic imaging markers and pathological confirmation.

Additionally, our findings align with prior studies suggesting that EPVS severity may have prognostic implications. The observed greater MoCA decline in patients with severe CSO‐EPVS, but without substantial levels of white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), supports the hypothesis that CSO‐EPVS may be associated with disease progression. This is consistent with longitudinal data demonstrating that perivascular space burden, particularly in the CSO, predicts progression in MCI. 23 However, when Aβ CL and tau SUVR were included as covariates, the association between CSO‐EPVS and cognitive decline was no longer significant. This finding suggests that the relationship between CSO‐EPVS and cognitive decline may be mediated by underlying AD pathology. Given the cross‐sectional imaging data and the complex interplay between vascular and neurodegenerative processes, it remains uncertain whether EPVSs represent a downstream consequence, or a parallel marker of amyloid and tau accumulation. From a clinical perspective, in settings where molecular imaging is unavailable, the presence of severe CSO‐EPVS on structural MRI may still pragmatically indicate risk of more rapid cognitive decline, although this should be interpreted with caution.

Interestingly, we also observed a higher prevalence of diabetes in groups with severe BG‐EPVS. This finding corroborates prior research linking BG‐EPVS to insulin resistance and diabetes, 24 , 25 suggesting that metabolic factors may contribute to EPVS development in the BG.

The subgroup analysis excluding patients with hypertension or diabetes provided additional insights into the relationship between EPVS and AD pathology. Among this subgroup, we observed that a high degree of CSO‐EPVS remained significantly associated with higher Aβ CL and tau SUVR in univariate analyses, suggesting that these associations are not solely driven by vascular dysfunctions in hypertension or diabetes. This finding strengthens the hypothesis that CSO‐EPVS may reflect mechanisms intrinsic to AD pathogenesis, such as tau‐ and amyloid‐related Wallerian degeneration, neuroinflammation, and impaired glymphatic clearance, 26 rather than being a mere consequence of vascular dysfunction. However, in multivariate analyses, the differences in Aβ CL and tau SUVR among the three EPVS categories (BG > CSO, BG = CSO, and BG < CSO) were not statistically significant. This discrepancy may reflect the reduced statistical power in the smaller subgroup.

Despite the insights provided, our study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size limits the generalizability of our findings. Replication in larger cohorts with more heterogeneous disease severity is essential to validate these results. Second, the study population consisted of patients with amnestic MCI or mild dementia who, by clinical characteristics, did not exhibit moderate‐to‐severe WMHs (Fazekas score ≥ 2) or MRI features consistent with probable CAA. While this homogeneity may reduce confounding from advanced small vessel disease, it may also limit the applicability of our findings to cognitively impaired populations with more prominent vascular pathology. This cohort characteristic may partially explain the lack of association between BG‐EPVS severity and hypertension observed in our study, which differs from findings reported in populations enriched for subcortical vascular lesions.

Third, other potential confounders related to small vessel disease (e.g., smoking status and medication use) were not comprehensively controlled for in our analyses. Given the established links between small vessel disease and EPVS development, future studies should incorporate detailed adjustments for these factors. Fourth, the semi‐quantitative grading of EPVS, while widely used, may introduce subjectivity. Although we minimized this by using blinded, repeated assessments with high intra‐rater reliability, automated quantitative methods could enhance objectivity and reproducibility in future research. Last, incorporation of correlation analyses with fluid biomarkers will also be useful to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationships between EPVS and AD pathology.

CONCLUSIONS

CSO‐EPVS severity is associated with greater amyloid and tau burden and with cognitive decline in patients with amnestic MCI and mild dementia. However, the association with cognitive decline appears to be influenced by underlying AD pathology, highlighting the need for longitudinal studies to clarify causal relationships.
