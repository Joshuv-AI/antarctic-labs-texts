Correlation between atherogenic index of plasma and retinal vessels in the fundus: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Zhenyan Wu, Yu Xin, Xue Jiang, Guoyong Zhang, Saiguang Ling, Caixia Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Atherogenic Index of Plasma (AIP) is a novel logarithmic index that combines fasting triglyceride and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) concentrations and is associated with the burden of atherosclerosis. Currently, the noninvasive nature of fundus photography combined with the rapid development of deep learning techniques for quantitative retinal microvascular parameters is advancing rapidly. The aim of this study was to evaluate the correlation between retinal vascular characteristics and AIP using quantitative analysis of retinal vessels in the fundus, thereby further exploring the relationship between glucose and lipid metabolism and atherosclerotic markers with microvascular sclerosis.MethodsThis study was based on a cohort of 651 inpatients from the Cardiovascular Center database of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University. Patients were divided into four groups (Q1-Q4) according to the quartiles of the AIP index. Fundus images were captured using a nonmydriatic 45° fundus camera for each patient. A deep learning–based segmentation model was employed to perform fully automated quantitative measurements of retinal vascular parameters on color fundus photographs (CFP), yielding multiple quantitative fundus data points, including fractal dimension (FD), vessel density (VD), and mean blood vessel diameter (MBVD). Linear regression and subgroup analyses were conducted to investigate the correlation between retinal vascular characteristics and AIP.ResultsIn this study, significant differences were observed among the groups in terms of age, history of hyperlipidemia, diabetes, smoking, number of patients diagnosed with coronary artery disease (CAD) during the current admission, Gensini score from coronary angiography during the current admission, BMI, fasting blood glucose (FBG), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), HDL-C, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) (P < 0.05). In the quantitative retinal vascular results, significant differences were found among the groups in fractal dimension (FD), vessel density (VD), mean blood vessel diameter (MBVD), and mean venous diameter (MVD) (P < 0.05). Further comparisons revealed that FD differed significantly between Groups Q1 and Q4, as well as between Groups Q2 and Q4, while VD, MBVD, and MVD differed significantly between Groups Q1 and Q4 (P < 0.05). In the linear regression analysis between AIP levels and ocular quantitative parameters, after adjusting for age, sex, medical history, personal history, BMI, and results of glucose and lipid tests (Model 4), FD and VD were negatively correlated with AIP levels. In addition, the restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis indicated no significant nonlinear relationship between AIP and FD or VD (P for nonlinear > 0.05). In subgroup analyses, the associations between AIP and FD as well as VD were more pronounced in individuals aged ≥ 65 years (P for interaction < 0.05).ConclusionAIP levels are significantly negatively correlated with FD and VD in ocular quantitative results. Subgroup analyses revealed that in individuals aged ≥ 65 years, FD and VD are correlated with AIP levels, with significant interaction effects.Keywords: Atherogenic index of plasma, Retinal, Fundus, Heart

Introduction

The Atherogenic Index of Plasma (AIP) was introduced by Dobiásová et al [1]. in 2000 as a valuable metric defined as the logarithm of the ratio of serum triglycerides (TG) to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). This index quantitatively reflects the balance between atherogenic and anti-atherogenic lipoprotein components and has become a useful tool for assessing cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk associated with metabolic syndrome (MetS) [2]. Choudhary et al. [3] demonstrated that AIP is independently correlated with arterial stiffness in patients with normal blood pressure. Nam et al. [4] showed a significant association between AIP and the progression of coronary artery calcification (CAC) in subjects without cardiovascular disease (OR 2.27, 95% CI 1.61–3.19, P < 0.01). Huang et al. [5] reported in a Chinese community-based study that participants in the highest AIP quartile (Q4) had a higher incidence of carotid atherosclerosis compared to those in the lowest quartile (Q1) (OR 1.18, 95% CI 1.12–1.25). Zheng et al. [6] conducted a retrospective cohort study with a median follow-up of 11.03 years, revealing that cumulative exposure to AIP was associated with a higher risk of ischemic stroke. These studies highlight AIP as a notable biomarker for identifying atherosclerosis in plasma. In addition, AIP has been shown to be associated with periodontitis [7], renal calculi [8], and sarcopenia [9] in multiple studies. However, to date, no studies have explored the correlation between AIP and retinal vascular variables.

Recent advancements in retinal imaging technology have significantly enhanced the potential of the fundus vasculature as a vital window for assessing systemic vascular health. Notably, the retinal microvasculature is the only blood vessel system in the human body that can be directly visualized, offering a unique opportunity to investigate its potential associations with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases [10]. Studies have found that changes in the caliber of retinal microvessels not only reflect the status of microvascular function and perfusion but are also closely associated with systemic atherosclerosis [11–13]. Based on these findings, the retinal microvasculature, primarily composed of retinal arterioles and venules, provides an unprecedented opportunity for noninvasive visualization of the systemic microvasculature [14]. Meanwhile, advanced imaging software technologies have emerged, enabling precise measurement of subtle abnormalities in the retinal microvasculature [15]. Therefore, this study aims to analyze the correlation between AIP and quantitative retinal results based on the color fundus photographs (CFP), clarify the impact of AIP on retinal vessels, and elucidate their associations in different populations through subgroup analyses.

Patients and methods

Study population

This retrospective study analyzed 727 patients admitted to the Cardiovascular Center of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University, between January 2024 and September 2024, who underwent coronary angiography due to suspected angina symptoms. Patients were divided into four groups based on the quartiles of the AIP. The inclusion criteria were as follows: ① age above 18 years; ② underwent binocular CFP centered on the macula during hospitalization; ③ voluntarily signed the informed consent form. The exclusion criteria were as follows: ① patients with a history of severe valvular heart disease, heart failure, cardiomyopathy, or congenital heart disease; ② patients with acute or chronic infectious diseases or autoimmune diseases; ③ patients with a history of chronic renal failure, hepatitis, cancer, or steroid treatment; ④ patients with ophthalmic diseases affecting the fundus vessels and optic disc structure, such as retinal artery or vein occlusion, retinal macroaneurysm, glaucoma, or congenital optic disc anomalies; ⑤ patients with severe opacification of the ocular media affecting the quality of fundus photography; ⑥ patients who had undergone ophthalmic surgery within the past six months;⑦patients with incomplete clinical data. An a priori sample size calculation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). For the primary outcome of Mean branch Angle, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was planned to compare differences across the four quartile groups defined by the AIP. Based on pilot data (n = 68), a small-to-medium effect size (f = 0.1394) was estimated. With the α error probability set at 0.05 and statistical power at 0.80, the calculation indicated that a total sample size of 568 participants (142 per group) was required. The final cohort of 651 participants exceeded this requirement, ensuring adequate power for the analysis(calculated based on the variable with the largest required sample size).The screening process is shown in Fig. 1. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University (Ethics Approval Number: TREC2024-KY036), and all patients provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Data collection

Baseline data were collected for each patient, including personal information (name, gender, age, height and weight) and medical history (history of hyperlipidemia, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, alcohol consumption, etc.). Fasting blood samples were obtained within 24 h of admission to measure glucose and lipid levels, including Fasting Blood Glucose (FBG), Glycated Hemoglobin (HbA1c), Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)], TG, Total Cholesterol (TC), Low-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (LDL-C), and HDL-C. The AIP was calculated using the formula: AIP = log10 (TG/HDL-C). Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2).

Coronary angiography results from the current admission were collected to determine the presence of Coronary Artery Disease (CAD), defined as ≥ 50% stenosis in at least one vessel with a diameter ≥ 2 mm. The extent of coronary artery disease was quantified using the Gensini score16. The coronary artery tree was divided into 16 segments, with each segment assigned a score based on its functional importance (left main stem = 5 points, distal segments = 0.5 points). Each segment was scored according to the degree of stenosis (0%, 1–49%, 50–74%, 75–99%, or 100%), with the percentage reduction in lumen diameter scored as 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4, respectively, and the scores were weighted and summed to calculate the total Gensini score.

Fundus photography and quantitative analysis of retinal vascular morphology

In this study, 45° color fundus photography was performed using a nonmydriatic fundus camera (SYSEYE Kestrel 3100 m.). For the acquisition of CFP, the right eye was prioritized as the primary target, with the macula as the center of the imaging field. If the right eye was uncooperative or produced suboptimal image quality, the CFP from the left eye was used as an alternative. A single-field imaging approach was employed, with the midpoint between the macula and optic disc as the center of the imaging field, ensuring that the imaging area covered at least a 60° retinal region. Pupillary dilation was not required, and the entire process, including imaging both eyes, took approximately 30 s, with immediate results available after capture. The CFP images were analyzed using the EVisionAl fundus image analysis system (Yiwei Technology Co., Ltd., China) to quantify the morphological features of retinal vessels [15]. This system, based on the principles of human visual simulation, integrates advanced AI image processing techniques such as computer vision and deep learning. For the pre-processing of color CFP images, operations including region of interest (ROI) extraction, noise reduction, normalization, and enhancement were performed to remove nonfundus structures, minimize inter-image variability, and enhance the clarity of retinal feature edges [17]. The retinal vascular areas were manually modified and corrected by two senior ophthalmologists specializing in fundus diseases. One physician performed the initial correction, and the other reviewed and refined the annotations to produce the final ground-truth vascular images. The annotated images were randomly split into a training set and a validation set at a ratio of 8:2. The training set was fed into a ResNet101-U-Net architecture for model training. The loss value of the network model was computed on the validation set, and model parameters were iteratively optimized based on the loss values. Training was terminated when the loss values on both the training and validation sets ceased to decrease, yielding the final vascular segmentation model. This model was subsequently applied to perform vascular segmentation. Subsequently, the deep target detection network was combined with computer vision edge extraction algorithms to achieve precise segmentation of the optic disc and cup [18]. For peripapillary atrophy (PPA) and vessel identification, the software employed a deep learning segmentation network model, ResNet 101-UNet, to differentiate between arterial and venous vessels based on color, brightness, texture, connectivity, and topological relationships of retinal vessels [19]. Morphological parameters including Mean Branch Angle (MBA), Fractal Dimension (FD), Vessel Density (VD), Average Vessel Tortuosity (AVT), Average Arterial Curvature (AAC), Average Vein Curvature (AVC), Mean Blood Vessel Diameter (MBVD), Mean Arterial Diameter (MAD), Mean Venous Diameter (MVD), and Arteriovenous Ratio (AVR) were obtained based on the segmentation results. The model accuracy and specificity are both over 0.9 [15]. Leveraging this system, our study aims to utilize AI-based image processing to gain deeper insights into fundus abnormalities and achieve intelligent assessment and detection of the fundus. Statistical methods

Data organization and statistical analysis were performed using SPSS version 26.0 and R version 4.5.0. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared among multiple groups using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) [M(P25, P75)] and compared among multiple groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages (%) and compared among multiple groups using the chi-square test. If significant differences in fundus parameters were detected between groups, post hoc multiple comparisons were conducted, and violin plots were generated. Univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses were used to evaluate the correlation between AIP and quantitative fundus parameters. In addition, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed based on the results of the multivariate linear regression model (Model 4) to assess the nonlinear relationship between AIP and fundus parameters. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age (≥ 65 years), sex, medical history (presence of diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, CAD), and personal history (smoking). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with a significance level of α = 0.05.

Results

Among the 727 participants, 651 patients’ CFPs were included in this study after excluding those with poor-quality images, incomplete clinical data, nonmacular-centered CFPs, and those who had undergone ophthalmic surgery within the past six months (see Fig. 1). Individuals were divided into four groups based on AIP levels: Group 1 (n = 163) with AIP < − 0.14, Group 2 (n = 162) with − 0.14 ≤ AIP < 0.05, Group 3 (n = 163) with 0.05 ≤ AIP < 0.26, and Group 4 (n = 163) with AIP ≥ 0.26. Significant differences were observed among the groups in terms of age, history of hyperlipidemia, diabetes, smoking, number of patients diagnosed with CAD during the current admission, Gensini score from coronary angiography during the current admission, BMI, FBG, TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, and HbA1c (P < 0.05). Across Groups Q1 to Q4, the proportion of individuals with a history of diabetes and CAD increased progressively with increasing AIP levels (P < 0.05). Detailed information is presented in Table 1.

Discussion

The AIP is a novel logarithmic index that combines fasting triglyceride levels and HDL-C levels. Traditional biomarkers such as TC or LDL-C cannot fully explain the role of triglycerides in the formation of atherosclerosis. In contrast, AIP provides a more direct reflection of lipid imbalance and serves as a more comprehensive marker for atherosclerosis and cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease risk. Dobiášová et al. [1] initially proposed AIP as a marker of plasma atherogenicity and noted its negative correlation with LDL-C particle size. Studies have shown that small dense LDL (sdLDL) particles, a subtype of LDL-C, are more prone to oxidation. Oxidized LDL-C is engulfed by macrophages, which then differentiate into foam cells that more readily infiltrate and deposit in the arterial wall, contributing to the formation of atherosclerosis. This process is closely related to AIP [20]. Zhou et al. [21] demonstrated in a multivariate logistic regression analysis that AIP is an independent risk factor for CAD in diabetic patients. Wu et al. [22] showed a significant correlation between multivessel coronary artery disease and elevated AIP levels. Hu et al. [23] found that in male diabetic patients, AIP can serve as a reliable marker for assessing multivessel coronary artery disease, although no statistical significance was observed in females. Süleymanoğlu et al [24]. discovered that AIP is independently associated with no-reflow phenomenon in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction undergoing primary percutaneous coronary intervention, with significantly higher AIP values in the no-reflow group compared to the reflow group (0.50 [0.38–0.65] vs. 0.39 [0.25–0.49], p < 0.001). Assempoor et al. [25] conducted a meta-analysis indicating that elevated AIP is associated with higher incidences of stroke in patients with antibody-associated vasculitis [26] and myocardial infarction (MI) in patients with obstructive sleep apnea [27]. These studies confirm the correlation between AIP and atherosclerosis and reaffirm that AIP is considered a reliable indicator of atherosclerosis in multiple studies.

In the recent years, with the rapid development of retinal imaging technology, the fundus vasculature has garnered widespread attention as a potential biological window for reflecting systemic vascular health. The performance of nonmydriatic fundus photography has been proven to be comparable to that of traditional mydriatic photography, making the acquisition of retinal microvascular features more convenient [28, 29]. However, the commonly used retinal vascular parameter analysis software still has many limitations: although objective quantitative measurements can be achieved, they require manual data entry by professionals and repeated corrections, resulting in a lengthy operational process, low analysis efficiency, and the potential for human error. To address this issue, this study employed a fully automated analysis method based on deep learning and computer vision, achieving rapid and accurate measurements of multiple retinal microvascular parameters, including FD and VD, while ensuring the precision of vascular segmentation [19].

Our study found that FD and VD were significantly negatively correlated with AIP after multivariate adjustment, and this negative correlation was consistent across multiple subgroups. FD refers to the complexity of retinal vascular branching within a given area, reflecting the efficiency with which complex structures occupy space and serving as a measure of vascular irregularity. Previous studies have shown that lower FD is associated with the extent and severity of CAD [30]. For instance, Sarah B et al. [31] used a semi-automated software to obtain a series of quantitative retinal vascular geometric measurements from retinal photographs and found that lower FD was associated with higher severity of CAD. Fu et al. [32], in a study following 57,947 patients from the UK Biobank for a median of 11.0 years, demonstrated in a multivariate Cox proportional hazards model that FD was closely related to the incidence of CAD (HR = 0.80, 95% CI 0.65–0.98, p = 0.033). Dong et al. [33], through optical coherence tomography (OCT)/OCT angiography, found that retinal thinning and reduced VD were associated with the onset of CAD. Hannappe et al. [34] reported that patients hospitalized for acute coronary syndrome (ACS) had significantly lower retinal VD compared to those without cardiovascular risks (p = 0.004). Wang et al. [13] found that in the outer retina and choriocapillaris layer, retinal VD was significantly lower in CAD patients compared to healthy controls (p < 0.05), and that stenosis in the left main coronary artery, proximal left anterior descending artery, proximal left circumflex artery, and proximal right coronary artery was significantly associated with reduced retinal and choriocapillaris VD. Consequently, an increasing number of researchers in the field of cardiovascular disease are focusing their investigations on retinal vascular changes.

An increasing number of studies are exploring risk factors that impact retinal vasculature. Jiang et al. [35] developed a machine learning model and found significant differences in FD (P < 0.001) and VD (P < 0.001) among normal, prediabetic, and diabetic groups. He et al. [36] reported in an OCT-A–based study that individuals with LDL-C levels exceeding 2.6 mmol/L exhibited reduced VD in the retina (including the optic disc and macular regions), with a negative correlation between LDL-C levels and retinal VD. Wan et al. [37] demonstrated in a study on the association between the TyG index and diabetic retinopathy (DR) that the TyG index is positively correlated with DR risk. Compared with the lowest TyG quartile (Q1), the highest TyG quartile (Q4) was significantly associated with increased DR risk (OR: 1.289; 95% CI 1.008–1.648, P < 0.05). These studies highlight the close relationship between glucose and lipid metabolism and retinal vascular changes. As a marker of atherosclerosis, AIP has not yet been studied in relation to the aforementioned retinal parameters. Our study is the first to demonstrate a correlation between AIP and FD and VD. However, further research is needed to explore the underlying mechanisms.

In the subgroup analyses of our study, we found that among individuals aged ≥ 65 years, elevated AIP was closely associated with decreased FD and VD, while FD in the < 65 year-old group was not significant (P for interaction = 0.065), and VD in the < 65 year-old group was significant (P for interaction = 0.019), showing a consistent trend with the ≥ 65 year-old group but with a larger difference (β: − 0.61, 95% CI − 1.13, − 0.1, p = 0.019 vs. β: − 1.70, 95% CI − 2.32, − 1.08, p < 0.001). This suggests that age is an important modulator of the relationship between AIP and FD and VD. Previous studies have also shown a clear correlation between increasing age and decreased FD [38] and VD [39]. Therefore, AIP may serve as a marker for retinal microvascular changes in the elderly population, but this hypothesis requires further confirmation through larger-scale studies.

Furthermore, a substantial body of literature has confirmed significant associations between other traditional cardiovascular risk factors and retinal fundus characteristics. For instance, a study by Kazantzis et al. [40] on patients with retinal vein occlusion found that hyperreflective foci were associated with higher triglyceride levels (p = 0.024), while the presence of cystic lesions was also significantly correlated with elevated triglycerides (p = 0.010). The research by He et al. [36] further demonstrated that patients with LDL-c levels exceeding 2.6 mmol/L exhibited a significant reduction in vessel density in the optic disc and macular regions, showing a negative correlation between the two. Particularly noteworthy is the study by Stefanutti et al. [41] on patients with familial hypercholesterolemia (FH), which revealed that even under medication, patients exhibited thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer and enlargement of the foveal avascular zone, suggesting the presence of chronic ischemic microvascular damage. These changes may precede clinical symptoms. Collectively, these findings support our hypothesis that dyslipidemia may impair the structure and function of retinal microvasculature by promoting atherosclerosis and vascular endothelial dysfunction. On the other hand, the impact of abnormal blood pressure on retinal microcirculation has also been widely substantiated. Population-based studies by Cheung et al. [42, 43] demonstrated that reduced large artery compliance was significantly associated with narrower retinal arteriolar caliber (p = 0.002), decreased arteriolar compliance was significantly correlated with increased retinal venular caliber (p = 0.001), and reduced aortic expansion rate was significantly linked to a higher likelihood of retinal arteriolar narrowing. More compelling evidence comes from a prospective interventional study by Hughes et al. [44], which directly observed that antihypertensive treatment improved retinal features such as arteriolar narrowing, widened arteriolar branching angles, and increased arteriolar density. In addition, a meta-analysis by Tan et al. [45] conclusively showed that hypertensive patients had significantly reduced vessel density in both the superficial and deep capillary plexuses of the macular region (P < 0.01). These longitudinal and interventional studies provide strong support for the causal relationship that hypertension leads to retinal microvascular rarefaction. Moreover, smoking has been confirmed as a significant negative factor for retinal microvascular health. Studies by Dogan [46] and Zhu [47] both indicated that smokers, particularly those with high cumulative smoking exposure, exhibited significantly reduced vessel density in the deep retinal capillary plexus. This may be attributed to mechanisms such as smoking-induced vasoconstriction, reduced blood flow, and endothelial dysfunction. In summary, although this cross-sectional study cannot directly confirm the causal direction between the AIP and retinal parameters, the aforementioned literature from various study designs provides a reasonable basis for inference. Based on this prior evidence, we propose that the atherogenic lipid profile represented by AIP, as a systemic cardiovascular risk factor, may preferentially or concurrently induce structural and functional remodeling of the retinal microvasculature, leading to alterations in quantitative parameters such as vessel density. Therefore, the significant associations observed in this study likely reflect a potential causal effect of AIP on retinal microvasculature. Although the possibility of reverse causality (i.e., retinal pathology affecting systemic lipid metabolism) cannot be entirely ruled out, it is less plausible from a pathophysiological perspective. Future prospective cohort studies and interventional trials targeting dyslipidemia will be necessary to elucidate the exact causal relationship.

As previously mentioned, the retinal microvasculature—being the only vascular system in the human body that can be directly observed in vivo—often serves as a “window” reflecting the status of systemic microcirculation and large-artery atherosclerosis. Changes in its structure and function make it a powerful tool for investigating systemic vascular diseases. Numerous studies have confirmed a strong correlation between retinal pathology and the risk of systemic vascular events. For example, Liu et al. [48] found that retinal arteriosclerosis was significantly associated with the incidence of carotid atherosclerosis, particularly in males; Morellón et al. [49] reported that patients with retinal vein occlusion (RVO) had a significantly increased risk of major cardiovascular events such as stroke, acute myocardial infarction, and heart failure; Landecho et al. [50] even observed cotton-wool spots in asymptomatic COVID-19 patients, suggesting a link to systemic vascular damage and future vascular event risk. Based on these associations, researchers are actively working to transform retinal imaging into practical clinical risk assessment tools. Deep learning technology, in particular, has shown great promise in this field. Several studies have successfully developed automated models based on color fundus photographs to predict cerebrovascular risk [51], identify acute central retinal artery occlusion within the treatment time window [52], and assess the risk of stroke recurrence [53]. In the cardiovascular domain, McGeechan et al. [54] found that retinal vessel calibers could improve the prediction of coronary heart disease risk in women; Chang et al. [55] constructed a deep learning classification model using Xception as the backbone network and proposed a “Deep Learning Fundus Atherosclerosis Score (DL-FAS)”, which was significantly associated with long-term cardiovascular mortality risk. In a cohort with a median follow-up of 7.6 years, the group with DL-FAS > 0.67 had significantly higher cardiovascular mortality than the group with DL-FAS < 0.33, with a multivariable-adjusted HR = 8.83 (95% CI: 3.16–24.7). Meanwhile, Rim et al. [10] developed a Reti-CAC model that used only fundus photographs to achieve cardiovascular risk stratification performance comparable to that of the coronary artery calcium score [56] (C-index 0.71 vs. 0.71). These groundbreaking studies collectively demonstrate that quantitative analysis based on CFP holds promise as a powerful, noninvasive biomarker for assessing systemic vascular health, performing risk stratification, and enabling early warning. Against this backdrop, our study further confirms a strong association between AIP—an emerging and comprehensive index—and quantitative retinal parameters. Our findings may contribute new evidence to the field of retinal and even systemic atherosclerosis research. Incorporating AIP into retinal image-based assessment models could potentially help identify high-risk individuals for atherosclerosis earlier and more accurately. In clinical practice, if such a noninvasive, rapid screening method can be combined with routine clinical features, it may form an effective complementary strategy to optimize risk stratification and guide more personalized preventive interventions. Although large-scale prospective validation is still needed before clinical implementation, this study undoubtedly reinforces the considerable potential of retinal imaging as a tool for monitoring systemic vascular health and lays a foundation for future intelligent cardiovascular risk management tools that integrate multi-dimensional information.

Our study included 651 CFPs from 651 patients. Based on the quantitative results of retinal morphology obtained from the fundus image analysis system, we clarified the correlation between AIP and fundus quantitative data. Our findings highlight the differences in retinal characteristics across various subgroups, laying the groundwork for future research on the correlation between fundus quantitative results and glucose and lipid metabolism as well as atherosclerosis. The strengths of our study are as follows: First, this is the first study to investigate the correlation between the AIP index and quantitative fundus parameters. Previous studies have mainly focused on the severity of retinal lesions without a detailed analysis of specific quantitative fundus parameters. Second, compared with previous computer-aided detection of retinal vessels, our study achieved fully automated measurement of vascular parameters by first automatically detecting and segmenting retinal vessels and then automatically calculating vascular feature parameters. The fully automated process saves a significant amount of time, reduces labor costs, and avoids subjective errors introduced by manual measurement or semi-automated measurement.

Of course, our study also has some limitations. Firstly, this study is a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size, and the study population consists of patients admitted for suspected angina. The findings may therefore lack generalizability. Second, as a cross-sectional study, our findings only demonstrate the correlation between AIP and fundus parameters without establishing causality. Future cohort studies could be designed to investigate the relationship between AIP and the progression of fundus parameters. Finally, we used 45° central fundus photographs in this study. These photographs cover a limited area, which may restrict the measurement of peripheral vessels. In addition, in the zonal analysis, we only performed annular zones without further dividing them into quadrants, which may have overlooked some meaningful results.

Conclusion

In summary, leveraging a deep learning–based vascular segmentation model, we conducted a correlation study between AIP and fully automated quantitative measurements of retinal vascular parameters from CFP. This finding paves the way for future research on the correlation between retinal microvascular parameters and glucose and lipid metabolism, as well as atherosclerotic diseases. It also further confirms the feasibility of establishing studies on glucose and lipid metabolism and systemic atherosclerosis based on retinal imaging features.
