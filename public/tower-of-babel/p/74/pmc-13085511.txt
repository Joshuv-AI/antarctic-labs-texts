Impact of school canteens on children’s nutrition and food behavior: a systematic review
Authors: Damipie Bomboma, Tiatou Souho, Essoyodou Marcelle Kabassina, Abel Essowereou Abli, Sabiba Kou’santa Amouzou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSchool canteens play a central role in the school food environments and influence children’s nutritional status and eating habits. They are key settings for the promotion of-healthier and more sustainable dietary behaviors.MethodsThis systematic review examines impacts of school canteens on children’s nutrition and their eating behaviors, based on published evidence. The sanitary and nutritional quality of meals served in school canteens were also assessed. The literature search was performed in PubMed and ScienceDirect databases from inception to the search date. Retrieved hits underwent rigorous screening by two reviewers in order to identify relevant articles.ResultsIn several countries, school canteens face challenges regarding nutritional and hygiene quality. Some canteens fail to meet sanitary standards, and meals are often nutritionally imbalanced with, high contents of saturated fat, sugar, and salt, and low in essential micronutrients. The impact of school canteens on the nutritional status of children is difficult to quantify but it is clear that the presence of nutrients such as saturated fatty acids, sugar and salt combined of micronutrients decreases the potential for children to have a nutritional status. Regarding children’s eating behavior, most studies underlined the fact that school canteens are key settings for the nutritional education of children toward healthier and sustainable behaviors.ConclusionsOverall, the implementation of school canteen programs should take into consideration the necessity to provide good quality meals but also the opportunity to provide nutritional education and sustainability awareness to children as childhood habits-are more likely to persist into adulthood.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06723-8.

Introduction

Beyond their primary function of serving as a place for pupils to eat, school canteens have become important strategic components of education systems. This importance has led authorities across the world to design and implement national school food policies that serve as regulatory framework for the proper management of school canteens [1, 2]. The interest in school canteens comes from some recorded added values especially in low- and middle- income countries where school canteens have improved pupils’ scholar concentration and performance, and promoted local economy with food supply in local markets [3, 4]. Moreover, in a world challenged by climate change, school canteens are among other infrastructures that bear the ambition of training adolescents in sustainable food production and processing [5].

By considering the newly promoted settings of school canteens in its autonomous form, it is reasonable to assume that school canteens are intervention settings that contribute to attaining many sustainable development goals and provide at least one meal to infants and adolescents in regions where children don’t necessarily have access to three meals per day [6, 7]. They also constitute a friendly client for local raw food producers and contribute to the education of pupils by being places where pupils learn good food consumption habits and tips for recycling, water management, food waste reduction and hygienic behavior during food cooking and consumption [5, 8, 9].

School canteens are external factors that should be domesticated and seriously considered in school children food habits. Indeed, they are influencing factors that could alter even infants’ behavior regarding food at home. Several strategies have been implemented to reduce overweight, undernutrition as well as micronutrient deficiencies among children and adolescents [2, 4, 10]. However, it is not clear to what extend school canteens contribute to the improvement of individual nutritional status of pupils attending these canteens. Therefore, the present study was designed with the purpose of synthesizing existing evidence on the nutritional contribution of school canteens and their impact on children’s dietary behaviors. By examining findings across different countries and contexts, this study seeks to clarify the role of school canteens in improving children nutrition and to identify relevant factors that may explain variations in their effectiveness.

Methods

Identification of articles

The present study was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [11]. The literature search was performed using the PubMed and ScienceDirect databases. The search was conducted on March 11th, 2025, using the following terms: (“school canteen”) AND ((“nutrition”) OR (“nutritional status”) OR (“eating habits”) OR (“eating behavior”) OR (“food quality”)). All retrieved records were exported into reference management software, where duplicate entries were removed prior to screening. Only articles published in English were included. No restrictions on publication year were applied.

Articles were selected in two stages. At the first stage, titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers against the eligibility criteria. Articles considered potentially relevant, unclear, or ambiguous based on the abstract were forwarded for full text reviews. In the second phase, full text articles were assessed independently by two reviewers. Disagreements at any stage were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Studies were included if they were conducted in schools with an operational school canteen or organized meal service; and reported at least one relevant outcome of school canteen on children’s nutrition and behavior or food quality. Observational both and implementation study Studies were included. conducted in schools without a school canteen, or those lacking relevant nutritional or behavioral outcome data, were excluded.

Reviewers independently extracted data on general study characteristics (authors, publication year, country, and design), indicators of nutritional status (BMI, prevalence of overweight/obesity, stunting, or nutrient intake), eating behaviors (food choices, consumption patterns, dietary habits, behaviors observed in the canteen setting), indicators of food quality, and, where available, menu composition, nutrient content, and quality control measures. Extracted data were cross-checked to ensure accuracy, completeness and internal consistency.

The heterogeneity observed across studies has directed the choice to perform a narrative synthesis was performed instead of a meta-analysis. Data extracted from selected articles were organized into three thematic categories aligned with the review objectives: impact on nutritional status, impact on eating behaviors, and indicators of food quality.

The protocol was registered in PROSPERO under the number CRD420261299108.No amendments were made to the original protocol.

Study’s PICO framework

The population included school-aged children and adolescents attending primary and secondary schools with a school canteen. The exposure of interest was the school canteen, including the nutritional quality of meals provided and their influence on children’s nutrition and food behaviors. No external comparator was defined, as studies conducted in schools without a school canteen were excluded. The outcomes of interest included nutritional outcomes (diet quality, nutrient intake, body mass index, overweight and obesity) as well as children’s food-related behaviors (food choices, eating habits, and food preferences).

Main quality issues observed in meals served in school canteens

Several studies reported excessive amounts of obesogenic nutrients such as fat, sugar and salt in meals served in school canteens. Indeed, excessive fat and saturated fatty acids were recorded in Australia, Ecuador, France, Indonesia, Turkey and the United Kingdom [12–17]. Regarding sugar, 29.1% of Malaysian school canteen meals exceed recommended limits [18]. similarly, high sugar-containing meals were reported in Australia and Ecuador [16, 17, 27, 28]. The level of salt in school canteens’ meals was also reported in high amounts in several studies in Australia, Ecuador, Indonesia, Portugal, and United Kingdom [15–17].

Another concern observed in school canteens’ meals is the insufficiency of micronutrients and certain essential nutrients. This was reported in several countries including France with some meals lacking vitamins B2, B12 and D; and Spain where low levels of essential fatty acids, minerals (zinc and iodine) and vitamin D were reported [12, 19, 20]. In Turkey, meals are insufficient in energy, carbohydrates, folate, and calcium [13]. Breakfasts and snacks offered in canteens are often low in calcium, vitamins A and C, and iron [21, 22].

One of the most observed characteristics in school canteens as reported in selected studies is the low representation of fruits and vegetables. In contrast, there is a high representation of unhealthy foods causing an imbalance in micronutrient intake like in Indonesia, Burkina Faso, New Zealand, and Saudi Arabia [23–29, 32]. Several other studies also reported low amounts of fruits and vegetables in school canteens’ meals [17, 18, 33, 34]. In Indonesia, Burkina Faso, New Zealand, and Saudi Arabia, studies revealed high representation of unhealthy foods with an imbalance in micronutrient intake. Overall, the compliance with nutritional standards in school canteens remains low in several countries as reported in selected studies. In Iran, 54.7% of dishes do not meet national guidelines [35]. In Spain, 15.7% of fried foods exceed acrylamide limits [36]. In several countries, the sale of prohibited or non-compliant foods continues despite regulations [15, 30, 31, 37].

Impact of school canteens on children nutritional status

Numerous studies have highlighted the direct impact ofschool food environments on students’ health. In India, the high prevalence of overweight and obesity among private school students compared to public school students has been linked to the easy availability of sugary drinks, high-fat, and nutrient-poor foods in schools. A study conducted in Netherlands demonstrated that participation in the healthy school canteen program significantly improved the food offerings and helped prevent overweight among students [38].

Impact of school canteens on children’s food consumption habits

It was reported in Norway that young adolescents who regularly use the school canteen consume more salty snacks, pastries, and sugary drinks, and skip breakfast at home more often. Similar patterns were reported in Indonesia, where school canteen users had high intakes of sugary drinks and low consumption of fruits, vegetables, animal-source foods, and milk [39]. Providing healthy meals in school canteens is essential, as a large portion of children’s caloric intake comes from school meals [40]. In line with this, the Netherlands established a nutrition intervention center to support schools in improving the quality of food offered [41].

Children’s food consumption habits can be impacted by the school food environment as shown in several studies. The integration of nutrition education in school canteen programs promoted healthier and sustainable eating practices in Australia and Spain [42, 43]. In New Zealand, analysis of the school canteen food environment highlighted the importance of nutrition education in aligning menus with health objectives [44]. Finally, in primary school canteens, the availability of educationally guided foods encourages healthier purchases and dietary behaviors among children. Discussion

Schools are particular settings designed to host school aged children several hours a day and several months a year. This makes school food environments important contexts that are associated with children’s food habits and overall, their nutritional status. Interventions in school food environments present therefore have the potential to influence children’s food consumption behavior and their nutrition. Given that the central element of school food environments is the school canteen, interventions in this element are often considered relevant strategies to promote healthier, sustainable and equitable food consumption habits. By considering that behaviors developed during childhood may be associated with nutritional outcomes in adulthood, school canteens should be given serious attention with robust standards for food production and preparation, hygiene, food procurement and food promotion.

The present study was interested in school canteens and performed a systematic review of studies that examined the association between school canteens and children’s nutritional health and dietary behavior around the world and investigated main sanitary quality issues that should be monitored. It confirms that, despite the widely recognized role of school canteens in fostering healthy dietary habits, the nutritional quality of school meals is frequently reported with insufficiencies.

Several studies reported excessive levels of nutrients of concern such as saturated fats, sugar, and salt in served meals [12, 13, 15–17]. These nutritional imbalances are often reported alongside low consumption of fruits and vegetables and a high prevalence of high energetic foods with reduced micronutrients’ content [18, 23, 26]. The poor quality of meals may be partly associated to an inconsistent and often incomplete implementation of school nutrition policies.

Despite the existence of nutritional standards, compliance remains limited, with a significant proportion of meals exceeding recommended thresholds for harmful nutrients [15, 35, 37]. This situation is further influenced by socioeconomic and geographic inequalities that affect both the availability and accessibility of healthy food options in schools [47, 48]. It is important to mention that failing to meet nutritional quality requirements in school canteen meals may be associated with a higher risk of different forms of malnutrition including deficiencies and overweight/obesity. Stakeholders involved in school canteens should invest their efforts in achieving the objective of serving at the end of the chain, healthy meals to children.

One of the main purposes of the present study was to explore the association between school canteens and the nutritional status of children. Very few studies have really studied this question and that seems normal. In fact, il is difficult to estimate the particular contribution of school canteens that generally provide only one meal per day, from children’s daily dietary intake. Most studies therefore report potential risks or associations, that may not fully reflect the actual effect of school canteens on children’s nutritional status. Some studies reported an association between regular consumption of school meals and a higher prevalence of overweight and obesity among children while a study conducted in Côte d’Ivoire found a high prevalence of anemia among children attending school canteens in Abidjan [49–51]. Conversely, the implementation of free and universal school meal programs has been associated with more favorable health outcomes [52]. This mitigation in nutritional outcomes of school canteens is due to the fact that these canteens are different from one region to another. The key element potentially associated with children’s nutritional status is the balance of nutrients in meals. The composition depends on food supply strategy, food preparation processes and nutritional standards used in the school. Finally, given that the school food environment is only one part of the children’s food environment, the nutritional status of a child depends on several factors observed in school food environment as well as in the food environment at home. Therefore, the school canteen should present the characteristic of helping children in acquiring sustainable behaviors that could potentiate healthier habits even outside schools.

Data collected in the present study indicate that school canteens may be associated with both positive and negative eating habits among children. Integrating nutritional education within canteen programs appears to enhance the effectiveness of these initiatives in encouraging healthier eating behaviors, particularly in intervention-based studies [42, 43]. Moreover, school canteens can be perfect places to initiate sustainability in food consumption habits like reducing food waste, adopting short food supply chains [53].

Issues in sanitary quality of meals served in canteens are mostly related to the lack of appropriate trainings for the personnel in school canteens. Moreover, when school canteens are implemented without providing high quality facilities, hygiene practices become insufficient to provide good sanitary quality meals. The lack of proper facilities can be attributed to reduced budgets like in low- and middle-income countries where school canteens are often financed by international institutions of non-governmental organizations. The quasi-impossible systematic quality control of meals is also a factor to be considered. Besides, the noncompliance with nutritional standards can be attributed in several countries to the lack of national dietary guidelines or school food policies. In such context, each program manager tends to do what he thinks is better. There is a need for national dietary guidelines and specific policies for school canteens. However, it is important to mention that some countries with these documents also presented some issues especially in providing sufficient amounts of fruits and vegetables. The continuous information and trainings for school canteens stakeholders are necessary and budgets should also be adjusted to adapt with the markets given that prices of fruits and vegetables are not stable throughout the year. To strengthen the impact of school canteens on children’s nutrition, concrete policy recommendations should be considered. Minimum menu standards should be established, including sanitary standards as well as the menus composition regarding fruits and vegetables. as implemented in the healthy school canteen guidelines in the Netherlands [54] and the national healthy school canteens guidelines in Australia [55]. A regular monitoring system, such as semi-annual audits of menu offerings and the availability of healthy foods, could allow adjustments based on compliance data [56]. Staff training in nutrition, balanced menu planning, promotion of healthy foods, and cost management could also be with added value [57]. Procurement policies should favor fresh fruits and vegetables, preferably sourced locally, to improve nutritional quality and support sustainable practices [58, 59]. Integrating these measures in a realistic and supported manner is critical to ensuring that school canteens contribute sustainably to healthier eating behaviors among children.

Finally, it is important to highlight that the family environment plays an important role and is strongly associated with children’s shaping eating behaviors. Indeed, parental involvement is consistently associated with healthier dietary practices among children. It is therefore a strong fostering factor [60, 61]. These findings consistently show the necessity to adopt inclusive approaches in establishing school food policies and implementing school canteens programs. School canteens should be implemented by taking into consideration the fact that they can shape the behavior of children regarding their health but also their environment for an equitable and sustainable use of resources.

Conclusion

The present work provides an overview in school canteens’ impact on the nutrition and eating habits of children attending schools. It is established that school canteens are places where children must be served meals free from pathogens and necessarily containing all required nutrients for children’s well-being and proper development. Investigations in several countries shed light on several issues in sanitary quality as well as nutritional quality. This is a demonstration that school canteens need to be continuously monitored and undergo interventions to increase the practice of hygienic measures and set standards to provide meals with all necessary nutrients. The most prominent impact school canteens play in children is the acquisition and adoption of healthy and sustainable eating habits. Apart from this impact, the direct nutritional effect of canteens is difficult to estimate as it is not easy to distinguish between the effect of foods in canteens and those consumed at home. However, a perfect implementation of school canteens should at least increase the food diversity score of pupils by incorporating more fruits and vegetables in the meals.
