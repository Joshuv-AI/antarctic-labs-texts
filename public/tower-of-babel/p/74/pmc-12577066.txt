Women’s views about the use of gendered Corporate Social Responsibility strategies by harmful industries
Authors: Monique Murray, Hannah Pitt, Simone McCarthy, Grace Arnot, Samantha Thomas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCorporate Social Responsibility (CSR) initiatives are part of a suite of marketing and promotional strategies used by industries whose products and/or practices may be harmful to health and equity. These include the tobacco, alcohol, gambling, fossil fuel and pharmaceutical industries. CSR initiatives have included support for gender equity and empowerment initiatives, such as associations with women’s organisations and charities, educational activities, and sports. Limited research has qualitatively investigated women’s perceptions of these types of gendered CSR initiatives, including their opinions about appropriate public health and policy responses.MethodsThis study sought the opinions of n=506 Australian women, aged 18 years and over. Data was collected through an online qualitative survey. Participants were asked about the CSR strategies of a range of harmful industries, including gendered marketing, sponsorship of women’s sport, and support for women’s causes. Data were interpreted using a reflexive approach to thematic analysis.ResultsThree themes were constructed from the data. First, while participants acknowledged that CSR was a profit-making tactic used to enhance the image of harmful industries and mitigate reputational risk, they also thought there could be benefits associated with some CSR strategies – particularly related to women’s sport. Second, while many women perceived that CSR strategies could positively influence women’s views about harmful industries, others felt women needed to take responsibility for engaging with messages from these industries. Finally, while participants supported increased government regulation of harmful industry promotional tactics, they also recommended educational initiatives to expose industry tactics, and increased responsibility from organisations partnering or taking funding from these industries.ConclusionsThe insights from this study contribute to the growing evidence about the influence of the corporate tactics of industries who are harmful to women’s health and equity. While mapping and monitoring of marketing tactics are essential in public health arguments for regulatory reform of these practices, this study shows the important role that community opinions play in understanding the impact of these tactics, and public expectations of policy and public health actions to prevent harms. There is broad support for increased government regulation, and independent education initiatives focused on exposing industry tactics.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Women’s health, Commercial determinants of health, Corporate social responsibility, Marketing, Harmful industries, Qualitative

Background

The impact of commercial determinants on women’s health and equity

There is increased attention from health promotion and public health scholars on the impact of powerful corporations on health and social harms [1]. The Commercial Determinants of Health (CDoH) encompass “the systems, practices, and pathways through which commercial actors drive health and equity” ([2] p.1195). These include corporate practices such as marketing, lobbying and policy interference, funding research to obscure harms and manufacture doubt, and shaping norms and discourses through reputational management [2]. While much of the existing research on the CDoH has focused on those harmful industries associated with significant global health burdens, such as tobacco [3], alcohol [4], and ultra-processed foods [5], there is increasing recognition of the health equity and planetary impacts associated with the corporate influence of other industries, including commercial gambling [6], vaping [7], fossil fuels [8], and pharmaceutical industries [9].

Women experience a disproportionate burden from the adverse impacts of practices associated with harmful corporations and commercial actors [10, 11]. Most CDoH research with women has focused on how traditional advertising tactics may influence women’s consumption of unhealthy products [4, 12]. This has included analysis of the sophisticated marketing strategies that industries have used to leverage gender norms and stereotypes, often appealing to women through aspirational messaging linked to ideals of beauty and empowerment [13, 14]. For example, Atkinson et al. [4] found that alcohol brands used lifestyle messages and imagery relating to slimness and weight, the colour pink, and all-female friendships to market their alcohol to women. Similarly, Hill and Friel [3], showed that the alcohol and tobacco industries sought to appear socially progressive by aligning with women’s causes, while simultaneously reinforcing gender stereotypes to expand markets and drive consumption among women. The alcohol industry has also promoted products to mothers through industry sponsored ‘mummy drinking’ events which frame alcohol use as a form of social connection and stress relief for young mothers, normalising and encouraging alcohol consumption [15].

Public relations strategies as a central part of the corporate playbook

Researchers have recognised that marketing includes a “full range of direct and indirect advertising, promotion, sponsorship, incentives, public relations, lobbying, and donations”, which are used by harmful industries to promote “products, gain publicity, attract new customers, shape social and cultural attitudes, and build corporate and product image and support” ([16] p. 3). These broader definitions have contributed to an increased focus on how harmful industries use a range of public relations strategies to promote their products, soften perceptions of risk, create community engagement, and resist regulatory reform [17]. Corporate social responsibility (CSR) initiatives are one public relations strategy increasingly used by corporations whose products cause harm and are typically adopted by industries facing scrutiny and regulatory pressure. CSR initiatives involve “commercial entities making voluntary commitments to uphold ethical norms and refrain from causing harm” ([2] p.1202). However, these initiatives have been criticised for being superficial and strategically designed to build reputation, deflect criticism, and influence policy outcomes in ways that undermine public health [2, 18]. CSR activities may include sponsorships, sustainability initiatives, workplace training and equity programs, youth engagement programs, and third-party awards [19–23]. Harmful industries may also fund harm minimisation educational initiatives, which are used to position the industry as responsible stakeholders in mitigating the harms associated with their products [24], but which are often include content which primarily focuses on individual responsibility and safer consumption of products [25, 26].

Some researchers have examined how harmful industries may use a range of CSR strategies to align with gender equity and women’s health, including co-opting women’s views in support of their products and practices [27–29]. We defined gendered CSR strategies as those which were explicitly targeted at women or linked to women’s issues, and are often framed as efforts to promote equity and empowerment, support health outcomes, or contribute to community economic wellbeing [10]. Much of the existing research has focused on the alcohol industry’s gendered CSR activities. This includes sponsorship of International Women’s Days events [30]; breast cancer awareness campaigns - despite alcohol being a known risk factor for cancer [31]; and partnerships with women’s sport [32]. Research examining women’s opinions about the gendered CSR activities of harmful industries show that women hold mixed views about risks and benefits. Atkinson and colleagues [33] study of feminist messaging in alcohol marketing (including CSR activities) found a mix of views ranging from positive views about the alcohol industry and brands as being socially responsible, scepticism about the use of this messaging as a mechanism to generate profit, and beliefs that there was an inherent contradiction between social responsibility messages and the risks and impacts of alcohol products on the health and wellbeing of women.

While there is growing attention in the research literature to the impact of commercial determinants on women’s health and equity [10], there is still limited research that seeks women’s opinions about the practices of harmful industries. Much of the existing public health literature on the CSR activities of harmful industries have largely relied on document analyses [26, 34, 35], with limited research examining community opinions about these strategies. The following research sought to contribute to the evidence base by investigating how women conceptualise the impact and influence of gendered CSR tactics from harmful industries, and the range of strategies that could be used to respond. The research was guided by three research questions:

How do Australian women conceptualise the use of gendered CSR strategies by harmful industries?

How do Australian women conceptualise the use of gendered CSR strategies by harmful industries?

What are their attitudes towards the impact and influences of such strategies?

What are their attitudes towards the impact and influences of such strategies?

What do they recommend should be done to address any negative impacts of these types of strategies?

What do they recommend should be done to address any negative impacts of these types of strategies?

Methods

Approach

The data presented in this paper was part of a larger online qualitative survey that explored the perspectives of n=506 Australian women aged 18 years and over about the tactics and strategies of harmful industries. This paper focuses specifically on the questions relating to women’s perceptions of CSR strategies. The study used an online qualitative survey to capture a diverse range of attitudes and experiences from Australian women. These types of surveys are increasingly used in public health research as an accessible and flexible way for different population groups to have a say about important health and social issues [36]. While these surveys do not necessarily collect the detailed level of qualitative information gathered in in-depth interviews, they do provide a “wide-angle lens” that captures diverse perspectives, and engage individuals who otherwise may not be willing to take part in a longer form interview ([37] p.70). The anonymity of such surveys are also beneficial in reducing power dynamics or social desirability between researchers and participants, and may enable more open responses from participants [36].

Sample and recruitment

The study aimed to recruit approximately n=500 Australian women to provide enough information power to answer the aims and research questions [38, 39]. While there is limited clear guidance on sample sizes for online qualitative studies, we were guided by other online qualitative studies that sought community opinions about the CDoH, including in the areas of gambling [40–43]; alcohol [44, 45]; climate [46–49] and more broadly relating to harmful industry practices [50, 51]. Large sample sizes also help to account from the smaller textual ‘chunks’ of data as compared to in-depth interviews. For this study, participants were required to live in Australia, and have a level of English comprehension that would enable them to understand and answer the questions in English.

The survey was hosted on the Qualtrics platform, which also facilitated recruitment through its partner panel providers. While the research did not aim to be representative of the Australian population, soft quotas were used to ensure diversity by age and geographic location. The research team supplied Qualtrics with the survey link, which was distributed to eligible participants. Individuals who were signed up to a panel company and who met the recruitment criteria received an email invitation with a link to the survey and the Plain Language Statement. Once agreeing to take part, participants were screened out if they did not meet the eligibility criteria or if the quotas had already been filled. Participants could withdraw at any time by closing their browser, and only completed surveys were retained in the final dataset. Participants received points from the panel provider for completing the survey.

Data collection

The survey took participants about 20–25 min to complete. Prior to the full launch, the survey was piloted with 22 women in July 2024 to ensure the comprehension of the questions and address any technical issues with the survey. After piloting, small modifications were made to the survey to ensure questions were prompting more detailed open-ended answers, with the pilot data removed from the final data set. A second pilot was conducted in August 2024 with 50 participants. There were no further changes, and the full survey was launched and all data collected by the end of August 2024. Participants were instructed that they could write as much or as little as they wanted and there were no right or wrong answers.

For the broader survey participants were asked a range of socio-demographic questions (including age, gender, state of residence, geographic location, level of education, and employment status). One discrete question was used to assess whether participants thought that CSR strategies could offset the harms that corporations might cause (yes/no response). Participants were then asked open-ended questions about a range of CSR strategies including:

The influence of such industries sponsoring women’s sports;

The influence of such industries sponsoring women’s sports;

Whether CSR initiatives help to improve the public image of harmful corporations;

Whether CSR initiatives help to improve the public image of harmful corporations;

Opinions about these industries partnering with women’s causes and promoting women’s equality and activities;

Opinions about these industries partnering with women’s causes and promoting women’s equality and activities;

The overall influence of these CSR activities on women;

The overall influence of these CSR activities on women;

What, if anything, they thought should be done to counter such CSR initiatives.

What, if anything, they thought should be done to counter such CSR initiatives.

To ensure the accessibility and comprehension of the survey, participants were provided with brief explanations and definitions of key concepts – for example: “There are a range of industries or corporations that may have an impact on the health and wellbeing of women. These range from the tobacco, alcohol and gambling industries, big tech and the media, the wellbeing and beauty industries, and even the pharmaceutical and fossil fuel industries”. Visual images (for example newspaper headlines of CSR activities about International Women’s Day) were used to increase understanding of the concepts that we were asking women to consider, and to prompt ideas and thoughts about these topics.

Data interpretation

Data interpretation was guided by a reflexive approach to thematic analysis (RTA) [52]. RTA views the researcher’s own knowledge and values “as a resource for research” ([53] p. 2). We took a Big Q approach to data interpretation, grounded in the social construction of knowledge and focused on actively engaging with and interpreting the data, looking for rich and nuanced meanings rather than surface level [54, 55]. The six steps of RTA are well-suited for analysing the diverse types of data that is collected in online qualitative surveys and is focused on constructing meaning across the dataset [36]. Data interpretation was not a linear process. The first step of familiarisation began with reviewing participant responses and removing those deemed nonsensical or poor quality (n=123). This included those who did not appear to make a genuine attempt to complete most of the questions in the survey, or who responded “yes”, “no” or “I don’t know” to every question, writing random letters, or words that were unrelated to the question. These participants were excluded from the dataset and replaced with new participants through the panel provider. This is a subjective process but contributed to our reflexive practice, whereby we considered each response and discussed these as a team before deciding whether to include or remove a participant’s responses. Data were then downloaded from Qualtrics into SPSS and Excel, and the full dataset was read and re-read by members of the research team. Initial reflections and analytical notes were developed during this phase and discussed in team meetings to begin shaping the direction of the analysis. The second step involved generating codes with M.M. identifying patterns and key concepts in the data according to the research questions which were then discussed as a team. In the third step, initial codes were organised into themes and subthemes. These were constructed by analysing patterns and clustering codes that captured similar concepts or perspectives. Step four involved reviewing and refining the themes to ensure they were distinct from one another and representative of the dataset. During team meetings, the researchers had robust discussions to challenge assumptions and positions, referring to the literature to help guide thinking. Step five involved clearly defining and labelling the themes. In step six, the themes were written to present a narrative of women’s perceptions of gendered CSR strategies with quotes selected to illustrate the diversity of women’s views and to ensure women’s voices were at the centre of this inquiry. After peer reviewer feedback we reflected on the construction of some themes, and whether some quotes were appropriate to illustrate particular concepts and revised these where necessary, and revised the names of the themes.

Three themes were constructed from the data.

Theme One: A profit-making tactic to enhance a corporation’s image

When asked if they thought that CSR strategies offset the harms caused by harmful industries, most participants selected a ‘no’ response (n=308, 60.9%). However, when analysing the qualitative responses related to this question there were a broader range of perspectives that were reported. Many participants understood CSR initiatives as primarily a form of marketing aimed at enhancing a corporation’s public image. They reflected on how CSR initiatives were often employed to align corporations with socially desirable causes, even though their core activities may contradict these very causes. Some women perceived that CSR strategies were a calculated strategy to build brand loyalty and expand market reach by appearing socially responsible. For example, one participant emphasised that corporations, regardless of their philanthropic activities, were ultimately driven by financial interests:

Corporate social responsibility is a tactic to make profit. ….it is simply another vehicle for companies to continue existing and making profit. - 24 years old, Victoria

Participants were particularly critical of corporations whose harmful products were perceived to be at odds with the social causes they claimed to support. Many participants expressed discomfort with the idea that companies whose products contributed to health or social problems could simultaneously support causes like women’s empowerment or health initiatives. Participants described these corporations as “greedy” or “DISGUSTING”, and that the use of CSR initiatives were “a manipulation tactic”. Others thought that most individuals would also view these types of promotional tactics with a degree of cynicism:

Everyone takes advertising with a grain of salt, honestly, most women aren’t that silly. What really works is the glamorisation of unhealthy behaviours. - 32 years old, Victoria

They shouldn’t even be promoting themselves for a cause like women empowerment—it’s just contradictory. - 38 years old, Queensland

Some participants pointed out the ethical challenges involved in balancing corporate profits with social responsibility. This included describing the CSR strategies used by harmful industries as “unethical” or “ethically wrong”, although some participants also maintained a neutral position towards the corporations themselves despite these views. Others thought that while CSR strategies might contribute positives, overall they did not outweigh the harms these industries created:

I think it can partially be from a good place, such as by making the production of the product more ethical, however, it does not counteract the harm these corporations do to women and therefore, overall, are unethical and condescending – 26 years old, New South Wales

A broader view that CSR strategies were “just another way big companies expand their marketing” led some participants to view these activities as superficial. Some participants perceived that these industries were rarely concerned with “who they exploit along the way”, and that CSR strategies were simply a marketing tool that served to mask the corporation’s primary aim of promoting their brand and products:

I kinda feel it’s too obvious for words, but it’s just bullshit advertisements…. - 52 years old, South Australia

It is not promoting equity, it is promoting their products. - 38 years old, New South Wales

However, not all participants shared this view. For example, some participants saw the corporation’s CSR initiatives as distinct from the harmful potential of their products. While acknowledging that product consumption could have negative effects, they viewed the company’s CSR efforts as separate:

Women using their products can be bad, but the company promoting corporate social responsibility to women are two different things. - 36 years old, Queensland

There were also more positive views about CSR strategies in relation to women’s sports. Sponsorship of women’s sports by corporations, regardless of the nature of their products, was seen as more acceptable. Some justified this by using equality narratives, including that similar practices were common and accepted in men’s sports for decades. Participants perceived that corporate sponsorships were an integral part of the sports landscape and were necessary for the growth and visibility of women’s sports. For example, some felt that CSR initiatives aimed at promoting gender equity in sports could be justified if they addressed a genuine need, and in particular, contributed financial support:

When asked earlier in this survey whether or not I agreed with harmful products promoting empowering women I said I was against it. But this was a comment made on a statement without context. When we are talking about sports and women in sports, then it is a different story. They need all the financial assistance they can get to help promote women in sports and to be able to be as competitive in their field as the men are. - 48 years old, Victoria

Similarly, a few participants noted the practical side of CSR initiatives, acknowledging that while corporations could cause harm, they had played a necessary role in establishing and promoting women’s sports. They stated that the negative outcomes of these sponsorships were not as great as the consequences of under-funded women’s sports.

Without the sponsorships maybe women’s sport wouldn’t be taking off the way it has, which would be more detrimental. - 47 years old, Queensland

Theme Two: A strategy to shape and influence women’s perceptions of harmful industries

Many participants expressed concerns about the potential negative impact CSR initiatives may have on women’s attitudes and behaviours towards harmful industries and their products. Several participants indicated that CSR efforts could shape how women perceived companies, and “definitely have influence on how women think”. Others worried that women might not understand the motives of the industry. The use of CSR to present companies as caring and socially conscious was viewed as a calculated attempt to deceive the public. Participants, and particularly older women, felt that these initiatives were effective in making women believe that corporations cared about their wellbeing, despite profit-driven motives:

Many women may not see the hidden agenda of these corporations. - 68 years old, Western Australia

They believe what they see and hear—sadly, they are duped. - 71 years old, Western Australia

Younger women were seen as particularly vulnerable to these tactics, especially in the age of social media and paid influencers. Participants noted that younger women may be more easily influenced by targeted marketing campaigns, which often used peers or online figures to promote products:

Younger women could be influenced by paid influencers. - 59 years old, Tasmania

I think particularly young women would be easily influenced and may realise in the long term when they have problems with their health. - 72 years old, Victoria

Some participants felt these strategies could lead some women to associate these companies with positive causes, thereby overshadowing the harmful nature of their products. One participant described a CSR campaign shown in the survey as an example of “pinkwashing”. This participant highlighted the risk of CSR campaigns generating positive feelings about the companies:

This pinkwashing can be effective to some women and get them to feel more positively about the corporations. - 24 years old, Victoria

Participants also expressed concerns that CSR initiatives might normalise harmful behaviours or present information in a manner that might (inadvertently) deceive consumers. By associating harmful products with positive social causes, companies could make these products “seem acceptable, responsible, desirable or even glamourous”. Some wrote that CSR efforts could ultimately encourage women to increase their consumption of harmful products under the belief that they were supporting a good cause:

It will influence women to purchase these products and think the corporations are sympathetic to women’s issues. - 51 years old, South Australia

While some participants perceived CSR as either positively or negatively influencing women’s behaviours, others believed that CSR initiatives had little to no impact on women’s attitudes or decisions. For these participants, CSR initiatives were seen as irrelevant to their personal choices, as they believed that women should take responsibility for their own decisions, independent of corporate messaging:

We are adults and should be responsible for our own choices. Advertisements will always be around its actually up to us to filter through all this and make our own informed decision. - 48 years old, Victoria

Participants discussed individual agency and critical thinking as having a more significant role in shaping behaviours than corporate initiatives. They commented that women would rely on their own personal judgements or values to navigate corporate initiatives, and were fully capable of making informed decisions without being swayed by CSR efforts:

I really think that not enough credit is given to women that it is possible for them to make up their own minds about whether to indulge in an unhealthy habit or not. - 79 years old, New South Wales

While many participants viewed CSR initiatives as harmful, others saw them as having a positive impact on women’s behaviours. Some participants, particularly when considering strategies aligned with charities thought this was a way to enhance respect for women and promote gender equality. Companies that engaged in socially responsible practices were perceived as contributing to a broader culture of care and support for women:

It makes people respect and care for women. - 18 years old, New South Wales

Theme Three: Strategies to counter the impact and influence of CSR strategies on women

A significant number of participants emphasised the need for governmental intervention to address any potential adverse effects of CSR initiatives by harmful industries. Participants proposed various actions and interventions that governments should take to mitigate these impacts. Many called for outright bans on advertising by industries that produce harmful products. They argued that just as tobacco advertising faced stringent regulations, similar measures should be applied to alcohol, junk food, and gambling:

Shut them down completely. If you’re making something harmful, you should be stopped. 69 years old, Victoria

While very few participants specifically mentioned total bans, they supported government interventions to reign in the tactics of these industries. This included the need for “government regulations”, and that “government policies should be brought in”. There were strong sentiments about the need for government leadership in enforcing actions to address harmful industry marketing as a measure to prevent harms:

The government needs to take things in hand. Enough with the huge sponsorship and letting these industries get away with the harm they do just because they have money. - 57 years old, Queensland

Governments can implement and enforce stricter regulations to hold corporations accountable for the health impacts of their products. – 43 years old, New South Wales

Participants also called for clear and enforceable guidelines, and designated regulatory bodies to monitor marketing and policymaking processes and who could take effective actions where violations occurred. In particular, younger participants advocated for impactful financial consequences including that increased taxes and heavy fines and penalties should be applied:

They should have to take responsibility to mitigate harmful effects, not use disinformation or misinformation and warn of the harms their product may have. If they fail to do this, they should be legally pursued. - 18 years old, Victoria

Transparency and accountability were also key concerns. Participants called for enforced transparency from companies about their products, the strategies they used, and the harms they caused. Some stated that increased transparency would expose the true motives behind CSR initiatives, allowing consumers, especially women, to make more informed decisions:

Forced transparency for such corporations and their actions would be helpful to not only expose their actions, but also to help the public question whether the corporations’ motives are true. - 32 years old, Victoria

However, some participants advocated for voluntary measures, calling on industries to take more responsibility, to behave more ethically, and to stop spreading misinformation. These participants urged companies to focus on promoting healthy lifestyles and to be more considerate of their messaging. Some suggested that increasing female representation within companies, particularly in leadership roles, could mitigate some of the negative impacts of CSR strategies. They believed that women in positions of power would be better equipped to address the issues and set ethical standards within these corporations:

We need more women in these corporations to set rules and make a change – 56 years old, Queensland

Others stated that education campaigns were needed to create awareness among women about the range of tactics used by harmful industries to promote their products. Many participants suggested that women needed to be better informed about the harms associated with these industries and the strategies they used, and advocated for educational campaigns to help women to recognise the true motives behind industry tactics:

A social campaign to uncover the main objectives of these organisations. - 36 years old, Western Australia

Finally, a few participants suggested that charities, not for profits, and sporting organisations should take more responsibility for deciding to partner and take funding from harmful industries. They felt that these organisations should critically evaluate the sources of their sponsorships and reject those that conflicted with their mission:

Let the organisations themselves refuse to accept the big corporations’ involvement. - 79 years old, New South Wales

Discussion

This study qualitatively explored Australian women’s perceptions of gendered Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) initiatives by industries known to be harmful to women’s health. The findings demonstrate that Australian women mostly perceived CSR strategies as a profit-making tactic used to enhance a corporation’s image and were concerned about the way these strategies could shape and influence how women thought about these industries. They recommended a range strategies that could be implemented to address these strategies, including increased regulations, greater transparency and accountability of industries, and education to increase awareness of industry tactics. The findings raised three points of discussion.

First, participants viewed CSR initiatives from harmful industries as a strategic method to influence public perception, particularly among women. Rather than being seen as genuine contributions to social good, CSR was viewed as a tactic to enhance corporate reputation, increase profits, and deflect attention from the harms associated with their products. This scepticism reflects broader public distrust of CSR from health-harming industries [56]. For example, a study with young people found that some were critical of the overall benefit of CSR and public relations strategies and questioned the intent of these industries who were causing harm and using these strategies [50]. However, there were some who perceived that these initiatives offered benefits when tied to education, youth development, or health initiatives [50]. Research has found that CSR efforts from health-harming industries were being designed to obscure the harms associated with their products [56]. This highlights the need for greater scrutiny of how CSR strategies functions as part of the broader CDoH. CSR strategies may be deflecting industry accountability and contributing to the normalisation of health-harming behaviours. Tailored public health responses are needed to address the range of potentially positive messages that different at-risk groups receive about health-harming industries and their practices. As Freudenberg [57] argues, there is a need for context-specific research into how the CDoH operate across different political economies, as well as governance mechanisms that can more effectively limit harmful corporate influence. In the case of gendered CSR, these campaigns may play a significant role in the normalisation of behaviours that are detrimental to women’s health, thereby complicating public health efforts to curb their consumption. Policy responses must move beyond regulating traditional advertising to also address CSR and public relations strategies that promote industry interests while presenting themselves as socially responsible. This is particularly important when campaigns are gender-targeted or associated with health-related causes.

Second, participants viewed CSR initiatives as harmful because of their ability to shape perceptions and behaviours around harmful products. Most believed that CSR efforts, particularly those involving sponsorships and marketing aimed at women, served to glamorise and normalise the consumption of health harming products, such as alcohol and tobacco. This aligns with research showing that industry sponsorship in sectors like sport may reinforce consumption norms. O’Brien et al. [56] raised concerns about the ethical risks of allowing alcohol industry sponsorship in sports, suggesting that such sponsorships can have harmful public health implications. While many participants were critical of such CSR initiatives, some perceived them as either not harmful or providing some benefits. As a result, public health strategies must not only address the strategic nature of CSR but also develop targeted interventions to counter its influence on the behaviours and attitudes of certain groups of women. This could include public health campaigns that aim to raise awareness of the tactics of health-harming industries and building capacity in identifying and evaluating these strategies, particularly for those who are known to be at most risk of harm such as young people and women. Freudenberg [57] notes that tackling the CDoH requires interdisciplinary collaboration, with scholars, policymakers, and advocates working together to dismantle siloed approaches to public health. Researchers argue that governments have an ethical responsibility to protect at-risk populations from the harmful influence of corporate marketing strategies [10]. This also includes policies aimed at restricting the ability of harmful industries to engage in CSR efforts and that public policy is protected from industry involvement.

Third, participants were able to discuss a range of strategies that could counter the negative impact of CSR activities on the health and equity of women. Women advocated for public health responses such as stronger government regulation, increased transparency, and greater consumer education to expose how CSR strategies can mask the harms associated with health harming products. Participants also identified the need for alternatives to reduce reliance on harmful industry funding, particularly in sport and public health sectors. In the absence of government regulation, there is a need for the development of ethical, sustainable funding models that move away from a dependency on health harming industry sponsorship and donations and avoid reinforcing corporate influence. Such alternatives may include increased government funding or partnerships with health-promoting organisations to ensure women’s health and sport were not financially reliant on health-harming industries [32, 58]. The findings also highlight the importance of providing opportunities for women to be involved in the discussions and solutions about issues that directly impact them such as the use of gendered CSR strategies. They are capable of providing unique insights into their own experiences, and it is important that public health responses enable ways where their perspectives and recommendations can be included in the development of future research, policy, and practice. Freudenberg [57] suggests that addressing the CDoH requires creation of collaborative spaces that bring together public health actors, policymakers, and affected communities to design governance mechanisms that counter commercial influence.

Limitations

This study aimed to provide a starting point for understanding women’s views about harmful industry promotional and public relations strategies beyond traditional forms of marketing. Just as with the social determinants of health, some groups of women may be particularly targeted or influenced by the tactics of harmful industries. This includes how CSR tactics may be socially situated or contextualised. For example, future research could consider how positive connections to CSR activities for women may be formed through specific activities such as sport, or in areas facing socioeconomic disadvantage where there are few funding alternative for women’s causes. While corporate playbooks are largely similar across harmful industries, further research should examine any industry specific strategies, or whether there are different views about the activities of different industries.

Conclusions

Understanding how different population subgroups view corporate tactics is an important part of understanding how to respond to the commercial determinants of health. As marketing is regulated in some countries, health harming industries will seek novel strategies to improve their corporate image, build support from community groups, and engage new potential consumers of product. Ensuring that CSR initiatives are fully considered as part of a broader suite of marketing tactics will be important in protecting women from the tactics of health harming industries.
