Phonocardiopulmography: the future of perioperative auscultation
Authors: Sung-Hoon Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditionally, anesthesiologists have recommended performing auscultation of breath and heart sounds with a precordial or esophageal stethoscope during anesthesia. The stethoscope is generally considered the most important single device used for monitoring. Unfortunately, many anesthesiologists neglect the perioperative use of a stethoscope, even though it provides a wealth of information about the circulatory system, heart, and lungs. Although point-of-care ultrasound appears to offer greater diagnostic value in some situations, it is premature to dismiss the stethoscope, and its diminished perioperative role should be reconsidered. Recent technological advances have allowed changes in the roles of these modalities to help attending anesthesiologists perform easy assessments using clinically meaningful information from auscultation. The phonocardiopulmography (PCPG), a real-time visual acoustic monitor, is a rediscovered and renovated technique that allows non-invasive continuous heart-lung function monitoring. However, the novel role of the PCPG as a continuous acoustic monitor, particularly for S1 and S2 heart sounds and their respiratory variations, remains relatively unexplored. This device may convey information about cardiac contractility, cardiac output, and fluid responsiveness. Furthermore, continuous lung sound visualization has potential as a monitoring modality during anesthesia, including during bronchospasm and endobronchial intubation. With its ease of use and intuitive mechanism, the PCPG can be applied during general anesthesia and follow-up in both post-anesthesia and intensive care unit settings, particularly for the detection of acute adverse events during the perioperative period.Keywords: Auscultation; Monitoring, physiological; Phonocardiography; Phonocardiopulmography; Stethoscopes

HISTORY OF AUSCULTATION AND PHONOCARDIOGRAM (PCG)

Auscultation is an essential bedside diagnostic method used in modern medicine. The practice of auscultation was first described by Hippocrates in roughly 460 to 370 BC [1]. This rudimentary form of auscultation was conducted by applying a physician’s ear to the patient’s chest to listen to sounds from within the body. Although Harvey formally described heart sounds in 1628, immediate auscultation was employed less frequently than palpation or percussion. In 1816, Rene Laennec invented the first stethoscope, comprising a perforated wooden cylinder, a funnel-shaped plug, and a stopper. The stethoscope gained popularity and became an essential medical tool in the 19th and 20th centuries. Today, however, auscultation is increasingly being replaced by evolving technologies such as echocardiography, which provides qualitative and quantitative information with greater ease. Consequently, the role of auscultation is being redefined, transitioning from a comprehensive diagnostic tool, to a practical, inexpensive, and rapid screening method that can aid general clinicians in making timely decisions at the bedside [2].

The PCG, which provides a graphic record of heart sounds, was developed by Einthoven and Geluc in 1894 to provide objective information on auscultation [3]. Through the 1930s and the 1940s, monitoring and recording equipment for PCG was developed, and standardization began in 1950. A PCG system manufactured in 1965 was used in at least one of the Gemini project crewed spaceflights to monitor the heartbeat of astronauts during flight (Fig. 1). The PCG records heart sounds using a microphone, and the resulting acoustic signal is transformed into a visual waveform. With recent advances in digital techniques, PCG can now characterize heart sounds and murmurs based on their acoustic patterns, components, timing, and frequency. Currently, however, the PCG is not commonly utilized by cardiologists, and physicians are unaware of its existence. Although this technology has advanced and remained available for several decades, it has largely been supplanted by echocardiography. Nevertheless, several recent studies have evaluated the efficacy of PCG in the diagnosis of structural heart disease [4], ventricular dysfunction [5], and heart failure [6,7]. Recently, commercialized PCG with electrocardiography (ECG) recording devices have been introduced for outpatient screening [8]. Furthermore, the development of machine learning and artificial intelligence has dramatically improved their utility [9].

USE OF STETHOSCOPES IN THE FIELD OF ANESTHESIA

According to the American Society of Anesthesiologists standards for basic intraoperative monitoring, every patient receiving general anesthesia should undergo continuously evaluation using qualitative clinical signs, including auscultation of breath sounds, to ensure adequate ventilation. In earlier literature, it was recommended that anesthesiologists auscultate breath and heart sounds with a precordial or esophageal stethoscope during routine, pediatric, thoracic, regional, and monitored anesthesia care [10,11]. The anesthesia stethoscope has also been identified as the most important single device used for monitoring [12]. Although the precise impact of stethoscope use during anesthesia on patient outcomes remains unclear, a theoretical analysis of critical anesthesia incidents utilizing esophageal stethoscopes suggested that vigilant continuous auscultation could prevent 54% of such events [13]. Despite these recommendations, perioperative auscultation is often neglected, and has been largely replaced by capnography and pulse oximetry. The use of esophageal or precordial stethoscopes during anesthesia markedly decreased from 70–87% in the mid-1980s to 28% in the late-1990s [14]. In some reports, the stethoscope is considered inferior to point-of-care ultrasound for differentiating tracheal and bronchial intubation [15,16]. Although recent data are lacking, a minority of anesthesia staff and trainees may actively auscultate heart and breath sounds with a stethoscope during anesthesia. Unfortunately, many anesthesiologists neglect the use of a stethoscope perioperatively, even though it provides a wealth of information about the circulatory system, heart, and lungs [17]. Still, many anesthesiologists do not support abandoning the stethoscope and acknowledge its utility in a variety of clinical situations, when properly used. However, it is important to recognize the low sensitivity and specificity of auscultation, even in the hands of experienced clinicians [15]. To overcome this limitation, digitalized stethoscopes have been introduced and applied clinically in procedural sedation [18], preoperative patient evaluation [19], and postoperative care using wearable patch forms [20]. Although intraoperative PCG is listed in the Korean healthcare claim book, there are currently no commercially available PCG devices, except those used in experimental equipment. Recently, attention has been paid to changing the role of this technique in helping attending anesthesiologists perform assessments easily using clinically meaningful information from auscultation.

HEART SOUND MONITORING

Normal heart sounds consist of the S1 (atrioventricular valve closure, marking the onset of the ventricular systolic phase) and S2 (aortic and pulmonary valve closure, marking the end of the ventricular systolic phase) components. Physiological and pathological changes in the heart sounds can predict cardiovascular abnormalities. Cardiac murmurs can be easily detected by auscultation, and the PCG offers a more intuitive and objective assessment of the operating room (Fig. 1). Perioperative PCG, a simple and noninvasive procedure, warrants further investigation as a potential method for monitoring cardiac performance.

The usefulness of intraoperative PCG as a non-invasive monitor for cardiac performance has previously been investigated. Pharmacological treatments can influence heart sounds; for example, inotropic agents (β-adrenergic drugs) have strong impacts on S1, while systemic vascular resistance–modifying drugs (afterload) have a relatively prominent effect on S2. In canine models, both anesthetic depression and dopamine stimulation of the cardiovascular system were clearly demonstrated using PCG [21]. Changes in the amplitude of the S1 heart sound were found to closely correlate with changes in left ventricular pressure. In a swine model, the amplitude of the S1 heart sound was significantly correlated with cardiac contractility during the administration of dobutamine and esmolol [22]. In contrast, changes in vascular resistance caused by the administration of phenylephrine and nicardipine did not show any significant correlation. In beagles, the amplitude of the S1 heart sound correlated significantly with changes in cardiac output under various conditions [23]. The heart rate measured using an esophageal stethoscope closely matched the ECG values, showing a strong positive correlation. In humans, S1 amplitude correlated positively with the cardiac index in patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery [24]. In 17 patients undergoing liver transplantation, the S1 heart sound was found to be predominantly modulated by ephedrine/esmolol, whereas the S2 heart sound was mainly modified by phenylephrine/nicardipine, but not vice versa [25].

Proportional changes in heart sounds were observed when the blood pressure was changed using inotropes or volatile anesthetics [26]. The relationship between S1/S2 and the change in blood pressure showed a positive correlation in the inotropic experimental model. In the volatile anesthetic experiment, the heart sounds decreased as the minimum alveolar concentration increased. Using an esophageal stethoscope and wireless Bluetooth transmission, the ratio of S1 to S2 showed a greater correlation with systolic blood pressure than S1 alone [27]. It has previously been reported that the beat-to-beat measured S1–S2 interval using an esophageal stethoscope was closely correlated with the corresponding pulse pressure [28]. Continuous monitoring of the intraoperative S1–S2 interval has the potential to act as an indirect estimator of beat-to-beat arterial pulse pressure, and respiratory variations of the S1–S2 interval appears to mirror pulse pressure variation with moderate accuracy (Fig. 2), In 30 liver transplantation recipients, the beat-to-beat monitoring of the S1–S2 interval and its respiratory variation was shown to predict fluid responsiveness, and may be useful as a non-invasive hemodynamic index for fluid management [29]. These results indicate that intraoperative PCG may be useful in detecting cardiovascular changes, such as alterations in cardiac output, contractility, and afterload, which commonly occur in patients receiving anesthesia.

LUNG SOUND MONITORING

Normal lung sounds consist of inhaled and exhaled components. Changes in the pitch and frequency of the lung sounds can be applied to detect abnormalities in the respiratory system. In patients receiving mechanical ventilation, the lung sound signals typically show different morphological patterns depending on the ventilator mode (Fig. 3). Preoperative meticulous auscultation of the lungs helps to determine whether wheezing or rhonchi should be initiated intraoperatively.

Several reports have previously described the features of lung sounds under certain clinical conditions. In the porcine model, the severity and distribution of oleic acid-induced lung injury can be assessed by acoustic changes recorded over the trachea and chest wall [30]. The changes in dependent lung sound transmission had high within-subject correlations with venous admixture and dynamic compliance. Kato et al. [31] developed a system that allows the visualization of breath sounds that display the left and right lung sound simultaneously. During advancement of the tracheal tube, changes in the shape of the visualized acoustic signals using the visual stethoscope appeared before the changes in breath sounds were detected by auscultation. The bilateral breath sounds disappeared when the tip of the tracheal tube advanced beyond the carina. In pediatric patients, the visual stethoscope detects changes in breath sounds and unilateral breath sounds as the tracheal tube advances [32].

The spectral analysis of breath sounds obtained from the esophageal stethoscope can be a useful non-invasive respiratory monitor for intratracheal secretion accumulation [33]. Detecting thick intratracheal secretions with power ratio of 80–200 Hz and 300–400 Hz showed the highest discriminating ability. Song et al. [34] reported that intraoperative bronchospasms can be detected early through real-time acoustic visualization using a digitalized esophageal stethoscope. The visualization of lung sounds facilitates the rapid identification of expiratory wheezing and the abnormal spectrogram patterns characteristic of bronchospasms. To meet these clinical needs, a six-channel acoustic monitoring system for use in surgical patients during perioperative period was developed, and various types of microphone designs were evaluated [35]. The displayed information included time and frequency domain signals, and tools to aid in the detection of adverse events. We hypothesized that such an arrangement may help anesthesiologists to recognize clinical conditions such as wheezing, bronchospasm, endobronchial intubation, and apnea. Respiratory acoustic monitoring (Acoustic Respiration Rate [RRa®], Masimo Inc.) is a commercialized device used to monitor the respiratory rate in patients recovering from anesthesia. Under general anesthesia with a laryngeal mask airway and spontaneous ventilation, RRa® rapidly detects changes in the respiratory rate with minimal bias [36].

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES OF THE USE OF THE VISUAL ACOUSTIC MONITOR

The use of PCG as a monitoring modality in the field of anesthesia has several limitations. First, the signal quality is influenced by ambient background noise. Although the operating room is quiet and still, acoustic signals can be inherently contaminated by endogenous and exogenous noise. Adequate noise processing filters are therefore required to ensure robust signal quality [37]. Secondly, optimal positioning of the microphone is important for obtaining a proper auscultation signal. Well-established auscultation positions and landmarks are recommended for precordial probes. In contrast, placement depth greatly affects the quality of auscultation sounds using an esophageal probe. A depth of 28–32 cm is recommended for clinical use; S1, S2, and inspiratory and expiratory sounds have a high amplitude in that range [38]. Third, the sample sizes of recent studies have not been large enough to establish robust cutoff values for each parameter. Further studies with larger sample sizes are therefore required to generalize these results.

Recently, the application of machine learning and deep learning to PCG data has increased. For example, constructive probabilistic neural network and radial basis function network have been reported to classify a broad range of tracheal-bronchial breath sounds [39]. Integration of the convolutional Fourier modules further ensures the robust performance of PCG segmentation, even amidst heart murmurs and background noise, thereby significantly contributing to the advancement of cardiac diagnostics [40]. Shin et al. [41] previously proposed temporal convolutional network-based feature extraction and information fusion to integrate asynchronously acquired PCG recordings at the patient level. The proposed framework accommodates variable-length, multi-channel PCG recordings, making it an accurate and flexible solution for detecting pediatric cardiac abnormalities, particularly in low-resource settings.

The novel role of the PCG as a continuous acoustic monitor, particularly for S1, S2 and their respiratory variations remain relatively unexplored. Furthermore, continuous lung sound visualization also has potential monitoring modality in the field of anesthesia. Technically, digitized auscultation data share the same signal source and can be separated into heart and lung sound components using specific frequency filters. In this respect, we propose the concept of phonocardiopulmography (PCPG), a technique which enables the simultaneous visualization of both the heart and lung sounds (Fig. 4). Further larger scale follow-up data are required to define the role of the PCPG, a visual acoustic monitor, as a cost-effective bedside tool to identify adverse events to supplement physical examination, which is commonly neglected in busy clinical settings.

CONCLUSION

Innovation should always remain an important aim as we strive to improve the safety and reliability of anesthesiology. Although point-of-care ultrasound appears to offer greater diagnostic value in various situations, it is premature to dismiss the stethoscope, and its diminished perioperative role should be reconsidered. Visual acoustic monitoring (PCPG) is a novel technique that allows non-invasive continuous heart-lung function monitoring. Owing to its ease of use and relatively low cost, the PCPG can be applied during general anesthesia and follow-up in both post-anesthesia and intensive care unit settings, particularly for the detection of acute adverse events during the perioperative period.
