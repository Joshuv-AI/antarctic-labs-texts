Survival and clearance dynamics of Salmonella in litter, carcasses, and internal organs of chickens fed different dietary fat sources
Authors: Shijinaraj Manjankattil, Jinquan Wang, Sabin Poudel, Matthew Bailey, Yagya Adhikari, Karla Casco Gomez, Arjmand Fatima, Rabin Raut, Farazi Abinash Rahman, Samuel Rochell, Dianna Bourassa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study evaluated the efficacy of four different fat sources in broiler feed on Salmonella presence in litter, spleen, liver, ceca, and carcasses. In this study, 1152, day-old chicks were allocated into four different treatments randomized among 48 pens. Fat source-based treatments included Soy Oil (T1), Poultry Fat (T2), Soap Stock (T3), and Palm Oil (T4). All birds were orally challenged on day 7 with 10^8 CFU of S. Enteritidis and S. Typhimurium cocktail. On days 14, 21, 28, 35, and 42, litter was sampled from each pen using boot swabs and spleen, liver, and cecal contents were sampled from one bird from each pen for Salmonella recovery. On day 43, 16 post-pick carcass rinsates per treatment were collected for Salmonella recovery. Data was analyzed using PROC GLM of SAS and Chi-square test in R. On day 14, Salmonella was detected from 16 %, 25 %, 41 %, and 33 % of spleen and 83 %, 66 %, 50 %, and 75 % of liver samples from T1, T2, T3, and T4, respectively (P > 0.05). Salmonella prevalence decreased in liver to 0 % at day 42 in all treatments except for 1 liver from T1. At day 14, cecal contents were 92 %, 83 %, 92 %, and 92 % Salmonella positive for T1, T2, T3, and T4 with an average of 3.5 to 4.6 log10CFU/g. However, by day 42, Salmonella was not detected by direct plating in cecal contents and prevalence decreased to 25 %, 45 %, 42 %, and 25 % from T1, T2, T3, and T4, respectively. Post-pick carcass rinses were 100 %, 100 %, 100 %, and 81 % positive from T1, T2, T3, and T4, respectively. No significant differences in Salmonella counts or prevalence were observed among different dietary fat source treatment groups. These results indicate that the clearance pattern of Salmonella from internal organs over time was not influenced by fat sources. Although internal Salmonella contamination appeared to have mostly cleared by day 42, the continued presence and survival of Salmonella in the litter may have resulted in post-pick carcass contamination.Keywords: Dietary Fat, Broiler, Salmonella, Carcass, and Dissemination

Introduction

Salmonellosis in broiler production is a complex, multifactorial challenge involving numerous potential sources of infection and diverse transmission routes (Adhikari et al., 2025a). Transmission can occur horizontally through contaminated feed, water, litter, dust, and rodents, as well as vertically via sperm and transovarian transmission from infected breeder flocks (Byrd et al., 1998; Cason et al., 1994; Jones et al., 1991; Reiber et al., 1995; Wang et al., 2023). Additionally, feed withdrawal prior to slaughter has been associated with increased bacterial loads in the foregut and commingling during transport further elevates the risk of carcass contamination (Ramirez et al., 1997 and USDA FSIS, 2021). Many Salmonella serotypes isolated across the poultry production continuum carry multiple clinically significant antibiotic resistance genes (Adhikari et al., 2025b; USDA FSIS, 2025) and virulence plasmids, contributing to enhanced survival, pathogenicity, environmental persistence and public health concern (Diarrassouba et al., 2007). Given the complexity of Salmonella transmission and the numerous critical control points, the application of robust preharvest interventions is crucial to mitigate the risk of foodborne, antibiotic-resistant Salmonella entering the food chain. Current strategies include the use of organic acids, phytochemicals, directly fed antimicrobials, prebiotics, postbiotics, probiotics, and bacteriophages (Naeem and Bourassa, 2024; Nair et al., 2018; USDA FSIS, 2021). Among the alternatives, short- and medium- fatty acids have received increasing attention for their antimicrobial properties and potential application as dietary interventions in poultry production (Manjankattil, 2020). Long chain fatty acids have primarily been used as an energy source in poultry diet.

In addition to the role of energy sources, fat is also known to have extra calorie effects for improving nutrient utilization by increasing transition time in the gastrointestinal tract (Mateos et al., 1982). The beneficial effect of dietary fat on broiler performance is mainly related to the chain length and degree of unsaturation (Sanz et al., 2000). Additionally, nutritional emulsifiers often used as feed additives to improve the metabolizable energy of fat thus to improve growth performance and carcass characteristics (Chen et al., 2019a; Wang et al., 2020). However, limited research has focused on the effect of dietary fat in relation to avian gut microbiota particularly in the foodborne pathogens compared to starch or protein. One reason is that the digestion and absorption of fats is far more complex than that of starch and protein, as it requires emulsification, micelle formation, and involves numerous variables and interactions. Previous study has indicated that tallow in broiler diet increased C. perfringens population compared to soybean oil diet (Knarreborg et al., 2002). Several studies have demonstrated that the type of dietary fat included in poultry diets can influence not only growth performance but also immune competence and resistance to diseases (Khatibjoo et al., 2011; Świątkiewicz et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2000).

Soybean oil has been established as a beneficial fat source in poultry nutrition, demonstrating advantages across multiple production parameters. In laying hens, comparative studies revealed that soybean oil supplementation improved productivity metrics relative to coconut and fish oils (Dong et al., 2018). Beyond performance benefits, soybean oil has shown immunomodulatory effects. Pinto et al. (2014) reported enhanced antibody titers following Newcastle disease vaccination in soy oil-supplemented groups compared to other lipid sources. Recent research under challenging conditions demonstrated soybean oil's particular efficacy in heat-stressed broilers, where it outperformed corn, olive, and fish oils in maintaining meat quality parameters (Elbaz et al., 2023).

Poultry fat has emerged as a viable alternative to plant-based oils, demonstrating comparable efficacy as either a partial or complete replacement for soybean oil while maintaining growth performance and meat quality parameters (Saleh et al., 2021). Interestingly, while supplementation with poultry fat showed no significant effects on body weight, feed conversion ratio (FCR), or chilled carcass weight, it resulted in reduced abdominal fat pad deposition compared to diets containing palm or soybean oils. Additionally, Pesti et al. (2002) reported that poultry fat produces a distinct reddish coloration in the fat tissue, differing visibly from the appearance of fats derived from soybean or palm oil supplementation. Limited studies have investigated the effect of poultry fat on Salmonella clearance. A recent study reported that fat sources with higher levels of polyunsaturated fatty acids can enrich short-chain fatty acid–producing taxa in the gut (Jadhav et al., 2024), and the role of these taxa in Salmonella clearance has been well established (Nair et al., 2021). However, specific studies utilizing poultry fat are still needed to elucidate its involvement in pathogen clearance.

Soybean soapstock, an acidulated byproduct of oil refining with compositional similarity to crude soybean oil, has been extensively investigated as an alternative lipid source in poultry nutrition. Research spanning broilers and layers has demonstrated its dual benefits on both production performance and carcass characteristics. Notably, Shahryari et al. (2021) revealed that beyond these conventional metrics, soapstock supplementation positively influences intestinal morphology and nutrient digestibility, suggesting potential benefits for gut health. These findings position soapstock not merely as an economical alternative, but as a functional feed ingredient warranting further investigation into its impacts on gastrointestinal health and overall bird physiology.

Palm oil represents another plant-derived fat source with demonstrated nutritional benefits in broiler production. Comprehensive studies have established its multifunctional role in enhancing growth performance, antioxidant status, carcass traits, and fatty acid profiles (Saminathan et al., 2022). Notably, palm oil supplementation has shown particular promise in mitigating heat stress. Zulkifli et al. (2003) reported significant improvements in survivability under thermal challenge conditions. Beyond productivity and stress resilience, Smink et al. (2008) reported enhanced juiciness and firmness in breast meat from palm oil supplemented birds. Research on the impact of palm oil on cecal microbiota remains limited. Available evidence indicates that oligosaccharides derived from palm kernel expeller do not significantly influence beneficial bacterial populations, including Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium (Rezaei et al., 2015)

Soybean oil, poultry fat, palm oil, and soapstock are among the most utilized lipid sources in poultry nutrition, representing vegetable oil, animal fat, saturated fat, and by-product fat categories, respectively. Despite their widespread use, studies evaluating their differential effects on the clearance dynamics of Salmonella enterica in poultry remain limited. Considering the influence of dietary fat on host physiology, it was hypothesized that the strategic selection of fat sources may contribute to improved bird health while reducing colonization of pathogens such as Salmonella. Variations in fatty acid profiles among these fat sources have been well documented in recent studies (Izzudin et al., 2023; Saleh et al., 2021), and the antimicrobial activity of fatty acids has been shown to depend on their chain length and degree of saturation (Babu et al., 2009; Van Immerseel et al., 2004). Nevertheless, only a limited number of studies have directly examined the effects of fat sources as such on Salmonella colonization and survival. The present study aimed to assess the effects of four dietary fat sources (Soybean Oil, Poultry Fat, Soapstock, and Palm Oil) on Salmonella prevalence in broiler ceca, litter, spleen, liver, and carcasses.

Materials And Methods

Experimental design & bird management

The study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC #2024-5409) at Auburn University. The birds were raised in the Charles C. Miller Jr. Poultry Research and Education Center at Auburn University. A total of 1,152, day-old Ross 708, non-vaccinated male chicks were randomly assigned to four treatment groups, with each group allocated to 12 pens (a total of 48 pens). From day 0 to day 3, all birds were provided with a basal diet. On day 3, the birds were transitioned to one of four experimental diets, which were randomly distributed among the 48 pens. The treatment fat sources used in this study included Soy Oil (T1), Poultry Fat (T2), Soapstock (T3), and Palm Oil (T4). The percentage of fat in the starter, grower, and finisher diets for the treatment groups were 0.934 %, 1.373 %, and 2.061 %, respectively. The percentage of fat inclusion was formulated to minimize energy variations as a potential confounding factor. Accordingly, all diets were formulated to be isolipidic with minimal differences in energy, while fully meeting standard nutritional requirements. The birds were provided with ad libitum access to both feed and water. Age-appropriate temperature and humidity conditions were maintained in accordance with standard practices. Wood shavings were used as bedding material in the pens. Birds were inoculated with 10⁸ CFU Salmonella cocktail on day 7 and samples were collected on weekly basis on day 0, 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, and 42. On day 43, the birds were processed at the Charles C. Miller Jr. Poultry Research and Education Center pilot poultry processing facility at Auburn University. Carcass rinsates were collected at the post-pick stage of processing for Salmonella enumeration. The pen facility was equipped with individual water lines and floor drains. Each pen initially housed 24 birds on day 0, providing a floor space of 967 cm² per bird. The number of birds per pen decreased weekly due to sampling, resulting in the following stocking densities: 23 birds from day 1 to day 7 (1009 cm²/bird), 22 birds from day 7 to day 14 (1075 cm²/bird), 21 birds from day 15 to day 21 (1105 cm²/bird), 20 birds from day 22 to day 28 (1161 cm²/bird), 19 birds from day 29 to day 35 (1222 cm²/bird), 18 birds from day 36 to day 42 (1290 cm²/bird), and 17 birds on day 43 (1366 cm²/bird).

Inoculum preparation and challenge

Salmonella Enteritidis (SE) strain 13076 was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), and nalidixic acid (NA; VWR, PA, USA), resistant Salmonella Typhimurium (ST) was obtained from Dr. Nelson Cox’s laboratory at the USDA ARS, Athens, GA. Both serotypes were used for inoculum preparation in this study. Each strain was independently cultured in tryptic soy broth (TSB; Becton, Dickinson and Company, NJ, USA) by inoculating 100 µL of the stock culture into 10 mL of TSB and incubating overnight at 37°C. After the initial overnight culture, increasing concentrations of NA dissolved in 1 N sodium hydroxide (NaOH; Fisher Chemical, PA, USA), were added to the TSB on subsequent days to induce resistance for SE. Bacterial cultures were gradually exposed to higher concentrations of NA until a final concentration of 200 ppm was reached. Once resistance was established, both strains were cultured in TSB containing 200 ppm NA for three consecutive passages. A large-volume culture (1,500 mL TSB) was then prepared by incubating the resistant strains at 37°C for 24 hours. The inoculum was prepared by centrifuging the bacterial culture at 3,600 rpm for 15 min, discarding the supernatant, and resuspending the bacterial pellet in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to achieve the desired dose per mL. On day 7, birds were challenged with 1 mL of 10⁸ CFU of a 1:1 mixture of SE and ST through oral gavage (Wang et al., 2024).

Sample Collection

One bird from each pen was randomly selected without considering individual bodyweights of birds, at each week for sampling. Birds were euthanized via carbon dioxide asphyxiation and subsequently necropsied. For each organ type and litter, 12 samples per treatment group were collected weekly across seven sampling points (12 samples × 4 treatment groups × 7 time points). Liver, spleen, and cecal samples were collected and placed separately in 24 oz Whirl-Pak® bags (Whirl-Pak, Nasco, WI, USA) for further analysis. Prior to entering the facility, personnel wore clean, dry boots with new plastic boot covers. A clean pair of boot covers were donned upon entering each pen and a boot swab (Envirobootie, Hardy Diagnostics, CA, USA) were worn by each individual while walking through the pen during sample collection. After walking across each pen, the swabs were aseptically removed and returned to sample bags. All collected samples were stored on ice during transport and until processing in a biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) laboratory. On the day of processing (day 43), 64 carcasses were collected at the post-pick stage of processing for bacterial recovery, with 16 samples per treatment group. To minimize cross contamination, defeathering equipment was rinsed with hot water between treatments. Carcasses were aseptically placed into poultry carcass rinsing bags (Poultry Rinsing Bag, Nasco, WI, USA), and 400 mL of buffered peptone water (BPW; Neogen, MI, USA) was added. The samples were shaken for one minute to ensure thorough rinsing, and the carcass rinsates were collected and stored on ice until further processing.

Salmonella recovery

Liver and spleen were enriched in 25 mL BPW and incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. Cecal samples in 5 mL BPW were homogenized by using a stomacher (Stomacher 400, Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) at 260 rpm for one minute and serially diluted. Appropriate dilutions were plated onto Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate (XLD) agar (HiMedia, PA, USA) supplemented with NA at 200 ppm, and the plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. Boot swabs collected in sample bags were resuspended in 100 mL of BPW. Carcass rinsates were also incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. After incubation, all enriched samples were streaked onto XLD + NA, XLD without NA, and brilliant green sulfa (BGS) agar (Becton, Dickinson and Company, NJ, USA), and incubated at 37°C for 24 hours.

Salmonella confirmation using 3 M MDS

Overnight enriched samples were analyzed for Salmonella prevalence using the 3M™ Molecular Detection Assay (MDS; Neogen, MI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 20 μL of each enriched sample was added to the lysis solution provided in the kit. Samples were then heated at 100°C for 15 min using a heating block. After heating, tubes were allowed to cool on a chill block at room temperature for 5 min. Subsequently, 20 μL of the lysed samples was transferred into reagent tubes and loaded into the 3 M MDS instrument tray. A negative control (containing only the kit reagents) and a positive control (containing a known pure Salmonella culture) were included in each run. The 3 M MDS software was used to initiate and monitor the Salmonella detection assay.

Serotyping

The Salmonella-positive samples recovered before the challenge phase (day 7) were identified and confirmed using both culture method and MDS. Samples that grew on XLD or BGS and showed positive results on MDS were collected for serotyping. Specifically, one representative colony from each of three XLD plates corresponding to ceca, liver, and spleen sample was sequenced. For the positive XLD plates, a representative colony was selected and cultured in TSB by incubating at 37°C for 24 hours through three successive sub-cultures. From the third culture, a bacterial stock culture in glycerol was prepared for further investigations, and the remaining cultures were pelleted by centrifugation. The bacterial pellets were stored at −20°C until processing. DNA samples were extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen,MD, USA). The extracted DNA was sequenced using the Rapid Barcoding Kit 24 V14 (Oxford Nanopore Technology, NY, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The prepared library was then loaded onto the MinION Flow Cell (FLO_MIN114 R10.4.1) for whole genome sequencing. MinKnow was used for the sequencing and demultiplexing the raw reads. Bacterial genomics workflow of EPI2ME Platform was used for the denovo assembly for bacterial genomes assembly, serotyping, and AMR gene identification. The assembled genome sequences were mapped to reference genome sequence using Geneious Mapper in Geneious Prime (https://www.geneious.com) and serotyping.

Statistical Analysis

This study was conducted using a completely randomized design. The sample size was determined based on the power analysis with a targeted power of 80 % and above. Bacterial counts enumerated from XLD + NA plates were log-transformed after applying the appropriate dilution factors and adjusting for organ weights. Samples with no detectable colonies on direct plating but positive after enrichment were assigned a log value of 0.9. Statistical analyses were performed using PROC GLM in SAS® OnDemand for Academics (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Differences in means were assessed using Tukey’s Studentized Range Test. For categorical data derived from enrichment results of liver, spleen, and carcass samples, Chi-square tests were conducted using R software. Statistical significance was determined at P ≤ 0.05

Effect of dietary fat sources on colonization of Salmonella in the ceca

Salmonella counts from chicken ceca samples at different sampling points of post inoculation are depicted in Table 2. No significant differences in bacterial counts and prevalence were observed among treatment groups at any sampling point (P > 0.05) (Table 2). The nalidixic acid-resistant Salmonella recovered from the ceca of chickens at day 14 (7 days post-inoculation) was 4.67, 3.45, 3.97, and 4.41 log10CFU/g for T1, T2, T3, and T4, respectively (P > 0.05) (Table 2). By day 21, Salmonella counts in the ceca were 4.14, 2.99, 3.77, and 3.79 log10CFU/g for T1, T2, T3, and T4, respectively. On day 28, bacterial counts declined to 2.58, 2.33, 2.56, and 2.80 log10CFU/g in T1, T2, T3, and T4, respectively. By day 35, Salmonella was recovered at 0.61 log10CFU/g in T3 and T4, whereas counts of 1.60 and 1.87 log10CFU/g were observed in T1 and T2, respectively. A similar bacterial count of 0.61 log10 CFU/g was observed in T3 on day 42. In contrast, bacterial counts declined to final values of 0.78, 0.55, and 0.41 log10CFU/g in T1, T2, and T4, respectively. On day 14, the prevalence of Salmonella-positive cecal samples was 91.6 %, 83.3 %, 91.6 %, and 91.6 % for T1, T2, T3, and T4, respectively. On day 21, the prevalence was 91.6 % for T2 and T4, and 100 % for T3 and T4 (P > 0.05). By day 28, T1 and T3 maintained a prevalence of 91.6 %, T2 dropped to 81.8 %, and T4 decreased to 75 %. On day 35, T1 had the prevalence of 58.3 %, while T2 remained high at 81 %, and T3 and T4 were at 45 % and 50 %, respectively. By day 42, the prevalence for T1 and T4 was reduced to 25 %, while T2 and T3 had 45.4 % and 41.6 %, respectively (P > 0.05).

By day 42, all samples from the different treatment groups tested negative, except for 8 % of positive liver samples from T1 (P = 0.3947).

Effect of dietary fat sources on dissemination of Salmonella in spleen

The effect of different dietary fat sources on Salmonella dissemination to the spleen from day 14 to day 42 is illustrated in Table 5. No significant differences were observed in the percentage of Salmonella-positive spleen samples among treatment groups at any sampling point. On day 14, Salmonella was detected in 16 %, 25 %, 41 %, and 33 % of spleen samples after enrichment in BPW followed by incubation (P = 0.5689). By day 21, the percentage of positive samples was 25 %, 50 %, 16 %, and 16 % for T1, T2, T3, and T4, respectively (P = 0.691). On day 28, no positive samples were detected in T3 and T4, whereas 8 % and 9 % of samples tested positive for Salmonella in T1 and T2, respectively (P = 0.534). By days 35 and 42, all samples tested negative after enrichment and MDS detection, except for 2 out of 12 samples (16 %) from T3 on day 42 (P = 0.1072).

Effect of dietary fat sources on dissemination of Salmonella on carcasses

Table 5 illustrates the percentage of Salmonella-positive carcass rinsates at the post-pick stage of processing across different dietary treatments. All carcass rinsate samples from T1, T2, and T3 collected at the post-pick stage of processing tested positive for Salmonella by both direct plating and MDS. In contrast, 3 out of 16 samples from T4 tested negative for Salmonella using both detection methods. A significant difference was observed in the percentage of Salmonella-positive carcasses, with T4 having a lower positivity rate (81 %) compared to the other treatment groups (P = 0.0239) (Table 5).

Discussion

Dietary interventions and nutritional modulations play a critical role in controlling Salmonella during the broiler grow-out phase. The antimicrobial role of specific fatty acids in controlling Salmonella colonization in poultry has been extensively documented. Numerous studies have highlighted the efficacy of medium-chain fatty acids, particularly caprylic acid in reducing Salmonella colonization in broilers and laying hens. Supplementation of this fatty acid has been demonstrated to significantly reduce Salmonella counts in the ceca, cloaca, liver, spleen, and even within the internal contents of eggs in Salmonella Enteritidis-infected birds (Kollanoor-Johny et al., 2012; Manjankattil, 2020; Upadhyaya et al., 2015). Similarly, lauric acid supplementation has been shown to enhance immune responses and reduce mucosal injury following lipopolysaccharide challenge in broilers (Wu et al., 2021). In addition to medium-chain fatty acids, long-chain fatty acids, such as the n − 3 fatty acids, α-linolenic acid (ALA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), have shown superior efficacy in clearing Salmonella Typhimurium in-vitro cell models, compared to stearic acid (SA) and the n − 6 fatty acid, arachidonic acid (ARA) (Babu et al., 2009). Furthermore, recent review reports have evidenced that long-chain free fatty acids influence the regulation of pathogenic gene expression in Salmonella (Børreby et al., 2023). Although the antimicrobial properties of individual fatty acids have been widely explored, limited attention has been given to evaluating whole fat sources. A previous study reported that feeding broilers with fermented soybean meal significantly reduced S. Typhimurium counts in the crop, ileum, and ceca by day 10, with complete clearance from liver and spleen by day 24 (Jazi et al., 2019). While not always targeting Salmonella specifically, multiple studies have shown that alterations in fat sources can influence overall immune responses in poultry (Fritsche et al., 1991). Although immune responses were not directly measured, the absence of differences in bacterial clearance in this study suggests that the fat sources tested exhibit comparable antimicrobial effects. Linoleic acid is the major component in soybean oil, poultry fat, and acidulated fat, whereas palmitic acid predominates in palm oil. The compositional similarities among the fat sources, combined with the relatively low dietary inclusion level, may also account for the absence of significant differences observed among the treatment groups. Another potential explanation for the lack of significant differences in Salmonella clearance among dietary fat sources is their comparable effects on immune function and gut microbiota modulation. Although only a limited number of studies have examined these fats, available evidence indicates that supplementation with soybean oil or palm kernel oil produces similar shifts in cecal microbiota, notably increasing Lactobacillus populations (Chen et al., 2019b; Li et al., 2020). Likewise, Saleh et al. (2021) observed no differences in plasma profiles when comparing soybean oil with poultry fat. Additional research is needed to clarify whether these fat sources exert equivalent effects on immune response. Given the lack of significant variation in Salmonella prevalence, poultry producers may select among the fat sources examined based on factors such as cost, availability, and production performance, without concerns regarding negative impacts on Salmonella control.

Although no significant changes were observed associated with dietary fat sources, further investigation into the patterns of Salmonella colonization and clearance remains essential. There are many factors influencing the clearance of Salmonella from the internal organs of broiler chickens as well as its survival in litter samples. Age, method of inoculation, dose, strain type, immune status, and management practices play critical roles in Salmonella colonization and shedding in broilers (Sadler et al., 1969). This study provides insights into Salmonella survival in the ceca and its spread to internal organs over a 42-day period, in response to dietary interventions. This study also identifies the Salmonella serotype in the chickens before challenge, suggesting a potential hatchery origin contamination. Consistent with our findings, other studies have reported the prevalence of Salmonella serotype Hadar with similar antimicrobial resistance patterns in both hatchery and broiler production environments (Diarra et al., 2014; Muhammad et al., 2010). The low recovery of the challenged Salmonella strain one-week post-inoculation may be attributed to early colonization by isolated Salmonella serotype (S. Hadar) and a subsequently activated host immune responses and competitive exclusion. The absence of 100 % prevalence with non-challenged serotypes may account for the greater variability observed in cecal Salmonella counts in individual birds. Similar to the Salmonella clearance pattern observed in the current study, Wang et al. reported a comparable decline in Salmonella levels in the ceca, liver, and spleen on day 35 after the chickens were challenged with a cocktail of three different Salmonella serotypes (S. Typhimurium, S. Infantis, and S. Reading) at three different inoculation doses (Wang et al., 2024). In another study, chicks inoculated with S. Enteritidis through the aerosol method showed a decrease in Salmonella prevalence in the ceca over time (Pal et al., 2021). Interestingly, they noted a higher prevalence in liver and spleen on day 21 at a 10⁶ CFU dose, with reduced prevalence at a higher dose (10⁹ CFU), underscoring the impact of both challenge route and inoculum level on pathogen clearance patterns.

In alignment with our findings, other studies have also reported that Salmonella was consistently recovered from litter samples across the study duration, with no clear association to cecal bacterial loads (Pal et al., 2021). Likewise, Bourassa et al. (2018) demonstrated that a challenge using a seeder chick model with S. Typhimurium at 10⁴ CFU per bird was sufficient to maintain Salmonella-positive litter for the entire six-week trial, even as cecal prevalence declined by week six. These results underscore the environmental persistence of Salmonella in litter, independent of gastrointestinal colonization levels. To date, few studies have evaluated the impact of feed or water additives used as preharvest antimicrobial interventions on microbial populations within litter. Bourassa et al. (2018) reported no significant effect of such additives like organic acids on Salmonella counts in litter samples, highlighting the stability and resilience of certain Salmonella serotypes in the environment. Notably, strains that persist for extended periods in litter have been shown to harbor larger copies of Col plasmids and antimicrobial resistance genes, raising concerns (Oladeinde et al., 2024). Another important but unaddressed factor in the current study is the role of feed withdrawal prior to processing and its associated impact on Salmonella population. Corrier et al. (1999) demonstrated that increased pecking activity during the feed withdrawal period can lead to greater ingestion of contaminated litter, thereby elevating Salmonella prevalence in the crop. This preharvest stressor may contribute to carcass contamination risk during processing. Previous study indicates that presence of Salmonella in litter directly reflects carcass prevalence (Bhatia et al., 1979), it can be due to the close contact of feathers and skin with contaminated litter throughout the grow-out period. Several factors can affect Salmonella survival in litter, including moisture content, organic carbon, pH, and microbial populations (USDA FSIS, 2021). Although these litter parameters were not measured in this study, the use of fresh, uniform litter across treatments and consistent environmental conditions in all pens likely minimized their impact. Collectively, these findings emphasize the necessity of incorporating litter-focused intervention strategies such as pH modification, moisture control, and targeted microbial amendments after the bird placements alongside traditional bird-focused antimicrobial measures. Addressing both bird and environmental reservoirs of Salmonella is critical to achieving comprehensive pathogen control in broiler production systems.

Other dietary fat sources eliminated Salmonella from internal organs but were associated with carcass positivity, whereas the palm oil group exhibited a significantly different carcass prevalence without corresponding changes in litter counts. The underlying mechanism remains unclear. Palm oil has been reported to exert antibacterial activity against E. coli in vitro (Edo et al., 2022), and palm olein oil has demonstrated efficacy against Salmonella on chocolate production line surfaces at elevated temperatures (Yehuda et al., 2025). Since compositional changes of fatty acids in skin samples were not evaluated in this study, further investigation is warranted to elucidate how palm oil alters tissue level fatty acid composition and its role in modulating Salmonella attachment to the skin.

Based on the outcomes of this study, the elevated number of Salmonella-positive carcasses may be attributed to the persistent presence of Salmonella in the litter environment. Prior research supports the potential for carcass contamination to originate from Salmonella-laden litter (Bhatia et al., 1979). It is also possible that Salmonella attached to the skin and feathers during periods of high cecal colonization or prevalence, and persisted throughout the grow-out phase, even if cecal counts declined at later stages. Previous findings have identified skin folds and feathers as key reservoirs of Salmonella during the grow-out period, even when cecal counts decline closer to market age (Rimet et al., 2019). Similarly, Mountzouris et al. (2015) demonstrated higher Salmonella prevalence and counts in cloacal and skin samples during processing, despite relatively low cecal levels, following dietary yeast supplementation and S. Enteritidis challenge. This observation underscores the need to focus not only on pre-harvest strategies but also on effective post-harvest interventions to address Salmonella reservoirs associated with skin and feathers.

In summary, the findings of this study offer valuable insights into potential intervention points, both antimicrobial and management-related, for reducing carcass contamination, beyond the influence of dietary fat sources. The data also emphasize the importance of comprehensive sampling strategies that include multiple tissues and environmental sites such as the crop, litter, and skin to more accurately detect Salmonella contamination within a flock prior to processing.

Conclusions

The findings of this study indicate that the clearance pattern of Salmonella from the ceca and internal organs was independent of the type of fat source included in the diet. Although Salmonella counts in the ceca were very low towards the end of the study, carcasses remained positive for Salmonella. This suggests that the presence of Salmonella in litter contaminating the external surface of bird including feathers may be a major source of carcass contamination. These results highlight the need for further investigation into litter amendment strategies along with other preharvest interventions to mitigate Salmonella contamination in poultry carcasses.
