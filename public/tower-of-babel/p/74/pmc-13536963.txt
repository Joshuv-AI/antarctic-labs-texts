Declines in endurance swimming performance with age: Analysis of 40 years of Masters one-hour distance swims
Authors: Robert T Rubin, Sosi Dzhugarian, Jason Clague, Jordan Young, Gina Karimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMasters swimmers’ sprint performance decrements with age have been <1% per year from ages 30–35 to ages 65–75, with an accelerated decline thereafter. We studied participants in the U.S. Masters swimming 1-hour virtual championship, which represents endurance rather than sprint swimming. We hypothesised a similar pattern of performance decline with age in this endurance event. We analyzed 40 years of 1-hour swims, 1985–2024, >50,000 entries total, using segmented regression analysis. Women’s performance decline (distance covered) with age was linear at approximately 0.5% per year until age 69 ± 1.8 (the transition age), after which their decline accelerated in a quadratic fashion. Men’s performance decline with age was linear at approximately 0.9% per year until age 75 ± 3.8, after which their decline also accelerated in a quadratic fashion. Our analysis of Masters 1-hour endurance swimming is consistent with earlier studies of Masters sprint swimming. The average transition age was approximately 6 years earlier for women (age 69) than for men (age 75), suggesting better training or tolerance for endurance swims among men. Some of the best swimmers continued their top performances for 10–13 years.KEYWORDS: Masters swimming, 1-hour swims, distance-yards, age groups, performance decrements

Introduction

As the world’s population continues to grow older, understanding the mental and physical toll of aging becomes increasingly important. Competitive athletes are a useful group for studying age-related physical performance decrements, because their activity levels are reasonably consistent, and their structured training regimens generally limit adverse events, such as sports injuries. Masters sports programs, in which athletes compete in 5-year age groups up to 100+ (Lepers et al., 2016), are flourishing internationally, and Masters athletes offer a reliable source of data for assessing age-related physical decline in physically fit women and men (Lepers & Stapley, 2016). Masters swimming, in particular, offers a rich dataset for study (Rubin & Rahe, 2010), because a greater number of older athletes continue swimming than high-impact sports such as running.

Competitive athletes generally experience a similar rate of decline in peak performance as does the general population, although they start from a higher baseline of physical fitness (Wiswell et al., 2001). Prior studies of performance decrements with aging in female and male Masters swimmers have consistently reported a decline of approximately 0.6%–0.7% per year from ages 30–35 to about ages 65–75 (the transition age), with an accelerated, quadratic decline thereafter (Bortz & Bortz, 1996; Donato et al., 2003; Fair, 2007, 2025; Fairbrother, 2007; Rubin et al., 2013; Tanaka & Seals, 1997, 2008). The primary physiological mechanism for the decline in performance appears to be a progressive decrease in VO2max, resulting from decreases in maximal cardiac stroke volume, heart rate and arteriovenous O2 differences (Tanaka & Seals, 2008).

Prior studies of swimming performance with age have focused mainly on sprint and intermediate distances, from 50 to 1500 yards or metres. In contrast, the U.S. Masters swimming (USMS) 1-hour virtual championship determines the maximum distance achieved in a one-hour period and represents endurance rather than sprint swimming. To date, only one study has examined 1-hour swimming results: Bongard et al. (2007) analysed the performances of 4271 women and men across the years 2001–2003. The results were presented in 10-year age groups from 19 to 79 years, plus a final group of swimmers >80. Nonlinear regression indicated a quadratic decline in performance across all age groups; mean performance decrements were similar to those found by others for shorter distance events. Men outperformed women at all ages by approximately 10% (416 yards).

In contrast to Bongard et al.’s 3-year dataset (2001–2003), our study includes more than 50,000 female and male entries across 40 years of Masters 1-hour championships, 1985–2024. This provided the opportunity, in a very large sample of endurance swimmers, (1) to test our hypothesis of an age-related linear decline followed by a quadratic decline, similar to the pattern of performance decline with age shown in sprint swimmers; (2) to extract the largest set of non-dominated swimming data to date (defined below) to determine a theoretical maximum performance with age; and (3) to evaluate secular trends in endurance swimming performance over a 40-year period.

Methods

Subjects

The USMS 1-hour virtual championship, held each year, is open to swimmers ages 18 and older, registered with the USMS and equivalent organisations in other countries. There are both individual and relay events; in this study, we consider only individual swimmers’ performances. USMS rules regarding swimwear, no assistive devices, etc., must be followed (https://www.usms.org/events/national-championships/virtual-championships/). Any stroke is legal (freestyle is by far the major stroke used); any pool of 25 yards or longer can be used; and metres are converted to yards for final distance. All events must take place in the month of January. Each swimmer must have a verifier to count laps and record cumulative split times, and an electronic timer must be used to time the event. Lap numbers and cumulative times are recorded on an official split sheet and submitted to the USMS.

Each swimmer’s name, sex, age, Masters club affiliation and total distance in yards are made public on the USMS website (https://www.usms.org/events/national-championships/virtual-championships/). We downloaded the 1-hour virtual championship individual swim records between 1985 and 2024. Because the records are publicly available, no informed consent or Institutional Review Board approval was required. For each year, the records for female and male swimmers ages 35-100 were transcribed into separate Excel spreadsheets. Based on earlier reported analyses of Masters swimming data (Tanaka & Seals, 2008; Fair, 2025, Bortz & Bortz, 1996; Donato et al., 2003; Fair, 2007, Fairbrother, 2007; Rubin et al., 2013; Tanaka & Seals, 1997), performance decline was assumed to have begun no earlier than age 35.

Six-letter identifiers were constructed from the first two letters of swimmers’ first names and the last four letters of their last names to serve as a flag for duplicate entries. Duplicate, and a few triplicate, six-letter identifiers were checked to determine whether the names and ages were the same, and duplicates were eliminated. On a few occasions, the same swimmer was recorded at two ages one year apart at different times. None of these entries were dominant (had the greatest distance) in either age group, thus not affecting the aggregated results. The younger age entry, therefore, was arbitrarily eliminated.

For each of the 40 years, the cleaned data were sorted by yearly age (35– 100) and by distance (shortest to longest) within each age to allow determination of the dominant (greatest) distance for each age, 35–100, across the 40 years of competitions, and the non-dominated distances across ages 35–100. Non-dominated distances are those distances not surpassed by a greater distance at a later age – a proxy for the theoretical maximum performance with age (Fair, 1994, 2025). For example, the maximum distances covered by women ages 36–38 (dominated distances; not shown) were all less than the ages 35 and 39 distances of 5645 and 5560 yards, respectively. Similarly, the maximum distances covered by women ages 40–42 (not shown) were all less than the ages 39 and 43 distances of 5560 and 5550 yards, respectively and so forth. Non-dominated data represented approximately half of the total yearly age data for both women and men.

Additionally, for each age, we identified the year within the 40-year period in which the dominant (greatest) distance occurred to examine temporal trends in maximum distance achieved.

Statistical analysis

As stated in the Introduction, we hypothesised a linear decrement in performance (distance) between age 35 and a transition age (65–75), after which the decrement would accelerate in a quadratic fashion. To determine this transition age, we applied segmented regression, which allows the estimation of breakpoints at which the linear relationship between the predictor and response variables shifts, providing insight into structural changes in the data. Because the model is fit directly to the data points across the transition, the linear and quadratic portions meet without a discontinuity.

We transformed the distance data as the natural log (ln) of the reciprocal of the distance in yards. The ln transformation permits estimation of percent decreases with age, and the reciprocal transformation allows the age-related decline to appear on the graphs as increasing for ease of perception (Fair, 1994, 2025).

Initially, an ordinary least squares (OLS) regression model was fitted, with age as the independent variable and ln(1/distance in yards) as the dependent variable. This preliminary model served as the foundation for the segmented regression analysis. Next, the segmented() function (v2.1-4) in R (v4.4.2) was applied (Muggeo, 2008, 2003; R Core Team, 2024), with age as the segmented variable, to introduce a breakpoint in the linear relationship. The segmented method estimates the breakpoint that minimises the model’s residual variance (mean squared error) through an iterative, score-based procedure.

An initial breakpoint age estimate of 36 was provided to facilitate convergence of the iterative estimation process, except for the non-dominated male data, for which an initial value of 44 was used, because the earliest observation occurred at age 43 (Table 1). The initial breakpoint age estimate was the year following the starting age to ensure that at least one observation existed in the first (linear) segment of the model. In each instance, with a likelihood-based approach, the segmented regression model refined this estimate in fewer than 25 iterations, optimising the breakpoint to where the change in slope was most statistically significant. The 95% confidence intervals for the breakpoint estimate were obtained to assess its accuracy, and the mean squared error and root mean squared error of the residuals were computed to evaluate the predictive accuracy of the overall regression model. The final model regression equation was Y = a + b1Xage ≤ breakpoint + b2Xage > breakpoint + b3Xage > breakpoint2.

For both women and men, there were sufficient participants until age 75 to provide dominant distance values across 38 of the 40 years, 1985–2024. At age 85, there were 20 years of participation across the 40 years for women and 25 years of participation for men. At age 90, participation decreased to 10 years out of 40 for women and 12 years out of 40 for men. Between ages 91 and 100, a steep decline in years of participation occurred, with a correspondingly large increase in regression variance. Thus, to preserve a sufficient sample size at each age, the maximum age for the regression analyses was truncated at 90.

Finally, Cook's distance was used to assess unduly influential data points in the regression analyses (Cook, 1977, 2011). The value for women at age 89 was a clear outlier (Cook’s distance = 2.66 vs. 0.09 at age 88 and 0.38 at age 90) and thus was eliminated. All the other data were used in the analyses.

Participants of both sexes increased in number over the 30 years 1985–2015, sharply decreased during the COVID-19 pandemic years 2020–2022, and slowly increased thereafter. For all women (Figure 1), 55 data points were used (ages 35–90 with the outlier at age 89 removed). Performance decline with age was linear at approximately 0.5% per year until age 69 ± 1.8 (the transition age) (Table 3), after which the decline accelerated. For the non-dominated women (Figure 2), there were 23 data points; performance decline with age again was linear at approximately 0.6% per year until age 70 ± 2.3, after which the decline accelerated.

For all men (Figure 3), there were 56 data points (ages 35–90). Performance decline with age was linear at approximately 0.9% per year until age 75 ± 3.8 (the transition age) (Table 3), after which the decline accelerated. For the non-dominated men (Figure 4), there were 23 data points; performance decline with age was linear at approximately 0.7% per year until age 76 ± 6.4, after which the decline accelerated.

Note that the signs of the quadratic coefficients are opposite for the women and the men. These results indicate that for women, the quadratic component introduced a slight convex shape in the curves (Figures 1 and 2), whereas for men, the quadratic component introduced a slight concave shape in the curves (Figures 3 and 4).

Figures 1 and 2 (women) versus Figures 3 and 4 (men) visually corroborate the differences in statistical uncertainty reflected in the standard errors (SEs) of the transition age estimates (Table 3, last column). The women exhibit sharp performance transitions at their estimated breakpoints, manifesting as a distinct “elbow” in the curves where the rate of decline accelerates abruptly. This structural clarity supports relatively precise breakpoint estimation (SEs ≈ 2 years).

In contrast, men exhibit gradual transitions characterised by less abrupt curve changes, making the transition age less definitive and resulting in greater estimation uncertainty (SEs ≈ 4–6 years). The two- to three-fold wider confidence intervals for men (15–25 years) vs. women (7–9 years) reflect this difference in transition sharpness.

Discussion

Performance decrements with ageing in Masters swimmers have been linear from ages 30–35 to a transition age (65–75), with an accelerated, quadratic decline thereafter. This decline has been shown primarily for sprint swimming events. In contrast, we studied performance decrements with ageing in Masters swimmers ages 35–90 over 40 years of USMS 1-hour virtual championships, an endurance event for the longest distance achieved in one hour. By accessing publicly available data from the USMS website over the years 1985–2024, we were able to include >25,000 female entries and >25,000 male entries.

As mentioned in the Introduction, Bongard et al. (2007) is the only other study to date that has analyzed 1-hour swimming results. The performances of 4271 women and men between 2001 and 2003 are presented by 10-year age groups. Nonlinear regression showed a quadratic decline in performance with age, but a segmented algorithm was not used, and a transitional age between linear and quadratic components was not estimated. In contrast, our data on >50,000 entries across 40 years were analysed by segmented regression, which indicated a linear decline in performance until a transition age of 69 ± 1.8 years in women and 75 ± 3.8 years in men (Table 3), after which there was an accelerated, quadratic performance decline. This finding is consistent with many earlier studies showing a similar, biphasic decline in swimming (and other sports) performance with aging (Tanaka & Seals, 2008; Fair, 2025, Bortz & Bortz, 1996; Donato et al., 2003; Fair, 2007, Fairbrother, 2007; Rubin et al., 2013; Tanaka & Seals, 1997).

The longest Masters swimming event distance for comparison to our 1-hour results is 1500 metres (1500 M). There are some differences in the events and data sets that make strict comparisons difficult: The 1500 M event is competitive against other swimmers and takes approximately 15 min, whereas the 1-hour swim is a single event. The 1500 M is a non-stop, speed race, whereas in the 1-hour event, some older swimmers may take a few seconds’ rest interval every 100 yards or so.

Additionally, our data are for ages 35–90, whereas Fair’s data (Fair, 2025) are for ages 40–95. Fair used his Fair-Parke Program (https://fairmodel.econ.yale.edu/fp/fp.htm) for segmented regression analysis, which is written in Fortran 77, whereas we used the segmented package in R (Muggeo, 2008, Muggeo, 2003; R Core Team, 2024). We attempted to replicate the Fair-Parke program in GNU Fortran 14.2.0, but some of the Fair-Parke commands did not work in the later Fortran version, so this was not possible.

Nevertheless, several meaningful comparisons can be drawn. Table 7 presents regression parameters for non-dominated data: Based on our large sample size, we were able to analyze twice as many data points for both women and men than were available to Fair. The transition ages for women are remarkably close (Fair: 69.3 years vs 1-hour: 70 years), but the transition ages for men are 16 years apart (Fair: 59.7 years vs 1-hour: 76 years). The standard errors of the transition age estimates (SE kˆ*) are similar for the 1-hour analyses and Fair’s analyses, suggesting similar precisions for the two regression techniques. Finally, it should be noted that Fair’s and our definitions of slopeαˆ are different. Fair (10) used the linear slope between ages 30–39, whereas we defined the slope as the linear segment immediately following the transition age before the quadratic component. Our values therefore are more positive than Fair’s values, reflecting the steeper slopes. Because we used Fair’s transform for our data, the differences indicated in Table 7 are most likely due to the different regression techniques used, as noted above.

Fair (2025) also discussed the problem of survivor bias in studies of performance decrements with aging – as aging progresses, weaker subjects drop out at a faster rate, resulting in over-representation of more talented, surviving subjects, and estimated rates of decline that are too small (e.g., 6). Fair (2025) used world records, indicating that this avoids survivor bias, although a problem of small sample sizes still may have existed in his data. In the USMS 1-hour virtual championships, there are no world records, but our use of the longest distances at each yearly age recorded over 40 years of these competitions might be considered equivalent to a world record. We then calculated the non-dominated times from these 40-year best distances, yielding the largest non-dominated dataset likely available. We also truncated our analyses at age 90, where we still had 10 values for the women and 12 values for the men across the 40 years, from which we extracted the longest distances at that age. We therefore consider both survivor bias and the small-sample problem to be of minimal influence in our analyses.

Frontier regression, which attempts to model optimal rather than average behaviour, is another possible approach to data analysis (Fair, 2025; Troutt et al., 2003). Frontier regression forces all errors to be positive, thus lying on or above the estimated frontier. As Fair (2025) indicated, the use of frontier regression vs. nonlinear least squares regression on his 1500 M swim data produced little effect on the slope coefficients and kˆ*, primarily affecting the estimate of the constant term, which was not of concern. This supports Fair’s consideration of his world record data as a good estimate of the biological frontier. Given this information and our very large sample size (also a good estimate of the biological frontier), we did not attempt frontier regression analysis on our data.

Hartley and Hartley (2024) examined U.S. Masters swimming national records and the top 10 swimmers’ times for 1981 and for 2021, a 40-year gap. The performance of both women and men improved between 1981 and 2021, averaging 0.52% per year, with women improving more than men. A linear decline until approximately age 65, with an accelerated, quadratic decline thereafter, was evident in all four cohorts of swimmers. Factors Hartley and Hartley (2024) postulated as influential included a gradual increase in the size and strength of swimmers, an increase in the intensity of training swims, more hydrodynamically efficient swimsuits, and a constituent year effect, whereby swimmers tend to compete for the first year or two years and then drop out temporarily.

We found a similar increase in performance over the 40 years from 1985 to 2024, as indicated in Table 5. For women, 58% of cases occurred in the last 10 years, and 90% occurred in the last 20 years. For men, 64% of the cases occurred in the last 10 years, and 97% occurred in the last 20 years. In line with Hartley and Hartley (2024), we attribute this improvement in endurance swimming to several factors, including, inter alia, a greater number of competitors in the later decades (Table 4) with better nutrition and physical conditioning, improvements in swim-suit design, and newer, more efficiently designed and constructed swimming pools.

Some women and men of all ages, including well into their 90s, dominated in their age groups over two or more years, as noted above (Table 6). For women, the longest periods were three years for swimmers DD (ages 66–68) and HH (ages 96–98). For men, the years of dominance were longer: 13 years for swimmer JJ (ages 35–47, with 2 years omitted), 10 years for swimmer LL (ages 77–86, with 1 year omitted), and 7 years for swimmer OO (ages 92–98, with 1 year omitted). These accomplishments attest to the ability of swimmers to maintain their peak performance for considerable periods of time and over many decades of life.

Conclusion

Prior studies of swimming performance decrements with age have focused mainly on sprint and intermediate distances, from 50 to 1500 yards or metres. In contrast, we studied swimmers in the U.S. Masters swimming (USMS) 1-hour virtual championships from 1985 to 2024, representing 40 years of endurance swimming. The sample size was an average of 668 female and 670 male participants per year, a total of >25,000 entries for each sex.

The results of this very large study accord reasonably well with the findings of earlier studies of sprint swimming, in that performance decrements occurred linearly between age 35 and a transition age (69 for women and 75 for men), after which an accelerated, quadratic decline occurred. The transition age occurred six years earlier for women than for men, suggesting that better training or tolerance for endurance swims among men. Most of the best performances occurred in the years 2015–2024, and some of the best swimmers, particularly men, continued their top performances for 10–13 years.

Our results indicate that endurance swimming, in addition to competitive sprint swimming, is a sport for all ages. Some endurance records have been held for several years by swimmers at 90s. The 1-hour swim, as a virtual event held throughout the month of January, is available to everyone with an accessible regulation pool and a person to be the timer. For older individuals, pausing for a few seconds periodically during the swim is permitted and can enhance performance. The relative ease of participation and accomplishment in the 1-hour virtual endurance swim makes it an excellent complement to the more traditionally organised Masters swimming competitions.
