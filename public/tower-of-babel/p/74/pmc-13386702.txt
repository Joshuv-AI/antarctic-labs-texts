Adapting the Serious Illness Conversation communication training workshop from the Western to an Asian context in Singapore
Authors: Gillian Li Gek Phua, Radhika Das, Anna So Youn Lee, Annushkha Sharanya Sinnathamby, Prabavathi D/O Balachandran, Swee Noi Tang, Alethea Chung Pheng Yee, Shirlyn Hui-Shan Neo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/objectivesThe Serious Illness Care Program (SICP) was developed in the United States and consists of three main componentsthe–Serious Illness Conversation Guide (SICG), Serious Illness Conversation (SIC) communication training, and quality improvement system tools like dashboards to track SIC processes and uptake. We anticipated that SIC training would need to be contextualized locally for training to be more effective for local clinicians. Our purpose now is to present our adaptation of the SIC communication training program from the Western to the Asian context.MethodsThe adaptation was carried out in three phases. The first phase was a virtual SIC workshop in July 2022; feedback was analysed quantitatively and qualitatively to inform localisation of training methodology. In phase two, we piloted a 1.5-day localised face to face workshop in November 2022; feedback was analysed quantitatively and qualitatively for acceptability. In phase three, ten maintenance runs of local SIC workshops were conducted from March 2023 to May 2024. Feedback from attending healthcare practitioners (HCPs) was analysed quantitatively to evaluate the impact of the workshops on learners over the 26 months.ResultsForty-six, 38 and 402 HCPs were trained in each phase of the SIC Workshops, respectively. Feedback from phase one indicated the need for context-specific materials for Singapore and enhancement of learning experience through group discussions. This was incorporated in phase two, wherein post-workshop 100% of the participants had increased self-reported knowledge regarding SICs in the local context. Evaluation of the maintenance runs of the local SIC workshops over 26 months demonstrated consistent feedback; six-months after the workshop more than 85% of the respondents had used the SICG in their practice.ConclusionsAs literature on cross-cultural adaptation is scarce, we present a study of the adaptation of a training workshop from the Western context to the Asian context. Adapted workshops were highly rated by participants with increased self-reported knowledge and confidence in the use of the SICG. Our adaptation process offers reflection points for international healthcare settings that are considering scaling their educational programs cross-culturally.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09504-0.

Introduction

Serious illness is defined as a “health condition that carries a high risk of mortality and either negatively impacts a person’s daily function or quality of life or excessively strains their caregivers” [1]. Patients with serious illness experience a poor quality of life and emotional distress due to high symptom burden and uncertainty about their illness trajectory [2]. Serious illness conversations (SICs) between healthcare practitioners (HCPs) and patients about their values and priorities help clinicians align treatments with patients’ goals, improving their quality of life [3]. These conversations sit within the “advance care planning (ACP) continuum”, which includes communications to help people plan for future end-of-life decisions [4].

However, evidence suggests that such conversations occur infrequently in clinical practice [3]. Studies published internationally and in Singapore on barriers to SICs/ACPs have shown that lack of clinician training, confusion over what are SICs/ACPs and concerns about causing patients’ distress through communication are common barriers to conversations [5, 6]. The Serious Illness Care Program (SICP) was developed by Ariadne Labs in the United States to address these barriers and improve communication between clinicians and patients with serious illnesses [7]. The program consists of three main components – (1) the Serious Illness Conversation Guide (SICG), a structured communication framework that guides conversations between a HCP and their patient with serious illness, (2) serious illness communication training for HCPs to learn and practice using the SICG, and (3) quality improvement and documentation tools to help HCPs integrate serious illness care into their routine work [7]. A randomized controlled trial to evaluate the SICP in oncology practice showed that the intervention, readily accessible in electronic medical records of these patients, led to more, earlier, and better conversations regarding values and priorities of seriously ill patients, resulting in reduced anxiety and depression symptoms in patients [8, 9].

The original US SIC training workshop is a short 2.5-hour session [7]. It begins with a brief didactic session (< 30 min) on the evidence for ACP discussions, followed by a demonstration on the use of the SICG (30 min) and individualised practice using role-plays with trained actors serving as standardised patients (> 60 min). Role-play is carried out in small groups of six to ten participants per session with feedback from attending palliative care physicians.

The SICP and/or SICG has been adapted in Western countries [10–15], but there is limited research on the adaptation and implementation of the SICP in non-Western settings [16]. In Singapore, an intersection of an aging population [17] and a rising prevalence of individuals living with serious or chronic illnesses [18] underscores a compelling need for healthcare institutions to equip clinicians with fundamental skills in conducting SIC. Though we have previously developed a culturally adapted SICG that can be used in Singapore’s multicultural society [19], the use of this tool still requires the buy-in of HCPs and competency through training HCPs to use it. Given that communication is influenced by cultural norms, successful implementation would involve adapting SIC communication training to an Asian context [20], by providing HCPs insights on navigating SICs locally, which is not addressed in training meant for US clinicians.

Our rationale for this is due to several unique cultural factors that are present in the Asian context. First, whilst it was very common traditionally for family members to be involved in medical decision-making [21] in Asia [22, 23], there has been a slow shift in Asian patients’ preferences for more information about their life-threatening illnesses [20, 22]. However, few patients are involved in such discussions. Therefore, there needs to be specific training related to the local context that will allow HCPs to navigate these nuances [24].

Second, religious and cultural beliefs also influence Asian patients’ attitudes towards talking about death and dying, an essential topic of consideration during conversations regarding future care-planning and decision-making [25–27]. Singapore is a multi-racial Asian society which is predominantly ethnically Chinese (74.3%), Malay (13.5%) and Indian (9.0%), and religious affiliations include Buddhism (31.1%), Christianity (18.9%), Hinduism (5.0%), and Islam (15.6%); clinicians thus need to be aware of the sensitivities regarding discussing death with patients of diverse races and religions, as well as the factors that contribute to their readiness to engage in an SIC [28].

Third, there is a need to empower allied healthcare professionals to conduct end-of-life conversations, as they typically defer to attending physicians in the paternalistic medical model in Asia [29]. This is necessary as allied healthcare are also a valued part of the overall care team and patients may have more rapport with them than the attending physicians, hence allowing such conversations to happen more organically.

Finally, HCPs need to be trained to respond to “passively expressed emotions”, such as hidden and non-verbal expression of sadness, anger, and/or disbelief, which are encountered routinely in clinical practice in Asia [30, 31].

Thus, to enable a successful SICP in Singapore, we anticipated that SIC training would need to be contextualized locally to allow training to be more effective for local HCPs. Our team aimed to carry out this localisation using a three phase process.

Methodology

Recruitment

Through each phase, participants were recruited by advertising the workshops on Lien Centre for Palliative Care (LCPC)’s website, mailing list, social media channels, and word-of-mouth publicity. No prerequisites were required from workshop registrants other than being HCPs who care for patients with serious illness and proficiency in English. The workshops were registered with the relevant professional bodies for participants to receive Continuing Medical Education (CME) credits. Demographic data of participants was collected.

Cultural adaptation frameworks

Hofstede’s cultural dimension theory explains how national cultures differ in their values and provides a framework that predicts differences in communication styles, leadership, teamwork and decision-making. The six key dimensions identified include distribution of power, individualism versus collectivism, masculinity versus femininity, avoidance of uncertainty, long-term versus short-term orientation, indulgence versus restraint in gratification of desires. This theory related to the conceptualization of what was important for the team to consider, as we sought to culturally adapt the workshop to fit out local cultural needs which may affect local learners’ communication behaviour [32].

We also relied broadly on cultural adaptation frameworks for program intervention adaptation and education collaboration to define the tasks we needed to do, taking guidance from two studies. The first was a scoping review of cultural adaptation frameworks or models for public health that included the tasks of information gathering about the community, developing a preliminary adapted program that they then pilot tested with over 50% making refinements thereafter [33]. The second described an international partnership between a Chinese and American university in adapting a US based master’s program for occupational therapy in China, where the authors shared a framework for curriculum adaptation based on their experience that was like the first scoping review [34].

Phase 1 - timelines and data collection methodology including training of local trainers/early adopters for local adaptation of SIC training

To allow our local trainers and early adopters in Singapore to understand how to conduct SIC from the experts who first derived SIC, the first SIC workshop in Singapore was held virtually in July 2022 for 46 participants, with resources and faculty provided by Ariadne Labs. Local Singaporean trainers were invited to co-facilitate with the faculty from Ariadne Labs (timetable in Supplementary Table 1). The curriculum was adapted from the US format but was extended to three days of 2.5 h each to enhance the learning experience through a cognitive constructivist approach with guided discussions, group interaction, individual and group reflections [Supplementary Table 1].

The first day consisted of didactic lectures on the evidence for SICP in the US, participant reflection, and demonstration of the SICG using a video clip from the US, for participants to understand the benefits of conducting SICs.

The second day was developed for participants to understand the steps of the SICG and practice use of the SICG language in scripted role-plays (between paired participants within small groups, where one participant reads the script of the HCP, and the other of the patient; scripts were based on Ariadne labs’ SICG). Such role-plays create a psychologically safe space for participants to practice using the guide, different from the US training which involves standardized patient encounters. The scripted role-plays were followed by small group discussions and then a large group debrief. After this session, participants were asked to use the SICG in their own clinical practice to practice their skills; this is also an additional component to the SIC training conducted in the US.

The last day was dedicated to small and large group discussions on the HCPs’ experience with using the SICG, barriers and enablers, and finally a short lecture on the similarities and differences between SIC and ACP.

As part of this workshop, both quantitative and qualitative feedback were reviewed on how the SIC workshop could be localised to the Singapore context [35]. Based on feedback, the training workshop was adapted for our subsequent phase (pilot run of the first local SIC workshop).

Description of quantitative data collection and analysis

After completing the last day of the workshop, participants completed a mandatory post-workshop evaluation (Microsoft Forms) to indicate their learning and overall experience on a Likert scale (Supplementary File 2 A). Quantitative data was analysed using descriptive statistics. Six months after the workshop, an optional online survey was sent to workshop participants to assess their use of the SICG in their clinical setting. This survey was sent to understand participants’ use of the SICG in their respective contexts (online survey form in Supplementary File 2B).

Description of qualitative data collection and analysis

Upon completing the last session of each workshop day, participants were asked to share their daily reflections in a Google document. Large group sharing sessions were recorded verbatim by LCPC course administrators and transcribed. Indirect feedback on the role-play activities was gathered by facilitators and shared during facilitator debriefing sessions; this feedback was recorded verbatim by the LCPC course administrators. This qualitative data was compiled and coded by one of the authors AS, for analysis. The research team comprised clinician-educators involved in course delivery; reflexivity was maintained through regular discussions to examine potential biases. Thematic analysis was conducted inductively, with codes generated iteratively and refined through constant comparison. Rigor was enhanced through independent review of a subset of transcripts by multiple authors (ASL, AS, GP), with consensus-based resolution of discrepancies and maintenance of an audit trail. Methodological triangulation across reflections, discussions, and facilitator feedback supported interpretation. Representative quotes illustrated themes. Formal member checking was not performed; findings were reviewed within the facilitator group for consistency.

Phase 2 - pilot run of first local SIC workshop

We conducted the first local SIC workshop in a 1.5-day face-to-face format in November 2022 (timetable in Supplementary Table 1). Thirty-eight participants attended the workshop. Similar surveys and analyses were run as in phase one to gather feedback on the first pilot run.

Phase 3 - maintenance run of local SIC workshops

The localised format of the SIC workshop was used for 10 SIC face-to-face workshops conducted from March 2023 to May 2024. Twenty to fifty participants were enrolled in each SIC workshop.

Similar post-workshop surveys were conducted and data analysed quantitatively to collect feedback. At that point, only minor iterations were made to the curriculum.

About one-third of the participants (32.6%) completed the six months outcome survey with 80% reporting that they had used the SICG. All respondents (100%) agreed that the SICG helped them understand their patients’ values and goals of care. Additionally, 73.34% agreed or strongly agreed that the SIC can be done in a reasonable amount of time.

Changes in curriculum based on survey results, qualitative analysis and feedback

Instead of three 2.5-hour sessions over three days as per the first US-SG workshop, local learners indicated a preference for a full-day, in-person workshop to optimize their time. Based on this feedback from the first workshop, it was agreed that the subsequent pilot SIC workshop would be run in a 1.5-day face-to-face format locally, with more time allocated to evidence for SIC, demonstration of the SICG and practice as well as group learning in the Singapore clinical context.

Additional steps were also taken to contextualize SIC within the current ACP landscape in Singapore. These included replacing testimonials from US clinicians with those from Singaporean HCPs to make the content more relatable, and an activity on “Which of these critical abilities matter most to me” was introduced to facilitate group interaction.

The SICG Practice session was also refined with the “scripted role plays” adapted to enhance relevance, applicability, and authenticity by incorporating communication micro-skills, adding Singapore-specific expressions, and creating specialty-specific scenarios (oncology, renal, hospice, advanced dementia, cardiology, and cardiothoracic) addressing family dynamics and narratives unique to Singapore. Participants were grouped to practise the scripted role-play most appropriate for their clinical specialty.

The second half-day (after the participants had practised a SIC in their own clinical practice) was also more focused on group learning, with sharing of participant reflections and group discussions on enablers and barriers for implementation of SICs in each clinician’s setting using guided questions (such as patients’ selection for SIC).

Phase 2: local SIC workshop pilot run, November 2022

Participant demographics (Table 1)

Thirty-eight participants attended the SIC workshop in November 2022, including 12 doctors (31.58%), 15 nurses (39.47%), six MSWs (15.79%) and five other allied HCPs (13.16%). All participants were either from restructured hospitals (n = 16, 42.11%) or community settings (n = 22, 57.89%).

Quantitative analysis of feedback for phase 2: immediate post-workshop evaluation (Table 2)

Ninety-four point 75% of participants completed the post-workshop evaluation. Though all participants “agreed” or “strongly agreed” that they gained new knowledge or skills from the workshop, a much higher proportion of participants than in phase one had “strongly agreed” with this. Furthermore, as compared to phase one, more participants “strongly agreed” that they could apply the knowledge/skills to their work (55.56 vs. 72.22%), and that they would use what they learnt in the workshop (60.00 vs. 77.80%). Similarly, more participants “strongly agreed” that the coverage and depth of the workshop was sufficient (51.11 vs. 69.44%), that they were satisfied with the workshop (57.78 vs. 75.00%) and would recommend the workshop to their peers (62.22 vs. 80.56%). 94.44% of the participants indicated that the duration of the workshop was “just right”.

About one-third of the participants (31.6%) completed the survey. Phase 3: maintenance runs of local SIC workshops

Ten SIC workshops were conducted from March 2023 to May 2024 (over 26 months) in the locally adapted format. Iterative adaptations were made to the scripted role-plays, such as including multiple versions of scenarios within the same specialty (for example, oncology scenarios included versions set in an inpatient hospice or an acute ward) and caregiver scripted role-plays. The SICG was also simultaneously being adapted locally in Singapore [17], and the most updated version was used for each workshop.

Participant demographics (Table 1)

Four hundred and two participants attended the SIC workshops. More than half of the participants were nurses (n = 232, 57.71%). The rest were doctors (n = 57, 14.18%), MSWs (n = 50, 12.44%) and other allied HCPs (n = 63, 15.67%). Most worked in either restructured hospitals (n = 233, 57.96%) or community settings (n = 136, 33.83%), with only 33 participants from other organisations (8.21%).

Ninety-four point 53% of the participants completed the post-workshop evaluation. Consistent with the pilot local SIC workshop, 98.68% “agreed” or “strongly agreed” that they gained new knowledge and skills from the workshop, wherein almost three-fourths “strongly agreed” that they gained new knowledge or skills from the workshop (74.47%), that they can apply these knowledge/skills to their work (72.11%), and that they would use what they learnt in the workshop (76.58%). Similarly, 98.95% of the participants “agreed” or “strongly agreed” that they were satisfied with the workshop, with almost three-fourths of these participants strongly agreeing that the coverage and depth of the workshop was sufficient (72.11%), that they were satisfied with the workshop (75.53%) and that they would recommend the workshop to their peers (77.63%). Sustainability review at 6 months (phase 3) (Table 3)

Twenty-seven point 61% of the participants responded to the six-month outcome survey. More than 85% of the respondents had used the SICG. Most respondents were based in acute hospitals. Of the respondents who had conducted SICs, more than 80% estimated speaking with one to ten patients within the six months. Similar to phase two, 99.09% of the respondents agreed that the SIC helped them understand their patient’s values and goals of care. Ninety-nine-point-1% found the guide easy to use, with 90.99% indicating that the SIC can be done in a reasonable amount of time without affecting clinical work. Approximately 65% of the respondents indicated that the SIC made their patient’s emotional state, and their own anxiety about having these conversations, “much better” or “slightly better”. The outcomes were relatively consistent across different clinical professions- for example, 80% of doctors who responded agreed or strongly agreed that SICs can be done in a reasonable amount of time, whilst 100% of nurses and MSWs strongly agreed or agreed with this.

No qualitative data was collected in phase 3.

Discussion

We report our successful adaptation of the SIC communication training workshop from the US to Singapore context in three phases. The first phase highlighted the need for localisation of the US lectures and “scripted role-plays” for practice of the SICG in the Singapore context, as well as for more group discussions for experiential learning. This was included in the first pilot run of the locally adapted SIC workshop held in November 2022 in a face-to-face format (phase two); all participants agreed that they gained new knowledge and skills immediately after the workshop. Qualitative feedback also showed that participants understood how to conduct SICs in their own local work setting with an intention to use the SICG in clinical practice. Participants appreciated the depth of discussion and insights gained during the SIC workshop and understood the role of SIC within the ACP continuum in Singapore.

In the maintenance runs of the locally adapted SIC workshops (phase three, from March 2023 to May 2024), our quantitative results showed that the training was consistently highly rated over the 26 months Immediately after the workshop, 98.68% of the participants indicated that they gained new knowledge and skills from the workshop. Moreover, the training was found to be sustainable, with more than 85% of the respondents to the six-months outcome survey having used the SICG in their practice. All respondents reported that the SICG helped them understand the values and goals of their patients, with 90% indicating that the SIC could be done in a reasonable amount of time without affecting clinical work.

We initiated numerous changes from the US-based SIC workshops [5] for the local adaptation in Singapore emphasising contextualised, collaborative, and reflective learning, these are broadly summarised in Table 4 in the appendix and discussed in detail below.

Firstly, we changed the workshop format and duration to a face-to-face format, spread over 1.5 days instead of being conducted virtually in two to three hours (as was done in the US) [7]. Even though short virtual trainings can be time-effective for busy clinicians, the face-to-face workshops were well attended and provided enhanced opportunities for group-based learning. Participants appreciated the time spent in discussion with experienced faculty to gain practical insights into conducting SICs. The debriefing and peer exchange of personal experiences created greater authenticity and meaning. The large group exchange of practice wisdom was deliberately designed as part of reflective practice to share ideas on the use of the SICG and the opportunities and challenges encountered, allowing participants to co-construct their own schema regarding the importance of SICs and use of SICG in the respective settings. Debriefing and reflective practice has been shown to be an effective learning method in healthcare workers [35]. Group learning in specialty-specific groups was also relevant to the participant’s own clinical practice.

Secondly, to ensure that SICG was practised with religious and cultural nuances that were appropriate to the Singapore context, we made sure to include colloquial expressions, and general patient deference to family’s decisions prevalent in Asia and Singapore [20, 21]. In line with cultural norms, it was emphasised that sections such as sharing prognosis were optional and needed to be individualised based on the patient/family’s readiness, and the HCP’s profession as non-physicians may not be comfortable with sharing prognosis but can still discuss the other components of the SICG. Inclusion of scenarios specific to a variety of specialities and grouping of participants to practice the scripted role-play most relevant to their speciality also increased participant’s understanding of the applicability of SICs in their own setting. We also included a scripted role-play for conducting SICs with caregivers, as it is common for SICs to be conducted with family members in Asia.

Thirdly, we used testimonials from local Singaporean HCPs to convince our learners about the benefits of conducting SICs and providing holistic patient-centred care for their patients with serious illness. SICs were also contextualized within the ACP landscape in Singapore, so that participants could distinguish the two and understand the timing and role of conducting SICs with their patients. It was also emphasised that SIC could be done over several sessions with the same patient (to factor in time constraints in the clinic) and that HCPs could use the parts of the SICG relevant to their clinical practice (for example, the “prognosis” portion was optional for nurses) [36].

Fourthly, we also included localised “scripted role-plays” to practice the SICG framework, instead of role play with standardised patients (SPs). Role plays with SPs put learners under the scrutiny of the class and have been deemed to be stressful for learners [37]. This anxiety may impede the learning process. Additionally, using SPs can limit the variety of clinical scenarios and strategies that can be introduced [38, 39]. In contrast, reading a scripted role-play with peers from the same specialty offer several advantages; they are standardizable, low-risk, and easily repeatable. By incorporating communication micro skills into scripted role-playing exercises, where participants assume both HCP and patient roles, valuable teaching opportunities are created to reinforce empathetic behaviour, which is essential for effective serious illness conversations [37].

Lastly, guided questions were provided for discussion and learning on integrating SICs into one’s own clinical practice (for example, what setting to start, how to identify suitable patients for SIC, where to document the SIC and the stakeholders to engage) [40]. This is specifically relevant to the implementation of SICs in Singapore, given that the SIC initiative is at its nascent stage, and enablers as well as tools for documentation are yet being established. Another goal of spreading the workshop over two days was to introduce task-based learning in the curriculum [36]. Day two of the workshop was intentionally conducted a week after day one to allow participants to practise at least one SIC in their own clinical practice during that week. Faculty guided the participants on appropriate patient selection, and the participants were able to conduct and discuss their experiences in conducting SICs with the faculty and the group thereafter. Social cognitivist in nature, this experiential learning was critical as competency cannot be achieved through passive learning alone in adult learners [41]. As participants partake in cognitive and social processes of reflection, interaction and exchanges with members of the community, self-reflection and processing, they acquire knowledge, skills, values and these may effect behavioural changes as reflected in the six-months outcome survey.

Our study has several strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported adaptations of the SIC training workshops to the cultural context in Asia. Other common communication tools developed for more Western contexts have previously been successfully adapted, whilst similarly highlighting the need for cultural adaptation to handle sensitivities regarding family involvement in medical decision-making [19]. For example, the VitalTalk program has been culturally adapted in Japan, by incorporating training on identifying suppressed emotions [30, 31]. Similarly, another ACP communication training programme in South Korea emphasised the need to empower nurses to conduct such conversations, as they might take a passive stance due to a lack of knowledge and skills, and they consider such conversations (which also involve providing prognostic information) as the task of physicians [29]. This too was taken into consideration whilst developing our workshop, with the majority of our SIC workshop participants being nurses.

There are several limitations to our study as well. Firstly, we used participant self-reported surveys to gauge the gain in skills and knowledge after the SIC workshops. There were no pre-workshop assessment, control group or faculty-administered assessments after the workshop to objectively assess participants’ gain in knowledge and skills. Evidence suggests that self-assessment of clinical skills has limitations [42, 43]. Nonetheless, self-assessments are routinely used in communication training programmes in the palliative care setting [44]. Moreover, the fact that a large proportion of respondents to the six-months outcomes survey conducted SICs in clinical practice is indicative of skill acquisition. Secondly, the participants of these workshops were likely inherently interested in acquiring SIC skills, thus the post-workshop evaluation and six-months outcomes may not be representative of the typical Singaporean clinician. Nonetheless, given that almost 500 HCPs from different clinical backgrounds and institutions across Singapore attended the workshop with similar outcomes, it is likely that the results are generalizable to clinicians practising in Singapore. Lastly, it is unlikely that seriously ill communication training alone will be enough to result in sustained improvement in patient outcomes and clinician satisfaction [45]. In the six-months outcomes survey, only 65% of the respondents indicated that the SIC made their patient’s emotional state, and their own anxiety about having these conversations “much better” or “slightly better”, highlighting the need for system changes to support clinicians in a sustained manner to deliver better patient-centred conversations and patient support. Future work should address these limitations. For more robust assessment, our team has plans to assess how knowledge and skills developed during the SIC workshop translates to communication in patient settings. For this, our team is piloting the multi-component SICP in Singapore. Clinicians trained in the Singapore context to use the locally adapted SICG [19] are being provided system-level tools for documentation and quality improvement initiatives (such as nudges for patient selection and reminders about conducting SICs) to help them integrate serious illness care into their usual work.

Conclusion

Adapting the SIC workshops to Singapore’s healthcare context has increased self-reported clinician knowledge and skills on communication skills needed to conduct SICs in the local setting. Our localisation provides valuable lessons for similar efforts in other regions. Ongoing assessment of the SIC workshops and implementation of the SICP in Singapore will be crucial to ultimately contributing towards better patient outcomes and enhanced clinician satisfaction.
