Giant neonatal cystic hygroma with delayed airway obstruction: A case report of emergency surgery in a resource-limited setting
Authors: Mathayo Shadrack, Helga Naburi, Nimwindael Stephen Msangi, Ibrahim Mkoma, Abbas Musa Mungia, Zaituni Bokhary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCystic hygroma is a rare congenital lymphatic malformation, typically presenting in the cervicofacial region. While large lesions usually cause immediate respiratory distress, delayed airway obstruction can occur, especially in low-resource settings with limited prenatal care and neonatal support.Case presentationA term male neonate was delivered in a rural setting with a large right cervicofacial mass consistent with cystic hygroma. Introduction

Cystic hygroma (CH) is a rare congenital lymphatic anomaly, with an incidence of 1 in 6000 live births and a marked predilection for the cervicofacial region [1,2]. It represents a subtype of lymphangiomas and often arises from failure of embryonic lymphatic sacs to establish communication with the venous system. CH is commonly diagnosed during prenatal imaging or presents immediately after birth as a soft, cystic, often translucent mass.

Large CHs can obstruct the airway, and in many cases, emergency airway management is required at birth [3,4]. However, in rare instances, even giant CHs may not compromise airway function immediately, only to cause delayed obstruction days or weeks later due to rapid cyst expansion, hemorrhage, or infection [5,6].

In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), access to surgical care for such conditions is limited by geography, infrastructure, and shortage of trained specialists [7]. Early referral and prompt communication with surgical centers are vital. This case illustrates the unique clinical course of a giant CH that transitioned from a stable to a critical state within weeks, emphasizing both the challenges and feasibility of high-stakes neonatal surgery in a resource-limited environment. It was reported in accordance with SCARE Guideline for transparency and completeness [8].

Case presentation

A term male neonate was delivered via cesarean section in a rural health outpost approximately 500 km from the nearest tertiary facility. The infant, born at 39 weeks with a birth weight of 4.8 kg, had a large, soft, fluctuant mass over the right side of the neck, extending into the face and shoulder. He was otherwise stable, with no respiratory distress, cyanosis, or feeding difficulties.

Local clinicians recognized the anomaly as a likely cystic hygroma and promptly contacted a tertiary pediatric surgical center using mobile communication. Despite the distance and logistical barriers, the neonate was transported within 72 h by basic ambulance services with ongoing monitoring.

Upon arrival, examination confirmed a large, transilluminant cervicofacial mass measuring approximately 15 cm Fig. 1. Ultrasound showed a multiloculated cystic lesion with thin septations and no solid components. Contrast-enhanced CT scan revealed a 17 × 12 × 9 cm multiloculated cystic mass Fig. 2 involving the posterior cervical triangle, parapharyngeal, parotid, and carotid spaces Fig. 3, extending to the upper chest wall Fig. 4. The lesion displaced the airway to the left. Fig. 5A and B, but did not cause obstruction at the time. A second subcutaneous cyst was identified over the right upper chest.

CT scan of the brain revealed no intracranial extension of the lesions and demonstrated normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow Fig. 6. The patient remained stable for two weeks under close observation. However, by the third week, he developed progressive inspiratory stridor, suprasternal retractions, and cyanotic episodes suggestive of upper airway obstruction from mass effect. A multidisciplinary meeting was convened involving pediatric surgeons, ENT, anesthetists, and neonatologists.

The axial CT scan of the brain shows no evidence of intracranial lesions and demonstrates normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation. The lateral ventricles (blue arrow), third ventricle (red arrow), and fourth ventricle (green arrow) are all appropriately filled with CSF, indicating unobstructed flow and normal ventricular system configuration.

An emergency operation was planned. Through a transverse cervical incision Fig. 7A and B, the cystic mass was dissected carefully from surrounding neurovascular structures Fig. 8A and B, including the internal jugular vein, carotid artery, and vagus nerve. The lesion contained 2.3 l of clear serosanguinous fluid and was excised completely Fig. 9, including the smaller anterior chest wall component, which had no thoracic extension.

A and B: Image A illustrates the transverse cervical surgical incision (blue arrow) used for access, while Image B shows successful exposure of the cystic neck mass (yellow arrow). The approach provided excellent visualization of the lesion, facilitating careful dissection and minimizing injury to surrounding vital structures during the surgical procedure.

A and B: Demonstrates meticulous dissection of the cystic mass from adjacent structures. Vital structures, including the sternocleidomastoid muscles (black arrow) and major neck vessels, were clearly identified following complete cystectomy. Additionally, a small subcutaneous cystic lesion (blue arrow) was discovered in the right upper anterior chest wall, with no evidence of intrathoracic communication.

Shows successful skin closure with hemostasis achieved (black arrow). The cystic fluid was aspirated and appeared macroscopically serosanguinous (blue arrow), indicating a mixture of blood and serum. The procedure concluded without complications, ensuring both structural and cosmetic integrity of the surgical site.

Postoperatively, the child was monitored in the NICU. He recovered uneventfully, stridor resolved, and oral feeds were restarted within 48 h. No tracheostomy was needed. Histopathology confirmed a macrocystic lymphatic malformation (cystic hygroma) Fig. 10. The child was discharged on postoperative day 7 and is currently thriving under outpatient follow-up.

Discussion

Cystic hygroma arises from developmental failure of lymphatic-venous communication. While 75–80 % of lesions occur in the cervicofacial region, not all cause immediate distress [2]. Macrocystic types may remain asymptomatic unless they expand rapidly, leading to delayed airway obstruction, as in our case.

Most giant CHs identified prenatally prompt planned delivery with EXIT (Ex Utero Intrapartum Treatment) procedures or immediate airway support [9]. However, in LMICs, prenatal detection is rare and neonatal airway support limited. Our case is unusual in that the child, though harboring a massive lesion, remained asymptomatic for weeks, allowing time for referral and planning.

The CT findings were crucial in mapping the lesion's extent and planning the surgery. The absence of airway narrowing at baseline falsely reassured the team, though the subsequent progression justified emergency surgery. Rapid fluid accumulation in lymphatic channels due to minor trauma, hemorrhage, or infection may explain the delayed presentation [4,5].

Surgical excision remains the gold standard for symptomatic lesions, especially in LMICs where access to repeated sclerotherapy is constrained. The successful removal in this case was made possible by preoperative planning, effective airway anticipation, and multidisciplinary collaboration, a vital model for future emergencies in similar settings [6,10].

Importantly, this case also underscores the feasibility of complex neonatal surgery even in resource-limited hospitals, provided communication, referral, and operative readiness are in place [7].

Conclusion

This case highlights the rare delayed airway compromise caused by a giant cervicofacial cystic hygroma in a neonate who initially appeared stable. It emphasizes the importance of early recognition, timely referral, and multidisciplinary coordination. In resource-limited environments, emergency surgical intervention for neonatal CH is achievable and can yield excellent outcomes when supported by systems for early communication and decision-making.
