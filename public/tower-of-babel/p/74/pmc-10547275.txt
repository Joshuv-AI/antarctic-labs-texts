Association of PCSK9 Inhibitor Initiation on Statin Adherence and Discontinuation
Authors: Christopher LaFratte, Samuel K Peasah, Yan Huang, Deanne Hall, Urvashi Patel, Chester B Good
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPCSK9is (proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitors) are well tolerated, potently lower cholesterol, and decrease cardiovascular events when added to statins. However, statin adherence may decrease after PCSK9i initiation and alter clinical outcomes. We evaluate the association of PCSK9i initiation on statin discontinuation and adherence.Methods and ResultsIn this retrospective pre‐post difference‐in‐difference analysis, new PCSK9i claims were propensity matched with statin‐alone users (April 2017–September 2019). The primary outcomes were statin adherence (proportion of days covered) and statin discontinuation (absence of statin coverage for at least 60 days) 12 months following PCSK9i initiation. Secondary outcomes included low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels after 1 year. A total of 220 538 statin users and 700 PCSK9i users were identified, from which 178 on PCSK9i were included and matched to 712 on statins alone. At 12 months, mean statin proportion of days covered decreased from 67% to 48% in the PCSK9i group but increased from 68% to 86% in the statin‐alone groups (P<0.0001). Statin discontinuation rates increased from 11% to 39% in the PCSK9i group and from 7% to 9% in the statin‐alone group (P=0.0041). Patients with low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol <70 mg/dL increased from 5% to 68% with PCSK9i but increased from 16% to 24% with statins alone (P<0.0001). Changes in hospitalization rates were similar between both groups during the follow‐up period.ConclusionsPCSK9i initiation was associated with decreased low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, higher statin discontinuation, and reduced statin adherence.Keywords: adherence, discontinuation, low‐density lipoprotein, PCSK9, statinSubject Categories: Health Services

Statins, also known as 3‐hydroxy‐3‐methylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase inhibitors, are first‐line therapy to lower cholesterol and prevent cardiovascular events in at‐risk patients. 1 However, patient tolerability and adverse effects may lead to suboptimal statin medication adherence. Previous reports show statin discontinuation rates of 50% at 1 year and 70% at 2 years after initiation. 2 , 3 In high‐risk patients in whom poor tolerance or response to statins limits therapeutic benefit, the 2018 American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association blood cholesterol guideline recommends adding other lipid‐lowering medications, including the PCSK9is (proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitors), such as alirocumab and evolocumab. 1 PCSK9 is a ubiquitous enzyme that binds to low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) receptors, leading to their degradation. PCSK9is are monoclonal antibodies that bind to PCSK9, thus blocking their effects on LDL receptors, leading to lower plasma LDL. 4 Clinical and cardiovascular outcome trials have demonstrated an additional 43% to 64% reduction in LDL cholesterol (LDL‐C) with PCSK9i in statin‐treated patients with cardiovascular disease and an overall reduction in cardiovascular events (attributable to the significant reduction in LDL‐C) compared with placebo. 1 , 5 , 6 , 7

Most evidence demonstrating benefits of PCSK9i is in the setting of combination therapy with statins. Statins may offer benefits beyond lowering cholesterol, known as pleiotropic effects, and the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association recommend continuation of statins with PCSK9i when possible. 1 However, because of potent LDL‐C–lowering properties, PCSK9i initiation may lead to discontinuation or deescalation of statins as well as other lipid‐lowering agents. A 2020 retrospective study reported that 43.8% of patients experienced an interruption in any lipid‐lowering therapy during the first year of PCSK9i use, with statin use decreasing from 31.6% to 22% in the cohort. 8 Beyond that study, there is a paucity of information about statin adherence after PCSK9i initiation.

The purpose of this study was to determine the association of PCSK9i initiation on statin adherence and discontinuation. We hypothesized that statin nonadherence and discontinuation would increase after PCSK9i initiation.

METHODS

The data used for this study are not publicly available but proprietary information owned by University of Pittsburgh Medical Center (UPMC) Health Plan, Inc, or its affiliates. Researchers may inquire about data availability by contacting S.P. but should understand that requests for data may be denied, subject to additional restrictions, and may be conditional on acceptance of a data use agreement. This study was a retrospective pre‐post analysis of a real‐world cohort at a large regional health plan. The study was deemed exempt by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board; therefore, informed consent was not required.

Prescription claims (using National Drug Code from April 1, 2017, to September 30, 2019) were used to identify patients treated with statins and those who were newly initiated on a PCSK9i, either alirocumab or evolocumab. Patients were included in the statin‐only cohort/group if there were at least 2 fills of the same statin medication within 6 months. Patients were included in the PCSK9i (pre‐post) group if there were at least 2 fills of a new PCSK9i in the post period and at least 2 fills of a statin medication in the pre period. Patients with PCSK9i fills within 3 months before the end of the study period were excluded. Patients were required to be at least 18 years old, be continuously enrolled with medical and pharmacy benefits during the study period, and have no concurrent prescription claims for ezetimibe.

The index date was defined as the date of first new PCSK9i fill and no PCSK9i claim in the previous 12 months (in the PCSK9i cohort) and the first statin fill (in the statin‐only cohort/group). Medications were identified by their unique National Drug Codes. International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition (ICD‐10), codes were used to identify a history of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) events (I25 and I70), unstable angina (I20), myocardial infarction (I21, I22, and I24), ischemic stroke (G45, I63, I65, and I67), and coronary revascularization (0210–0213 and 0270‐0273) in medical claims both inpatient and outpatient. Comorbidities were populated from secondary and tertiary ICD‐10 codes listed as the reason for hospitalization, emergency department visit, or outpatient provider office visits. Laboratory values, including LDL‐C and total cholesterol, and body mass index were obtained as a data transfer of reports populated from either claims' data sets or the electronic health record.

Baseline characteristics were defined by data within 6 months of the index date and included socioeconomic demographics (age, sex, race and ethnicity, area deprivation index, and insurance type), body mass index, statin intensity category, statin proportion of days covered (PDCs; 12 months), use of antihypertensive medications, LDL‐C nearest the index date, smoking status, history of ASCVD, and Charlson Comorbidity Index. Statin intensity was classified as high, moderate, or low based on categorical assignment in the 2018 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association blood cholesterol guidelines. 1

The primary outcomes were statin medication adherence over a 12‐month period, defined by PDC based on prescription claims; and discontinuation, defined as the absence of statin coverage for at least 60 consecutive days beyond the prescription claims' day supply. Secondary outcomes included LDL‐C levels across months 3 to 6, 6 to 12, and 12 to 24; and proportion of patients achieving LDL‐C <70 mg/dL (based on American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines for high‐risk ASCVD) 1 at any time past the index date.

Statistical Analysis

Patients taking PCSK9i were propensity matched to patients taking statin only using greedy nearest neighbor 9 matching (without replacement) in a 1:4 ratio. Patients were matched based on 11 variables, which included age, sex, race and ethnicity, prior ASCVD events, Charlson Comorbidity Index, cholesterol, baseline statin PDC, baseline statin intensity (low, medium, or high), baseline antihypertension medication use, baseline number of prescriptions, and insurance type (commercial, Medicaid, or Medicare) at the time of the index date. We also used the Mahalanobis matching method to validate the robustness of our findings. 9

Descriptive statistics were summarized using Student t test for continuous variables and χ2 test for categorical variables. Statistical significance was indicated by a 2‐tailed P<0.05.

Outcomes were analyzed either as a within cohort using paired t test pre‐post (or McNemar where appropriate) or between cohorts using a difference‐in‐difference model. A difference‐in‐difference analysis was used to compare statin adherence (PDC), statin discontinuation rate, and proportion achieving LDL‐C <70 mg/dL between the PCSK9i and statin‐only cohorts/groups. A difference‐in‐difference model compares the differences in the pre‐post changes between the study group and control group over time. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 For in‐between cohorts, we used unadjusted repeated‐measure ANOVA for continuous outcome and a marginal population‐level model (general estimating equations) for binary outcomes, as a binomial distribution with a logit link for relative risk reduction. We adjusted for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, baseline statin PDC, baseline statin intensity, race and ethnicity, insurance type, history of ASCVD, baseline antihypertension medication use (1/0), baseline number of prescriptions, and LDL test values. Analyses were completed using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC).

Pre and post mean LDL‐C significantly decreased at each time interval for participants in both groups, except months 3 to 6 in the statin‐only cohort/group (Table 2). LDL‐C reduction was significantly greater in the PCSK9i cohort compared with the statin‐only cohort/group at all time intervals (P<0.0001 for 3–6, 6–12, and 12–24 months). Although statistically significant, the reductions in mean LDL‐C at any time interval in the statin‐only cohort/group were significantly lower than in the PCSK9i group (Table 2). The percentage of patients achieving LDL‐C <70 mg/dL across the study period increased from 5% to 61% (P<0.0001) in the PCSK9i group and from 16% to 24% (P<0.0075) in the statin‐only cohort/group. In the adjusted general estimating equations model with logit link, the RRR was 59 (95% CI, 16–22; P<0.0001), meaning a 59 times higher ratio of proportion of patients achieving an LDL‐C<70 mg/dL in the PCSK9i group compared with the statin‐only cohort/group. Changes in hospitalization rates were similar between both groups during the follow‐up period.

DISCUSSION

A significant decrease in adherence to statin medications after starting a PCSK9i occurred in this retrospective difference‐in‐difference analysis. At 12 months following PCSK9i initiation, statin medication adherence (PDC) decreased by 19%, and statin discontinuation occurred in 28% of patients. Comparatively, matched patients remaining on statins alone had an 18% increase in statin PDC despite a 2% increase in statin discontinuation rate over the same period. Further subgroup analysis shows that there were twice as many adherent people in the post period (75%) compared with the pre period (34%), and most came from the Medicare subgroup (33% of those who were not adherent in the pre but became adherent in the post versus 28% who were adherent in both pre and post). Statin adherence is a Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services Star measure, and health plans are penalized if they do not meet adherence measures for specific drugs, including statins. 15 Health plans often have quality initiatives to improve adherence, and this might explain the significant PDC increase in the statin‐only cohort/group. Because the PCSK9i group had a significant reduction in LDL in the 12‐ to 24‐month follow‐up period (LDL‐C, 131 to 69 mg/dL) compared with the statin‐only cohort/group (LDL‐C, 113 to 97 mg/dL), lack of incentive to continue taking the statin might also account for the reduction in statin adherence and discontinuation in the PCSK9 group. This is concerning because, of the 69 patients in the PCSK9i group who discontinued their statins, their baseline LDL‐C was higher than those who did not discontinue (140 versus 109 mg/dL). Alternatively, the higher baseline LDL‐C in those who discontinued statins after starting PCSK9is may have reflected patients who were having more difficulty taking statins because of adverse events, and thus clinicians were more likely to concur with discontinuation in those patients. Regardless, because statins contributed to the significant reduction in their LDL‐C and given the price differential of PCSK9i versus statins, prescribers should encourage patients who could tolerate statins to remain on them. Continuation of combination therapy with statins holds further importance for high‐risk patients, in whom an even lower LDL‐C target of <50 mg/dL is recommended, as recommended by the American College of Cardiology Solution Set Oversight Committee. 16

Our findings of decreased statin adherence following PCSK9i initiation are consistent with Rymer et al, who reported that the proportion of statin‐treated patients in their cohort decreased from 27.7% to 22.4% at 1 year following PCSK9i initiation. 8 They also reported PCSK9i therapy interruption in approximately half of all PCSK9i users within the first year. In our PCSK9i cohort, there was greater statin discontinuation and less PCSK9i discontinuation than in the cohort of Rymer et al. The latter could be reflective of PCSK9i becoming less cost prohibitive in recent years, although still more expensive than even high‐intensity statins.

Statins have several proposed benefits in addition to lowering cholesterol that support efforts to continue their use in combination with other lipid‐lowering therapies. Statins have antiproliferative, anti‐inflammatory, and immunomodulatory properties; increase NO bioavailability; improve endothelial dysfunction; and stabilize atherosclerotic plaques. 17 , 18 In preclinical studies, PCSK9is have also demonstrated additional effects, such as the reduction of cytokines, atherosclerosis biomarkers, and venous thromboembolism risk. 19 , 20 A 2022 prospective, double‐blinded, randomized trial of 300 patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention for acute myocardial infarction PACMAN‐AMI (Effects of the PCSK9 Antibody Alirocumab on Coronary Atherosclerosis in Patients with Acute Myocardial Infarction) found that concomitant initiation of PCSK9i alirocumab and high‐intensity statin therapy resulted in greater coronary plaque regression compared with placebo and statin therapy, suggesting an underlying mechanism and theoretical rationale for early dual PCSK9i‐statin treatment. 21

Clinical evidence suggests that continuation of statins with PCSK9i results in favorable cardiovascular and health system use outcomes. A 2019 secondary analysis of the FOURIER (Further Cardiovascular Outcomes Research with PCSK9 Inhibition in Subjects with Elevated Risk) trial, which compared evolocumab with placebo in 27 564 statin‐treated patients with stable ASCVD, found fewer myocardial infarctions, strokes, and hospitalizations at 2‐year follow‐up in those who received both evolocumab and a statin. 7 However, the analysis did not account for medication adherence in the real‐world setting. A 2022 meta‐analysis of 83 660 patients found combination PCSK9i and maximally tolerated statin resulted in reduced nonfatal myocardial infarction and stroke in high‐risk patients but not in those with moderate or low cardiovascular risk, 22 highlighting the clinical benefits of statin continuation after PCSK9i initiation. Overall, our findings of reduced statin adherence after PCSK9i initiation may highlight a possible need for education on continuation of both medications in alignment with current evidence‐based guideline recommendations.

Limitations

There are several limitations of this study. As an observational study, we cannot claim causality but only association. Although we strengthened the study design through propensity score matching, using the nearest match, this only accounts for observable differences. Second, our data were based on administrative claims, and as such, medication adherence was an estimate and not confirmatory of actual medication use. Third, the study did not capture adverse effects of statins that may have contributed to dosage deescalation or alternate‐day dosing frequency, which would impact PDC and discontinuation rates. In the setting of switching to potent cholesterol‐lowering PCSK9i, clinicians or patients may elect to interrupt statin therapy based on clinical judgment, prior concerns about tolerability of statins, and risk‐benefit discussion. 23 , 24 Although the discontinuation of statins could also have been a result of intolerability of statin in the PCSK9 group, the adherence rate in the pre period was comparable in both groups (≈66%). Because our study only included administrative data, we are unable to determine if differences in perceived statin intolerance existed between matched cohorts. Our study excluded the use of ezetimibe and did not capture use of other lipid‐lowering therapies, such as ω fatty acids, bile acid resins, fibric acids, inclisiran, or bempedoic acid, all of which could impact the interpretation of clinical outcomes. Moreover, the PCSK9i cohort had higher baseline LDL levels, as expected, which therefore may have impacted the higher reduction in LDL levels after PCSK9i initiation compared with the statin‐only cohort/group. Finally, the proportion of non‐White (eg, Black, Hispanic, Asian) participants was low, which reduces the external validity of our study.

CONCLUSIONS

In this retrospective difference‐in‐difference analysis, there was a significant increase in statin nonadherence and discontinuation within 12 months of PCSK9i initiation. LDL‐C levels were significantly lower in the PCSK9i cohort by the end of the study period, despite higher baseline LDL‐C and higher rates of statin discontinuation. These findings suggest favorable outcomes of dual PSCK9i‐statin treatment compared with statins alone. Further study through larger, long‐term prospective trials is warranted to confirm the clinical and economic impact of statin discontinuation after PCSK9i initiation. Of particular interest would be a comparison of dual PCSK9i‐statin use to PCSK9i monotherapy after statin discontinuation.
