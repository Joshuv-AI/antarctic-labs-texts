Amino-acids-mTORC1-driven DDA1 phosphorylation promotes DNA repair and glioblastoma progression
Authors: Xing Chen, Zhixing Wang, An Yan, Yunpeng Liu, Ruimin Zhu, Li Li, Doudou Xu, Ruixue Liu, Xiangwen Zhan, Bin Yin, Wei Han, Xiaozhong Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDDA1 is a protein involved in protein degradation, cell cycle regulation, and DNA damage repair. Although its expression varies across tumor types, the precise role of DDA1 in gliomagenesis remains unclear.MethodsWe investigated the function of DDA1 in multiple glioblastoma cell models using biochemical assays, phosphorylation analysis, subcellular localization studies, and integrated genomic and transcriptomic profiling to determine its signaling interactions and downstream effects.ResultsWe identified a physical association between cytoplasmic DDA1 and Raptor, a core component of lysosome-associated mTORC1. Amino acid stimulation triggered phosphorylation of DDA1 at serine 33 promoting its nuclear translocation and involvement in DNA damage repair. Integrated transcriptomic analyses revealed that the mTORC1-DDA1S33-DNA repair axis regulates the expression of a subset of metabolic genes, including ENO2, CA12, and NMRK1. Functional assays further suggested that these genes contribute to the survival capacity of glioblastoma cells, particularly under DDA1-deficient conditions. Consistently, DDA1 deficiency markedly impaired glioblastoma growth and induced compensatory upregulation of metabolic activity.ConclusionOur findings identify DDA1 as a previously unrecognized phosphorylation target downstream of mTORC1 and a critical mediator of the mTORC1 driven DNA damage response. Through its involvement in DNA repair and metabolic gene regulation, DDA1 appears to support glioblastoma progression, providing mechanistic insight into mTORC1 related gliomagenesis and suggesting potential therapeutic relevance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-026-03028-2.Keywords: mTORC1, DDA1 phosphorylation, DNA repair, Glioblastoma progression

Background

Damage-specific DNA-binding protein 1 (DDA1) has recently emerged as a core component of the Cullin-RING E3 ubiquitin ligase complex CRL4CSA, which plays a critical role in DNA damage recognition and repair, particularly in transcription-coupled nucleotide excision repair (TC-NER) [1–4]. The CRL4CSA complex, composed of CUL4A, DDB1, RBX1, the substrate receptor CSA (ERCC8), and the regulatory subunit DDA1, facilitates the ubiquitination of stalled transcription-associated factors and promotes chromatin remodeling to enable efficient DNA repair [1, 2, 5, 6]. As a regulatory adaptor of CRL4CSA, DDA1 contributes to the recruitment and ubiquitination of key substrates involved in the resolution of transcription-blocking DNA lesions, thereby maintaining genome integrity under genotoxic stress [1, 2, 7]. Disruption of CRL4CSA or its accessory subunits, including DDA1, impairs TC-NER efficiency, leading to persistent DNA damage and genomic instability-a hallmark of cancer. Recent studies have demonstrated that DDA1 is frequently upregulated in a range of human malignancies, including breast, colorectal, and lung cancers [8–10]. While the mechanisms by which DDA1 contributes to tumorigenesis vary across cancer types, its consistent overexpression suggests a conserved role in promoting DNA damage tolerance and supporting tumor cell survival under therapeutic or metabolic stress. Cancer cells may exploit the CRL4CSA pathway to maintain genome stability in hostile microenvironments [5, 11–13]. Notably, DDA1-mediated modulation of DNA repair not only facilitates tumor progression but also confers resistance to genotoxic therapies, underscoring its potential as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target [10, 14]. Despite these insights, the molecular mechanisms linking nutrient-sensing signals to DDA1 activity and CRL4CSA-dependent genome maintenance in cancer remain incompletely understood.

The mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) is a central serine/threonine kinase complex that integrates environmental cues-including amino acids, energy status, oxygen availability, and growth factors-to coordinate anabolic and catabolic processes [15, 16]. As a master regulator of cellular metabolism, mTORC1 controls protein synthesis, nucleotide biosynthesis, autophagy, and lipid metabolism through phosphorylation of key downstream effectors such as S6K1 [17, 18] and 4E-BP1 [19]. mTORC1 activity is tightly regulated on the lysosomal membrane by a host of factors, including Rag GTPases [20, 21], the Ragulator complex [22], GATOR1/2 [23], and amino acid sensors such as Sestrin2 [24, 25], SAMTOR [26], and CASTOR1 [27]. Aberrant mTORC1 signaling is implicated in numerous human diseases, ranging from metabolic disorders and neurodegeneration to cancer [28–31]. Importantly, mTORC1 hyperactivation is a hallmark of glioblastoma and other aggressive tumors, linking nutrient sensing to uncontrolled cell proliferation and impaired DNA damage response [32–37].

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most malignant and prevalent primary brain tumor in adults, characterized by rapid progression, therapy resistance, and dismal prognosis despite aggressive treatment regimens [38, 39]. Genomic and epigenetic profiling of GBM has revealed complex molecular heterogeneity involving alterations in TP53, PTEN, EGFR, and IDH genes, as well as dysregulation of multiple oncogenic pathways, including mTOR signaling [40–44]. GBM cells exhibit profound metabolic plasticity and heightened DNA repair capacity, enabling them to withstand genotoxic stress induced by radiotherapy and alkylating agents [45–48]. Mounting evidence supports a critical role for nutrient-sensing pathways in fueling tumor growth and adaptation to metabolic constraints [49, 50]. However, how mTORC1 integrates with nuclear responses and DNA repair machinery to drive gliomagenesis remains incompletely defined.

In this study, we identify DDA1 as a previously unrecognized phosphorylation target of mTORC1 in glioblastoma. Through integrated biochemical, cellular, and transcriptomic analyses, we demonstrate that amino acid stimulation triggers mTORC1-dependent phosphorylation of DDA1 at serine 33, promoting its nuclear translocation and facilitating DNA damage repair. Mechanistically, phosphorylated DDA1 regulates the expression of a distinct metabolic gene program—including ENO2, CA12, and NMRK1-that supports tumor cell survival under genotoxic stress. Genetic ablation of DDA1 markedly impairs glioblastoma progression in vivo and disrupts the metabolic plasticity required for stress adaptation, rendering tumors less responsive to nutrient availability. Clinically, elevated DDA1 expression correlates with poor patient prognosis. Together, our findings delineate a nutrient-responsive mTORC1-DDA1S33-DNA repair axis that promotes glioblastoma growth through coordination of genome maintenance and metabolic reprogramming.

Methods

Cell culture

The cell lines used were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). U118-MG, LN18, LN229, A172, SHG44 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), T98G, U87-MG, SF126 cells were maintained in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) and 10% FBS, SF762 cells were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute Medium (RPMI-1640) with 20% FBS, and HA cells were maintained in Astrocyte Medium (1801, ScienCell). All these cells were cultured in a humidified incubator equilibrated with 5% CO2 at 37℃.

Antibodies and reagents

Details of the antibodies and reagents used in this study are provided in the Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Transfection

Cells were seeded and transfected with cDNA constructs or siRNAs using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX. Experiments were performed 36–48 h post-transfection. Three independent siRNAs were tested per gene, and the most efficient was selected. For details see Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Immunoprecipitation and western blotting

Cell lysates were immunoprecipitated with specific antibodies or anti-FLAG beads, washed, eluted, and analyzed by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting with appropriate primary and secondary antibodies. For details see Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Mass spectrometry

FLAG-tagged DDA1 or Raptor complexes were affinity-purified and eluted with 3×FLAG peptide, followed by LC-MS/MS analysis on a Q-Exactive MS for protein identification and quantification. For details see Supplementary Materials and Methods.

4D-Labelfree quantitative phosphoproteomics analysis

U118-MG cells were lysed, digested, and phosphopeptides enriched by Fe-NTA chromatography. LC-MS/MS data were analyzed using MaxQuant with stringent localization probability filtering. For details see Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Pull-down assays

GST fusion proteins were incubated with in vitro-translated products and glutathione beads. Bound proteins were washed, eluted, and detected by western blotting. For details see Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Fluorescence confocal microscopy

Cells were fixed, permeabilized, blocked, and stained with primary and Alexa Fluor-conjugated antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI and imaged on an Olympus microscope. For details see Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Formalin-fixed paraffin sections underwent antigen retrieval, blocking, and staining with primary antibodies and DAB detection, followed by hematoxylin counterstain and microscopic imaging. For details see Supplementary Materials and Methods.

RNA-seq

Total RNA was sequenced on BGI500, aligned to GRCh38/hg38 with STAR, and analyzed for differential expression using DESeq2 with P < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1. For details see Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Cell viability/proliferation assay

CCK-8 assays were performed in 96-well plates to measure cell viability. Absorbance at 450 nm was recorded after 2 h incubation at 37 °C. For details see Supplementary Materials and Methods.

qPCR

Total RNA was reverse-transcribed and gene expression quantified using SYBR Green qPCR with gene-specific primers and Roche LightCycler® 480 II system. For details see Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Lentiviral production and infection

Recombinant lentiviruses expressing FLAG-Vector and FLAG-DDA1 were constructed by Wuhan Saiweizhen Biotechnology. Concentrated viruses were used to infect 5 × 105 cells in a 60-mm dish with 8 µg/ml polybrene. Infected U118-MG cells were then subjected to sorting target expression.

CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene knockout

Cas9-expressing U118-MG cells were transduced with sgRNAs targeting DDA1, RagA, or Rheb. Puromycin-selected clones were genotyped and immunoblotted to confirm knockout. For details see Supplementary Materials and Methods.

CPD quantification

Cells were UV-irradiated, DNA was extracted and denatured, and CPDs quantified by ELISA according to manufacturer’s protocol. For details see Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Immunofluorescence staining for CPDs

UV-irradiated cells were fixed, DNA denatured, blocked, and stained with CPD antibodies and secondary fluorophores. Images were acquired by confocal microscopy. For details see Supplementary Materials and Methods.

In vitro mTOR kinase assay

The in vitro mTOR kinase assay was performed based on a previously reported protocol with minor modifications (Bio-protocol 6(11): e1827; DOI: https://doi.org/10.21769/BioProtoc.1827). For details see Supplementary Materials and Methods.

TUNEL assay

Apoptotic DNA fragmentation was assessed using the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit (Roche). Cells were UV-irradiated, fixed, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and incubated with TUNEL reaction mixture for 1 h at 37 °C. Fluorescence was visualized using a confocal microscope and quantified with ImageJ.

MTT assay

Cell proliferation was measured using the MTT assay (Sigma). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated as indicated. After 48 h, 0.5 mg/mL MTT solution was added (4 h), followed by solubilization in DMSO. Absorbance at 570 nm was recorded using a plate reader.

Colony formation assay

U118-MG cells were maintained in culture media in 6-well plate for 10 days, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and then stained with crystal violet. Each experiment was performed in triplicate and repeated at least three times.

Amino acid starvation and restimulation

Cells were seeded in culture dishes and grown to approximately 70–80% confluence. Before amino acid stimulation, cells were washed twice with pre-warmed PBS and incubated in amino acid-free, serum-free medium (R9010-01, USBiological) for 1 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. After starvation, amino acid stimulation was performed by directly adding a 1× mixture of 20 amino acids (R7131, Sigma) to the existing amino acid-free, serum-free medium, followed by incubation for 30 min.

Bioinformatics

Gene expression and survival analyses were performed using TCGA, CGGA, and GEO datasets. Differential expression and survival curves were computed using standard pipelines. For details see Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Statistical analysis

Data from biological triplicate experiments are presented. An unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t test was used for 2-group comparisons. ANOVA with Bonferroni’s correction was used to compare multiple groups. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical results were determined using Prism 10 software. Before statistical analysis, variations within each group and the assumptions of the tests were checked.

Data availability declaration

RNA-seq and proteomics data are deposited in GEO (GSE303079) and provided in Supplementary Data 1. All source data are available within the article. For details see Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Results

DDA1 physically associates with Raptor, a key component of the lysosome-associated mTORC1 complex.

As stated above, DDA1 is implicated in protein degradation and DNA damage repair [7]. Interestingly, recent studies have shown aberrant expression of DDA1 across multiple tumor types [8–10, 51]. To further investigate the biological function of DDA1, we employed an epitope-tagged proteomics screen combining immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry to define the in vivo DDA1 interactome. FLAG-tagged DDA1 (FLAG-DDA1) was stably expressed in human glioblastoma U118-MG cells. Cell lysates were subjected to anti-FLAG affinity purification followed by mass spectrometric analysis. Proteins co-purified with DDA1 included DDB1 and CUL4A (components of the CRL4CSA complex), LAMTOR1, LAMTOR2, LAMTOR3, and LAMTOR5 (Ragulator subunits), and Raptor (mTORC1 component), indicating that DDA1 associates with core elements of both CRL4 and mTORC1 machinery (Fig. 1a) (Supplementary Data 1). A reciprocal screen using FLAG-tagged Raptor in U118-MG cells identified DDA1 among the co-purified proteins, alongside RagA, RagC, and RagD (Rag GTPases), and Ragulator complex (Fig. 1b) (Supplementary Data 1).

To further validate the interaction of DDA1 with the components of the mTORC1 signaling system, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) in FLAG-DDA1-expressing U118-MG cells. Immunoprecipitation with anti-FLAG followed by immunoblotting with antibodies against the components of the mTORC1 signaling pathway showed that Raptor and RagA were all efficiently co-immunoprecipitated with DDA1, whereas no interaction was detected for DDA1 with WDR24 (GATOR2 component), mTOR, RagC, RagD, and LAMTOR1/2 (Fig. 1c). DDB1 and CUL4A, components of the CRL4CSA complex, were also confirmed as DDA1-interacting partners (Fig. 1d). In reciprocal co-IP experiments, FLAG-Raptor immunoprecipitates from U118-MG cells yielded DDA1 as a binding partner (Fig. 1e). Immunofluorescence staining showed that a portion of DDA1 and mTOR co-localize with the lysosomal marker Lamp2, supporting the notion that the DDA1-raptor interaction occurs at the lysosomal surface (Fig. 1f).

To further support the physical interaction of DDA1 with the mTORC1 machinery, glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assays with bacterially-purified GST-DDA1 and in vitro transcribed/translated components of the mTORC1 system (RagA, RagC, Raptor) and CRL4CSA (CSA) showed that DDA1 was capable of interacting with Raptor and CSA, but not with the other components of the mTORC1 signaling system that we tested (Fig. 1g). GST pull-down experiments with GST-fused RagA, RagC, Raptor or CSA and in vitro transcribed/translated DDA1 corroborated these observations (Fig. 1g). Additionally, we performed protein-protein interaction prediction analysis between Raptor and DDA1 using the bioinformatics tool AlphaFold3. According to recent studies, AlphaFold3 has demonstrated prediction accuracy comparable to, or even exceeding, that of X-ray crystallography [52]. The results revealed extensive binding interfaces between DDA1 and Raptor (Fig. 1h). Collectively, these observations suggest that DDA1 interacts with Raptor, a component of mTORC1, at the lysosomal surface.

DDA1 functions downstream of mTORC1 and is phosphorylated by mTORC1

To investigate the functional significance of the physical interaction between DDA1 and mTORC1, we first examined the position of DDA1 within the mTORC1 signaling cascade. In U118-MG cells, siRNA-mediated knockdown of DDA1 did not alter the phosphorylation level of S6K1, a downstream target of mTORC1, nor did it affect the protein levels of mTORC1 components (Raptor and mTOR) or CRL4CSA complex components (CSA, DDB1, CUL4A, and RBX1) (Fig. 2a). Notably, DDA1 depletion significantly reduced the phosphorylation of another mTORC1 substrate, 4E-BP1 (Fig. 2a). Conversely, DDA1 overexpression in U118-MG cells did not influence the phosphorylation levels of S6K1 or 4E-BP1, nor did it affect the levels of mTORC1 or CRL4 complex components (Fig. 2b). These findings suggest that DDA1 does not act upstream of mTORC1 but may instead function downstream, possibly as a substrate of mTORC1 kinase activity.

Due to the lack of commercially available phospho-specific antibodies against DDA1, we employed 4D-Labelfree quantitative phosphoproteomics using next-generation ion mobility mass spectrometry to investigate phosphorylation changes regulated by mTORC1 in U118-MG cells. Given that mTORC1 activity is modulated by amino acids, we designed three experimental conditions: (1) amino acid starvation to inhibit mTORC1 activity, (2) amino acid stimulation to activate mTORC1, and (3) amino acid stimulation combined with the mTOR inhibitor Torin1 to identify phosphorylation events specifically mediated by mTORC1. Differentially modified peptides were identified using thresholds of log 2 (fold change) > 2.0 and P < 0.05. Compared to the starvation condition, amino acid stimulation resulted in the upregulation of 994 phosphoproteins, while the addition of Torin1 reversed phosphorylation in 1,276 phosphoproteins relative to the amino acid-stimulated group (Fig. 2c) (Supplementary Data 1). Cross-comparison of these datasets yielded 609 phosphoproteins that were regulated by both amino acids and Torin1 (Fig. 2c) (Supplementary Data 1). Among them, phosphorylation of Serine 33 on DDA1 was induced by amino acids and suppressed by Torin1. GPS analysis predicted 18 potential phosphorylation sites on DDA1, including Ser33 (Fig. 2d).

To validate the phosphoproteomics findings, we generated a custom phospho-specific antibody against Ser33 of DDA1 (AtaGenix). Western blotting revealed that amino acid supplementation following incubation in amino acid-free medium increased the phosphorylation of DDA1 at serine 33 in U118-MG cells, while Torin1 co-treatment abrogated this effect (Fig. 2e). To further determine whether DDA1 is directly phosphorylated by mTOR, we performed an in vitro kinase assay using FLAG-mTOR immunopurified from U118-MG cells and purified recombinant GST-DDA1 or the phospho-deficient GST-DDA1S33A mutant. FLAG-mTOR increased DDA1 phosphorylation in vitro, whereas Torin1 treatment markedly reduced this signal. In contrast, phosphorylation was largely diminished when GST-DDA1S33A was used as the substrate (Supplementary Fig. 1a), supporting Ser33 as a major mTOR-dependent phosphorylation site of DDA1.

Activation of mTORC1 is orchestrated not only by the canonical amino acid-Rag GTPase axis, but also by the PI3K-AKT-mTORC1 pathway (ref). To delineate the upstream regulatory landscape governing DDA1 phosphorylation, we established stable U118-MG cell lines with CRISPR-mediated knockout of either RagA, a core component of the amino acid-sensing branch, or Rheb, the GTPase linking PI3K-AKT signaling to mTORC1 activation. Immunoblot analyses revealed that loss of either RagA or Rheb markedly reduced phosphorylation of the mTORC1 substrate S6K1 (Fig. 2f), confirming effective pathway inhibition. Importantly, phosphorylation at DDA1 Ser33 was attenuated in both RagA and Rheb deficient cells, indicating that DDA1 phosphorylation is under direct control of mTORC1 and is modulated by both nutrient and growth factor signals in glioma cells (Fig. 2f). DDA1 appears to emerge early in evolution; homologs are identified not only in vertebrates such as Mus musculus and Xenopus tropicalis but also in other established model organisms including Danio rerio (Fig. 2g). Potential homologs are also found in higher invertebrates such as Drosophila mojavensis (Fig. 2g). Remarkably, all these homologs harbor conserved residues corresponding to human Ser33 within structurally analogous regions, suggesting that this phosphorylation site is functionally important and evolutionarily conserved. Together, these findings demonstrate that DDA1 functions downstream of mTORC1 and is directly phosphorylated by mTORC1 at Serine 33.

Phosphorylated DDA1 undergoes nuclear translocation

To dissect the functional consequence of the physical interaction between DDA1 and mTORC1, we first analyzed the stoichiometric changes associated with this interaction. Using amino acid stimulation to modulate mTORC1 activity in U118-MG cells transfected with either FLAG-DDA1 or FLAG-Raptor, co-immunoprecipitation assays revealed that mTORC1 activation markedly reduced the interaction between DDA1 and Raptor or RagA, while enhancing its association with CRL4CSA complex components CUL4A, DDB1, and RBX1 (Fig. 3a). These findings suggest that mTORC1 activity reshapes DDA1-binding stoichiometry, potentially through phosphorylation at serine 33. To directly test this, we generated phospho-deficient (S33A) and phospho-mimetic (S33D) mutants of DDA1 and expressed them in U118-MG cells. Co-immunoprecipitation assays showed that the DDA1S33D mutant exhibited significantly increased binding to CRL4CSA components compared to the DDA1S33A mutant (Fig. 3b), implicating S33 phosphorylation as a critical switch for CRL4CSA complex assembly.

Given that the CRL4CSA complex primarily functions in the nucleus [53], we hypothesized that phosphorylation of DDA1 by mTORC1 may induce its nuclear translocation. As controls, we first examined the subcellular localization of DDB1 and CSA under varying mTORC1 activity states. Immunofluorescence analyses demonstrated that amino acid deprivation, re-stimulation, or Torin1 treatment did not alter their nuclear localization (Fig. 3c). In parallel, amino acid stimulation promoted mTOR accumulation at lysosomes, whereas amino acid withdrawal or Torin1 disrupted this lysosomal localization (Fig. 3d). Focusing on DDA1, we observed that amino acid deprivation or Torin1 suppressed its nuclear localization, while amino acid stimulation led to nearly complete nuclear translocation of DDA1 (Fig. 3e). To validate this observation biochemically, we performed nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation followed by immunoblotting. Amino acid stimulation markedly increased nuclear DDA1 and p-DDA1 levels, with a concomitant decrease in cytoplasmic DDA1 and a modest rise in cytoplasmic p-DDA1 (Fig. 3f). Together, these findings support a model in which mTORC1 activation triggers S33 phosphorylation of DDA1, promoting its nuclear translocation and potentially facilitating CRL4CSA complex formation and function.

Phosphorylation of DDA1 promotes CRL4CSA-mediated DNA repair and safeguards cellular viability

To delineate the functional consequences of mTORC1-mediated DDA1 phosphorylation in genotoxic signaling, we investigated the role of phosphorylated DDA1S33 in DNA damage repair. DDA1 has previously been identified as a component of the CRL4CSA E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, which participates in nucleotide excision repair (NER) of UV-induced DNA lesions [1]. To functionally assess this, we established U118-MG cell lines with stable depletion of DDA1 via lentiviral transduction (sgDDA1) alongside control cells (sgControl) (Fig. 4a). Immunoblot analysis confirmed that DDA1 loss did not perturb the expression of core CRL4CSA components or downstream mTORC1 effector p-S6K (Fig. 4a). To monitor NER efficiency, we quantified cyclobutene-pyrimidine-dimers (CPDs), a hallmark of UV-induced DNA lesions, using ELISA. Amino acid re-stimulation substantially reduced CPDs levels in control cells, while DDA1-deficient cells retained high CPDs burdens despite nutrient repletion, indicating compromised repair capacity (Fig. 4b). Immunofluorescence staining with CPDs-specific monoclonal antibodies corroborated these findings: amino acid stimulation mitigated UV-induced CPDs accumulation in control cells, whereas DDA1 knockout cells exhibited significantly elevated CPDs signals, which were only partially alleviated upon amino acid supplementation (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 2a). These results establish DDA1 phosphorylation as a key determinant of CRL4CSA-mediated repair in response to nutrient signaling.

Persistent DNA damage can precipitate apoptosis. TUNEL staining revealed that amino acid stimulation suppressed UV-induced apoptosis, whereas DDA1 knockout markedly elevated apoptotic DNA fragmentation, partially reversed by amino acid re-supply (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 2b). In parallel, MTT assays demonstrated that amino acid re-stimulation enhanced cell proliferation, while DDA1 depletion impaired proliferative capacity (Fig. 4e). Notably, treatment with the NEDD8-activating enzyme (NAE) inhibitor MLN4924 strongly inhibited cell growth; however, its suppressive effect was attenuated in DDA1-deficient cells, potentially due to compromised CRL4 complex activity (Fig. 4e). Colony formation assays further supported these observations: amino acid re-stimulation promoted clonal expansion under both basal and UV-stressed conditions, whereas DDA1 loss diminished overall colony-forming potential, though responsiveness to nutrient cues was preserved (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 2c). To further assess the functional requirement of Ser33 phosphorylation, DDA1-KO U118-MG cells were rescued with FLAG-DDA1, the phospho-deficient FLAG-DDA1S33A mutant, or the phospho-mimetic FLAG-DDA1S33D mutant. Re-expression of wild-type DDA1 restored colony formation, whereas the S33A mutant showed a markedly reduced rescue effect. In contrast, the S33D mutant enhanced clonogenic survival, supporting a functional role for DDA1 Ser33 phosphorylation in promoting cell survival (Supplementary Fig. 2d). These data collectively suggest that DDA1 promotes both DNA repair and survival, acting as a critical nexus between nutrient sensing and genotoxic stress response. Finally, we assessed markers of DNA damage (γ-H2A.X) and apoptosis (cleaved PARP1). Immunoblotting showed that amino acid stimulation suppressed both γ-H2A.X and cleaved PARP1 levels, while DDA1 depletion resulted in their pronounced upregulation irrespective of UV treatment (Fig. 4g). These findings converge on a model in which mTORC1-dependent phosphorylation of DDA1 enhances CRL4CSA function to promote DNA repair, suppress apoptosis, and sustain tumor cell proliferation under stress conditions.

DDA1 is associated with DNA repair and metabolic gene regulation in glioma

To further elucidate the biological relevance of the mTORC1-p-DDA1-DNA damage axis in glioma, we performed transcriptomic analyses to characterize the downstream gene expression changes associated with DDA1 phosphorylation at Ser33 in the context of DNA repair. We conducted RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) in three glioma cell lines (LN229, T98G, and U118-MG) after stable DDA1 knockdown using lentivirus-delivered sgRNAs. Total RNA was extracted and processed for cDNA synthesis, library preparation, and 50 bp-end sequencing using the BGI-seq 500 platform (APTBIO, Shanghai). Raw reads were quality-checked with FastQC, and low-quality reads (≥ 5 ambiguous bases or Phred score < 15) were removed using fastp. Under the threshold of P < 0.05 and absolute log 2 (fold change) > 1, we identified 42 consistently differentially expressed genes across the three models, including 36 upregulated and 6 downregulated genes (GSE303079) (Fig. 5a). Gene ontology analyses indicated that many upregulated transcripts were enriched in pathways related to cellular metabolism and growth. Notably, upregulated targets included: CA12, a carbonic anhydrase critical for regulating intracellular and extracellular pH by catalyzing the reversible hydration of CO₂ [54, 55]; NMRK1, a member of the nucleoside kinase family that converts nicotinamide riboside to NMN in NAD⁺ biosynthesis [56, 57]; ENO2, also known as neuron-specific enolase, which catalyzes the final step of glycolysis converting 2-phosphoglycerate to phosphoenolpyruvate [57, 58].

To validate the RNA-seq findings, quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) validation confirmed significant upregulation of CA12, NMRK1, and ENO2 following DDA1 knockout in all three glioma models (Fig. 5b). Immunoblot analyses corroborated these findings at the protein level (Fig. 5e). Conversely, overexpression of FLAG-tagged DDA1 led to significant downregulation of these genes at both transcript and protein levels (Fig. 5c, d, and f). Given prior evidence linking DNA damage repair defects to metabolic reprogramming [59], we propose that DDA1 deficiency impairs DNA repair and induces a stress-adaptive transcriptional response involving CA12, NMRK1, and ENO2. This shift likely supports metabolic flexibility, sustains energy homeostasis, and prevents apoptosis, thereby promoting cell survival under genotoxic stress.

To further assess whether DDA1 associated metabolic reprogramming contributes functionally to cell survival, we focused on three representative metabolic genes, CA12, ENO2, and NMRK1. Efficient knockdown of these genes was confirmed by western blotting (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Colony formation assays showed that depletion of CA12, ENO2, or NMRK1 reduced clonogenic survival in U118-MG cells, and this inhibitory effect was more pronounced in DDA1-KO cells (Supplementary Fig. 3b-d). These results suggest that DDA1 associated metabolic reprogramming genes functionally support the survival capacity of glioblastoma cells, particularly under DDA1 deficient conditions.

To assess the clinical relevance of our findings, we analyzed public transcriptomic datasets from GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE4290) comprising astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma, and glioblastoma specimens. Expression of CA12 was uniformly elevated across all tumor types, whereas ENO2 and NMRK1 levels were frequently decreased (Fig. 5g). These trends may reflect loss of neuronal identity during mesenchymal or stem-like transitions in glioma, as well as metabolic rewiring that favors NAD⁺ generation via the NAMPT pathway over NMRK1 [59, 60]. Hypoxic tumor cores may further drive CA12 upregulation, facilitating extracellular acidification, stromal invasion, and immune evasion [61, 62]. Consistent with these observations, analysis of the TCGA GBM cohort (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) revealed the same pattern. CA12 was significantly upregulated and ENO2 was downregulated, while NMRK1 showed a similar downward trend that did not reach statistical significance but remained directionally concordant with the GEO data (Fig. 5h). To further evaluate the clinical significance, survival analysis using the CGGA dataset (mRNAseq_693) (https://www.cgga.org.cn/download.jsp) demonstrated that low ENO2 expression and high CA12 expression were significantly associated with poor patient prognosis (Fig. 5i). Together, our data support a model in which mTORC1-dependent DDA1 phosphorylation regulates DNA damage repair and orchestrates metabolic reprogramming, revealing a previously unrecognized axis that links genome integrity to metabolic adaptation in glioma.

DDA1 is overexpressed in glioma and contributes to tumorigenesis

Enhanced DNA repair capacity and dysregulated metabolism are hallmarks of malignant glioma, contributing to both intrinsic and acquired resistance to standard therapies [63, 64]. As a central regulator of both DNA repair and metabolic homeostasis, mTOR plays a pivotal role in gliomagenesisn [65, 66]. To comprehensively evaluate mTORC1 activity in glioma, we compared nine human glioma cell lines (LN18, LN229, U87-MG, U118-MG, SHG44, A172, T98G, SF126, SF763) with non-malignant human astrocytes (HA). Immunoblotting revealed markedly elevated mTORC1 activity in glioma cells compared to HA cells, as evidenced by phosphorylation of downstream targets S6K1 and 4E-BP1 (Fig. 6a and b). Among these, LN229, U87-MG, U118-MG, A172, and T98G exhibited pronounced mTORC1 activation. Notably, DDA1 and other components of the CRL4CSA DNA repair complex were upregulated in glioma cells with hyperactive mTORC1 (Fig. 6c). Amino acid re-stimulation experiments further demonstrated that DDA1 phosphorylation at Ser33 was universally induced in glioma cells in an mTORC1-dependent manner (Fig. 6d). We also profiled phosphorylation of additional mTORC1 downstream targets, including ULK1 and AMPKα, key sensors of autophagy and energy stress respectively, which exhibited a similar pattern of upregulation, consistent with heightened mTORC1 signaling in glioblastoma (Fig. 6d).

To probe the functional relevance of DDA1 in glioma biology, we performed CCK-8 proliferation assays in U118-MG cells. Knockout of DDA1 suppressed proliferation, whereas its stable overexpression enhanced growth (Fig. 6e). Consistent results were obtained in colony formation assays, where DDA1 loss impaired clonogenicity and its overexpression markedly increased colony numbers (Fig. 6f, Supplementary Fig. 4a). Notably, mTORC1 activation via amino acid supplementation further promoted colony formation in control cells but failed to rescue growth in DDA1-deficient cells. Conversely, DDA1 overexpression enhanced colony formation regardless of mTORC1 activation status (Fig. 6g, Supplementary Fig. 4b). These findings indicate that mTORC1-driven DDA1 expression and phosphorylation support glioma growth.

To validate these observations in vivo, we established an orthotopic intracranial glioma model. DDA1-knockout U118-MG cells (5 × 10⁵) were stereotactically implanted into the striatum of immunodeficient BALB/c nude mice. MRI performed on day 7 confirmed tumor formation and served as a baseline (Fig. 7a). Mice were then randomized into control and amino acid-supplemented groups, the latter receiving daily oral administration of a defined amino acid mixture for four weeks. Follow-up MRI on day 35 revealed significantly reduced tumor growth in DDA1-deficient mice, whereas amino acid supplementation accelerated tumor progression relative to controls (Fig. 7b). Immunohistochemical analysis of paraffin-embedded brain sections demonstrated elevated γH2A.X levels in DDA1-deficient tumors, indicative of increased DNA damage, which was moderately attenuated by amino acid supplementation (Fig. 7c). To further confirm pathway activation in tumor tissues, western blot analysis was performed using tumors harvested from the orthotopic xenograft model. Consistent with the in vivo tumor growth phenotype, amino acid supplementation increased the phosphorylation levels of S6K1 and 4EBP1 in DDA1 deficient tumors, indicating enhanced mTORC1 activity. In parallel, phosphorylation of DDA1 was also elevated following amino acid supplementation, supporting activation of the mTORC1-DDA1 signaling axis in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 5a).

Finally, clinical relevance was assessed via survival analysis using CGGA datasets (mRNAseq_325 and mRNAseq_693) (https://www.cgga.org.cn/download.jsp). High DDA1 expression was significantly associated with poorer patient prognosis (Fig. 7d). Additionally, multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed with adjustment for radio status, chemo status, IDH mutation status, and MGMT promoter methylation status. DDA1 remained significantly associated with patient outcome in the CGGA cohorts after multivariate adjustment (Supplementary Fig. 5b). In addition, correlation analysis showed a positive association between DDA1 expression and mTOR expression, supporting a clinical link between DDA1 and mTOR related signaling in glioma patients (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Collectively, our findings identify DDA1 as a novel mTORC1 downstream effector whose phosphorylation enhances its nuclear translocation and function in DNA repair. This signaling axis promotes metabolic adaptation and glioma progression, providing new insights into the crosstalk between mTORC1 signaling, DNA damage responses, and tumor metabolism (Fig. 7e).

Discussion

mTORC1 has long been recognized as a central regulator of cellular metabolism, protein synthesis, and growth in response to nutrient and growth factor signals [67, 68]. However, how mTORC1 signaling directly interfaces with genome maintenance mechanisms has remained an underexplored frontier. While previous studies have hinted at a possible link between mTORC1 and DNA damage responses, such as through indirect regulation of FANCD2 or modulation of homologous recombination repair [69], the precise molecular mediators remained elusive. Our identification of DDA1 as a phosphorylation target downstream of mTORC1 that contributes to DNA repair provides additional insight into the broader functions of mTORC1 signaling.

This discovery integrates two seemingly distinct processes nutrient sensing and genome integrity into a unified molecular framework. The phosphorylation-dependent nuclear translocation of DDA1 highlights how lysosome-associated mTORC1 signaling extends beyond its classical cytoplasmic functions to exert nuclear control over DNA repair fidelity. Notably, the involvement of DDA1 in modulating the CRL4CSA complex, a key player in nucleotide excision repair (NER) [1, 70], positions DDA1 as a potential mediator between nutrient responsive signaling and chromatin associated DNA repair machinery.

Our findings also resonate with emerging concepts that DNA repair is tightly coupled to metabolic state. Recent work has shown that efficient DNA repair requires adequate nucleotide pools, redox balance, and ATP availability [71, 72]. Consistent with this, the compensatory upregulation of metabolic genes such as ENO2, CA12, and NMRK1 in DDA1-deficient glioma cells may reflect a tumor-intrinsic stress response aimed at sustaining bioenergetic and biosynthetic demands under persistent genotoxic stress. However, this adaptation is ultimately insufficient, as evidenced by increased apoptosis and impaired tumor growth upon DDA1 loss.

Interestingly, the mTORC1-DDA1 axis may represent a broader mechanism applicable beyond glioblastoma. For example, DDA1 has been implicated in protein degradation and cell cycle control in other cancer types [4, 9, 73], suggesting that phosphorylation-dependent functional switching of DDA1 could be a generalizable regulatory strategy in cancer biology. Whether this mechanism extends to other DNA repair pathways, such as homologous recombination or base excision repair, remains an open question deserving further investigation.

Clinically, our data suggest that DDA1 is overexpressed in glioma and its elevated expression correlates with poor prognosis in patient datasets. In vitro and in vivo models demonstrate that DDA1 loss significantly impairs glioma cell proliferation and tumor growth. Notably, amino acid supplementation enhanced mTORC1 activation and DDA1 phosphorylation in glioma cells, promoting tumor progression, whereas DDA1 knockout rendered tumors less responsive to nutrient cues. These findings underscore the role of DDA1 as a critical downstream effector of mTORC1 that integrates extracellular nutrient availability with nuclear DNA repair machinery to promote gliomagenesis. Our results may also have therapeutic implications. The mTORC1-DDA1 axis represents a potential vulnerability in glioma that may be exploitable using strategies that simultaneously target mTORC1 signaling and DNA repair mechanisms. Given the reliance of DDA1 function on mTORC1 activity and nutrient availability, metabolic interventions or inhibitors of mTORC1 may sensitize glioma cells to DNA-damaging agents by disrupting this axis. Moreover, pharmacologic inhibition of CRL4 E3 ligase activity or specific blockade of DDA1 phosphorylation may provide additional therapeutic possibilities, although these strategies require further experimental validation.

Despite these findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although our data support a role for DDA1 phosphorylation in linking mTORC1 signaling to DNA repair and tumor growth in glioma models, the precise metabolic consequences of DDA1 associated changes in metabolism related gene expression remain to be clarified through direct metabolic flux analyses and functional metabolic assays. In addition, further studies using patient derived models would help strengthen the clinical relevance of our findings. While the mTORC1-DDA1 axis may represent a potential therapeutic direction, the feasibility, specificity, and safety of directly targeting DDA1 phosphorylation or DDA1 associated complexes remain to be established.

In conclusion, our work identifies DDA1 phosphorylation as a molecular event that connects nutrient responsive mTORC1 signaling to DNA repair and tumor growth in glioma models. These findings expand our understanding of mTORC1 related signaling and provide a framework for further investigation of nutrient-DNA repair crosstalk in glioblastoma. The mTORC1-DDA1 axis may therefore represent a potential therapeutic direction that warrants further exploration in preclinical models.
