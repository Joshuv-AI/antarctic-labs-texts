Establishment of a nomogram for early risk prediction of severe trauma in primary medical institutions: A multi-center study
Authors: Wang Bo, Ming-Rui Zhang, Gui-Yan Ma, Zhan-Fu Yang, Rui-Ning Lu, Xu-Sheng Zhang, Shao-Guang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo analyze risk factors for severe trauma and establish a nomogram for early risk prediction, to improve the early identification of severe trauma.MethodsThis study was conducted on the patients treated in 81 trauma treatment institutions in Gansu province from 2020 to 2022. Patients were grouped by year, with 5364 patients from 2020 to 2021 as the training set and 1094 newly admitted patients in 2020 as the external validation set. Based on the injury severity score (ISS), patients in the training set were classified into 2 subgroups of the severe trauma group (n = 478, ISS scores ≥25) and the non-severe trauma group (n = 4886, ISS scores <25). Univariate and binary logistic regression analyses were employed to identify independent risk factors for severe trauma. Subsequently, a predictive model was developed using the R software environment. Furthermore, the model was subjected to internal and external validation via the Hosmer–Lemeshow test and receiver operating characteristic curve analysis.ResultsIn total, 6458 trauma patients were included in this study. Initially, this study identified several independent risk factors for severe trauma, including multiple traumatic injuries (polytrauma), external hemorrhage, elevated shock index, elevated respiratory rate, decreased peripheral oxygen saturation, and decreased Glasgow coma scale score (all p < 0.05). For internal validation, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.914, with the sensitivity and specificity of 88.4% and 87.6%, respectively; while for external validation, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.936, with the sensitivity and specificity of 84.6% and 93.7%, respectively. In addition, a good model fitting was observed through the Hosmer–Lemeshow test and calibration curve analysis (p > 0.05).ConclusionThis study establishes a nomogram for early risk prediction of severe trauma, which is suitable for primary healthcare institutions in underdeveloped western China. It facilitates early triage and quantitative assessment of trauma severity by clinicians prior to clinical interventions.

Introduction

Severe trauma constitutes a major contributor to the disability and death in young and middle-aged people worldwide. Annual deaths from traffic accidents and other accidents exceed 4 million around the world according to the World Health Organization.1 Moreover, in China, trauma has become the 5th leading cause of death overall and the primary cause among young people over the past 5 years based on the official data from the National Health Commission.2 Consequently, this condition places a heavy economic burden on citizens and society, especially in developing countries such as China.3 The prognosis of patients with severe trauma depends directly and predominantly on the standardization and timeliness of treatment, and the optimization of trauma care relies mainly on early, accurate injury assessment. Noticeably, “golden hour” principle for trauma treatment has been highly concerned by the internationally recognized advanced trauma life support guidelines (ATLS®).4 However, in the actual medical practice, traumatic patients often take over 60 min to receive medical care in underdeveloped countries and regions. Given the post-trauma decline in pathological conditions, patients may usually loss the optimal treatment window due to long-distance transport. Therefore, recently, researchers pay more attention to the achievement of rapid injury triage and evaluation, improvement of the recognition of severe trauma in underdeveloped areas, and reduction of ineffective transport. Current mainstream trauma assessment methods have certain limitations in clinical application. For example, anatomical scoring systems such as the abbreviated injury scale (AIS)-injury severity score (ISS) rely on imaging examinations, which is not conducive to assessing injury progression considering its inability to identify trends in the physiological deterioration of trauma patients.5 Glasgow coma scale (GCS) and other physiological scoring tools are suitable for assessment of patients with craniocerebral injury in pre-hospital settings, with poor assessment accuracy for patients with language difficulties or those who have undergone intubation and sedation.6 The revised trauma score, with the integration of GCS, respiration, and circulation indicators, is an effective approach for assessing patients’ physiological derangement, but exhibits poor performance for evaluating multiple injuries, with the possibility of missed diagnoses.7 In addition, mixed scoring systems such as the trauma and ISS and the abbreviated severity characterization of trauma both are scoring systems for survival probability, with the incorporation of anatomical and physiological parameters, as well as various types of risk factors for trauma.8 Nevertheless, similar injury assessments may be resulted from different injury mechanisms (e.g., firearm penetrating injuries and blunt trauma), while these injuries have significantly different prognoses.9 There is currently no available methods offering reliable identification, which undoubtedly impairs rapid diagnosis and effective treatment of severe trauma. Accordingly, the present study was conducted to construct a predictive nomogram model applicable in both pre-hospital and in-hospital settings based on the utilization of the trauma database of Gansu province, China. By integrating anatomical and physiological parameters, this predictive model was designed for multiple, complex injuries, aiming at addressing the issue of complex, time-consuming combined assessment scales clinically. Through this investigation, it is anticipated to narrow disparities in the early detection and assessment of severe and complex injuries at primary medical institutions in underdeveloped areas, enhance the rapid triage and efficient transfer of traumatic patients, and further optimize the development of regional standardized trauma treatment system.

Methods

Data sources and processing

Prior to the initiation of our research, ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Gansu Provincial People's Hospital (Ethics No. 2025-073). The requirement for informed consent from the patients was waived by the ethics committee given that this was a retrospective study and the data had been de-identified (anonymized personal data), making personal identification impossible.

From 2020 to 2022, the medical records of a total of 72,931 trauma patients were collected by the National Trauma Medicine Center from 81 trauma centers (7 Level I, 21 Level II, and 53 Level III) in Gansu province. Using the outcome variable (ISS score) and the completeness of medical records, we compiled and verified the data for the 6469 selected patients. This study aims to construct a multi-centre predictive model for primary healthcare. The study subjects must include representative patients from all age groups, with each age group representing more than 5% of the sample; the ages range from 1 to 92 years. Then, based on the patients' age distribution and predefined age screening criteria, we excluded 11 patients who did not meet the age requirements, resulting in a final study population of 6458 patients.

The extracted baseline data comprised demographic characteristics (gender and age), date and time of visit, and cause of injury. Clinical data included polytrauma (defined according to the Berlin definition10), fractures, superficial hemorrhages, and comorbidities. Physiological indicators include systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure, heart rate (HR), shock index (SI), respiratory rate (RR), and oxygen saturation (SpO2), recorded by the vital signs monitor during transport. Additionally, random capillary blood glucose (BG), body temperature, and GCS are obtained by medical staff at the bedside.

The data of this study were retrospectively collected and recorded from cases managed by medical workers following trauma treatment. The integrity of the data depends on voluntary reporting by medical workers. We consider that no loss of data occurred due to prioritizing the rescue of critically ill patients. In addition, by excluding cases with incomplete medical record information, we believe that no significant selection bias existed.

According to the inclusion criteria, a total of 6458 patients were included. Among them, 5364 patients from 2020 to 2021 served as the training set, while 1094 patients from the year 2022 were used as the external validation set to avoid overlap. A logistic regression model was developed, and the bootstrap method was utilized for model validation. During the data cleaning phase, we found that there were missing values for pre-hospital random fingertip BG measurements, body temperature, and GCS scores in the training set and external validation set. The missing rates for BG, body temperature, and GCS scores in the training set were 54.30%, 4.96%, and 1.25%, respectively. The missing rates for BG, body temperature, and GCS scores in the external validation set were 45.80%, 4.66%, and 0.37%, respectively. To handle these missing values, we used multiple imputation methods to fill in the missing data of key variables.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) patients diagnosed with trauma clearly; (2) patients aged between 1 and 92 years (The frequency distribution of the sample age greater than 5% has population representativeness); (3) patients with complete medical records, including chief complaints, diagnoses, sequelae, as well as important physiological and biochemical indicators.

Exclusion criteria: (1) patients with incomplete baseline data; (2) patients with contradictory descriptions in medical records; (3) patients whose reliable test results cannot be obtained due to interference from alcohol, drugs, or other factors.

Research methods

Severe trauma referred to an ISS ≥25 according to the International Guidelines by the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma. This study used univariate analysis and multivariate binary logistic regression analysis to determine independent risk factors. The results of these analyses were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0 was used for sample selection and data organization. In RStudio 4.1.1, the rms package was first loaded to fit a multivariate binary logistic regression model for selecting independent risk factors. Then, the lrm function was used to draw nomograms. Subsequently, the calibrate function was applied for internal validation using bootstrap resampling 1000 times to generate calibration curves; external validation was performed using an independent dataset. Next, the roc function was used to draw the receiver operating characteristic curve. Finally, the ggDCA package (dca function) was employed to perform decision curve analysis to evaluate the net benefit of the model at different risk thresholds.

Screening and basis for candidate variables

Candidate variables were determined based on the 3 criteria of post-severe trauma clinical and pathological mechanisms, the consensus among international experts, and the feasibility of data collection. Regarding clinical and pathological mechanisms, multiple injuries were defined as having 2 or more AIS-90 scores of ≥2 in different anatomical regions, with at least 1 region having an AIS score of ≥3, according to the An international consensus process and proposal of the new 'Berlin definition'.11 This definition served as the anatomical basis for assessing severity of trauma, and this criterion was considered as a candidate variable.

Furthermore, in view of the core monitoring indicators of the ATLS® international consensus, candidate variables also included: SBP, HR, SI, external bleeding, RR, SpO2, BG, referring to capillary BG levels, body temperature, and GCS score.

Fractures exhibit intimate associations with the ISS. Hemorrhagic shock and fat embolism would occur in cases of long bone, pelvic, or vertebral fractures.12 Consistent with the epidemiological characteristics of local traffic injuries and falls, the incidence of fractures was 74.9% (preliminary data) in this study. Therefore, the presence of fractures, due to their high prevalence and clinical significance, was included as a separate candidate variable.

Additionally, comorbidities are another major contributor to physiological compensatory capacity.13 As a result, comorbidities were also included as a candidate variable given the health management needs of chronic diseases in primary healthcare services in Gansu province, China.

Statistical methods

Data analysis, including sample selection and data organization, was performed using SPSS version 27.0. Use R 4.1.1 and related R packages for model building and validation. Data distributed normally were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and inter-group comparisons were completed based on an independent samples t-test or an analysis of variance. Non-normally distributed data were represented by the median (Q1, Q3), and inter-group comparisons adopted the Mann-Whitney U test. Meanwhile, categorical variables were represented by the frequency (percentage), and inter-group differences were assessed by the Chi-square (χ2) test. A p ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Multivariate binary logistic regression analysis to screen independent risk factors

The 12 indicators from Table 2 were classified, with continuous variables including SI, blood pressure, HR, RR, BG, temperature, SpO2, and GCS, while multiple injuries, external bleeding, and fractures were binary. Collinearity diagnosis revealed significant collinearity among blood pressure, HR and SI (VIF = 6.608), leading to the selection of only the SI as the circulatory indicator. Nine other continuous and binary variables were used as candidates, with an ISS of 25 or higher defined as severe trauma for prediction.

Independent risk factors were identified via multivariate binary logistic regression to create the prediction model. The model formula is: ln (p/1-p) = 1.167 + 0.765 ∗ SI+ 0.040 ∗ RR+ 0.025 ∗ T (°C) + 0.001 ∗ BG- 0.025 ∗ SaO2 (%) - 0.331 ∗ GCS + 2.189 ∗ multiple injuries + 0.294 ∗ external bleeding - 0.059 ∗ fractures (where p represents the probability of severity being 1, and 1-p represents the probability of severity being 0).

The study showed that pre-hospital randomly recorded body temperature and BG levels, as well as the presence of bone fractures, are not independent risk factors for severe trauma outcomes (p > 0.05). The presence of multiple injuries, external bleeding, and elevated SI and RR are risk factors for severe trauma outcomes (p < 0.05), with odds ratios (OR) of 8.928, 1.342, 2.150, and 1.041, respectively. Construction of a nomogram model for severe trauma risk prediction

A nomogram model for predicting was established based on these risk factors identified from the logistic regression analysis. In this model, the total score was obtained by summing the scores of all risk factors. As a result, the probability of severe trauma could be quickly determined using the risk axis displayed at the bottom of the nomogram. For example, a total score of 115 points corresponded to approximately an 30% risk of severe trauma. The relative importance of each variable in the model was ranked from largest to smallest, including GCS score, SI, SpO2, multiple injuries, RR, external hemorrhage, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Among these, higher SI and RR indicated higher risk of severe trauma. In contrast higher SpO2 and GCS suggested a lower risk. Additionally, the presence of external hemorrhage would increase the risk, but surface hemorrhage contributed the least to the total score of the model (Fig. 2).Fig. 2The nomogram model for severe trauma risk prediction.Fig. 2

Clinical benefit assessment of the risk prediction model

Decision curve analysis for the risk prediction model constructed in this study revealed a favorable net benefit, within the high-risk threshold range of 20%–80% (Fig. 3). At a risk threshold of 20%, the net benefit of the trauma model approximated 60%, indicating significant clinical benefits when providing appropriate interventions to some low-risk trauma patients. In addition, for the low threshold range below 20%, the model also exhibited a clinical benefit given that the decision curve remained above the full intervention line. Collectively, the above results supported a stable performance of the model, meeting the requirements for both the development and clinical application of the trauma model in Gansu province (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Decision curve analysis (DCA) curve. Validation of the performance of the model in identifying severe trauma

Based on the training set, this study constructed this nomogram model for predicting severe trauma, with good identification performance (Fig. 4). For internal validation, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was 0.914 (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.899–0.929), with the sensitivity and specificity of 84.4% and 87.4%, respectively, when the threshold was set at 4.3%. In external validation, the AUC was 0.936 (95% CI: 0.902–0.969), with sensitivity and specificity of 84.6% and 93.7%, respectively, when the threshold was set at 3.2%. Consequently, the AUC was greater than 0.90 for both internal and external validations. Therefore, this model was suitable for use by grassroots medical institutions in Gansu province with limited resources and equipment.Fig. 4Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Model calibration performance and generalizability validation

Bootstrap resampling was performed following the establishment of this predictive model. The calibration curve was utilized to evaluate the calibration performance and generalization ability of this model. The calibration curve of the training set closely approximated the ideal calibration line, indicating that the predicted probability roughly matched the actual risk. A good model fitting was observed based on the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (p = 0.060). Besides, the error rate in the low-risk area was less than 3% (Fig. 5A). In the external validation set, the model overestimated risk by 0.20 in the low-risk interval (predicted probability <40%). This overestimation could be explained by slight differences (e.g., injury causes and pre-hospital emergency response times) in baseline characteristics among centers (Fig. 5B).Fig. 5Calibration curve. Discussion

The present study was performed with the identification of risk factors for severe trauma, and subsequent development of a predictive model to improve its early detection eventually. Its significance lies in the addressing of key challenges in trauma treatment in the agricultural and pastoral areas of Gansu province, including the lack of dynamic assessment tools in grassroots centers, inconsistent sorting and grading of multiple injuries, time-consuming assessment processes, and high rates of ineffective transfers. In our study, based on the data collected from 81 trauma centers across Gansu province, we successfully developed a predictive nomogram model integrating anatomical injuries (multiple injuries and surface hemorrhage) with physiological functional status (circulatory, respiratory, and neurological indicators). Consequently, vital signs (SI, RR, and SpO2) and GCS scores were identified to be important predictive indicators. Significantly, the established nomogram model demonstrated good stability, which can be conveniently adopted to identify severe trauma accurately (Table 2, Table 3). Moreover, metabolic indices (body temperature and fingertip BG), fractures and comorbidities were not primary factors related to the severity of trauma. These factors determined by this model may be attributed to the natural environment, levels of medical resources, characteristics of the trauma spectrum, residents' awareness of chronic disease management and other aspects in the agricultural and pastoral areas of Gansu province.

As demonstrated in our study, decreased SBP, increased HR, increased RR, decreased SpO2, increased SI and reduced GCS score can all serve as independent risk factors for predicting severe trauma in Gansu province (Table 3). These findings are consistent with the characteristics of the pathophysiological process during the compensatory phase of shock. Therefore, monitoring these factors may contribute to a direct monitoring of post-trauma physiological disturbances and changes in the dynamic physiological state more comprehensively compared to the AIS-ISS system. Furthermore, the geographical factor, i.e., high-altitude characteristics of Gansu province, was also considered when establishing this model, with the incorporation of factors such as HR and GCS score. Meanwhile, it also intended to minimize measurement variability to address the challenge of low baseline SpO2 in the western plateau region of China.14 Simultaneously, the practicality of this model was also considered by excluding parameters (e.g., lactate levels and coagulation) that require more complex equipment to monitor. Grassroots medical staff can assess specific populations using a blood pressure monitor and pulse oximeter easily. For example, 1 patient can be determined to be critically ill if the GCS score is <9 or SpO2 is <90%. This approach also minimizes ineffective transfers of patients with minor wounds from grassroots facilities to Level I and II centers, thereby concentrating provincial high-quality medical resources on truly critically ill patients, such as those from Gannan Tibetan autonomous prefecture with transfer times exceeding 3 h.

In our study, univariate analysis found no significant difference in body temperature among the trauma subgroups in the training set (Table 3), although hypothermia is a core element of the medically recognized triad of death.15 This discrepancy may be attributed to the timing of measurement (e.g., pre-hospital exposure, etc.) or confounding variables like age and infections,16 which may also be explained by the actual medical practice variability in Gansu province. The significance of temperature maintenance in pre-hospital settings, particularly under high-altitude conditions, has been widely recognized, highly valued, and extensively implemented, especially following the mountain marathon incident in Baiyin city, Gansu province, where thermoregulatory interventions have been further reinforced as an essential skill in pre-hospital emergency care.17 The risk of hypothermia may be partially offset by applying these measures; while a direct implementation of international standards may underestimate the effectiveness of treatment in Gansu. For more holistic optimization of this model, further modeling can be carried out by including localized parameters, such as temperature management during transport.

Severe trauma-induced stress response can lead to fluctuations in glucose levels, in turn inducing acidosis, electrolyte imbalance, and disorders of energy metabolism in brain cells possibly. However, this study failed to conclude that alteration in BG levels was an independent predictive factor for severe trauma (Table 3). The lack of predictive value for this important parameter of stress-induced BG changes highlights a blind spot in BG monitoring within grassroots hospitals of Gansu. It is speculated to be related to high BG data missing rate, with a missing data rate of 54.3% and 45.5% according to prior in statistics training set and external validation set, and measurement deviations in fingertip BG values, possibly arising from device accuracy, individual variability, etc. In rural areas of Gansu, it is a challenge to perform timely venous BG measurements in grassroots medical institutions due to issues such as delays in reporting and difficulties in sampling. Moreover, venous BG testing is affected by multiple factors such as local testing equipment, operational standards, hematocrit levels, and the natural environment. Therefore, fingertip BG measurements cannot replace venous BG monitoring. Consequently, there is a limited feasibility to employ fingertip BG measurements to assess injury risk at the grassroots level in Gansu, underscoring that excluding this indicator from the predictive model improves its applicability in clinical practice.

Among various injury factors in the province, baseline data summary indicated the significantly high proportion of traffic accidents and falls from heights (>35%), and injuries to the head/neck and extremities (>26%). Moreover, patients with extremities and pelvic fractures account for over 35%, aligning with the results of global trauma epidemiology surveys, indicating higher possibility of injury due to high-energy injury mechanisms in these areas.18,19 However, the presence of fractures was not an independent predictor in the model, which may be explained by the lack of diagnostic coding for imaging examination results of patients in grassroots medical institutions in Gansu province.20,21 Given this unexpected finding, the lack of specific classification as well as subtyping of fracture sites and types after injury may yield results that cannot indicate the true incidence and classification of fractures accurately. In the future, the model established in our study should be further improved via more comprehensive subgroup analyses on fracture sites and diagnostic subtypes.

Chronic diseases themselves have a significant impact on post-trauma physiological compensatory functions.13 In our study, comorbidities were set as candidate factors to correct the overestimation of the impact of various risk factors in severe trauma assessment and better adapt to the chronic disease management models used by grassroots hospitals in Gansu.22 However, there was no statistical difference in this indicator between the 2 subgroups at the predefined threshold (Table 2); and this factor was excluded from subsequent regression analysis. This lack of significance may be related to residents' insufficient understanding of chronic disease management and record establishment, as well as the overwhelming pathological effect of high-energy injury factors.23 Consequently, our future work should emphasize on promoting awareness of chronic diseases and health management among grassroots communities, gradual refinement of the stratification and classification of chronic diseases, as well as the exploration of correlation with injury mechanisms.

The data of this study follow the international trauma life monitoring parameters; however, surface hemorrhage contribute the least to the total score of the model. Additionally, there is a lack of standardized quantitative measures for both surface hemorrhage and the amount of bleeding among grassroots medical institutions in Gansu. In the future, to avoid underestimation the severity of bleeding-induced trauma, it is important to quantify the amount of bleeding, such as by measuring the area of gauze saturation or hemoglobin levels, or to combine these measures with the injury mechanism,24 rather than conflating surface hemorrhage with the amount of bleeding.

It should be acknowledged that in trauma treatment, there are significant limitations to the concept of multiple injuries, which, however; was rarely included as an assessment indicator by existing models. Previously, multiple injuries were defined in clinical expert consensus as injuries occurring in at least 2 anatomical sites caused by a single injurious factor, with at least one injury being life-threatening. Nevertheless, An international consensus process and proposal of the new 'Berlin definition' removed the requirement of “at least 1 injury being life-threatening”. The current definition on multiple injuries is based solely on the anatomical coexistence of multiple injuries, without directly determining whether there is a life threat.11 In response to the inaccuracies in detecting multiple and complex injuries using the revised trauma score, this model incorporated the multiple injuries in this Expert Consensus (2014 edition) as a predictive variable.

Nomogram has been applied to the prognosis assessment of multiple diseases such as tumors, sepsis, and coronary artery disease,25, 26, 27 yet with few nomograms predictive models for severe trauma based on multi-center data in the country. The nomogram model constructed in our study is robust against variability in individual factors affecting overall results, revealing significant convenience even for healthcare personnel without a statistical background. This nomogram for predicting severe trauma was created by considering core monitoring indicators from the ATLS®, in combination with anatomical injuries (e.g., multiple injuries and surface hemorrhage) and physiological functional status (e.g., circulatory, respiratory, and neurological). This model was validated internally and externally to have a good fitting, with a concordance index greater than 0.87. In addition, we found that the net clinical benefit derived from the decision curve analysis applied to the external validation set becomes negative when the probability threshold exceeds 0.85. This finding aligns with observed clinical outcomes because the patients included in this study comprise both pre-hospital and in-hospital deaths. When the predicted probability threshold for pre-hospital death approaches its maximum value (i.e., near 1), no intervention can provide effective clinical benefit (Fig. 3B). These results have great significance for the widespread application of this model in regions such as Gannan prefecture, border towns in Gannan prefecture, and Qilian county, where there are scarce medical resources and difficult inter-hospital transfers. On one hand, the model allows for an efficient identification of critically traumatic patients by grassroots hospitals, preventing non-severe trauma patients from being referred to higher-level hospitals, thereby reducing futile transportation costs and alleviating pressure on higher-level hospitals. On the other hand, it enables the improvement of consultation work, enhancement of collaboration, and avoidance of overly dispersed diagnosis and treatment for tertiary hospitals within the province.

Even with promising results described as above, the data in this study need improvement, such as the lack of universal diagnostic coding for primary-level imaging. To address these issues, our future search will continue to: (1) specify the classification of various variables, such as subdividing anatomical locations of fracture clearly; (2) use artificial intelligence to convert natural language descriptions and injury details into structured data; and (3) closely monitor changes in RR or SpO2 trends to optimize indicators related to dynamic risk assessment.

While our study included multiple centers, all were located within Gansu province, which may limit the external generalizability of the model. The demographic characteristics, trauma patterns, and clinical resources of primary medical institutions in Gansu may differ from those in other provinces (e.g., eastern coastal regions or ethnic minority areas). Thus, validation in more geographically diverse settings is needed to confirm the model's broader applicability.

In summary, this study established a nomogram with 6 predictors of multiple injuries, surface hemorrhage, SI, RR, SpO2, and GCS score, providing a reliable tool for screening patients with severe trauma. Findings in this study may provide potential foundation for identifying severe trauma in primary healthcare institutions in underdeveloped areas of western China, offer valuable guidance for developing other models in resource-limited regions, and facilitate the development of a standardized regional trauma treatment system.
