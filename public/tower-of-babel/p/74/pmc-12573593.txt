Prevalence of amyloid deposition in distal biceps tendon ruptures: a retrospective review of intraoperative biopsy
Authors: Tessa C Griffin, Blake A Samsill, Lisa M Kruse, Samuel J Mosiman, A Neil Salyapongse, Lauren N Summers, Josef N Tofte, Brian F Grogan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn a previous study, one-third of patients with heart failure secondary to wild-type transthyretin amyloidosis were found to have distal biceps tendon (DBT) ruptures, and a large proportion of these ruptures were spontaneous or asymptomatic. This rate is higher than the 2.5% incidence of DBT ruptures seen in patients with other causes of heart failure. DBT ruptures can be used as a screening opportunity for early detection of systemic amyloidosis. However, when a subsequent study investigated the rate of amyloid deposition in 30 patients with acute DBT ruptures, only 3% had evidence of amyloid deposition. Although that study was powered to detect a DBT rupture prevalence of 33%, we suspect the real prevalence of amyloid deposition in patients presenting with DBT ruptures is lower, and a higher-powered study would help to better understand the role of intraoperative biopsy of DBT ruptures for systemic amyloidosis screening.MethodsThis study is a retrospective review of 53 patients with DBT ruptures who underwent operative repair at a single institution between June 2022 and June 2024. At the time of surgery, a tissue sample was taken from the DBT and sent as a specimen for evaluation. Specimens were reviewed by board-certified pathologists under microscopy, and Congo red staining was performed to evaluate for amyloid deposition. Specimens that stained positive for amyloid were sent to an outside facility for liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry for confirmation and subtype identification. Patient demographic data were collected by a retrospective chart review.ResultsOf the 53 included patients, 4 (9.43%) were positive for amyloid deposition on Congo red staining and liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry. No patients had a diagnosis of amyloidosis or cardiac manifestations of amyloidosis at the time of repair.ConclusionIntraoperative biopsies of DBT ruptures may be a valuable tool for early detection of amyloid deposition.Keywords: Distal biceps tendon rupture, Distal biceps tendon repair, Amyloidosis, Heart failure, Elbow, Upper extremity

Systemic amyloidosis is rare disease affecting approximately 50 per 1 million people per year.4,12 Insoluble amyloid fibrils, the product of misfolded proteins, deposit in the extracellular matrix and disrupt normal organ structure and function.10,15 Fifty percent of patients with systemic amyloidosis have cardiac manifestations,1,20 which is typically a restrictive cardiomyopathy due to the decreased wall compliance and occasional conduction disorders due to conduction system infiltration.16 Cardiac amyloidosis can be divided into light chain amyloidosis and transthyretin amyloidosis (ATTR).10 Light chain amyloidosis is a clonal plasma cell dyscrasia, which leads to the clonal production of misfolded immunoglobulin light chains.10 ATTR is less common, and occurs when the transthyretin protein produced by the liver becomes unstable, dissociates into monomers, and aggregates into amyloid fibrils.10,13,19 Wild type ATTR (ATTRwt) is due to sporadic mutations, whereas mutant or hereditary ATTR displays autosomal dominant inheritance.13

No current therapy is able to reverse the course of the disease once amyloid has been deposited in the tissue.4,8,10,27 Rather, treatment strategies are focused on supportive management of the volume overload associated with heart failure and preventing further amyloid deposition.4,8,10,27 There is only 1 Federal Drug Administration–approved disease-modifying therapy for ATTRwt.21 Tafamidis meglumide, a first-in-class selective transthryretin inhibitor, binds to transthryretin to prevent misfolding and amyloid formation.17 In a multicenter, international, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 3 trial, tafamidis meglumide significantly reduced all-cause mortality and cardiovascular hospitalizations compared to placebo, with the strongest effect seen in early stage disease.14 Therefore, early diagnosis and initiation of treatment are the key for improving patient outcomes.

Orthopedic manifestation of systemic amyloidosis often precedes cardiac manifestations by 5 to 10 years.4,5,7,11 Orthopedic manifestations of amyloidosis are numerous, and include carpal tunnel syndrome, trigger finger, distal biceps tendon (DBT) rupture, rotator cuff tear, and spinal stenosis.5,6,11,22,24 The role of intraoperative biopsy for detection of systemic amyloidosis during common orthopedic procedures is an area of ongoing investigation.2,5,7

In this regard, DBT ruptures are of particular interest. In a previous study, 33.3% of patients with heart failure secondary ATTRwt were found to have DBT ruptures, compared to only 2.5% of patients with other causes of heart failure.7 In the patients who were able to recall the onset of DBT rupture (62.2%), DBT rupture occurred at a median of 5 years before their heart failure diagnosis.7 However, when a subsequent study investigated the rate of amyloid deposition in 30 patients with acute traumatic biceps tendon ruptures, only 3% had evidence of amyloid deposition.2 Notably, that study was powered to detect a prevalence of 33%.2 We suspect the true prevalence of amyloid deposition in patients presenting with DBT ruptures is lower than the 33% prevalence of DBT ruptures that has been reported for patients with heart failure secondary to ATTRwt. As such, we hypothesized that a study with a larger sample size may demonstrate a higher prevalence of amyloid deposition in patients presenting with DBT ruptures than has previously been reported in the literature.

Material and methods

Before this study, routine intraoperative biopsy for Congo red staining with reflex to liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS) was adopted as the standard of care for all DBT ruptures at our institution. An institutional review board approval was obtained to retrospectively review these results. This study is a retrospective consecutive cohort study of patients who underwent operative repair of a DBT rupture between June 2022 and June 2024. A tissue biopsy was sent for every patient undergoing operative repair of a DBT rupture during this time period. No patients declined tissue biopsy. All 53 patients who elected for surgical repair of a DBT rupture between June 2022 and June 2024 were included in this study. Patients who did not undergo surgical repair were excluded from this study.

DBT repairs were performed by 4 surgeons (B.G., L.K., N.S., and J.T.) at a single institution. At the time of surgery, a tissue sample was taken from the DBT and sent as a specimen for evaluation. Specimens were reviewed by board-certified pathologists under microscopy, and Congo red staining was performed to evaluate for amyloid deposition. Specimens that stained positive for amyloid were sent to Mayo Clinic Laboratories for LC-MS for confirmation and amyloid subtype identification. Patient demographic data including age, sex, injury laterality, tobacco use, alcohol use, American Society of Anesthesiologists class, and comorbid conditions including heart failure, aortic stenosis, atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, pacemaker, diabetes, dialysis, statin use, testosterone replacement, peripheral neuropathy, spinal stenosis, and family history of amyloidosis were collected by a retrospective chart review. Data regarding previous diagnosis or surgical management of carpal tunnel syndrome, trigger digit, rotator cuff tear, and contralateral DBT rupture were also collected.

Results

There were 53 patients with DBT ruptures included in this retrospective chart review. Demographic data are presented in Table I. Of the 53 patients, 49 (92.45%) were male with a mean age of 48.36 years. No patients had evidence of a cardiovascular condition at the time of the DBT repair. No patients had a family history of amyloidosis. Three (5.66%) patients had spinal stenosis. Three (5.66%) patients had carpal tunnel syndrome, 1 (1.89%) of whom had undergone carpal tunnel release. Two (3.77%) patients had a diagnosis of trigger finger, 1 (1.89%) of whom underwent trigger finger release. Six (11.33%) patients had rotator cuff tears, 3 (5.66%) of whom had undergone a treatment for rotator cuff tear. Three (5.66%) patients had contralateral DBT ruptures, 2 (3.77%) of whom had undergone contralateral DBT rupture repair previously.

Five patients (9.43%) were found to have evidence of amyloid deposition based on Congo red stain and LC-MS. Of these 5 patients, 1 (20%) had carpal tunnel syndrome. None of the patients with amyloid deposition had spinal stenosis, trigger finger, rotator cuff tear, or a contralateral DBT rupture.

All patients could recall the onset of DBT rupture and described a traumatic mechanism of injury, although the energy level of their mechanisms of injury ranged from picking up an object to fall from a height.

Discussion

In patients with presenting with DBT rupture and undergoing surgical repair, we identified a 9.43% prevalence of amyloid deposition. This is higher than the previously described rate of 3%.2 This prevalence is more in keeping literature on the prevalence of amyloid deposition in patients undergoing a carpal tunnel release. A recent study identified 10.2% of men over the age of 50 and women over the age of 60 undergoing carpal tunnel release had evidence of amyloid deposition on Congo red staining of tenosynovial tissue.23 However, the reported rates of amyloid deposition in patient undergoing carpal tunnel release does vary from 2.3% to 34%.5 With that in mind, the amyloidosis screening criteria for patients undergoing carpal tunnel release were proposed in 2019 by the American Society for Surgery of the Hand.5 A follow-up study demonstrated a yield greater than 20% when focusing on nonobese men over the age of 70.26 No such screening criteria exists for DBT ruptures.

There is no known mechanism to reverse or decrease existing amyloid deposits, but with the introduction of disease-modifying therapies, such as tafadimis meglumide, further amyloid deposition can be prevented in patient with known amyloidosis.17,14 However, a preventative therapy can only be initiated after a tissue diagnosis is obtained. Thus, early detection and initiation of the treatment is the only known avenue to prevent progression to cardiomyopathy and remains crucial for improving patient outcomes. The typical age of onset for cardiac manifestations of amyloidosis is in the seventh and eighth decades of life,3,25 The mean age in our cohort was 48, and no patient, including those who sustained positive for amyloid, had known cardiac disease at the time of repair. This would suggest that DBT ruptures present an opportunity for early detection of amyloidosis, earlier even than the current literature on screening patients with carpal tunnel syndrome, which targets patients in their 60s and 70s.5,18,23,26

This study does have several limitations. First, a priori power analysis demonstrated that high precision would require a sample size of 340. Even with including all possible patients, we were unable to achieve this number of records due to relatively low incidence of DBT ruptures. However, until further studies with a larger cohort or a meta-analysis of smaller case series are undertaken, we feel that the potential utility of screening at the time of DBT surgical repairs should not be discounted.

Second, although all patients with DBT ruptures undergoing surgical repair were included, all patients in the study group had an acute traumatic mechanism of injury. In the Geller et al study, of the 37 patients with heart failure due to ATTRwt who had DBT ruptures, 14 patients (37.8%) were unaware of the onset or existence of DBT rupture, which would suggest an atraumatic DBT rupture. Atraumatic DBT ruptures are less common than acute traumatic tears, with 1 epidemiologic study finding only 4 of 92 (4.3%) DBT ruptures in the general population were atraumatic.9 We suspect that a cohort of atraumatic DBT ruptures would have a higher rate of amyloid deposition than the 9.43% observed in this study or the 3% observed by Baylor et al.

Third, the presence of amyloid deposition does not confirm that these patients will go on to develop systemic amyloidosis. Studies with longer follow-up are still needed. Fourth, this study was conducted at a single institution. Geographic differences in the prevalence of amyloidosis are not well-understood, but could impact the utility of screening in difference geographic regions. Finally, due to the practical considerations of obtaining a tissue biopsy, the current research into DBT ruptures has focused on patients who elect for operative repair. As such, patients who elect for nonoperative management, who may also be older, frailer, or have a higher rate of preexisting heart failure, were not considered for amyloidosis screening. Alternative screening strategies could be considered in the future for these patients.

Conclusion

We found that 9.43% of all patients with DBT ruptures had amyloid deposition in the DBT at the time of surgical repair as determined by Congo red staining and LC-MS. Intraoperative biopsies of DBT ruptures may be a valuable tool for early detection of amyloid deposition. However, further research is needed to amass a larger cohort of patients and determine which patients would most benefit from screening.
