Many physicians consider pregnancy or become pregnant during their residency, fellowship training, and the early years of independent practice. However, childbearing surgeons report negative reactions from their colleagues and the institutions within which they work and have disproportionately high rates of prenatal and obstetrical complications.1–3 Concerns regarding pregnancy and family planning may contribute to the gender disparity seen in surgical specialties; in 2020, only 39.8% of surgical trainees in Canada were female (excluding the specialty of obstetrics and gynecology).4 Institutions and the surgical profession must urgently address the barriers faced by childbearing surgeons to protect their health and well-being and optimize their retention in the surgical workforce.

Surgeons have a disproportionately high incidence of fertility-and pregnancy-related complications compared with other pregnant individuals.4 A recent study from the United States found that female surgeons had significantly higher risks of infertility and major pregnancy complications, including pregnancy loss, than the female partners of male surgeons, even after controlling for confounders.2 High rates of infertility and pregnancy-related complications have been observed across surgical subspecialties including otolaryngology, neurosurgery, plastic surgery, and orthopedic surgery.3,5,6 Compared with family physicians, the cumulative probability of childbirth was significantly lower among surgeons.7

Female surgeons also face colleagues’ negative perceptions and stigma related to pregnancy. In a recent online survey of 272 surgical residents in Canada, 56 women reported 76 pregnancy events during training; 62.5% had complications. In comparison to male respondents, female surgeons reported substantially higher rates of stigma because they were parents; more than half of female respondents (58.5%) reported hearing negative comments related to pregnancy during training from staff or co-residents and many (41.4%) said they delayed childbearing to avoid negative perceptions from faculty and other residents.8 Perceptions around family planning have an important effect on specialty choice. In a 2021 qualitative study of 34 fourth-year medical students in Toronto, participants perceived that surgical subspecialties generally offered relatively poor support for family building.9 However, the findings of a 2023 survey of graduates of obstetrics and gynecology residency programs suggest that the culture surrounding pregnancy and parenthood may be different in this specialty. Most respondents who had become parents “perceived appropriate support from their colleagues and the program administration” when they were pregnant during residency. 10 This suggests that support from colleagues and programs can be beneficial in changing the surgical culture around pregnancy and parenthood.10

In Canada, support for pregnancy and parental leave is provided by federal and provincial governments. In addition, provincial residency associations have negotiated that pregnant residents not be subject to overnight call beginning from 20–31 weeks’ gestation. However, no regulations limit exposure to fluoroscopy or airborne toxins such as cement products or specify operative case duration, breaks from standing, or time needed for nutrition, voiding, and lactation.5,11,12 Furthermore, no standardized institutional policies regarding pregnancy accommodations or return to work exist for surgical fellows or staff.

Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH) and Brigham and Women’s Hospital, both affiliated with Harvard University, recently introduced the Supporting Our Physician Parents program, which serves as an example of how childbearing surgeons could be supported. 1 The structured program for postpartum junior faculty physicians and fellows facilitates return to work with coaching sessions, lactation stipends, support groups, and a program for emergency backup pump supplies. A post-implementation survey showed strong agreement that the program contributed positively to physicians’ health and well-being.1 Following this success, MGH implemented their guide for expectant surgical residents and new parents, detailing accommodations in clinical duties during pregnancy and lactation, parental leave, and the transition back to work.13

All health care institutions should consider implementing similar programs, tailored to the needs of their surgeons. Without protective policies and measures, high rates of pregnancy complications and experiences of stigma are unlikely to improve. Surgeons at all levels of seniority should champion the initiative. Accountability for residents should lie within the surgical program’s residency program committee, and practice groups and the fellowship director should oversee accountability and implementation of the policy for fellows and staff. These policies should be reviewed iteratively through a quality improvement process to ensure ongoing improvements.

Although provincial residency associations dictate pregnancy and parental leave for surgical residents, fellows and staff do not have the same protection, leaving them at the mercy of their institutions or needing to secure locum coverage. Surgical waitlists and locum coverage are challenges that practising surgeons face during pregnancy and parental leave.11 Locum advertising can be done through provincial (e.g., Health Force Ontario, Nova Scotia Health) or federal job sites (https://www.jobbank.gc.ca/), and through surgical professional societies (e.g., Canadian Orthopaedic Association, Canadian Society of Plastic Surgeons). Announcements to university-affiliated surgical programs can target final-year residents and fellows. If locum coverage is not available, surgical practice groups should develop policies for redistribution of the wait-list of surgeons taking leave to minimize disruption and stress on childbearing surgeons or their patients. Returning to work postpartum also remains a challenge for surgeons at all levels; however, MGH’s postpartum program has had encouraging results.1

In summary, successful policy and practice changes should incorporate standardized institutional pregnancy and parental leave for surgical fellows and staff surgeons, similar to those detailed for residents;2 institutional assistance with locum coverage for staff and practice group guidance in the event locum coverage is not secured;3 institutional pregnancy and return-to-work policies, including lactation and specialty-specific occupational exposure guidance;4 and engagement and recognition from residency program committees, fellowship directors, and institutional surgical staff. Such policies, implemented through the collaborative efforts of residents, staff, and departmental leads will not only benefit and protect childbearing surgeons, but also their colleagues and patients. They may also enhance institutions’ ability to recruit and retain female trainees and surgeons.
