Personalized artificial intelligence based left ventricular ejection fraction and systolic dysfunction assessment
Authors: Geerthy Thambiraj, Sandeep Chandra Bollepalli, Adam Johnson, Rajeev Malhotra, Eric M Isselbacher, Jagmeet P Singh, Antonis A Armoundas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeft-ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (LVEF) is a fundamental measure of cardiac function, typically assessed with resource-intensive imaging techniques, such as transthoracic echocardiography (TTE). We evaluated the electrocardiogram (ECG) as an alternative, easily accessible data to estimate LVEF in a large cohort of 191,941 patients, comprising 236,623 ECG/TTE pairs. Using either the ECG data alone or with structured features, we developed convolutional and probabilistic neural network models to estimate LVEF and quantify its uncertainty. The ECG-only model achieved a mean-absolute-error (MAE) of 7.71% and a root-mean-square-error (RMSE) of 10.36%, while the hybrid model achieved an MAE of 7.84% and an RMSE of 10.52%. Personalized models significantly improved performance, achieving MAEs of 5.98% (ECG-only) and 6.75% (hybrid). LV systolic dysfunction (LVEF ≤ 40%) was identified with an AUC of 0.88, sensitivity of 0.92 and negative predictive value of 0.98. The presented models demonstrated excellent performance in estimating LVEF and screening of LV systolic dysfunction.Subject terms: Cardiology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) is a central clinical prognostic parameter in assessing progression of heart failure1, risk of sudden cardiac death2, and complications from valvular heart dysfunction3. There exist several methods for estimating LVEF, including invasive left-ventriculography during cardiac catheterization, and non-invasive imaging techniques such as transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), cardiac computed tomography, nuclear imaging, and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (cMRI). However, each method has important limitations. cMRI is expensive and limited to specialized settings, nuclear imaging involves radiation exposure, and TTE, while portable and more accessible, has high intra- and inter-observer variability4.

Given these challenges, as well as recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), there has been growing interest in estimating LVEF by applying AI algorithms to the electrocardiogram (ECG)5,6, which is affordable and widely available in under-resourced settings. To that end, several studies have developed deep learning-based models to estimate the TTE-derived LVEF, from ECG signals7–9. Using the ECG as an image has resulted in a mean absolute error (MAE) of 5.84%10, while a 30-day interval limit between the ECG recording and the TTE date, achieved an MAE of 5.28%11, and an cMRI-based ground truth for estimating LVEF resulted in an MAE of 4.64%12.

Despite various attempts to estimate LVEF, concerns regarding the reliability of these models still persist. Most prior studies have focused primarily on point estimates, often overlooking the uncertainty inherent in these estimates. A recent study employing an 82-layer probabilistic convolutional neural network (CNN), reported an MAE of 7.5% employing the standard deviation (SD) of the estimation as the uncertainty metric13. However, uncertainty stems from various sources, including inter-observer measurement variability in clinical measurements14 and limitations in model’s knowledge of personalized natural history factors15, raising questions about the reliability of point estimates. Therefore, evaluating a model’s uncertainty in its estimations is imperative for making informed decisions about treatment and diagnosis16–18. An alternative approach to reduce the estimation error, in the era of personalized medicine, is to obtain personalized predictions by developing models that learn to adapt to unique patient-specific characteristics.

In this study, we define two practical use cases for ECG-based LVEF estimation; first, the primary intended use is in settings where traditional imaging modalities are not available or are delayed, an ECG-based estimate of LVEF may serve as interim decision support to guide prioritization and care escalation until definitive imaging can be obtained; second, screening/triage, leveraging the low cost and ubiquity of ECG to help rule out LV systolic dysfunction and prioritize confirmatory imaging for higher-risk cases. To achieve these objectives we explore various CNN architectures to estimate LVEF, using TTE-derived LVEF as the ground truth. We also investigate the uncertainty quantification using a probabilistic neural network to derive prediction intervals for LVEF estimates across different LVEF ranges. Furthermore, we hypothesize that personalized deep learning improves LVEF estimation performance compared to a generalized model by learning from individual longitudinal ECG data. Additionally, the developed foundational CNN model is externally validated using the external MIMIC-IV dataset.

Results

CNN configurations and architectures

CNN models were assessed using the initial data across eight different configurations, by varying the number of layers, fusion schemes, and input signal types. To obtain the optimal architecture, we employed a tuning mechanism using the Keras tuner that evaluated each of the eight configurations through 50 trials. We reported the MAE and RMSE for the training, validation and testing datasets. The choice of number of filters, kernel size, activation functions used in the tuning process to obtain the optimal model is summarized in Table S1.

The results indicate that the configuration utilizing the median ECG signal as input outperformed the configuration using the full signal, as shown in Table S2. Additionally, Table S2 indicates that the model employing the 2-layer (2 L) configuration demonstrates slightly better performance compared to the 1-layer (1 L) and 10-layer (10 L) configurations for the ECG-only architectures. We also observed that the 2 L early fusion configuration performs marginally better than the 2 L late fusion configuration.

Furthermore, it is evident from Table S2 that the 2 L early and late fusion configurations performed nearly identically in terms of MAE and RMSE, 8.46% and 11.26% vs 8.39% and 11.11%, respectively. However, favoring computational efficiency, we chose the 2 L early fusion configuration, using the median ECG beat as the optimized architecture for further analysis. The results of the tuning process for each configuration are presented in Supplementary Data S1–S12.

CNN model performance

Once the optimized model configuration was obtained, we incorporated a fully connected layer in the optimized model (a 2 L early fusion configuration, using the median ECG beat as an input). We ran the Keras tuner for this configuration over 50 trials, and the performance metrics, including MAE and RMSE improved to 7.68% and 10.35%, respectively; detailed results are presented in Data S13.

To evaluate the optimal configuration, we implemented a 5-fold stratified cross-validation approach. The training, validation and testing datasets were concatenated into a single dataset, which was then split into five folds, maintaining a similar range of LVEF values in each fold. In each iteration, one fold served as the test set, while the remaining four served as the training set, allowing us to assess the model performance across different subsets of the data. We observed consistency, with a mean ± SD of 7.72 ± 0.03, across the 5-folds, as shown in Data S14.

To investigate whether the extracted tabular features improved LVEF estimation, we built a hybrid model based on the selected optimal CNN configuration: the 2 L, the median ECG beat, and the early fusion configuration. This hybrid model utilized ECG as the input to the CNN, and tabular features as input to the MLP network, yielding an MAE and RMSE of 7.86% and 10.56%, respectively. The evaluation metrics suggest that the hybrid model performs similarly to the ECG-only model. This aligns with previous findings indicating that features extracted from the ECG signal contributed minimally to the overall model performance10. The tuner results across 50 trials are presented in Data S15.

We further optimized the network by performing a fine search of the hyperparameter space, described in Table S3, which resulted in an MAE of 7.83% and an RMSE of 10.64% for the ECG-only model, and an MAE of 7.66% and an RMSE of 10.30% for the hybrid model. The tuner results for both the ECG and hybrid models are detailed in Data S16–S17.

Given the wide distribution of LVEF values in the study population (Fig. 1), we implemented L2 regularization to mitigate overfitting and ensure equal importance is assigned to all labels. The Keras tuner results for 50 trials, yielded an MAE and an RMSE of 7.71% and 10.36% for the ECG-only model, and 7.84% and 10.52% for the hybrid model; while the detailed results are presented in Data S18 and S19. Henceforth, we refer to the best performing models as the foundational ECG-only (Fig. S1), and the foundational hybrid model (Fig. S2) respectively. We also presented the error distribution box plots in Figs. 2 and 3 for all LVEF ranges to assess the model’s performance for both the ECG and hybrid models. It is observed that for both models, the MAE is higher in the extreme LVEF ranges (<25 and >75). This was attributed to the relatively limited number of samples in the very high and low LVEF values.

Additionally, we trained separate foundational ECG-only models, on either male-only or female-only cohorts to investigate the sex-based impact on LVEF estimation. The male cohort consisted of 128,118 ECGs, while the female cohort consisted of 100,681 ECGs. Overall, we observed a slightly better performance in the female-only cohort (MAE and RMSE: 8.27% and 11.29%, Data S20) compared to the male-only cohort (MAE and RMSE: 11.14% and 14.69%, Data S21).

We found the MAE distributions of the male and female cohorts to be significantly different (p < 0.05), using the independent t-test with equal variances. A possible explanation for this observation may lie in the differences of the LVEF value distributions between females and males. Previous studies have shown that women tend to have higher LVEF values compared to men19, with the female distribution of LVEF values being shifted towards higher values compared to men (see also, Fig. 1)20. In our study, the female cohort exhibited a similar pattern, with more LVEF values in the upper ranges, particularly in the 70–85% range, leading to a higher mean LVEF of 62.13%, compared to the male cohort’s mean of 57.86%, as shown in Fig. 1. The female cohort demonstrated a lower MAE, likely due to its higher number of values around the average LVEF, resulting in improved model’s performance. In contrast, the male cohort exhibits a greater proportion of LVEF values in the lower ranges, which introduces more variability and makes the predictions less accurate, resulting in a higher MAE.

When both sexes are combined, the resulting broader distribution of LVEF values appears to mitigate sex-based performance differences, allowing the model to generalize better, manifested by an improvement of the foundational ECG-only model performance.

Prediction uncertainty estimation

To quantify uncertainty through prediction intervals, we implemented the PNN architecture for both the foundational ECG-only and hybrid models, with hyperparameter tuning/optimization using the Keras Tuner, as presented in Data S22–23.

The error distribution and prediction interval plots across the whole range of the LVEF for both ECG-only and hybrid models are shown in the Online Supplement Figs. S3–S6.

Figures S3 and S5 present the MAE for LVEF values ranging from 5% to 90% along with the corresponding sample sizes for the ECG-only model and the hybrid models of the PNN architectures. We observed that the foundational ECG-only PNN model’s performance was comparable to the CNN’s, with MAE and RMSE of 7.97% and 10.67%, respectively. Using the foundational hybrid PNN model, the MAE and RMSE were 7.89% and 10.58%, respectively.

We observe similar prediction intervals for both the ECG-only and hybrid models, as shown in Figs. S4 and S6, with LVEF values below 30% and above 70%, falling outside these prediction intervals (the box plots in Figs. S4 and S6 illustrate the lower and upper bounds of the prediction intervals for each respective range). Interestingly, the prediction intervals are wider at the lower end of the LVEF values, and narrow as the LVEF values approach the mean of the LVEF distribution. While for lower-end LVEF values we observe broader prediction intervals, the overlap between the upper and lower bounds indicates a high level of certainty that the LVEF values fall within these intervals, suggesting limited variability. Furthermore, in the 60–75% range, the data points are closely clustered, indicating greater precision in the prediction intervals around the true mean of the LVEF distribution. These observations highlight the fact that both the true mean and the number of samples of the distribution influence the width of the prediction intervals.

Model interpretability

To gain insights into the significant contributions of each ECG lead during the model’s decision-making process, we performed a GRAD-CAM analysis. While the 2-layer early fusion approach delivered the best performance, we aimed to examine the contribution of each lead independently; therefore, we selected the 2-layer late fusion configuration, where each lead was processed individually through its corresponding CNN layer. This enabled us to generate heat-maps for each lead, highlighting the most influential ECG segments in the model’s LVEF estimation. We then calculated the median heat-map for each lead’s corresponding median beat.

In the Echo cohort, we observed significant contributions from several regions of the ECG, from different leads, such as the PR interval of Lead I, the PR segment and QRS duration of aVL and V4, the QT interval of aVF, and the T-wave of V2 (Fig. S7). Additionally, nearly all segments of Leads II, aVR, and V6 contributed to the model’s performance. These findings are in agreement with those of a recent study that emphasized the importance of Leads I, II, and V2, in LVEF prediction21.

Algorithm external validation

The foundational ECG–only architecture was validated using the MIMIC-IV ECG records22, MIMIC-IV notes23, and MIMIC-IV Echo data24, as described in the Methods; the model achieved an MAE of 8.79% and an RMSE of 11.83% during external validation in a cMRI cohort, that were slightly higher than the corresponding values obtained in our own cohort (Data S18); a finding that may be attributed to this imaging modality shift or population demographic differences.

Development and testing of a personalized CNN model

The cohort for the personalized model was constructed by including all available records of all subjects. Subjects with a single report only were included in the training and validation datasets, while those with multiple records were allocated to the test cohort. The cohort was comprised of 306,306 ECG records, corresponding to 78,677 and 8742 unique subjects in the training and validation cohorts, respectively. The test cohort was comprised of 218,887 ECG records corresponding to 64,988 unique subjects. We utilized the foundational ECG-only model and the Keras tuner for 50 trials was employed; the results are presented in Data S24. The best generalized model was selected from Data S24. The generalized ECG-only model achieved an MAE of 8.98% and an RMSE of 12.21%.

For the personalized models, the training process was iterative: the best generalized model was trained on the first of i samples and tested on the (i + 1)th sample in the test cohort; that is initially, the model was trained on the first sample, and in each subsequent iteration, it added one more sample to the training set, while testing on the next sample for each subject. During each iteration, all layers of the model were fine-tuned by unfreezing them, and the model was recompiled with a learning rate of 1e-5. This approach allowed the model to continuously learn by incorporating new samples while being evaluated on previously unseen data. To prevent overfitting, early stopping was implemented by monitoring the relative error between the model’s estimated value and the true label on the training data, requiring the training to stop if the relative error fell below a predetermined threshold of 1%. To compare the personalized model performance for each subject’s sample with the generalized model, we also tested each sample of the subject on the best generalized model.

The personalized model was then trained and tested for each subject in the test cohort. To evaluate the performance of the generalized and personalized models, we calculated the relative error (between the model’s estimated value and the true label) for each subject’s sample during testing; where, the first sample from each subject was excluded, as it was used solely for training. The results are based on the second sample onwards in each subject for both the generalized and personalized models. The relative errors for all samples within each subject was grouped by sample number. This allowed us to test the hypothesis that as the number of sample number increases for each subject, the personalized model’s error decreases.

Assessment of Left Ventricular Dysfunction

An LVEF ≤ 40% is indicative of left ventricular systolic dysfunction19,25. To assess the models’ performance in identifying subjects with left ventricular systolic dysfunction, we used the same hyperparameters of the foundational ECG-only and hybrid CNN model architectures, modified the loss function to binary cross-entropy, changed the final layer to a sigmoid activation, and calculated confusion matrices using a predefined classification threshold of 0.05. The ECG-only classifier model achieved an AUC of 0.88 with a standard error of 0.0044, and an accuracy of 0.67. Similarly, the hybrid model achieved an AUC of 0.88 with a standard error of 0.0044, and an accuracy of 0.68, in predicting left ventricular dysfunction; this threshold was selected with the objective of minimizing false negative results by maximizing the product of sensitivity and negative predictive value. We also calculated the 95% confidence intervals for the AUC to be (0.871, 0.889) for both the ECG-only and hybrid classifier models. Although the classifier was tuned for screening (sensitivity 0.92; NPV 0.98; Table 1), the PPV of 0.24 implies a substantial downstream burden of confirmatory testing. In the test cohort (TP = 2314; FN = 189; FP = 7240; TN = 13,182; N = 22,925), the ECG-only classifier generated 9554 positive screens (41.7%). Thus, in a clinical workflow in which a positive ECG screen prompts confirmatory TTE, 7240/9554 (75.8%) of confirmatory studies would be performed in patients without LVEF ≤ 40%, corresponding to ~3.1 additional confirmatory echocardiograms per true case identified (7240 false positives per 2314 true positives). Expressed per 1000 screened at the observed prevalence (~10.9%), ~417 would be referred for echocardiography (≈101 true positives and ≈316 false positives). Because PPV is prevalence-dependent, targeting deployment to higher pre-test probability populations would be expected to improve PPV and reduce confirmatory-imaging burden.

Finally, the personalized ECG-only CNN model (AUC: 0.939; Sensitivity: 0.977; Specificity: 0.591; PPV: 0.925; NPV: 0.835; Accuracy: 0.915) performed better than the generalized ECG-only CNN model (AUC: 0.867; Sensitivity: 0.989; Specificity: 0.147; PPV: 0.857; NPV: 0.720; Accuracy: 0.853), in detecting left-ventricular systolic dysfunction.

Discussion

LVEF is a fundamental clinical parameter used to guide diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis across a range of cardiovascular conditions. Traditionally, LVEF estimation relies on diagnostic imaging such as echocardiography and cMRI, but these methods are expensive and not widely available. This study explores the use of inexpensive and readily available electrocardiographic data combined with deep learning techniques to estimate the LVEF. Our findings highlight several key insights, first, the early fusion configuration slightly outperformed the late fusion approach and was also more computationally efficient; second, incorporating structured features did not improve model performance, as the ECG-only and hybrid models demonstrated similar results; third, the model including both female and male data, outperformed the sex-specific models; fourth, Grad-CAM analysis revealed that the segments of the PR interval, QRS duration, QT interval, and T-wave, from various leads, contributed significantly to the LVEF estimation; fifth, estimation of the prediction interval to quantify uncertainty of the LVEF estimate, indicates that most LVEF estimations are close to the ground-truth LVEF values, except at extreme LVEF values; sixth, the personalized model exhibited the lowest absolute error compared to the generalized model; seventh, the foundational model exhibited a good performance in an external validation cohort; eighth, the foundational ECG-only and hybrid classifier models exhibited very high sensitivity and negative predictive value in predicting left-ventricular systolic dysfunction.

Our study, utilizing a simple two-layer CNN architecture to estimate LVEF from ECG, achieved an MAE of 7.71% for the ECG-only model and 7.84% for the hybrid model, and exhibited similar performance as several state-of-the-art methods10,11,26. Other studies that have employed much more complex architectures, such as EfficientNet10, achieved an overall MAE of 5.84% in internal validation (with LVEF ≤ 40% MAE of 6.69%, LVEF > 40% and ≤ 50% MAE of 8.08%, and LVEF > 50% MAE of 5.41%), and an external validation MAE of 6.14% using ECG input as an image; DenseNet-12111 achieved an MAE of 5.28% for balanced samples of LVEF > 50% and ≤50% in internal validation, with an MAE of 9.56% in external validation. Another study, using ECG12Net26 obtained an MAE of 7.43%. Additionally, in this study, the foundational ECG-only and hybrid classifier models achieved an AUC of 0.88, comparable to prior work of achieving 0.9410, 0.96127 and 0.8428.

To effectively screen for left ventricular systolic dysfunction, we chose metrics to optimize the model’s performance that minimize Type II (false negatives) errors29, resulting in the classifier’s clinically significant, high sensitivity and negative predictive values. Unavoidably, the observed low positive predictive value may result in additional confirmatory TTE evaluations, and increased healthcare resource utilization.

With a simpler and more computationally-efficient architecture, our model performs comparably to more complex models, demonstrating the effectiveness of lightweight models for this regression task, and permitting deeper model introspection, transparency and explainability as shown in Fig. S7. The addition of tabular features in our foundational hybrid model showed similar performance, consistent with findings from another study10. A likely explanation is that the raw ECG waveform already provides a high-dimensional representation of conduction timing and morphology (including information closely related to heart rate and interval-based features) and may implicitly encode demographic and clinical signatures, making many structured covariates redundant. Additionally, our hybrid model used a relatively simple early-fusion strategy (feature concatenation), which may not optimally capture cross-modal interactions. Future work should evaluate more expressive multimodal fusion approaches (e.g., late-fusion, gated or attention-based fusion, multimodal transformers) and incorporate richer longitudinal EHR features to identify complementary information beyond the ECG.

While more complex architectures have shown minor improvements10–12,26,30, our results suggest that well-optimized simpler models can achieve comparable predictive power. Furthermore, deploying and fine-tuning simpler models for real-world applications is more resource-efficient.

Prediction intervals are commonly used to quantify uncertainty associated with model estimations17, and are particularly valuable in clinical settings. By providing a probable range for LVEF, prediction intervals can enhance diagnostic confidence. Although the results for both the CNN and PNN architectures were similar, the primary advantage of the PNN model lies in its ability to provide prediction intervals that reflect the uncertainty of its estimates. The figures indicate that extreme LVEF values are often overestimated, and the model lacks confidence in these estimations, reflected by the observation that the prediction intervals do not contain the actual LVEF values. In contrast, the model demonstrates more reliable estimations for LVEF values within the range of 45–70%, where the intervals are narrower and the estimated LVEF values fall within the prediction intervals (as shown in Figs. S4 and S6).

It is important to note that employing the standard deviation to assess the uncertainty of point estimates13 limits the clinical interpretability, as it does not directly provide the probability that an individual outcome falls within a specific range. In contrast, our model outputs both a mean LVEF estimate and subject-specific prediction intervals, which offer a probabilistic estimate of where the true LVEF is likely to fall. In practice, for example, a narrow prediction interval around an LVEF estimate may increase confidence in diagnosing heart failure with reduced LVEF, potentially prompting treatment initiation. Conversely, a wide interval especially near diagnostic thresholds (e.g., LVEF = 40%) may prompt additional testing, such as echocardiography, to reduce the risk of misclassification.

We have observed that the foundational ECG-only model identified lead aVR as one of the significant leads in the estimation of the LVEF. This finding aligns with existing literature, which highlights the significance of the aVR lead in identifying heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, defined as LVEF > 50%31. Specifically, T-wave changes in lead aVR, which are strongly associated with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction28, are consistently selected as significant features by GRAD-CAM.

The personalized model, estimated the LVEF with lower error compared to the generalized model, as shown in Fig. 4, and with progressively lower uncertainty and fewer outliers (as shown in Fig. 4), with each additional ECG sample from the same subject, but its use presupposes longitudinal ECG availability and an updating workflow. In practice, personalization is most realistic in care settings where patients accumulate repeated ECGs as part of routine management (e.g., chronic heart failure/cardiomyopathy follow-up, recurrent emergency/inpatient encounters, or structured outpatient surveillance). By leveraging subject-specific data, the model adapted to individual ECG patterns through an iterative training process, where it continuously learned from each subject’s prior samples. This led to a consistent decrease of the relative error (Figs. 4 and 6), demonstrating improved performance with more data per subject. Furthermore, the personalized model achieved significantly lower error across all LVEF ranges, confirming its robustness (Figs. 5 and 7). These findings underscore the value of personalization in clinical tasks with high inter-subject variability at the small cost of model retraining as new data becomes available (approximately, per each new ECG, 2.99 s in an Intel Core i9-9900 CPU @ 3.10 GHz and 1.30 s in a NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPU). For each new unseen sample, the model uses the most recent personalized weights to generate a prediction. Personalized models are stored as lightweight parameter sets containing only the model weights, which are incrementally updated as new data becomes available. This approach minimizes both storage and computational demands. Personalization also introduces trade-offs, including added operational complexity (update scheduling, model governance/versioning, and quality control) and potential overfitting if updates are performed on sparse or noisy individual-level data; in our implementation, overfitting is mitigated through early stopping based on a relative error threshold of less than 1% and regularization. However, for patients with few or no prior ECGs, the generalized model remains the appropriate default and personalization is expected to confer limited incremental benefit. Future work should evaluate scalable approaches that reduce per-patient updating burden, such as parameter-efficient personalization (e.g., small patient-specific adapters/heads), meta-learning, or cohort-level adaptation, while quantifying how performance gains vary by the number and how recent are the available ECGs as well as by clinical subgroup, to better characterize who benefits most.

When traditional imaging modalities are unavailable or delayed, the model may provide an interim LVEF estimate to support triage decisions (e.g., prioritizing limited imaging capacity, escalation of monitoring, or earlier referral), while recognizing that this scenario demands careful attention to calibration and clinical thresholds and does not replace definitive imaging when it becomes available. Longitudinal monitoring represents a distinct application requiring prospective evaluation of within-patient stability and change-detection performance. It is conceivable that, given the high prognostic value of LVEF and the growing research and development in making ambulatory monitoring devices more “medical grade”32–34, accurate, efficient, portable, convenient, interoperable35,36 and accessible37, LVEF could be measured in an out-of-office setting periodically, conveniently and comfortably, on an as needed basis, similarly with other vital sign signals38.

Although all the data were sourced from a single metropolitan area, the data were collected from many hospitals, attracting patients with very diverse demographics, which is representative to the demographics of the whole US. Another limitation is the reference-standard “noise” inherent to TTE-derived LVEF, which is known to exhibit substantial intra- and inter-observer variability4. In this study, LVEF labels were obtained from routine TTE reports using a rule-based NLP extraction approach, which may reflect heterogeneous clinical readers and measurement practices across sites and over time. This variability likely imposes an effective lower bound on achievable regression error, such that the ECG-only MAE of 7.71% and personalized MAE of 5.98% should be interpreted in the context of measurement uncertainty in the ground truth. Future work should evaluate higher-fidelity supervision (e.g., standardized quantitative reads or core-lab adjudication) and methods that explicitly incorporate label uncertainty (e.g., repeated-measure modeling or noisy-label learning) to further improve calibration and reduce residual error.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates the ability to accurately estimate the LVEF using CNN and PNN models, leveraging the ECG and structured features, with TTE imaging as the ground truth. It also explores the uncertainty in LVEF estimations across the whole LVEF range. The proposed personalized model and the PNN model for uncertainty estimation offer significant potential for improving diagnostic accuracy and optimizing clinical decision-making, at a lower cost, and in under-resourced communities. We envision that the proposed model could serve as an effective tool for determining LVEF and left-ventricular dysfunction, offering a cost-effective method for improving care and prevention strategies, in remote and under-resourced communities39,40.

Methods

Dataset sources

This retrospective study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Mass General Brigham (MGB), Boston, MA (IRB Protocol: 2021P000809), and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. We queried the Research Patient Data Registry (RPDR) database at MGB, for adult TTE reports that were sourced from multiple hospitals within the MGB system.

Data requests to RPDR were performed in two phases in order to maximize the volume of the data we employed, without delaying execution of the study. Therefore, TTE reports concerning the period from 1981 to July 2022 were requested in July 2022, and reports concerning the period from March 2022 to September 2023 were requested in September 2023. Overall, we obtained 894,127 TTE reports. Duplicate reports were then eliminated.

Natural language processing of cardiology reports

We extracted LVEF values from cardiology reports using a natural language processing (NLP) approach based on regular expressions (Regex). The extraction process comprised of three key steps to capture variability of keywords representing LVEF: Step 1. Identifying phrases related to LVEF that included numerical values; Step 2. Detecting numerical ranges and values indicated by greater than or less than symbols (e.g., >30, <50); and Step 3. Recognizing descriptive phrases associated with LVEF, such as “lower limits of normal range,” “hyperdynamic,” “severely depressed,” and “preserved”, “hyperkinetic”, “baseline”, “impaired”, “decreased” and “reduced”.

Upon obtaining the relevant phrases and numerical values, we excluded reports containing values represented by greater than or less than symbols, as well as those lacking specific sections on LVEF information. For reports with text descriptions, we assigned numerical values based on clinical significance. Specifically, “Low Normal” was defined as a range of 50–55, while “Normal LVEF” was assigned a value of 62.5, representing the mean of the normal range (55–70). Values classified as “Hyperdynamic” were assigned a value of 70, and the “Mildly Impaired” category included values ranging from 40 to 50. Text descriptions such as “Severely Impaired,” “Depressed,” and “severely reduced” given values less than 30 during LVEF extraction from TTE reports. However, during the cohort creation the reports that contains terms such as “Impaired,” “Preserved,” “Similar,” “Reduced,” “Unchanged,” “Decreased,” “Determined,” “Evaluated,” “Assessment,” “Improved,” and “Depressed” were excluded from further analysis. For reports that presented LVEF values as a range, we assigned the midpoint of the range.

Furthermore, to extract Mitral Regurgitation (MR) score from the cardiology reports, we followed a two-step process. First to extract the numerical score related to the MR valve. Secondly, the text description relevant to MR description was extracted. We assigned MR severity scores based on specific terms identified in the reports. Descriptions indicating trace, mild, trace to mild were assigned a score of 0, reflecting low severity. Terms indicating no MR, such as “normal” or “physiologic,” also received a score of 0. Reports indicating “moderate” mitral regurgitation alone received a score of 1. A score of 2 was given to descriptions stating “at least moderate” or “moderate to severe” regurgitation, indicating significant MR. Severe regurgitation was assigned a score of 3. Extraction of LVEF and MR scores yielded a total of 610,788 reports associated with 283,147 unique subjects. Later, we additionally analyzed 112,130 TTE reports for 78,413 unique subjects.

The MIMIC-IV database

We used the MIMIC-IV ECG records22, MIMIC-IV notes23, and MIMIC-IV data24, for external validation. We chose the cMRI instead of the TTE imaging data, because MIMIC-IV notes do not include original TTE reports and the imaging events mentioned in the discharge summaries do not correspond to the actual date the TTE was performed.

We extracted the cMRI reports from the radiology reports of the MIMIC IV notes and identified 1710 cMRI reports. Of these, 108 reports with incomplete or missing information were excluded. Using the MIMIC-IV cMRI records list which includes the acquisition date and time of ECG, we obtained cMRI reports that occurred within one week of an ECG. This resulted in 737 matched cMRI and ECG reports, which were processed as described in the “Natural Language Processing of Cardiology Reports” section to extract the LVEF values.

ECG feature extraction

We also extracted annotations41 and time domain features42 from the ECG signals to assess their potential significance in estimating LVEF42. These features included the PR interval, adjusted Tpeak, QT interval, QTc interval, from 12 leads (extracted 4 features from each lead, totaling 48 features) and heart rate. The adjusted T-peak (Tpeak_Adj) was derived by subtracting the baseline voltage from the T-peak, while the corrected QT interval (QTc) was calculated using Bazett’s formula to account for heart rate variations.

Other tabular features included the mitral regurgitation (MR) score and, demographic data such as age and sex. In total, we extracted 52 tabular features, including the MR score from TTE reports, ECG and demographic features.

Cohort creation

To create the cohort, we excluded LVEF values outside the established pre-specified range of 5–90%. We then identified 215,984 ECGs that were paired with the TTE reports of the initial database query, and 23,967 ECGs that were paired with TTE reports from the subsequent database query. After extracting time domain features from the ECG records, and excluding records lacking time-domain features, the final cohort comprised 236,623 in ECGs total (212,984 ECGs from the initial database query and 23,639 ECGs from the subsequent query). To retrieve ECG data associated with these reports, we searched within a time window that extended seven days before and after the date of the TTE. These ECGs were then divided into training, validation, and testing datasets, ensuring that unique subjects were included in the validation and test groups. To augment the training dataset cohort and ensure significant diversity of the data, multiple records (ECG-EF pairs) for the same subject were included when pairs were at least 180 days apart. The formation of the cohort is described in Fig. S8.

Model architecture

Two-dimensional CNN were built to estimate LVEF using either the median ECG beat or the full ECG waveform, as the primary input signal42.

We assessed different CNN configurations by varying the model hyperparameters, the input signals, and using two distinct fusion approaches, namely early fusion and late fusion. In early fusion, all ECG leads were combined and fed directly into the CNN layer. Conversely, in late fusion, each ECG lead was separately processed through a CNN block to extract features, which were then concatenated before reaching the output layer. The CNN architectures underwent optimization using the KerasTuner, where parameters including filter length, kernel size, learning rate, optimizer, and activation functions were tuned across 50 trials for each configuration described above.

Multi-layer perceptron (MLP) architecture for tabular data

For hybrid (ECG and tabular data) architectures, we utilized the above mentioned CNN architecture to extract spatial features and the multi-layer perceptron (MLP) configuration for processing the tabular data. The MLP input layer accepted 52 time domain extracted features, including age, sex and MR score, and is followed by two dense layers. The outputs from these MLP layers are then concatenated with the output from the CNN layer. Finally, the combined output is fed into a fully connected layer that outputs the LVEF value. The hybrid model architecture was also optimized using the KerasTuner for CNN and MLP hyperparameters. Details of the optimization parameter search space are provided in the Table S1.

Probabilistic neural network (PNN) architecture

To estimate the model’s uncertainty in predicting the LVEF, we developed a probabilistic neural network (PNN) that generates a distribution for each predicted value, which is characterized by its mean and standard deviation. Unlike the traditional neural networks, which produce single point estimates, the PNN allows for the calculation of prediction intervals, indicating the likely range of the estimated LVEF values.

The probabilistic model simultaneously captures both the aleatoric (data-related) and epistemic (model-related), uncertainties. To estimate the epistemic uncertainty, we applied Monte Carlo dropout during inference by performing multiple stochastic forward passes with dropout enabled, introducing variability through random deactivation of neurons. By aggregating the mean predictions and standard deviation across these stochastic runs, we quantified the epistemic uncertainty. The model’s standard deviation of the output was used to quantify the aleatoric uncertainty, capturing the data noise and variability.

By incorporating probabilistic layers in the output and minimizing the negative log-likelihood function, the model adjusts its parameters to align the predicted LVEF distribution with the actual distribution. In the implemented PNN architecture we adopted the foundational CNN model architecture obtained in this study, employing either the ECG only or the hybrid models.

Uncertainty estimation using PNN architecture

To probe the performance of the PNN’s model’s predictions, we present prediction intervals for each LVEF range. Given the uneven distribution and slight skewness of LVEF values, extreme values are often overestimated. Therefore, it is more effective to present prediction intervals that indicate the range within which the LVEF values are likely to fall. In PNNs, the model does not only provide a point prediction (mean, μ(x)) but also estimates the associated uncertainty (standard deviation, σ(x)) for each input. Statistical analysis

To assess model performance, we employed the MAE and RMSE. The paired t-test was conducted to assess the performance difference between the personalized and generalized models. The performance metrics used in the classifier model are the following: AUC, accuracy, confusion matrix, negative predictive value, positive predictive value, sensitivity and specificity. The standard error for AUC was estimated using the Hanley and McNeil approach, which was then used to construct 95% confidence intervals.

We calculated the 95th percentile of the relative error distribution because it provides a robust estimate of the upper bound of the model’s performance error, excluding only the most extreme 5% of outliers. It reflects the maximum relative error observed in 95% of subjects at a given sample number, offering a more reliable assessment of the model's performance. Unlike the default box plot whisker, which extends to 1.5 times the interquartile range and is sensitive to distribution shape and skewness, the 95th percentile serves as a consistent, interpretable threshold that more effectively captures the typical error in the upper bound, making it better suited for evaluating performance across the test cohort.

All data analyses were performed using MATLAB and Python. The probabilistic model was developed and evaluated using the TensorFlow Probability library within the Keras/TensorFlow framework.
