Massive Right Atrium due to Severe Pulmonary Hypertension Mimicking Biatrial Enlargement on ECG
Authors: Dzhem Farandzha, Petar Gatzov, Vasil Velchev
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA massively enlarged right atrium due to severe pulmonary hypertension can mimic biatrial enlargement on ECG, despite a structurally normal left atrium, highlighting the role of mechanical displacement in electrocardiographic pattern alteration.

Case Image

We report the case of a 70‐year‐old male with severe idiopathic pulmonary hypertension (PH) and marked enlargement of the right atrium and right ventricle, presenting with an unusual electrocardiographic (ECG) finding.

The patient was referred to our cardiology department due to progressive dyspnea on exertion. Upon examination, the patient exhibited signs of right‐sided heart failure, including peripheral edema and jugular venous distention. The ECG demonstrated findings typical of right atrial enlargement with a prominent positive deflection of more than 1.5 mm in lead V1 (Figure 1A—red arrows). However, it also showed an atypical negative deflection exceeding 1 mm in amplitude and 40 ms in duration in the same lead (Figure 1A—blue arrows), a pattern typically associated with left atrial enlargement [1]. This peculiar ECG appearance raised initial concerns for biatrial enlargement, which were not supported by imaging. Diagnostic workup included echocardiography, which confirmed significant enlargement of the right atrium and ventricle (Figure 1B1,B2) along with elevated pulmonary artery pressures. Notably, left atrial size and function were within normal limits. Given the absence of left atrial enlargement, a novel hypothesis was proposed to explain the ECG findings: the severely enlarged right atrium might be mechanically displacing the heart or altering the electrical orientation, thus mimicking the ECG features of left atrial enlargement. This was visible on computed tomography (CT), which also did not show any signs of pulmonary embolism (Figure 1C1,C2). Right heart catheterization was performed (Figure 1D) and idiopathic pulmonary hypertension was asserted as a diagnosis after other causes for PH were excluded. The patient was started on combination therapy with ambrisentan and sildenafil.

(A) Twelve‐lead electrocardiography of a 70‐year‐old patient with severe idiopathic pulmonary hypertension. The red arrows point to p‐pulmonale in lead V1, while the blue arrows point to a negative deflection in V1, typical of left atrial enlargement. (B1) Apical 4‐chamber view on transthoracic echocardiography showing a massive right atrium, which is displacing the left atrium. (B2) Parasternal short‐axis view showing an enlarged right ventricle, displacing the left ventricle. (C1) Computed tomography showing a normal‐sized left atrium. (C2) Computed tomography with contrast media showing right atrial and right ventricular enlargement. (D) Pulmonary artery hemodynamics during right heart catheterization. LA, left atrium; LV, left ventricle; RA, right atrium; RV, right ventricle; PA, pulmonary artery.

This case highlights a rare ECG manifestation potentially attributable to mechanical factors related to the anatomical changes in severe pulmonary hypertension, namely left atrial displacement due to severe right atrial enlargement, mimicking the ECG features of biatrial enlargement. While a biphasic P wave in lead V1 is most commonly associated with left or biatrial enlargement, this pattern can also occur in isolated right atrial enlargement, provided the initial positive component is prominent and of significant amplitude [2].
