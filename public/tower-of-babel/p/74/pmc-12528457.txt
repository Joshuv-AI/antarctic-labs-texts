Evaluation of seven Theobroma cacao clones grown in Terai region of India for nutritional composition and bioactive compounds
Authors: Koyelika Deb, Shrilekha Das, P. S. Medda, A. K. Sit, S. Elain Apshara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Analysis of biochemical composition along with the yield and morphological characteristics of cocoa clones provide a stronger base for identification of clones for commercial cultivation. Seven cocoa clones released from ICAR-CPCRI Research Station, Vittal, India, were evaluated for their morphological and biochemical quality parameters after processing, when cultivated in Terai region of West Bengal, India, being one of the non-traditional areas for cocoa cultivation having favourable soil and climatic conditions. The seven cocoa clones included in this study were VTLCH 2, VTLC 19 A, VTLC 61, VTLC 5, VTLC 8, VTLC 11 and VTLC 66. Experiments were conducted following Completely Randomized Design (CRD) with 3 replications. All quality parameters studied varied significantly (p ≤ 0.05) among the cocoa clones. Fermentation of all the cocoa clones was completed in 7 days, as observed by cut test and fermentation index. The clone VTLC 61 had higher pH, lowest free fatty acid and titratable acetic acid content, highest carbohydrate content, whereas highest fat content was observed in VTLC 66. But both the clones VTLC 61 and VTLC 66 showed certain amount of germinated and slaty beans after 7 days of fermentation. The clone VTLC 5 had low free fatty acid and titratable acetic acid content, highest protein content, good fat and carbohydrate content, highest anthocyanin and polyphenol content and antioxidant capacity. Considering all the quality parameters and the yield performance evaluated earlier, the clone VTLC 5 was identified as potential clone for commercial cultivation and processing in the Terai region of West Bengal in India.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-02406-z.

Introduction

Plantations of cocoa are usually situated within 20° North and South of the Equator1. Cocoa is a significant cash crop in the global economy, primarily used in chocolate production, generating substantial income for many tropical countries, particularly in West Africa. Ivory Coast, Ghana, Nigeria and Cameroon which are the four West African countries, supply about 70% of the world’s cocoa beans. In India, although production is lower, cocoa is still a vital commercial crop, contributing to the domestic confectionery industry and offering income opportunities for farmers. India earns foreign exchange worth 154.54 million US$ through exports of cocoa bean and its products during 2022-232. The primary growing regions of cocoa in India are Kerala, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu. The estimated area under cocoa production during 2020–2021 was 1,03,376 ha with total production of 27,072 MT. Andhra Pradesh ranks first with 39,714 ha under cocoa and a production of 10,903 MT3.

The genotype and origin of the cocoa tree play a major role in the quality of beans4. In India, the popular clones of cocoa, released by Kerala Agricultural University (KAU) are CCRP-1, CCRP-2, CCRP-3, CCRP-4, CCRP-5, CCRP-6 and CCRP-7 and 3 hybrids CCRP-8, CCRP-9 and CCRP-10. ICAR-Central Plantation Crops Research Institute (CPCRI) Regional Research Station, Vittal, Karnataka has released one clone VTLCC-1 and 4 hybrids VTLCH-1, VTLCH-2, VTLCH-3 and VTLCH-4. There were significant variations among the clones in terms of yield, morphological characteristics like pod size, pod weight, etc. as well as in fat content and shelling percentage. While cultivated in the state of Assam in the North-Eastern region of India, VTLC 19 was reported to be the best performing variety in terms of higher fresh bean weight, greater dry bean weight per pod along with highest dry bean yield when they were 8 years old5,6. Quality of cocoa beans is largely dependent on the origin, location of plants grown as well as climatic parameters (temperature, rainfall pattern, sunshine period, soil condition and so on) of the location7. Different research studies have revealed that cocoa beans having different origin show distinct flavour characteristics with variation in different biochemical parameters4.

Cocoa bean processing includes collecting cocoa pods, shattering the collected pods, fermenting the wet mass of beans, drying to a moisture level of 6 to 8%, and storing the dried beans till use8. Among these steps, fermentation is the most important as it aids the degradation of the pulp surrounding the cocoa beans so that the beans can be easily dried. This process induces biochemical transformation within the beans that leads to formation of colour, aroma and flavour precursors9, subsequently leading to the quality improvement of dried cocoa beans which has the main economic importance. Fresh cocoa seeds are rich in fat (30–32%), sugars (10–15%) and also contains about 15% polyphenols (59% proanthocyandins, 37% catechins or flavan-3-oles, 1–2% theobromine, 1% acids, 1% caffeine and 4% anthocyanins)10,11. Cocoa genotype influences the proximate composition of beans including protein, carbohydrate content and polyphenol content12. These components are degraded during fermentation and drying to form flavor precursors. Concentration of phytochemicals in cocoa beans, including monomeric (epicatechin and catechin) and oligomeric (procyanidin) flavanols make cocoa consumption beneficial for cardiovascular diseases and diabetes. They also have role in preventing cancer by inhibiting tumour growth and angiogenesis13.

Traditionally, bean quality is assessed by visual inspection for colour and appearance. The industrial acceptance regarding cocoa quality is mainly dependent on fat and moisture content as they directly influence the flavour and texture of the products. But other quality parameters like pH/acidity, sugar content, free fatty acid, titratable acidity, amount of bioactive compounds present as well as protein and carbohydrate content also influence the flavour and quality till the very end14. Different biochemical components of beans contribute to various biological activities and physiological effects. Analyzing these components is crucial for effective utilization of cocoa. Study of the composition and their impact in cocoa quality should also account for the variability between different cocoa clones15.

Cocoa is in high demand now-a-days for its contribution in different health factors. Currently domestic production in India is not sufficient to meet the industrial demand. Cocoa production in India may get a boost by introducing and popularizing its cultivation to non-traditional areas where arecanut and coconut-based cropping system has been commercially adopted. In Terai region of West Bengal, India, arecanut is an important plantation crop. Terai region is one of the non-traditional areas for cocoa cultivation having favourable soil and climatic conditions16. Some cocoa clones released from ICAR-CPCRI Research Station, Vittal, Karnataka are being grown in this region since 2015. However, their performance in this region in terms of quality and morphological parameters are still unknown. The Indian clones, released from KAU and ICAR-CPCRI, are mainly evaluated for their yield, morphological characters, shelling percentage and fat content5,6,17,18. Detailed evaluation of biochemical parameters of the cocoa clones would help in identification of clones with better flavour and quality characteristics for profitable production in Terai region. This study aims to evaluate a number of biochemical parameters of seven cocoa clones cultivated in Terai region of West Bengal, India, so as to provide a platform for selection based on quality along with yield for commercial cultivation.

Materials and methods

Collection of cocoa pods

This study is collaborative research of UBKV, West Bengal and ICAR-CPCRI. Matured cocoa pods of seven different clones were harvested and collected from ICAR-CPCRI, Research Centre, Mohitnagar, Jalpaiguri with coordinates of 26.5291°N latitude and 88.6705°E longitude and transported to Uttar Banga Krishi Viswavidyalaya (UBKV), Pundibari, West Bengal on the same day. The cocoa clones included in this study were VTLCH 2, VTLC 5, VTLC 8, VTLC 11, VTLC 19 A, VTLC 61 and VTLC 66. The cocoa pods were stored for 3 days on clean floor in a shady area, spread in a single layer before further processing. Experiments were conducted following Completely Randomized Design (CRD) with 7 treatments (clones) and 3 replications. Since, the experiment was conducted under controlled laboratory condition, CRD was used.

Processing of cocoa

The pods were broken manually and entire content of the pod, i.e. the mucilage along with beans were separated by hand from the husk and taken in a bamboo basket lined with banana leaves. Dimensions of the baskets were, diameter at surface, 20 cm and depth, 20 cm. Each basket could hold approximately 1 kg fresh bean with pulp and the beans were again covered with banana leaves and gunny bags. These baskets were kept for fermentation for 7 days. For maintaining uniform temperature during fermentation, the fermenting cocoa beans in the baskets were kept in an oven. The temperature schedule maintained during fermentation was 40℃ for the first two days, 42℃ for the next 3 days and again 40℃ for the last two days. Fermentation of cocoa beans at controlled temperature for uniformity among different treatments was also performed by Romanens et al.19. Periodical turning over of beans was performed at 2 days interval. After determining the optimum/proper fermentation of beans by visual inspection in cut test, beans were fermented for 7 days. The beans were then taken out from the basket, washed thoroughly and dried in a tray dryer at 75℃ until the shell could be removed from the nib when gently rubbed by hand. This drying took 6 h. Dried beans were cooled and stored in air tight zipped pouches until further use.

Cut test

Cut test is the most basic and extensively used method for determining the quality of a random sample of beans from a batch by visual inspection of the cut beans11. Cut test was performed by visual inspection20 from 5 th day of fermentation at 24 h interval.

Analysis of dried cocoa beans

Dried cocoa beans of seven different clones were analysed for its proximate composition and biochemical parameters like pH, anthocyanin content, free fatty acid and titratable acetic acid, total polyphenol content, antioxidant capacity, total sugar and reducing sugar content. To assess the extent of fermentation, Fermentation Index (FI) was evaluated.

HCl in a ratio of 97:3) and storing at refrigerated temperature for 20 h before filtering. Absorbance of the filtrate was read in a UV-VIS spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Model no: UV1800ENG240 V, SOFT) at 460 nm and 530 nm with methanolic HCl as blank. Determination of pH

pH of dried cocoa beans was measured as per the method described by Pontillon and Cros22.

Determination of titratable acetic acid and free fatty acid

Titratable acetic acid and free fatty acid content was evaluated by AOAC method23 titrating against 0.5 M ethanolic KOH and was expressed as molar equivalent of KOH per 1 g of cocoa nib.

Analysis of proximate composition

Protein, ash and moisture content of dried cocoa nibs were determined by following the procedures in AOAC23 methods 970.22, 972.15 and 931.04, respectively. Fat content was measured by using Soxhlet apparatus as per the method described by Torres-Moreno et al.24 and carbohydrate content by differentiation method 25.

It was kept in dark condition overnight in room temperature. After that, this mixture was filtered using filter paper (Whatman No. 4) and again kept for 90 min in dark condition at room temperature. The absorbance of the extract at 535 nm was recorded by using a UV-VIS spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Model no: UV1800ENG240 V, SOFT) using methanolic HCl as blank. Determination of total polyphenol content

Total polyphenol content was measured by Folin-Ciocalteu’s assay as per method described by Nsor-Atindana et al.27. Sample extract was prepared by mixing 1 g ground cocoa nib and 10 ml 80% ethanol, centrifuging, dissolving the supernatant in 5 ml distilled water. 100 µl of this sample aliquot was dissolved with 200 µl distilled water. Then 0.5 ml of 50% Folin-Ciocalteu’s Reagent was added and exactly after 3 min, 2 ml of 20% Na2CO3 was also added, followed by incubating in a hot water bath for exactly 1 min. The absorbance was taken at 650 nm using a UV-VIS spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Model no: UV1800ENG240 V, SOFT) against distilled water as blank. The total polyphenol content in the test samples was calculated on the basis of the calibration curve, prepared using gallic acid, and expressed in gallic acid equivalent per 100 g of product.

Determination of antioxidant activity

Antioxidant activity was determined by following DPPH (Di-Phenyl Picryl Hydrazyl) radical scavenging activity28. 5 g ground cocoa nib with 25 ml of methanol was incubated in shaker continuously for 72 h at 100 rpm. The filtrate was evaporated and the concentrate was again diluted with suitable amount of methanol to prepare the stock solution of 10,000 ppm. From this stock solution, different concentrated solution of 10 ppm, 25 ppm, 50 ppm, 75 ppm, 100 ppm and 125 ppm were made by diluting with methanol.

A DPPH 0.1 mM working solution was prepared and 0.5 ml of the different concentrated solution and 2.5 ml of DPPH working solution were taken and kept for 30 min in dark condition. The absorbance was recorded at 517 nm in a UV-VIS spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Model no: UV1800ENG240 V, SOFT) using methanol as blank.

Statistical analysis

The data recorded on various quality parameters were analyzed by using R-4.3.1 software30. The statistical significance as well as standard error and critical difference (CD) were calculated at 5% level of significance (p ≤ 0.05) to find out the superiority of one variety over the other.

Results and discussion

Cut test and fermentation index

Cut test

Figure 1(a) and (b) shows the pictorial view of the cut beans obtained on 5 th and 7 th day of fermentation, respectively. It was observed that majority of the beans belonged to pale purple and purplish brown category on 5 th day of fermentation. This indicated partial fermentation of the cocoa beans and thus fermentation process was continued. Few germinated beans were observed at 5 th day of fermentation during cut test. During fermentation, glycosidase hydrolyses anthocyanins (responsible for the purple colour) to anthocyanidins and sugars (galactose and arabinose)31 and the activity of polyphenol oxidase which led to the changes in anthocyanin and oxidation of polyphenols contribute to the bean’s brown pigment formation12,20. The result of cut test on the 7 th day of fermentation (Table 1) shows change in colour of beans with reduction in the percentage of purple beans, suggesting degradation of polyphenols and anthocyanins20. Anthocyanins usually disappear rapidly during the final stage of fermentation32. On 7 th day of fermentation, all the clones had more than 60% brown beans, which indicated adequate fermentation. Similar result was reported by Afoakwa et al.33, from their study, where more than 60% fully brown bean was obtained at 7 th day of fermentation. There was no deep purple bean on the 7 th day of fermentation in any of the cocoa clones. Highest percentage of brown bean was found in both VTLC 5 and VTLC 61 with a value of 60.67%. But VTLC 61 showed 6.67% of germinated bean which is not considered as good character. On an average, the percentage of purplish brown and slaty beans was ranged from 10 to 35% and 5–20%, respectively among all the clones. As per the research done by Daviron34, factors attributing to slaty beans could be inadequate fermentation days, insufficient fermentation heap size, lack of fermentation and lack of turning during fermentation.

Fermentation index

Fermentation index (FI) is one of the most important determinants of fermentative quality of cocoa bean which ultimately influences the flavour potential35. It is a colour index which measure the brownness of cocoa nibs to ascertain the degree of fermentation36. The characteristic purple colour of unfermented cocoa beans is mainly due to anthocyanin (a polyphenol compound)37, are hydrolysed to anthocyanidins during fermentation. Anthocyanidins and simple catechins are then polymerize to form complex tannins.

The fermentation index of different clones of cocoa is demonstrated in the Fig. 2. Majority of the cocoa clones show the fermentation index more than 1. Highest fermentation index was observed in VTLC 5 with a value of 1.73. The FI value of 0.96 in VTLC 61 may have been the result of presence of germinated beans, as observed from the cut test performed (Table 1). Under fermentation is indicated by FI value less than one and well fermented beans are those with FI values of one and above[21].

Fermentation index can be positively correlated with pre-fermentation pod storage. Due to water evaporation and sucrose inversion, pod storage decreases the pulp volume per seed while increasing micro-aeration in both the pulp and the fermenting mass. This might potentially increase the FI of the cocoa nibs by boosting the activity of polyphenol oxidase, which would oxidize the polyphenol compounds and generate brown colour21.

Evaluation of pH, titratable acetic acid and free fatty acid content

pH

The pH level of dried cocoa nibs of different cocoa clones is presented in the Table 2. The pH of the dried cocoa beans was found to be in the range of 5.66 to 6.01, which is slightly higher than normal (5.3–5.8)38, with the highest in VTLCH 2 and lowest in VTLC 66. The pH level among the different clones was non-significant at p ≤ 0.05. The pH level of cocoa is crucial for its health advantages since a lower pH (more acidic) in natural cocoa implies a higher concentration of beneficial flavanols, a kind of antioxidant38. The final chocolate product’s flavour and aroma are influenced by the cocoa beans’ pH. It is well known that cocoa beans with a pH of 5.0 to 5.5 have a greater potential for scent than beans with a pH of 4.0 to 4.5. Very low pH in cocoa beans results in acid flavour with reduced quality. The pH of cocoa beans is very much vital during fermentation, as it influences the beneficial microorganisms’ growth and the development of desirable flavor compounds39. All the organic acids like acetic, lactic, gluconic, oxalic, malic and propionic acid produced by microbial activity in cocoa pulp layer and their diffusion results in acidity in dried cocoa beans40.

Titratable acetic acid content

There was a significant variation (p ≤ 0.05) in the context of titratable acetic acid content among the cocoa clones (Table 2). The range was in between 2.81 and 5.61 mg KOH/g. The lowest acidity level was found in VTLC 61 (2.81 mg KOH/g) which was statistically at par with VTLC 5 (2.99 mg KOH/g) and VTLC 19 A (3.27 mg KOH/g); whereas the highest level of acidity was observed in VTLC 66 (5.61 mg KOH/g). The results were in accordance with the study done by Lasisi8. The lower the acidity level, higher will be the quality of fermented as well as dried cocoa beans41.

The overall quality and attributes of the finished chocolate product are greatly influenced by the amount of titratable acetic acid present in dried cocoa beans as it creates an impact on cocoa bean flavour development, processing and preservation. The acetic acid content is especially significant as it indicates the degree of fermentation. Determining the amount of acetic acid in cocoa beans can aid in evaluating the quality of fermentation, which is essential for creating premium chocolate with appealing flavour profiles39. Microorganisms in the pulp breakdown the sugar into alcohol and organic acids (mainly acetic acid) which then diffuse into beans and subsequently induce important biochemical reactions leading to well fermented cocoa beans. However, high acid production in the pulp may result in production of acidic beans.

Free fatty acid content

Cocoa beans’ nutritional value is mostly dictated by their fatty contents, but their diuretic impact, central nervous system stimulation and influence on cardiovascular activity are caused by their xanthine alkaloids concentration42,43. Free Fatty Acids (FFA) are carboxylic acids released from triglycerides due to lipase activity44. In this experiment, the free fatty acid level in cocoa beans ranged from 3.74 to 14.58 mg KOH/g, varying significantly among the cocoa clones (Table 2). The lowest free fatty acid level was noted in VTLC 61 having 3.74 mg KOH/g, which was statistically at par with VTLC 5 (4.21 mg KOH/g) and VTLC 19 A (4.49 mg KOH/g), indicating to enhanced quality of cocoa beans after drying. Increased FFA level lowers the hardness of cocoa butter and lowers the quality of fermented cocoa beans45; and have to be viewed as a commercial value reducing factor of raw cocoa both for producers and chocolate manufacturers. Therefore, the directive 73/241/EEC sets the maximum FFA content to 1.75% oleic acid equivalent in cocoa butter for quality related reasons. When converted to % oleic acid, free fatty acid content of the clones VTLC 19 A, VTLC 61 and VTLC 5 reached the standard level of < 1.75% as suggested by requirement of European Union46.

Proximate analysis

The proximate composition of cocoa nibs i.e. protein, fat, carbohydrate as well as ash content plays a major role in influencing quality of cocoa beans. This data is presented in Table 3. The fat, protein, ash and carbohydrate content in the cocoa clones ranged from 38.36 to 47.34%, 1.39–1.66%, 3.38–4.66% and 39.21–47.86%, respectively. The findings are similar with the report of Dewandari et al.47 in respect of fat, ash and carbohydrate content. The highest protein (1.66%) and ash content (4.66%) were found in VTLC 5. On the other hand, highest fat content was observed in VTLC 66 (47.34%) followed by VTLC 19 A (45.56%). VTLC 61 had the highest percentage of carbohydrate content (47.86%), followed by VTLC 5. Higher amount of fat, protein and carbohydrate content in cocoa nibs generally denotes higher quality of beans as the lipid fraction, amino acids and simple sugars act as precursors for aroma and flavour compounds. The chocolate manufacturing and confectionery industries also value higher amount of fat in cocoa beans as it leads to better taste and mouthful of their product. Apshara48 reported the fat content of VTLC series clones to be in the range of 42.5 to 53%, when cultivated in Karnataka, India. The fat and protein content were found to be slightly lower in the VTLC clones in this study. This can be attributed to different factors including location of cultivation, variation in climatic parameters (such as temperature, soil parameters, relative humidity and rainfall pattern) from traditional zone of cultivation, degree of ripeness of pods during ripening, pre-fermentation storage period, the fermentation technique followed, percentage of slaty, mouldy and germinated beans during fermentation etc.

Moisture content is one of the components of proximate analysis. With the same fermentation and drying parameters, the moisture content among the clones differed significantly. The highest moisture content was found in VTLC 8 (9.5%) which was statistically at par with VTLC 11 (9.43%), followed by VTLC 66, VTLC 61 and VTLC 5. VTLCH 2 showed the lowest moisture percentage of 5.26%. The moisture content should be ≤ 8%, as the more amount moisture leads to the development of mould results in off flavour14. But too little moisture i.e. less than 5.5% leads to more amount of brittle beans. With the same drying temperature and duration for all the clones, variation in moisture content of the dried beans may be attributed to different bean size, thickness of shell and differences in shell and nib percentage.

Reducing sugar and total sugar content

Table 4 shows the variation in reducing sugar and total sugar content in seven cocoa clones. Reducing sugar content was found to be in the range of 1.14 to 5.57% in dried cocoa beans. The result is in agreement with research done by Afoakwa et al.49.

The reducing sugar, especially glucose and fructose, are transformed during drying by Maillard or caramelization reactions to Amadori compounds, which then go through further transformations in subsequent reactions into colour and flavour development. This results in reduction in sugar content after drying50,51. In different cocoa clones, the sugar fractions might have participated in the reaction in varying degree. Besides, amino acid content in the beans also influences the rate of Maillard reaction52. This could be the reason for the wide range of reducing sugar content in dried beans. Among the cocoa clones, maximum reducing sugar content was found in VTLC 8 (5.57%) followed by VTLC 5 with a value of 3.51%.

Anthocyanin content

All the cocoa clones varied significantly in their anthocyanin content with a range of 27.89 (VTLC 11) to 76.08 mg/100 g (VTLC 5) (Table 5). Higher anthocyanin content indicated higher polyphenol content with enhanced antioxidant capacity; and thus, greater health benefits. The results are in accordance with the research findings of Brito et al.54 and Melo et al.26. Cyanidin-3-a-L-arabinoside and cyanidin-3-b-D-galactoside, the major anthocyanins in cacao beans, gradually reduce over fermentation period55, as they are polymerised with simple catechins to form complex tannins. This also contributes to the colour change of the cocoa nibs from purple to brown, as observed by cut test.

Total polyphenol content

For the seven cocoa clones studied, the total polyphenol content in cocoa nibs of different clones ranged between 0.39 and 1.61 g GAE/100 g (Table 5), which is in consonance with the research findings of Oracz and Nebesny56. The presence of polyphenols in chocolate and cocoa products has important economic significance for their contribution to the health benefits for consumers12. For instance, the bioactive compounds of cocoa components have pharmacological effects on inflammatory processes57,58.

Chocolate’s flavour and colour have long been linked with the polyphenols, which amounts to 12–18% of the total weight of beans. The overall quality of fermented cocoa beans is influenced by complex interaction between polyphenols to form tannins and their interaction with protein for chocolate production32. A higher polyphenol content promotes the Maillard reaction, which with the aid of polyphenol oxidase yields chocolate colour59. Changes in the composition of polyphenol during fermentation were the cause of cocoa bean’s decreased purple hue and increased brown colour intensity, as shown by the cut test. Additionally, polyphenols contribute to the antioxidant activity and effective impact against reactive oxygen species (ROS), because of hydroxyl group attached to the structure60. It means that a decrease in antioxidant activity of the product may be associated with lower polyphenol content.

Antioxidant capacity

The antioxidant content of dried cocoa beans is one of the most important elements influencing the flavour, health advantages, and general quality of cocoa products. Cocoa beans are full of bioactive substances that might affect human health, especially when processed and consumed in different forms (like chocolate). Due to its potential to improve human health and lessen the negative effects of oxidative stress, cocoa beans’ antioxidant qualities—which are mostly derived from flavonoids and polyphenols—have attracted a lot of interest. Cocoa has been recognized to have greater phenolic and flavonoid content, as well as total antioxidant capabilities, than other phytochemical-rich dietary regimens61.

IC50 value, presented in Table 5, indicates the ability to inhibit the oxidation process by 50% of a sample concentration. This IC50 value indicates the dried cocoa bean’s ability to suppress free radicals through antioxidant activity. A smaller IC50 value denotes increased antioxidant activity28. The IC50 value for different cocoa clones in this study ranged between 75.09 and 148.08. The lowest IC50 value i.e., highest antioxidant capacity was noted in VTLC 5 as the anthocyanin content of this variety (76.08 mg/100 g) as well as the total polyphenol content (1.61 g GAE/100 g) were also higher.

A wide variety of antioxidants, especially flavonoids, a subclass of polyphenolic compounds, are abundant in cocoa beans. Flavanols, particularly epicatechin and catechin, are the most prevalent flavonoids in cocoa and have been thoroughly investigated for their potential as antioxidants. These substances neutralize dangerous reactive oxygen species (ROS) that can harm cells by acting as strong free radical scavengers. The antioxidant properties of cocoa components are reported to alter insulin resistance and lower the incidence of diabetes62.

Dried VTLC 5 beans showed reduced titrable acidity and free fatty acid content, the greatest protein content, and a fair quantity of fat and carbohydrate content. The highest anthocyanin and polyphenol content, as well as antioxidant capacity, were found in VTLC 5, indicating high quality and increased health benefits. Among all the cocoa clones investigated in the Terai area of India, VTLC 5 demonstrated greater consistency in chemical quality indicators. After reviewing all the parameters in terms of morphology, nutritional composition as well as bioactive compounds, the clone VTLC 5 was found suitable for commercial exploitation in Terai region of West Bengal, India. This clone maybe recommended for commercial cultivation and processing in the Terai region of West Bengal in India.

Conclusion

The morphological characteristics, composition of nutritional and bioactive compounds of seven cocoa clones grown in the non-traditional cocoa growing area of Terai region in India was thoroughly evaluated in this study, so as to find the cocoa clone(s) with suitable traits to promote commercial cultivation in this region. The results of cut test and fermentation index indicated that all the clones were well fermented after 7 days of fermentation. VTLC 61 had some germinated beans and the amount of slaty beans was high in VTLC 66, VTLC 8 and VTLC 19 A. Dried beans of VTLC 5 had lower titratable acidity and free fatty acid content, highest protein content, good amount of fat and carbohydrate content. The anthocyanin and polyphenol content along with antioxidant capacity were also maximum in VTLC 5 indicating good quality with greater health benefits. Though, beans of VTLC 66 and VTLC 61 had the highest amount of fat and carbohydrate, high percentage of germinated and slaty beans rendered them unsuitable for production of good quality product. Among all the cocoa clones studied from the Terai region, West Bengal, India, VTLC 5 showed better consistency in biochemical quality parameters. Using these biochemical parameters as selection criteria, in addition to traditional approach, which is based on fat and moisture content and appearance, will result in improved product quality, with greater health benefits.
