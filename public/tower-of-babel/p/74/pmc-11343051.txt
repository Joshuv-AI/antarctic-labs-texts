Dietary supplementation of Cannabis sativa residues in broiler chickens affects performance, carcass characteristics, intestinal morphology, blood biochemistry profile and oxidative stability
Authors: Yusup Sopian, Apinya Sartsook, Chaiwat Arjin, Chompunut Lumsangkul, Korawan Sringarm, Panneepa Sivapirunthep, Chanporn Chaosap
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Public interest in the cannabis plant has increased after its legalization in many countries. Cannabis sativa residues (CR) are a part of the plant waste in the cannabis industry. The CR contain medicinal properties that could be used as a feed additive in poultry production. The trial was conducted to investigate the effects of CR on growth performance, carcass characteristics, intestinal morphology, and blood biochemistry profile of broiler chickens. In a completely randomized design, 256 one-day-old male Ross 308 broilers were randomly allocated to 4 treatments with 8 replicates and 8 birds per replicate. These 4 dietary treatments included a basal diet with 0, 0.5, 1 and 2% CR for 40 d. The results showed that 2% CR supplementation reduced feed intake (FI) in the starter phase (d 3–23, P < 0.05). The birds in the CR groups had lower FI in the finishing phase (d 24–40, P < 0.01) and the whole raising period (d 3–40, P < 0.01) than the control. However, the body weight and carcass yield were not different (P > 0.05). In addition, the CR diet had no adverse effects on the blood biochemistry profile, including total cholesterol, triglycerides, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, total protein, globulin, albumin, and direct bilirubin (P > 0.05). In addition, total bilirubin and malondialdehyde were better in the plasma of CR-supplemented birds than in the control groups (P < 0.05). The observations on intestinal morphology showed that CR supplementation improved the ratio between villus height and crypt depth in the ileum (P < 0.05). In conclusion, CR supplementation can improve intestinal morphology and oxidative stability of broiler chickens. This suggests that CR could potentially be used as an alternative feed additive in broiler production.

INTRODUCTION

The global demand for meat has increased due to the growing human population. Poultry products, especially broilers, account for the largest share of meat consumption and are associated with lower prices and healthier products (OECD/FAO., 2021). However, intensive genetic selection for fast-growing chickens and intensive farming practices make broilers susceptible to oxidative stress, which affects growth performance and meat quality (Estévez, 2015). Therefore, the poultry industry has been using antibiotic growth promoters for several decades to increase production (Mehdi et al., 2018), as well as synthetic antioxidants such as butylated hydroxyanisole and butylated hydroxytoluene to prevent lipid oxidation in feed and meat products (Estévez, 2015). Adeyemi et al (2021) reported that a combination of antibiotics and butylated hydroxyanisole in feed can protect broilers from oxidative stress and improve broiler performance and meat quality. However, the excessive recommendations for the use of antibiotics could promote bacterial resistance in treated animals and antibiotic residues in animal products (Mehdi et al., 2018). In addition, the use of synthetic antioxidants has shown carcinogenic and toxicological effects in animal models, including lung and liver cancer (Wang et al., 2021). The growing concern about these adverse effects has led researchers to find an alternative to synthetic antioxidants and antibiotics in broiler production (Sadeghi et al., 2016; Adeyemi et al., 2021).

Currently, herbs and medicinal plants are recommended as safe and environmentally friendly alternatives to synthetic antioxidants and antibiotics in animal production (Norkeaw et al., 2022; Dosu et al., 2023; Farag et al., 2024). These phytogenic additives contain secondary metabolites that are able to exert antioxidant properties through various mechanisms, including reducing lipoperoxidation and scavenging free radicals (Liu et al., 2023). Numerous studies have shown that medicinal plants provide promising results in broiler production. Almuhayawi et al. (2023) showed that olive leaf powder can improve the growth performance, carcass characteristics and antioxidant activity of broilers. In addition, Zhao et al. (2024) reported that supplementation with the aerial parts of Angelica sinensis can improve broiler performance, intestinal health, and antimicrobial resistance.

Cannabis sativa L. is an annual plant of the Cannabaceae family (Mirzamohammad et al., 2021). Traditionally, C. sativa has been used in many cultures worldwide as a medicinal plant to treat various illnesses. However, the use of C. sativa began to decline when it was classified as an illegal substance in the early 20th century due to its psychoactive compound (Balant et al., 2021). Since the legalization of cannabis in several countries, interest in cannabis products for both medicinal and recreational use has been growing (Zinboonyahgoon et al., 2021). In 2019, Thailand became the first and only country in Southeast Asia to legalize cannabis use for medical and research purposes (Government of Thailand, 2019). Initially, 8 governmental institutions were licensed by the Thai Food and Drug Administration as cannabis cultivators, producers and processors to produce medical-grade cannabis products (Somwaiya and Saardphak, 2019). Thereafter, the number of producers meeting the requirement standard to obtain a license from the government increased rapidly (Kalayasiri and Boonthae, 2023). Typically, the main cannabis products are extracted from the inflorescences of C. sativa (Valizadehderakhshan et al., 2021), resulting in cannabis residues such as leaves, stems, branches and roots. Therefore, to achieve zero-waste production in the C. sativa industry, it is crucial to identify potential applications for these residues. In addition, previous studies have reported that cannabis residues contain several medicinal compounds such as cannabidiol (CBD), tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) and other phytochemicals such as terpenes and flavonoids (Lewis et al., 2017; Fordjour et al., 2023). Jin et al. (2020) reported that cannabis leaves contain CBD (1.04%), THC (0.57–0.93%), flavonoids (0.34–0.44%), and terpenoids (0.13–0.28%), while stem bark and roots contain sterols (0.06–0.09%) and triterpenoids (0.05–0.24%). These bioactive compounds can have a positive effect on animal health.

A recent study has shown that C. sativa biomass can improve the antioxidant capacity of finishing lambs (Parker et al., 2022) and the plasma immune indices of Holstein dairy cows (Wang et al., 2023). The antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects of C. sativa may be attributed to its bioactive phytochemical compounds, including polyphenols and other secondary metabolites (Isahq et al., 2015; Nakkliang et al., 2022). Other studies showed that C. sativa has no adverse effects on productive performance and carcass characteristics in sheep (Krebs et al., 2021) and goats (Khamhan et al., 2023). Interestingly, C. sativa has been reported to possess potent anti-inflammatory (Atalay et al., 2020; Fordjour et al., 2023), antioxidant (Mirzamohammad et al., 2021), antimicrobial (Muscarà et al., 2021), antifungal (Isahq et al., 2015) and neuroprotective agents (Marsh and Smid, 2021). In addition, studies with rats have shown indications of a therapeutic effect of C. sativa (Aziz et al., 2019), appetite-stimulating properties (Mazidi et al., 2014) and positive changes in the gut microbiota (Zandani et al., 2022). However, to our knowledge, there are no studies that have investigated the potential use of C. sativa residues (CR) in poultry feed. Due to the expected high demand in the cannabis industry, CR are expected to increase in the coming years. Therefore, CR should be thoroughly investigated before they are approved as feed additives in Thailand. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to investigate the effects of CR on growth performance, carcass characteristics, intestinal morphology, and blood biochemistry profile of broiler chickens.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The trial was conducted at the Agricultural Innovation, Demonstration and Training Center of the Faculty of Agriculture, Chiang Mai University, Chiang Mai, Thailand. The experimental procedures in this trial were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Faculty of Agriculture, Chiang Mai University under protocol number AGIACUC015/2665.

Cannabis Residues Collection and Phytochemical Analysis

CR in air-dried form were purchased from the Pethlanna joint venture in Lampang Province, Thailand. The CR consisted of leaves and stalks of the C. sativa plant (Figure 1). The CR was oven dried at 60°C for 4 h until the sample crumbled easily and then ground into powder form using a hammer mill (Retsch SM100) with a 2-mm sieve. The CR powder was stored at room temperature in airtight bags until further use. Proximate analysis of CR was performed according to the method described in the guidelines of the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC, 2005).Figure 1Cannabis plant parts and usage: Cannabis flowers and roots are used for medicinal purposes. The total phenolic content was determined according to the method described by Norkeaw et al. (2022). In brief, 12.5 µL of the aqueous CR solution was mixed with 50 µL of distilled water and 12.5 µL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. After a 6-minute reaction time, 125 µL of 10% sodium carbonate (w/v) and 100 µL of distilled water were added to the mixture. The solution was then incubated at room temperature for 1.5 hours. The absorbance was measured at 750 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices SpectraMax M3, CA). A calibration curve was generated using gallic acid at various concentrations as a standard. The total phenolic content was expressed as millimolar gallic acid equivalents per gram (mM GAE/g) of dried material.

Antioxidant activities were measured with three different assays using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices SpectraMax M3, CA) according to Norkeaw et al. (2022) and Wisetkomolmat et al. (2023). First, the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay was performed according to Norkeaw et al. (2022). In brief, 50 µL of the extract solution in ethanol was mixed with 200 µL of a 0.6 mM DPPH solution in a 96-well plate. The mixture was incubated for 30 minutes at 25°C in the dark. A control consisting of 200 µL of a 0.6 mM DPPH solution without extract and 50 µL of ethanol was added. After incubation, the absorbance was measured at 517 nm. DPPH values were expressed as millimolar Trolox equivalents per gram of dried material. Second, the 2,2ʹ-azinobis-3-ethyl-benzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid (ABTS) assay was performed as described by Norkeaw et al. (2022). A stock solution was prepared by combining equal parts of 7.4 mM ABTS solution and 2.6 mM potassium persulfate solution, which reacted for 12 hours at 25°C in the dark. The stock solution was then diluted with methanol until an absorbance of 0.7 ± 0.02 units at 734 nm was achieved. In a 96-well plate, 10 µL of the methanol-based extract solution was mixed with 190 µL of the ABTS working solution and incubated for 10 minutes at 25°C in the dark. Controls consisted of 190 µL of ABTS working solution without extract and 10 µL of methanol. Absorbance was measured at 734 nm and ABTS values were expressed as millimolar Trolox equivalents per gram (mM TE/g) of dried material. Third, the ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay was performed according to Wisetkomolmat et al. (2023). A FRAP working solution was prepared by mixing acetate buffer (0.3 mol/L, pH 3.6), ferric tripyridyltrinazine solution (10 mmol/L in 0.04 mol/L HCl) and FeCl3 solution (20 mmol/L) in a ratio of 10:1:1. Subsequently, 2.85 mL of the FRAP working solution was mixed with 150 µL of the diluted sample. The mixture was incubated for 4 min and the absorbance was measured at 593 nm. FRAP values were expressed as millimolar equivalents of ferric oxide per gram (mM Fe2+/g) of dried material, based on the absorbance change correlating with increasing Fe2+ concentration in the sample.

The analysis of CBD and THC was performed by HPLC/UV (Shimadzu HPLC LC-20A, Japan). A total of 1 g of CR powder was mixed with 100 mL of 80% methanol. The samples were then shaken for 1 h at 60°C and 120 to 150 rpm in a shaking incubator and filtered using filter paper No. 1 (Whatman). The volume was then made up to 100 mL with 80 ethanol and the solution filtered through a 0.45 µm filter before injecting 5 µL per sample into the HPLC. A Raptor ARC-18 150 mm x 4.6 mm ID x 2.7 µm column (Restek Corporation, Bellefonte, PA) was used at a column temperature of 30°C. The mobile phase (A) consisted of water containing 5 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid and (B) acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid at a flow rate of 1 mL/min and the following conditions: 0 min, 25% A, 75% B; 9 min 25% A, 75% B. The UV detector was set to 228 nm (Restek, 2018).

Experimental Design and Diets

A total of 256 male day-old chicks (Ross 308) were purchased from Charoen Pokphand Company Limited, Thailand. The chicks were randomly divided into 4 groups in a completely randomized design. Each group comprised 8 replicates pens (8 birds per replicate) in an evaporative housing system. These 4 dietary treatments included a control basal diet, a basal diet + 0.5% CR, a basal diet + 1% CR and a basal diet + 2% CR. The basal diet was a commercial corn-soybean meal-based diet formulated to meet the nutritional requirements of broilers according to NRC (1994). The commercial crumble diets used in this experiment were purchased from Charoen Pokphand Foods Public Company Limited, Bangkok, Thailand. The starter and finisher feed were formulated using corn, soybean meals, fish meal, rice bran, vitamins, and minerals. The analyzed chemical composition of the experimental feed is shown in Table 1. To achieve the intended amount of CR addition, the CR powder was first mixed with a small amount of the basal feed and then thoroughly mixed with the required amount of feed using a feed mixer (Model MI1HP3V01, Siam Farm Service Co., Ltd., Lampang, Thailand). The feed was prepared every five days and offered in the form of crumbles. The chemical composition of the feed mixtures was analyzed according to the methods described by the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (2005). The chicks were fed a basal diet for the first 2 d of the acclimatization period. Subsequently, the control group received a basal diet, while the other groups received a basal diet supplemented with CR powder in the starter diet (d 3–23) and in the finisher diet (d 24–40). The chicks were housed in 32 floor pens measuring 120 × 100 × 50 cm and provided with clean rice husks as bedding material. Each pen was equipped with a feeding trough and a nipple drinker. The birds had free access to feed and water during the entire experimental period. The lighting was switched on for 24 h in the first week and was reduced to 16 h of light and 8 h of darkness in the following weeks. Growth Performance and Carcass Characteristics

The initial body weight (BW) was determined at the beginning of the experiment. Thereafter, body weight was recorded weekly for each pen. In addition, the feed offered and the feed refused were weighed daily. The recorded data was used to calculate average daily gain (ADG), feed intake (FI), feed conversion ratio (FCR) and weight gain (WG) during the starter, finisher and total periods. FCR was calculated for each pen after adjusting for mortality.

On d 40, a total of 2 birds per replicate were randomly selected and transported to the slaughterhouse for examination of carcass characteristics and internal organs. The birds were slaughtered by dislocating the neck, scalded at 60°C for 3 min, de-feathered with a rotating drum picker for 2 min and then eviscerated manually. The slaughter weight and eviscerated carcasses were recorded. The internal organs were calculated as a proportion of the slaughter weight and the dissected part of the carcass as a percentage of the eviscerated carcasses (Chaosap et al., 2020).

Blood Biochemistry

A total of 5 milliliters blood were taken from the wing vein of a randomly selected bird per replicate at 34 d of age. The samples were collected in the tubes with EDTA as anticoagulant. Plasma was collected by centrifuging the samples for 15 min at 1,700 g and 4°C. The plasma was stored at -20°C until further analysis. The concentrations of metabolites in serum were measured using an ultra-compact analyzer (BioMajesty JCA-BM6010/C) according to the manufacturer's protocol. These included total cholesterol, triglycerides, total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, indirect bilirubin, globulin, albumin, alkaline phosphatase, aspartate aminotransferase, total protein, and alanine aminotransferase.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was analyzed using an HPLC system (Agilent 1220 Infinity II, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) equipped with an Ultra Aqueous C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 µm) (Restek, Bellefonte, PA) according to Norkeaw et al. (2023). In brief, 0.2 mL of plasma, 0.75 mL of 0.44 mol/L phosphoric acid, 0.25 mL of 42 mmol/L aqueous thiobarbituric acid, and 0.3 mL of distilled water were mixed. The mixture was then heated to 90°C in a water bath for 60 minutes and then cooled on ice. After cooling, 0.5 mL of alkaline methanol (4.5 mL of 1 mol/L NaOH and 50 mL of methanol) was added. After centrifuging the mixture at 2,500 g for 3 minutes, 20 µL of the sample was injected into the HPLC system. At a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min, the mobile phase consisted of methanol (40:60 v/v) and 50 mmol/L phosphate buffer (pH 6.8). The fluorescence detector was calibrated with Ex = 515 nm and Em = 543 nm. The MDA standard solution was prepared from 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane.

Intestinal Morphology

On d 34, one bird per replicate was randomly selected and slaughtered. The intestinal tissue was immediately removed for histological evaluation. After rinsing with normal saline, a total of 3 intestinal segments (duodenum, jejunum and ileum) were stored in a 10% neutral buffered formalin solution. Slides were prepared by sectioning the preserved material with a rotary microtome (5 μm). After hematoxylin and eosin staining, villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were measured at 40× magnification using a digital camera (Motic MC2000) connected to a compound microscope (CX21, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). VH and CD were taken from 5 different, well-preserved villus and crypt structures on each section. The ratio of villus height to crypt depth (VH/CD) was then determined (Hosseini-Vashan et al., 2020).

Statistical Analysis

The experiment was a completely randomized design with 8 replicates per treatment. The pen with 8 birds was considered as experimental unit for growth performance, while an individual bird randomly selected from each pen was the experimental unit for other measured parameters (2 birds from each pen for carcass characteristics and internal organs assessments, and 1 bird from each pen for blood biochemistry and intestinal morphology data). The equation of the statistical model is:Yij=μ+Ti+eijwhere Yij is the observed dependent variable, µ is the overall mean, Ti is the effect of treatments, and eij is the random error.

To analyze the collected data, a one-way analysis of variance was performed using the software program SPSS v.29.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). The statistical significance of the differences between the treatments was determined using the Tukey's post hoc test if a significant value (P < 0.05) was found.

DISCUSSION

The content of phytochemicals and the antioxidant activities vary greatly between the individual medicinal plants. In the present study, we have shown that CR contains bioactive compounds such as total phenol, CBD and THC. These bioactive compounds may be related to the antioxidant activity of CR. The total phenol and antioxidant activities of CR are in partial agreement with the studies of Dawidowicz et al. (2021) and Phupaboon et al. (2022). Nadeem et al. (2012) reported that the antioxidant activities in leaves and stems of C. sativa ranged from 1.47 to 121.85 mM TE/g and total phenol ranged from 0.339 to 3.562 mg/L GAE. In addition, Jin et al. (2020) reported that the CBD content in the leaves of C. sativa ranged from 0 to 1.04% and the THC content ranged from 0.57 to 0.93%. The differences in phytochemical content in the earlier studies may be related to variations in plant parts, chemovars, extraction methods and solvents used (Jin et al., 2020; Fei and Wang, 2022). Nevertheless, it can be assumed that the active compounds in CR cause changes in growth performance, carcass characteristics, intestinal morphology and blood biochemistry profile in broiler chickens.

The results of this study showed that broilers supplemented with different levels of CR consumed less feed than the control chickens throughout the period. However, no significant differences in BW, WG, or FCR were observed. The significant effects of CR on feed consumption could indicate that CR affects the palatability of the feed. The positive effects of CR supplementation, which could maintain growth performance comparable to the control, might be due to the content of phytochemicals in C. sativa, such as alkaloids, cannabinoids, flavonoids, phenolics, steroids, and terpenoids (Isahq et al., 2015; Jin et al., 2020; Nakkliang et al., 2022). The effects of C. sativa supplementation were mainly investigated in animal models. In contrast to our results, Mazidi et al. (2014) reported that C. sativa extract from the leaves and flowers had a positive effect on appetite in rats via ghrelin secretion. The increase in this peptide hormone triggers eating and increases energy intake. However, recent studies showed that C. sativa leaves did not alter feed intake and body weight in mice (Aziz et al., 2019; Maqbool et al., 2023). Meanwhile, Abey (2018) pointed out that C. sativa leaves at a dosage of 5% and 10% in the diet decreased body weight in rats. The discrepancies between these studies could be due to the differences in the part of C. sativa, level of intake, phytochemical composition, and other experimental conditions.

The effects of CR supplementation on carcass yields and internal organ weights were not significant compared to the control group. This similarity can be attributed to the comparable energy and protein content of all dietary treatments during the experimental period, which is also reflected in the similar body weights observed among the treatments. Similarly, dietary supplementation with Launaea taraxacifolia and Crescentia cujete leaves did not affect carcass characteristics and relative organ weights of broiler chickens despite their high total polyphenol content (Adeyemi et al., 2021). However, supplementation of CR at 1% and 2% to the broiler diet resulted in an increase in relative heart weight compared to the control diet. This increase can be partly attributed to the presence of THC in CR. Previous studies have shown that THC can activate cannabinoid type 1 receptors through the activation of the transient receptor potential ankyrin type 1 channel (Goyal et al., 2017). Activation of cannabinoid type 1 receptors has been found to associate with cardiac hypertrophy and myocyte enlargement in various mouse models (Lu et al., 2014; Abey, 2018).

Intestinal morphology is important for digestion and absorption, and indicators such as VH, CD and VH/CD ratio are commonly used to assess gut health (He et al., 2023). Nevertheless, there is little evidence on how CR affects the gut anatomy and functionality of broiler chickens and how it influences performance. In this study, we found that the ileum of 1% CR group had a significantly greater VH/CD ratio than that of the control group, suggesting that the small intestine has a higher digestive capacity. These results may partially explain why the CR-fed broilers had comparable body weights to the control broilers but consumed less feed. However, in the current study, no detectable effect of CR diet on VH and CD of duodenum, jejunum and ileum was observed. Previous studies in mice suggest that C. sativa inflorescence extract could improve gut health by reducing intestinal inflammation and altering the gut microbiota, which in turn affects the number of inflammation-related bacteria (Zandani et al., 2022). Therefore, the potential benefit of C. sativa on intestinal morphology could be related to its antibacterial and antioxidant effects on the gut microflora (Muscarà et al., 2021).

The activities of alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, and total bilirubin in the blood are indicators of liver function, which usually increases in hepatotoxicity (Wu et al., 2023). It has been reported that oxidative stress caused by diquat led to an increase in total protein, aspartate aminotransferase, and total bilirubin in broiler chickens (Zha et al., 2023). In this study, the blood biochemistry profile of CR-fed broilers showed comparable values to those of the control diets, except for total bilirubin, indirect bilirubin, and malondialdehyde. These results suggest that CR supplementation had no adverse effects on the liver and had a protective effect on hepatic oxidative damage in broilers by lowering total bilirubin and indirect bilirubin levels. Our results are in agreement with those of Wu et al. (2023), who found that dietary supplementation with phenolic compounds (curcumin) attenuated the adverse effects of diquat treatment on the liver by lowering total bilirubin levels. Therefore, our results may be attributed to the antioxidant activities of CR. The phytochemical content of C. sativa plant may act as a natural antioxidant by reducing the activity of pro-oxidant enzymes, chelating transition metal ions and interrupting free radical chain reactions (Muscarà et al., 2021). Similarly, C. sativa extract had no effect on the serum biochemical and lipid profile of parrots (Sosa-Higareda et al., 2023) and mice fed a high-fat diet (Zandani et al., 2022). In contrast, Vaughn et al. (2021) reported that cannabidiol oil altered liver function in healthy dogs by increasing liver enzymes, including aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, and total bilirubin. It is also reported that leaf extract of C. sativa had an unfavorable effect on biochemical and enzymatic activities in Cyprinus carpio (L.) fingerlings (Audu et al., 2015). Smith and Gruber (2023) suggested that the adverse effect of C. sativa on the liver was related to the strong inhibition of cannabidiol in hepatic drug metabolism involving cytochrome P450 enzymes. Therefore, this discrepancy may be attributed to the different metabolism of cannabinoids in the different species, the form of C. sativa and the routes of administration in the studies.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, the results show that CR supplementation can improve intestinal morphology and plasma oxidative stability of broiler chickens. In addition, the present study suggests that CR supplementation of 0.5 to 2% of the diet has no adverse effects on growth performance, carcass yield and blood biochemistry profile. These results suggest that CR could potentially be used as an alternative feed additive in broiler production. However, further studies are needed to investigate the effects of CR on antioxidant and anti-inflammatory gene expression in broiler chickens as well as the effects on meat quality.
