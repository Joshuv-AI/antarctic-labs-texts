Micro-CT evaluation of dentin removal in the danger zone and dentinal microcrack formation in primary molars prepared with pediatric rotary file systems: an in vitro study
Authors: Derya Doygun, Burcu Nihan Yüksel, Arda Büyüksungur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe aim of this study was to evaluate the amount of dentin removed and the incidence of dentinal microcrack formation in the “danger zone” of the mesial and distal root canals of mandibular primary molars using three different root canal instrumentation systems.MethodsMesial and distal root canals of mandibular primary molars (n = 66) were included in the study. The teeth were randomly assigned to three groups according to the instrumentation technique: conventional hand instrumentation, Kedo-SG, and Pro-Af Baby Gold rotary systems. Micro-Computed Tomography (Micro-CT) scans were obtained before and after root canal preparation. The amount of dentin removed from the danger zone and the presence of dentinal microcracks were evaluated using the reconstructed images.ResultsNo statistically significant differences were observed among the groups in terms of the amount of dentin removed from the danger zone at either measurement level in both mesial and distal roots (p > 0.05). Similarly, no statistically significant differences were found among the groups regarding dentinal microcrack formation in either root. However, a lower incidence of microcrack formation was observed in the Kedo-SG rotary file system. When evaluated according to root regions, microcracks were most frequently detected in the coronal third, followed by the middle third, and least frequently in the apical third in both roots.ConclusionNo significant differences were observed among the instrumentation systems in terms of dentin removal from the danger zone or dentinal microcrack formation. Although the Kedo-SG group showed fewer microcracks numerically, no statistically significant difference was found. Further studies are needed to evaluate the potential clinical advantages of single-file rotary systems in primary teeth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09083-y.

Introduction

Preservation of primary teeth that have lost vitality and prevention of their premature loss constitute one of the fundamental objectives of pediatric dental treatment. The success of root canal therapy in this context is closely related to the consideration of anatomical characteristics specific to primary teeth during canal preparation and shaping procedures [11].

In mandibular primary molars, anatomical variations and differences in dentin thickness are critical factors that may influence the outcome of endodontic treatment. The furcation region is considered a structurally weak area because of the marked reduction in dentin thickness and its increased susceptibility to procedural complications such as perforation. This region, commonly referred to as the “danger zone,” has been reported to be located approximately 2–4 mm apical to the furcation [1, 17, 25].

Most studies evaluating the danger zone in mandibular primary molars have focused on mesial roots, as these roots often present pronounced curvature and reduced dentin thickness near the furcation [18, 23]. However, distal roots may also present anatomical variations and canal curvature, suggesting that they should not be excluded from structural evaluations [7, 8, 20].

Excessive dentin removal in the danger zone may increase the risk of perforation and may also contribute to dentinal microcrack formation. Dentinal microcracks may develop during root canal shaping procedures and can compromise root integrity over time. Therefore, the design characteristics of instrumentation systems, including taper, tip design, cross-sectional geometry, and metallurgy, may influence dentin removal and microcrack formation [9, 10, 23].

Pediatric rotary file systems have been developed to better accommodate the anatomical characteristics of primary teeth. Among these systems, Kedo-SG and Pro-AF Baby Gold are commonly used pediatric rotary systems, whereas conventional hand files remain widely used in clinical practice. However, these systems differ in design, taper, active length, and metallurgical properties, which may influence their effect on dentin removal and root canal wall integrity [3].

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) enables non-destructive three-dimensional assessment of dentin thickness and dentinal microcrack formation before and after instrumentation [15]. A review of the literature indicates that studies evaluating dentin thickness reduction and microcrack formation within the danger zone of primary molars remain limited. Furthermore, available studies have mainly focused on mesial roots of primary second molars, whereas distal roots and primary first molars have received limited attention [6, 23].

Therefore, the present study aimed to comparatively evaluate dentin removal from the danger zone and dentinal microcrack formation in the mesial and distal root canals of primary molars prepared using conventional hand files, Pro-AF Baby Gold, and Kedo-SG rotary file systems.

The null hypothesis was that there would be no statistically significant difference among the file systems in terms of dentin removal from the danger zone at the 2-mm and 4-mm levels apical to the furcation, or in terms of dentinal microcrack formation.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Ankara University Faculty of Dentistry (Approval No:36290600/54; Date: 06 May 2024). A power analysis was performed for sample size determination, with a type I error rate set at 0.05, an effect size of 0.4, and a statistical power of 0.80. Based on this analysis, it was planned to include a total of 66 teeth in the study, consisting of 33 primary first molars and 33 primary second molars. The extracted primary molars included in the study had been removed for clinical reasons, including extensive carious destruction, infection, or a restoratively hopeless prognosis, and were not indicated for endodontic treatment. Informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all patients whose teeth were scheduled for extraction.

The study was conducted in accordance with the CRIS (Checklist for Reporting In-vitro Studies) guidelines, which were developed to enhance the scientific quality and transparency of in vitro research. The extracted teeth were cleaned under running water using a soft brush to remove residual soft tissues and were subsequently stored in 0.1% thymol solution for a maximum period of six months until experimentation.

The mesial and distal root canals of mandibular primary molars with root resorption not exceeding one-third of the total root length were included in the study. Teeth presenting a root curvature between 0° and 20°, as determined according to Schneider’s method, and a minimum root length of 7 mm met the inclusion criteria.

Access cavities were prepared under water cooling using a round bur (FG 001–012, Meisinger, Düsseldorf, Germany) with a high-speed handpiece. Residual pulp tissue within the pulp chamber was removed by irrigation with 1% sodium hypochlorite. The working length was determined using a size 10 K-file (Dentsply/Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland), set at 1 mm short of the apical foramen.

Following access cavity preparation, the teeth were scanned using a micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) device (Bruker Skyscan 1275, Kontich, Belgium) to obtain preoperative images required for the initial phase of the study. Pre-instrumentation micro-CT images were evaluated by an experienced examiner before canal preparation. Dentinal microcracks were defined as clearly visible hypodense lines or defects within the dentin structure observed on consecutive micro-CT slices. Suspicious findings were confirmed in consecutive sections and different planes when necessary to distinguish true microcracks from anatomical irregularities or imaging artifacts. Specimens with pre-existing defects or cracks, including those possibly caused during access cavity preparation, were excluded.

After micro-CT scanning, the specimens were randomly assigned into three groups with an equal number of samples for root canal preparation. All instruments were used according to the manufacturers’ instructions. During canal preparation, the files were advanced with light apical pressure and allowed to follow the original canal path without forcing the instrument against the canal walls. When resistance or binding was felt, the instrument was withdrawn from the canal, cleaned, and irrigation was performed before continuing the preparation.

Group 1 (Conventional hand instruments)

In this group, root canal preparation was performed using stainless steel K-type hand files (Foshan Senda Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., Foshan, China). Instrumentation was carried out sequentially with files of sizes 20/0.02, 25/0.02, 30/0.02, and 35/0.02 until the predetermined working length was reached. Following each instrument change, the root canals were irrigated with 2 mL of 2.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl).

Group 2 (Pro-AF baby gold rotary file system)

Root canal preparation was performed using the Pro-AF Baby Gold rotary file system (Kids-e-Dental Pvt. Ltd., India). The mesiobuccal and mesiolingual canals were prepared using B1 (#20/0.04) and B2 (#25/0.04) files, while the distal root canals were prepared using B2(#25/0.04) and B4 (#30/0.04) files, with an endodontic motor set at 300 rpm and a torque of 3 N·cm. Initial canal enlargement was carried out using a size 15 K-type hand file, as recommended by the manufacturer. Before advancing to the next file, the canals were irrigated with 2 mL of 2.5% NaOCl.

Group 3 (Kedo-SG rotary file system)

In this group, root canal preparation was performed using the Kedo-SG rotary file system (Reeganz Dental Care Pvt. Ltd., India). Due to the narrower anatomy of the mesiobuccal and mesiolingual canals, D1 files rotating in a clockwise direction at 360° were used, whereas the wider distal canals were prepared using E1 files. Initial canal enlargement was performed with a size 15 K-type hand file, in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. The rotary system was operated at a speed of 300 rpm and torque values ranging from 2 to 2.4 N·cm.

During instrumentation, irrigation with 2 mL of 2.5% NaOCl was performed before progressing to the subsequent file. After completion of canal preparation, a final rinse was performed with sterile saline solution. No lubricant, EDTA, or other chelating agent was used during canal preparation.

No sterilization or autoclaving procedure was performed between repeated uses of the instruments. Each instrument was used for a maximum of three times and was then replaced with a new one. In all groups, irrigation was delivered using a syringe fitted with a 27-gauge needle (Ayset Medical Products, Adana, Türkiye), positioned 2 mm short of the working length. Preoperative micro-CT scans were used to assess root canal morphology before group allocation. Teeth with accessory canals, additional canals, atypical canal morphology, complex root configurations, marked morphological differences, or pre-existing dentinal defects were excluded and were not instrumented. To ensure standardization, only specimens with comparable canal morphology were included in the study. Upon completion of root canal preparation, all specimens were rescanned using the micro-CT device to obtain postoperative images.

Micro–computed tomography evaluation

In this study, a high-resolution desktop micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) system (Bruker Skyscan 1275, Kontich, Belgium) was used to evaluate the specimens before and after root canal preparation. Scanning was performed using an X-ray source set at 80 kV and 125 µA, with an image resolution of 17 μm/pixel and a 1-mm aluminum (Al) filter. Image acquisition was carried out over a 360° rotation with a rotation step of 0.2°.

Following scanning, the raw data were reconstructed using NRecon software (version 1.7.4.2, Bruker Skyscan, Belgium). During reconstruction, smoothing was set to 3, ring artifact correction to 7, and beam hardening correction to 38%. The grayscale range was defined between 0 and 0.04.

Micro-CT analysis

After completion of scanning, specimen evaluation was performed using DataViewer software (Bruker SkyScan). The furcation area of each tooth was defined as the 0-mm reference point. Linear dentin thickness measurements were performed on axial micro-CT sections at 2 mm and 4 mm apical to the furcation in both the mesial and distal roots (Figs. 1 and 2). For each level, dentin removal was calculated as the difference between the pre- and post-instrumentation dentin thickness values obtained from the corresponding sections. Accordingly, the post-instrumentation dentin thickness value was subtracted from the corresponding pre-instrumentation value to determine the amount of removed dentin.

Since root length varied among specimens, the fixed number of slices was not predetermined. Each root canal was divided into coronal, middle and apical thirds according to its individual root length, and all consecutive axial micro-CT sections withing each third were evaluated. The slice thickness corresponded to the scanning voxel of 17 μm.

Micro-CT scanning was performed after access to cavity preparation. This approach enabled accurate identification of pre-existing dentinal microcracks that may have occurred during access cavity preparation and ensured their exclusion from the analysis. Initially, pre-existing cracks observed in the pre-instrumentation scans were identified and documented. Subsequently, only newly formed cracks detected in the post-instrumentation images were included in the evaluation (Fig. 3).

Microcracks formed during root canal preparation were recorded separately for the coronal, middle, and apical thirds of the root canals. Microcracks evaluation was primarily performed on consecutive axial micro-CT sections withing each third. All micro-CT imaging and analyses were performed by an experienced investigator. In addition, the micro-CT images were evaluated twice by the same examiner to confirm suspicious areas and improve the consistency of the assessment. To minimize bias, the evaluator was blinded to group allocation, and all analyses were conducted under blind conditions.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive data were presented as numbers and percentages. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons of non-normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis test were used. Categorical data were analyzed using the chi-square test. A significance level of 0.05 was adopted; p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, whereas p > 0.05 was considered not statistically significant.

However, when an overall evaluation was performed based on the location of microcracks within the mesial root, a statistically significant difference was observed. Discussion

Considering the morphological characteristics of primary teeth, it is widely emphasized that dentin removal from the thin canal walls should be limited to what is “necessary and sufficient.” In this context, evaluating both the amount of dentin removed from the danger zone and the potential formation of dentinal microcracks during canal preparation is of considerable clinical relevance. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of preserving dentin thickness in primary molars to minimize the risk of procedural complications [6].

When the existing literature is reviewed, it is evident that conventional hand files are frequently used as a comparison group, consistent with the methodology adopted in the present study [4, 16]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no previous study has simultaneously evaluated the pediatric-specific rotary file systems Kedo-SG and Pro-Af Baby Gold using micro-computed tomography while also assessing both dentin removal in the danger zone and dentinal microcrack formation in the mesial and distal root canals within the same experimental design. Therefore, the present study uniquely contributes to the literature by providing a comparative evaluation of these two rotary systems alongside conventional hand instruments.

In the present study, no statistically significant differences were detected among the three instrumentation techniques in terms of the amount of dentin removed at the 2-mm and 4-mm levels in the mesial and distal root canals of mandibular primary molars. Although no study with an identical methodology exists for direct comparison, similar findings have been reported in investigations evaluating post-instrumentation dentin thickness in primary teeth, suggesting that both rotary and manual systems may preserve dentin comparably when applied under controlled conditions [5, 24]. In contrast, Rao et al., [14] reported less dentin removal with conventional hand instrumentation than with rotary systems in a study focusing exclusively on mesial roots necertheless. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in study design, tooth selection, canal anatomy, measurement techniques, and the rotary systems evaluated.

With respect to studies investigating the danger zone in primary teeth, the literature reveals a notable lack of research directly evaluating distal roots. Only a single study has reported an assessment of the distal roots of mandibular primary first molars; however, that investigation did not specifically target the danger zone. Instead, dentin removal was measured at 2-mm, 4-mm, and 6-mm levels along the root surface [18]. Although this measurement, levels partially overlap with the anatomical region commonly defined as the danger zone, the data can only be considered indirectly comparable. To date, no study has directly examined dentin removal and microcrack formation specifically within the danger zone of distal root canals in primary molars using high-resolution three-dimensional imaging techniques.

Taken together, these findings highlight the need for further research focusing on both mesial and distal roots in primary molars, particularly using advanced imaging modalities such as micro-CT. The present study addresses an important gap in literature by providing a comprehensive evaluation of dentin removal and microcrack formation in both roots, thereby contributing valuable data to guide safer and more conservative endodontic treatment approaches in pediatric dentistry.

In the present study, no statistically significant differences were observed among the groups in either mesial or distal roots with respect to the incidence of dentinal microcrack formation. Unlike conventional ISO hand K-files, which have a standard 0.02 taper, the pediatric rotary systems used in the present study had greater and system-specific taper values. Kedo-SG files have a 0.04 taper, whereas Pro-AF Baby Gold files have taper values ranging from 0.04 to 0.06. In addition to taper, differences in active cutting design, cross-sectional geometry, file length, and metallurgy may influence dentin removal and stress distribution during canal preparation [19, 3]. Therefore, the absence of statistically significant differences among the groups should not be interpreted as indicating complete equivalence among the systems, but rather as showing that all tested systems produced comparable outcomes under the conditions of the present in vitro study.

Although the Kedo-SG group showed a lower incidence of dentinal microcracks descriptively, this difference was not statistically significant. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed by further studies. This finding may be related to the single-file design of the Kedo-SG system, which could result in lower mechanical stress within the root canal during instrumentation. Previous studies have suggested that systems with greater taper and multiple-file sequences may be associated with an increased risk of dentinal microcrack formation [2, 22]. However, taper alone may not be a decisive factor, and microcrack formation is likely influenced by a combination of structural and technical variables. In this respect, the lower torque setting recommended for the Kedo-SG system may have contributed to the lower incidence of dentinal microcracks observed in this group, whereas higher torque values used with other systems may increase stress on dentinal walls during preparation. Nevertheless, these interpretations remain speculative and should be confirmed by further experimental studies. Supporting this interpretation [12], reported that preparations performed with the Pro-Af Baby Gold system in mandibular molars resulted in the highest microcrack incidence in the middle third, followed by the coronal and apical thirds.

In the present study, the incidence of dentinal microcrack formation in the apical region was lower for all preparation systems. This finding may be related to the lower taper values of the instruments used in the apical third of the root canal. Previous studies have demonstrated that the stress generated during root canal preparation is approximately three times greater in the cervical and middle regions than in the apical region [13]. Moreover, stress accumulation has been reported to be more pronounced on the buccal and lingual aspects of canal walls, particularly in the coronal and middle thirds, which may explain the higher frequency of microcracks observed in these regions [21].

The Kedo-SG and Pro-Af Baby Gold rotary systems used in the present study have taper values comparable to those of conventional hand instruments and, unlike rotary systems originally developed for permanent teeth, incorporate design features adapted to the anatomical characteristics of primary teeth, such as shorter file length and modified active tip design. These characteristics may support their use in primary teeth under appropriate clinical protocols. However, further experimental and clinical studies are needed to better define their performance and confirm their clinical relevance in pediatric endodontics. The present study has several limitations. First, although the concept of the danger zone may also be applicable to maxillary primary molars, only mandibular primary molars were included in this study, which limits the generalizability of the findings to maxillary primary teeth. Additionally, the teeth used for microcrack evaluation were obtained from different individuals, which may represent a potential limitation. Individual variations in the physical and chemical properties of dentin, as well as patient-specific factors such as occlusal loading conditions encountered in clinical settings, may influence root dentin behavior and microcrack formation. Therefore, extrapolation of the present findings to clinical conditions should be undertaken with caution. Furthermore, the use of different torque settings for rotary systems represents another factor that may have influenced microcrack incidence. Although thymol solution was used to maintain hydration and minimize cross-contamination, storage for up to 6 months and exposure to thymol may have influenced the mechanical properties of dentin and its susceptibility to dentinal microcrack formation. Therefore, this should be considered as a limitation of the present study.

In the present study, access cavities were prepared while the teeth were held manually and were not embedded in any material, following the methodology described by Paqué et al. This approach was preferred to facilitate access cavity preparation and standardize the operative procedure. However, the absence of periodontal ligament and surrounding alveolar bone simulation may have altered stress distribution during canal instrumentation. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, as this in vitro setup may not fully reflect clinical conditions. Finally, the lack of comparable studies in literature simultaneously evaluating dentin thickness in the danger zone and dentinal microcrack formation using pediatric-specific rotary systems and micro-computed tomography limits direct comparison of the present results.

Based on the findings of the present study, the null hypothesis could not be rejected, as no statistically significant differences were observed among the instrumentation systems in terms of dentin removal from the danger zone at the 2-mm and 4-mm levels or dentinal microcrack formation.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, no statistically significant differences were observed among conventional hand files, Pro-AF Baby Gold, and Kedo-SG rotary systems in terms of dentin removal from the danger zone at the 2-mm and 4-mm levels or dentinal microcrack formation in primary molars. Although the Kedo-SG group showed a lower incidence of dentinal microcracks descriptively, this difference was not statistically significant. Therefore, all tested instrumentation systems demonstrated comparable outcomes under the experimental conditions of the present study. Further studies with larger sample sizes and different anatomical variations are needed to confirm these findings and to better clarify the clinical relevance of pediatric rotary file systems in primary teeth.
