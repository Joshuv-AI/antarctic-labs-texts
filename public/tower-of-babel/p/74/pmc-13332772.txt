Trastuzumab-Conjugated Nanoliposomes for Targeted Capecitabine Delivery to Suppress HER2-Positive Breast Tumor: Efficacy Analysis in NOD SCID Mice Xenografts
Authors: Shubhashree Das, Bhabani Sankar Satapathy, Mohamed Rahamathulla, Yahya Alhamhoom, Sovan Pattanaik, Saurabh Chawla, Gurudutta Pattnaik, Diptirani Rath, Mohammed Muqtader Ahmed, Ismail Pasha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTreatment of advanced breast cancer has proven to be difficult. Routine chemotherapy has limitations due to unavoidable and dreaded adverse effects during and after treatment, necessitating the development of tailored therapies. Capecitabine is a commonly used anticancer drug that comes in tablet form for oral administration. However, high doses and frequent administration-related toxicities limit its efficacy in HER2-positive breast cancer patients.MethodsThe current work seeks to target capecitabine using experimental nanoliposomes coupled to trastuzumab (TZ) (HER2-targeting monoclonal antibody) and assess anticancer activity in a NOD SCID mice xenograft model of breast cancer. Experimental capecitabine-loaded trastuzumab-nanoliposomes (TZ-CNLs) were developed by a previously reported method. FESEM, AFM, TEM, zeta potential, and drug release studies were used to characterize TZ-CNLs. In vivo effectiveness of CNLs/TZ-CNLs was carried out in experimental mice and compared with the free capecitabine-treated group. Hematological, biochemical, and histopathological analyses of experimental CNLs/TZ-CNLs were conducted across different treatment groups compared with the control.ResultsExperimental TZ-CNLs were spherical, smooth, and uniformly distributed, with an average size (198.6 nm), a zeta potential (−27.91 mV), and a drug loading (9.6±0.51%). The AFM indicated that TZ conjugation had no effect on texture. TZ-CNLs released 81.5 ± 3.05% capecitabine after 96 h, compared to CNLs/free capecitabine. After 12 days of treatment, TZ-CNLs significantly reduced tumor development (97.91 ± 3.0 mm3) compared to unconjugated CNLs/free capecitabine in NOD SCID mice xenografts. The enhanced antitumor activity observed with TZ-CNLs is consistent with HER2-targeted delivery mediated by TZ conjugation. Furthermore, TZ-CNL-treated animals maintained a healthier body-weight profile compared with free capecitabine and CNL-treated groups, suggesting improved treatment tolerability and reduced systemic toxicity. Plasma pharmacokinetic analysis indicated that administration of TZ-CNL-3 resulted in greater systemic availability of capecitabine, as well as prolonged mean residence time and increased volume of distribution, compared to free capecitabine and CNL-3. The study depicted an overall improvement in key biochemical parameters toward normal in animals treated with TZ-CNLs.ConclusionOverall, the promising preclinical outcomes, including tumor inhibition, improved hematologic and biochemical safety indices, and maintenance of organ histopathology, support the need for future preclinical investigations to determine whether TZ-CNLs may have application in HER2-positive breast cancer therapy.Keywords: capecitabine, trastuzumab, targeted drug delivery, nanoliposomes, breast cancer, in vivo efficacy, NOD SCID mice, xenograft

Introduction

Targeted drug delivery has been identified as a significant strategy in cancer therapy, providing the selective delivery of therapeutic agents to neoplastic cells while sparing surrounding healthy tissues.1 This strategy accomplishes selectivity through exploiting molecular differences, such as overexpressed receptors in a cancer cell. Breast cancer surface receptors, such as HER2, have been effective therapeutic targets. Functionalization through ligands (and easily monoclonal antibodies) can enable enhanced intracellular drug uptake, receptor-mediated endocytosis, and reduction of off-target exposure.2 Breast cancer, at present, is the most widespread kind of cancer in women throughout the globe, and the second most common cause of death from cancer.3–5 Breast tumors are categorized according to the expression of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor-2 (HER2).6–9 HER2 is a transmembrane receptor, highly expressed over breast tumor cells and leads to malignant transformation.6 About 30% of all breast cancers are HER2-positive, which is considered as most aggressive cancer type with treatment resistance and tumor recurrence.10–14 Common chemotherapeutics for HER2-positive breast cancer, such as paclitaxel, doxorubicin, docetaxel, tamoxifen citrate, etc., usually show low targeting specificity, leading to a high degree of damage to normal tissues.10,15–21 In this contest, monoclonal antibodies (anti-HER2 antibody) mediated targeting is being heavily explored over the years to enhance tumor selectivity. Further nanocarrier based delivery systems, such as nanoliposomes functionalized with a specific antibody, have been shown to increase the therapeutic index of drugs with reduced systemic toxicity.12–14,22

Capecitabine (a carbamate prodrug of 5-Fluorouracil [5-FU]) is widely prescribed for the management of HER2-positive breast cancer due to its tumor-targeted activation and a more favorable safety profile than that of other treatments.23 Capecitabine is gradually converted by enzymes to 5-FU. As, 5-FU has a shorter plasma half-life and is rapidly metabolized by dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase,24 we have selected capecitabine rather than 5-FU in the current study in view of its good chemical stability and higher pharmacological efficacy.25 Further, conventional capecitabine therapy is often associated with poor bioavailability, the requirement for relatively high therapeutic doses, and off-target toxicities, which limit its clinical effectiveness.3 Therefore, the development of targeted drug delivery systems that enhance tumour-specific drug accumulation while minimizing systemic exposure remains highly desirable.

Trastuzumab (TZ), a monoclonal antibody that selectively targets HER2, is the suggested first-line treatment for HER2-positive breast cancer.26–28 Many research studies have investigated the potential of TZ to target HER2-positive breast cancer cells. Vaidya T. and colleagues developed doxorubicin-encapsulated dual-antibody (TZ and OKT-3) conjugated trifunctional liposomes. TZ specifically targeted breast cancer cells and enhanced the efficacy of doxorubicin, whereas the OKT-3 antibody stimulated immune cells to attack HER2-positive breast carcinoma cells (BT-474 cells). These liposomes surpassed the efficacy of TZ and doxorubicin monotherapies.29 Amin M. et al produced TZ-conjugated liposomes for the targeted delivery of idarubicin to HER2-positive breast cancer cells (SKBR-3 cells). Superior therapeutic efficacy was depicted for TZ-conjugated liposomes compared to non-TZ-conjugated and free idarubicin liposomes.30

Previous studies have analysed TZ-conjugated liposomes for the delivery of cytotoxic agents, including doxorubicin and idarubicin.29,30 Capecitabine was selected for the present research due to its tumor-activated prodrug characteristics and its clinical efficacy in breast cancer therapy.23 Unlike directly, capecitabine undergoes preferential enzymatic conversion to 5-FU within tumor tissues. This process may reduce systemic toxicity while maintaining therapeutic efficacy.24 High doses and non-specific distribution often limit its clinical application. To address this, we used nanoliposomal encapsulation to improve drug stability, enable controlled drug release, and extend circulation time. We also conjugated TZ to achieve active HER2-mediated targeting and enhanced cellular uptake.26

In our previous study, TZ-conjugated capecitabine-loaded nanoliposomes (TZ-CNLs) were developed and evaluated for anticancer effectiveness in vitro against the MCF-7 and SKBR-3 breast cancer cell lines.31 Encapsulation of capecitabine through nanoliposomes showed preferable cellular uptake as depicted from fluorescence microscopy, with higher cytotoxicity in the tested breast cancer cells. Further TZ-conjugation substantially improved cellular uptake and cytotoxic effect than unconjugated nanoliposomes. The outcomes of the in vitro study indeed prompted us to test the in vivo effectiveness of the experimental TZ-CNLs/CNLs in a suitable breast cancer-induced model. Encapsulation of capecitabine in suitable nanocarriers (nanoliposomes) functionalized with TZ would improve its stability and limit its off-target degradation in vivo.32 It is hypothesized that following intravenous (i.v.) delivery, the targeted nanoliposomes would specifically accumulate breast tumor cells, following TZ interaction with HER2.31 The nanoliposomes would then penetrate across tumor cells owing to their nanosize and cell-mimicking phospholipid composition.33 Subsequently, encapsulated capecitabine would convert to 5-FU inside the tumor cells via enzymes like cytidine deaminase/ thymidine phosphorylase etc., and induce its apoptotic effect by preventing DNA synthesis.34

Till now no pre-clinical trials have been demonstrated the efficacy of TZ-conjugated nanoliposomal systems for the targeted delivery of capecitabine in HER2-positive breast cancer. The combination of capecitabine-loaded nanoliposomes (CNLs) with TZ has not been reported, especially for in vivo validation in HER2-positive xenograft models, despite their separate investigations or use with other drugs. In this context, the present study addresses this gap by developing targeted nanoliposomal delivery system for capecitabine utilizing TZ (to target HER2) and evaluating pre-clinical effectiveness of the TZ-CNLs/CNLs in the NOD SCID mice xenograft model.

Experimental CNLs were developed by the previously reported method and subsequently utilized carbodiimide chemistry for TZ coupling. CNLs/TZ-CNLs were subsequently characterized by measuring average size, zeta potential, surface morphology, texture, elemental composition, drug loading percentage, and in vitro drug release, among other parameters. Antitumor efficacy of CNLs/TZ-CNLs was investigated in NOD SCID mice xenograft. Further biochemical, haematological, and histopathological parameters were performed to substantiate the efficacy data. We believe that the outcome of the study would promote futuristic clinical validation of TZ-CLNs for breast cancer.

Experimental Section

Capecitabine was acquired as a complimentary sample from Ace Bright (India) Pharma Pvt. Ltd. in Bengaluru, Karnataka. SL was acquired from Hi-Media Laboratories Pvt. Ltd. in Mumbai, India. CH was obtained from E Merck Ltd., located in Mumbai, India. Butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) was procured from Qualigens Fine Chemicals in Mumbai, India. TZ (Eleftha, Intas Pharmaceuticals) was acquired from the SUM Hospital pharmacy in Odisha, India. DSPE-PEG (2000)-COOH was obtained from Avanti Polar Lipids, Inc., located in Alabaster, AL, USA. EDC and NHS were obtained from Sigma Aldrich, USA. Chloroform has been bought from Aladdin Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All other compounds used were of analytical grade.

Cell Culture

The MCF-7 cells were obtained from the National Centre for Cell Science in Pune, India. The cell line was cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma-Aldrich), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 U/mL streptomycin. The cells were grown in the appropriate media inside a 5% CO2 incubator (MCO-15AC; Sanyo, Tokyo, Japan) at 37°C.

Animals

This study used female Nonobese diabetic (NOD)/severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) mice (6–8 weeks old) housed in a Specific Pathogen-Free (SPF) facility at National Center for Animal Research and Experimentation (NCARE), School of Biological Sciences (SBS), National Institute of Science Education and Research (NISER), Khurda, Odisha, India (Animal licence no. 1634/GO/ReBi/S/12 /CPCSEA). The animals were maintained under controlled temperature (22 ± 2°C), humidity (30–70%), and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Mice were provided with a balanced pellet diet ad libitum (free feeding) and free access to autoclaved drinking water. Mice were group housed in individually ventilated cages. The experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC Approval No. NISER/SBS/AH-313). The animal experiments were conducted from 12th August to 24 September 2024. Also, the use and maintenance of laboratory animals were supervised in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Animals were anesthetized using isoflurane 2.5–5% administered via inhalation before the invasive procedures. At the end of the study, animals were euthanized using CO2 asphyxiation followed by confirmation of death.

Method of Development of Experimental CNLs

The experimental CNLs were developed following a previously published report from our laboratory (Figure 1). The primary phospholipid used in formulation development was SL. Additionally, we used CHL and DSPE-PEG (2000)-COOH. In a 250 mL round-bottom flask, the measured amounts of capecitabine, SL, CH, and DSPE-PEG (2000)-COOH were dissolved in the necessary volume of chloroform. Since all phospholipids are typically susceptible to oxidation, BHT (2% w/v) was added as an antioxidant to this combination. Then, the mixture was put into a rotating vacuum evaporator (Rotavap, PBU-6, Superfit, Mumbai, India) that was connected to a water bath and allowed to evaporate. The temperature of water bath was maintained at 40°C. When the chloroform evaporated, a thin layer was deposited around the inside surface of the round-bottom flask. The flask was then placed in a desiccator overnight, facilitating the further removal of any remaining organic solvent in the thin layer. On the second day, the produced thin film was hydrated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4 for about one hour while rotating at 110 rpm at 60 °C. Throughout this interval, the produced film was entirely distributed on PBS. After hydration, the mixture was sonicated using a bath-type sonicator (Trans-o-sonic, Mumbai, India) set at 40 W for 30 minutes. Sonication facilitates the reduction of big vesicles to the required ultra-small size range. Following sonication, the formulation was left at ambient temperature for 1 hour, followed by refrigeration overnight at 4 °C. The next day (day 3), the sample underwent cold centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 40 minutes using a Sigma Lab Centrifuge (UK). Following centrifugation, the supernatant was removed, and the resulting particles were collected and preserved at –20°C overnight. The pre-cooled samples were then lyophilized for 12 hours using a laboratory lyophilizer (Kolkata, India) to produce a dry powdered formulation, which was then kept in a refrigerator at 4°C.31

The lyophilized CNLs were weighed after each batch run to determine the percent yield of each formulation batch. The yield of CNLs was obtained in triplicate, and the yield percentage was calculated as: % yield = (Amount of CNLs obtained after lyophilization / Total amount of components used in the formulation batch) ×100.

Trastuzumab Conjugation to the Nanoliposome Surface via EDC-NHS Chemistry

The CNLs were reconstituted in 1 mL of MES buffer at pH 5.5. The -COOH functional groups on the liposome surface were activated by incubating with 200 µL of EDC (4 mg/mL) and 200 µL of NHS (6 mg/mL) for 30 minutes at 25 ± 2 °C on a shaker. The activated nanoliposomes underwent centrifugation, were washed twice with MES buffer at pH 5.5, and were resuspended in 1 mL of MES buffer. The EDC-NHS-activated nanoliposomes were then treated with 200 µL of TZ (Intas Pharmaceuticals Ltd., Ahmedabad, India) monoclonal antibody (2.5 mg/mL) and incubated for 4 hours at 25±2 °C on a shaker. The liposomes were centrifuged at 17,000 rpm for 40 minutes, washed twice with PBS (pH 7.4), resuspended in PBS (pH 7.4), and stored at 2–8°C for further investigation. A TZ: capecitabine ratio of 1:10 (w/w) was employed during formulation development.36 To get optimal TZ density over CNLs for efficient receptor targeting, the chosen ratio was employed without causing surface hindrance.

UV-Visible Spectroscopic Analysis

UV–Visible spectroscopy was used to make sure that TZ had successfully conjugated onto CNLs. Absorption spectra of the free capecitabine, free TZ, and TZ-CNLs were measured at a wavelength interval ranging from 200 to 400nm with a UV-Visible spectrophotometer. This was performed in accordance with established methods previously reported in the case of antibody conjugates with nanoparticles.38 Each sample was diluted accordingly in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to a level that their absorbance lay within the linear detection range, with PBS as the baseline solution. All measurements were replicated thrice, and characteristic λ_max values and spectral shifts were evaluated.

Determination of Vesicle Size, Polydispersity Index (PDI), and Zeta Potential (ZP)

Vesicle size, PI, and surface charge of CNLs were evaluated using the Zetasizer (Nano ZS 90, Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK). After diluting with deionized water, lyophilized CNLs were sonicated for two minutes. At 25 ± 0.5°C, the sample was placed in the dip cell. Three runs of the experiment were carried out, and the results were shown as mean standard deviation.39

Determination of Morphology by Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM)

FESEM was employed to analyze the surface structure of the experimental CNLs. Lyophilized samples were applied to a stub using carbon tape. The sample underwent vacuum drying, subsequent platinum coating, and was examined via FESEM (JSM 6100; JEOL, Tokyo, Japan).40

Energy Dispersive X-Ray (EDX) Analysis

EDX, an integrated component of the FESEM (JSM 6100; JEOL, Tokyo, Japan), was used to analyze the elemental composition of the prepared CNLs.

High-Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HR-TEM)

The internal morphology of CNLs was examined using an HR-TEM (Tecnai G2 Spirit BioTwin FP 5018/40, FEI, Hillsboro, USA). The carbon-coated copper grid was immersed in the CNLs dispersion in deionized water and then air-dried for several hours. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy images were obtained and analysed.41

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was conducted using a JPK NanoWizard 4 system (Bruker, Berlin, Germany) to assess the particle size, surface morphology, and three-dimensional topography of CNLs and TZ-CNLs. Lyophilized CNLs and TZ-CNLs were reconstituted in Milli-Q water at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL, thereafter subjected to 5 minutes of ultrasonication and 5 minutes of vortexing to achieve uniform dispersion. A 10 µL droplet of each sample was meticulously placed onto a pre-cleaned mica substrate and subjected to vacuum drying for 30 minutes to get a homogenous particle layer. Imaging was performed in PeakForce Quantitative Nanomechanical Mapping (QNM) mode with a silicon nitride probe with a resonance frequency of 150–350 kHz, a spring constant of 0.4 N/m, and a tip radius of less than 10 nm. Scanning was performed across regions of 2×2 µm2 and 5×5 µm2 at a frequency of 0.5–1.0 Hz under ambient conditions. The topographical and mechanical property maps were analyzed using JPK Data Processing software to ascertain particle size, surface roughness, and morphology, with sample pictures chosen for examination.42

In vitro Capecitabine Release From Experimental Nanoliposomes

A measured quantity of capecitabine (5mg) and CNLs/TZ-CNLs (5mg equivalent amount) were dispersed in PBS at pH 7.4 and placed into a dialysis bag with a molecular weight cutoff between 12,000 and 14,000 Da (Himedia dialysis membrane-60, Mumbai, India). The dialysis bag’s two ends were stitched with cotton thread, and the assembly was immersed in 50 mL of PBS within a beaker. The complete assembly was positioned on a magnetic stirrer set to 300 rpm. A 1 mL sample from the drug release medium was collected at predetermined intervals, and an equivalent volume of fresh release medium was added. The samples were analyzed using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer, calibrated to 303.0 nm, with PBS as the blank reference. All measurements were replicated thrice. The drug concentration in the sample was derived from the standard calibration curve.43

Drug Release Kinetics Study

Drug release mechanisms of the experimental nanoliposomes were evaluated by using different kinetic models on in vitro drug release studies. Models included zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, Korsmeyer-Peppas, and Hixson-Crowell. They measured the percentage of drug remaining, the cumulative drug release against the square root of time, and the logarithmic cumulative drug release versus logarithmic time. The calculated R2 values were used to assess the linearity of the graphs. The model exhibiting the highest R2 value was identified as the best fit for describing the release kinetics. The release exponent (n) obtained from the Korsmeyer–Peppas model was applied to determine the drug release mechanism, including Fickian diffusion, anomalous transport, or case-II transport.31

In vivo Preclinical Efficacy Evaluation of Optimized Capecitabine-Loaded Liposomes: Experimental Study Design

MCF-7 cells were grown and processed in sterile conditions before injection. For the study, female NOD SCID mice were randomly categorized into four experimental groups (n=6). A total of 24 animals were included in the study based on the inclusion criteria. The sample size of the study was determined by using the primary outcome measure, which was tumor volume reduction. All animals received subcutaneous injections of 1×105 MCF7 cells into the right breast fat pad. Selection of the MCF-7 cell line to establish xenografts was based on its tumorigenic potential and consistent growth in immunodeficient mice.45 This selection corresponds to the results of our previous study, where the TZ-modified nanoliposomes showed significant anticancer activity in both MCF-7 and SKBR3 cells.31 Thus, MCF-7 cells were selected for comparative evaluation of the therapeutic efficacy and safety of the developed formulations in vivo. Tumor development was assessed twice a week, and treatments started when tumors reached a palpable size of roughly 5 mm. A single blind study was adopted. The following treatment schedule was followed at 2-day intervals for 12 days for the animal groups.

Group I (control group) animals were administered a vehicle (5% normal saline, 10 mL/kg, intravenously).

Group II animals were administered free capecitabine (20 mg/kg, intravenously).

Group III animals were administered CNL-3 (20 mg/kg, intravenously).

Group IV animals were administered TZ-CNL-3 (20 mg/kg, intravenously).

Although capecitabine is usually given orally, the free capecitabine group was administered intravenously to enable a direct and pharmacologically relevant comparison with the nanoliposomal formulations. The study’s main goal was to evaluate how nanoencapsulation and TZ-mediated targeting affect capecitabine’s pharmacokinetic and therapeutic performance, so using the same administration route for all treatment groups was essential. Intravenous administration reduced variability related to gastrointestinal absorption and first-pass metabolism, thereby allowing for more precise evaluation of the benefits associated with the nanoliposomal delivery system.

Body weight and tumor volume measurements were conducted biweekly. Subsequently, animals were euthanized, tumors were excised, weighed, and photographs of the tumors were obtained.14

Measurement of Tumor Volume

We used a digital Vernier caliper to measure the tumor’s length (L) and width (W) in two different directions to find its tumor volume. Measurements were obtained twice a week, from when the tumor could be felt until the completion of the research (12 days). Measurement of Body Weight

We weighed the NOD SCID mice with breast tumors twice a week using a calibrated digital scale from before the tumor cells were injected to the completion of the study. Frequent checkups are useful in monitoring overall health, finding toxicities affecting treatment, and the presence of cachexia, where a loss of over 10% of body weight may be a sign of systemic toxicity or tumor burden. Measurements were done at the same time of day, and mice were weighed into a clean container to reduce their stress levels. Those mice that had lost 20% or more of their body mass were euthanized to prevent suffering.47

Measurement of Tumor Weight

After in vivo study, all animals were euthanized. Tumors were individually excised, rinsed with PBS to remove residual blood and debris, and dried using sterile cotton. Subsequently, tumor masses were measured in milligrams per mouse using a digital analytical balance. This approach is commonly adopted in preclinical studies to evaluate the anticancer potency of different nanoformulations, as many prior reports have suggested. Thus, the decreased tumor weight compared to the control group refers to therapeutic efficacy. Considerable care was taken to avoid the inclusion of the surrounding tissues in the excision of the tumor to obtain accurate measurement of the tumor weight.48

Pharmacokinetic Study

A pharmacokinetic investigation was performed in NOD SCID mice to determine the systemic exposure of capecitabine after the last treatment dosage on day 12. Mice (body weight 18–21 g) were randomly allocated into four groups (n = 6 per group): Group I acted as the control, whilst Groups II, III, and IV were administered a single intravenous dose of free capecitabine (12 mg/kg), CNLs, and TZ-CNLs, respectively, each containing an equal dosage of capecitabine. Blood samples (approximately 50 µL) were collected from the retro-orbital sinus of each mouse at specified intervals (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 2, 4, 8, 10, 12, and 24 h post-injection). Blood was collected from isoflurane-anesthetized mice using an autoclaved and heparinized glass pipette. Samples were centrifuged to obtain plasma, which was stored at −80 °C until analysis.49 Quantification of capecitabine in plasma was conducted using the liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) technique. In summary, 100 µL of plasma was combined with 300 µL of acetonitrile containing tolbutamide at a concentration of 500 ng/mL as an internal standard. Samples were vortexed for 10 min and then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min at 10 °C. A 100 µL aliquot of the supernatant was diluted with 100 µL of distilled water, mixed thoroughly, and then analyzed by LC-MS/MS. The analytical equipment included an AB-SCIEX API 4000 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer, controlled using Analyst 1.6 software. Chromatographic separation was accomplished with a YMC Triart C18 column (30 × 2.1 mm, YMC Corp., Japan) with a gradient elution of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in an 80:20 acetonitrile: water mixture). The mobile phase flow rate was set at 0.8 mL/min, with a total run time of 3.0 min. To evaluate the effect of TZ conjugation and lipid-based nanoformulation on the pharmacokinetic behavior and systemic exposure of capecitabine, pharmacokinetic parameters were determined and compared across treatment groups.4,50

Haematological AnalysisCD-340

A haematological study was conducted to evaluate the systemic effects of the TZ-CNLs-treated group, compared to the control, free capecitabine, and CNLs-treated groups, with breast cancer. To prevent coagulation, blood samples from all groups were obtained through retro-orbital incision under anesthesia at the end of the investigation and collected into EDTA-coated containers. A fully automated 5-part haematology analyser, such as the Mindray BC-5140, utilises impedance technology for cell enumeration and photometric analysis for haemoglobin measurement to perform complete blood counts (CBC). The analyzer offered extensive information of haematological profiles, including white blood cell (WBC) counts with differential enumeration of neutrophils, basophils, eosinophils, monocytes, and lymphocytes; red blood cell (RBC) indices like haematocrit (HCT), haemoglobin (HGB), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC), mean corpuscular haemoglobin (MCH), and red cell distribution width–coefficient of variation (RDW-CV); as well as platelet indices including mean platelet volume (MPV), and platelet count (PLT). The hematological and physiological impacts of the treatments were assessed through comparison of haematological parameters across experimental groups.51

Biochemical Parameters Analysis

In NOD SCID mice with breast tumors, biochemical parameters were tested to determine metabolic state, electrolyte level, kidney and liver function, among experimental groups. After the study, a retroorbital puncture was done to collect specimens of blood while the subjects were under an anastatic condition. The blood specimens were kept at room temperature to clot, then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes to obtain the serum. Until analysis, the serum was stored at −20°C. Serum levels of alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT/SGPT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST/SGOT), total bilirubin, and direct bilirubin were measured to assess hepatic function. Measurements of serum creatinine, blood urea, and uric acid (UA) were used to evaluate renal function. Electrolyte levels were also studied, including ionized calcium (iCa2⁺), total calcium (Ca2⁺), sodium (Na⁺), chloride (Cl−), and potassium (K⁺). We also assessed metabolic and lipid profiles by measuring total cholesterol (TC), serum glucose, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), triglycerides, serum albumin, and total protein (TP). Following the manufacturer’s directions, a commercial semi-automated chemical analyzer (Rayto Life and Analytical Sciences Co., Ltd., China) was used to measure all of the parameters using standard colorimetric procedures. A spectrophotometer was used to get absorbance measurements. After that, the biochemical data from each group were subjected to statistical tests to compare the safety and effectiveness of the TZ-CNLs with those of the control and other treatment groups.50,52

Histopathological Analysis

The study employed selected NOD SCID mice for all the experimental groups. After the completion of the treatment schedule on day 12, the animals were sacrificed according to ethical guidelines that help minimize pain. The breast tumor tissues, along with the primary organs such as kidneys, liver, and lungs, were removed from the animals and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 48 hours. After fixation, dehydration was performed, and the tissues were embedded in paraffin. The sections were prepared from the tissues by a microtome with a section thickness of 5 µm and were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for the microscopic evaluation of the necrotic regions, changes in morphology, cellular degradation, and inflammatory changes at a magnification of 40X.28 Further alteration in the tissues consists of inflammatory cell infiltration, cellular degeneration, necrosis, vascular congestion and architectural distortion, which were assessed by a blinded pathologist on a 0–4 scale, with 0 (absent), 1 indicating minimal (<10%), 2 indicating mild (10–25%), 3 indicating moderate (26–50%) and 4 indicating severe (>50%) tissue damages (Table 1). This was confirmed with histopathology scoring.53,54

Statistical Analysis

All values are expressed as mean ± SD (n=3) for in vitro studies to reflect data variability, whereas mean ± SEM (n=6) is used for in vivo experiments. Statistical analysis was carried out applying ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for comparison between groups using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0) software. A p-value of ≤ 0.01 was considered statistically significant.

% Drug Loading, % Yield, and % Loading Efficiency of Experimental CNLs

Among the four accepted formulations, CNL-3 was identified as the optimal type. The drug loading percentage for CNL-3 was 9.6 ± 0.51%, while for CNL-1, CNL-2, and CNL-4, the values were 8.1 ± 2.2%, 4.3 ± 0.9%, and 3.6 ± 1.5%, respectively. CNL-3 demonstrated higher loading efficiency (91.3 ± 0.8%) and yield percentage (83.6 ± 1.3%) compared to the other three formulations (Table 2).

In the study, among the four selected formulations, CNL-3 showed the highest drug loading, though the drug loading % across all reported formulations was reasonable, with slight significant variation. It was observed that drug loading % was varied in relation to the drug: lipid ratio. However, this was neither directly proportional nor inversely related. Changes in the drug: lipid ratio initially altered drug loading %, which might be due to alterations in bilayer packing and local drug-lipid interactions. But a similar trend was not observed for CNL-3. On further increase in drug: lipid ratio (CNL-4), the drug loading % rather decreased. Hence, overall, the data indicate that an increase in the drug-to-lipid ratio did not increase the drug content in the formulation. Similar findings were also reported in other research groups.40 It can then be inferred that the enhanced drug loading of CNL-3 may be due to the optimized drug-to-lipid ratio (1:3), which probably gave a sufficient balance of lipids in the matrix to aqueous core structure to facilitate effective drug encapsulation and hence reduced loss of drugs and increased entrapment efficiency.55 According to these observations, the most appropriate formulation was regarded as CNL-3, which was chosen to be conjugated to TZ to be investigated further in vitro and in vivo.

In vesicular nanostructures like nanoscale liposomes (size < 200 nm), achieving a higher drug loading % is always a challenge. In view of the vesicular architecture in nanoliposomes comprising a hydrophilic core and hydrophobic outer coating, there exists a constraint on drug loading, unlike the matrix nano systems.56 In our case, we achieved a drug loading of 9.6 ± 0.51% (loading efficiency as 84.3 ±0.8%) for the selected nanoliposomal formulation, which is in similar vein with concurrent published data. It is hypothesized that the nano structure and targetability of the formulation towards specific HER2 receptor, the dose of the drug is substantially reduced compared to its conventional dosage forms. Hence, the low drug loading might be compensated in the requirement of a relatively much lower dose of TZ targeted nanoliposomes than free capecitabine.

TZ Conjugation Efficiency on CNL-3

The conjugation efficiency of TZ with CNL-3 was evaluated by the BCA protein assay, which measured the concentration of the free antibody remaining in the supernatant following conjugation. The conjugation efficiency was found to be 67.2 ± 1.43%, indicating successful conjugation of TZ with the surface of CNL-3. The weight ratio of 1:10 for TZ and capecitabine was maintained for effective ligand concentration for binding with HER2 receptors. Based on the entrapment efficiency obtained (84.3 ± 0.8%) and conjugation efficiency (67.2 ± 1.43%) of the drug and TZ, respectively, the incorporated weight ratio is found to be approximately 1:12, indicating that more drug is retained in the liposomal core than the antibody conjugation efficiency, as observed in previous studies carried out on ligand-functionalized nanoparticles.31

The dynamic light scattering test and the zeta potential test were performed to identify the physical and chemical differences between CNL-3 and TZ-CNL-3. The test results proved that the physical structure of the carrier changes when the TZ is attached. CNL-3 had a negative surface charge of −21.85 ± 1.3 mV, as demonstrated in Figure 3C, while TZ-CNL-3 had a higher negative charge of −27.91 ± 3.5 mV, as demonstrated in Figure 3D, indicating that this charge is more stable and therefore better suited to withstand electrostatic repulsion in a colloidal system and to exist in a stable dispersion in a physiological environment. All of the above data display that TZ conjugation modifies the particle size of the nano lipid system.4,37

In vitro Drug Release

The in vitro cumulative release profile of capecitabine from the formulations CNL-3 and TZ-CNL-3, along with free capecitabine, was evaluated over 96 hours, as shown in Figure 8. The free capecitabine showed a rapid release pattern, reaching a maximum release of 95.7 ± 2.6% within the first 12 hours. This could be attributed to the high solubility of the drug, classified as BCS Class III. In addition, the drug was in its free form and not encapsulated. The CNL-3, however, showed a more controlled and gradual release pattern, reaching a maximum release of 99.2 ± 0.46% at 96 hours. The most sustained release pattern was observed in the TZ-CNL-3 formulation, reaching a maximum release of 81.5 ± 3.05% at 96 hours. The slow-release pattern in the TZ-CNL-3 could be attributed to the steric hindrance effect caused by the TZ on the surface of the nanoliposomes. This may have caused a denser outer surface, thereby slowing down the release pattern. The sustained release pattern in the TZ-CNL-3 formulation could be advantageous in that it may help to prolong the systemic circulation and tumor specificity. This may also reduce the frequency of drug dosage. These are critical factors in enhancing the therapeutic index in the treatment of HER2-positive breast cancer.10,35

To further elucidate the release mechanism of capecitabine from the developed nanoliposomal formulations, the in vitro drug release data were fitted to various kinetic models, including Zero-order, First-order, Higuchi, Korsmeyer–Peppas, and Hixson–Crowell models. The kinetic parameters and corresponding regression coefficients (R2) are presented in Table 4. Free capecitabine exhibited comparable fitting to both the Zero-order and Higuchi models (R2 = 0.976), suggesting that the release profile may be described by either concentration-independent release kinetics or diffusion-controlled release behavior. In contrast, CNL-3 showed the highest correlation with the Korsmeyer–Peppas model (R2 = 0.966), followed closely by the Higuchi model (R2 = 0.955), indicating that diffusion played a dominant role in drug release from the nanoliposomal matrix. TZ-CNL-3 exhibited the highest fit to the First-order model (R2 = 0.945), with comparable correlation to the Higuchi model (R2 = 0.942), suggesting that drug release was influenced by the concentration of capecitabine remaining within the formulation while still retaining a significant diffusion-controlled component.

In vivo Anticancer Activity Reflected by the Tumor Volume Reduction

The antitumor efficacy of the developed nanoformulations was evaluated using an NOD SCID breast cancer xenograft model. Free capecitabine, CNL-3, and TZ-CNL-3 were administered intravenously at an equivalent capecitabine dose of 20 mg/kg every two days over a 12-day treatment period. This experimental design facilitated a direct comparison of the therapeutic performance of the nanoformulations with that of the free drug under standardized conditions.

The study evaluated the growth of breast tumor xenografts in NOD SCID mice to assess the anticancer activity of various formulations over a 12-day period. The results, illustrated in Figure 9, show that the control group experienced continuous tumor growth, with the volume increasing from approximately 108.4 ± 1.05 mm3 on day 0 to 435.6 ± 2.71 mm3 by day 12, indicating aggressive tumor proliferation. Mice treated with free capecitabine demonstrated noticeable tumor growth, with volumes reaching around 260.8 ± 7.62 mm3 by day 12. This suggests that the treatment was only partially effective, likely due to non-specific distribution and systemic toxicity, consistent with earlier research.28 In contrast, the CNL-3 group exhibited significantly better tumor suppression, maintaining a tumor volume of approximately 119.8 ± 5.72 mm3 by day 12 (p < 0.01 compared to free capecitabine). This improvement is likely attributed to enhanced drug retention and controlled release mechanisms. The tumor volume was reduced to 97.91 ± 3.04 mm3 in the TZ-CNL-3 group, which had the highest tumor suppression (p < 0.01 versus all groups). This significant suppression may be attributed to the combined effects of nanoliposomal delivery and TZ-mediated targeting. However, additional mechanistic and biodistribution studies would be required to directly confirm receptor-mediated uptake and targeting efficiency. Compared to free capecitabine, the clinically relevant control, TZ-CNL-3, showed improved antitumor efficacy without observed additional toxicity. These findings suggest that TZ-mediated targeting and nanoencapsulation may offer a therapeutic advantage over conventional drug administration. In line with earlier studies on antibody-functionalized nanocarriers, these results demonstrate improved antitumor efficacy of TZ-CNL-3 compared with free capecitabine and CNL-3 under the experimental conditions employed.10

Body Weight as an Indicator of Treatment Tolerability

Changes in the weight of the bodies of tumor-bearing NOD SCID mice are an indirect but critical indicator of systemic toxicity and stress induced by treatment. As shown in Figure 10, the weight of the bodies of the control group increased progressively during the 12 days of the experiment, from 22.12 ± 0.2 g to 23.54 ± 0.19 g. This indicates that the health of these mice remained stable during this period. The weight of the bodies of the free capecitabine-treated mice decreased significantly during this period, from 21.35 ± 0.21 g to 20.61 ± 0.2 g on day 12 (p < 0.01). This indicates that capecitabine induced systemic toxicity and treatment intolerance with cachexia in these mice, as has been reported in previous studies on the systemic toxic effects of capecitabine.57,58 The weight of the bodies of mice treated with CNL-3 decreased during the initial stages of the experiment but returned to normal by day 6 and remained so for the rest of the experiment, with a final weight of 21.95 ± 0.19 g on day 12 (**p < 0.01 vs. free capecitabine). This is an indication of reduced toxicity, which is attributed to the encapsulation and release of the drug. There was a marked increase in body weights of mice that had been administered with TZ-CNL-3, with a weight of 22.93 ± 0.2 g by day 12 (**p < 0.01 compared to free capecitabine). This is an indication of a better therapeutic index and reduced toxicity of TZ-CNL-3, which is attributed to its targeted delivery to HER2, tumor localization, and reduced side effects. This is in agreement with other research on targeted nanomedicine platforms, which emphasized weight preservation as an important aspect of system tolerability.4,10,28,59

Distribution of free capecitabine throughout vital organs may have increased systemic exposure to the cytotoxic effect of the drug, thus contributing to the body weight loss. In contrast, body weights of TZ-CNL-3-treated and CNL-3-treated groups remained comparable to those of the untreated group, possibly due to reduced off-target exposure and improved tolerability provided by nanoencapsulation and targeted delivery. This interpretation is further supported by the favorable haematological, biochemical, and histopathological findings observed in the TZ-CNL-3-treated group.

Pharmacokinetic Study

Plasma PK study depicted a clear difference in the key parameters in terms of AUC, AUMC, MRT, etc., among the free capecitabine, CNL-3, and TZ-CNL-3 (Table 6). Following administration, the free capecitabine was detectable in plasma up to 12 h, after which the concentration fell below the limit of detection, while the same was detectable till 24 h in the case of CNL-3/TZ-CNL-3. AUC0–t value for free capecitabine was found as 9844.3 ± 211.2 ng·h/mL, whereas the same was 76321.7 ± 521.1 ng·h/mL for CNL-3 and 67502.01 ± 121.7 ng·h/mL for TZ-CNL-3, respectively. The substantially higher AUC values observed for the nanoformulations indicate enhanced systemic exposure and prolonged circulation of capecitabine compared with the free drug following intravenous administration. Similarly, AUMC and MRT also followed a similar pattern. The AUMC0–∞ as reported for TZ-CNL-3 (158,584.2 ± 2053.1) was almost two-fold higher than that of the free capecitabine (77244.3 ± 721.5). Data overall suggested that nanoencapsulation and antibody conjugation had brought significant modulation in the PK profile of capecitabine (Figure 14). The enhanced systemic exposure, prolonged circulation time, and reduced clearance associated with TZ-CNL-3 may contribute to its improved therapeutic efficacy in the breast cancer xenograft model.4,50

Haematological Analysis

Haematological characterization of treatment groups demonstrated that free capecitabine-induced toxicity considerably affected systemic haematopoiesis, which was gradually alleviated by CNL-3/TZ-CNL-3. WBC was 2.08 ± 0.15 × 103/μL, RBC was 7.37 ± 0.11 × 106/μL, and HGB was 12.46 ± 0.23 g/dL in the control group (Table 7). In contrast, the free capecitabine-treated group (Group II) showed significant hematotoxicity, as evidenced by a significant decrease in RBC (6.74 ± 0.08 × 106/μL), HGB (10.75 ± 0.43 g/dL), and PLT (223 ± 1.05 × 103/μL), which is in line with research on capecitabine-induced bone marrow suppression in murine models.61 The CNL-3-treated group (Group III) somewhat mitigated these effects, with RBC levels increasing to 6.93 ± 0.10 × 106/μL and PLT levels increasing to 294 ± 1.5 × 103/μL compared with the free capecitabine-treated group, suggesting hematological recovery attributed to decreased systemic toxicity via lipid encapsulation. In the TZ-CNL-3-treated group (Group IV), RBCs (7.42 ± 0.04 × 106/μL), HGB (11.27 ± 0.16 g/dL), and PLTs (553 ± 1.08 × 103/μL) recovered significantly compared to the free capecitabine and CNL-3-treated groups. These values approached physiological ranges for healthy female NOD SCID mice, demonstrating improved haematological safety of the HER2-targeted formulation (TZ-CNL-3). Moreover, the percentages of neutrophils decreased from 71.3 ± 3.54% (Group II) to 51.4 ± 1.68% (Group IV), while the percentages of lymphocytes rose from 28.4 ± 3.2% to 48.6 ± 1.98%, demonstrating an immunological rebalancing. MCV, MCH, and MCHC remained within the reported physiological ranges for healthy female NOD SCID mice throughout the study, representing preservation of erythrocyte morphology and hemoglobinization. In contrast, RDW-CV reduced from 22.8 ± 0.8% (Group II) to 21.0 ± 0.5% (Group IV), showing enhanced erythrocyte appearance. Several hematological parameters in the TZ-CNL-3-treated group, such as WBC, MCV, MCH, and MCHC, fell within reported reference ranges. In the CNL-3/TZ-CNL-3-treated group, RBC, HGB, HCT, and PLT values improved markedly toward physiological levels compared with the free capecitabine-treated group. These findings suggest that CNL-3 and TZ-CNL-3 offer better haematological safety than free capecitabine treatment. TZ-CNL-3 shows the greatest restoration toward physiological ranges while preserving anticancer efficacy.51,62

Biochemical Parameters Analysis

This targeted nanodelivery approach provided clear therapeutic and safety benefits in mice treated with TZ-CNL-3. This was supported by the biochemical data (Table 8), which showed marked improvements in liver, kidney, and overall systemic parameters. Free capecitabine treatment (Group II) exhibited noticeable hepatotoxicity, reflected by elevated AST (76 ± 0.16 U/L), ALT (66 ± 0.35 U/L), and ALP (121 ± 1.45 U/L) levels. These animals also showed increased total bilirubin (0.46 ± 0.05 mg/dL) alongside reduced serum albumin (3.6 ± 0.2 g/dL), consistent with prior findings of hepatic stress associated with higher capecitabine doses.47,62 Nephrotoxicity was also found, as seen by higher blood urea (36 ± 2.54 mg/dL) and serum creatinine (0.35 ± 0.04 mg/dL) levels.63,64 Treatment with CNL-3 (Group III) minimized these adverse effects, significantly recovering AST (52 ± 1.15 U/L) and ALT (43 ± 0.14 U/L) levels, as well as renal function indicators, compared with the free capecitabine-treated group, nanoencapsulation reduced systemic toxicity. The TZ-CNL-3-treated group (Group IV) showed improved biochemical parameters, with AST (48 ± 1.11 U/L), ALT (41 ± 1.71 U/L), ALP (89 ± 2.17 U/L), urea (24 ± 0.62 mg/dL), and creatinine (0.23 ± 0.72 mg/dL) values indicated substantial recovery compared with both the free-capecitabine and CNL-3-treated groups and approaching the reported physiological ranges for healthy female NOD SCID mice. The recovery was further supported by the improved serum albumin (3.9 ± 0.1 g/dL) and total protein levels (6.3 ± 0.14 g/dL), suggesting that hepatic synthetic function was maintained. Electrolyte homeostasis (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca2⁺, Cl−) showed improvement following treatment with both CNL-3 and TZ-CNL-3. Free capecitabine increased cholesterol (159 ± 1.24 mg/dL), LDL (92 ± 3.63 mg/dL), and triglycerides (122 ± 4.7 mg/dL), indicating hepatic lipid dysregulation. The TZ-CNL-3-treated group showed a significant reduction in cholesterol (126 ± 2.17 mg/dL), LDL (70 ± 4.68 mg/dL), and triglycerides (83 ± 1.75 mg/dL), suggesting a potential hepatoprotective effect compared with free capecitabine treatment. Notably, both CNL-3 and TZ-CNL-3 treatment groups exhibited recovery of biochemical parameters toward the reported physiological ranges for healthy female NOD SCID mice. The TZ-CNL-3 group demonstrated the greatest improvement, with ALT, ALP, total bilirubin, albumin, total protein, blood urea, creatinine, and total cholesterol values falling within the reported reference ranges, while AST and certain electrolyte parameters showed marked recovery toward physiological levels compared with the free capecitabine-treated group. These results suggest that both CNL-3 and TZ-CNL-3 reduce biochemical alterations associated with systemic toxicity.50,51

Histopathological Analysis

Histopathological analysis provided crucial insights into the in vivo therapeutic efficacy and systemic safety of the formulated nanoliposomes, as illustrated in Figure 15. Tumour sections from the control group displayed characteristic structures of aggressive malignancy, such as hypercellularity, loss of glandular structure, pronounced pleomorphism, and large areas of necrosis. These observations are consistent with the rapid, uncontrolled tumour growth commonly seen in NOD SCID mice. The free capecitabine-treated group showed minor improvements in tissue architecture; however, areas of necrosis and inflammation infiltration were still evident, indicating only a partial therapeutic response with systemic toxicity. This observation aligns with the literature that capecitabine offers limited targeting efficiency.65 In contrast, tumours treated with CNL-3 exhibited reduced cellular density and only mild architectural variations, highlighting the nanoliposomes’ ability to improve drug delivery. Remarkably, tumours treated with TZ-CNL-3 showed the most pronounced therapeutic effect, with complete tissue architectural restoration, minimal necrotic areas, and fewer mitotic figures. These findings suggest effective TZ-mediated HER2-specific cytotoxicity, as previously reported by Jiaqun et al28 Analysis of systemic organs supported these findings. Yellow arrows in the lung sections demonstrated alveolar damage, inflammatory infiltration and thickening of the alveolar wall. Similarly, liver tissues indicate inflammatory alterations, sinusoidal dilation and hepatocellular deterioration. Likewise, in kidney sections, interstitial inflammation, tubular degeneration and glomerular injury were found. Control and capecitabine-treated mice exhibited these degenerations largely, whereas those treated with CNL-3 and TZ-CNL-3 largely maintained normal tissue structure across all major organs, including the liver, lungs and kidneys. Notably, mice treated with TZ-CNL-3 showed minimal signs of inflammation/degeneration in non-target organs, displaying its superior safety profile compared with free capecitabine and CNL-3 formulations.55,66 These findings align with emerging paradigms in nano-oncology, which emphasize targeted delivery systems to improve therapeutic effectiveness and reduce systemic off-target effects. Overall, these histopathological results confirm the dual benefit of TZ-CNL-3 treatment in reducing tumour burden more effectively and maintaining systemic organ health, key features of a clinically translatable nanomedicine. In addition, histopathological semi-quantitative scoring showed that the TZ-CNL-3-treated group had the lowest pathological scores among all treatments in breast tumour, lung, liver, and kidney tissues, indicating less tumour pathology and minimum treatment associated organ toxicity (Figure 16). When compared with the control, free capecitabine, and CNL-3 groups, TZ-CNL-3 displayed significantly lower histopathological scores in all tested tissues. Markedly decreased tissue injury scores in the breast tumor, lung, liver, and kidney (p < 0.01) indicate improved efficacy along with an enhanced safety profile, which is consistent with hematologic and biochemical results. This confirms that TZ-CNL-3 effectively suppressed tumour progression with minimal off-target tissue damage via targeted and controlled delivery of capecitabine.67

Recent advances in nanomedicine have emphasized the significance of rational nanocarrier design for enhancing anticancer efficacy and reducing systemic toxicity. Wang et al reported a tumor cell–targeting and tumor microenvironment–responsive nanoplatform with improved tumor uptake and superior therapeutic performance by integrating multimodal treatments.68 In addition to the above, Feng et al demonstrated that precise optimization of doxorubicin prodrug nanoassemblies improved nanoparticle stability, tumor accumulation, and antitumor efficacy69 Moreover, Yuan et al further showed that reduction-responsive epirubicin nanoassemblies greatly improved pharmacokinetic behavior, controlled drug release, and therapeutic performance in breast cancer models without causing severe side effects70 Consistent with such findings, our study indicated that nanoencapsulation enhanced therapeutic performance of capecitabine, with TZ-CNL-3 showing the best antitumor efficacy, sustained drug release, and favorable hematological and biochemical safety profiles relative to free capecitabine and non-targeted liposomes.

Compared with previously reported studies, TZ-CNLs that primarily encapsulate directly cytotoxic agents such as doxorubicin or idarubicin, the present formulation is distinguished by its incorporation of capecitabine, which is a tumor-activated prodrug. In contrast to anthracyclines, which exert immediate cytotoxic effects upon cellular entry and are associated with dose-limiting systemic and cardiotoxicities, capecitabine requires enzymatic conversion to 5-FU within the tumor microenvironment, providing a distinct mechanistic and potentially safer therapeutic approach. The combination of capecitabine with TZ-mediated HER2 targeting is therefore relevant, as receptor-mediated endocytosis may facilitate intracellular delivery while local enzymatic activation could further improve drug availability (Figure 17). Regarding the route of administration, the experimental TZ-CNLs are designed primarily for intravenous chemotherapy. Capecitabine is recommended in oral tablet dosage form with a relatively high dose (1000–1250 mg/m2, twice daily) with highly potent cytotoxicity. The present system aims to improve the therapeutic index of capecitabine through targeted nanocarrier-based delivery with a substantially lower dose. In silico ADMET analysis also suggests that capecitabine has low mutagenic and cardiotoxic risk and minimal CYP450 inhibition liability (SwissADME, pkCSM). These properties support its suitability for targeted nanoliposomal delivery in breast cancer therapy. Overall, these schemata have the potential to decrease off-target toxicity, reduce systemic exposure with a tolerance profile, thereby differentiating the current formulation from earlier TZ-nanoliposome platforms while also maintaining their translational relevance for HER2-positive breast cancer therapy.

HER2-Targeted delivery and tumor-selective activation of capecitabine via trastuzumab-conjugated-nanoliposomes.

The current TZ-CNLs loaded with capecitabine have a significant therapeutic advantage compared to the previous research on TZ-functionalized nanoliposomes containing cytotoxic drugs, because of the combination of tumor-specific enzyme activation and receptor-mediated delivery mechanism. Unlike the previous approaches using anthracycline-based drugs that often pose a risk of systemic toxicity and cardiotoxicity, the prodrug effect of capecitabine ensures conversion to 5-FU in the tumor environment.

The substantial improvement seen in the hematologic, hepatic, and renal parameters in the TZ-CNL-3 group reveals the protective effect gained through targeted nanoliposomal delivery of the drug. It is evident that the role played by the antibody-mediated internalization process in improving intratumoral concentration also improves the cellular uptake of capecitabine to be activated by the enzyme in the tumor tissue, including thymidine phosphorylase. Therefore, the therapeutic benefit gained from this study results from a synergistic effect of targeted delivery, controlled release, and enzyme activation in tumor tissue.

Although this present study showed favorable therapeutic efficacy and safety of TZ-CNL-3, the potential immunogenicity and in vivo stability of TZ-functionalized nanoliposomes need to be addressed. TZ is a humanized monoclonal antibody (mAb), widely used for the treatment of breast cancer patients, generally associated with low immunogenicity and systemic toxicity; however, repeated administration of antibody-conjugated nanocarriers might elicit immune responses that could impact circulation time, biodistribution, and therapeutic performance. Furthermore, preserving the stability of the antibody–liposome conjugate in vivo is crucial for maintaining its HER2-targeting ability and avoiding premature shedding of surface-tethered TZ. The physicochemical properties of TZ-CNL-3, including its particle size, zeta potential, and prolonged drug release profile, suggest good formulation stability. However, additional studies examining serum stability, antibody integrity, complement activation, and long-term immunogenicity are needed to advance the preclinical development of this targeted nanoliposomal system.

In addition to its promising therapeutic efficacy, there are also some important issues regarding clinical translation of TZ-CNL-3. While the success of marketed liposomal anticancer formulations indicates the potential for large scale manufacturing and regulatory approval of nanoliposomal drug delivery systems, the scalability of TZ-functionalized nanoliposomes needs to be optimized for reliable production and batch-to-batch consistency. Moreover, extensive storage stability studies must be conducted to assess long term retention of particle size, drug loading, antibody integrity, as well as targeting capability, under recommended storage conditions. From a regulatory point of view, a thorough assessment on product quality, standardisation of process parameters, environmental and long term toxicity, in vitro-in vivo correlation (IVIVC) will be required for regulatory approval.

Limitations of the Study

In spite of promising in vivo effectiveness results for TZ-CNL-3 in breast tumor-induced xenograft mice, the study holds some potential limitations. The study needs an in vitro-in vivo correlation (IVIVC) analysis to substantiate efficacy data. Further, protein expression analysis, biodistribution studies, and detailed mechanistic investigations need to be conducted to rationalize the clinical suitability of TZ-CNL-3 in breast cancer. In vitro studies such as ELISA and flow cytometry are essential to assess HER2-specific binding, antibody-receptor interaction strength, and cellular internalization of the TZ-CNL-3. Future studies will pursue these investigations to fully evaluate the targeting efficiency and therapeutic potential of the developed nanoliposomal system.

Conclusion

The study reported a facile yet novel targeted strategy of capecitabine for improved treatment of breast cancer through TZ-mediated HER-2 targeting. Experimental CNL-3 exhibited desirable physicochemical characteristics in terms of size, morphology, texture, and drug encapsulation capacity, which appear suitable for in vivo applications. TZ-conjugation induced moderate changes in particle size and surface morphology but maintained the spherical structure and drug-loading of the nanoliposomes. This supports the optimized selection of formulation and process parameters. Considerable suppression in the breast tumor volume and weight was observed in the TZ-CNL-3 treated group as compared to the free capecitabine treated group. The improvement in the plasma pharmacokinetic profile of TZ-CNL-3 over CNL-3 and free capecitabine further rationalized the in vivo efficacy results. Further, biochemical parameters in breast tumor-bearing SCID mice were also recovered towards normal following TZ-CNL-3 treatment. However, in the control group and the free capecitabine-treated group, significant alterations in key biochemical parameters were observed. TZ-CNL-3 group preserved tissue integrity across the tested organs, suggesting minimal systemic toxicity and favourable biosafety of the experimental nanoliposome compared to the free drug. Clearly, the outcome of the study indicated that nanoencapsulation and TZ-conjugation improved the therapeutic efficacy of capecitabine. The results of the current study provide supportive evidence for the further development of TZ-CNL-3 through IND-enabling studies in pharmacokinetics and toxicity, as well as scale-up studies, before progressing to patient testing in HER2-positive breast cancer. However, additional research on long-term storage stability, manufacturing scalability, immunogenicity, and regulatory issues is necessary to facilitate the clinical translation of TZ-CNL-3 for HER2-positive breast cancer therapy.
