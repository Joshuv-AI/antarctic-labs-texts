Breastfeeding status, micronutrient biomarkers, thyroid function, and Bayley-III developmental outcomes in Taiwanese toddlers
Authors: Shu-Chi Mu, Sing-Chung Li, Yi-Fang Liu, Yi-Ling Chen, Li-Yi Tsai, Yu-Hsuan Chien, Huei-Ting Kuo, Zi-Ying Chen, Chiao-Ming Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreastfeeding is generally associated with favorable neurodevelopmental outcomes, but toddlers who continue receiving breast milk may remain at risk of micronutrient inadequacy when complementary feeding is insufficient. This study examined associations among breastfeeding status at assessment, micronutrient biomarkers, thyroid function, and Bayley-III developmental outcomes in Taiwanese toddlers.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted at Shin Kong Wu Ho-Su Memorial Hospital, Taipei, Taiwan, between February 2019 and December 2020. Among 577 eligible toddlers aged 12–24 months screened during routine vaccination visits, 112 completed the study protocol. After excluding two toddlers with abnormal thyroid-stimulating hormone concentrations, 110 were included in the final analysis. Breastfeeding status was classified as breastfeeding at assessment (BFA; receiving any breast milk, n = 27) or not breastfeeding at assessment (NBFA; no longer receiving breast milk, n = 83). Laboratory measures included hemoglobin, ferritin, serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D3, urinary iodine concentration, thyroid-stimulating hormone, free triiodothyronine, and free thyroxine. Neurodevelopment was assessed using the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, Third Edition. Associations between breastfeeding status and developmental scores were examined using hierarchical multivariable linear regression.ResultsToddlers in the BFA group had lower ferritin concentrations than those in the NBFA group and a higher frequency of iron deficiency (6/27, 22.22% vs. 4/83, 4.82%). Vitamin D insufficiency was common overall and did not differ clearly between groups. Thyroid profiles were within age-appropriate reference ranges; however, free thyroxine was higher in the BFA group than in the NBFA group. In unadjusted linear regression, BFA was associated with higher cognitive scores (unstandardized regression coefficient [B] = 4.95, 95% CI 0.06 to 9.83) and language scores (B = 6.94, 95% CI 0.77 to 13.11), but not motor scores (B = 2.37, 95% CI -1.35 to 6.09). After adjustment for child age, sex, maternal age, and maternal education, the associations remained for cognition (B = 5.21, 95% CI 0.27 to 10.16) and language (B = 7.13, 95% CI 1.25 to 13.01). Free thyroxine was positively associated with Bayley-III cognitive, language, and motor composite scores.ConclusionAmong Taiwanese toddlers aged 12–24 months, breastfeeding at assessment was associated with lower ferritin concentrations, a higher frequency of iron deficiency, and higher cognitive and language Bayley-III composite scores after adjustment for child and maternal characteristics. Free thyroxine may represent a potential biological pathway linking breastfeeding status with early developmental outcomes, although larger longitudinal studies are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13006-026-00863-4.

Background

Breastfeeding provides extensive benefits for infants, mothers, and society. Human milk offers the ideal balance of nutrients and bioactive compounds essential for growth and immunity, protecting against respiratory and gastrointestinal infections, otitis media, and sudden infant death syndrome. It is also linked to lower risks of obesity, type 2 diabetes, asthma, and cardiovascular disease later in life, and supports neurocognitive development [1, 2]. For mothers, breastfeeding promotes uterine recovery, reduces postpartum hemorrhage, and lowers long-term risks of breast and ovarian cancers, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease [3]. It also strengthens maternal–infant bonding and may reduce postpartum depression. Societally, breastfeeding decreases healthcare costs, reduces reliance on formula, and supports environmental sustainability [4].

Exclusive breastfeeding is recommended with vitamin D supplements from birth [5]. However, when breast milk continues to serve as the primary source of nutrition beyond six months without the timely introduction of appropriate complementary foods, infants may become vulnerable to specific nutrient deficiencies. Adequate micronutrient intake during early childhood is critical for optimal brain development, and deficiencies in key nutrients have been associated with delayed cognitive development and potentially lasting adverse effects on learning and neurodevelopment. In our previous work, we found that breastfeeding during the first year of life was associated with an increased risk of iron and vitamin D deficiency [17, 18]. Taiwanese cohort data further indicate that vitamin D insufficiency is highly prevalent and persists into the toddler period; moreover, when complementary feeding may not fully meet iron requirements, breastfed children appear to be at higher risk of iron deficiency [19]. In addition, Taiwan was historically an iodine-deficient area. Mandatory salt iodization, introduced in 1967, substantially improved iodine nutritional status and reduced the prevalence of goiter in the population. However, after the salt iodization policy changed from mandatory to voluntary in 2003, iodine status in Taiwan began to vary across populations of different ages and physiological conditions, ranging from borderline sufficiency to mild deficiency [20–23]. Although iodine plays a crucial role in thyroid hormone synthesis and the thyroid axis is central to early neurodevelopment, routine surveillance of urinary iodine concentration in Taiwanese children aged 1–2 years is currently limited. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the associations among breastfeeding status, nutritional biomarkers (iron status, vitamin D–calcium metabolism, and the iodine–thyroid axis), and cognitive development in Taiwanese toddlers.

Materials and methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted in the Department of Pediatrics at Shin Kong Hospital between February 2019 and December 2020. Toddlers aged 12–24 months who attended routine vaccination visits were recruited. Maternal characteristics, including age, education level, employment status, and breastfeeding status; child characteristics, including age, sex, gestational age at birth, and birth weight; anthropometric measurements; and blood and urine samples were collected to assess growth, biochemical markers, and nutritional status. Baseline data and specimens were collected during the same study visit. To reduce discomfort and psychological stress, the Bayley-III (the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, Third Edition) developmental assessment was scheduled separately, usually approximately one week later, at a time convenient for the family.

Participant recruitment

Participants were recruited from children aged 12–24 months presenting for routine vaccination visits at the well-child clinic of Shin Kong Hospital. Children were eligible if they were judged to be healthy by a physician at the time of recruitment. Exclusion criteria included a history of prematurity (gestational age < 37 weeks), congenital anomalies or metabolic disorders, previously diagnosed gastrointestinal, cardiac, hepatic, or renal diseases, genetic disorders, and any acute infectious illness within the 1 month before enrollment. All potential participants’ legal guardians were informed of the study protocol by a physician or research assistant, and written informed consent was obtained prior to enrollment. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shin Kong Wu Ho-Su Memorial Hospital and conducted in accordance with the International Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects and the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Shin Kong Wu Ho-Su Memorial Hospital (IRB No. 20180901R) on November 8, 2018. At the study visit, toddlers were categorized into two groups according to breastfeeding status at the time of assessment: breastfeeding at assessment (BFA), defined as receiving any breast milk at the evaluation visit, and not breastfeeding at assessment (NBFA), defined as no longer receiving breast milk at the evaluation visit. Breastfeeding status was reported by the parent or guardian at the assessment visit.

Anthropometric measurements

Anthropometric measurements were performed using a hospital-calibrated stadiometer and scale to assess height and weight. Head circumference was measured with a non-stretchable plastic measuring tape, placed above the eyebrows and around the most prominent part of the occiput. All measurements were converted into percentiles based on the Taiwan Child Growth Standards issued by the Ministry of Health and Welfare [24].

Blood sample collection

A total of 3 mL of venous blood was collected primarily from the antecubital veins, including the cephalic or basilic vein. If the antecubital veins were not suitable for venipuncture, the dorsal hand veins were used as an alternative site. All blood samples were obtained by personnel who had received professional training in pediatric venipuncture. Collected blood samples were transferred into tubes containing EDTA and into tubes without anticoagulant. Serum was subsequently separated from the coagulated blood by centrifugation for biochemical analysis.

Urine sample collection

Approximately 3 mL of spot urine was collected during the clinic visit for the measurement of urinary iodine concentration (UIC). Samples were obtained using sterile pediatric urine collection bags or clean-catch methods, depending on the child’s age and ability. Immediately after collection, urine samples were aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C until laboratory analysis.

Biochemical measurements

Samples were delivered to Chung-Yi Medical Laboratory on the day of collection or the following day. Complete blood count was measured using an automated hematology analyzer (XN10, Sysmex, Japan). Serum 25(OH)D3, parathyroid hormone, ferritin, TSH, FT3, and FT4 were measured using automated immunoassays (Roche Cobas 6000, Germany), and serum calcium was measured using a colorimetric assay (Roche Cobas 8000, Germany). All analytical instruments used in this study were CE-IVD certified. Assay precision was assessed using intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation, and all values were within acceptable analytical ranges.

For urinary analysis, urinary iodine concentration (UIC) using the method recommended by the EQUIP (Ensuring the Quality of Urinary Iodine Procedures) program of the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). UIC was analyzed with the modified ammonium persulfate digestion on microplate method (APDM), followed by the Sandell–Kolthoff reaction, with both intra- and inter-assay CVs < 5%.

Cognitive development assessment

Cognitive development was assessed using the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, Third Edition (Bayley-III). The Bayley-III is an internationally standardized instrument for evaluating cognitive, language, and motor development in children aged 1–42 months and is widely used in both clinical and research settings [25]. The Chinese version of the Bayley-III has undergone cultural adaptation and psychometric evaluation, with satisfactory reliability and validity reported for developmental assessment in both clinical and research settings. In Taiwanese term and preterm infants, the Bayley-III has demonstrated excellent intra-rater reliability, with ICCs ranging from 0.92 to 0.99 for the cognitive scale, 0.88 to 0.98 for the language scale, and 0.85 to 0.98 for the motor scale. It has also shown good-to-excellent inter-rater reliability, with ICCs ranging from 0.87 to 0.97, 0.76 to 0.95, and 0.77 to 0.89 for the cognitive, language, and motor scales, respectively, together with small standard errors of measurement (SEM) [26]. Assessments were administered by a licensed clinical psychologist and included the Cognitive, Receptive Communication, Expressive Communication, Fine Motor, and Gross Motor subtests. Bayley-III cognitive, language, and motor composite scores were calculated according to the standardized scoring procedures in the Bayley-III manual. These composite scores are norm-referenced standard scores with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15. Percentile ranks were also derived from the standardized scoring system [27]. For descriptive and supplementary categorical analyses, composite scores were classified as above average (≥ 110), average (90–109), and below average (< 90), based on the standard descriptive classification of Bayley-III scores [28].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 18.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). In the original dataset, maternal age, maternal education level, maternal working status, and toddler sex were treated as categorical variables, whereas toddler age, birth weight, gestational age at birth, anthropometric measurements, biochemical indicators, and Bayley-III developmental scores were treated as continuous variables. Biochemical indicators were additionally converted into categorical variables according to predefined sufficiency or deficiency criteria.

Data distribution was assessed using histograms, normal Q–Q plots, and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the independent Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables are reported as median and interquartile range and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages [n (%)], and between-group differences were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, or Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test, as appropriate. Correlations between thyroid hormone concentrations and Bayley-III composite scores were examined using Pearson correlation analysis.

Bayley-III composite scores were used as continuous outcomes in the main analyses, including between-group comparisons, correlation analyses, hierarchical multivariable linear regression, and exploratory mediation analyses. Categorized Bayley-III outcomes were used only in supplementary categorical analyses.

To identify factors associated with child developmental outcomes, hierarchical multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted separately for each Bayley-III domain: cognitive, language, and motor. Breastfeeding status was the primary exposure and was coded as 1 for breastfeeding at assessment (BFA) and 0 for not breastfeeding at assessment (NBFA). Model 1 included breastfeeding status only and was unadjusted. Model 2 additionally adjusted for child-level covariates, including age at assessment and sex. Model 3 further adjusted for maternal age and maternal education level. Multicollinearity was evaluated using tolerance and variance inflation factors, and no evidence of problematic multicollinearity was observed.

Because thyroid function and nutritional status may represent possible biological pathways linking breastfeeding status with developmental outcomes [29], exploratory mediation analyses were performed for hemoglobin, 25(OH)D3, FT4, and FT3. Separate single-mediator models were constructed for each biomarker and each Bayley-III domain. In these models, breastfeeding status was specified as the independent variable, Bayley-III domain-specific composite score as the dependent variable, and each biomarker as a potential mediator. All mediation models were adjusted for child sex, age at assessment, maternal age, and maternal education level. Total, direct, and indirect effects were estimated using regression-based mediation analysis. Indirect effects were assessed using 5,000 bootstrap resamples, and mediation was considered statistically significant when the 95% bootstrap confidence interval did not include zero [30].

Results

Study flow

The study flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. A total of 722 toddlers aged 12–24 months attending routine vaccination visits at the Department of Pediatrics, Shin Kong Wu Ho-Su Memorial Hospital, were screened for eligibility. After exclusion of 145 children who did not meet the inclusion criteria, 577 were eligible. Among these eligible children, 427 declined to participate and 150 were enrolled. During the study process, 38 participants were subsequently excluded for the following reasons: withdrawal of consent (n = 33), failed blood sampling (n = 1), refusal to undergo cognitive assessment (n = 1), failed urine collection (n = 2), or incomplete dietary data (n = 1). Thus, 112 participants completed the study protocol. Of these, 2 were further excluded because of elevated TSH concentrations (> 10 µIU/mL), in order to minimize potential confounding by overt thyroid dysfunction. The final analytic sample therefore comprised 110 toddlers, including 27 in the breastfeeding at assessment (BFA) group and 83 in the not breastfeeding at assessment (NBFA) group.

Characteristics of mothers and toddlers

Baseline maternal and child characteristics according to breastfeeding at assessment (BFA) and not breastfeeding at assessment (NBFA) status are summarized in Table 2. Maternal age distribution was comparable between groups, with most mothers aged ≥ 30 years in both groups. Maternal education level was also comparable, with most mothers having a college education or above. However, maternal working status differed between groups, with a higher proportion of homemakers in the BFA group and a higher proportion of employed mothers in the NBFA group. Among toddlers, chronological age, sex distribution, birth weight, and gestational age at birth were similar between groups. Anthropometric measures including height, weight, BMI, and height- and weight-for-age percentiles did not differ significantly between the BFA and NBFA groups. In contrast, head circumference was smaller in the BFA group than in the NBFA group, and this difference was also reflected in head circumference percentile.

The results of cognitive development of toddlers was presented in Table 5. Overall, the results indicate a consistent trend toward more favorable developmental classifications among toddlers breastfeeding at assessment, although the study was underpowered to confirm significant between-group differences.

To further explore possible biological pathways linking breastfeeding at assessment with Bayley-III composite scores, mediation analyses were conducted for hemoglobin, 25(OH)D3, FT4, and FT3 (Supplementary Table S2). For cognitive and language outcomes, none of the biomarkers showed significant indirect effects because all bootstrap 95% confidence intervals included zero. FT4 showed the most notable pattern, with significant a and b paths for both cognitive and language scores, but the corresponding indirect effects were not statistically significant. For motor outcome, however, FT4 showed a significant indirect effect (indirect effect = 2.499, 95% CI: 0.838 to 4.744), whereas no significant indirect effects were found for Hb, 25(OH)D3, or FT3. These results suggest that FT4 may be involved in the pathway between breastfeeding and developmental outcomes, particularly motor development.

Discussion

Principal findings

In this cross-sectional study of 110 Taiwanese toddlers aged 12–24 months, we examined whether breastfeeding status at assessment (BFA vs. NBFA) was associated with micronutrient biomarkers, thyroid function, and Bayley-III developmental performance. Toddlers in the BFA group had significantly lower ferritin concentrations and a higher prevalence of iron deficiency than those in the NBFA group, whereas vitamin D status and urinary iodine concentration did not differ significantly between groups; all thyroid indices were within age-appropriate reference ranges, but FT4 concentrations were higher in the BFA group. Developmentally, the BFA group showed higher cognitive and language composite scores in unadjusted comparisons, and these positive associations persisted after stepwise adjustment for child and maternal sociodemographic factors (Models 1–3); however, the breastfeeding–development associations were attenuated and no longer statistically significant after additional adjustment for biochemical markers (FT4, FT3, 25(OH)D₃, and hemoglobin; Model 4). In contrast, FT4 showed consistent positive associations with cognitive, language, and motor composite scores, suggesting that thyroid-related variation within the reference range may be more closely linked to neurodevelopmental performance than breastfeeding status per se in this cohort.

Breastfeeding and iron status

In our study, toddlers who were breastfeeding at assessment had significantly lower ferritin concentrations and a higher prevalence of iron deficiency than those who were no longer breastfeeding, consistent with evidence that breast milk alone may not meet iron requirements beyond 6 months without timely, iron-rich complementary feeding [33–35]. Although iron deficiency has been linked to adverse neurodevelopment through disrupted myelination and altered neurotransmitter pathways [36, 37], We did not observe statistically significant differences in Bayley-III composite scores when toddlers were stratified by ferritin status (< 12 vs. ≥12 µg/L). Cognitive scores were 110.00 (105.00–110.00) in the ferritin < 12 µg/L group and 105.00 (100.00–115.00) in the ferritin ≥ 12 µg/L group; language scores were 106.00 (94.75–118.00) vs. 100.00 (91.00–112.00); and motor scores were 106.50 (100.00–110.00) vs. 107.00 (100.00–110.00), respectively. These null findings should be interpreted cautiously because the number of toddlers with ferritin < 12 µg/L was small (n = 10), limiting statistical power. Moreover, most children did not have severe anemia, which may be more likely to produce detectable developmental deficits. Taken together, while iron deficiency remains an important concern in early childhood, our results suggest that mild or early-stage deficiency in this cohort may not have been sufficient to produce measurable differences in Bayley-III outcomes at the time of assessment.

Vitamin D status and neurodevelopment

Evidence on early-life vitamin D status and neurodevelopment remains inconsistent, likely because studies differ in exposure window, population characteristics, baseline vitamin D status, and developmental measures used. Some observational studies have suggested associations between prenatal, cord-blood, or toddler vitamin D status and later neurodevelopment [38–40], whereas intervention studies have generally reported limited or inconsistent benefits of vitamin D supplementation on developmental outcomes [41, 42]. In our cohort, vitamin D insufficiency or deficiency was observed in 70 of 110 toddlers (63.64%), indicating that suboptimal vitamin D status is common in early childhood. Vitamin D concentrations also did not differ clearly according to breastfeeding status at assessment. Therefore, continued monitoring of vitamin D status in early childhood may still be warranted.

Iodine status, thyroid hormones, and neurodevelopment

Chronic iodine deficiency can impair thyroid hormone synthesis and, when sufficiently severe, lead to elevated TSH and reduced circulating T4/FT4 [43]. However, in this cohort of generally healthy toddlers aged 1–2 years, thyroid function indices (TSH, FT3, and FT4) were all within age-appropriate reference ranges, suggesting that any influence of iodine status on thyroid function was limited. In addition, previous NAHSIT surveys have mainly reported urinary iodine concentration (UIC) for individuals aged ≥ 6 years, and population-based data for Taiwanese toddlers remain scarce. In our study, the median spot UIC exceeded 100 µg/L, consistent with overall adequate iodine intake at the population level under WHO criteria [32].

Thyroid hormones are essential for early brain development, and FT4 in particular is important because it serves as the substrate for local conversion to biologically active FT3 in neural tissues [44]. In our study, FT4—but not FT3—was positively associated with cognitive, language, and motor composite scores, supporting the possibility that FT4 may be a relatively sensitive marker of neurodevelopmental integrity even within the reference range [45]. Although toddlers who were breastfeeding at assessment had slightly higher FT4 concentrations, available evidence suggests that thyroid hormone transfer through breast milk is minimal [46–48]. Therefore, the higher FT4 observed in our study is more likely to reflect endogenous thyroid activity than direct hormone transfer through breast milk.

Breastfeeding and developmental outcomes

Previous cohort studies have generally reported positive associations between longer breastfeeding duration and child cognitive or developmental outcomes [49–52]. In addition, a recent Brazilian cohort study showed that exclusive breastfeeding at 6 months was associated with higher Bayley-III cognitive and language scores, as well as a higher Bayley Global Score at 12 months; breastfeeding also appeared to buffer the adverse association between lower maternal education and early developmental outcomes, suggesting a potential protective effect under socioeconomic disadvantage [53]. Proposed mechanisms include not only nutrient provision but also enhanced mother–infant interaction [54]. In our study, breastfeeding at assessment (BFA) was positively associated with Bayley-III cognitive scores in the unadjusted model and after adjustment for child demographic factors and maternal socioeconomic variables (Models 1–3), which is broadly consistent with previous reports. Exploratory mediation analyses further showed that, among the biomarkers examined, only FT4 demonstrated a statistically significant indirect effect for the motor outcome. However, because both the total effect and the direct effect of breastfeeding at assessment on motor score were not statistically significant, this finding should be interpreted with considerable caution.

Although the Bayley-III is widely used to assess early cognitive, language, and motor functioning, its predictive validity for later childhood outcomes is variable and depends on the population and follow-up period. Some studies in high-risk infants have shown moderate to strong short-term associations with later cognitive performance [55]. but longer-term prediction to school age appears less reliable [56, 57]. Therefore, Bayley-III should be interpreted primarily as a standardized measure of current developmental status rather than a definitive predictor of long-term cognitive or academic outcomes. Longitudinal follow-up is needed to determine whether the biomarker-development associations observed in toddlerhood persist into later childhood.

From a clinical and public health standpoint, our results support the promotion of continued breastfeeding while underscoring the value of monitoring thyroid-related biomarkers as part of early-life developmental surveillance. At the same time, potential nutritional trade-offs should be considered. Consistent with prior evidence, prolonged breastfeeding may increase vulnerability to iron deficiency and iron-deficiency anemia if complementary feeding is inadequate; therefore, clinicians should encourage continued breastfeeding alongside routine assessment of iron status. Finally, the high prevalence of vitamin D insufficiency in our cohort—regardless of breastfeeding status—highlights an additional and widespread micronutrient concern in early childhood that warrants proactive attention through appropriate dietary counseling and supplementation strategies when indicated.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design allowed the assessment of associations among breastfeeding status, nutritional and endocrine biomarkers, and developmental performance; however, it precludes causal inference and does not establish the temporal sequence of these relationships. In addition, breastfeeding exposure was classified according to breastfeeding status at the time of assessment (BFA vs. NBFA), rather than breastfeeding duration or cumulative exposure. Detailed information regarding breastfeeding frequency, daily breast-milk volume, mixed feeding with formula, breastfeeding exclusivity, and the exact age at weaning was not collected. Consequently, exposure misclassification may have occurred, dose–response relationships could not be evaluated, and the observed associations should therefore be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Cognitive and developmental outcomes were assessed using the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, Third Edition (Bayley-III), because it was the only version with available reliability and validity data for Chinese populations when the study was initiated. Although Bayley-IV is shorter and may provide improved clinical sensitivity and accuracy, corresponding psychometric validation data for its use in Taiwan have not yet been established. Furthermore, the BFA group was relatively small [27/110 (24.55%)] compared with the NBFA group [83/110 (75.45%)], resulting in an imbalanced group ratio of approximately 1:3, which may have limited statistical power, particularly in adjusted and exploratory analyses. Residual confounding also remains possible because several important potential confounders, including the home stimulation environment, parental intelligence, psychosocial factors, sunlight exposure, supplement use, and comprehensive dietary intake, could not be fully accounted for. Detailed nutrient intake estimation was further constrained by limitations of the Taiwanese food composition database, which lacks complete vitamin D and iodine values for many foods. Moreover, because breastfeeding in this cohort predominantly involved direct breastfeeding, information on breast-milk volume and feeding frequency was unavailable; therefore, total nutrient intake may have been underestimated when calculations were based primarily on complementary foods. Urinary iodine concentration reflects recent iodine intake and is primarily recommended for population-level assessment, and thus iodine-related findings should be interpreted cautiously at the individual level. Additionally, multiple comparisons were performed across several developmental, nutritional, and biochemical outcomes, increasing the possibility of type I error; accordingly, findings from exploratory and supplementary analyses should be interpreted with caution. Finally, recruiting healthy toddlers for studies involving venipuncture is inherently challenging and may have introduced volunteer selection bias, as participation may have been more likely among mothers who were older or more highly educated, as previously noted by Jordan et al. [58]. Because participants were recruited from a single medical center and recruitment was affected by the COVID-19 pandemic, the representativeness and generalizability of the findings may also be limited.

Conclusions

Among Taiwanese toddlers aged 12–24 months, breastfeeding at assessment was associated with lower ferritin concentrations and a higher prevalence of iron deficiency, whereas vitamin D status and urinary iodine concentration were broadly similar between groups. Breastfeeding at assessment was also associated with higher cognitive and language Bayley-III composite scores after adjustment for child and maternal characteristics. FT4 was positively associated with cognitive, language, and motor composite scores, and exploratory mediation analyses suggested a possible pathway linking breastfeeding status with motor development. However, because the total and direct effects for motor outcome were not statistically significant, these mediation findings should be interpreted cautiously. Larger longitudinal studies with detailed breastfeeding exposure, complementary feeding data, and repeated measurements of biomarkers and developmental outcomes are needed to clarify temporality and underlying mechanisms.
