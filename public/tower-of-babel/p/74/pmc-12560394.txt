Risk factors predicting the reinfection in periprosthetic joint infection with sinus tracts following prosthesis removal and antibiotic bone cement spacer implantation: a case control study
Authors: Jiaming Zeng, Jiayi Zhu, Bo Peng, Chen Zhang, Yirong Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe sinus tract has been demonstrated to be a factor affecting the efficacy of periprosthetic joint infection. Reinfection after prosthesis removal and antibiotic bone cement spacer implantation is one of the reasons for the failure of two-stage arthroplasty. The purpose of this study was to investigate the risk factors predicting the reinfection in periprosthetic joint infection with sinus tracts following prosthesis removal and antibiotic bone cement spacer implantation.MethodsClinical data of 35 periprosthetic joint infection patients with sinus tract from February 2003 to December 2021 in our hospital were retrospectively analyzed. Follow-up was discontinued either (1) when patients developed reinfection before completing the second stage of the two-stage revision or (2) when patients were free of infection 2-years postoperatively, regardless of whether they had completed the two-stage revision. The sinus tract is defined as an abnormal channel with evidence of communication to the joint or visualization of the prosthesis. Postoperative reinfection was defined as meeting the diagnostic criteria for periprosthetic joint infection, including requiring hospitalization for intravenous antibiotics or surgeries to prevent infection. The Multifactor Cox proportional risk model was used to analyze the risk factors.ResultsOf the 35 patients, 14 (40.0%) of periprosthetic joint infection patients with one or more sinus tracts developed reinfection after removal of prostheses and implantation of antibiotic bone cement spacers. Multivariate analysis showed that age (p = 0.044), C-reactive protein (p = 0.023) and uric acid (p = 0.025) were independent risk factors.ConclusionWe found the following factors associated with reinfection in periprosthetic joint infection with sinus tracts following prosthesis removal and antibiotic bone cement spacer implantation: age, C-reactive protein and uric acid.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11849-8.Keywords: Arthroplasty, Periprosthetic joint infection, Sinus tract, Antibiotic bone cement spacer, Risk factor

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is a serious complication that continues to be observed following total joint arthroplasty (TJA). The presence of a sinus tract with evidence of communication to the joint or visualization of the prosthesis can be diagnosed as PJI [1].

According to international consensus, PJI should be treated as early as possible with surgery, which usually includes debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR), single-stage revision, and two-stage revision [2]. Two-stage revision has been regarded as the gold standard for PJI treatment in the United States due to its high surgical success rate and infection eradication rate [3]. The treatment process is to remove all replaceable parts during the first stage of surgery, then complete debridement, and implant an antibiotic spacer. After infection control, the antibiotic loaded cement spacer will be removed and a new prosthesis will be implanted.

At present, several studies have concluded that patients with one or more sinus tracts have a higher failure rate after two-stage revision [4, 5]. But the outcome index of these studies is the success rate of two-stage exchange arthroplasty.

Prosthesis removal and antibiotic bone cement spacer implantation are the key factors for successful two-stage revision, but patients are prone to recurrent infection during this treatment. Risk factors affecting the efficacy of this procedure have been reported [6–8], including preoperative C-reactive protein (CRP), Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate/C-reactive protein (ESR/CRP), presence of a sinus tract, and history of previous revision surgery. However, few studies have analyzed how to improve the eradication rate of the first stage in two-stage revision. Therefore, this study aimed to identify the risk factors for prosthesis removal and antibiotic bone cement spacer implantation in PJI patients with one or more sinus tracts, hoping to improve the success rate of treatment.

Methods

The presence of sinus tracts in PJI patients was determined by a manual review of admission, intraoperative, and outpatient records. It was defined as an abnormal channel with evidence of communication to the joint or visualization of the prosthesis. If the wound has multiple epithelialized cavities with passages connecting them, but the cavities are found to be disconnected from the prosthesis, they are not considered sinus tracts. A split incision due to trauma is also not considered a sinus tract.

Diagnostic criteria for periprosthetic Joint infection

The diagnosis of PJI is based on the diagnostic criteria of Muscular Skeletal Infection Society (MSIS) (2018 edition) [1]. Two positive cultures of the same organism or visible evidence of the sinus tract or prostheses that communicate with the joint can be diagnosed as PJI. General information of the participants

This study is a retrospective study, and informed consent will not be signed with eligible patients after obtaining the consent of the ethics committee. (The ethics approval number is K-2024-032). PJI patients with one or more sinus tracts who completed the first stage of two-stage revision at the first affiliated hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine from February 2003 to December 2021 were included in this study.

Study method

According to previous literatures [9–11], this study collected preoperative hematological indicators that may lead to PJI treatment failure from the case system, including: White Blood Cell (WBC), ESR, CRP, Platelet (PLT), Fibrinogen (FIB), Uric Acid (UA), Albumin (ALB), Globulin (GLO), and Albumin/Globulin (A/G).

The patients were selected into infected and non-infected groups based on whether they developed reinfection postoperatively. The non-reinfection group included patients who did not develop reinfections within 2 years of postoperative follow-up. Referring to international multidisciplinary consensus on the successful treatment of PJI [12] and guidelines for reporting of outcomes after surgical treatment of PJI from MSIS [13], the reinfection group was defined as cases in which the initial infection was not effectively controlled, or in which there was a mixed infection with the formation of a new pathogen that eventually developed into the reinfection. The diagnosis of reinfection in patients was strictly according to the diagnostic criteria of MSIS (2018 edition) for PJI. It should be emphasized that the follow-up was discontinued either (1) when patients developed reinfection before completing the second stage of the two-stage revision or (2) when patients were free of infection 2-years postoperatively, regardless of whether they had completed the two-stage revision.

Surgical process

Stage I: The sinus tracts were removed, the original prosthesis was safely removed, and the inflammatory necrotic tissue and secretions around the prosthesis were thoroughly cleaned. Tissue samples in at least three different locations in the joint were taken for intraoperative frozen section analysis, and then an antibiotic-loaded cement spacer was implanted. Each pack of 40 g bone cement was prepared with 4 g vancomycin. The use of calcium sulfate was determined according to the results of preoperative etiological culture and intraoperative rapid freezing pathological examination. 5 cc calcium sulfate is often mixed with 2 g vancomycin. Perioperative antibiotic administration is performed under the guidance of an experienced pharmacologist. Postoperatively, according to the results of the antimicrobial susceptibility test, sensitive antibiotics should be administered intravenously for more than two weeks, followed by oral sensitive antibiotics for > 4 weeks. Patients with negative culture susceptibility tests were given vancomycin intravenously for more than two weeks, followed by oral antibiotics for > 4 weeks under the guidance of a pharmacological expert. Anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of the right knee joint after spacer implantation (A); Anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of the right hip joint after spacer implantation (B)

Stage II: Outpatient review was performed 1, 3, and 6 months after surgery. Clinical symptoms and serological parameters (including WBC, CRP, ESR and D-dimer) were observed to determine whether the infection was controlled and whether oral antibiotics could be discontinued. According to the symptoms and signs of patients and the trend of decreasing infection index after the discontinuation of oral antibiotics, combined with the patients’ wishes, the decision was made whether to implant new prostheses.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 25.0 statistical software (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp. USA) was used to analyze the data. The measurement data of normal distribution were expressed as mean (X) ± standard deviation (SD), and the two groups were compared by t-test (corrected by the Bonferroni method). The measurement data of the skewed distribution were expressed as M(range), and the Wilcoxon test was used to determine the differences between groups. Enumeration data were expressed as frequencies or percentages, and compared using Fisher precision tests. Cox regression analysis was used for univariate analysis. Risk factors with p < 0.2 were included in the Cox model for multivariate analysis and the Hazard ratio (HR) was calculated to determine the risk factors that might affect the success rate of surgery. Survival rate was calculated by the Kaplan-Meier method and the survival curve was drawn by GraphPad.Prism.9.5 software (GraphPad Software Corp. USA). If the calculated risk factors were continuous variables, the threshold was calculated using the Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve method, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Of the 35 patients, 14 developed reinfected after completion of the first stage in two-stage revision, and 21 patients were free of reinfection during the 2-year follow-up. In the reinfection group, all patients with confirmed reinfection met the MSIS diagnostic criteria for PJI. Among them, 6 underwent antibiotic bone cement spacer exchange due to reinfection, 2 underwent debridement, 3 re-formed the sinus tracts with evidence of communication to the joint after completing the first stage of two-stage revision, and reinfection was confirmed in 3 patients by serological examination and examination of joint cavity puncture fluid. In the reinfection group, 5 had the same organism cultured from the joint as the initial PJI, 1 had a new organism cultured from the joint, 4 both had negative culture results before and after the reinfection, and 4 patients with positive culture had negative culture of organism in the joint cavity after the first stage of two-stage revision. In the non-reinfection group, 17 completed two-stage revision after infection control, and 4 did not complete re-implantation of the new prosthesis within 2 years of follow-up.

Discussion

Two-stage revision is still the recommended surgical procedure for PJI, which included antibiotic bone cement spacer replacement and new prosthesis reimplantation. However, a subset of patients developed infections after the first stage in two-stage revision and studies on this issue are still lacking.

Need to be mentioned is that in this study, additional inpatient intravenous antibiotic therapy following prosthesis removal and antibiotic bone cement spacer implantation was not clearly defined as treatment requiring antibiotic suppression or uncontrolled infection unfortunately. However, considering that these patients met the diagnostic criteria of MSIS, we included them in the reinfection group [12, 13].

The sinus tract has been shown to be an important factor affecting the treatment of PJI. Xu et al. who retrospectively analyzed 161 PJI patients, concluded that the sinus tract was an independent risk factor affecting the treatment of PJI patients. The specific reason for this finding is unclear and may be related to the poor soft tissue condition of patients with sinus tracts [5]. Alessandra et al. found that the sinus tract was a statistically significant risk factor for failure of two-stage revision [4].

McPherson et al. studied the staging system of PJI in the knee joint. The system has three components: infection type (temporal phase), systemic host grade, and local extremity grade. Assessment of local and limb status highlights issues of soft tissue function and integrity. The limb was classified into grades 1, 2, or 3 based on the number of comorbidities associated with the limb (no compromise factor, one or two compromise factors, and more than two compromise factors, respectively). Synovial cutaneous fistula is one of the compromise factors of local extremity grade [14].

In a study of one-stage revision for PJI patients with sinus tracts, there was a significant difference between the sinus tract group and the control group in the proportion of patients requiring reoperation after one-stage revision (p=0.04). Unfortunately, the study did not investigate whether the sinus tract was a risk factor for reinfection after one-stage revision compared to the control group. This study also proposed that the location of the sinus tract was a risk factor for reoperation including reinfection, aseptic revision, and wound complication (p = 0.047) but not for reinfection (p = 0.502) after one-stage revision. A sinus tract in line with the surgical wound was associated with a higher risk of reoperation than patients who had the sinus tract away from the wound. These results suggested that the presence of the sinus affects the optimal surgical approach and outcome of PJI [15].

Previous studies have also confirmed that compared with PJI patients without a sinus tract, PJI patients with one or more sinus tracts have significantly different microbiological characteristics and are more prone to multiple microbial infections. The reason for this is believed to be that the presence of the sinus affects the integrity of the soft tissues, making it easier for entry of organisms into the joint [16, 17].

Prosthesis removal and antibiotic bone cement spacer implantation is one of the keys to successful treatment of PJI patients. Considering the huge influence of the sinus tract, it is necessary to optimize the associated risk factors to reduce the incidence of postoperative infection in PJI patients with sinus tracts. In this study, UA, CRP, and age were independent risk factors.

At present, many studies suggest that gout is an independent risk factor for reinfection after primary TJA and two-stage revision. Samuel Rosas et al. found that patients with gout had a higher rate of complications after total knee arthroplasty (TKA), with multivariate analysis showing infection in patients with gout (odds ratio [OR] 1.229, p=0.019) [18]. Another study suggested that patients with gout had greater wound healing problems after TKA or THA compared to those without gout (12.2% vs. 5.0%, p=0.001). The inflammatory nature of gout can be harmful to soft tissues and delay the healing of surgical incisions, which may predispose people with gout to postoperative wound healing complications [19]. Wu Bao et al. constructed a novel rat model of gouty ulcers that reveals the mechanism by which urate monosodium urate (MSU) induced wound non-healing [20]. Compared to the control group, the ulcers in the model group did not fully heal by day 14. However, studies about the effect of the normal UA level on the treatment of PJI were still lacking.

This study is the first to propose that UA is a risk factor for reinfection after the first stage in two-stage revision in PJI patients with sinus tracts. The result of this study suggests that UA is a risk factor for prosthesis removal and antibiotic bone cement spacer implantation in PJI patients with sinus tracts, and the cutoff value of UA was found to be 338 µmol/l, suggesting the need to achieve a UA target of less than 338 µmol/l to effectively reduce the failure rate. However, according to the 2020 American College of Rheumatology Guidelines for Gout Management [21], patients with UA less than and equal to 9 mg/dl (≈ 540 µmol/l) were not recommended for uric-lowering therapy (ULT). But it is strongly recommended that all patients receiving ULT achieve and maintain a UA goal of less than 6 mg/dl (≈ 357µmol/l), which is similar to the cut-off for UA in this study. A review of UA management considers that long-term treatment of gout aims to reduce serum UA levels to less than 6 mg/dL, which is below the saturation point of MSU (6.8 mg/dL), to inhibit the formation of new crystals and to promote dissolution of existing crystals, which can help to reduce the incidence of gout flares as well as shrinkage and eventual disappearance of tophi [22].

Another retrospective study found the effect of hyperuricemia on functional outcomes and complications in patients with elbow stiffness after open arthrolysis combined with hinged external fixation [23]. In this study, patients were grouped by quartile method, including group Q1 (UA less than 293 mmol/L), group Q2 (293 ~ 348 mmol/L), group Q3 (348–441 mmol/L), and group Q4 (441–710 mmol/L). At the final follow-up, the data between the four groups showed statistical differences in functional performance, the Mayo Elbow Performance Score, and the visual analog scale for pain. This suggests that even in the normal range, serum UA can also affect the postoperative recovery of patients.

In general, we believe that for PJI patients with sinus tracts who also suffer from hyperuricemia, more aggressive ULT is needed before prosthesis removal and antibiotic bone cement spacer implantation. Controlling the blood uric acid target to less than 5.7 mg/dl (≈ 338 µmol/l) may improve the success rate of surgery. However, for PJI patients with normal UA and sinus tracts, there is currently no evidence to recommend preoperative ULT.

CRP is a commonly used indicator of inflammation, but there are few studies on whether CRP can predict failure after antibiotic bone cement revision. Jeffrey et al. found that, controlling for ASA levels, there was no statistically significant association between the percentage change in CRP before and after implantation of an antibiotic-loaded spacer and the risk of reinfection after two-stage revision. In this study, serum CRP values were recorded at two specified time points: (1) before resection and (2) 6 weeks after completion of a spacer implanted and intravenous infusion of organism-specific antibiotics [24]. Hartman et al. found that preoperative median serum CRP levels were statistically higher in patients with reinfection after secondary revision (12.65 g/dL) than in those without reinfection (5.0 g/dL) (p = 0.010), which may represent that the host is attacked by more potent pathogenic microorganisms, or that the immune system is fighting off infection by pathogens [25]. However, the endpoint of these two studies was reinfection after two-stage revision rather than reinfection after prosthesis removal and antibiotic bone cement spacer implantation. Huang et al. found that preoperative CRP in patients with PJI had a high value in predicting the failure of bone cement exclusion surgery (AUC = 0.776 95%CI: 0.660 ~ 0.867), and CRP >35.4 mg/L can provide a reference for predicting the failure of antibiotic bone cement exclusion treatment [10].

In this study, multivariate analysis of the Cox model proved that CRP was an independent risk factor (p = 0.023), but the Kaplan-Meier survival analysis of CRP > 33.8 showed no statistical significance between the reinfected and non-reinfected groups (p = 0.64). Multivariate COX regression analysis is a semi-parametric test, and the test efficacy is superior to a non-parametric test (Kaplan-Meier survival analysis). Therefore, in this study, we still consider CRP to be an independent risk factor, but do not consider CRP > 33.8 to be statistically significant in predicting the failure of the first stage of two-stage revision.

At present, there are few studies on whether age is a risk factor for postoperative failure after antibiotic bone cement exclusion. After age grouping and adjusting for confounding variables, age was not a risk factor for postoperative PJI in a study on the first TJA [26]. However, the subjects included in the study were not patients undergoing revision for infection. In another study of risk factors for single-stage and two-stage revision, age >80 years (OR 5.962; 95% CI 1.156–30.73; p = 0.033) was an independent risk factor for two-stage revision. However, this conclusion was not applicable to single-stage revision. The primary outcome measure of the study was infection not eradicated after completion of one-stage and two-stage revision. The study allowed patients undergoing two-stage revision to undergo repeated antibiotic cement displacements, so it cannot prove a relationship between age and failure after antibiotic cement displacements [27].

In this study, age was an independent risk factor for postoperative failure after displacement of raw bone cement, and survival analysis suggested that age > 60.5 was statistically significant. Failure rates may be higher in older patients with PJI with sinus tract after antibiotic cement exclusion.

Several limitations in our study should be mentioned. First, this was a single-center retrospective study, and due to the low prevalence of PJI associated with the sinus tract, the sample size was small. Second, considering that the sample eligible for this study may be small, two years was chosen as the follow-up termination time in non-reinfection group, which resulted in a shorter follow-up time. Third, the time span of this study is large, and some surgical techniques and materials have changed over time, so the difference in results caused by different surgeries cannot be eliminated. Fourth, the selection of risk factors for this study was based on multiple studies, which may not include all risk factors that may predict failure.

In summary, this study suggests that UA, CRP, and age are independent risk factors for the failure of removal of components and an antibiotic-loaded cement spacer placed in two-stage revision. The significance of this study is to suggest that for PJI patients with sinus tract who also have hyperuricemia, more aggressive ULT is needed before prosthesis removal and re-implantation of antibiotic bone cement prostheses. Controlling the uric acid target to less than 5.7 mg/dl (≈ 338 µmol/l) may improve the success rate of surgery in PJI patients with one or more sinus tracts. However, more prospective studies are needed to confirm this conclusion.
