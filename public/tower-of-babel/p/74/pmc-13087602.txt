Metabolomic insights into formation and dynamic changes of taste in semi-dried Silver Carp (Hypophthalmichthys molitrix) throughout the drying process: key roles of metabolites derived from protein and nucleotide degradation
Authors: Yuan Peng, Dan Ouyang, Mingli Tian, Yan Li, Shuang Li, Jinhong Wu, Faxiang Wang, Yongle Liu, Xianghong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the formation and dynamic changes of taste-active compounds during semi-dried silver carp drying. TCA-soluble peptides and free amino acids significantly increased after 8–16 h at traditional commercial drying temperature of 45 °C. Umami amino acids Glu exhibited the greatest increases from 1.85 mg/100 g (fresh) to 127.38 mg/100 g (48 h-dried). Umami nucleotide adenosine 5′-monophosphate (AMP) significantly increased after 8–16 h, as did bitter nucleotides hypoxanthine riboside (HxR) and hypoxanthine (Hx). Metabolomic analysis showed amino acid and nucleotide metabolism predominated in the first 8 h, contributing to distinctive savory taste. The synergistic balance of key taste-related metabolites, including oligopeptides (umami and bitter), amino acids (umami, sweet, bitter), nucleotides (umami and bitter), and organic acids (sourness and umami), played a key role in taste perception. Sensory evaluation confirmed the 8 h sample exhibited the highest taste acceptance. These findings provide valuable insights for taste regulation in semi-dried fish products.

Introduction

Dried fish products are not only traditional foods favored worldwide, but also essential ingredients for various further-processed items, such as ready-to-eat fish snacks (Cao et al., 2022; Cheng, Su, Yuan, & Tan, 2021). Drying operation triggers biochemical reactions (e.g. protein hydrolysis, lipid oxidation and nucleotide degradation) that impart distinctive flavors (taste and odor) to the fish (Jiang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025). The flavor of dried fish products have been the focus of numerous studies, with an emphasis on odor (Zhao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2022). However; taste compounds determine the overall palatability of food; directly influencing consumer decisions; and also serve as precursors to odor compounds; contributing to the characteristic flavor of certain foods (Li et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2025).

Diverse taste profiles of fish, including umami, sweetness, and bitterness, are produced by taste-active substances such as free amino acids and nucleotides (Jiang et al., 2024; Li, Sun, et al., 2022; Zhang, Wang, Liu, Xu, & Zhou, 2012). Growing evidence suggests that the taste quality arises not merely from the accumulation of individual compounds but from synergistic interactions among them (Shen et al., 2021; Zhai et al., 2024). Studies in our laboratory found that synergistic effects among IMP, AMP, Glu, and Asp in cold plasma-treated silver carp enhanced the umami intensity (Zhai et al., 2024). Studies have also indicated that organic acids and inorganic salts contribute to sweetness and umami modulation (Zhang, Ayed, Wang, & Liu, 2019). However, the comprehensive profile and formation mechanisms of taste-active compounds during drying of dried fish products remain insufficiently studied.

Most commercial dried aquatic products are processed through heat treatment (hot-air or heat-pump drying). Heat treatment affects metabolic processes, such as protein degradation, energy metabolism, and carbohydrate metabolism, thereby changing the formation and composition of taste-active compounds in dried aquatic products (Jiang et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2025). Moreover, drying temperature and duration play critical roles in balancing the accumulation and degradation of taste-active compounds (Qiu et al., 2023; Zhou & Wang, 2019). Qiu et al. (2023) reported that Oval pompano dried at lower temperatures for longer periods exhibited higher glutamic acid levels; which improved the overall taste profile. However; existing studies generally relied on static endpoint comparisons or limited sampling stages; providing limited insights into the dynamic evolution and underlying mechanisms of non-volatile taste-active compounds throughout the entire drying process (Nie, Wang, Tian, Huang, & Qin, 2025; Wang et al., 2024; Zhao, Hu, & Chen, 2022; Zheng et al., 2025). Recently, Metabolomics has emerged as a powerful approach for comprehensive metabolite profiling and elucidation of underlying metabolic mechanisms (Chen et al., 2026; Guan et al., 2026; Nie et al., 2025). Chen et al. (2026) used metabolomics to identify 49 key metabolites and key pathways, revealing that differences in taste-related metabolites underlie distinct flavor profiles among four freshwater fish species. Therefore, applying untargeted metabolomics to identify critical time points in taste compound changes may provide guidance for optimizing drying processes.

This study aimed to clarify formation and dynamic changes in taste-active compounds during the drying cycle of semi-dried silver carp and to reveal the role of drying time in shaping the taste profile. This research will fill the gap in dynamic taste analysis of dried fish and provide guidance for optimizing processing techniques and developing high-quality ready-to-eat dried fish products.

Materials and methods

Materials

Fresh silver carp (Hypophthalmichthys molitrix, 3.0 ± 0.5 kg) were obtained live from Gaoyun Agricultural Bazaa (Changsha, Hunan, China) and transported to the laboratory within 20 min in oxygen-supplied polyethylene bags inside insulated foam boxes. Upon arrival, fish were immediately and humanely stunned by percussion to the head, with unconsciousness verified before processing. Viscera, scales, skin, and adipose tissue were quickly removed, and dorsal muscle was carefully dissected. All handling, stunning, and slaughter procedures complied with World Organisation for Animal Health (2024) animal welfare guidelines.

Nucleotide standards with a purity of 98%, such as adenosine 5′-monophosphate (AMP), inosine 5′-monophosphate (IMP), hypoxanthine riboside (HxR), and hypoxanthine (Hx) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Located in Shanghai, China. Salt was obtained from Hunan Xuetian Salt Technology Development Co., Ltd. (Changsha, Hunan, China). The supplier of other analytical purity chemicals was Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Salted and dried fish samples preparation

Following our established protocol (Li et al., 2022; Zhai et al., 2024), rectangular blocks (4 × 3 × 2 cm, ∼20 g each) of white muscle were carefully excised from the mid-dorsal region of silver carp. In each independent experiment, dorsal muscles from three fish were collected, yielding 24 muscle blocks in total. Samples were marinated for 3 h in a 10% (w/v) NaCl solution (1:5 w/v, fish:solution) at 4 °C. Post-marination, the surface moisture was gently removed, and the samples were randomly and evenly placed in a drying oven at 45 °C for 48 h. At each drying time point (4, 8, 16, 24, 32, and 48 h), three muscle blocks were randomly selected for analysis. Fresh muscle samples (Fresh) and salted samples before drying (0 h) were collected in the same manner.

Moisture content, salt content, and water activity

The oven-drying method was used to determine the moisture content of the samples, as described in the Chinese National Food Standard (GB 5009.3-2016). The HD-3A water activity meter (Huake Instrument Co., Ltd., Wuxi, China) was employed to analyze the samples' water activity at 25 °C. The silver nitrate titration method was used to determine salt content, as described in the Chinese National Food Standard (GB 5009.44-2016) (Li, Shu, Wang, Huang, & Wang, 2025).

Trichloroacetic acid (TCA)-soluble peptides

TCA-soluble peptide content was measured using a modified method based on Yang et al. (2024). Due to weight loss from moisture evaporation during drying, sampling was based on the dry weight of fresh fish. Minced samples were mixed with a 5% (w/v) TCA solution (1:10 w/v, fish:solution) and homogenized for 30 s at 10,000 rpm. After incubating for 30 min at 4 °C, the mixture solutions were centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 15 min to extract the supernatant. The Lowry method was employed to calculate the supernatant's protein concentration, and results were reported in mg/mL.

Analysis of 5′-nucleotides contents

The method outlined by Zhai et al. (2024) was utilized for determining 5′-nucleotides. Minced samples were mixed with 10% (v/v) perchloric acid (1:10 v/v, fish:solution), homogenized at 10,000 rpm for 40 s before being vortexed for 1 min. To extract the supernatant, the mixture underwent 15 min centrifugation at 6000 rpm (4 °C). The precipitate was washed with 5% pre-cooled perchloric acid, re-homogenized and vortexed as described, and centrifuged under the same conditions. The combined supernatants were adjusted to pH 6.0 using 10 M and 1 M NaOH solutions. The resulting solution was then diluted to 50 mL with ultrapure water, and the filtrate was collected using a 0.45 μm filter membrane.

A 20 μL extract sample was analyzed using an LC-20A HPLC system (Shimadzu, Japan) with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. A CAPCELL PAK C18 AQS5 chromatographic column was used (4.6 × 150 mm, 5.0 μm, Shiseido, Tokyo, Japan) and the temperature was maintained at 35 °C. The mobile phase was a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of potassium dihydrogen phosphate and dipotassium hydrogen phosphate buffer (0.02 mol/L, pH 6.0).

Free amino acids content (FAAs)

The Zhai et al. (2024) method was used to analyze the content and composition of free amino acids. Minced samples were mixed with 5% (w/v) TCA solution (1:10 w/v, fish:solution) and homogenized for 30 s at 10,000 rpm. After a one-hour incubation at 4 °C, 2 mL of the extraction solution was centrifuged for 15 min at 11,000 rpm. Then, the supernatant was passed by a 0.22 μm membrane. The L-8900 automatic amino acid analyzer (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) was employed to analyze the filtrate.

Sensory analysis

Each dried fish sample was soaked in 100 mL of purified water for 8 h, steamed over boiling water for 10 min, cooled to room temperature, and stored in a lidded white porcelain bowl. The sensory panel comprised 20 trained panelists (10 males, 10 females; aged 20–30 years). According to the training procedures, a nine-point hedonic scale was used to evaluate the samples in terms of color, hardness, odor, saltiness, and umami (9-8 = extremely liked, 7-6 = moderately liked, 5 = neither liked nor disliked, 4-3 = moderately disliked, and 2-1 = extremely disliked) (Xia, Song, Zhong, Halim, & O'Mahony, 2020). Sensory evaluations were performed in a dedicated laboratory. All samples were randomly coded and presented to the assessors in random order. During the evaluation, panelists rinsed their mouths with purified water to prevent sensory fatigue, with a 5 min interval between sample assessments.

The sensory evaluation was approved by the relevant institutional ethics committee of Changsha University of Science & Technology, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The official approval certificate is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Untargeted metabolomic analysis

Metabolite extraction

Metabolite extraction was performed according to established untargeted metabolomics protocols (Hu et al., 2025; Nie et al., 2025). Accurately weigh 50 mg of fish sample into a grinding tube, mixed with methanol-acetonitrile-water (300 μL, 2:2:1, v/v), and ground at 60 Hz for 2 min at 4 °C. The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Then, residue sediment was re-extracted with 200 μL of the same solvent, vortexed for 30 s, and centrifuged under identical conditions; the supernatant was collected again. The combined supernatants were vacuum-dried, and dried samples were redissolved in 100 μL of 30% methanol-water, vortexed, and centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 20 min, 4 °C). The resultant supernatant was carefully transferred to injection vials for subsequent analysis. There were 5 biological replicates in each of the six groups, making a total of 30 samples. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal volumes (5 μL) from each individual sample. The pooled mixture was vortexed thoroughly to ensure homogeneity and used as the QC sample for monitoring analytical stability.

UHPLC-MS/MS analysis

LC-MS analysis was conducted using a UHPLC-Q Exactive system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) (Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Chromatographic separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters, USA) maintained at 45 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in methanol (B), delivered at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The gradient elution program was set as A/B 99/1 to 0/100 over 12 min, followed by a hold at 0/100 from 12 to 13 min. The injection volume was 1 μL.

Mass spectrometric detection was carried out using heated electrospray ionization (H-ESI) operated in both positive and negative ion modes, which were acquired in separate runs. Data were collected in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode, consisting of one full MS scan followed by 10 MS/MS scans, with normalized collision energies of 15 and 30. The full-scan mass range was m/z 67–1000, with a resolution of 70,000, AGC target of 1 × 106, and maximum injection time of 100 ms. For dd-MS/MS scans, the resolution was set to 17,500, with an AGC target of 5 × 105 and a maximum injection time of 50 ms. The spray voltage was set at 3.2 kV in positive mode and 2.8 kV in negative mode. The capillary temperature was maintained at 320 °C, and the S-lens RF level was set to 50.

Chlorophenylalanine was added as an internal standard during sample preparation to monitor analytical stability and reproducibility. Raw data were processed using Progenesis QI software (v2.3, Waters, USA) for peak alignment, peak picking, and deconvolution. Metabolites were annotated based on accurate mass and MS/MS spectral matching against the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB).

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was independently repeated at least three times. SPSS 26.0 (IBM, USA) was employed for analyzing the data using one-way ANOVA, and multiple comparisons were performed using Duncan's test. In each experiment, all samples were maintained at the same dry weight as the fresh fish samples. Statistical differences were regarded as significant at the level of p < 0.05. MetaboAnalyst 5.0 and the Metware Cloud online platform were used for principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), KEGG metabolic pathway enrichment. Data were log-transformed and Pareto-scaled prior to analysis. Supervised PLS-DA models were validated using 5-fold cross-validation, and their performance was evaluated with cumulative R2Y and Q2 parameters. To further verify model robustness and prevent overfitting, a permutation test with 200 iterations was conducted for each model. Unless otherwise stated, all quantitative data in this study are expressed on a wet-weight basis. Dry-weight–based values were calculated solely for supplementary analysis and are presented in the Supplementary Materials.

Results and discussion

Changes in moisture, salt content, and water activity

Fresh samples had a moisture content of 79.78%, which decreased to 78.62% after 3 h of salting (Fig. 1A). At this stage, the salt content was 2.79% (Fig. 1C). As drying progressed, moisture content decreased significantly (p < 0.05), with a marked decline after 8 h. Semi-dried fish products are generally defined as having a moisture content ranging from 20% to 50% (Zhu et al., 2022). In the present study; the moisture content began to approach this range after 8 h of drying and reached approximately 46.64% at 16 h; indicating the formation of a semi-dried state. After 48 h of drying; moisture content decreased to 23.54%; and salt content increased to 9.26%. Water activity remained relatively stable during the first 8 h of drying; then decreased from 0.90 to 0.67 between 8 and 48 h (Fig. 1B); further supporting the transition from a high-moisture to a semi-dried and progressively stabilized state. Lower moisture content and water activity are beneficial for preventing spoilage microorganisms growth; improving the shelf life of dried aquatic items (Shen et al., 2023). Changes in TCA-soluble peptides content

TCA-soluble peptide levels reflect the content of low-molecular-weight peptides (Chen et al., 2024). As illustrated in Fig. 1D; the TCA-soluble peptide content showed no significant difference between fresh and salted samples. After 16 h of drying; a significant increase was observed (p < 0.05); confirming that drying induced protein degradation in silver carp muscle. The results of TCA-soluble peptides calculated based on samples dry weight also showed an increasing trend in absolute content during drying (Fig. S2A). Heat-induced protein denaturation during drying caused structural unfolding; exposing hydrolysis sites and promoting protein degradation into peptides (Shen et al., 2023). Moreover; our previous studies also found a significant correlation between increased cathepsin B and L activity and elevated levels of small peptides and free amino acids during hot-air drying (Li, Luan, et al., 2022). It is well established that low-molecular-weight peptides are known to contribute to flavor as active components (Hao et al., 2024). As reported by Liu et al. (2020), extracts from Takifugu rubripes with molecular weights below 3 kDa exhibited enhanced umami taste.

Changes in free amino acids (FAAs) content and composition

FAAs are also important taste-active compounds in aquatic products (Wang et al., 2025). A total of 21 FAAs were found in semi-dried sliver carp throughout this investigation (Table 1). After salting; the total FAAs level dropped from 551.06 mg/100 g in fresh samples to 388.26 mg/100 g. A significant increase was observed after 16 h of drying (p < 0.05); and the total amino acid content reached 3024.50 mg/100 g after 48 h (Table 1). This increase resulted from protein denaturation and the hydrolysis of proteins and peptides by endogenous proteases during drying; which elevated the levels of FAAs (Li, Luan, et al., 2022; Wang, Jian, et al., 2025). On a dry-weight basis, the absolute content of free amino acids also increased significantly (Table S1, p < 0.05). The changes in umami, sweet, and bitter FAAs during drying were further examined (Fig. 1E). Umami FAAs, the primary umami contributors in aquatic products, increased significantly during drying (p < 0.05). Among them, Glu was present at much higher levels than Asp and exhibited the most pronounced increase, with concentrations in 48 h dried samples exceeding 60-fold those in fresh samples. Total sweet FAAs content also increased significantly during drying (p < 0.05), with Gly and Ala being the most abundant. For bitter FAAs, Met, aromatic (Phe), and branched-chain amino acids (Leu, Ile, Val) are many key flavor precursor compounds (Chen et al., 2024). The accumulation of bitter FAAs, while contributing to bitterness, likely contributes to the characteristic flavor of dried fish.

The TAV values of FAAs were calculated, as shown in Fig. 1F. TAV > 1 indicates a significant contribution to taste, whereas TAV < 1 suggests minimal contribution to taste perception (Lioe, Apriyantono, Takara, Wada, & Yasuda, 2005). The TAVs of total umami; sweet; and bitter FAAs increased progressively with drying time. Across all samples; Glu (umami); Ala (sweet); Lys (bitter); and His (bitter) contributed most to taste. Similarly; Chen et al. (2024) reported that Glu significantly contributed to the flavor of golden pompano during drying. In this study, it is noteworthy that the TAVs of Glu and Ala, Lys, and His exceeded 1 after 16 h of drying, suggesting that 16 h may serve as a critical time point for taste development in semi-dried silver carp.

5′-Nucleotides contents and equivalent umami concentration (EUC)

After slaughter, Adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP)-related compounds in fish muscle undergo an endogenous enzyme-mediated pathway, sequentially converting from ATP to Adenosine Diphosphate (ADP), adenosine 5′-monophosphate (AMP), inosine 5′-monophosphate (IMP), hypoxanthine riboside (HxR), and hypoxanthine (Hx). AMP and IMP are the primary taste-active 5′-nucleotides that contribute directly to umami taste. HxR and Hx, as the end products of ATP catabolism, contribute to the bitter or undesirable taste (Yu et al., 2025; Zhai et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 2, the contents of ATP-related compounds exhibited pronounced dynamic changes during the drying process. Compared to fresh samples (1.65 mg/100 g), AMP content decreased to 0.90 mg/100 g after salting (Fig. 2A). AMP content increased significantly within the first 8 h of drying (p < 0.05) and peaked at 11.16 mg/100 g after 48 h. During the early stage of drying, the rapid degradation of ATP and ADP, together with the relatively slower deamination of AMP, likely contributed to the transient accumulation of AMP observed in this study. In contrast, IMP content decreased significantly (p < 0.05), dropping from 223.89 mg/100 g (fresh sample) to 16.00 mg/100 g (48 h sample) (Fig. 2B). This rapid depletion is primarily attributed to the accelerated dephosphorylation of IMP into HxR, catalyzed by 5′-nucleotidase and related phosphatases, which likely maintained high activity during mid-temperature drying (45 °C) (Li et al., 2017). As drying progressed to 48 h, the accumulation of the terminal products HxR (339.17 mg/100 g) and Hx (73.65 mg/100 g) reflected a metabolic shift toward bitterness-contributing compounds (Fig. 2C–D). AMP and IMP, the primary taste-active nucleotides, not only directly contribute to umami but also enhance umami intensity by synergizing with amino acids (Qin, Wang, Wang, & Shi, 2020). The umami intensity was further assessed using the EUC method; which is commonly applied to evaluate the synergistic effect of umami nucleotides and amino acids (Zhai et al., 2024). The EUC values began to increase significantly after 16 h of drying (p < 0.05); reaching 3.40 g MSG/100 g in the 48 h samples (Fig. 2E). Since the taste threshold for monosodium glutamate (MSG) is 0.03 g/100 g; the EUC TAV increased significantly; from 29.74 (fresh sample) to 113.48 (48 h sample). Yang, Shi, Zhou, Qu, and Wang (2019) similarly reported that appropriate heat treatment increased the EUC value of aquatic products. Notably, the EUC TAV of 48 h samples (113.48) far exceeded the sum of individual umami compound TAVs (AMP, 0.22; IMP, 0.64; Glu, 4.25; Asp, 0.28), indicating synergistic interactions among taste compounds in semi-dried silver carp.

Analysis of sensory properties

The sensory radar chart of semi-dried silver carp was shown in Fig. 7. The results indicate that color scores were highest for the 4 h sample and lowest for the 48 h sample (p < 0.05). The hardness scores of the 4 h and 8 h samples were comparable to those of fresh sample. As drying time increased further, perceived hardness rose progressively, resulting in lower scores. In contrast, odor scores exceeded 6 after 24 h of drying, indicating that accumulation of odor-active compounds enhanced acceptability. Regarding the saltiness- umami attributes of the semi-dried fish, the saltiness scores of the six samples showed no significant differences (all >6, p > 0.05), indicating that the salted-drying process used in this study produced products with an acceptable saltiness level. Notably, the 8 h sample exhibited the highest umami score, significantly exceeding all other groups (p < 0.05). This observation was consistent with the marked accumulation of umami-related amino acids and nucleotides at this stage, suggesting that 8 h represents a key phase in dynamic taste evolution during drying. The enhanced presence of these umami-active compounds may contribute to the dominance of savory perception at this stage.

Partial least squares regression (PLS-R) analysis of taste-compound relationships

To further elucidate the relationship between taste-active compounds and sensory perception, a PLS-R model was established using NaCl, TCA-soluble peptide, free amino acids, and nucleotides as X variables and sensory attributes (umami and saltiness) as Y variables (Chen et al., 2026). The first two latent variables explained a high proportion of the total variance (>90%); indicating good model stability and reliability. The loading plot showed umami and salty scores on the positive side of Factor-1; closely clustered with NaCl; TCA-soluble peptide; most free amino acids; and nucleotides; indicating strong positive correlations with umami perception (Fig. S4). In addition; the above EUC results of this study confirmed the synergistic enhancement between umami amino acids (Glu; Asp) and nucleotides (AMP; IMP); which played a key role in boosting umami taste during fish drying. A growing body of research suggests umami perception arises from the integrated action of multiple taste-active compounds rather than linear contributions of single substances. Although bitter metabolites such as HxR and Hx accumulated during drying; their vectors aligned closely with the umami cluster in loading space; suggesting covariance with umami within the observed concentration range. Li et al. (2024) showed that umami-active compounds can modulate bitterness via receptor-level interactions and taste pathway cross-talk, with strong umami potentially shifting overall taste balance and bitter thresholds. Thus, the potential negative impact of bitter compounds accumulating in mid-drying (e.g., 16 h) may be mitigated by concurrent synergistic umami enhancement, helping maintain acceptable umami perception in sensory evaluation. Further studies will focus on interactions among taste-active compounds and their receptor mechanisms.

For aquatic products, the formation of distinctive taste profiles involves the balanced contributions of a broader array of taste-active compounds (Zhang et al., 2019). Therefore; this study employed a comprehensive metabolomics approach to more thoroughly elucidate the dynamic changes in taste-active compounds during the drying process; thereby providing deeper insights into the underlying mechanisms of taste formation in semi-dried silver crucian carp (Chen et al., 2026; Nie et al., 2025).

Oligopeptides

This study identified 208 differential oligopeptides in semi-dried silver carp, primarily dipeptides and tripeptides. Most peptides increased significantly in abundance during drying (p < 0.05; Fig. 5A). This trend aligns with the observed increase TCA-soluble peptides. Previous studies have demonstrated that umami peptides enhance umami perception by activating umami taste receptors (T1R1/T1R3). Most hydrophobic peptides, termed bitter peptides, contribute to bitterness due to high affinity for T2R receptors (Hao et al., 2024). The Umami_YYDS tool was used to predict umami and bitter taste properties (Cui et al., 2023). A total of 45 umami peptides; 158 bitter peptides; and 6 peptides identified as glutathione or its derivatives were detected. The abundance of 44 umami peptides markedly increased at 24 h; suggesting that drying promoted protein degradation. A similar trend was observed for bitter peptides (Fig. 5C). Glutathione has been recognized as a representative contributor to the full-bodied taste in fish (Kuroda, 2022). Glutathione and oxidized glutathione levels were highest in fresh samples but decreased with extended drying (Fig. 5B). Amino acids and derivatives

As shown in Fig. 5D, the heatmap illustrated dynamic changes in 115 differential FAAs and their derivatives during drying. Twelve FAAs were further classified by taste attributes: umami (Glu, and Asp), sweet (Arg, Gly, Thr, Ser, and Ala), bitter (Lys, and Met), and non-taste (Tau, Orn, and Gln). Levels of umami amino acids (Glu, and Asp), the sweet amino acid Arg, and the bitter amino acids Lys and Met increased significantly after 8 h of drying, consistent with the above targeted FAAs quantification results (p < 0.05). Some sweet amino acids (Thr, Ser, Gly, and Ala) showed inconsistencies with above FAAs data, probably due to differences between metabolomics (free and bound forms) and targeted analysis (free forms only). In addition, glycated amino acid derivatives, including maillard reaction end-products like N(ε)-(carboxyethyl) lysine and ε-(carboxyethyl) lysine, were detected and found to gradually accumulate in fish muscle. This suggested that hot-air drying promotes carbonyl–amine condensation between reducing sugars and amino acids, gradually forming advanced glycation end products (Shi et al., 2021). Appropriate Maillard reactions can impart attractive color and flavor to fish muscle (Sun et al., 2022).

Nucleotides and derivatives

5′-Nucleotides, key taste-active compounds, are generated from adenosine triphosphate (ATP) degradation (Matoba, Kuchiba, Kimura, & Hasegawa, 1988). The results of untargeted metabolomics identified 41 nucleotides and their derivatives. Untargeted metabolomics identified 41 nucleotides and their derivatives; including the four previously quantified nucleotides (AMP; IMP; HxR; and Hx); as well as xanthosine monophosphate (XMP) and guanosine monophosphate (GMP); both of which exhibit umami-enhancing properties (Yin et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2022). The heatmap in Fig. 5E shows that XMP abundance peaked significantly at 8 h of drying, but decreased with extended drying time. The abundance of GMP showed a marked decline as drying time increased. These indicated that XMP and GMP contributed minimally to umami intensity during late-stage drying.

Organic acids and derivatives

A total of 25 differential organic acids and their derivatives varied dynamically during drying, with more than half significantly upregulated (Fig. 5F). Several organic acids showed stage-specific abundance fluctuations. Succinic acid and L-lactic acid increased significantly after drying (p < 0.05), with succinic acid showing the greatest upregulation. Beyond contributing to sourness, succinic acid also enhances umami. Zhang et al. (2019) found that removing succinic acid significantly reduced umami intensity in taste reconstitution experiments with Takifugu obscurus. The noticeable increase in L-lactic acid likely resulted from anaerobic respiration during drying; contributing refreshing sourness and enhancing fish flavor richness (Liu et al., 2022). In conclusion, these findings suggest that organic acids play a role in enhancing taste (such as umami) during drying of semi-dried silver carp.

Inorganic salts

In this study, silver carp muscle was pre-treated with salt curing before drying to enhance sodium-based flavor. The dynamic changes of 9 differential inorganic salts (including chloride, sulfate, and phosphate species) during drying are shown in Fig. 5G. Sodium chloride (NaCl) content increased markedly, aligning with the above salt content results. In addition to contributing a salty taste, NaCl also functions as a synergistic enhancer of umami compounds (Zhang et al., 2021). The heatmap revealed the upregulation of sodium sulfate and phosphate derivatives (e.g.; disodium phosphate; potassium phosphate) during mid-drying (8–24 h). Liu, Zhang, and Chen (2013) reported that PO₄3− enhanced monosodium glutamate flavor and sourness while suppressing bitterness. This indicated that inorganic salt had a greater effect on taste during mid-drying (8–24 h).

Sugars and derivatives

Sweetness is also an important component of aquatic product flavor, with soluble sugars being one of its key contributors (Yue, Zhang, Jin, Deng, & Zhao, 2016). As shown in Fig. 5H, the majority of sugars were significantly downregulated during the drying process, while a few were significantly upregulated. d-glucose, an important sugar contributing to fish taste, declined sharply, indicating its consumption through carbohydrate metabolic pathways. Meanwhile, pentose phosphate pathway intermediates, including D-ribulose 5-phosphate, were strongly upregulated at 24–48 h. These changes imply that late-stage accumulation of soluble sugars contributes to taste development in semi-dried fish.

After drying began (0 h VS 4 h), key differential metabolites pathways were glutathione metabolism and nucleotide metabolism (e.g., riboflavin metabolism). The significant enrichment of riboflavin metabolism also explained the marked decline of GMP during the early drying stage, as GMP serves as an essential precursor for riboflavin synthesis (Zhao et al., 2022). After 4 h of drying; amino acid metabolism (e.g.; arginine biosynthesis and alanine; aspartate; and glutamate metabolism) became activated. Progressive degradation of myofibrillar proteins and peptides enhanced amino acid metabolism. Among these; arginine; glutamate; and aspartate are major metabolic products that contribute substantially to umami and sweetness (Wang, Jian, et al., 2025). In the mid-drying stage (and 8 h VS 24 h), organic acid metabolism (e.g., CoA biosynthesis) and carbohydrate metabolism (e.g., fructose and mannose metabolism) became more active. Intense biochemical reactions during this stage increased the number and diversity of enriched metabolic pathways, with a notable rise in differential metabolites involved, promoting metabolite accumulation. In the late drying stage (24 h VS 48 h), fewer enriched pathways and differential metabolites were observed, indicating stabilized metabolic reactions primarily focused on specific metabolite accumulation. Nucleotide metabolism (Purine metabolism) was predominant, with lower levels of amino acid metabolism, organic acid metabolism and carbohydrate metabolism.

Dynamic metabolic regulation of taste formation during drying process

The temporal evolution of metabolic pathways during the drying process of semi-dried silver carp was fundamentally driven by dehydration-triggered biochemical reactions, including proteolysis, nucleotide degradation, and stress-responsive metabolism. The results indicated that 8–16 h of drying was a critical turning point for changes in taste-active compounds and associated metabolic pathways. In the early drying phase, dehydration triggered intense biochemical reactions. Concurrently, protein degradation and ATP breakdown drove amino acid and nucleotide metabolism, which dominated the initial drying stage (0–8 h). Initial rapid dehydration disrupted cellular integrity and triggers the denaturation and structural unfolding of myofibrillar proteins (Zhang et al., 2020, Zhang et al., 2020). Endogenous proteases; such as cathepsins (B; L; D) and calpains; which are known to participate in post-mortem fish muscle proteolysis; may contribute to the progressive hydrolysis of myofibrillar proteins into taste-active oligopeptides and free amino acids (Li, Sun, et al., 2022; Wang, Jian, et al., 2025). Interestingly, the early up-regulation of glutathione metabolism likely represents a defensive response to reactive oxygen species (ROS) arising from air exposure and desiccation (Kuroda, 2022). ATP was stepwise degraded into umami-contributing AMP; IMP; and GMP; as well as bitter-tasting HxR and Hx; mediated by nucleotidases such as ATPase and AMP deaminase (Yu et al., 2025). Additionally, peak umami acceptability at 8 h suggested that the interactions among these umami substances (oligopeptides, amino acids, and nucleotides) may facilitate a better binding with the T1R1/T1R3 umami receptors, thereby enhancing the intensity of the umami perception. It should be noted that the critical sensory window (8–16 h) was identified specifically under 45 °C drying conditions in this study. As drying temperature can significantly influence moisture migration and the kinetics/stability of endogenous enzymes, the precise timing of this metabolic turning point may vary under different drying conditions or in other fish species.

In the intermediate stage (8–24 h), increased representation of organic acid and carbohydrate metabolism may be linked to enhanced metabolic flux from protein-derived intermediates entering branches related to the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and glycolysis (Wang et al., 2025). This shifts could contribute to the accumulation of organic acids (acting as umami or sourness enhancers) and a reduction in reducing sugars (potential Maillard reaction precursors under mild heating). The sustained umami acceptability at this stage may also be attributed to the enhancing effect of organic acids, which helps balance the negative impact of bitter components. At the late stage (24–48 h), the diversity of enriched pathways decreased, and nucleotide metabolism became relatively dominant. This trend may be associated with reduced water activity and substrate depletion, which would limit enzymatic activity and overall metabolic turnover.

Conclusion

This study combined targeted quantitative analysis, non-targeted metabolomics, and sensory evaluation to characterize the dynamic metabolic evolution of taste-active compounds during the drying of semi-dried silver carp (45 °C, 0–48 h). The results revealed clear stage-dependent metabolic transitions, with 8–16 h as a key transition point. During early drying (0–16 h), early dehydration triggered intense biochemical reactions, during which amino acid and nucleotide metabolism triggered by protein and ATP degradation dominated. In the later stage (24–48 h), metabolic pathway diversity decreased and nucleotide metabolism became predominant. Sensory analysis identified 8 h as a key sensory window with peak umami perception. EUC and PLS-R confirmed the synergistic enhancement between umami amino acids (Glu, Asp) and nucleotides (AMP), which played a crucial role in improving umami intensity during drying. In addition, this synergistic interaction may simultaneously mitigate the negative effects of bitter compounds, sustaining acceptable sensory perception. Overall, dynamic interactions among amino acid metabolism, nucleotide degradation, organic acid metabolism, and carbohydrate metabolism collectively shaped the sensory attributes during semi-drying. These findings provide a theoretical basis for optimizing drying strategies in ready-to-eat fish products.
