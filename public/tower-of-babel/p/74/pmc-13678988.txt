An individualized nomogram for survival prediction in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma: a population-based analysis
Authors: Hui Xie, Xiangyu Wang, Lingguang Shen, Fatao Hao, Yong Meng, Jianbin Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) is an aggressive malignancy with poor prognosis. Current prognostic systems, including TNM staging system, incompletely incorporate important clinical and pathological factors. This study aims to develop and validate an individualized nomogram for predicting overall survival (OS) in ICC patients.MethodsA total of 1,751 ICC patients diagnosed between 2011 and 2021 were identified from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database for model development and internal validation. A temporally distinct SEER cohort of 751 ICC patients diagnosed between 2000 and 2010 was used for temporal validation. Twelve candidate predictors were prespecified, and the final nomogram variables were retained from the multivariable Cox model according to a predefined retention threshold. Model performance was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index), time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (time-dependent AUC), calibration curves, decision curve analysis (DCA), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). Kaplan-Meier analysis was used for risk stratification.ResultsThe nomogram demonstrated good discriminative ability, with C-indices of 0.761 (training cohort) and 0.732 (validation cohort), and time-dependent AUC values ranging from 0.74 to 0.84 across 1-, 3-, and 5-year predictions. Calibration curves revealed good agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities in both cohorts. The model outperformed the TNM staging system, with IDI values of 0.197, 0.393, and 0.238 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year predictions, respectively (all P < 0.001). DCA indicated greater net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities. Risk stratification significantly differentiated high-risk from low-risk patients (log-rank P < 0.001).ConclusionThe proposed nomogram provides individualized prediction of 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS in ICC patients and may assist in risk stratification and prognostic assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12957-026-04509-x.Keywords: Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, Prognostic model, Nomogram, Overall survival, Cox regression

Introduction

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) ranks as the second common primary liver malignancy after hepatocellular carcinoma, accounting for 5–30% of all major hep- atic cancers [1, 2]. Despite its relatively lower incidence, the global burden of ICC has increased by more than 140% over the past four decades [3].ICC is known by high inva- siveness and a dismal prognosis, with reported 5-year overall survival rates remaining around 9% [4].Unfortunately, there are currently no universally accepted screening guidelines or standardized protocols for assessing the prognosis of ICC patients [5].

The 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system remains the most widely used framework for ICC classification. However, accumulating evidence suggests that this system may not be universally applicable to all ICC subtypes or patient populations [6].Moreover, substantial heterogeneity in recurrence and survival outcomes persists even among patients classified at the same disease stage [7]. Previous studies have proposed prognostic models based on tumor diameter and number [8, 9] as well as combinations of CA19-9 levels and imaging findings [10]. Nevertheless, reliance on isolated biomarkers or radiological features alone is insufficient for accurate outcome prediction, and many existing models fail to incorporate key patient-specific factors such as age, treatment modalities, or detailed pathological characteristics [11–15].Although some recent models include age and therapeutic approaches, the aggressive biology of ICC and the anatomical complexity of the liver necessitate the inclusion of additional critical indicators, such as intrahepatic tumor extension, to improve predictive accuracy [16].

Given the heterogeneity in study populations, predictor selection, validation strategies, and reported performance across existing ICC prognostic models, a concise comparison of representative published models is provided in Supplementary Table S1. This comparison helps contextualize the rationale and incremental focus of the present study by summarizing previous models according to their prediction targets, main variables, validation methods, and performance metrics.Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to develop and temporally validate an individualized nomogram for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival in patients with ICC using population-based SEER data. We hypothesized that a model integrating demographic characteristics, tumor-related factors, lymph node involvement, tumor extension, and treatment status would provide more individualized OS prediction and risk stratification than the AJCC TNM staging system alone.

Methods

Patient recruitment from the SEER database

The data for this retrospective cohort study were obtained from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, a nationally representative cancer registry program sponsored by the National Cancer Institute [17]. Using SEER*Stat software (version 9.0.42), we extracted records of patients diagnosed with primary ICC between 2011 and 2021 to establish the training cohort. The patient selection criteria were as follows: Inclusion criteria included (1) histopathological confirmation of cholangiocarcinoma (ICD-O-3 code: 8160/3) and (2) primary tumor site located in the intrahepatic bile ducts (ICD-10 code: C22.1). Exclusion criteria were (1) missing data for key study variables and (2) unclear survival information. After rigorous screening, a total of 1,751 patients were included in the final analysis. This study adopted a complete-case analysis approach, restricting the final cohort to patients with complete data on all key variables required for model development. Patients with missing, blank, or unknown information for candidate predictors or survival information were excluded during initial screening, as illustrated in Fig. 1. We did not perform multiple imputation because several excluded variables in SEER were recorded as “unknown” or “blank” categories rather than randomly missing continuous measurements, and reliable imputation of tumor size, metastatic status, tumor extension, and treatment information would require assumptions that could not be verified in this registry-based study. To address the potential selection bias introduced by complete-case analysis, we further summarized the missingness of each key variable in the original SEER extraction cohort and compared the available baseline and survival-related characteristics between included and excluded patients.

Because this study used de-identified data from the publicly available SEER database, it did not constitute human subjects research requiring formal institutional review board approval or informed consent. Access to the SEER database was obtained after completion of the SEER Research Data Agreement.

Cohort definition and variable recoding

This study used temporally distinct subsets of the SEER database for model development and validation. Patients diagnosed between 2011 and 2021 were used as the training cohort for variable selection and model construction. The 751 patients in the temporal validation cohort were not excluded from the training cohort; rather, they were independently identified from an earlier, non-overlapping diagnostic period within the SEER database. Specifically, ICC patients diagnosed between 2000 and 2010 were extracted using the same inclusion and exclusion criteria and were used for temporal validation. The 2011–2021 cohort was selected for model development because it reflects more contemporary registry coding and treatment-era patterns, whereas the 2000–2010 cohort was used to assess model stability across different calendar periods without overlap between the development and validation datasets. Because both cohorts were derived from the SEER database, this validation strategy should be interpreted as temporal validation rather than external validation using a non-SEER population. Compared with random split-sample validation alone, temporal validation provides an assessment of model performance across different diagnostic periods within the same registry, although it cannot replace validation in independent non-SEER datasets [18, 19] . The inclusion and exclusion criteria for the temporal validation cohort were kept strictly aligned with those of the training cohort to ensure comparability. The patient selection process is summarized in Fig. 1.

The primary statistical outcome was overall survival (OS), defined as the time from ICC diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow-up. The primary prediction targets were individualized 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS probabilities. And the analyzed variables included age, sex, bone metastasis, brain metastasis, lung metastasis, liver metastasis, tumor size, tumor extension, lymph node involvement, surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy status. In data processing, age and tumor size were categorized primarily to improve clinical interpretability and to maintain consistency with established prognostic classifications and previous ICC-related studies [20]. Age was stratified into 10-year intervals to provide clinically intuitive risk groups. Tumor size was dichotomized at 5 cm, consistent with the AJCC staging framework and prior literature [9, 21–23]. Tumor extension was classified into four categories based on SEER coding criteria, incorporating both tumor multiplicity and hepatic segment involvement; Treatment modalities (surgery/chemotherapy/radiotherapy) were coded as binary variables (yes vs. no). We acknowledge that categorizing continuous variables may introduce information loss and threshold effects. However, continuous modeling of tumor size was limited by the SEER coding structure and the substantial proportion of unknown or coded records; therefore, categorical modeling was adopted in the primary nomogram.The complete coding definitions and recategorization schemes are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Model development and validation

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (R version 4.4.3). Descriptive statistics were first applied to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics across the entire cohort. Between-group comparisons were performed using Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Twelve candidate predictors were prespecified based on clinical relevance, availability in the SEER database, and previous ICC-related literature. Univariable Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to describe the crude association between each prespecified candidate predictor and OS, rather than to determine eligibility for model entry. A multivariable Cox proportional hazards model including all 12 prespecified candidate predictors was then fitted to estimate adjusted associations. Stepwise selection methods were intentionally avoided to minimize overfitting and preserve the clinical interpretability of the candidate predictor framework. To construct a parsimonious and clinically interpretable nomogram, variables showing strong independent associations with OS in the multivariable Cox model at a predefined retention threshold of P < 0.001 were included in the final nomogram. For categorical predictors, if at least one non-reference category met this retention criterion, the entire categorical variable was retained in the nomogram to preserve its original coding structure and avoid partial representation of clinically related categories. Predictors retained in the final nomogram were interpreted as survival-associated predictors rather than causal risk factors.The final predictive model was visually presented as a nomogram, with variable inclusion adhering to Harrell’s guidelines [24]. To improve the transparency and reproducibility of individualized prediction, the complete Cox model coefficients and baseline survival estimates at 1, 3, and 5 years were provided in the Supplementary Materials. Individual Cox model-based survival probability was calculated as , where denotes the baseline survival probability at time t, and denotes the patient-specific linear predictor. A nomogram scoring sheet and a worked example were also provided to facilitate consistent manual calculation. Additionally, Variance inflation factors (VIF) were calculated to assess multicollinearity, ensuring model stability.The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, with detailed test statistics summarized in Supplementary Table S3. Exploratory sensitivity analyses were further conducted using continuous and restricted cubic spline-based age specifications to assess the influence of age categorization on model discrimination.

For model evaluation, prediction accuracy was assessed using the concordance index (C-index), which quantifies agreement between predicted and observed survival outcomes. Model discrimination—balancing sensitivity and specificity—was evaluated via time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and corresponding area under the curve (AUC) values. Model robustness was assessed through ten-fold cross-validation for internal validation of the training cohort and through temporal validation using a temporally distinct SEER cohort. The area under the time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curve (time-dependent AUC) calculated via bootstrapping, was used to evaluate discriminative ability. The predictive performance of the newly developed model was systematically compared with the 8th edition AJCC TNM staging system by evaluating receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, with particular emphasis on discrimination accuracy as measured by the area under the curve (AUC). Calibration was examined by plotting calibration curves at 1-, 3-, and 5-year intervals, while clinical utility was evaluated through decision curve analysis (DCA) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), to determine net benefit across these time points. Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on model-derived risk scores. Between group differences were assessed by log-rank tests and graphically represented using Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves to illustrate disparities in overall survival (OS) and actual survival probabilities.

Results

Description of the characteristics of included cases

To evaluate the potential impact of complete case selection, we summarized variable specific missingness in the original SEER extraction cohort and compared available baseline and survival related characteristics between included and excluded patients. As shown in Supplementary Tables S4 and S5, missingness was mainly observed in selected tumor related variables, and some differences were noted between included and excluded patients. These findings suggest that the final analytic cohort should be interpreted as a complete case population with relatively complete clinicopathological documentation.

A total of 1,751 eligible patients diagnosed between 2011 and 2021 were included in the training cohort, and 751 patients diagnosed between 2000 and 2010 were included in the temporal validation cohort. As presented in Table 1, demographic, metastatic, tumor-related, lymph node, and treatment-related characteristics were generally comparable between the training and temporal validation cohorts, with all between-cohort comparisons showing P > 0.05. No significant between-cohort difference was observed in sex distribution (P = 0.24), tumor size distribution (P = 0.49), tumor extension distribution (P = 0.85), lymph node involvement (P = 0.24), chemotherapy status (P = 0.69), surgery status (P = 0.173), or radiation status (P = 0.81) [25]. Metastatic pattern analysis in the training cohort showed relatively low incidence rates of bone metastases (5.4%) and brain metastases (0.5%), consistent with published literature [26]. Beyond intrahepatic spread, lung metastasis was observed in 8.8% of patients in the training cohort. When stratified by a 5-cm tumor size cutoff, tumors ≥ 5 cm accounted for 59.8% of the training cohort and 61.3% of the temporal validation cohort. According to SEER classification criteria, patients were categorized into four groups based on tumor number and hepatic lobe involvement, and solitary tumors confined to a single lobe constituted the largest subgroup in both cohorts. Multiple tumors involving more than one lobe accounted for 36.8% of patients in the training cohort and 37.2% in the temporal validation cohort.

Nomogram validation

Discriminative ability was evaluated using both the C-index and ROC curve analysis. The C-index measures the model’s ability to correctly rank patients by their risk of death, while ROC analysis evaluates the balance between sensitivity and specificity across different thresholds. In the training cohort, the model achieved a C-index value of 0.76 (95% CI= 0.74-0.78). The AUC values for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5- year overall survival (OS) were 0.83 (95% CI = 0.80-0.84), 0.84 (95% CI = 0.82-0.86), and 0.84 (95% CI = 0.82-0.86), respectively in Fig. 4A. Ten-fold cross-validation (internal validation) yielded a mean AUC of 0.81, confirming stable predictive performance in the training set in Fig. 4B. Time-dependent AUC analysis in Fig. 4C revealed consistent predictive performance in the training cohort throughout the 5-year follow-up period ranging from 0.74 to 0.83. Internal validation was further performed through bootstrapping (5,000 resamples) to evaluate the stability of the time-dependent AUC. The bootstrap corrected estimates maintained robust discriminative performance (range: 0.74–0.82) Fig. 4C. In the temporal validation cohort, the model achieved a C-index of 0.73 (95% CI= 0.705-0.759). Temporal validation showed good discriminative performance, with AUC values of 0.83(95% CI= 0.80-0.84), 0.83(95% CI= 0.81-0.85), and 0.83(95% CI= 0.81-0.85) for 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival (OS) predictions, respectively Fig. 4D. In the meantime, we further compared the predictive performance of the risk prediction model developed in this study with that of the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system Fig. 4E. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis revealed that the TNM staging system achieved areas under the curve (AUCs) of 0.67(95% CI= 0.66-0.68), 0.72(95% CI= 0.70-0.74), and 0.77(95% CI= 0.75-0.79) for predicting outcomes at 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year time points, respectively. In contrast, the risk prediction model developed exhibited superior discrimination across all time points. The IDI values for 1-,3-,5-year were 0.197 (95% CI = 0.16-0.23, P < 0.001) ,0.393(95% CI=0.34-0.44,P < 0.001) and 0.238 (95% CI = 0.19-0.28, P < 0.001). Calibration curve analysis revealed excellent agreement between predicted and observed probabilities in both cohorts in Fig. 5A. Specifically, our nomogram outperformed the TNM staging system in terms of calibration, as evidenced by lower Brier scores at 1 year (0.139 vs. 0.178 at 1 year; 0.147 vs. 0.191 at 3 years; 0.130 vs. 0.165 at 5 years). Notably, model optimism was minimal across all time points (-0.003 to 0.003), indicating favorable internal validity and stable predictive performance. In the temporal validation cohort in Fig. 5B, the calibration curve analysis showed good agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities Decision curve analysis in Fig. 5C demonstrated significant clinical net benefits (NB) across three prediction intervals, suggesting increasing clinical utility with longer follow up durations. These findings were generally consistent in the temporal validation cohort in Fig. 5D, supporting the temporal stability of the model within the SEER population. Sensitivity analyses showed broadly similar discrimination across categorized, continuous, and restricted cubic spline-based age specifications (Supplementary Table S8). Exploratory restricted cubic spline analysis showed no significant nonlinear age effect (P for nonlinearity = 0.681).

Risk Stratification Model Development and Validation

Using the final model,risk scores were computed for patients in the training cohort (range: 0.065–2.291) and dichotomized into high- and low-risk groups based on the median cutoff (1.0813) in Figs. 6 and 7. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with log-rank testing revealed statistically significant differences in survival outcomes between risk groups (P < 0.001) in both training and temporal validation cohorts. Notably, high-risk patients consistently experienced the poorest clinical outcomes, whereas low-risk patients exhibited the most favorable survival findings that strongly support the model’s ability to effectively stratify patient risk.

Discussion

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) is a highly aggressive malignancy associated with a poor prognosis [4]. To improve clinical outcome prediction, we developed and temporally validated a prognostic nomogram for ICC patients using population based SEER data.Although prior studies have developed nomograms for ICC based on SEER data, the current model represents several key advancements. First, it extends beyond conventional demographic and tumor related factors by incorporating treatment status as predictive information. Notably, radiotherapy status was retained as a predictor in the multivariable model,although this should be interpreted as a predictive association rather than evidence of treatment efficacy.Second, the model employs a refined classification of tumor extension, while considering the number of tumors and the involvement of liver segments, thereby more effectively capturing the anatomical complexity compared to models that rely solely on tumor size. Third, rather than relying solely on internal validation, we used a temporally distinct SEER cohort to assess model stability across different diagnostic periods. This strategy provides a more stringent assessment than random split sample validation alone, but it should not be interpreted as true external validation because both cohorts were derived from the SEER database. Finally, the resulting nomogram allows a practical and granular risk stratification, and effectively distinguishes patient subgroups with significantly different survival outcomes, which offers a practical and significant improvement for clinical applications compared to conventional TNM staging system.

Traditionally, the TNM staging system has been considered the standard for predicting prognosis in patients with ICC, demonstrating significant correlation with overall survival (OS). However, the 8th edition AJCC TNM staging system has notable limitations in clinical practice, as patients within the same stage often exhibit heterogeneous outcomes [6, 7]. This discrepancy primarily arises from two key shortcomings: (1) the system’s exclusion of baseline characteristics such as age and sex, and (2) the failure to incorporate critical treatment related factors including surgery and adjuvant therapy. To overcome these limitations, our study strictly followed the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines to develop a multidimensional prognostic nomogram integrating clinicopathological features and treatment variables [28]. Beyond its statistical robustness, the proposed nomogram delivers tangible clinical utility that complements and extends the conventional AJCC TNM system. By incorporating patient specific factors (e.g. age and sex), tumor biological behavior (as reflected by extension patterns), and treatment status, it generates individualized survival estimates—addressing a major limitation of TNM staging, which is largely restricted to anatomical extent.

This holistic assessment may be useful in multidisciplinary tumor board discussions by providing individualized prognostic information for patients with complex clinical presentations, including those with borderline resectable disease. Moreover, the dynamic risk stratification enabled by the model facilitates personalized surveillance strategies; individuals classified as high-risk may benefit from intensified monitoring, while low-risk patients could be spared unnecessary interventions. The nomogram’s graphical interface also supports shared decision making, allowing clinicians to visually communicate the influence of various prognostic factors during patient consultations. Last, by incorporating treatment related variables, the tool remains relevant across the entire clinical trajectory—from preoperative counseling to postoperative prognosis refinement—representing a significant advancement beyond the static nature of TNM classification.

Variable selection for our model was a deliberate process extending beyond mere statistical significance, guided by a combination of statistical criteria, evidence from the literature, and practical clinical experience. Although a 2022 study reported an increasing incidence rate among females, ICC remains more prevalent in male populations [29–31]. Our multivariate Cox regression analysis also identified sex as an independent risk factor for ICC prognosis (HR=1.20, 95% CI: 1.11-1.31, P<0.001), consistent with prior findings. Therefore, sex was included in the final model. Previous retrospective studies have identified advanced age as an independent predictor of poorer OS in ICC patients [32, 33]. Our results in Table 2 and Fig. 2 (forest plot) align with this evidence, confirming age as an independent risk factor for OS, with its prognostic impact intensifying with advancing age. In the AJCC TNM staging system, tumor size is a key determinant for staging, with 5 cm serving as the primary cutoff [34]. This study adopted the same threshold for tumor size stratification.Although most existing clinical prediction models for ICC incorporate tumor size as a core prognostic factor [16, 35]. Prior studies have demonstrated that liver and lung metastases are associated with similar median survival times, whereas bone metastases confer the worst prognosis among distant sites [36–38].Although distant metastasis is classified as a key staging criterion in the 8th edition AJCC TNM staging system [39], distant metastatic-site variables did not meet the stricter predefined retention threshold of P < 0.001 in the present model and were therefore not retained in the final parsimonious nomogram. However, bone, liver, and lung metastases still showed adjusted associations with OS at the conventional P < 0.05 level, whereas brain metastasis was not significant in the multivariable model. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as model-retention results rather than evidence that distant metastasis has no prognostic relevance in ICC. Given that liver metastasis is the most common metastatic pattern in ICC , a more detailed stratification of the clinical characteristics of liver metastases is warranted. Current evidence regarding prognostic differences between multi-lobar and single lobe tumor involvement remains limited. Due to intricate hepatic anatomy, the spatial distribution characteristics of tumors not only correlate significantly with their biological behavior but also critically influence surgical resection margins (particularly the feasibility of R0 resection) and subsequent therapeutic decision making, thereby exerting substantial effects on patient outcomes [40, 41]. In addition to the well established prognostic significance of tumor size and number, this study further investigated the relationship between tumor distribution characteristics (including both tumor number and hepatic lobe localization) and patient prognosis [8, 9].

In the management of ICC, surgical resection remains the primary curative-intent treatment [42].Although the overall curative rate is limited, previous evidence has reported a median disease-free survival (DFS) of 34 months after surgery in selected patients [43]. Regarding systemic therapy, prior studies have suggested that chemotherapy is associated with improved survival outcomes in ICC patients, including those with advanced-stage disease [44]. Radiotherapy has also been investigated as an important therapeutic modality in ICC management. A systematic review of retrospective studies by Yi-Xing Chen et al. reported that radiotherapy was associated with improved local disease control and overall survival [21], and its potential role has been discussed in both adjuvant and definitive treatment settings [26, 27]. However, these treatment-related findings should be interpreted cautiously in the context of retrospective registry-based prediction modeling. Treatment allocation may be influenced by indication, tumor burden, resectability, performance status, comorbidities, treatment intent, and institutional practice patterns. Moreover, the SEER database does not provide detailed information on chemotherapy regimens, chemotherapy intent, number of cycles, radiotherapy dose or field, surgical margin status, or sequencing of multimodal therapy. Therefore, in the present model, surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy should be interpreted as predictive variables associated with survival rather than causal indicators of treatment efficacy.

While the prognostic relevance of factors such as age, lymph node status, and sex is well established in ICC, existing models often do not integrate these factors with treatment status into a practical individualized prediction framework. The present study addresses this gap by developing a nomogram that synthesizes demographic, tumor-related, and treatment-related information for individualized survival prediction. In the present model, radiotherapy status was retained as a survival-associated predictor in the multivariable analysis; however, this association should be interpreted cautiously because treatment allocation may be affected by indication, tumor burden, resectability, performance status, treatment intent, institutional practice patterns, and other unmeasured confounders. Therefore, the nomogram should be understood as a predictive tool for prognostic assessment rather than an explanatory model for estimating treatment effects. Clinically, the model may provide individualized prognostic information that can be considered alongside established clinical, radiological, and multidisciplinary assessments. It may also support postoperative risk stratification and follow-up planning, with high-risk patients warranting closer surveillance according to clinical judgment. The visual format of the nomogram may facilitate communication of individualized prognosis during patient consultations, but it should not replace individualized multidisciplinary treatment decisions.

The use of a priori defined candidate predictors should also be interpreted in context. This approach was adopted to improve clinical interpretability, reduce reliance on purely data-driven variable selection, and ensure that the model was based on routinely available variables in the SEER database. However, it may also limit the novelty of individual predictors and may omit prognostically relevant information that is unavailable in SEER, such as molecular markers, laboratory parameters, performance status, detailed chemotherapy regimens, radiotherapy dose, surgical margin status, and treatment intent. Therefore, the novelty of the present study does not lie in identifying entirely new prognostic covariates, but in integrating established, clinically accessible demographic factors, tumor-related variables, lymph node involvement, tumor extension, and treatment status into a transparent nomogram for individualized 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS prediction, with evaluation through internal and temporal validation within the SEER population.

The use of complete-case analysis and the resulting exclusion criteria deserve careful consideration. In the SEER extraction cohort for 2011–2021, 10,104 patients had histopathologically confirmed intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, whereas 1,751 patients were retained in the final training cohort after applying the complete-case criteria. Thus, 8,353 patients were not included in the primary model-development cohort, corresponding to an exclusion rate of approximately 82.7% among histologically confirmed cases. The largest source of exclusion was missing, blank, or unknown tumor size information, followed by missing metastatic-site information, tumor extension, and treatment-related variables such as chemotherapy or radiotherapy status. These variables were required for nomogram construction and were therefore not treated as optional predictors.

This high exclusion rate may limit the representativeness of the final analytic cohort. Patients with complete tumor size, extension, metastatic status, and treatment information may differ systematically from excluded patients, for example by diagnostic work-up intensity, disease stage, treatment exposure, institutional reporting completeness, or survival duration. Consequently, the model may perform best in patients with relatively complete clinicopathological documentation and may be less generalizable to patients with incomplete staging or treatment records. Because several SEER variables were coded as “unknown” or “blank” rather than missing continuous measurements, multiple imputation would have required assumptions about tumor burden, extension, metastatic status, and treatment receipt that could not be verified in this registry-based study. We therefore chose a complete-case approach, but acknowledge that this decision may introduce selection bias and should be considered when interpreting the nomogram. For patients with incomplete key information, model-based predictions should be interpreted cautiously or deferred until the necessary clinical data are available.

Conclusion

In summary, this temporally validated nomogram showed favorable predictive accuracy, clinical utility, and risk stratification ability for survival outcomes in ICC patients within the SEER population. Further validation in independent non-SEER cohorts is warranted before broader clinical application.
