Ready for interdependent or independent or unsupervised or autonomous practice? What's in a name?
Authors: Olle ten Cate
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Short abstractWhat does ready for practice mean? The goal of competency‐based education is entrusted autonomy. Readiness for unsupervised practice reflects growing professional judgement, accountability, and the ability to thrive in an interdependent healthcare team. #MedEd

In an interesting study in this issue of Medical Education, Lingard et al address how health professionals often work in teams but are assessed as individuals while in training. 1 To summarise my understanding of this work, the research question Do clinicians discern interdependence when they judge trainee performance? was generally confirmed. However, many clinicians had difficulty recognising whether this was (in the authors' terminology): supportive interdependence (relying on help of others with one's own work) or collaborative interdependence (working together because the task inherently requires teamwork).

While the study is rather specific, the topic connects with broader questions about the aim of workplace‐based assessment. How do we call what we are training for? As we shall see, language is central to evaluating learners and determining their readiness for practice. It is good to think about the words we choose for this core component of the clinical educator task.

Workplace‐based assessment (WBA) differs from written or online assessment by its focus on contextual competence (the ability to work in a clinical context) as opposed to canonical competence (context‐independent knowledge and skill that all physicians or specialists in a domain should possess). 2 Working in a clinical context inherently includes collaboration with others and therefore interdependence. I would personally use the word ‘interdependence’ as mutual dependence (parties depending on each other) and ‘dependence’ as relying on help of others for one's own work.

With the advent of entrustable professional activities (EPAs) and entrustment decision making in competency‐based education (CBE), various terminologies have been proposed and used to signify the aim of readiness for practice. For example, the new regime of general surgery training in the United States simply uses ‘practice ready’ as the target level (preceded by levels of ‘limited participation’–‘direct supervision’–‘indirect supervision’). 3 The Dental Accreditation Standards in the United States state that ‘Graduates should possess the basic knowledge, skills, and values to practice dentistry, independently, at the time of graduation’. 4 Recommended terminology when making summative entrustment decisions include ‘unsupervised practice’ as a ‘level 4’ on an entrustment–supervision (ES) scale. While these words all roughly equate with the aim of autonomous practice, that is, practising with a specified level of autonomy, and while generally there seems to be consensus about what the goal of health professions education is, digging into the details of these terminologies opens up a pandora's box of interpretations.

Various terminologies have been proposed and used to signify the aim of readiness for practice.

Some suggest that the goal of ‘independent practice’ is a flaw of CBE with EPAs because no health professional works independently. 5 However, this interpretation of the word may be a misunderstanding. Independent of an educational supervisor does not mean independent of colleagues or a team. Nevertheless, in current ES scales, the word independent has been replaced by ‘unsupervised’ to avoid the confusion in this respect. At the same time, however, not all health professionals always work in teams. For example, dentists, physical therapists and some other primary care providers work largely alone or in very small teams. While dentists see ‘independent practice’ as a goal, physical therapists may prefer ‘unsupervised’. 6

Independent of an educational supervisor does not mean independent of colleagues or a team.

‘Unsupervised’, my own preferred term, also has its limitations. If we want to qualify a learner for a specific EPA as ready for ‘unsupervised’ practice before a programme is completed, we can run into trouble. While justified based on a learner's competence and on acceptable patient safety risks, legislative rules usually prohibit unsupervised practice so long as one is in training. This can be resolved by ‘supervision at a distance’ with the ultimate responsibility for the trainee's actions still remaining with a supervisor. This is what ‘summative entrustment’ during training means, but it can make educators feel uncomfortable. However, if clinical supervisors would not be willing to take responsibility for a trainee's unsupervised practice before graduation, why would they then be willing to sign off for completion of training? This prospective educator responsibility is important, but not easy to operationalise. Goldhamer et al have proposed a ‘promotion‐in‐place’: ‘Residents deemed competent for early graduation can transition to attending physician status within their training institution and benefit from a period of “sheltered independence” until the standard graduation date’. 7 Here, ‘independence’ is combined with a supervisory safety net. It also relies on the trainee's awareness of limitations and willingness to secure help if needed. This trainee quality must be judged and weighed into such major entrustment decisions and reflects readiness for interdependence.

Ready for ‘interdependent practice’ would not be a logical expression to use when qualifying for readiness for practice, but it does have the beauty of showing how the learner would be ready to be relied upon by others; not only by patients, but by colleagues in health care as well, besides the trainee's reliance on them. This trust mutuality, necessary for interdependent practice, is likely what Lingard et al allude to. 1

‘Autonomous practice’, the fourth option, is also not regularly used in ES scales, but it does link to the essence of the freedom to make own choices. This concept, from Greek ‘auto’ (self) and ‘nomos’ (law or rule), signifies self‐governance. Autonomy does not exclude external rules and boundaries. The term ‘relational autonomy’ was introduced to stress a context of interdependence. Deciding to entrust a trainee with critical responsibilities without supervision aims at this type of autonomy. Unsupervised practice by medical trainees does not imply autonomy without oversight but the fading of required educational supervision. 8 Autonomy is a central concept in self‐determination theory. ‘Autonomy, self‐determination and will pertain to acts that are experienced as freely done and endorsed by the self’ as Ryan and Deci put it. 9 At a more general level, autonomy and self‐determination, both in a bounded sense, reflect the pursuit of mature professionalism, with an individualised component that adds a personalised layer to medical competence. 2

Deciding to entrust a trainee with critical responsibilities without supervision aims at this type of autonomy.

There is no optimal wording, but on a scale that is defined by levels of decreasing supervision, ‘unsupervised’ is probably the best choice. It reflects increasing autonomy, with a readiness to be entrusted with patient care as the aim. The incorporation of trust and the entrustment with unsupervised practice within CBE merge the need to be accountable with the pursuit of the individual freedom to develop—not to become fully independent, but to become a mature professional in an interdependent world.
