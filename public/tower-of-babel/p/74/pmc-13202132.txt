Role of allied health professions in falls prevention for community-dwelling older adults: a scoping review
Authors: Amy Lawton, Nicholas Tripodi, Rebecca Wospil, Breanna M Wright, Hylton B Menz, Suzanne Martin, Daniel R Bonanno, Corey Linton, Andisheh Bastani, Andrew Ross, Rees Thomas, Daniel Corcoran, Timothy McNamara, Danielle Baxter, Brett Vaughan, Rebecca Lane, Jack Feehan

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFalls among community-dwelling older adults are prevalent and have serious individual, societal and economic consequences. Allied health professionals provide key falls prevention interventions yet their representation in current clinical practice guidelines is inconsistent. Increased recognition of allied health roles and delivering context-specific guidelines for falls care could help to address workforce gaps and optimise care approaches. This scoping review explored the roles of the allied health professions in falls prevention screening, assessment and intervention for community-dwelling older adults.DesignScoping review, using the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for scoping reviews.Data sourcesPubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, Cochrane, Web of Science and Allied and Complementary Medicine Database databases. Introduction

Worldwide, older adults are experiencing unmet healthcare needs due to the availability, accessibility, affordability and acceptability of services.1 The global ageing population is contributing to increases in the prevalence of chronic disease, healthcare service demands and economic expenditure.2 3 Education and economic-based inequalities compound barriers to accessing healthcare, making improved understanding of healthcare services vital to address these challenges and improve access to care. The diverse range of non-medical professions which constitute allied health (AH) are an integral part of the healthcare system and have the potential to improve access to services and enhance health outcomes yet often lack integration into primary healthcare teams.4

A significant healthcare concern for older adults is risk of falling. Approximately one-third of community-dwelling older adults fall each year, with rates increasing with advancing age and in those with a history of prior falls or fear of falling (FOF).5 The consequences of falls for older adults are significant and include physical disability, diminished quality of life, financial burden, FOF and reduced self-efficacy, social isolation, heightened caregiver dependency and early admission into aged care.6 7 Almost 17 million years of life were lost due to falls in 2017 alone, reflecting the critical global public health challenge and imposition on healthcare systems.8 The significant health, social and financial impacts of falling underscore the need to improve falls prevention care in alignment with the evolving needs of an ageing population.

Current falls prevention guidelines advocate a multifactorial approach to screening, assessment and intervention of the physiological, behavioural and environmental determinants of falls risk.9 Falls screening aims to identify individuals with risk factors that are associated with an increased likelihood of falling to enable early intervention, while assessment gathers more in-depth clinical information to aid in differential diagnosis of associated conditions or to quantify falls risk.10 Falls risk screening and assessment involve both intrinsic physical factors such as balance, depression and vision, and extrinsic environmental factors such as trip hazards, inappropriate footwear and falls risk increasing drugs.9 Established interventions for falls prevention include exercise, environmental modification, medication reviews and psychosocial supports.11 As these assessment and intervention strategies require skillsets possessed by medical and AH professions (AHPs), further understanding of coordinated interdisciplinary involvement to provide comprehensive care is required.

AH refers to a diverse range of professional health disciplines that are not part of the medical or nursing professions.12 The professions included under the AH umbrella differ between jurisdictions; however, worldwide, AHPs make up approximately one-third of the health workforce, growing at a faster rate than the medical and nursing professions.12 In the context of community-dwelling older populations presenting to AHPs, both primary and secondary falls prevention care aims to stratify falls risk, identify and address risk factors within the professions scope of practice, and contribute to interprofessional care through effective referrals to address concerns outside of an individual’s practice scope.13 14 Despite many recommended falls assessments and interventions being within the scope of AH practice, few studies or guidelines reflect these roles across the breadth of the AHPs. Overlapping competencies are common in AHPs; for example, a balance assessment and exercise intervention could be conducted by physiotherapists (PTs), also known as physical therapists, osteopaths, exercise physiologists (EPs) or podiatrists, yet the current literature is weighted towards the larger profession of physiotherapy (PT).15 Some larger AH disciplines, such as PT and occupational therapy (OT) have, due to their size, a more extensive description of their falls prevention roles in comparison to smaller professions, such as osteopathy, podiatry, dietetics and orthotics and prosthetics that remain under-represented.16 17

Given the increasing demand for AH services in the ageing population, understanding the roles and capabilities of all AHPs in falls prevention care is required to guide improvements in practice, foster effective collaboration and enhance patient outcomes. While there is some evidence available on the specific AHP roles for falls prevention, there is no evidence that explores the breadth of scope across all AHPs.

This scoping review maps the evidence outlining AHPs roles in falls prevention for community-dwelling older adults. It evaluates the evidence available on the role of each AHP in falls screening, assessment and intervention, and identifies knowledge gaps. This evaluation of the literature allows for more effective utilisation of AHPs in the community to provide key fall-reducing services and improve the health and well-being of older people.

Methods

A scoping review methodology was chosen to evaluate a broad research question and the heterogeneous emerging evidence in this field.18 This is influenced by varied AHP definitions and scopes of practice, as well as significant differences in the volume of literature available for each profession.

Search strategy and selection criteria

This scoping review was conducted and reported following the Joanna Briggs Institute’s methodology for scoping reviews,19 as well as the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews) guidelines.20 The review is registered via Open Science Framework and can be found at: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/7SV3F

The eligibility criteria were informed by the research question and objectives and were defined using the Population, Concept, Context (PCC) strategy. In the context of the research question, the population were AHPs (online supplemental table 1) as defined by Allied Health Professions Australia,21 the concept was falls prevention and the context was community-dwelling older adults. The screening process was piloted with all team members to ensure comprehension and accuracy.

To be included, the sources of evidence must have identified or discussed a specific role of at least one AHP in falls prevention care for older adults. No limitations on publication type or date were imposed due to the paucity of literature in some areas of this topic and to avoid the potential omission of important information being used clinically but disseminated through non-standard scientific channels. Study protocols and conference abstracts were excluded. Due to limitations in being able to accurately and ethically translate documents, only sources in the English language were included.

Relevant studies were identified using a three-step approach.22 First, an exploratory search of MEDLINE informed a list of keywords and Medical Subject Headings terms. These key terms were then used to build a comprehensive search strategy for the PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, Cochrane, Web of Science and the Allied and Complementary Medicine Database databases. Each search string was tested using 10 preselected key articles that met the research question inclusion criteria, and an additional 10 articles cross-checked at completion of the searches to ensure validity of the search strategy. During the cross-checking phase it was identified that the descriptor ‘community-dwelling’ resulted in key sources being excluded and it was therefore omitted as a search parameter and instead used as a key inclusion criterion. The search strategies used are presented in online supplemental table 2. The initial search was completed in November of 2023 with a secondary search in July of 2025 to identify sources published since the initial search.

EndNote X9 (Clarivate, USA) was used to remove duplicates, after which records were imported into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Australia) for screening and data management. Selection of sources of evidence then occurred over multiple rounds. Due to the large number of papers an initial screen of title only was performed to exclude unrelated topics and studies of falls in hospital or institutional aged-care settings. This screening strategy was validated by two authors (AL and NT). Agreement between reviewers using this strategy exceeded 90% over 200 titles, after which author AL completed the remaining titles. Once the title-only screen was complete, title and abstract screening were undertaken using the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Both title and abstract screening, followed by full text screening, required agreement between two independent reviewers for each source, with any conflicts resolved via discussion among research team members.

Patient and public involvement

Patients, consumers, community members or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this scoping review which was based on published literature.

Data extraction and analysis

Data items of interest were drawn from the PCC framework, and a data charting table and guide were developed in Microsoft Excel.23 Data items included digital object identifiers, author, year of publication, country of origin, source and evidence type, study aims, AHPs, other professional and participant populations as indicated, methodology and a summary of the results.24 No modifications were made to the data chart during the extraction process. Data were initially extracted using ChatGPT-4o (OpenAI, USA). The prompts used for this process can be found in online supplemental table 3. The full data-extraction for all sources was then cross-checked by the researchers for accuracy and updated as required. Inaccuracies in the ChatGPT-4o extraction were particularly noted for complex components identifying roles of the AHPs, and all inaccuracies were updated by the cross-checking researchers to ensure rigour and integrity of the dataset.25 26 A numerical descriptive analysis, and a qualitative content analysis were performed to answer the research questions.24 The numerical descriptive analysis summarised the number and type of sources per AHP, alongside countries and year of origin. The qualitative content analysis identified the roles of the AHPs in falls prevention screening, assessment and intervention, and grouped them into categories of care. The following definitions were used during the content analysis9:

Results

The initial search in November 2023 identified 34 566 records, of which 23 650 titles and 17 090 abstracts were reviewed for relevance. Following title and abstract screening, 866 sources of evidence were identified as potentially relevant, and the full texts were obtained. The full text screening resulted in 370 sources of evidence from which data was extracted. An updated search was conducted in June–July 2025, which identified an additional 2511 sources, with 2093 titles and 1956 abstracts reviewed, resulting in 169 full texts screened. Following the full text screen, an additional 72 sources were identified as relevant. This resulted in a total of 442 sources included for data extraction. Falls prevention screening roles by AHPs

13 AHPs were identified in having roles in falls screening including audiology, chiropractic, dietetics, OT, optometry, osteopathy, paramedicine, pharmacy, PT, podiatry, psychology, social work and speech pathology, with all other professions not identified in this context (figure 4). PT was the most commonly identified profession involved in screening (n=147), followed by OT (n=69), paramedicine (n=32), audiology (n=32) and pharmacy (n=24). Within the falls screening domain, five broad categories of care were identified: falls history; medical; physical capacity; environment; and behavioural and psychological. Within these categories, 26 individual screening practices performed by AHPs were identified. PT were identified the greatest range of screening practices (n=22), followed by paramedicine (n=19), OT (n=18) and audiology (n=16). These professions had practices across all categories of care. Nine professions, including audiology, chiropractic, OT, optometry, osteopathy, paramedicine, pharmacy, PT and social work, were identified as collecting falls history as part of their screening roles. Medical screening practices were most commonly described across PT (n=9), OT (n=6), paramedicine (n=6) and pharmacy (n=5). Of the 13 professions with screening roles identified, 11 performed at least one physical capacity screen (eg, balance evaluation), 7 considered environmental hazards and 8 screened for behavioural or psychosocial risk factors related to falling (eg, FOF). In 46 instances across nine professions, the specific falls risk screening provided was unspecified.

Discussion

This scoping review explored the current literature on AHPs roles in screening, assessment and intervention for falls prevention in community-dwelling older adults. As the average age of the population increases, increasing numbers of falls will have significant impact on individuals, the economy and the healthcare system.6 This scoping review demonstrates that AHPs have diverse and overlapping skillsets in falls prevention. Increased understanding and utilisation of AHP is fundamental to improving falls prevention care, addressing workforce constraints and responding to evolving aged care standards and practices globally. The evidence identified in the current work should inform future research priorities and the development of practice guidelines that better reflect the scope of practice.

Profession variability

The 17 AHPs identified from the literature contribute a diverse range of roles to falls prevention screening, assessment and intervention in community-dwelling older adults. Among these, OT and PT were substantially more represented in the literature compared with other AHPs. These two professions were represented across the full spectrum of evidence types, providing a substantive evidence base encompassing both quantitative and qualitative perspectives on falls prevention care. This evidence base likely reflects the strong background of OT in home and environment modifications and PT in physical performance,21 27 in addition to their prominent place in healthcare systems and funding globally.28 29 The depth of evidence presented in the current work suggests that PT and OT participate in roles spanning all domains of screening, assessment and intervention underscoring their inclusion in falls guidelines worldwide.9

Through the current work, AHPs including dietetics, EP, optometry, paramedicine, pharmacy, podiatry, psychology and social work exhibited a growing evidence base with respect to roles in falls prevention, with these roles often aligned to their core scope of practice. For example, several sources of evidence identified EP as playing a role in exercise interventions and optometry with a role in vision and eye health. For many of the aforementioned professions, knowledge syntheses dominate the evidence base for their role in falls care with few primary studies on clinical utilisation and stakeholder perspectives. This outcome provides an opportunity for these professions to undertake further research to enhance their role in falls care.

Several AHPs were not well represented in the data. For some, due to overlapping scope of practice with other more prominent healthcare professions, it is plausible that an individual profession could still contribute to falls prevention care. For example, counselling, psychotherapy and psychology have inter-related theoretical and clinical frameworks and skills.30 In such cases, knowledge of professional scopes of practice enables readers to infer possible providers. However, absence of explicit attribution of the individual profession in the literature presents a barrier to recognition of their role as providers of falls prevention care. This limited, or non-existent, recognition may result in exclusion from clinical practice guidelines, regulatory recognition, health policy and funding frameworks. Exclusion from these aspects of the healthcare system may hinder access to services, leading to an underutilisation of some AHPs in falls prevention care. In contrast, the absence of some AHPs with narrow, clearly defined roles (eg, perfusion and sonography) reflects that their scope of practice does not align with falls prevention.21

Screening practices

Current falls prevention guidelines recommend that all healthcare professionals should routinely screen older adults for falls, balance issues or worry about falling.9 Among the AHPs identified as having screening roles, most reported engaging in at least one of these screening questions, although only audiology, OT and PT report all three. Conversely, arts therapy, nutrition, EP and orthotics and prosthetics were not represented. Absence of representation of the latter professions may be due to the lack of clarity in the way screening and assessment roles are reported in the literature, however further research into the professions’ practices is required to aid understanding of this finding.

Implementation of additional screening practices, sometimes outside of a profession’s primary scope of practice, but within their skill set is important to guide appropriate individualised referrals and optimise healthcare utilisation.31 Although many of the AHPs demonstrated screening practices across multiple domains of care, the data highlights key gaps in all categories of care. Although many of the professions did not report engaging in many of the medical screening components, it is plausible that these activities are being undertaken in practice but are not explicitly framed in the context of, or recognised as contributing to, falls prevention specific care. Alongside this, screening for some important falls risk factors such as frailty, sarcopenia and malnutrition9 were rarely identified across the AHPs. These observations suggest a potential gap in awareness, education, research or reporting which should be explored further both preprofessional and postprofessional education of healthcare professionals. As screening plays a critical role in guiding clinical decision making and referral pathways, further research is needed to determine the clinical application of screening components and guidelines for all AHPs working with older adults.

Assessment practices

Assessment practices guide clinical decision making for diagnosis and intervention and the findings in this review indicate that the assessment roles identified often align with primary scope of practice, although there are some notable exceptions. For example, optometrists primarily conduct vision and eye-health related assessments in line with their primary remit, whereas pharmacy who are primarily focused on medication management, engage in physical capacity assessments.21

Aligning with balance and strength as key falls risk factors,9 physical capacity assessments were the most frequently reported across the AHPs, with significant contributions from musculoskeletal AHPs including osteopathy, podiatry and chiropractic. These physical performance measures such as strength and gait speed are used for stratification of falls risk which allows the individual to be classified as high, medium or low risk, guiding the level and urgency of care required.9 The same commonly identified physical capacity assessments are used to ascertain risk of frailty and sarcopenia. Where physical performance assessment, falls risk stratification and recognition of these conditions were not identified as roles for the individual AHP, further exploration of how these strategies are being used clinically should guide further research or education opportunities to improve care. In the context of AHPs for whom assessment, risk stratification and recognition is a core function in care of older people, this should be represented in preprofessional training, while for other AHPs, such education could be offered as continuing education.

Intervention practices

Across the domains of medical, physical capacity, environmental and behavioural and psychological interventions, strategies for falls interventions were observed in the current work to predominantly align with the professions’ primary scope of practice, more so than in assessment or screening. Notably, education on falls risk factors emerged as a central intervention strategy for nearly all AHP in the current review, except for dietetics and EP. While it has been argued that patient education is traditionally the remit of the medical and nursing professions, thereby marginalising the potential of the AHP,32 the present findings indicate that AHPs are and should be, playing a more central role as educators on falls risk. This educator role emphasises the importance of formally recognising AHP contributions in all domains of comprehensive care for falls in older people.

Older adults are clear in their preference for shared healthcare decision making, including face-to-face interaction, opportunities to discuss care options and continuity in healthcare relationships.33 This review supports that AHPs are well-placed to provide person-centred falls care that incorporates shared decision making by meeting these requirements. These characteristics of person-centred care have been documented in multiple falls-prevention domains such as de-prescribing and exercise interventions.34 35 Furthermore, referrals to community resources or programmes, other healthcare providers and falls prevention programmes were noted across most of the AHP, reflecting an awareness of boundaries in scope of practice and the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration to address multifactorial risk factors.

Exercise: physiotherapy, osteopathy, chiropractic, podiatry and exercise physiology

Exercise is an effective falls prevention strategy, both as stand-alone intervention and as a component of a multifactorial intervention.9 In the included studies, professions with an identified role in implementing exercise programmes included PT, OT, EP, osteopathy, podiatry and chiropractic. Exercise interventions described for included a mixture of balance, strength and functional exercises, alongside existing programmes such as the Otago Exercise Programme.17

Although chiropractic and osteopathy were traditionally manual therapy focused AHPs,36 contemporary education and clinical practice have increasingly shifted towards evidence-based musculoskeletal management, with increased inclusion of therapeutic exercise.37 38 Within this context, the scopes of neuromuscular and community PT, and contemporary osteopathy and chiropractic demonstrate considerable convergence39 that extends to community based falls prevention. Clinical exercise physiologists40 have significant scope overlap with PTs in the use of exercise for non-musculoskeletal presentations, and with osteopathy and chiropractic for musculoskeletal presentations. However, EP as a profession is non-diagnostic41 and therefore has a narrower remit, reflected in this review data which identifies their intervention contributions within exercise interventions alone and often as part of a multidisciplinary team. Physiotherapy is more integrated across healthcare systems than osteopathy and chiropractic, a distinction reflected in the larger volume and breadth of falls related PT literature and inclusion in falls care practice guidelines. The substantial PT evidence base in the context of falls care offers a valuable reference point for clarifying and strengthening the roles in for the comparatively smaller professions of osteopathy, chiropractic and EP.

In the current review, podiatry had a role in creating individualised exercise programmes, most often related to the foot and ankle.42 Related to these roles, podiatry was also identified as providing footwear advice (alongside OT and PT) and prescribing foot orthoses to address lower limb biomechanical factors. Orthotics and prosthetics and pedorthist custom makers also use these strategies, yet they were not identified as doing so in the current work. However, reference to orthotics was present throughout the evidence without the identification of a specific AHP,43 highlighting the need for improved reporting. Additionally, despite not typically considered an exercise-focused AHP, OT had significant representation in providing exercise programmes for falls prevention in the sources of evidence. These exercise interventions were mostly delivered using existing programmes or individualised home programmes prescribed and monitored by PTs to minimise safety and efficacy concerns. Evidence supports exercise trained AHPs assisting non-exercise AHPs, as well as non-healthcare professionals such as carers and community peers, to instruct or monitor exercise interventions in older people at risk of fall.44 45 This evidence-base presents an opportunity to improve reach and cost-effectiveness of care, by broadening and diversifying those who can safely and effectively deliver exercise interventions.

The current review suggests that all the AHPs discussed should be considered as valuable providers of preventative exercise care to community-dwelling older adults. With exercise being a cornerstone of evidence-based falls prevention care, recognising all AHPs relevant in this domain is essential to providing accessible, quality care, particularly in contexts of limited AH access.

Home environment, home visits and care coordination: occupational therapy, physiotherapy, social work and paramedicine

Assessment of older adults within the home environment is an important component of falls prevention care, given home environmental hazards contribute to 30–50% of incidents.46 Home-based care facilitates multifactorial assessment and intervention of the home environment, functional behaviour and at home exercise programmes. Occupational therapists often deliver home assessments as part of a structured Environment Assessment and Modification protocol including a structured home environment and behavioural assessment, provision of adaptive equipment and aids, practical strategies to improve safety and performance and education about falls risk factors.47 In the current review, OT was more robustly represented in home-based environmental and behavioural strategies, and overlapped with PT in providing both exercise and environmental modification services. This role convergence highlights the potential professional flexibility when supported by appropriate training. Further, this finding underscores the importance of recognising the expertise each profession brings, particularly when managing complex or high-risk falls cases that require personalised multifactorial intervention.9 27

Although less frequently represented in the sources of evidence, social workers possess extensive knowledge across social, disability and health domains, equipping them to potentially have a key role in falls prevention.48 Their broad knowledge base enables them to provide person-centred care and facilitate change by connecting individuals with appropriate community and clinical services. This connection role is reflected in the review which identified roles for social workers in falls, mental health and cognition screening, assessments of daily activity, social support and housing and education and referral interventions. Social workers also frequently perform home and community visits which means they are well positioned to assess the environmental, behavioural and social determinants of falls risk within the home and community settings and work alongside other AH in goal setting and problem solving for behavioural change.48

Paramedicine is uniquely placed in the healthcare system, providing emergency medical response, assessing the need for hospital transport and providing prehospital care if required.21 As frequent first responders to falls incidents, they are the first line of defence against repeat falls and trials have shown potential success of these critical early interventions including medical and physical capacity assessment and home environment safety advice, in avoiding unnecessary hospital transportation, alongside effective referral to follow-up care.49 Although the contribution of paramedicine to falls prevention is clear in this review, some gaps exist in both the evidence base and practical utilisation of their scope, given the heterogeneity of the regulatory frameworks under which they operate. For example, although paramedics have pharmaceutical training, medication screening was not identified as a role in this literature. This is likely because paramedics typically administer medication under strict clinical guidelines, with strict decision-making frameworks. Additionally, in some countries, paramedics may have extended capabilities related to community and primary care, thereby positioning them to engage in falls prevention care in planned community services.50 In countries where paramedicine is largely focused on emergency response, there is considerable opportunity to incorporate community-paramedicine models of care such as those seen in Australia and the UK, thereby extending paramedicine’s involvement in falls prevention care.

Hearing and vision: audiology and optometry

Audiology and optometry care focus on screening, assessing and intervening in the falls risk factors of hearing and balance disorders, and ocular health and vision respectively.51 Although both professions are similar in the breadth of their primary scope of practice, audiology was found to screen and/or assess a wide range of falls-related factors across all domains of care. In contrast, optometry predominantly focused on ocular health and vision, with falls history information and stratifying falls risk being the only other identified roles. This disparity is unlikely to reflect limitations in optometry’s capability, but rather a need for improved understanding of the guidelines, their roles and referral pathways.52 This gap in understanding creates a barrier to receiving care, amplified by poor knowledge of other healthcare providers and consumers regarding services offered.53 These outcomes from the current review highlight the need for better understanding of these service providers through increased professional education and public awareness, as well as broader integration of optometry into interdisciplinary models of practice.

Medication: pharmacy

Pharmacy’s role in promoting the safe and quality use of medicines,21 is well supported in the review data with strong roles in medication review, medication recommendations and referrals to prescribing providers. The review data also documents engagement across all the domains of community falls prevention care identified. Pharmacists were particularly well-represented in collecting falls information, identifying and stratifying falls risk factors and assessing physical capacity, supporting a potentially under-recognised role in falls prevention. Pharmacists are uniquely positioned due to their accessibility to the public within community pharmacy settings, representing an opportunity to further integrate pharmacists within multidisciplinary healthcare teams and the importance of their explicit inclusion in falls prevention guidelines. Enhancing formal recognition and visibility of pharmacists may promote early identification of falls risk and improve care coordination.

Nutrition: dietitians and nutritionists

Poor nutrition, vitamin D deficiency and a range of nutrition-related disorders such as frailty are established risk factors for falling, with up to 30% of community-dwelling older adults meeting the criteria for malnutrition.54 In this review, the roles of both dietitians and nutritionists lie predominantly within their primary scope of nutrition-related practice, with dietetics more strongly represented in line with their clinical focus on the individual. Although nutrition management features prominently in the falls prevention literature,9 the providers are often not specified, a finding potentially explained by the overlapping scope of practice with others such as general practitioners and nursing who also provide nutritional advice. Integration of an AH nutritional assessment and management should be considered for all individuals to ensure adequate dietary intake of key nutrients which allow for exercise-related adaptation of the neuromusculoskeletal systems and safe implementation of the exercise programme.55 This is especially important for those with nutrition-related conditions such as frailty and sarcopenia, where there are complex interactions between falls risk factors and therefore require multifactorial interventions across multiple AH and medical fields.56

Behavioural and psychological domains

In falls prevention, psychologist-led interventions predominated in the behavioural-psychological domain encompassing roles in falls-related education, problem solving and cognitive strategies and psychological care for mental health. Although there were many references to psychological interventions throughout the reviewed data, it was often not clear who the provider was, likely due to the broad nature of psychological strategies and the overlap with behavioural interventions such as cognitive behavioural therapy used by other AHPs such as OT and PT. These findings suggest an important but under-recognised role for psychologists in falls prevention care, warranting further empirical investigation.

FOF, with or without fear-related activity restriction (FAR), is prevalent among community-dwelling older adults and is associated with reduced functional capacity, quality of life, social engagement and mental health.57 Screening for FOF should be done by all health professionals,9 which was not reflected in the current work. Behavioural, cognitive and exercise interventions are used for reducing FOF and FAR by improving self-efficacy58 and are documented in this reviews’ data as interventions delivered by OTs, PTs and social workers. Some intervention models exemplify interprofessional care, with psychologists supplying cognitive strategies during an outdoors walk alongside the client, while OTs reinforce these messages in subsequent sessions.59 Such interprofessional interventions demonstrate that allied health professionals can reinforce each other’s messages, provided effective communication systems are in place to coordinate care. This underscores the value of integrated, team-based care with effective utilisation of overlapping scope of practice to improve outcomes.

Gaps in the literature and future directions

This review identified key gaps in the literature on AHP roles in falls prevention by examining the problem, population and context, and their implications for healthcare access and utilisation.

There is the need for foundational research in numerous AHPs to better understand their roles in falls prevention care and document their current clinical practices. Overlapping scopes of practice should be further examined as an opportunity to provide improved access to falls care. Although utilisation of the profession with the most specialised knowledge in an area is commonly recommended in the falls care context, this needs to be balanced with factors such as availability, financial resources, the level of intervention indicated by the apparent risk, consumer autonomy in decision making and efficient use of healthcare resources, which all impact best care in any individual case.9 31 These factors are especially salient in regional, rural or other areas with limited healthcare providers or funding challenges.

Profession-specific perspectives on falls prevention care roles should be further explored to understand the contrasting perspectives within AHPs such as paramedicine and optometry regarding the extent of their roles, and whether smaller professions not represented in the data view falls prevention within their remit, despite its alignment with their scope of practice.

Future studies should aim to improve the reporting of the professions delivering falls care to aid translation of the research findings into practice. Improved reporting will act to decrease ambiguity in providers which places the onus on the consumer or healthcare professional to determine appropriate referral pathways, potentially creating access barriers, particularly in settings with limited-service availability.

Further investigation into falls prevention in key specific contexts is required. For example, in FOF, which spans cognitive, psychological, social and environmental domains, research should seek to understand how the different AHPs can contribute to care, and how they can work together to increase the quality and effectiveness of care. Special populations including cognitive decline, poststroke and visual impairment are present in the data, however further investigation is needed to understand how prepared AHPs are to adapt their roles to these circumstances. Several AHPs highlighted the need for further training to confidently provide falls prevention care and the nature of these skills and knowledge gaps should be further explored to guide tertiary and continuing education.

It is essential that current and future knowledge is operationalised via improvement in clinical guidelines, improved healthcare graduate knowledge of the full AHP spectrum, workforce development and service provision to ensure meaningful impact on patient care that reflects flexibility in AH utilisation.46 To aid operationalisation and understand impact, perspectives of the acceptability and clinical applicability of the AHP roles should be explored with older adult consumers, healthcare professionals and other relevant stakeholders.

Limitations

Several limitations are noted in this scoping review. First, some publications involving AHPs may have not been included in this review, as they did not readily identify AH within the title or abstract. This was a pragmatic decision to ensure a manageable quantity of data, and it is likely that many of these publications did not focus on AHPs roles sufficiently to add to the discussion. As a scoping review, we do not believe that this detracts from the impact of the discussion. second, some populations needing specialist consideration around ageing, such as First Nations people’s, may not have been adequately represented in this data. As risk of falls and other adverse outcomes associated with ageing commonly rises before 65 in these populations, they may not be comprehensively represented here and warrant a detailed stand-alone review. Finally, although practice guidelines recommend that falls prevention care should entail screening, assessment and intervention, many studies did not clearly distinguish between these different roles when discussing falls prevention strategies that fall within specific scopes of practice. Contextual information from the sources, alongside knowledge of AHPs scope of practice, was used to attribute the practice to the correct level of screening or assessment. However, this may have resulted in slight variations in the classifications.

Conclusion

This scoping review has demonstrated the broad range of knowledge and skills that AHPs bring to falls prevention care for community-dwelling older adults worldwide. The data supported assessment and intervention within the core scope of each profession, as well as demonstrating the primary care role that allied health professionals play in broader screening and referrals. Notwithstanding these profession-specific delineations, there is clear evidence of overlapping scope of practice, underscoring the potential for recognising a broader scope for some professions or the upskilling of AHPs to mitigate resource and service gaps, particularly in regional and rural settings and in contexts of workforce shortage. Further exploration of these roles, followed by the development of guidelines that highlight this breadth of skill, contextualised for the region’s healthcare landscape, will help to ensure policy makers, healthcare providers and consumers are more informed of who can be included in falls prevention care, with the potential to improve care and outcomes for older people living in the community.
