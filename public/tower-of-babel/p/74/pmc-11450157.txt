Prospective study of bipolar disorder and neurodegenerative diseases
Authors: Xinming Xu, Yaqi Li, Hanyu Lu, Han Wang, Yi Guo, Alexandru Dregan, Liang Sun, Yun Shen, Tingting Geng, Xiang Gao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBipolar disorder (BD) is linked to an increased risk of neurodegenerative diseases such as dementia and Parkinson disease (PD), yet several uncertainties still remain and the extent to which the associations could be explained by BD-related medications (antipsychotics, lithium, and antiepileptics) was unknown. This study included 501,233 UK Biobank participants (mean [standard deviation] age, 56.5 [8.10] years; 54.4% women), free of dementia and PD at baseline. After a median 13.8 year follow-up, 9422 cases of dementia and 3457 PD cases were identified. Participants with BD had a significantly higher risk of dementia (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] 2.52, 95% CI 2.00–3.19) and PD (adjusted HR 2.88, 95% CI 2.03-4.08). Findings suggest that up to two-thirds of the association of neurodegenerative diseases with BD may be mediated by BD-related medications. Further research is needed to confirm these findings and explore the underlying mechanisms.Subject terms: Neurodegenerative diseases, Epidemiology

Introduction

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a chronic severe mental illness characterized by recurring episodes of mania alternating with depression. Dysregulation in the dopaminergic system (hyperdopaminergia for the manic phase and hypodopaminergia for the depressive phase) has been considered as a pathological hallmark of BD1. Meanwhile, the role of dopamine has been well-established in pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases, such as Parkinson disease (PD) and dementia. For example, PD was featured in death of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra2 and reduced levels of dopamine and its receptors were reported in Alzheimer’s diseases (AD) cases3. There are also several other shared mechanisms between BD and neurodegenerative diseases, including neuroinflammation and dysfunction of the neurotransmitters (e.g., serotonin, and glutamate)4.

Several studies have linked BD with PD5–10 and dementia8,11–15, with additional evidence of a temporal association through prospective study designs7,8,16. Nevertheless, there are still many questions left unresolved within this field. For instance, it remains unclear whether BD acts as an independent risk factor for dementia and PD or if it merely presents as a prodromal symptom, given that other psychiatric syndromes such as depression and anxiety have been considered as both risk factors and early-stage clinical symptoms of neurodegenerative diseases17,18. The impact of BD medications (mood stabilizers and antipsychotics), which might lead to drug-induced parkinsonism that shares clinical similarities with PD, on the associations also remains uncertain. Additionally, whether all subtypes of BD are associated with neurodegenerative diseases requires further study.

The primary objective of this study was to prospectively investigate the associations between BD and neurodegenerative diseases (including dementia/dementia subtypes and PD) in over 500,000 adults and to further examine the proportion of the associations between BD and neurodegenerative diseases that could be mediated by BD-related medications (lithium, antipsychotics, and antiepileptics). To understand whether BD is a prodromal symptom of dementia and PD, we further examined the associations between BD and the onset of these neurodegenerative diseases at different times during the follow-up (the first 10 years of follow-up and beyond).

Associations of BD and neurodegenerative diseases

During a median follow-up of 13.8 years, we identified incident 9422 dementia cases (4307 AD cases and 2127 vascular dementia (VaD) cases) and 3457 PD cases. Incidence rates increased with age in 5 year increments and were greater in men compared with women (Supplementary Table 1). Compared to those without BD, participants with BD exhibited a higher risk of developing dementia (HR, 2.52; 95% CI, 2.00 ~ 3.19), AD (HR, 1.95; 95% CI, 1.29 ~ 2.94), VaD (HR, 2.90; 95% CI, 1.87 ~ 4.51), PD (HR, 2.88; 95% CI, 2.03 ~ 4.08), Parkinson’s disease dementia (PDD) (HR, 3.34; 95% CI, 1.07 ~ 10.45), and other specified degenerative diseases of nervous system (HR, 2.88; 95% CI, 1.43 ~ 5.79), after adjusting for age, sex, ethnicity, BMI, smoking status, education level, alcohol status, physical activity, coffee intake, history of cancer, cardiovascular disease (CVD), hypertension, diabetes, and polygenic risk score (PRS) for BD (BD-PRS) (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 2). We further categorized BD status into BD without and with concurrent use of mood stabilizers or antipsychotics, and found that the associations between BD status without concurrent use of mood stabilizers or antipsychotics and dementia or PD attenuated substantially but remained statistically significant; and people with both BD status and BD-related medication use (antipsychotics, lithium, or antiepileptics) had a higher risk of dementia or PD, compared to those without BD (Table 2). When considering BD subtypes, we found that participants with prevalent BD type I or other BD had higher risk of both dementia (BD type I: 1.59 [95% CI, 0.96 ~ 2.64]; other BD: 2.99 [95% CI, 2.30-3.89]) and PD (BD type I: 2.05 [95% CI, 1.02 ~ 4.11]; other BD: 3.33 [95% CI, 2.22-4.97]), compared to participants without BD (Table 3). Among respondents to the SCID-I questionnaire, individuals with BD type I displayed a higher risk of dementia (HR = 1.75; 95% CI, 1.05 ~ 2.92) and PD (HR = 2.16; 95% CI, 1.07 ~ 4.35) compared to those without mood disorders (Table 3), but no significant results were observed for individuals with BD type II.

Mediation analyses

Partial mediating effects on the association between BD and dementia were 25.3% (95% CI, 15.7 ~ 36.0) for antipsychotics, 17.5% (95% CI, 6.82 ~ 34.1) for lithium, and 29.7% (95% CI, 23.5 ~ 37.0) for antiepileptics (Table 4). Likewise, they showed mediating effects of 49.2% (95% CI, 31.9 ~ 80.7), 14.4% (95% CI, -1.28 ~ 33.4), and 28.1% (95% CI, 21.2 ~ 42.6) on the association between BD and PD. Of note, the direct effects, indicating the independent impact of BD on incident neurodegenerative diseases without the mediators (the three groups of medications in this case), were significant (Table 3). Multiple mediator analysis showed a combined effect being 56.6% (95% CI, 45.5 ~ 76.7) for dementia, and 69.2% (95% CI, 54.2 ~ 77.9) for PD (Table 4). The mediation results from the updated use of medications during follow-up were consistent (Supplementary Table 3).

Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses

Findings were generally consistent across multiple subgroups regarding the association between BD and dementia or BD and PD; except for age group (P for interaction = 0.01) in the association between BD and dementia (Supplementary Table 4 and 5). The adjusted HR for dementia was 3.65(95% CI, 2.43 ~ 5.47) in participants < 60 y, and 2.01(95% CI, 1.51 ~ 2.67) in participants ≥ 60 (Supplementary Table 4). After propensity score matching, the 2 groups (mentally healthy versus BD present) were comparable in all covariates (standardized mean difference < 0.10) (Supplementary Table 6). Similar associations between BD and dementia/PD were observed after matching (Supplementary Table 7). The association between BD and dementia was significant, with an initial HR of 2.16 (95% CI, 1.62 ~ 2,89) in the first 10 years of follow-up, which attenuated (HR = 1.70; 95% CI, 1.14 ~ 2.55) in the subsequent 10 year lagged analysis. Likewise, a similar pattern between BD and PD was observed, where a stronger association in the first 10 years (HR = 2.93; 95% CI, 1.69 ~ 5.07) than in the 10 year lagged analyses (HR = 2.22; 95% CI, 1.41 ~ 3.49) (Supplementary Table 8). Adjusted HRs did not vary in magnitude or statistical significance in other sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table 9-14).

Discussion

In this large-scale prospective study, we observed significant associations between BD and neurodegenerative diseases. Although the relationships between BD and neurodegenerative diseases were largely mediated by BD-related medications, 20 ~ 30% for dementia, and 30 ~ 60% for PD, BD per se remained an independent predictor for the risk of developing these neurodegenerative diseases.

The observed associations between BD and dementia/PD were consistent with previous studies (also two meta-analyses) that BD may be a risk factor for dementia and PD5–15,19. For example, two meta-analyses reported a pooled OR of 2.36 (95% CI, 1.36 ~ 4.09) for dementia and a pooled OR of 3.35 (95% CI, 2.00 ~ 5.60) for PD comparing people with vs. without BD11,19. Regarding BD types identified by SCID-I in our study, significant association was only observed in individuals with BD type I and the findings for BD type II remained inconclusive, possibly attributable to the extreme small sample size (only 9 dementia and 3 PD cases with BD type II) and thus cannot generate a stable estimate regarding the true association between BD type II and neurodegenerative diseases risk. Some studies have found that BD-related medication use itself can increase the risk of PD20 and most studies lacked consideration of medication use when assessing the relationship between BD and neurodegenerative diseases except two previous studies7,8. To date, the mediating roles of lithium, antipsychotics, and antiepileptics in the associations between BD and neurodegenerative diseases have not been studied.

There are some potential mechanisms that can explain the associations between BD and neurodegenerative diseases. First, dysregulation in the dopaminergic system has been observed in people with BD (reduction in dopaminergic activity). This hypothesis has been highly suggested in a recent study for the reason that mood changes (mania phase/depressive phase) are similar to the on-off phenomena in PD19. Second, changes in the serotonergic system, which contributes to the emergence of tremors and dyskinesias, and other non-motor symptoms, such as disturbed sleep, cognitive dysfunction, and neuropsychiatric symptoms, could explain the association between BD and dementia or PD4,21. Third, shared alterations in the hippocampal area, such as a decline in hippocampal neurogenesis, were observed in patients with BD and AD22. Fourth, BD is also associated with increased subsequent risk of PDD and other specified degenerative diseases of the nervous system (mainly dementia with Lewy bodies), where these two disorders had overlaps in abnormal aggregation of alpha-synuclein. Future study are warranted to find the role of alpha-synuclein in their potential aetiological association. Fifth, the similar profile of BD and AD could result from a neurotoxic environment, like the presence of inflammation, microglia activation, and oxidative stress, leading to cell death and tissue degeneration in the brain22. BD could also be a prodromal symptom of dementia or PD, as suggested by the observation that the magnitude of significant associations of BD with dementia and PD was more pronounced within the first 10 years, relative to those observed in the 10 year lag analysis. We regarded BD-PRS as a potential confounder at first, considering the possibility that the relationship between BD and dementia or PD might be potentially explained by the pleiotropic genes associated with BD, as suggested in a previous study23. The estimates for dementia or PD did not diminish upon adjustment for BD-PRS (data not shown), as evidenced by comparing the two models, one with and one without BD-PRS included in the full model.

The significant associations between BD and neurodegenerative diseases, could be attributable to BD-related medication. For example, antipsychotics could block dopamine receptors, potentially worsening parkinsonism and leading to misdiagnosis of PD, which is associated with mortality risk among PD or dementia patients24,25. A recent study indicated an association between antiepileptics use and incident PD, and a trend for a greater number of antiepileptics use with a higher risk of PD in the UK Biobank20. However, evidence supported the neuroprotective effect of lithium and its potential to modulate amyloid-beta and tau protein metabolism, and found the inverse relationship between lithium and the incidence of AD among BD patients26. The discrepancy could be attributed to the potential for medication usage to serve as an indicator of disease severity, in addition to the possibility that BD-related medication itself influenced the association. Of note, the observed associations of BD with dementia and PD were more pronounced in individuals with concurrent use of BD-related medications relative to those without, suggesting a potential combined effect BD and its related medications on the risk of neurodegenerative diseases. Furthermore, we found that antipsychotic use, as opposed to lithium, explained greater proportions for the associations between BD and dementia/PD in the mediation analysis. However, the information on BD severity and duration was not collected in the current study, which could lead to residual confounding.

Several limitations should be noted. First, it is plausible that BD is associated with drug-induced parkinsonism, which could be misdiagnosed or misrecorded as idiopathic PD although we merely included International Classification of Diseases (ICD)9 code (332.0) and ICD10 code (G20) as the outcome instead of other drug-induced Parkinsonism. However, we removed participants with concurrent BD-related medications and found similar results. Second, details on psychiatric illness were lacking (i.e., the number of psychiatric admissions and mood episodes) and information on age of BD onset identified by questionnaires or self-reported was not available. Third, participants with BD may be misclassified with depression. However, because the ascertainment of neurodegenerative diseases was independent of BD, any measurement errors are likely to be non-differential and bias the true associations towards the null. Fourth, age-specific dementia or PD incidence rates were substantially lower than in the general UK population as indicated in Supplementary Table 1. Unlike community-based cohort studies that conducted routine screening tests for older participants, our study might be susceptible to diagnostic bias because we identified dementia or PD cases from electronic linked health records. We attempted to mitigate this bias by further adjusting for the number of hospital inpatient events and found that the variable itself was associated with higher incidence of dementia (HR = 1.004, 95% CI: 1.004-1.005) or PD (HR = 1.003, 95% CI: 1.002-1.004). Notably, the associations between BD and the risk of dementia (adjusted HR, 2.46; 95% CI, 1.95 ~ 3.12) and the risk for PD (adjusted HR, 2.84; 95% CI), (2.01 ~ 4.04) remained stable after the adjustment (Supplementary Table 7). Fifth, the relatively young age of this cohort (participants from UK Biobank were 56.5 years old at baseline and the mean follow-up was of 13.8 y) limited the number of cases of PD and dementia, restricting to identify associations of neurodegenerative diseases at older ages. Sixth, subsequent medication usage during follow-up was restricted to a small percentage (<5%) of participants, which limited our ability to capture the BD-related medications started at a later stage in the entire population. Additionally, self-reported medication data, which may be underestimated as it only recorded concurrent prescriptions, could introduce measurement bias; however, a previous study showed a positive predictive value of 66.7% for self-reported BD medication records when compared to UK Clinical Record Interactive Search findings among a subset of UK Biobank cohorts (n = 854)27. Seventh, we could not establish a causal relationship between BD and neurodegenerative diseases because of the nature of the observational study. Last, healthy volunteer bias might occur because participants in the UK Biobank were more likely to be white British, women, well-educated, and from more affluent areas where rates of smoking and alcohol drinking were lower compared to the general UK population. This selection bias restricted the generalizability of findings.

In conclusion, our population-based study found that participants with BD had a higher risk of developing neurodegenerative later in life compared to those without BD after adjustments, and the observed associations were largely mediated by the BD-related medications. Early detection and targeted interventions for neurodegenerative conditions are crucial for those people diagnosed with BD. Future studies are warranted to further explore the role of BD-related medications in the aetiology of neurodegenerative diseases.

Methods

Study population

The UK Biobank is a large population-based prospective cohort, including over 500,000 volunteers aged between 40 and 69 years from 22 centers across England, Scotland, and Wales recruited between 2006 and 2010. Details regarding this study could be found at https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk. All UK Biobank participants provided informed consent via an electronic signature at the beginning of the process. The UK Biobank had ethical approval from the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee (REC reference16:/NW/0274). The present study was approved by UK Biobank Application 96083.

501,233 participants were included in this study after removing participants who withdrew from the UK Biobank and prevalent dementia or PD cases (n = 1274) (Fig. 1).

Ascertainment of BD

The BD status was determined by multiple sources, including hospital inpatient admission records based on the incorporated diagnostic International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 10 codes for BD (F30 and F31) at baseline and symptoms within the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV axis I disorders (SCID-I) (Data-Field 20126) (Supplementary Table 15 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Participants who met any of the above-mentioned criteria were defined as prevalent BD cases. In a prior study utilizing the UK Biobank, the prevalence of mood disorders was determined by categorizing participants in the final 2 years of the baseline recruitment phase according to their SCID-I questionnaire responses. This classification distinguished individuals with single or recurrent episodes of major depression, bipolar type I or type II disorder, or no apparent mood disorders, and those identified as having bipolar I or II disorder through the questionnaire were classified as having bipolar disorder status28. The classification of participants was subsequently validated using additional demographic and clinical data obtained from the UK Biobank, and the lifetime prevalence rates were found to align with similar estimates observed in the general population for each disorder among both sexes28. To avoid potential survivor bias associated with incident hospitalized bipolar disorder during follow-up, participants with incident bipolar cases were censored at the date of diagnosis of BD.

Assessment of the covariates

At baseline, data on sociodemographic factors (age, sex, education, and ethnicity), BMI, and lifestyle factors (physical activity, smoking status, alcohol use, and coffee intake) were collected through questionnaires and verbal interviews. Weight and height were measured by nurses during physical measurements. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters and classified into three categories based on the World Health Organization’s criteria: <25.0; 25.0–29.9; ≥30 kg/m2. Educational level was self-reported and categorized as University or College degree, A-levels or equivalent, O-levels or equivalent, and none of the above. Regular physical activity was identified as at least 150 min/week of moderate activity or 75 min/week of vigorous activity (or equivalent combination). Smoking status was self-reported as never, former, or current smoker. Alcohol drinker status was self-reported and classified as current, previous, and never. Coffee intake was derived from the question “How many cups of coffee do you drink each day” and categorized as <1, 1–3, and ≥4 cups of coffee per day. In addition, we obtained comorbidities information about cancer, CVD, hypertension and diabetes based on medical diagnoses from inpatient hospital data, self-reported data, and cancer registry data (Supplementary Table 16).

All self-reported medications (antiepileptics, antipsychotics, and lithium use) were recorded and subsequently confirmed by a trained nurse in an interview conducted during the same visit were collected at baseline (code list seen in Supplementary Table 17). We further updated the self-reported medications information based on online follow-up data available in a subset of participants (<5%).

We also considered BD-PRS as a potential confounder. BD-PRS was developed using meta-analysis of external genome-wide association study data only and definitions of BD phenotype used in those external studies and was standardized into the distribution of approximately zero mean and unit variance in each ancestry group (European/East Asian/South Asian/Sub-Saharan African/Native American).

Ascertainment of incident neurodegenerative disease cases

Incident dementia and PD cases were identified from the UK Biobank using hospital inpatient records where these conditions were documented as either primary or secondary diagnosis, or linkage to death register data using the ICD coding system (Supplementary Table 16). Subtypes of dementia including AD, VaD, PDD, and other specified degenerative diseases of the nervous system were further classified using the ICD codes (Supplementary Table 16). The accuracy of codes for identifying persons with dementia or PD by hospital admissions and mortality data in combination was validated in a subset of participants through clinical expert adjudication, with positive predictive values of 84.5% and 84.0% (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/ukb/ukb/docs/alg_outcome_main.pdf).

Statistical analysis

We summarized the baseline characteristics of the study stratified by BD. The incidence rates for dementia or PD were examined across 5 year age categories, starting at the age of 55 years, and were analyzed separately for both men and women. Person-time was calculated from the baseline to the occurrence of study outcomes, censor events (death, or incident BD diagnosis), or the end of follow-up (October 30, 2022 [England], August 31, 2022 [Scotland], and May 31, 2022 [Wales]), whichever came first. Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to examine the associations of BD with the risk of neurodegenerative diseases. We tested the proportional hazard assumption with Schoenfeld residuals and did not detect any significant violation of this assumption. The results were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), adjusting for age (years), and sex (women; men) in model 1. In full model, ethnicity (white British; others), BMI (<25.0; 25.0–29.9; ≥30 kg/m2), smoking status (current; former; never), education level (College or University degree; A levels or equivalent; O levels or equivalent; none of the above), alcohol drinker status (current; previous; never), regular physical activity (no; yes; missing indicator), coffee intake (<1;1–3; ≥4 cups of coffee per day), previous cancer (no; yes), previous CVD (no; yes), previous hypertension (no; yes), previous diabetes (no; yes), and BD-PRS (in quintiles) were further adjusted. We categorized BD status into BD without or with mood stabilizers or antipsychotics in the secondary analysis29. We also conducted the analysis limited to participants who responded to the SCID-I questionnaire and were not categorized as having single or recurrent major depression. The prevalence of missing data was low (<5%) on almost all covariates (Supplementary Table 18). In cases where variables had a missing rate ≥5%, the missing data was coded as an independent category; for variables with missing data below 5%, the missing data was coded with median values for continuous variables or categorized into the mode for categorical variables.

The study conducted a regression-based approach using the R ‘CMAverse’ package for mediation analyses. The method estimated effects on the HRs and 95% CIs, with two models estimating the mediation effects: a mediator model (logistic regression) and an outcome model (Cox regression). The mediator model examined the relationship between the exposure (BD, binary) and mediators (medications for BD, binary), adjusting for the covariates in the full model, while the outcome model examined the relationship between the mediator and incident dementia or PD (survival data), with the exposure, the mediator, and the covariates in the full model adjusted. The total effect was decomposed into direct effect (DE) and indirect effect (IDE), with IDE representing the effect of BD on incident dementia or PD that was explained by the mediators in the model, while DE represented BD’s effect on incident dementia or PD independent of the mediator. Nonparametric bootstrapping (500 times) was used to estimate 95% CI and P-values. The method estimated the proportion of the association by the mediator (IDE / [DE + IDE]) to quantify the magnitude of mediation. We also updated the mediator variables in the sensitivity analysis.

Stratified analyses were performed according to the following factors: age (<60; ≥60 years), sex (women; men), ethnicity (White British; others), smoking status (current; past; never), education level (college or university degree; A levels or equivalent; O levels or equivalent; none of the above), alcohol drinker status (current; previous; never), regular physical activity (no; yes), coffee intake (low; moderate; high level); history of cancer (no; yes), history of CVD (no; yes), history of hypertension (no; yes), history of diabetes (no; yes) and BD-PRS (quintiles). Interactions between BD and these factors, in relation to dementia or PD, respectively, were tested using the likelihood ratio test, with the adjustment for the aforementioned covariates.

To minimize the impact of confounding variables, we conducted sensitivity analysis using a propensity score matching approach. The greedy closest neighbour matching approach and a caliper value of 0.02 were used to determine propensity score matching. We matched participants with BD to those without any mental illness (1:2 ratio) on a range of covariates that were adjusted in the full model. After matching, all covariates had a standardized mean difference between the two groups that was <0.10 and were evenly distributed. We also conducted several sensitivity analyses to minimize the influence of bias: (1) calculated the hazard ratios for dementia or PD over two time intervals after baseline: during the first 10 years of follow-up or 10 year lagged analysis; (2) restricted to participants who were over 60 years old; (3) excluded neurological conditions (ICD10, G00-G99; i.e., epilepsy) at baseline to minimize diagnostic bias and physician bias, and also excluded CVD and schizophrenia at baseline to prevent affecting cognitive functioning and nonaffective severe mental illness; (4) restricted to participants with complete data on covariates; (5) multiple imputations for missing data with chained equations to test if missingness influence the result; (6) limited to participants with any BD-medication; (7) removed participants with concurrent use of BD-related medications to address confounding effects by indication or drug-induced parkinsonism or drug-induced dementia in the secondary analysis29; (8) defined BD only by hospital data to avoid selection bias; (9) defined BD from the SCID-I, hospital data (F30 and F31 within ICD10), and self-reported BD during nurse-led interviews; (10) additionally adjusted number of hospital inpatient events before the outcome or the end of the follow-up to account for availability of healthcare resources; or (11) applied the Fine and Grey competing risk model to account for the presence of competing events (death from all causes except for neurodegenerative diseases).

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 3.6.3. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.
