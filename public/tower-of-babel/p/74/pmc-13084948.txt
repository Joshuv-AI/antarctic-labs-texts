EGR1 lactylation induces tumor cell senescence and immunosuppressive microenvironment in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma
Authors: Yuan Liang, Rui Zhang, Yu Li, Hao Wang, Lei Qiao, Qingfa Bu, Zibo Xu, Zheng Liu, Ziyan Song, Tianning Huang, Mu Liu, Jinren Zhou, Qi Wang, Qiuyang Chen, Ling Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•ISG-high senescent tumor cells are associated with poor prognosis in iCCA.•Lactylation at EGR1 K422 promotes its nuclear translocation and induces tumor cell senescence.•Senescent tumor cells recruit immunosuppressive MACRO+ TAMs via LGALS9-CD44 and THBS1-CD47.•MACRO+ TAMs recruit Tregs through CXCL12-CXCR4 and enhance immunosuppression.

Introduction

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) is a malignant epithelial tumor arising from the bile ducts located above the confluence of the right and left hepatic ducts. It represents the second most common type of primary liver cancer after hepatocellular carcinoma and accounts for approximately 15% of all liver cancer cases globally [1,2]. iCCA is characterized by remarkable biological aggressiveness and extremely poor clinical outcomes. In recent years, iCCA has become one of the most lethal hepatic malignancies posing a serious threat to human health [3,4].

Despite recent advances in targeted therapies and immunotherapies, the overall survival of patients with iCCA remains poor. Cellular senescence (CS) is a stable and essentially irreversible state of cell cycle arrest, accompanied by progressive structural and functional deterioration [5]. CS is involved in a wide range of physiological and pathological processes. Early studies suggested that CS acts as a barrier to tumorigenesis, functioning as an intrinsic tumor-suppressive mechanism that limits malignant transformation and tumor progression [6]. However, accumulating evidence suggests that senescent cells can instead facilitate tumor progression toward a more aggressive phenotype by secreting senescence-associated secretory phenotypes (SASPs), a heterogeneous group of factors that includes cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and matrix metalloproteinases. These SASP components can profoundly remodel the tumor microenvironment (TME), thereby promoting immune evasion and tumor progression [7]. iCCA is a highly aggressive malignancy that arises within a complex TME characterized by extensive stromal expansion and dynamic interactions among cancer-associated fibroblasts, extracellular matrix components, and immune cells [8,9]. Notably, immunosuppressive cell populations—including myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and regulatory T cells (Tregs)—play critical roles in establishing an immunosuppressive TME, thereby driving iCCA progression, poor clinical outcomes, and resistance to immunotherapy [9,10]. Nevertheless, the mechanisms by which senescent cells interact with immunosuppressive immune cells within the TME to promote iCCA progression remain poorly understood and have not yet been systematically explored. Recent advances in multi-omics technologies, particularly single-cell and spatial transcriptomics [11,12], have enabled the comprehensive dissection of cellular composition, cell states, and intercellular interactions within tumors at single-cell and spatial resolution. These advances provide a powerful technical framework for investigating the role of cellular senescence within the complex tumor microenvironment.

In this study, we leveraged bulk transcriptomic data and previously reported senescence-associated gene signatures to systematically construct an iCCA senescence-related gene signature (ISG) comprising 101 genes. We found that senescence-related features were significantly elevated in iCCA tumor tissues compared with normal tissues, and patients with high ISG expression (ISG-high) exhibited pronounced immunosuppressive features and a poorer prognosis. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis further revealed that malignant cells exhibited the strongest senescence signals, and that the ISG-high malignant cell subpopulation displayed enhanced proliferative and stem-like properties. This subpopulation also exhibited marked metabolic reprogramming, mediated by the transcription factor EGR1, which synergistically activated senescence and glycolytic pathways to shape a distinct transcriptional program. Mechanistically, the K422 site of EGR1 undergoes lactylation, thereby enhancing its nuclear translocation, which in turn promotes CDKN1A transcription and induces a senescent phenotype. Furthermore, the cell-cell interaction network revealed that ISG-high malignant cells recruited and modulated immunosuppressive MACRO+ TAMs and Tregs, collectively establishing an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.

Methods

Collection of iCCA datasets

RNA-sequencing data and corresponding clinical information for iCCA were obtained from multiple public sources. This study incorporated five independent iCCA cohorts: TCGA-iCCA from The Cancer Genome Atlas (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), GSE32225 [13], GSE107943 [14], GSE89747 [15] from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.govgeo/), and the FU-iCCA cohort from Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University [16]. Additionally, three iCCA scRNA-seq datasets, GSE138709 [17], GSE171899 [18], and GSE189903 [17], were downloaded from the GEO database for high-resolution cellular analysis.

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the prcomp function in R based on cluster-averaged gene expression values, and results were visualized using the factoextra package (v1.0.7). For comparative analyses of malignant cell subpopulations, average gene expression levels were calculated for each defined malignant cell cluster. PCA was subsequently conducted on the resulting cluster-averaged expression matrix.

Gene set variation and signature scoring

Gene signature scores (e.g., ISG score and AgingMap signature score) were calculated using Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) as implemented in the GSVA R package (v1.44.2) [18]. The gsva function was executed with default parameters(kcdf=’Gaussian’; min.sz=1; max.sz=Inf) to estimate pathway activity enrichment.

Survival analysis

To evaluate the prognostic value of the ISG signature, survival analyses were performed in two independent iCCA cohorts with available follow-up data: the FU-iCCA cohort [16] (n = 159) and the GSE89747 cohort [15] (n = 81). ISG scores were calculated for all samples, and patients were stratified into ISG-high and ISG-low groups based on the median ISG score. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated using the survminer R package (v0.4.9), and differences in overall survival were assessed using the log-rank test.

Differential gene expression analysis

Differential expression analysis of bulk RNA-seq data was conducted using the limma R package (v3.52.1) [19], with an absolute log2 fold change ≥ 0.5 and an adjusted p value ≤ 0.05. For single-cell RNA-seq data, differentially expressed genes between ISG-high and ISG-low malignant cells were identified using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat (v4.1.1), using the default parameters of logfc.threshold = 0.25 and min.pct = 0.25 [20].

Functional enrichment and gene set enrichment analysis

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was performed using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.7.1.2) [21]. Pathways with an adjusted p-value ≤0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

Tumor microenvironment composition was characterized using the IOBR R package (v0.99.9) [22]. To evaluate the overall tumor microenvironment, the deconvo_tme function was applied with method = "estimate" to calculate immune and stromal scores. For quantification of specific immune cell populations, the same function was used with method = "quantiseq" to estimate the relative abundances of 10 immune cell types.

Single-cell data analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing data were analyzed using the Seurat R package (v4.1.1) [20]. Raw count matrices were filtered to exclude cells expressing fewer than 200 genes and genes detected in fewer than three cells. Data were normalized using the LogNormalize method with a scale factor of 10,000. The top 4000 variable features were identified using the vst method, and data scaling was performed while regressing out mitochondrial gene percentages. Batch effects were corrected using Harmony (v0.1.0) [23]. Cells were annotated based on established canonical marker genes.

Cell cycle and differentiation potential analysis

Cell cycle phases (G1, S, and G2/M) were assigned to individual cells using the CellCycleScoring function in Seurat. The differentiation potential of malignant cells was inferred using CytoTRACE (v0.3.3) [24], applied to log-normalized expression data with default parameters.

Metabolic pathway specificity analysis

A specificity score for metabolic pathways in malignant cells was calculated based on Jensen–Shannon divergence using the philentropy R package (v0.6.0) [25].

Single-cell gene regulatory network analysis

Regulon activity scores were inferred from raw count matrices using pySCENIC (v0.9.19) [26]. Protein activity in malignant cells was predicted using regulatory interactions from DoRothEA (v1.2.2) [27]. Signed transcription factor–target interactions with confidence levels A, B, or C were retained for downstream analyses. The TRRUST database [28] was queried to identify transcription factor–target regulatory relationships, and regulatory networks were visualized using the netplot R package (v0.1.1).

Intercellular communication analysis

Intercellular communication was inferred from scRNA-seq data using the CellChat R package (v2.1.2) [29]. Differential ligand–receptor interactions and signaling pathway activities were identified and visualized between predefined cell groups, including ISG-high and ISG-low malignant cells.

Cell culture and lactate treatment

Cholangiocarcinoma cell lines (HuCCT1, RBE, and SB1) were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Shanghai Institute of Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences and maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% carbon dioxide and cultured in complete medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. HuCCT1 and SB1 cells were grown in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco), whereas RBE cells were cultured in DMEM (high glucose; Gibco). All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and routinely tested negative for mycoplasma contamination prior to experiments. To simulate stimulation by glycolysis-derived lactate, cells were treated with sodium lactate (NaLa) at a concentration of 30 mM. Untreated cells served as the control.

Generation of EGR1k422rknock-in cell lines

CRISPR/Cas9-based gene editing was employed to generate EGR1K422R point mutation knock-in cell lines in HuCCT1, RBE, and SB1 cells. A sgRNA targeting the region adjacent to the EGR1 K422 site was designed, and a single-stranded oligonucleotide (ssODN) containing the K422→R mutation was synthesized as a homologous recombination template. Cells were co-transfected with Cas9, sgRNA, and the ssODN, followed by monoclonal screening. Successful knock-in of the point mutation was verified by PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing. Confirmed clones were used for subsequent experiments.

Immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting

To detect lactylation modification of EGR1, cells were lysed after treatment with NaLa. For exogenous assays, Flag EGR1-transfected cells were subjected to immunoprecipitation (IP) using an anti-Flag antibody. For endogenous assays, IP was performed using an anti-EGR1 antibody, with IgG as a negative control. Immunoprecipitated proteins were separated by SDS PAGE and analyzed by immunoblotting with an anti-pan lysine lactylation (pan Kla) antibody. Input samples were used to assess protein expression and loading control. Lactylation levels were quantified by normalizing the Kla signal to that of EGR1 or Flag EGR1.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractionation

After NaLa treatment, nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were isolated using a Thermo Fisher Scientific nuclear cytoplasmic fractionation kit. The subcellular distribution of EGR1 was analyzed by immunoblotting, with β-actin and Lamin B1 serving as markers for the cytoplasm and nucleus, respectively.

Luciferase reporter assays

Different fragments of the CDKN1A promoter or its binding site mutants were cloned into a luciferase reporter vector. HuCCT1 cells were co-transfected with the reporter construct, EGR1 or EGR1K422R expression plasmids, and a control plasmid, followed by NaLa treatment. Luciferase activity was measured using a dual luciferase assay system and normalized to the control signal.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)–qPCR

Cells were cross-linked with formaldehyde after NaLa treatment. Chromatin was sheared and subjected to ChIP using an anti-EGR1 antibody, with IgG as a control. Recovered DNA was analyzed by qPCR to detect enrichment at different regions of the CDKN1A promoter. Results are presented as fold enrichment relative to IgG.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining

SA β-gal staining was performed in wild-type, EGR1K422R, and relevant control HuCCT1 cells. Positive cells were counted from multiple randomly selected fields, and the percentage of SA β-gal-positive cells relative to the total cell number was calculated.

Treg suppression assay

CFSE-labeled effector T cells (Teff) were co-cultured with Tregs derived from different co-culture conditions at varying ratios (1:2, 1:4). Teff proliferation was assessed, and the suppression rate was calculated.

Flow cytometry analysis

In vitro, surface expression of CXCR4 on Tregs was analyzed by flow cytometry. In vivo, tumor tissues were dissociated mechanically and enzymatically to isolate tumor-infiltrating immune cells for flow cytometric analysis. Key markers included: M2-like tumor-associated macrophages (F4/80+ CD206+), MACRO, CD44, and CD47 expression; the proportion of FOXP3+ CD4+ Tregs; and CXCR4 expression on tumor-infiltrating Tregs. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Orthotopic cholangiocarcinoma mouse model

To establish an in vivo cholangiocarcinoma model, EGR1 WT or EGR1K422R B6 mouse cholangiocarcinoma SB1 cells were injected into the livers of C57BL/6 mice to generate an orthotopic cholangiocarcinoma model. Four weeks after tumor formation, tumor tissues were harvested for immune cell analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses for single-cell data were performed as described in the corresponding Method Details sections. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to assess associations between continuous variables. Comparisons between two independent groups were conducted using the unpaired Wilcoxon test. All statistical tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction. All multi-omics analyses were performed using R (v4.1.1).

Results

Identification of two senescence-related molecular subtypes in iCCA

To elucidate the role of cellular senescence in the pathogenesis of iCCA, we comprehensively compiled senescence-associated gene signatures from four well-established resources: Fridman et al. [30], Roberto et al. [31], HAGR [32], and CellAge database [31]. A consensus set of 101 senescence-related genes identified in at least two of these resources was defined as ISG for subsequent analyses (Fig. 1A). Evaluation of ISG expression in the TCGA-iCCA cohort revealed marked upregulation in tumor tissues compared with normal tissues, indicating an enhanced senescent state in iCCA (Fig. 1B). To independently validate these findings, two additional RNA-seq datasets were analyzed (GSE32225 [13] and GSE107943 [14]. ISG scoring calculated by the GSVA method confirmed significantly elevated senescence phenotypes in iCCA tumors relative to normal tissues, thereby further supporting a potential link between cellular senescence and iCCA development (Fig. 1D). We next assessed the clinical relevance of ISG using two independent cohorts with survival information. Samples were ranked by ISG score and stratified into ISG-high and ISG-low subgroups using the median value as the cutoff. The ISG-high subgroup exhibited significantly higher AgingMap signature score [33], supporting the robustness of ISG in capturing senescence phenotypes (Fig. 1E). Importantly, overall survival analysis revealed that the two ISG-associated iCCA subtypes exhibited distinct clinical outcomes, with patients in the ISG-high subgroup experiencing significantly poorer overall survival than those in the ISG-low subgroup (Fig. 1F). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ISG not only discriminates iCCA tumor tissues from normal tissues but also serves as a robust predictor of patient outcomes, implicating cellular senescence as a key driver of iCCA progression and aggressiveness.

Immunosuppressive microenvironment underlying the poor prognosis of the ISG-High iCCA subtype

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the divergent prognoses of ISG-high and ISG-low iCCA subtypes, we performed a comprehensive transcriptomic analysis. Differential expression analysis, followed by KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, was conducted using the FU-iCCA and GSE89747 RNA-seq datasets. In total, 1995 and 622 genes were upregulated in the ISG-high subtype in the FU-iCCA and GSE89747 datasets, respectively, whereas 2771 and 141 genes were upregulated in the ISG-low subtype (Fig. 2A, C). Pathway enrichment analyses revealed highly concordant activation patterns. Genes upregulated in the ISG-high subtype were significantly enriched in cellular senescence pathways and multiple pro-tumorigenic hallmark pathways, including IL-17 signaling, HIF-1 signaling, ECM–receptor interaction, NF-κB signaling, and TNF signaling pathways (Fig. 2B, D). In contrast, the ISG-low subtype showed enrichment in metabolic pathways, including bile secretion, steroid biosynthesis, and arginine biosynthesis (Fig. 2B, D). We next investigated differences in the immune microenvironment between the two iCCA subtypes. Using the ESTIMATE algorithm [34], we assessed overall tumor microenvironment composition. Both RNA-seq datasets consistently demonstrated significantly higher immune and stromal scores in the ISG-high subtype compared with the ISG-low subtype, consistent with the functional enrichment results (Fig. 2E, G). The elevated stromal score indicates an increased abundance of stromal components, including cancer-associated fibroblasts, which are established drivers of tumor progression and immune evasion [[35], [36], [37]]. Further immune deconvolution using the quanTIseq algorithm [38] revealed significant differences in the relative abundances of ten immune cell types, most notably a pronounced enrichment of immunosuppressive Tregs and M2 macrophages in the ISG-high subtype across both datasets (Fig. 2F, H). Previous studies have highlighted the immunosuppressive roles of Tregs and M2-like macrophages in the tumor microenvironment [[39], [40], [41]], and the robust presence of these populations in the ISG-high subtype provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for its unfavorable prognosis. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the ISG-high iCCA subtype is associated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling identifies an ISG-high malignant subgroup

Leveraging scRNA-seq to resolve cellular heterogeneity and tumor microenvironment composition, we integrated and analyzed three independent datasets, GSE138709, GSE171899, and GSE189903, to generate a comprehensive single-cell atlas of iCCA. This atlas enabled high-resolution dissection of senescence-associated cellular mechanisms in iCCA. After stringent quality control, a total of 65,145 high-quality cells were retained for downstream analyses. Batch effects were effectively corrected using the Harmony algorithm, and unsupervised clustering partitioned the cells into 24 distinct clusters (Fig. S1A). Based on classical marker genes, we annotated 13 major cell types: B cells (MS4A1, CD79A), cDC1 (CLEC9A, IDO1), cDC2 (CLEC10A, CD1C), endothelial cells (GNG11, FCN3), fibroblasts (DCN, ACTA2), hepatocytes (APOA2, ALB), macrophages (C1QB, C1QC), malignant cells (KRT19, KRT7), neutrophils (S100A8, S100A9), NK cells (KLRD1, KLRF1), pDCs (LILRA4, PLD4), plasma cells (JCHAIN, MZB1), and T cells (CD3E, CD3D) (Fig. 3A, B, D). A comparison of cellular composition between tumor and normal tissues revealed marked differences, with malignant cells predominantly confined to tumor tissues, whereas normal tissues were enriched in myeloid and NK cell populations (Fig. 3C and S1B). We next evaluated senescence-associated phenotypes across cell types by calculating ISG scores using the GSVA algorithm. Malignant cells exhibited significantly higher ISG activity compared with other cell types, while immune cells displayed the lowest scores (Fig. 3E), consistent with the elevated ISG scores observed in bulk RNA-seq tumor samples (Fig. 1D). Subclustering of malignant cells identified eleven distinct subclusters (Fig. 3F). Among these, Cluster 1 (C1) exhibited significantly higher ISG scores than all other subclusters (Fig. 3G) and was therefore designated as the ISG-high malignant subgroup; the remaining clusters were classified as ISG-low (Fig. 3H). Functional characterization revealed that the ISG-high subgroup was significantly enriched in S-phase cells, indicating enhanced proliferative capacity (Fig. 3H). Furthermore, CytoTRACE analysis predicted a higher differentiation potential for the ISG-high subgroup compared with the ISG-low subgroup (Fig. 3I). More importantly, we assessed the transcriptomic signature of this ISG-high subgroup, which robustly predicted poorer overall survival in independent iCCA cohorts (Fig. 3J, K). Collectively, these findings identify a distinct ISG-high malignant cell population in iCCA with low proliferation and strong stemness, suggesting that this subgroup represents a key cellular source driving senescence-associated phenotype in iCCA. Moreover, this cellular phenotype is strongly linked to adverse patient outcomes.

EGR1-driven metabolic reprogramming in the ISG-high malignant subgroup

To molecularly characterize the identified ISG-high malignant subpopulation, we conducted a comparative transcriptomic analysis. Differential expression analysis revealed that ISG-high malignant cells markedly upregulated classical senescence markers (e.g., CDKN1A), stemness-associated genes (e.g., STMN1), and numerous proliferation-related genes, including PCLAF, UBE2C, and HMGB2 (Fig. 4A). These genes were significantly enriched in pathways associated with cellular senescence, p53 signaling, cell cycle regulation, DNA replication, and ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling. In contrast, ISG-low malignant cells preferentially expressed genes such as ITM2B, CLDN10, and AGR2 and exhibited enrichment in metabolic processes, including sphingolipid and cholesterol metabolism, lysosomal function, and cell junction–related pathways (Fig. 4B). Given the distinct enrichment of metabolic pathways between the two subgroups, we further investigated metabolic heterogeneity among malignant subclusters based on metabolic pathway activity. ISG-high malignant cells (C1) formed a distinct cluster that was clearly separated from other malignant subclusters (Fig. 4C). Specificity scoring of individual metabolic pathways confirmed that ISG-high cells exhibited heightened glycolytic activity, whereas ISG-low cells were characterized by enhanced oxidative phosphorylation, suggesting a pronounced divergence in energy acquisition strategies between the two malignant subgroups (Fig. 4D). To elucidate the regulatory mechanisms underlying the ISG-high malignant phenotype, we inferred transcription factor (TF) activity using pySCENIC and DoRothEA. Using pySCENIC, we identified 103 upregulated TFs in ISG-high malignant cells, whereas DoRothEA identified 97 upregulated TFs. Integration of results from both algorithms, along with the 101 identified ISG genes, identified EGR1, TP53, FOXM1, and E2F1 as potential key regulators (Fig. 4E). A comprehensive evaluation of TF activity and expression levels further identified EGR1 as a pivotal regulator specifically associated with metabolic alterations in ISG-high malignant cells (Fig. 4F). Early growth response factor 1 (EGR1) is a member of the EGR family and is widely expressed in many cell types, performing important functions in tumor cell proliferation, angiogenesis, invasion, and immune responses [42]. Notably, EGR1 activity showed strong positive correlations with both cellular senescence and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (Fig. 4G). Interrogation of the TRRUST database indicated that EGR1 could up-regulate TP53 and CDKN1A, providing a mechanistic link between EGR1 activity, the observed senescence phenotype, and enhanced glycolytic activation (Fig. 4H). Lactylation at the K422 site of EGR1 promotes its nuclear accumulation and induces cellular senescence

To delineate the molecular mechanism underlying the relationship between EGR1, glycolysis, and the senescence phenotype, we first examined the lactylation status of EGR1 in cholangiocarcinoma cell lines. Immunoprecipitation with an anti-Flag antibody followed by immunoblotting with a pan-Kla antibody revealed that EGR1 underwent robust lactylation in both HuCCT1 and RBE cell lines, which was further enhanced upon sodium lactate (NaLa) treatment (Figs. 5A-B). To identify the specific lactate-mediated modification sites on EGR1, we employed the online prediction algorithm DeepKla [43] and identified several high-scoring lysine (K) residues, including K422, K323, and K317 (Fig. 5C). Given that arginine (R) substitution minimally perturbs protein structure while preserving positive charge, K→R substitutions are commonly used to mimic the unmodified lysine state [44]. Based on predictive scoring, we generated mutants for K422R, K323R, and K317R. Notably, only the K422R mutation significantly attenuated EGR1 lactylation in NaLa-treated cells, whereas mutation of K323 or K317 had no appreciable effect compared with wild-type EGR1, indicating that K422 is critical for mediating EGR1 lactylation (Fig. 5D). Sequence alignment revealed that K422 is evolutionarily conserved across species (Fig. 5E), prompting the generation of CRISPR/Cas9-mediated EGR1K422R point-mutant cell lines in both HuCCT1 and RBE cell lines. Endogenous immunoprecipitation using an anti-EGR1 antibody confirmed that, whereas wild-type cells exhibited pronounced EGR1 lactylation, the K422R mutation abolished this modification (Figs. 5F-G). As EGR1 functions as a transcription factor critically dependent on nuclear localization, we next investigated whether K422 lactylation influences EGR1 nuclear translocation. Nuclear–cytoplasmic fractionation analysis demonstrated that NaLa treatment markedly increased nuclear EGR1 levels in wild-type cells, an effect that was significantly attenuated in K422R mutants (Figs. 5H-I). Reconstitution with wild-type EGR1 restored NaLa-induced nuclear accumulation, whereas EGR1 knockdown reduced nuclear EGR1 levels irrespective of lactate status (Figs. 5H-I). These findings suggest that K422 lactylation is crucial for the lactate-induced nuclear translocation of EGR1. Given that nuclear enrichment of EGR1 is essential for its transcriptional regulatory function, we next evaluated whether K422 lactylation modulates EGR1 transcriptional activity by interrogating the JASPAR database for putative EGR1 binding motifs within the CDKN1A promoter. Four potential EGR1 binding regions were identified: Region 1 (539–552), Region 2 (1054–1067), Region 3 (1737–1750), and Region 4 (1932–1945) (Figs. 5J-K). Luciferase reporter assays employing deletion or site-directed mutagenesis revealed that regions 1 and 2 are essential for EGR1-mediated transactivation (Fig. 5L). In wild-type HuCCT1 cells, NaLa treatment significantly enhanced EGR1-binding promoter activity, whereas the K422R mutation markedly blunted this response (Figs. 5M-N). Consistently, chromatin immunoprecipitation-qPCR (ChIP-qPCR) demonstrated that NaLa promoted EGR1 binding to regions 1 and 2 in a K422-dependent manner (Fig. 5O). Thus, K422 lactylation selectively enhances EGR1 occupancy at regions 1 and 2 of the CDKN1A promoter.

Finally, to assess the functional consequences of EGR1 K422 lactylation on cellular senescence, we performed SA-β-galactosidase staining in wild-type and EGR1K422R HuCCT1 cell lines. The K422R mutation significantly reduced the proportion of SA-β-gal-positive cells compared with wild-type controls (Fig. 5P). Overexpression of EGR1 in EGR1K422R cells partially restored SA-β-gal activity, whereas EGR1 knockdown diminished senescence markers (Fig. 5P). Collectively, these results demonstrate that glycolysis-derived lactate induces EGR1 K422 lactylation, which is required for nuclear accumulation, enhanced binding to the CDKN1A promoter, and subsequent induction of cellular senescence.

To further validate these in vitro findings within the in vivo tumor microenvironment, we generated an SB1 cholangiocarcinoma cell line derived from C57BL/6 mice harboring the EGR1K422R point mutation and established an orthotopic cholangiocarcinoma model. Tumor-infiltrating immune cells were isolated for comprehensive analysis. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that, compared with EGR1-WT tumors, the EGR1K422R mutation significantly reduced the abundance of M2-like (F4/80⁺ CD206⁺) macrophages within the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 7D). Concurrently, MACRO expression levels in tumor-infiltrating macrophages were markedly decreased in the EGR1K422R mutant group (Fig. 7E), further corroborating the role of EGR1 K422 lactylation in regulating MACRO⁺ macrophage infiltration. Moreover, compared with EGR1-WT tumors, the EGR1K422R mutation significantly downregulated the expression of CD44 and CD47 in tumor-infiltrating macrophages (Figs. 7F-G), consistent with the previously predicted LGALS9-CD44 and THBS1-CD47 pairs. In addition, EGR1-WT tumors harbored a significantly higher proportion of FOXP3⁺ CD4⁺ Tregs compared with the K422R mutant group (Fig. 7H). CXCR4 expression levels in tumor-infiltrating Tregs were also significantly elevated in the EGR1-WT group relative to the K422R mutant group (Fig. 7I), further substantiating the pivotal role of the CXCL12–CXCR4 axis in this regulatory network. Collectively, these in vivo results demonstrate that EGR1 K422 lactylation promotes the formation of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by orchestrating coordinated signaling interactions among tumor cells, macrophages, and Tregs.

Discussion

iCCA is the second most common primary liver cancer after hepatocellular carcinoma, with a steadily increasing incidence worldwide. Although novel therapies, including targeted therapies and immunotherapies, have been developed in recent years, they have not yet resulted in substantial improvements in overall survival, with the 5-year relative survival rate remaining below 10% [1,45]. Accumulating evidence suggests that senescent cells contribute to tumor progression by promoting the development of more aggressive malignant states through the secretion of senescence-associated factors, which profoundly remodel the tumor microenvironment and negatively impact patient prognosis [46,47]. Nevertheless, the key cellular subtypes and associated molecular determinants driving this process remain largely unexplored. To address this knowledge gap, we performed a comprehensive analysis of senescent cells and the tumor microenvironment in iCCA using bulk and single-cell transcriptomic data, thereby elucidating how senescent cells cooperate with immunosuppressive cells to drive iCCA progression.

In this study, we systematically characterized the molecular features and regulatory mechanisms of cellular senescence in iCCA through the integration of multi-omics datasets and investigated its association with the immune microenvironment. First, we constructed ISG based on multiple senescence-associated gene sets and stratified iCCA samples into ISG-high and ISG-low subgroups. The ISG-high subgroup was associated with significantly worse overall survival compared with the ISG-low subgroup, supporting a pro-tumorigenic role of cellular senescence in iCCA progression. This finding is consistent with experimental evidence demonstrating that, although senescent cells are growth-arrested, they secrete SASP factors that remodel the tumor microenvironment, thereby promoting tumor cell proliferation, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the ISG-high subtype was significantly enriched in multiple pro-tumorigenic signaling pathways, including IL-17, NF-κB, and HIF-1 signaling, and was accompanied by elevated immune and stromal scores. These results indicate that the senescence-associated phenotype not only reflects intrinsic cellular alterations but is also closely associated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Deconvolution analysis of bulk transcriptomic data further demonstrated that immunosuppressive cell types, particularly Tregs and M2 macrophages, which play key roles in tumor immune evasion, were enriched in the ISG-high subgroup. Collectively, these findings suggest that cellular senescence in iCCA promotes tumor progression, at least in part, by reshaping the immune microenvironment.

Malignant cells exhibited the strongest senescence signals, and a malignant cell subpopulation with higher ISG scores was identified. We observed that the ISG-high malignant subgroup, which exhibiting low proliferation and strong stemness. Meanwhile, the ISG-high malignant cells upregulated the hypoxia and glycolysis pathways, exhibiting significant differences in their metabolic profiles. Furthermore, we identify the key transcriptional factor EGR1 within the ISG signature and demonstrate its regulatory role in the expression of CDKN1A. The K422 site of EGR1 undergoes lactylation, which promotes its nuclear translocation and further regulates the transcription of CDKN1A, ultimately inducing the senescence phenotype. Moreover, cell-cell interaction analysis revealed that ISG-high malignant cells recruited and regulated immunosuppressive MACRO+ TAMs through the LGALS9-CD44/THBS1-CD47 axis, and the immunosuppressive MACRO+ TAMs could also recruit Tregs through the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis, forming a cascade of immunosuppressive loops. These results provide a novel mechanistic insight into immune evasion in iCCA, highlighting the pivotal role of senescent tumor cells in shaping an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.

Conclusion

In this study, we identified senescent tumor cells in iCCA based on integrated analysis of bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq, and identified EGR1 as a central regulator of this population. Through a series of in vitro and in vivo experiments, we demonstrated that glycolysis-derived lactate induces lactylation at K422 site of EGR1. This modification promotes the nuclear translocation of EGR1, enhances its binding to the CDKN1A promoter, and ultimately drives the activation of senescence program. Furthermore, we revealed that these senescent tumor cells actively shape an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by recruiting immunosuppressive MACRO+ TAMs via the LGALS9-CD44/THBS1-CD47 axis. These MACRO+ TAMs promote the infiltration of Tregs through the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis, thereby forming a TME which facilitates immune escape. Our study provides valuable insights into the role of senescent tumor cells in iCCA and suggests that targeting the EGR1 lactylation may represent a novel therapeutic strategy to reverse immunosuppression and enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy in iCCA.
