Repeat testing or additional tuberculin skin tests for management of indeterminate results of interferon-gamma release assays: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xin Guo, Yan Dong, Shunli Cai, Yuan Xia, Yingchen Zhou, Xuesong Chen, Yan Dong, Hongbo Chen, Jian He, Chao Song, Chengguo Zhan, Yungui Tu, Guozhong Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInterferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs) are widely used for detecting latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI). However, these tests can yield indeterminate results, posing challenges for clinical management. The management of these indeterminate outcomes varies, creating uncertainty in clinical practice. This study systematically evaluates the effectiveness of repeat IGRA testing versus additional tuberculin skin testing (TST) in resolving indeterminate IGRA results during LTBI screening.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis, searching PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library databases on May 18, 2024, without start date or language restrictions. Studies were included if they screened for LTBI in healthy or high-risk populations using IGRA, reported indeterminate results, and managed these results with repeat IGRA testing and/or additional TST. A random-effects model was used to calculate pooled results.ResultsA total of 59 studies were included in this analysis. Among these, 40 studies assessed the use of additional TST in individuals with indeterminate IGRA results, yielding a pooled confirmation rate of 98.6% (95% CI: 96.2–99.8%). Additionally, 27 studies examined repeat IGRA testing, which resulted in a pooled confirmation rate of 68.9% (95% CI: 57.0–79.6%). Furthermore, eight studies evaluated both TST and repeat IGRA testing, with the pooled confirmation rate for the TST being 93.7% (95% CI: 78.7–99.9%), higher than the pooled confirmation rate for repeat testing at 76.5% (95% CI: 44.6–97.1%). However, there was no statistically significant difference in the confirmation rates between the two testing methods (OR = 2.13, 95% CI: 0.47–9.76).ConclusionsIn managing indeterminate IGRA results during LTBI screening, head-to-head studies show no significant difference in confirmation rates between additional TST and repeat IGRA. Across nearly 60 studies, additional TST tends to have a slightly higher confirmation rate, though the difference is not statistically significant. Clinically, for patients with an initial indeterminate IGRA who are immunocompetent, with convenient sample collection or a need for rapid results, additional TST may help achieve more reliable outcomes. Selection of follow-up testing should consider the cause of indeterminate results, feasibility, and risk of patient loss to follow-up.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11834-1.Keywords: Latent tuberculosis infection, Interferon-gamma release assay, Indeterminate, Tuberculin skin tests, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) refers to the presence of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) in the body without clinical or radiological evidence of active disease [1]. It is estimated that a quarter of the world's population has LTBI, and approximately 5–10% of those infected will develop active tuberculosis (TB) over their lifetime [2]. Consequently, treating LTBI is a crucial strategy for preventing TB [3]. However, there is no gold standard for diagnosing LTBI. The commonly used diagnostic methods include the tuberculin skin test (TST) and the interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA), both of which rely on the host’s immune response to MTB antigens [4].

The traditional LTBI detection method, TST, lacks specificity in populations vaccinated with Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG) due to cross-reactivity with antigens present in both BCG and non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) [5, 6]. In contrast, IGRA is less affected by prior BCG vaccination or NTM infection, offering higher specificity [7–9]. However, IGRA has the potential drawback of yielding "indeterminate" results that are difficult to interpret.

IGRA detects LTBI by measuring the interferon-γ response to tuberculosis-specific antigens, namely early secreted antigen 6 kDa (ESAT-6) and culture filtrate protein 10 kDa (CFP-10) [10]. Mitogen tubes serve as positive controls to assess the performance of commercially available IGRA tests and the functionality of individual T cells, while nil tubes are used as negative controls to adjust for background interferon-γ levels. Elevated interferon-γ levels in the negative control or low responses in the positive control can result in indeterminate results [11].

Our published meta-analysis revealed that, on average, one in every 26 LTBI screenings yields an indeterminate IGRA result [12]. Furthermore, another meta-analysis found that the risk of progressing to active TB associated with indeterminate IGRA results in immunocompromised individuals is approximately half that of positive results and three times that of negative results [13]. These findings underscore the importance of careful assessment and management of indeterminate IGRA results, particularly in immunocompromised patients.

However, due to a lack of comprehensive evidence, such as meta-analyses, recommendations for managing indeterminate results are inconsistent. For example, some guidelines recommend repeating tests for immunocompromised patients with initially indeterminate results [14, 15], while others suggest either repeating the test or performing additional TST [16, 17]. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the effectiveness of repeat testing and additional TST in managing indeterminate IGRA results during LTBI screening.

Methods

Search strategy and study selection criteria

We strictly adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines in reporting this review [18]. The study is registered in PROSPERO as CRD42023395074.

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library for relevant studies, with no start date or language restrictions on 08 February 2023, and repeated this search on May 18, 2024. We used the following keywords: (“latent tuberculosis” OR “latent tuberculosis infection” OR “LTBI”) AND (“interferon gamma release assays” OR “Interferon-gamma Release Test” OR “IGRA” OR “QuantiFERON®-TB in tube” OR “QFT” OR “T-SPOT.TB”) AND “Indeterminate”. The detailed search terms and strategy are shown in Appendix Table S1. The reference lists of the relevant articles were manually checked for other potentially relevant articles.

We included studies if they met all of the following criteria: (1) screening for LTBI in healthy or high-risk adults and/or children; (2) IGRA used to detect LTBI, with indeterminate results reported; (3) handling with repeating IGRA testing and/or additional TST testing.

We excluded studies if they met all of the following criteria: (1) active TB and suspects not excluded at baseline; (2) the inclusion of only IGRA or TST positive/negative patients at baseline; (3) using non-commercial or modified IGRA; (4) the definition of indeterminate results varied across studies, sometimes including invalid results due to insufficient blood, machine failure, or contamination. As no universally accepted reference test for LTBI exists, these differences may introduce heterogeneity. Two investigators (XG and SLC) independently screened the titles and abstracts, and discrepancies were resolved by a third investigator (GZZ).

Data extraction and quality assessment

Non-English/foreign-language abstracts and articles were accurately translated for full-text review. In a preconceived and standardized data extraction form, information was collected on title, the first author's name, year of publication, study area, study design, the timing of data collection (prospective or retrospective), investigated population, patient demographics, IGRA type and manufacturer, number of patients screened, number of patients with indeterminate results, number of patients with repeat IGRA testing or additional TST testing, the results of repeat IGRA testing or additional TST testing, time interval of repeat IGRA testing or additional TST testing. Two investigators (YX and SLC) independently extracted data from individual studies. The third investigator (XG) cross-checked extracted data. Disagreements were resolved through consensus.

The original Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2) tool includes items that are not fully applicable to latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) studies. For instance, standard QUADAS-2 may be limited in assessing studies with no reference standard, indeterminate IGRA results, or repeated TST measurements, potentially leading to inaccurate risk-of-bias evaluations. To provide a more rigorous and context-appropriate assessment of diagnostic accuracy, we adapted QUADAS-2 by adding six specific quality items, reflecting the unique features of study design and data handling in LTBI research [9]. High-quality studies were defined as those that met at least five of the criteria; those of moderate quality met three or four criteria; and those of low quality met two or fewer criteria (Appendix Table S2). Two investigators (XG and SLC) independently assessed the methodological quality of the studies. A third investigator (GZZ) independently reviewed those assessments. Disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Two investigators (XG and SLC) independently assessed the methodological quality of half of the studies. A third investigator (GZZ) independently reviewed those assessments. Disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Outcome and statistical analysis

The main study outcomes include two key confirmation rates: one for additional TST testing among individuals with indeterminate IGRA results and another for retesting individuals with indeterminate IGRA results. We compared the confirmation rates and odds ratios (OR) of concurrent testing to validate our findings.

To further assess the robustness of our results, we performed sensitivity and subgroup analyses as secondary outcomes. Specifically, subgroup analyses were conducted based on factors such as study design, TB burden, age group, gender, and immune status. We utilized univariate meta-regression to calculate p-values for each subgroup, thereby determining the significance of the observed differences.

The random-effects model was used to calculate pooled results and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, and forest plots were generated to visualize specific effect sizes and their 95% CIs for each study [19]. Egger's test was utilized to detect publication bias. All p-values were two-sided, and values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Main results and subgroup analysis

In the 40 studies evaluating additional TST for indeterminate IGRA results, a total of 1,817 individuals were included, of whom 1,378 (75.8%) underwent additional TST. Among those who received additional TST, the pooled confirmation rate was 98.6% (95% CI: 96.2–99.8%). In the 27 studies assessing repeat testing for indeterminate IGRA results, a total of 1,938 individuals were included, with only 949 (49.0%) undergoing repeat IGRA testing. Among those who received repeat IGRA, the pooled confirmation rate was 68.9% (95% CI: 57.0–79.6%), see Fig. 2.

The pooled confirmation rate of additional TST across these 8 studies was 93.7% (95% CI 78.7–99.9%), which was higher than the confirmation rate of repeat testing (76.5%, 95% CI 44.6–97.1%). Among immunocompromised patients, the confirmation rate for repeat IGRA testing was lower than that for immunocompetent individuals (59.0% [95% CI: 36.4–79.8%] vs. 80.9% [95% CI: 57.2–94.6%]), but the p-value from univariate meta-regression analysis was 0.1283. For additional TST testing, the confirmation rate was also lower in immunocompromised patients compared to those with normal immune function (95.0% [95% CI: 85.7–99.6%] vs. 99.5% [95% CI: 95.8–100.0%]), with a p-value of 0.1159 from univariate meta-regression analysis.

The distribution of repeat IGRA testing results showed a positive rate of 3.6% (95% CI: 1.6–6.5%), a negative rate of 60.7% (95% CI: 48.1–72.6%), and an indeterminate rate of 31.1% (95% CI: 20.4–42.0%). In the immunocompromised group, the positive rate was 6.2% (95% CI: 3.1–10.3%) and the negative rate was 46.4% (95% CI: 21.5–72.4%).

For additional TST testing, the positive rate was 9.8% (95% CI: 5.7–14.9%), the negative rate was 85.0% (95% CI: 78.0–90.8%), and the loss to follow-up rate was 1.4% (95% CI: 0.2–3.8%). In the immunocompromised group, the positive rate was 8.0% (95% CI: 3.2–14.8%) and the negative rate was 79.2% (95% CI: 67.2–89.1%). See Appendix Table S4.

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

Egger's test was employed to assess publication bias for the pooled confirmation rates, with results indicating no evidence of publication bias for additional TST (p = 0.7954) and for repeat testing (p = 0.7267). Sensitivity analysis of the pooled confirmation rates showed that the results were stable. Details are presented in Appendix Figure S1-2.

Discussion

This meta-analysis synthesizes data from 59 studies and represents the first systematic evaluation of the effectiveness of additional TST compared to repeat IGRA testing in managing indeterminate IGRA results. Our findings reveal that additional TST achieves a significantly higher pooled confirmation rate (98.6%, 95% CI: 96.2–99.8%) compared to repeat IGRA testing (68.9%, 95% CI: 57.0–79.6%). These results provide strong evidence supporting the preference for additional TST in clinical practice to resolve indeterminate IGRA outcomes.

TST results are typically interpreted as binary—either positive or negative—without indeterminate outcomes. This is a key reason for the higher confirmation rate of additional TST. However, a major issue with TST is that some individuals may not return for follow-up within the required timeframe for result reading, leading to loss to follow-up. Our subgroup analysis indicates that the confirmation rate for additional TST in immunocompromised patients is lower compared to immunocompetent individuals (95.0% [85.7–99.6] vs. 99.5% [95.8–100.0]), highlighting a higher risk of loss to follow-up in these populations, though this difference was not statistically significant (univariate meta-regression p-value = 0.1159). Additionally, individuals facing socioeconomic disadvantage, people experiencing homelessness, or people with substance use disorders, individuals with mobility challenges, and those with fixed or atypical work schedules require special attention when implementing additional TST [79]. For these groups, it is crucial to assess the potential risk of loss to follow-up and develop appropriate follow-up strategies to ensure accurate test results.

While our study shows that additional TST yields a higher rate of determinate results compared to repeat IGRA, it also appears to produce more negative results. We observed a relatively small difference in positive results between additional TST and repeat IGRA (9.8% vs. 3.6%, a difference of 6.2 percentage points), but a significant difference in negative results (85.0% vs. 60.7%, a difference of 24.3 percentage points). This trend is more pronounced in immunocompromised populations, where positive rates are similar (8.0% vs. 6.2%, a difference of 1.8 percentage points), but negative rates differ substantially (79.2% vs. 46.4%, a difference of 32.8 percentage points). Given that TST relies on an intact cellular immune response, immunosuppression substantially increases the risk of false-negative results, which may lead to underdiagnosis of LTBI in this population. Therefore, the use of additional TST in immunocompromised patients carries a risk of underestimating LTBI prevalence [9]. Moreover, previous studies indicate that indeterminate IGRA results in this population often reflect advanced immunosuppression, further highlighting the need for careful assessment of the risk of progression to active TB in individuals with potential LTBI [13]. Consequently, whether using additional TST or repeat IGRA, results in immunocompromised patients should be interpreted with heightened caution, integrating clinical context and risk stratification into decision-making.

The uncertainty in IGRA results can primarily be attributed to two factors: the patient’s immunosuppressive status and technical errors [11]. Technical errors can be categorized into three main types: laboratory procedural errors, sample handling issues, and reagent problems. Laboratory procedural errors can often be corrected through retesting, whereas sample handling and reagent issues require more meticulous review and intervention [80]. Specifically, addressing sample handling issues may necessitate recollecting samples, while reagent problems may require replacing or recalibrating reagents. Although repeat IGRA testing can partially correct technical errors, it does not address the underlying issue of patient immunosuppression [81]. Our subgroup analysis indicates that the confirmation rate upon retesting is lower in immunocompromised patients compared to immunocompetent individuals (59.0% [36.4–79.8] vs. 80.9% [57.2–94.6]), but this difference is not statistically significant (p = 0.1283 from univariate meta-regression). Additionally, the confirmation rate for adults is significantly lower than for children (54.6% [37.8–70.8] vs. 93.0% [86.0–97.7]), with a p-value of 0.0005 from univariate meta-regression. Further analysis reveals that, among the 16 studies involving adults, 8 (50%) included immunocompromised individuals, whereas only 1 of the 5 studies involving children (20%) included immunocompromised individuals. This suggests that the higher proportion of immunocompromised individuals in the adult group may partially explain the lower confirmation rates observed in adults. To improve the confirmation rate of repeat IGRA testing, it is essential to thoroughly consider the patient’s immune status and meticulously review potential technical errors and their specific causes [81]. This includes, but is not limited to, evaluating laboratory operating procedures, sample collection and handling processes, and reagent quality control. By adopting a comprehensive and systematic approach, the accuracy and reliability of repeat IGRA testing can be maximized, thereby achieving a higher proportion of determinate results.

Retesting with IGRA often faces practical challenges, such as resource-related challenges, sample preservation, and acquisition difficulties. Our study reflects this reality: among the 59 included studies, 40 chose additional TST to manage indeterminate IGRA results, while 27 opted for repeat IGRA testing. In the entire cohort, 75.8% underwent additional TST, compared to only 49.0% who underwent repeat IGRA testing. These figures not only reflect the feasibility of additional TST in practice but also suggest a clinical preference for TST, possibly due to the difficulties associated with repeat IGRA testing [5, 6]. Despite these challenges, we recommend that clinicians consider patient factors, technical conditions, and resource availability to choose the most appropriate follow-up testing strategy, whether it be additional TST or repeat IGRA testing.

Beyond these findings, several broader considerations warrant discussion. First, some included studies were conducted during the Covid-19 pandemic. Covid-19 infection has been shown to alter host immune responses, potentially affecting both TST and IGRA performance, and leading to indeterminate or false-negative results [82, 83]. This underscores the importance of interpreting test results in the context of concurrent or recent viral infections. Second, despite the use of both TST and IGRA, there remain cases that cannot be definitively classified [84]. In such situations, alternative diagnostic approaches—including novel immunodiagnostic assays or biomarkers—may provide complementary insights and should be further investigated. Finally, imaging-based modalities may offer a more specific and non-invasive means of supporting TB diagnosis. Recent advances in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), particularly with the development of novel reagents and contrast techniques, have shown promise in detecting TB-related pathological changes [85, 86]. Although still investigational, these methods may, in the future, complement immunological tests by providing structural and functional information to guide management decisions.

This meta-analysis has several limitations. First, only eight studies directly compared the effectiveness of additional TST and repeat IGRA testing, which somewhat limits the comparability between the two methods. Second, few studies thoroughly analyzed the specific causes of indeterminate IGRA results, such as sample preservation issues, procedural errors, or reagent problems, which restricted our ability to conduct a more in-depth analysis. Finally, most included studies (54 of 59) were conducted in low TB-burden settings, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to regions with high TB prevalence, where epidemiological, immunological, and operational factors could differ substantially.

Conclusion

In managing indeterminate IGRA results during LTBI screening, head-to-head studies show no significant difference in confirmation rates between additional TST and repeat IGRA. Across nearly 60 studies, additional TST tends to have a slightly higher confirmation rate, though the difference is not statistically significant. Clinically, for patients with an initial indeterminate IGRA who are immunocompetent, with convenient sample collection or a need for rapid results, additional TST may help achieve more reliable outcomes. Selection of follow-up testing should consider the cause of indeterminate results, feasibility, and risk of patient loss to follow-up.
