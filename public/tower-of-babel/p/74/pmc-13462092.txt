Homologous cell type markers highlight subdivisions of the domestic chick hippocampal formation
Authors: Toshiyuki Fujita, Naoya Aoki, Chihiro Mori, Koichi J. Homma, Shinji Yamaguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The hippocampal formation (HF) is a brain region essential for both cognitive and emotional functions in mammals. These complex functions are mediated by neural circuits connecting the subdivisions of the HF. To clarify the evolutionary continuity of the HF, the avian HF has recently attracted increasing attention. However, the lack of consensus regarding its subdivision has hindered detailed comparisons. This study aimed to establish a consensus on the avian HF subdivision. We investigated the molecular anatomy of the chick HF using recently identified homologous cell markers that correspond to those in the mammalian HF. Using these markers, we obtained expression patterns consistent with the subdivisions of the V-shaped complex, dorsal medial region, and dorsal lateral region, suggesting homology with corresponding subdivisions of the mammalian HF. Visualizing these cells within the HF provides a strong foundation for establishing a unified framework for avian HF subdivision.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55962-3.

Introduction

The hippocampal formation (HF) in mammals is a brain structure that plays important roles in neural functions, including spatial navigation, learning, memory, and emotion1–4. A notable feature of the mammalian HF is the presence of cells that exhibit specific neural activity related to spatial representation, including the place, head-direction, and grid cells5–9. To clarify the evolutionary continuity of these HF characteristics, substantial interest has focused on the HF of non-mammalian animals with advanced spatial navigation capabilities, particularly birds10–13. In recent years, breakthroughs have been made using several bird species suitable for behavioral analysis, including the discovery of place and head-direction cells in avian HFs that support spatial representations similar to those in mammals. The physiological characteristics of these cells are now being clarified14–20. However, the lack of anatomical consensus on the avian HF subdivision has hindered comparisons at the neural circuit level between avian and mammalian HFs21,22.

The HF of all mammals has a histologically well-conserved structure and is subdivided into the dentate gyrus (DG), Cornu ammonis (CA, Ammon’s horns) fields CA1 and CA3, and the subiculum23,24. The external and internal connectivity of HF neurons is fundamental to its function, and substantial progress has been made in understanding neural circuits, such as the traditionally known trisynaptic circuits from the DG to the CA3 and from the CA3 to the CA11,25. Furthermore, neuronal heterogeneity within the HF based on gene expression has become apparent25–27, and recently, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology has been used to catalog cell types within the mouse HF from analyses of over one million cells28,29.

On the other hand, in birds, the HF has been identified at the brain surface, and there is consensus regarding its homology with the mammalian HF11,30,31. Gene expression studies in avian HFs and scRNA-seq analyses of reptilian brains further support the conservation of HFs within amniotes32,33. To date, various criteria, including histology, immunohistochemistry, connectivity, and developmental origin, have been used to subdivide the HF in various bird species24,34–42. In chickens, the most widely used avian model, more than five subdivision schemes have been recently reviewed by Morandi-Raikova and Mayer21, (see Morandi-Raikova and Mayer21) and when compared with the situation in other birds, it appears that the subdivision of the HF into the V-shaped complex (V), dorsal medial region (DM), and dorsal lateral region (DL) is commonly used21,22 (Fig. 1). More recently, scRNA-seq analysis of the chicken brain identified cell types in the chicken HF that are believed to be homologous to the mammalian HF regions, including the DG, CA3, CA1, and subiculum43. However, the distribution of these cells in chicken HF remains unclear.

Therefore, this study aimed to establish a consensus on the avian HF subdivision. Using post-hatched day 1(P1) chick HF, we identified the expression regions of HF homologous markers43; zinc finger and BTB domain containing 20 (ZBTB20) as a pan-hippocampal marker, prospero homeobox 1 (PROX1) as DG marker, carboxypeptidase A6 (CPA6) as CA3 marker, KIT proto-oncogene, receptor tyrosine kinase (KIT) as CA1 marker, nuclear receptor subfamily 4 group A member 2 (NR4A2) as subiculum marker, as well as lymphoid enhancer binding factor 1 (LEF1), previously identified as a population marker in chicken HF39,44. We found that the V, DM, and DL could be clearly demarcated as subdivisions based on gene expression, supporting the proposal that they correspond to the DG, CA3, and CA1 regions of the mammalian HF, respectively. In parallel, we identified the expression of chick orthologs of several genes commonly used in mouse HF that are involved in the cellular properties (such as neurochemistry and neurogenesis) of HFs. The results showed that the expression patterns of many of the orthologs differed from those in mouse HFs, suggesting that cellular properties related with these genes are not conserved between mammalian and avian homologous brain regions. This is consistent with previous research showing the difficulty of identifying homologous regions based on expression of these genes encoding molecules, such as neurochemical markers45. Using these HF homologous cell markers allowed the successful visualization of chick HF subdivisions. Accumulating similar data across avian species can significantly contribute to establishing a consensus on avian HF subdivisions.

Results

Expression of the HF homologous marker genes in the chick HF

We performed in situ hybridization (ISH) analysis to investigate the expression patterns of the HF homologous marker genes identified by Zaremba et al.43 ZBTB20, PROX1, CPA6, and KIT, using coronal sections around A8.0–A5.0, in the forebrain of chicks on post-hatching day 1 (P1). We detected ZBTB20 signals in the HF region (Fig. 2a–c, Supplementary Fig. 1). A magnified view of the HF regions showed that the ZBTB20 signals were densely distributed in the V and sparsely distributed in the DM (Fig. 2d–f, Supplementary Fig. 1). PROX1 signals were detected in the HF regions (Fig. 3a–c, Supplementary Fig. 2). A magnified view of the HF regions showed that the PROX1 signals were densely distributed in the V region and barely detectable in the other regions (Fig. 3d–f, Supplementary Fig. 2). CPA6 signals were not detected throughout the forebrain, including in the HF regions (Supplementary Fig. 3). KIT signals were detected in the HF regions (Fig. 4a–c, Supplementary Fig. 4). A magnified view of the HF regions showed that the KIT signals were broadly distributed in the DL and barely detectable in the V and DM regions (Fig. 4d–f, Supplementary Fig. 4).

Comparison of the ZBTB20, PROX1, KIT, and LEF1 in the chick HF

The ZBTB20-positive and PROX1-positive regions appeared to overlap, whereas ZBTB20- and KIT-positive regions did not appear to overlap. To further investigate the overlap between these marker gene expression regions, we examined these probes in ISH analysis using serial sections along with LEF1, which is selectively expressed in the ectopic part of the rostral area parahippocampalis (APHre)39,44, a subregion of the DM. We detected signals using these probes in the HFs at A7.2‒7.0 (Supplementary Fig. 5) and A5.6‒5.4 (Supplementary Fig. 6). In the magnified view, both ZBTB20- and PROX1-positive regions were detected in the V; the ZBTB20-positive region included an LEF1-positive region in the DM, and only KIT was predominantly detected in the DL (Fig. 5, Supplementary Fig. 7).

Expression of NR4A2 in the chick HF

A previous study has shown that in the chicken brain, cell types highly similar to those in the mouse subiculum are NR4A2-positive, KIT-negative, and distributed in the apical hyperpallium (HA), which marks the border between the anterior HF and the outside of the HF43. However, the distributions of NR4A2 and KIT in the posterior HF remain unknown. Therefore, we examined the expression patterns of NR4A2 and KIT in the posterior HF using serial sections. NR4A2 and KIT were detected within the HF at A5.4 and A4.6 (Fig. 6a, b, g, h, Supplementary Fig. 8). A comparison of NR4A2 and KIT expression regions in serial sections revealed that the cortex piriformis (CPi) regions were NR4A2-positive and KIT-negative (Fig. 6d, e, j, k, Supplementary Fig. 8).

Expression of neurochemical marker genes in the chick HF

To expand our knowledge of the neurochemistry of chick HF cells, we focused on the expression patterns of glutamate receptor ionotropic, kainate (GRIK) family genes in chick HF. Except for the GRIK5 ortholog, which was not registered in the public database, we performed ISH analysis of all chicken GRIK orthologs–GRIK1, GRIK2, GRIK3, and GRIK4–in P1 chick HF. We detected clear signals for GRIK1, GRIK2, and GRIK3 in brains containing HFs (Fig. 7, Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10), but no signal for GRIK4 (Supplementary Fig. 11). To clarify the regions within the HF where GRIK1, GRIK2, and GRIK3 are expressed, we performed ISH analysis using serial sections together with the DG marker PROX1 and APHre marker LEF1. GRIK1 signals were intense in part of the V, sparsely distributed in the DM, very sparse in the APHre, sparse throughout the DL, and prominent in the superficial layer on the lateral side (Fig. 7d and Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). GRIK2 signals were faintly detected in a small portion of the V and sparsely detected in the DM and DL (Fig. 7e and Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). GRIK3 signals were sparsely detected in the APHre and the superficial layers of the DL (Fig. 7f and Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13).

Expression of neurogenesis-related marker gene in the chick HF

To expand our knowledge of neurogenesis in chicken HF cells, we focused on the chicken ortholog of SRY-related HMG-box gene 11 (SOX11), a neurogenesis-related gene in mouse HF. Because Sox11 is selectively expressed in the DG of mouse HF46, we performed ISH analysis of the DG markers PROX1 and SOX11 using serial sections from P1 chicks (Fig. 7, Supplementary Fig. 14). SOX11 signals were widely detected throughout the brain, including the HF (Fig. 7g, h, m, n, Supplementary Fig. 14). Within the HF, SOX11 signals were densely detected in the V and sparsely detected in the DM and DL (Fig. 7j, k, p, q, Supplementary Fig. 14).

Discussion

Although various avian HF subdivisions have been proposed, no consensus has been reached in the bird research community11,21,22,24,30,31,34–42. Recent advances in scRNA-seq technology have revealed the presence of cell types in the avian HF corresponding to the mammalian DG, CA3, CA1, and subiculum43. However, the distribution of these cells within the avian HF remains largely unknown. To contribute to establishing a consensus on avian HF subdivisions, we mapped the expression regions of previously identified population markers43 and several markers related to HF function across multiple sections along the anterior–posterior (AP) axis of the P1 chick HF.

First, we found that the pan-HF marker ZBTB20 was expressed in the V and DM, the DG marker PROX1 was expressed in the V, and the CA1 marker KIT was expressed in the DL (Figs. 2, 3, 4). The relationship between these expression regions was robust across multiple sections from A8.0 to A5.0 (Fig. 5), suggesting that these genes can be used as subdivision markers for avian HF (Fig. 8a). Zbtb20 is a mammalian pan-HF marker that is predominantly expressed in the granule layer (GL) of the DG and the pyramidal layer (PL) of CA3 and CA1 in adult mice HF47–49. Unlike the DG and CA3, which show an adult-like Zbtb20 expression pattern at birth, Zbtb20 expression in the PL of CA1 is initially absent; however, it becomes prominent with postnatal development. Furthermore, Zbtb20 has been shown to be essential for CA1 identity formation50–52. It is also widely expressed in the reptilian HF homologous region33. Our chick ISH analysis revealed clear ZBTB20 expression in the V and DM; however, no distinct expression was observed in the DL, suggesting an absence in this region. This result may be due to the young chicks (P1) used in this study, whose HF is still developing, similar to that of mammals immediately after birth; however, this remains to be clarified. Prox1 is a mammalian DG marker that is highly selectively expressed in the GL in the mouse HF, regardless of the dorsal‒ventral axis47–49. PROX1 is also expressed in homologous DG populations in reptilian HF homologous regions33. In birds, PROX1 has previously been used as a marker for developing cell populations that are homologous to DG cells in chicken embryos38,39, and has also been shown to be selectively expressed in the V of the HF in adult pigeons41. Here, we demonstrated that PROX1 is selectively and robustly expressed along the AP axis in the V of the HF in P1 chicks, consistent with its expression site in adult chickens43, suggesting that the positional alignment of homologous cell populations along the hippocampal axis is conserved between mammals and birds. In mice, Cpa6 is primarily expressed in the olfactory bulb and is also detected in HF; however, it has not been specifically treated as an HF marker53. In adult chickens, CPA6 has been identified as a marker for CA3 homologous cell types43. In our study, signals were not detected in the HF or brains of P1 chicks; this point is discussed in the next section. Zaremba et al. (2025) showed that the KIT-expressing population exhibits a high similarity score with the CA1 population within the HF in mice, and only a small proportion of the KIT-expressing population expresses ZBTB2043. We found that KIT expression was observed throughout the DL of P1 chicks, but not in the DM. These results suggest that the DL, but not the DM, corresponds to the CA1 region of the mouse HF. ZBTB20 expression in the DM and KIT expression in the DL appeared to be mutually exclusive. However, the boundary between the DM and DL may represent a population that co-expresses ZBTB20 and KIT, or the expression patterns may change in adult HF. scRNA-seq analysis using adult chickens revealed that populations co-expressing ZBTB20 and KIT were small43, suggesting that the boundary region is unlikely to be mutually exclusive (Fig. 8b). Furthermore, in the posterior HF, KIT expression was seamlessly observed from the DL to the area of the corticoideae dorsolateralis (CDL), indicating that the DL/CDL boundary could not be determined solely by KIT expression. Based on projection relationships and developmental origins, the CDL is believed to correspond to the entorhinal cortex (EC); however, the location of the DL/CDL boundary is unknown18,24. If the pan-HF marker properties of ZBTB20 are conserved in birds, it is possible that only the ZBTB20 + /KIT + population corresponds to CA1, and the ZBTB20-/KIT + population corresponds to EC. In any case, further detailed investigation of the KIT-expressing population, potentially using additional markers, would be valuable.

CA1, cornu ammonis field 1; CA3, cornu ammonis field 3; DG, dentate gyrus; DL, dorsal lateral region; DM, dorsal medial region; V, V-shaped complex.

The failure to obtain a CPA6 signal may be due to technical issues, as well as the use of P1 chicks in this study. CPA6 was identified in the previous study as a marker for CA3 homologous cell type in adult chicken HF43. The lack of CPA6 signals from P1 chicks may be because the population that will eventually acquire CA3 identity is still immature. In this study, GRIK4 (described below), which is used as a specific neurochemical marker for CA3, was not detected at all in the entire P1 pallium; however, its signal pattern was detected in adult pallium in a previous study43, which supports this idea. The homologous cell type markers used in this study could not clearly identify the CA3 homologous region of the P1 chick with a single marker. In mammalian HF, the trisynaptic circuit represented by “from DG to CA3, from CA3 to CA1” is important for connectivity within the HF1,25. Regarding avian HF, the connectivity within pigeon HF has been investigated, and independent research groups have repeatedly reported the connectivity relationship “from V to DM, from DM to DL” (Fig. 8c)35,36,54,55. The combination of HF homologous cell type marker expression we presented here, as well as previous studies on hodological relationships, all support the correspondence between DM and CA3.

Additionally, regarding the subiculum, the major hippocampal output structure, we detected that its marker, NR4A2, is expressed in the DL, CDL, and CPi in the posterior HF. In particular, an NR4A2 + /KIT- population was observed in the CPi. Previous studies on NR4A2 expression in the chick brain have focused on cell populations homologous to the claustrum and the deep layers of the neocortex56,57. Zaremba et al. (2025) demonstrated that a subset of NR4A2 + /KIT- cells is homologous to the subicular cell population and is expressed in the HA adjacent to the anterior HF43. The mammalian HF has different connections to the extrahippocampal regions along its axis, and different brain functions are involved along the axis. For example, in mice, the dorsal HF has inputs and outputs with cognitive brain regions and is involved in cognitive functions, whereas the ventral HF has inputs and outputs with limbic brain regions and is involved in emotional function2,25,58. Recently, it has become clear that the ventral HF in mice is important for the memory of others based on individual recognition and controls social behavior59–61. The avian HF also has different inputs and outputs along the AP axis54. The NR4A2 + /KIT- cell population detected in the CPi identified in this study may correspond to the subiculum of the posterior HF in birds. However, as mentioned above, since NR4A2 is a gene also expressed in other parts of the pallium56,57, further investigation is needed to determine whether NR4A2 expression in the CPi is related to the HF region. In this study, data for NR4A2 was limited to a small number of samples compared to other probes (Supplementary Table 1). Therefore, in addition to confirming the subiculum identity of this population using other markers, sample size expansion is necessary. Nevertheless, elucidating the connectivity and functions of this population might shed light on the relationship between the mammalian and avian hippocampal axes.

Next, to deepen our understanding of the expression of genes encoding neurochemical markers commonly used to examine brain region homology, we selected GRIK genes and identified their expression regions in chicken HF. Grik genes showed distinct expression patterns in mouse HF. Grik1 is sparsely expressed throughout the HF; Grik2, Grik3, and Grik5 are primarily expressed in the GL of the DG and the PL of CA3 and CA1; Grik4 is selectively expressed in the PL of CA3 and is widely used in neuroscience as a CA3-selective driver line (KA-1 line)47–49,62–64. The expression patterns of all GRIK genes in chick HF did not resemble those of their mouse orthologs, suggesting that the neurochemical properties of GRIK are not necessarily similar between homologous cell populations in chicken and mouse HF. This observation aligns with findings from scRNA-seq of the songbird brain, which indicate that similarities in regulatory genes, such as transcription factor combinations, do not necessarily correspond to similarities in neurochemical or other functional effector gene expression45. To clarify whether the functions of homologous regions in the mammalian and avian brains are conserved, it would be useful to continually examine the neurochemical and physiological properties of the avian brain in each species. Because chicken GRIK5 was not registered in the public database, we were unable to examine its expression region in this study. However, there were entries of GRIK5 in other birds, reptiles, and fish. Whether the loss of GRIK5 is unique to the chicken genome or due to an issue with annotation technology remains to be determined.

We also found that in chick HF, the neurogenesis marker SOX11 was not only prominently expressed in the V, but also throughout the HF. Adult neurogenesis is an intriguing property of mammalian HF58,65. The avian brain has attracted attention because, unlike the mammalian brain, adult neurogenesis occurs on a larger scale66,67. Although research on the avian HF has progressed using various neurogenesis and proliferation markers54,68,69, in this study, we investigated the expression of SOX11, a neurogenesis marker that has received little attention, in the avian HF to deepen our understanding of the cellular properties of the avian HF. The soxC family of transcription factors (including vertebrate sox11) is expressed in certain stem cell-like cells that exhibit transitional or migratory states during animal development and regeneration70,71. In mouse HF, Sox11 is selectively expressed in the GL of the DG46. The prominent expression of SOX11 in the V indicates functional conservation of DG-homologous cell populations, while its broader expression throughout the HF supports extensive neurogenesis in the avian brain, at least in P1 chicks. Determining whether this pattern persists in chicken HF at later developmental stages would be valuable.

In this study, we conducted a systematic distribution analysis of recently identified chicken HF homologous cell type markers using only P1 chicks. Since these marker genes were identified from scRNA-seq analysis of adult chickens43, it is important to note the postnatal maturation of HF cells. As mentioned in the section of CPA6, this study did not draw conclusions regarding the possibility that mature CA3 cell identity remains undetermined in P1 chicks, and the need for direct confirmation of the overlap range between ZBTB20 and KIT. Future research using these marker sets to conduct a similar-scale distribution analysis in adult chicken HF cells would clearly be beneficial.

In conclusion, using P1 chicks, we were able to clearly visualize the V, DM, and DL regions based on the expression of chicken homologous cell markers in the DG, CA3, CA1, and subiculum of the mouse HF, suggesting that the V, DM, and DL are homologous to the DG, CA3, and CA1, respectively (Fig. 8). Furthermore, we elucidated the expression regions of GRIK genes in chick HF, indicating that neurochemical properties are not necessarily conserved between homologous regions in mammalian and avian brains. We also observed the expression of the neurogenesis marker SOX11, supporting the role of the V in chick HF neurogenesis. These molecular anatomical data provide a powerful foundation for establishing a consensus on avian HF subdivisions.

Materials and methods

Animals

Fertilized eggs of domestic chickens (Gallus gallus domesticus, Cobb strain) were obtained from a local dealer (3-M, Aichi, Japan). Eggs were hatched at Teikyo University (Kaga, Itabashi-ku, Tokyo). Animal experiments were conducted as described by Yamaguchi et al.72,73 Newly hatched chicks (P0) were transferred to dark plastic enclosures in a dark, warm cage at 30 °C and raised for 1 day (P1). Twenty-one chickens were used in this study, the details of which are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. All procedures were performed after being reviewed and approved by the Committee on Animal Experiments of Teikyo University, and followed the guidelines of the national regulations for animal welfare in Japan.

Histological preparations

Deep anesthesia of P1 chicks was achieved by intraperitoneal injection (0.40 mL/individual) of a 1:1 solution of ketamine (10 mg/mL, Ketalar-10, Sankyo Co., Tokyo, Japan) and xylazine (2 mg/mL, Sigma, St. Louis, Missouri, USA). Anesthetized chicks were transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.5; PFA-PBS), and the brains were surgically dissected with minimal trauma. The dissected brains were immersed in PFA-PBS overnight at 4 °C, then immersed in 18% sucrose/PFA-PBS solution for 2 days at 4 °C for cryoprotection. The sucrose-substituted brains were embedded in Tissue-Tek OCT compound (Sakura Finetek, Tokyo, Japan), immediately frozen on dry ice, and stored at − 80 °C until use.

Sectioning

The frozen brain blocks were sectioned at 18 µm thickness using a cryostat (Leica CM3050S or Leica CM1850, Leica Biosystems, Nußloch, Germany). Serial coronal sections were mounted on glass slides (Platinum PRO, cat. # PRO-01; MATSUNAMI Glass Ind., Osaka, Japan) and prepared from level A8.8 to A4.6 of the Kuenzel and Masson’s atlas34.

cDNA cloning

Total RNA was extracted from a chick hemibrain using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and reverse-transcribed with SuperScript III kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) using an oligo (dT) primer, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RT-PCR was performed using the following gene specific primer (forward and reverse) pairs: ZBTB20:5′- GGACAGGATCTACTCAGCCC-3′ and 5′- CAGTGGCATCCTCAGATTGC-3,’ respectively; PROX1:5′- GCCATGAATCCCCAAGGTTC-3′ and 5′- GCACATCTCGTTGTCTGACC-3,’ respectively; CPA6:5′- GTCCCTCCCTGCGTATAACT-3′ and 5′- CAGTCCATAGAGCTGCCAGA-3,’ respectively; KIT: 5′- TCCAAAGCATGAAGAGCACG-3′ and 5′- GGGGCATTATCGGAAAGCTG-3,’ respectively; GRIK1:5′- CAAGGATCAGAGCTGATGC-3′ and 5′- CTGCTCAATATCACTGTTTTTC-3,’ respectively; GRIK2:5′- AGCAGTTCCCACAGATGACT-3′ and 5′- ACCCAAGTAAGCCAGCAGAA-3,’ respectively; GRIK3:5′- CTCGCCGCCTTCCTGACAGT-3′ and 5′- CTATGGGAACACGGGCGTCA-3,’ respectively; GRIK4:5′- GTGTATGGGCGCCTCTGG-3′ and 5′- CCTTCAGAAACATGCCAGTGG-3,’ respectively; SOX11: 5′- CGGTGGAAAATGCTGAAGGA-3′ and 5′- CATGCTGCGAGAAGTTGAGG-3,’ respectively. The PCR products were subcloned into a pGEM-T Easy Vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). The sequences of the target products were confirmed using Sanger sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 15). For NR4A2 and LEF1 probes, we used previously generated plasmids44,57.

RNA probe preparation

Plasmids containing cDNA fragments of ZBTB20, PROX1, CPA6, KIT, NR4A2, LEF1, GRIK1, GRIK2, GRIK3, GRIK4, and SOX11 were amplified by PCR using the M13 primer pair. Amplicons containing the T7 and SP6 promoter sites were purified using a PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, United States). The digoxigenin (DIG)-labelled sense and antisense RNA probes were prepared by in vitro transcription using a DIG RNA labelling kit (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A summary of the probe information used in this study is shown in Supplementary Table 2.

ISH

ISH was performed as previously described with some modifications74. Brain sections were re-fixed in 4% PFA-PBS, pretreated, and hybridized with DIG-labelled riboprobes at 70 °C overnight. After stringent washes, hybridized probes were immunohistochemically detected using an alkaline phosphatase-conjugated anti-DIG antibody (1:1000; Roche, Basel, Switzerland). For visualizing the signals, a chromogenic reaction with a nitro blue tetrazolium/5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl phosphate was performed at room temperature for the following duration: ZBTB20, 18 h 15 min to 21 h 30 min; PROX1, 18 h 15 min to 20 h 15 min; CPA6, 18 h 50 min to 21 h 30 min; KIT, 18 h 15 min to 21 h 30 min; NR4A2, 18 h 55 min to 19 h 10 min; LEF1, 18 h 15 min to 20 h 15 min; GRIK1, 18 h to 20 h 15 min; GRIK2, 18 h to 19 h 30 min; GRIK3, 18 h to 19 h 30 min; GRIK4, 18 h 15 min to 39 h 30 min; and SOX11, 16 h 15 min to 19 h 5 min. In all experiments, sense probes were used as negative controls.

Imaging and data processing

The brain sections were semi-automatically acquired as digital slides using a NanoZoomer 2.0HT or NanoZoomer XR systems (Hamamatsu Photonics, Shizuoka, Japan). Digital photographs of the fields of interest were manually extracted from digital slides using NDP.view2 software (Hamamatsu Photonics, Shizuoka, Japan). The entire extracted image was converted to an 8-bit image, and the brightness and contrast were adjusted using ImageJ software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/).
