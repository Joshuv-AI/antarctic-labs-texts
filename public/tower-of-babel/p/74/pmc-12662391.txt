Mediation effect of body mass index on the association between serum uric acid to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio and osteoporosis: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Ge Huang, Shunwu Feng, Shengwang Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Emerging evidence suggests that serum uric acid to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (UHR) is associated with bone loss. This study aims to explore the mediation effect of body mass index (BMI) underlying the relationship between UHR and osteoporosis risk in older adults, and compares the diagnostic performance of UHR and BMI for osteoporosis. This cross-sectional study analyzed data from 7388 adults aged 60 years and older from National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2005–2020). Multivariable linear regression models, multivariable logistic models, restricted cubic splines, subgroup analysis, threshold effect analysis, mediation analysis, receiver operating characteristic curve, and net reclassification improvement analysis were used. Results revealed a positive association between UHR and bone mineral density at the femoral neck, total femur, and lumbar spine. We found that UHR was negatively associated with osteoporosis prevalence (odds ratios (OR) = 0.734, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.599–0.899, P = .005). In overall population, an L-shaped relationship between UHR and osteoporosis was identified, with a threshold of 0.096. Among those aged 60 to 74 years, UHR was consistently and negatively associated with osteoporosis prevalence (UHR < 0.096: OR = 0.305, 95% CI: 0.136–0.686, P = .005; UHR ≥ 0.096: OR = 0.498, 95% CI: 0.329–0.754, P = .002). BMI mediated the association between the UHR and osteoporosis, accounting for 58.4% of the mediation (P < .001). BMI had a higher area under the curve value (0.695 vs 0.669, P = .012) and a greater net reclassification index (95% CI: 0.06–0.27, P = .009) compared to UHR. In conclusion, a higher UHR was associated with a lower prevalence of osteoporosis, particularly in adults aged 60 to 74 years. BMI mediated this relationship and demonstrated superior diagnostic performance compared to UHR, suggesting that BMI monitoring may be more effective for assessing osteoporosis risk. Although causality cannot be inferred due to the cross-sectional design, these findings provide valuable insights for future prospective research.

1. Introduction

Osteoporosis is a systemic skeletal disorder characterized by low bone mineral density (BMD), deteriorated bone microarchitecture, and an increased risk of fragility fractures.[1] In the United States, the age-adjusted prevalence of osteoporosis among adults aged ≥ 50 years is 12.6%.[2] With global population aging, the prevalence of osteoporosis and osteoporosis-related fractures is increasing, contributing to a growing global health burden.[3,4] Currently, osteoporosis diagnosis primarily relies on BMD measurements. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry remains the most commonly used method for assessing BMD and diagnosing osteoporosis; however, high equipment costs and examination fees restrict its use in routine screening across populations.[5] Thus, there is a pressing need to identify a clinically accessible indicator for the early detection and prevention of osteoporosis.

Serum uric acid (UA)[6–8] and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C)[9–12] have both been associated with osteoporosis, though these findings remain inconsistent. This inconsistency may stem from the influence of factors such as renal excretion and lipid metabolism, which can limit their predictive accuracy. Recently, the serum UA to HDL-C ratio (uric acid to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio [UHR]) has garnered increased attention as a promising marker associated with various disease, including cardiovascular disease,[13–16] nonalcoholic fatty liver disease,[17] insulin resistance,[18] and metabolic syndrome.[19] Many disorders are associated with bone loss through mechanisms such as arteriosclerosis, hypogonadism, chronic inflammation, and abnormal metabolism.[20,21] As a novel inflammatory and metabolic marker, UHR is closely correlated with these mechanisms of osteoporosis, highlighting its value in risk assessment.

Emerging evidence suggests that UHR is negatively associated with osteoporosis risk.[22] This finding contrasts with earlier studies that identified high UHR as a risk factor for various disorders. To date, the potential mechanisms underlying the association between UHR and osteoporosis has not been fully understood. Given that body mass index (BMI) is negatively associated with risk of osteoporosis,[23] we hypothesize that BMI may mediate the protective effect of UHR on osteoporosis. Therefore, we conducted this study to clarify the relationship between UHR and osteoporosis and to investigate the mediating role of BMI through a cross-sectional analysis of data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). In addition, to assess the clinical practicality, we compared the diagnostic performance of UHR and BMI separately in identifying osteoporosis.

2. Methods

2.1. Data source and population selection

The NHANES is a comprehensive, multistage survey conducted biennially by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention for public health surveillance. The protocol of NHANES was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Institutional Review Board (Protocol #2005–06; Protocol #2011–17; Protocol #2018–01). All participant provided informed written consent prior to participation. Additional institutional review board approval was waived for this analysis because of the publicly available and deidentified data. Detailed information on study design, data collection, and measurement protocols is publicly available at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/.

Data were obtained from 8 NHANES cycles from 2005 to 2020, totaling 85,750 participants. First, 68,848 individuals aged < 60 years were excluded, leaving 16,902 elderly participants. Next, 6606 individuals missing BMD data were excluded. Participants with a history of tumors, those taking estrogen, and those taking glucocorticoids were further excluded. Finally, 350 participants with missing UHR data were removed, resulting in a final sample of 7388 participants included for analysis (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of participants selection from the NHANES 2005–2020. NHANES = National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey.

2.2. Exposure variable and outcome variables

The UHR was calculated as serum UA divided by HDL-C, was considered the exposure variable in this study. The primary outcome variable was osteoporosis, defined based on BMD measurements taken by certified radiologic technologists using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. The reference group for calculating T-scores consists of Non-Hispanic White females aged 20 to 29 years from the same cohort, with osteoporosis defined as T-scores ≤ −2.5, indicating a value 2.5 standard deviations (SD) or more below the mean.

2.3. Covariates

Demographic data and potential variables affecting UA, HDL-C, and BMD were considered: age, sex, race, marital status, education level, household income, BMI, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, cardiovascular disease, vitamin D, serum calcium, phosphorus, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, albumin, and creatinine. No significant multicollinearity was found by calculating the variance inflation factor for each variable (all values < 5, Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q742).

Household income was classified into 3 groups based on family poverty-to-income ratio: low (≤1), middle (1 < poverty-to-income ratio < 4), and high (≥4).[24] Smoking status was divided into never (smoked < 100 cigarettes in life), former (smoking > 100 cigarettes in life but does not smoke now), and now (smoking ≥ 1 cigarette every day). Drinking status was categorized as never (<12 drinks in lifetime), former (consumed ≥ 12 drinks in a single year/ lifetime but did not drink last year), and now (≥12 drinks over the past year).[25] Physical activity was defined as having done at least 10 minutes of moderate or vigorous in a typical week. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mm Hg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mm Hg, self-reported hypertension, or receiving anti-hypertensive medication. Hyperlipidemia was defined as triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL, total cholesterol ≥ 200 mg/dL, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥ 130 mg/dL, HDL-C ≤ 40 mg/dL for males and ≤50mg/dL for females, or receiving lipid-lowering medications.[26] Diabetes was defined as glycated hemoglobin A1c ≥ 6.5%, fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, self-reported diabetes, or use of insulin or hypoglycemic medication.[27] Cardiovascular disease was defined based on self-reported history of coronary heart disease, congestive heart failure, heart attack, stroke, or angina.

2.4. Statistical analysis

We employed weighting methodologies following NHANES guidelines. UHR levels were categorized into 4 quartiles (Q1–Q4), with Q1 as the reference group. Continuous variables are presented as the weighted means with interquartile ranges, while categorical variables are expressed as the percentages (%). Chi-square tests and analysis of variance were utilized to assess differences in baseline characteristics.

Multivariable linear regression model was used to investigate the association between UHR and BMD, with results presented as β and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multivariate logistic regression model was used to assess the association between UHR and osteoporosis, with results presented as odds ratios (OR) and 95% CI. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) model was used to graphically represent the dose-response relationship. A threshold effect analysis was conducted to assess the associations in the segmented logistic regression model. Subgroup analyses and interaction tests were performed to explore the potential effects of confounding. Mediation analyses were conducted using the Sobel test, bootstrap method, and the quasi-Bayesian Monte Carlo method with 1000 simulations based on normal approximation. Receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) was used to test diagnostic ability. Additionally, we applied the continuous net reclassification index (NRI) to evaluate the risk reclassification. To address missing data, multiple imputation by chained equations was employed to minimize the impact of missing variables on the results.

Three models were established according to STROBE statement[28]: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age, sex, and race), and Model 3 (further adjusted for marital status, household income, education level, BMI, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, cardiovascular disease, and serum levels of vitamin D, calcium, phosphorus, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, albumin, and creatinine). All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3). A P-value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

3.2. Association between UHR and BMD

As described in Table S2 (Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q742), weighted liner regression models revealed the positive associations of continuous UHR and UHR quartiles with BMD at 3 anatomical sites. In Model 3, each SD increase in UHR was associated with a 0.012 (95% CI: 0.004–0.02, P = .005) increase in femoral neck BMD, a 0.01 (95% CI: 0.001–0.019, P = .031) increase in total femur BMD, and a 0.013 (95% CI: 0.002–0.025, P = .027) increase in lumbar spine BMD. Compared with the lowest UHR quartile, the highest quartile was associated with an increase in femoral neck BMD (β = 0.028, 95% CI: 0.008–0.049, P = .009), as well as in total femur (β = 0.027, 95% CI: 0.005–0.049, P = .019) and lumbar spine BMD (β = 0.036, 95% CI: 0.005–0.068, P = .025). All trend tests indicated a significant linear trend (all P for trend < .05).

3.3. Association between UHR and osteoporosis

As shown in Table 2, weighted logistic regression models revealed the negative associations of continuous UHR and UHR quartiles with osteoporosis risk in 3 models. In Model 3, each SD increase in UHR was associated with a 0.734-fold risk of osteoporosis (OR = 0.734, 95% CI: 0.599–0.899, P = .005). This trend remained statistical significance among the UHR quartiles (P for trend = .002), with participants in Q2 (OR = 0.622, 95% CI: 0.41–0.943, P = .033), Q3 (OR = 0.532, 95% CI: 0.347–0.816, P = .007), and Q4 (OR = 0.503, 95% CI: 0.327–0.772, P = .004) having progressively lower osteoporosis risk compared to those in Q1.

3.4. Dose-response relationship and threshold effect analysis

After full adjustment, an L-shaped relationship was identified between UHR and osteoporosis risk (P-non-linear = 0.001), with a cutoff value of 0.096 determined by the RCS model (Fig. 2). A two-piecewise logistic regression model assessed the association of UHR with osteoporosis on either side of this cutoff (Table 3). Threshold effect analysis revealed a significant negative association below the cutoff (OR = 0.393, 95% CI: 0.249–0.619, P < .001), but no significant association above it (OR = 0.953, 95% CI: 0.769–1.181, P = .661).

3.5. Subgroup analysis and interaction test

To determine whether the association between UHR and osteoporosis risk persists across specific populations, subgroup analyses were performed for high-risk (UHR < 0.096) and low-risk (UHR ≥ 0.096) groups. In the high-risk group, a consistently negative association was observed in most subgroups (Fig. 3). However, this association was not significant among individuals aged ≥ 75 years or those with diabetes or cardiovascular disease. Notably, this association persisted in both high-risk group (OR = 0.305, 95% CI: 0.136–0.686, P = .005) and low-risk group (OR = 0.498, 95% CI: 0.329–0.754, P = .002) among individuals aged 60 to 74 years. Interaction tests confirmed that the relationship between UHR and osteoporosis was significantly influenced by age (high-risk group: P for interaction = .046; low-risk group: P for interaction < .001) and race (high-risk group: P for interaction = .031).

3.6. Mediation analysis

Figure 4 illustrated the mediating role of BMI in the association between UHR and osteoporosis. We found that UHR was positively associated with BMI (β = 1.795, 95% CI: 1.574–2.017, P < .001), and higher BMI was significantly associated with a lower risk of osteoporosis (OR = 0.860, 95% CI: 0.829–0.893, P < .001). A significant mediating effect of BMI (proportion of mediation: 58.4%, P < .001) in the association between UHR and osteoporosis was observed. The average causal mediation effect of BMI was −0.020 (95% CI: −0.029–−0.015, P < .001), while the average direct effect was −0.014 (95% CI: −0.017–0.008, P = .384), indicating that BMI fully mediates the association between UHR and osteoporosis (Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q742).

3.7. Diagnostic ability

A pairwise comparison of ROC curves (Fig. 5A) showed that BMI had a significantly better diagnostic ability than UHR (P = .012). Regarding risk reclassification, the continuous NRI for BMI compared to UHR was 0.17 (95% CI: 0.06–0.27, P = .009), indicating a 17% increase in the proportion of correct reclassification for BMI relative to UHR (Fig. 5B).

4. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to identify an L-shaped association between UHR levels and osteoporosis, with BMI serving as a full mediator. Our findings show that higher UHR is associated with a lower risk of osteoporosis, particularly below the cutoff value of 0.096. Furthermore, BMI mediated this relationship and demonstrated superior diagnostic performance compared to UHR.

Previous studies have explored the association between UA and BMD at various skeletal sites. A meta-analysis by Veronese et al[29] found that elevated serum UA levels were associated with increased BMD at the femoral neck, hip, and lumbar spine, as well as a lower prevalence of osteoporosis and reduced fracture risk. Similarly, Kim et al[30] found a positive association between UA and BMD in the femoral neck, total femur, and lumbar spine in men over 50 years, suggesting a protective effect of serum UA against bone deterioration. However, a cross-sectional study in a healthy population found no association between UA levels and BMD in middle-aged and elderly men.[31] Interestingly, a positive association was observed only in the normal weight group, where higher UA levels correlated with increased BMD at all skeletal sites.[31] The relationship between serum UA and BMD in postmenopausal women remains controversial.[32] In addition, a recent Mendelian randomization study provided compelling genetic evidence against a causal association between serum UA and osteoporosis.[8] These inconsistencies highlight the need for a comprehensive UA index to more accurately assess its correlation with BMD.

The novel index UHR, which combines UA and HDL-C to reflect inflammation and metabolic status, has emerged as an effective biomarker for predicting various disorders and mortality, including insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular disease.[13–19] Our results confirm a strong association between UHR levels and osteoporosis, although the precise biological mechanisms underlying this relationship remain unclear. These findings may be explained by the antioxidant effects of circulating UA, which help mitigate oxidative stress-related bone loss.[33] At normal or elevated levels, UA is associated with increased BMD. However, in cases of hyperuricemia or gout, elevated UA may increase fracture risk due to heightened oxidative stress and inflammatory cytokine activity.[33] This supports our findings that UHR is associated with a lower risk of osteoporosis below the cutoff value but loses its protective effect above this threshold. In addition, HDL-C is known for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, which contributes positively to bone metabolism. However, epidemiological studies on the relationship between HDL-C levels and BMD have produced mixed results.[9–12] This complex relationship complicates the interpretation of the observed association between UHR and osteoporosis risk.

It is important to note that full mediation of BMI in the association between UHR and osteoporosis. Higher body weight exerts mechanical loading on bones, which stimulates bone formation and helps maintain bone density.[34] Previous studies have shown that BMI is positively correlated with BMD, particularly in weight-bearing bones, and is inversely related to both the prevalence and incidence of osteoporosis.[35] While being underweight is linked to a lower risk of cardiovascular disease, low body weight can adversely affect health through suboptimal nutrition and reduced muscle mass and strength.[36] In short, decreased mechanical loading and muscle stress on bones can potentially lead to lower peak bone mass and increased bone loss. These mechanisms support that lower BMI mediates the association between low UHR and a higher risk of osteoporosis.

Our findings suggest that BMI provides a significantly better prediction of osteoporosis risk than UHR. Therefore, monitoring BMI is a more effective method for assessing osteoporosis risk than relying on UHR. Nonetheless, UHR may still be valuable for identifying individuals who could benefit from comprehensive metabolic assessments, especially when metabolic dysfunction and inflammation impact bone health. A recent study found significant positive correlations between UHR and the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular disease.[37] Since chronic conditions such as cardiovascular and kidney disease are risk factors for osteoporosis, monitoring UHR could help identify individuals at greater risk for bone health deterioration.

This study has several limitations. First, causality cannot be definitively established. Second, cross-sectional studies, while informative, are vulnerable to confounding variables that could introduce bias. Despite efforts to account for these factors, unknown variables – such as diet quality, nutritional supplements, and exercise types – or biases may still be present, potentially leading to inaccuracies. Finally, serum estradiol was not included in the analysis due to missing data in the NHANES 2011 to 2012 and 2017 to 2018 cycles. The omission of estradiol, particularly in postmenopausal women where its decline significantly impacts bone density, may have introduced residual confounding. It is possible that some of the associations observed in our study could be partially mediated or attenuated by unmeasured variations in estradiol. Therefore, a cautious approach is necessary when interpreting these findings, and further validation under different conditions is required.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study found a positive association between UHR and BMD at 3 anatomical sites (femoral neck, total femur, and lumbar spine) and identified an L-shaped relationship between UHR and osteoporosis prevalence in older adults. BMI mediated the association between UHR and osteoporosis and provided better diagnostic power for osteoporosis. Therefore, monitoring BMI may be more effective than relying on UHR for assessing osteoporosis risk.
