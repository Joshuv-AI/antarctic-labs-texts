Rarely listed essential medicines in 158 national lists
Authors: Moizza Zia Ul Haq, Camila Heredia, Amal Rizvi, Aine Workentin, Nav Persaud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Background Health needs are relatively similar across the planet as there are few conditions that are truly unique to one region or country, and most conditions are common around the world. Differences in health needs do not explain differences in national essential medicines lists. Factors other than health needs may well lead different countries to select different medicines. The purpose of this study is to identify and describe medicines listed by only a small number of countries. Methods Using the Global Essential Medicines database of NEMLs that was updated in 2023 and 2025, we identified medicines listed by 2 or fewer countries that were not listed on the 2023 World Health Organization’s Model List of Essential Medicines. We characterized these rare medicines and their indications based on information obtained from the NEMLs that listed them, and also performed literature searches for each rarely listed medicine. We also identified which countries listed many rare medicines (5 or more) and performed literature searches to explore possible explanations for them being rarely listed. Results We identified 460 rarely listed essential medicines, including 317 medicines that were listed in the NEML of only one country, and 143 medicines that were listed by two countries. A total of 83 countries listed at least one rare medicine, including 30 countries that listed many (5 or more) rarely listed medicines and 53 that listed few rare medicines. Countries that listed many rare medicines included Greece, Australia, and the Russian Federation. Conclusion Certain medicines are prioritized for access in just one or two countries, and some countries list many such medicines. Further work is needed to understand why certain medicines are rarely listed and whether changes to lists are needed.

Background

Health needs are relatively common across the planet and yet medicine usage varies considerably. The World Health Organization (WHO) Model List of Essential Medicines includes medicines selected in accordance with disease prevalence, public health relevance, efficacy, safety and cost-effectiveness (The selection and use of essential medicines 2023: Executive summary of the report of the 24th WHO Expert Committee on the Selection and Use of Essential Medicines, 2023). The Model List serves as a guide for countries to adapt their own National Essential Medicines Lists (NEMLs) in accordance with national priorities.

There are few conditions that are truly unique to one region or country, and most conditions are common around the world. Many medicines are commonly listed on NEMLs, such as amoxicillin, acetylsalicylic acid, atenolol, diazepam, insulin, metformin, salbutamol, and zidovudine (Persaud et al., 2019). However, NEMLs vary greatly when compared with each other and the WHO Model List, even when accounting for healthcare expenditure and WHO Region (Persaud et al., 2019; Piggott et al., 2022). A 2017 analysis of 137 NEMLs found that 60% of all listed medicines were listed by a relatively small proportion of the countries (10% or fewer) (Persaud et al., 2019). Differences in health needs do not explain differences in NEMLs.

Factors other than health needs may well lead different countries to select different medicines. For example, if a country produces a medicine, it may be preferentially listed over a medicine with similar clinical effects that need to be imported (Local Production for Access to Medical Products: Developing a Framework to Improve Public Health, 2011). At the same time, other actors such as clinicians, patients and pharmaceutical companies may advocate for the listing of particular medicines, whether or not they meet a priority health need. The selection of a limited number of medicines that are deemed essential is intended to streamline the procurement and distribution of quality-assured medicines and lower healthcare system costs. Essential medicines must be carefully selected to ensure they facilitate equitable access to medicines and promote their appropriate use. Medicines listed by relatively few countries warrant further examination to assess whether they meet the intended goals of NEMLs and whether they should be removed or perhaps added by other countries. The purpose of this study is to identify and describe medicines listed by only a small number of countries and explore possible reasons for them being rarely listed.

Methods

We searched for NEMLs in May 2023, by performing web searches for NEMLs and government (e.g. Ministry of Health) websites, contacting listed technical officers responsible for access to health care, and contacting regional technical officers and experts studying essential medicines. We included lists published in all languages. We included outpatient and inpatient lists across different levels of care (primary, secondary, tertiary, and quaternary), and excluded prescribing guideline documents that were not lists of medicines. In April 2025, the WHO launched the global repository for NEMLs (World Health Organization, 2025). We searched the repository and performed additional web searches in April 2025 to obtain updated NEMLs. The sources of the NEMLs included in our analysis are listed in the Supplemental Material.

We abstracted medicines listed by each country to create an updated Global Essential Medicines (GEM) database, as described in detail elsewhere (Persaud et al., 2019). We used the database to identify rarely listed medicines, defined as those listed by either only 1 or 2 countries and not listed in the 2023 WHO Model Essential Medicines List. We manually checked NEMLs to confirm that these medicines were rarely listed and to characterize the use or indication of the medicines as described in the list documents.

We explored possible reasons for these medicines being rarely listed by performing literature searches. For countries that listed many rarely listed medicines, we focused on understanding country characteristics that could explain why some countries tend to list many rarely listed medicines. We searched Medline and Google Scholar in August 2024, using the search terms [country name] AND either (essential medicines, essential drugs), or (pharmaceutical). We explored patterns between the total length of the NEMLs as well as healthcare expenditure and the number of rarely listed medicines. We obtained health expenditure data for the year 2021 from the Global Health Observatory (The World Health Organization, 2023), or other sources if the data were not available in the Global Health Observatory (Amnesty International Press Release, 2010; Brink, 2017).

For rarely listed medicines included by countries that do not list many rarely listed medicines, we conducted literature searches focused on the characteristics of the medicines. For these medicines, we searched Medline in August 2024 for systematic reviews and clinical trials. We also searched each medicine on the DrugBank database for the medicine indications and conducted Google searches with the search terms [medicine name] AND either [discovery], [patent], or [manufacturer] (Knox et al., 2024). We also searched the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) databases for information on regulatory approvals. We repeated the searches in December 2025 to characterize additional rarely listed medicines identified in the updated lists obtained in April 2025.

Results

We assessed 2153 medicines listed by 158 countries. We identified 460 rarely listed medicines (21% of 2153); 143 (31% of 460) were listed by two countries and 317 (69%) by only one country.

A total of 83 countries (53% of 158) listed a rare medicine. There were 30 countries that listed many (5 or more) rarely listed medicines (Table 1) and 53 that listed few (4 or fewer) rarely listed medicines. The countries that listed the most rarely listed medicines included Greece (73 medicines), Australia (49 medicines), the Russian Federation (32 medicines), Maldives (30 medicines), and Ireland (28 medicines). Country characteristics

We found that countries that list many rare medicines tend to have longer NEMLs overall (Figure 1). For example, the NEMLs for Australia and Ireland contain all the medicines that may be reimbursed according to the countries’ benefits schemes. As a result, these lists include several medicines (Australia’s NEML lists 807 medicines and Ireland’s NEML lists 745 medicines) and thus include a greater number of rare medicines. Some countries that list many rare medicines have high healthcare expenditures, such as Australia and Ireland. However, this is not always the case, as some countries with high healthcare expenditures may list fewer number of rare medicines, such as Sweden (healthcare expenditure is 6901 USD per capita, but lists only 2 rare medicines). Others may have high pharmaceutical expenditures in particular – for instance, Greece has had one of the highest expenditures on retail pharmaceuticals per capita among OECD countries. In 2007, Greece’s pharmaceutical expenditure comprised 24.8% of total health expenditure, the 3rd highest among OECD countries (Kanellopoulou, 2012). Pharmaceutical spending was 667 USD PPP per capita in 2007 (OECD, 2023). Other countries such as the Islamic Republic of Iran or the Russian Federation may list many rare medicines due to sanctions that impact the import of finished products and pharmaceutical raw materials and policies promoting local production of medicines (Asadi-Pooya et al., 2022; Peach, 2011). While the Islamic Republic of Iran included many rarely listed medicines in earlier NEMLs (such as the 2017 list), the most recent NEML (from 2021) includes no rarely listed medicines. Medicine characteristics

We conducted literature searches for 53 medicines listed exclusively by countries that listed few rarely listed medicines to understand why they are rarely listed. Some medicines were recently discovered or approved. Valbenazine, for instance, was listed by Ethiopia, was first discovered in 2015, and approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2017 under an orphan drug designation (Drugs@FDA n.d.; Food and Drug Administration, 2017; O’Brien et al., 2015). Lutetium (177lu) oxodotreotide, listed by Kenya, is used in neuroendocrine tumours and was first approved by the EMA in 2017, and the FDA in 2018 (European Medicines Agency, 2018; Food and Drug Administration, 2019). Rimegepant, for use in migraines, is listed by Estonia and received its first global approval by the FDA in 2020 (Scott, 2020). Development for sotrovimab, which is listed by Uganda, first began upon the emergence of COVID-19 (Drysdale et al., 2024). Patiromer calcium and sodium zirconium cyclosilicate are for the treatment of hyperkalemia and were listed by Jordan. Patiromer calcium was approved by the FDA in 2015 and by the European Medicines Agency in 2017 (Veltassa (Patiromer) Powder for Oral Suspension, 2017). Sodium zirconium cyclosilicate was FDA approved in 2018 (Drugs@FDA n.d.). Of these newer medicines, some are indicated for rare diseases, such as the bile cancer medicines pemigatinib, listed by Estonia and approved in 2020, and ivosidenib, listed only by Latvia, which was developed in 2018 and first approved by the FDA in 2021 (Patel et al., 2023; Popovici-Muller et al., 2018; Food and Drug Administration, 2024). Eteplirsen (listed by Kazakhstan) is used to treat Duchenne muscular dystrophy (Lim et al., 2017; McCafferty & Scott, 2019).

We identified some medicines that were developed or manufactured in specific countries or regions, explaining why they were listed in certain country’s NEMLs but not most others. For example, ormeloxafine is a contraceptive medicine that was discovered in India in the late 1990s, and has since been only marketed in India (Kumar et al., 2018). Another example is the Ebola vaccine, which is listed on the NEML of Guinea, which, in 2014, was home to the most severe Ebola outbreak in recorded history (Keita et al., 2021). Ebola vaccine trials have been conducted in Guinea since 2015 (Henao-Restrepo et al., 2017). Guinea faced another outbreak of Ebola in early 2021 and nearly 11,000 people were vaccinated (Ebola outbreak, 2021- N’Zerekore, Guinea n.d.; Keita et al., 2021).

For most medicines, there was no clear reason identified to explain being listed in the NEML of only one or two countries. Some of these medicines were in the same class as other more commonly listed medicines. For example, cyclopenthiazide, was listed only by Zambia, whereas another thiazide diuretic of the same class, hydrochlorothiazide, is listed by 143 countries. Similarly, brompheniramine is listed only by Thailand, but clorpheniramine, which is an antihistamine of the same class, is listed by 96 countries. For five medicines, literature searches retrieved few or no clinical trials demonstrating their effectiveness or safety. These include pipenzolate, quinisocaine, pramiracetam, fenoxazoline, and bemegride.

Pipenzolate, listed by Bahrain, is an anti-muscarinic agent used in the treatment of gastrointestinal motility disorders, including peptic ulcers (Chen 1954; Pipenzolate, 2021). Only a few early studies in humans have examined the effect of pipenzolate on factors such as gastric secretion (Vincent et al., 1967), pancreatic secretion (Bock et al., 1968), and peptic ulcer management (Duggan, 1965). Its use as per Bahrain’s NEML is for ‘gastrointestinal colic in children’; however, a 2008 article in the Bahrain Medical Bulletin claimed that while pipenzolate was commonly prescribed for this indication, this use is not supported by scientific literature (Al Hajeri, 2008). Quinisocaine, also known as dimethisoquin, is listed by Madagascar’s NEML as an anti-hemorrhoidal. One randomized controlled trial, based in Germany, has studied its effects on patients with anorectal conditions (Rothhaarr & Bauleke, 1996), and a study conducted in 1957 examined its effect on pruritic dermatoses (Robinson, 1957). No other studies of this medicine in humans were identified. Quinisocaine is available under the trade name Haenal. There have only been a few clinical trials examining pramiracetam: one examined the effect of pramiracetam in treating memory and other cognitive problems of 4 males who have sustained brain injuries (McLean et al., 1991), and one examined its effects on scopolamine-induced amnesia in 12 healthy males (Mauri et al., 1994). Pramicracetam is approved nationally and authorised in some European countries (Pramiracetam: List of nationally authorised medicinal products, 2020). The FDA granted it approval under an orphan drug designation in 1991, but that approval has since been withdrawn (Pramicateram sulfate). Only one trial involving fenoxazoline was identified, which studied the effects of an internal nasal dilator and fenoxazoline on nasal airflow resistance in 17 healthy adults (Lorino et al., 1999).

Finally, bemegride is described as a respiratory stimulant and a treatment for barbiturate overdose. Some case reports and a few clinical trials from the 1950s and 1960s have examined the role of bemegride in barbiturate or other sedative poisoning, with mixed results (Gershon & Shaw, 1957; Kavelman, 1960; Powell & Schonell, 1962; Worlock, 1956).

Discussion

More than one-fifth of medicines in at least one NEML are listed by two or fewer countries. Some countries such as Greece and the Russian Federation list many rare medicines potentially due to country factors such as trade sanctions and economic policies. Some medicines may be rarely listed as they were recently approved (e.g. valbenazine, ivosidenib, lutetium (177lu) oxodotreotide, pemigatinib, rimegepant, and sotrovimab) or discovered or manufactured locally (e.g. ormeloxifene). But often there is no obvious explanation of why the medicine is listed by only one or two countries. Together these results suggest that there are often factors other than the effectiveness of medicines or the health needs that they meet that determine inclusion in NEMLs.

Other studies have similarly found that NEML decisions might be based on considerations other than health. Economic sanctions, particularly those that disrupt the procurement of finished pharmaceutical products or active pharmaceutical ingredients negatively impact access to essential medicines for citizens (Asadi-Pooya et al., 2022; Gross, 2024; Sajadi et al., 2023). Sanctions may also result in changes to the pharmaceutical systems of affected countries, including increasing domestic pharmaceutical production and reducing reliance on imported goods and materials (Yousefi et al., 2019). This, in turn can impact which medicines are deemed essential. While it is recommended to select essential medicines based on the best available evidence, many countries face barriers in doing so, including limited leadership and institutional capacity, lack of locally relevant data, and lack of knowledge or experience regarding evidence-informed decision-making (Peacocke et al., 2022). Furthermore, NEML selection processes may be influenced by other factors such as procurement processes, dependence on donors, and lobbying by pharmaceutical companies (Mori et al., 2014; Peacocke et al., 2022).

Strengths and limitations

We included a large number of countries in this analysis. We considered both country-level and medicine-level factors impacting rarely listed medicines. We did not assess country processes for selection of medicines for their NEMLs. Some of the rarely listed medicines may not be prescribed or used often because countries use their lists for formularies in different ways. Countries could have provided explanations for listing decisions that we did not identify in list documents.

Conclusion

We identified 460 rarely listed medicines and 83 countries that list at least one rarely listed medicine in their NEML. This analysis indicates that there are opportunities to strengthen the processes for selection of medicines listed on NEMLs to ensure they are selected based on the extent that they meet health needs as recommended by international guidance.
