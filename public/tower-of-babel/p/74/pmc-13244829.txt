Development and preliminary validation of a multidisciplinary 3D‑printed skull base model for endoscopic endonasal surgical training
Authors: B. Sanz-Peña, M. Suffo, L. Galán-Romero, A. Jiménez-Alba, V. García-Alcántara, I. Iglesias-Lozano, F. Rodríguez-Peña
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe aim of the article is to present the development and application of a 3D-printed skull base model, which includes the internal carotid arteries, tumour and the optic nerve, to train neurosurgeons and ear, nose and throat (ENT) in performing the endonasal endoscopic approach (EEA). This procedure is challenging due to the proximity of critical structures such as nerves, blood vessels and brain.MethodologyThe 3D model was generated using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT) scans, which provided detailed anatomical data. These scans were processed with 3D reconstruction software to accurately replicate the key areas, including the tumour and surrounding structures. Neurosurgery and ENT consultants and residents, practised the EEA using these printed models. After completing the procedures, participants filled out a questionnaire to evaluate the realism and reliability of the model by applying a statistical analysis appropriate to the study.ResultsThe participants reported that the 3D-printed models provided a highly realistic simulation of the anatomical structures involved in the EEA. The model was deemed effective in replicating the key areas of interest, allowing them to safely practise the procedure in a controlled environment. Our results show that in two days approximately and for less than 10€, the surgeon may have the opportunity to train the surgery after establishing the printing parameters. A strong positive correlation was found among these variables (p < 0.01), suggesting that those who found the model useful also perceived a greater clinical applicability and believed that the 3D-printed model could help improve their surgical skills.ConclusionsThe study demonstrates that 3D-printed models based on MRI and CT scans offer a reliable and realistic method for surgical training. These models provide an essential tool for practising complex procedures, such as the EEA, improving the safety and effectiveness of surgical training and potentially enhancing patient outcomes.

Introduction

The continuous evolution in the representation of digital medical imaging [1, 2], as well as the manufacture of biomodels through 3D printing, is generating a revolution within hospitals and biosanitary centres [3]. It is already a reality in such centres where methods of representing medical images based on printed X-rays have been replaced by colour-segregated images of each tissue or organ of interest [4–6]. There are multitude of studies as these [7–10], which demonstrate the ability of these new technologies versus the conventional techniques and, economic savings, the quality, and the customization that is achieved with them, especially for complex anatomical situations. In the same way, these and other studies have also contributed to the implementation of surgical training techniques in disciplines such as traumatology and neurosurgery [11, 12]. The involvement of engineering (biomedical, biomechanical, or other related fields) is contributing to achieving the results required by medicine in this new biotechnological paradigm, as demonstrated [13–15]. Together they are creating multidisciplinary teams to resolve complex cases helping in the presurgical planification and, in the design and fabrication of 3D biomodels with high anatomical accuracy, using sustainable materials and techniques in economical and ecological terms. 3D-printed models facilitate the 3D visualisation of these structures, allowing surgeons to meticulously plan and rehearse surgical interventions. In addition, the use of Fused Filament Fabrication thermoplastic (FFF) materials, such as Polylactic acid “PLA” or Thermoplastic polyurethane “TPU”, that simulate soft tissues allows surgeons to interact with a realistic anatomical environment that emulates the conditions and sensations of a real surgery [4, 16]. These models, in addition to being anatomically accurate, are cost-effective and can be produced in a short amount of time [10], enabling neurosurgeons to practise complex procedures in a safe and controlled environment, accurately replicating the unique anatomy of individual patients. Traditionally, this type of training has been conducted in anatomy laboratories, which are expensive to maintain and not accessible to all neurosurgeons or Ears, Nose and Throat (ENT).

Due to these challenges, 3D printing can facilitate this training without the burden of maintaining an anatomy laboratory. Every neurosurgical or ENT department can use these models as a training tool. There is a growing body of evidence showing that these models improve surgical skills, allowing surgeons to advance in their learning curve.

One of the most common of these complex pathologies is tumours located at the base of the skull, which require the EEA [17]. The study performed by [10], showed that the two-dimensional (2D) endoscopic view, limited operative field, and demanding operational skills require careful training and practice, because of the complicated and critical nature of the anatomic structures, the EEA carries extremely high risk with potentially fatal complications. The increasing complexity of surgical procedures, particularly in anatomically delicate regions such as the skull base has driven the need for advanced methods in surgical training and preoperative planning [18–20]. EEA is a minimally invasive technique that provides access to the skull base through the nasal passages, and presents particular challenges due to the proximity of critical nerves, blood vessels, and other sensitive tissues [21, 22]. Progress in surgical instrumentation together with an enhanced level of surgical proficiency [23], have made possible the development of new, increasingly demanding surgical approaches and novel types of operations.

This work evaluates the reliability of these new techniques in the training prior to such critical surgical interventions for ENT specialists and neurosurgeons, due to the proximity of the surrounding vasculo-nervous system and decrease the possibility of complications related with the lack of practice associated with such a complex corridor and techniques [24].

Materials and methods

Image acquisition and 3D reconstruction

DICOM images from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with and without contrast, as well as a CT angiography (CTA) with a slice thickness of 0,5 mm, tube voltage of 120 kVp, tube currents of 200 mA, and a standard reconstruction kernel [4]. MRI protocol for cranial slicing should include a post-contrast 3D T1-weighted sequence (e.g., MPRAGE or vendor-equivalent) acquired volumetrically with isotropic voxels. Typical acquisition parameters comprise a slice thickness of 1 mm with no interslice gap, yielding approximately 170–220 slices for full cranial coverage, a field of view of approximately 240–260 mm, and a matrix of roughly 256 × 256. The volume should extend from the skull base to the vertex to ensure accurate multiplanar reconstruction.

To obtain the digital model in STL, segmentation was performed using the 3DSlicer v4.10.2 software (available at: http://www.3dslicer.org), and was used to obtain detailed anatomical information. CT has been used for segmentation of the skull, nose and carotid arteries, while the tumour and the rest of the optic nerves have been segmented with MRI. Figure 1a and b show the 3D Slicer segmentation of each of the parts, these images were processed to perform segmentation of the skull bone, internal carotid arteries, optic nerve, tumour, and part of the nasal structure.

The process follows a sequential order designed to ensure reproducibility and anatomical accuracy. The workflow begins with the native DICOM import (Step 1), which preserves spatial metadata and enables verification of voxel resolution and orientation through volume rendering (Step 2). Segmentation is initiated by creating a new label in the Segment Editor (Step 3), from which a preliminary mask of the cranial structures is generated using threshold-based extraction (Step 4). Subsequent steps focus on refining the primary segmentation mask. Unwanted regions and disconnected components are removed using the Islands tool (Step 5), followed by spatial cropping with Scissors to isolate the desired anatomical volume (Step 6). A controlled smoothing operation (Step 7) is then applied to minimize image noise and surface irregularities while preserving relevant anatomical detail. The refined segmentation is validated through multiplanar visualization in the 3D Viewer (Step 8), ensuring consistency across axial, coronal, and sagittal planes. Once validated, the segmentation is converted into a printable surface mesh via the Export to Models function (Step 9), typically generating STL or OBJ files. A final mesh repair step (Step 10) is performed using external software to close residual holes, correct surface normals, and ensure manifold geometry suitable for 3D printing or computational modeling. This stepwise workflow allows consistent generation of high-fidelity cranial models and supports transparent reporting of segmentation procedures in digital anatomy studies.

To ensure the stability of the entire structure during the simulation, a support was added to the posterior part of the skull. This support served to provide a stable base, facilitating accurate simulation conditions. Subsequently, each of these structures was further refined using the open-source Meshmixer software, version 3.5.474 (Autodesk, San Rafael, Calif) [4], enabling the smoothing of surfaces, filling of gaps, and enhancing the overall anatomical accuracy of the models. The combination of these techniques facilitated the creation of high-fidelity 3D reconstructions suitable for both clinical and research applications.

3D printing

UltimakerCura® software was used to laminate the models in preparation for 3DP. This software enabled the adjustment of printing parameters and optimization of the settings for each model based on the material properties and the requirements of the study.

Three cranial bones were fabricated using a rigid thermoplastic material (PLA), resulting in a solid and stable structure that accurately represents osseous anatomy. In contrast, the remaining parts, including the internal carotid arteries, optic nerve, tumour, and nose, were printed in flexible thermoplastic material (TPU), which allows for greater flexibility and realism when representing soft tissues and other anatomical structures.

The anatomical components were printed separately and subsequently assembled onto the 3D-printed skull. First, the tumor model was positioned within the sella turcica and secured using a high-density adhesive suitable for plastic structures. Both internal carotid arteries were then inserted through the respective foramen lacerum, following their normal anatomical course lateral to the tumor, until they rested alongside the sella turcica. The optic chiasm and optic nerves, printed as a single integrated structure, were placed superior to the tumor and affixed at their entry point through the optic canal using the same adhesive.

The printing parameters used for each part are listed in Table 1. The Support Interface parameter of UltimakerCura® has been enabled to print two of the skulls and the PVA material, which is soluble in water, has been placed on the extruder of the support interface. This way, a better finish is achieved in the structure of the skull and, in addition, the PLA supports are easier to remove. All parts have been printed at 1:1 scale on the Ultimaker S5 Pro Bundle printer.

We performed an endoscopic endonasal visualisation of all the bony structures and initiated the approach to the sphenoidal sinus. We introduced the endoscope through both nostrils and simulated a four hands surgery as in the operating theatre. A high-speed drill was introduced through the left nostril and the endoscope through the right, we began the drilling of the different bony structures and exposed the tumour in the sella.

Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 3.4.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing) [4]. After printing the 3D models, a demonstration was conducted with our skull base team, which included neurosurgeons and ENT, both attending physicians and residents. Overall, the satisfaction with the models was high, and all participants agreed that the model could be effectively used as a training tool. Three models were printed, each using a different protocol. Ultimately, the model with the highest number of slices was chosen. However, an important limitation arose: the original CT scan and posterior segmentation did not contain enough slices, which resulted in an incomplete printing of the ethmoid and sphenoid sinus and the lamina papyracea. Additionally, due to the consistency of the material, although the texture closely resembled human bone, the heat generated by the high-speed drill caused the plastic to melt after prolonged manipulation of the model. This last issue is easily solved by instilling saline solution as in the real operating theatre.

In this case, having a larger number of evaluators made the results more statistically reliable.

Discussion

EEA training has been conducted in anatomy laboratories, which are expensive to maintain and not accessible to all neurosurgeons or ENT [25–28]. Maintaining an anatomy laboratory presents several significant challenges that must be addressed to ensure efficacy and safety. One of the primary issues is ensuring the sterility of surgical instruments and preventing contamination, especially in facilities lacking specific surgical instruments. Anatomical laboratories must adhere to stringent protocols to avoid cross-contamination and infection risks. This requires rigorous sterilisation procedures, which increase the operational costs of the facility. Any lapse in these protocols can lead to the spread of pathogens, posing serious health risks to medical personnel and compromising the integrity of surgical training.

Another major challenge is the high cost associated with maintaining these facilities. The financial burden includes the procurement and preservation of body donor anatomy specimens, which often require refrigeration or chemical preservation techniques. These expenses can be prohibitively high, particularly for institutions with limited budgets, potentially restricting access to such laboratories for educational purposes. Ethical considerations further complicate the operation of anatomy laboratories. The use of body donor anatomy specimens in medical education and research is subject to strict ethical guidelines, requiring informed consent from donors or their families and respectful handling of human remains. It is necessary to balance the educational benefits of body donor use with the moral and cultural sensitivities surrounding death and the treatment of the deceased. Rebekah et al. 2018 [29], a study conducted in the Australian population revealed that even healthcare workers, who during their early university days needed access to such facilities, are not inclined to donate their bodies after death. In contrast, they had no issues with organ donation. The main reason for not donating their bodies was discomfort with the concept and uncertainty about what might be done with their bodies.

As described by the group of Matassa, another possible training is the anatomical training using a swine body donor or in vivo model [30, 31]. This kind of training facilitates the access to the models leaving behind the ethical considerations described previously in the human body donor practice but does not resolve the financial burden and possible cross contamination between species.

Due to the different approach and surgical techniques, some groups have developed a surgical training program using a capsicum and tomato model. It brings the opportunity to get familiar with the different instruments but it does not allow to improve the understanding of the nasal cavity anatomy or the touch of the anatomical structures but on the other hand, it is a very cheap surgical training with no ethical implications associated [32].

That is why 3D printing techniques have emerged as a new training tool, much more accessible and safer than an anatomy laboratory [15, 33–36].

Our model exemplifies this potential through its optimized technical specifications and low-cost profile: using Ultimaker S5 FFF printing with dual-extruder (PLA for skull at 0.25 mm layer height, 15% infill, €3.6+€2.5; TPU for tumor/nerves/vessels/nose at 0.15 mm layer height, 10–50% infill, €2.9 total), the complete anatomically faithful simulator was produced in 48 h for €8.40 (Table 1). Precise multi-material assembly (tumor in sella turcica, carotids via foramen lacerum, optic structures in canal) and saline irrigation during drilling ensured structural stability and realistic haptic feedback, demonstrating hospital-level scalability without specialized equipment.

Huang et al. 2021 [37], compared the application and usefulness of a 3D-printed model for a consultant neurosurgeon and a resident. In both groups, satisfaction was high with the information provided and the ability to practise each case before surgery. Although not statistically significant, the resident group found these models particularly useful, likely due to their lack of experience and desire to acquire new surgical skills. Another important fact is that the EEA in the paediatric population is very rare, and opportunities to operate on these cases are scarce. Having these models allows surgeons to practise as much as necessary, enabling them to gain the confidence needed to manage this pathology and It is worth noting that our results are very similar to the ones published before [38]. The residents from their study and ours, find the models more useful to understand the surgical approach and anatomical landmarks than the consultants. It may be explained because they are at the beginning of their learning curve and feel that every possibility to understand the approach may be useful.

One of the main issues before printing our case was patient selection. Although our centre is the reference centre for our demographic area and we have access to all radiological studies, most of the studies were not suitable for analysis. Most CT scans did not cover the entire nasal cavity or had a larger slice thickness, making it impossible to print that part of the access. In the case of MRIs, the main problem was that the studies focused on the sellar compartment did not include as many slices as needed, making reconstruction impossible. Regarding DICOM segmentation limitations, particularly in the ethmoid and sphenoid sinuses due to insufficient slice coverage in standard imaging protocols, alternative software such as Blender could enhance post-processing refinement in these complex sinonasal regions. This would complement Meshmixer (v3.5.474), which was effectively used for surface smoothing and gap filling in this study, and could offer additional optimization possibilities for future models. If we make these printings routine in our daily practice, we need to modify data acquisition so that every patient can benefit from prior training on their tumour.

Another interesting aspect of 3D printing is its utility in patient counselling. Jiménez et al. 2023 [39], used 3D-printed models to counsel and show parents and patients the defects and tumours. They reported that their understanding of the pathology, the surgical approach, and the potential complications increased after visualising their models. There are other studies in 3D printing in low grade gliomas which had similar results as mentioned before and helped the neurosurgical team understand the localization of the tumour and its relationships with the motor cortex and pyramidal tract [21].

These technical characteristics: €8.40 cost, 48-hour production, multi-material durability, position our model as a scalable body donor alternative, establishing 3D printing as the accessible paradigm for EEA training across neurosurgery/ENT departments.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that 3D-printed models, generated from MRI and CT scans, are an effective and realistic tool for surgical training, particularly in complex procedures such as EEA. The models allow for precise simulation of key anatomical structures, such as bones, nerves and blood vessels, enhancing the safety and accuracy of surgeons by enabling practice in a controlled environment prior to the definitive surgery.

The models were fabricated using 3D printing techniques, utilising materials such as polylactic acid (PLA) for bone structures and thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) for soft tissues. These materials provide a realistic and anatomically detailed tactile experience, though some limitations were identified, such as deformation associated with the heat emitted by high-speed drills.

Statistical analyses revealed high satisfaction among participants, with significant differences between residents and attending physicians. Residents placed greater value on the utility of the models for understanding anatomy and the surgical approach, likely because they are in the early stages of their learning and have not yet acquired the necessary knowledge to orient themselves and recognize essential anatomical structures in such a complex approach. Nonetheless, the results showed general consensus on the effectiveness of these models in enhancing surgical skills, potentially serving as a substitute for body donor training.

To maximise the potential of this technology, we propose standardising imaging acquisition protocols and improving the selection of printed materials, allowing all surgeons to benefit from this methodology in their preoperative training. This study demonstrates that the multidisciplinary collaboration between our engineering team and the physicians in our skull base unit will enhance the technical capabilities of our practitioners and allow us to increase the complexity of the procedures performed at our centre.

In conclusion, 3D-printed models represent a practical, cost-effective, and highly efficient solution for surgical training, with the potential to improve surgical outcomes and patient safety.
