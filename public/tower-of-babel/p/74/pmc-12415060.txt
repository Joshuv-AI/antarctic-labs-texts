Critical Assessment of Glyphosate and Alpha‐Lipoic Acid Effects on Granulosa Cells
Authors: Umair Arshad, Hafsa Riaz, Abdul Basit Rasheed, Muhammad Kashif, Mirza M Hadeed Khawar
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Even though we consider the efforts made by Kafshgiri et al. towards their study on the impact of alpha‐lipoic acid (ALA) on human granulosa cells treated with glyphosate, we pen this letter to share our grave doubts about some conclusions in their article [1]. Despite the authors' contributions to the accumulating literature on reproductive toxicology, we also note that several points regarding scientific and methodological issues are of paramount importance, as they put the validity of their conclusions and the overall implications of their findings under scrutiny.

The claim in the study that glyphosate causes severe reproductive toxicity at 250 μg/mL is contrary to what has been broadly concurred upon as a regulatory study carried out by numerous agencies across the globe. The more recent conclusion by both the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) in recent comprehensive assessment was that glyphosate does not fit any standard definition of endocrine disruption, and that it is unlikely to be a risk to the reproductive health of man using the products following the approved guidelines [2]. These results are integrated with large‐scale testing batteries where 11 Tier 1 assays were performed to assess the possibility of disrupting estrogen, androgen, thyroid, and steroidogenesis pathways, which collectively did not interact with these vital reproductive pathways

The linear take of the authors on glyphosate toxicity disregards a widely described issue of hormetic dose–response association with this herbicide. The recent literature has consistently demonstrated a biphasic action of glyphosate, and it has been reported numerous times that low doses appear to yield stimulatory, rather than inhibitory, effects. Duke et al. (2024) reported that the hormetic dose of glyphosate led to the growth‐promoting impacts, followed by increased expression of genes related to photosynthesis and metabolic processes [3]. The authors, however, did not conduct experiments involving lower concentrations, as they limited the number of doses to a single level, which may have overlooked essential effects of hormesis that could have altered the authors' conclusions regarding the reproductive effects of glyphosate.

The conclusions drawn by the study regarding the universally protective effects of ALA do not support recent clinical evidence that revealed situation‐specific and even harmful effects of ALA supplementation. A meta‐analysis conducted by Habibi et al. (2023) on the use of ALA supplementation in couples with recurrent pregnancy loss determined that some parameters had a positive effect. However, the outcomes were mixed and dose‐dependent, and many of the parameters showed no significant difference in their measurement [4]. These results suggest that the effects of ALA are more complex and context‐dependent than the authors appear to acknowledge, which undermines their generalized statements regarding its protective potential.

The use of isolated cultures of human granulosa cells by the authors is a significant limitation in their research, as it reduces the clinical relevance of the study. The scope of single‐cell‐type in vitro models in predicting in vivo reproductive outcomes is extremely limited by recent trends in reproductive toxicology. A 2025 study on improved granulosa cell isolation procedures acknowledged that “the limited half‐life and proliferative potential of human granulosa cells put additional challenges for their study, making the conventional approaches for cultivation less applicable” [5]. Intercellular, granulosa cell, theca cell, and oocyte communications are extremely complicated within the microenvironment of follicles and cannot be sufficiently recapitulated in systems of isolated cells. These natural shortcomings significantly undermine the authors' inferences about the real human reproductive physiology.

The inconsistencies described in the section above significantly call into question the validity and further implications of the authors' conclusions. We ask authors to clarify these issues of concern to science, especially inconsistency with the well‐developed regulatory frameworks, the management of hormetic dose–response effects, and the selection of their experimental model. Future studies should incorporate more comprehensive dose–response analyses, account for the differences between pure chemicals and commercial formulations, and utilize multi‐cellular culture systems that are more representative of human reproductive biology. In the meantime, we suggest that the reader treat this evidence with sufficient care until these fundamental scientific concerns are addressed by implementing further, more rigorous research methods.
