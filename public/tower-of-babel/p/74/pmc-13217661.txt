Ostreae Testa enhances anti-tumor immunity by activating T cells via antigen-presenting cell stimulation
Authors: Ju A Shim, So Min Lee, Hyori Kim, Hyo-Jeong Cha, Yuna Jo, Jun Hong Park, Changwan Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOysters are known for their rich nutritional profile and medicinal properties documented in traditional medicine. Their bioactive compounds exhibit antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and osteogenic activities, leading to widespread applications in food and medicine. Oyster shells—composed of > 90% calcium carbonate and various minerals and amino acids—are traditionally used as antacids in East Asia. Despite these recognized benefits, the mechanisms by which oyster shells modulate immune responses remain unclear. In this study, we elucidated the immunomodulatory effects of Ostreae Testa water (OTW) extract from oyster shells, focusing on OTW’s ability to modulate T cell responses.MethodsMouse splenocytes were treated with OTW extract, and T cell activation was assessed. Introduction

Oysters are a globally renowned low-fat seafood, rich in protein, vitamins, and minerals [1–3]. Traditional medicine, a comprehensive text on the history of traditional Korean medicine, recognizes oysters as a vital medicinal ingredient for treating and preventing various health issues. Their benefits — including tonic effects, calming properties, osteoporosis prevention, skin health improvement, and immune system enhancement — are well-documented in traditional Korean medicine. The therapeutic properties of oysters are utilized to produce biologically active compounds, which are widely applied in the food and medicine [4]. The biological role of oyster extract continues to be reported [5–7]. Oyster peptides (OPs) derived from Crassostrea gigas protect against intestinal oxidative damage induced by cyclophosphamide in mice, supporting intestinal health [8]. In particular, oyster shells, comprising > 90% of the oyster’s content, are widely used as traditional antacid medicines in China, Japan, and Korea. Oyster shells primarily contain CaCO3 and small amounts of CaSO4, CaPO4, various minerals, and amino acids [9, 10].

Recent studies have reported that oyster shell extracts exhibit immunomodulatory properties, suggesting their potential to regulate immune and metabolic functions. For instance, oyster shell extract was shown to inhibit inflammatory responses in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells [11], and Ostreae Testa water (OTW) extract derived from oyster shells reduced lipid accumulation and lipolysis in fully differentiated 3T3-L1 adipocytes [12]. Despite these preliminary findings, the exact mechanisms through which oyster-derived materials, particularly OTW extract, modulate the immune system remain unclear. Given the historical use of oysters in traditional medicine as described in traditional medicine, investigating their immune-regulating potential could lead to new insights into their therapeutic applications, independent of specific compositional analysis.

Therefore, this study aimed to elucidate the potential immunostimulatory role of OTW extract on T cell responses. Specifically, we investigated whether OTW extract could enhance immune functions by activating dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages, which subsequently lead to T cell activation through cytokine secretion. Our findings demonstrate that OTW extract indeed exerts immunostimulatory effects, promoting T cell-mediated anti-tumor responses. This work highlights OTW’s potential as a valuable immunomodulatory agent, offering promising avenues for addressing immune dysregulation and improving immune responses in various immunological disorders.

Methods

Animals

C57BL/6 (B6) mice were obtained from Koatech, Korea. OT-I and B6.SJL (CD45.1) mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). All experimental mice were maintained in a specific pathogen-free facility at Pusan National University (PNU) and were 6–8 weeks old of either sex. All animal experiments were approved by the PNU Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (PNU-2023-3243).

Herbal extraction

OTW and Ostrea Gigas water (OGW) extract were prepared from Ostrea gigas shells collected in Yeosu, Jeollanam-do, South Korea. Ostrea gigas shells were ground into a powder. Ostrea testa or gigas powder (1 kg) was extracted with 15 L distilled water under reflux at 100 ± 3 ℃ for 3 h. The extract was filtered using a 53 μm sieve, and the filtrate was evaporated in an air vacuum. The crude extract powder was obtained using a freeze-dried method.

Cell culture and stimulation with herbal extraction

Lymph nodes (LN), spleen (Sp), LN T, Sp T cells, and splenic antigen-presenting cells (APCs) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich), L-glutamine, 100,000 U/mL penicillin plus 100 mg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), non-essential amino acids (Sigma-Aldrich), sodium pyruvate (Sigma-Aldrich), and β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco). All cells were stimulated with CaCO3 (25 µg/mL or 200 µg/mL), OGW extract (200 µg/mL), and OTW extract (200 µg/mL) for 16 h.

Isolation of T cells from lymph nodes and spleen

LN and Sp were collected from mice and minced to isolate LN and Sp cells. Total LN and Sp cells were incubated for 3 h and harvested for each cell suspension. Harvested suspension cells were incubated with MagnaBind™ goat anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 40 min on ice to isolate LN T and Sp T cells via negative selection. The purity of the isolated total LN T and Sp T cells was confirmed using the Attune NxT Flow Cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Flow cytometry analysis

Organs were processed into single-cell suspensions, stained, and analyzed via flow cytometry (Attune NxT Flow Cytometer; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Dead cells were excluded using forward light-scatter gating and propidium iodide staining. Data were analyzed using Flow Jo version 10.3 (TreeStar). Antibodies with the indicated specificities were used for staining: CD69 (H1.2F3), TCRβ (H57-597), CD4 (GK1.5), CD8α (53-6-7), CD44 (IM7), CD80/B7-1(16-10A1), CD11b (M1/70), F4/80 (BM8.1), CD45R/B220 (RA3-6B2) CD45.2 (104) (all from Tonbo, San Diego, CA, USA), CD11c (N418), Gr-1 (RB6-8C5) interferon γ (IFNγ, 4 S.B3), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α, MP6-XT22) (all from BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), MHC-II (AF-120.1) (all from eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA), MHC-I H-2Kb (AF6-88.5), H-2Db (KH95) and human CD3 (Leu4) (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). APC-conjugated H-2Kb tetramers loaded with OVA257–264 and unloaded controls were obtained from NIH tetramer facility. An anti-mouse CD16/32 antibody (2.4G2; BioLegend) was used to block the Fc receptor. For intracellular cytokine staining, the cells were stimulated with PMA (12.5 ng/mL) and ionomycin (1 µM, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in the presence of brefeldin A (BioLegend), and subsequently fixed and permeabilized using an intracellular fixation buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, eBioscience).

Splenic antigen-presenting cell isolation

Sp were collected from mice and minced to isolate splenic cells. The suspension cells were removed after incubating Sp cells in a 37 °C incubator for 3–6 h (Figure S2A) [13]. The adherent cells, exhibiting DCs and macrophages, characteristics were re-suspended in cold phosphate-buffered saline for splenic APCs collection.

Co-culture system

Cells were cultured using a cell co-culturing model with LN T cells on a 0.4 μm transwell membrane (upper chamber) and splenic APCs in the bottom chamber with CaCO3, OGW extract, or OTW extract treated with conditional media. After 24 h, LN T cells and splenic APCs were harvested and analyzed using flow cytometry.

Cytokine array and image analysis

Mouse cytokine profiling was performed using a Proteome Profiler Mouse Cytokine Array Kit, Panel A (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), which detects 40 mouse soluble cytokines and chemokines (Table S1). The mouse cytokine array was incubated overnight at 4 °C with 400 µL of the cell culture supernatant. Following incubation with a detection antibody cocktail, antibody conjugation, and recommended washes, immunoblots were developed on the membrane using the Chemiluminescent Substrate Reagent Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) and exposed using an Amersham Imager 680 (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). The summary graph indicates the densitometric analysis of cytokine/chemokine spots as intensity ratios of cytokine/chemokine to positive control using Image J software.

Tumor cells and tumor growth

E.G7-OVA cells were maintained as per ATCC guidelines. Mice were inoculated subcutaneously with 1 × 106 tumor cells on the right back. Tumor size was monitored twice a week, and tumor volume was calculated as follows: volume (mm3) = width × width × length/2. Mice were euthanized when the longest tumor diameter exceeded 20 mm.

Adoptive cell transfer in vivo models

LN OT-I cells and splenocytes isolated from OT-I mice were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and stimulated in vitro with OVA257–264 (SIINFEKL; 0.1 ng/mL) for 2 d in the presence of the indicated treatments (OTW extract, OGW extract, or CaCO3 control). Activated OT-I cells (5 × 106 cells/mouse) were transferred intravenously into tumor-bearing B6.SJL recipient mice that had been injected subcutaneously with E.G7-OVA cells 11 d before adoptive transfer. Tumor growth kinetics were monitored by serial caliper measurements several times per week, and tumor burden was assessed by tumor volume over time and tumor weight at the experimental endpoint.

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte isolation

Tumors were resected from tumor-bearing mice, minced with scissors, and placed in a flask containing a dissociation solution of collagenase IV, hyaluronidase, and DNase IV (Sigma-Aldrich). The tumor mass within the flask was rotated, and red blood cells were removed using RBC lysis buffer. Cells underwent Histopaque (Sigma-Aldrich; density, 1.083 g/mL) density gradient centrifugation (Eppendorf Centrifuges, 1,025 × g for 20 min at 20 °C). Lymphocytes were collected at the interface for further analysis or isolation.

Statistical analyses

Data are presented as the mean ± SEM. Statistical differences were analyzed using two-tailed Student’s t-tests or one-way ANOVA. Statistical significance was set at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism.

Results

OTW extract promotes T cell activation in splenocytes

To investigate the effects of oyster shell on T cell activation within the immune system, mouse splenocytes were isolated. The impact of OTW extract was examined using untreated cells, CaCO3 (the primary constituent of Ostreae Testa), and OGW extract as controls. T cell activation was assessed by analyzing CD44⁺CD69⁺ expression in total T cells, CD4⁺ T cells, and CD8⁺ T cells. A significant increase in the frequency of CD44⁺CD69⁺ T cells was observed in splenocytes stimulated with OTW extract, which was not seen in the untreated, CaCO3, and OGW extract groups (Fig. 1A-B). CD44⁺CD69⁺ T cell frequency, including CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, was upregulated in a dose- and time-dependent manner following OTW extract treatment (Fig. 1C-F). These results suggested that OTW extract effectively enhances T cell activation in splenocytes.

Crucially, to rule out the possibility that this immune activation was an artifact of bacterial endotoxin contamination—a common confounding factor in crude water extracts—we performed a Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) assay alongside a comparative functional assay. The LAL assay, performed with a β-glucan blocker to prevent false positives, revealed only a negligible baseline endotoxin level of approximately 1 EU/mL in the OTW extract (Figure S1A). To determine the functional impact of endotoxin in our system, splenocytes were treated with purified LPS at various concentrations (0, 1, 10, and 100 EU/mL) as a positive control. While LPS induced T cell activation in a dose-dependent manner (Figure S1B), the magnitude of activation induced by the OTW extract was comparable with that induced by even the highest LPS concentration tested (100 EU/mL) (Figure S1B). Moreover, the baseline endotoxin level of 1 EU/mL was confirmed to be entirely sub-threshold, failing to induce meaningful T cell activation (Figure S1B). Taken together, these results conclusively suggest that OTW extract effectively and intrinsically enhances T cell activation in splenocytes, independent of endotoxin contamination.

Differential immune responses to OTW extract in spleen and lymph nodes

Next, we investigated whether OTW extract similarly affects T cells in LN, another secondary lymphoid organ. Notably, the increase in CD44⁺CD69⁺ T cells observed in Sp cells treated with OTW extract was absent in LN cells, similar to untreated CaCO3 and OGW extract groups (Fig. 2A-B). To examine potential differences between Sp and LN, we analyzed immune cell subsets. A higher proportion of TCRβ⁻B220⁻ cells (double-negative cells), characteristic of APCs such as DCs and macrophages, was observed in Sp compared to that in LN (Fig. 2C). As CD11c and CD11b are key markers of APCs, we hypothesized that these cells contribute to OTW extract-mediated T cell activation. Thereafter, Sp T and LN T cells were isolated, excluding B cells, macrophages, and DCs, and their responses were compared (Figure S2A). Sp T cells showed no significant activation in isolation, unlike splenocytes with APCs (Fig. 2D-E). Similarly, OTW extract-treated LN T cells showed no CD44⁺CD69⁺ increase, and CaCO3 and OGW extract groups in Sp T and LN T cells had no effect on T cell activation (Fig. 2D-E). Additionally, when OTW extract stimulated LN, and Sp cells except for B cells, T cell activation increased, similar to the OTW extract stimulated whole lymphocytes and splenocytes cells (Figure S2B-C). These findings suggested OTW extract indirectly activate T cells via interaction with splenic APCs, excluding B cells.

OTW extract-mediated splenic antigen-presenting cells drive T cell activation

To confirm that OTW extract activates T cells via splenic APCs, they were isolated (Figure S3A) and co-cultured with either Sp T or LN T cells in increasing APC-to-T cell ratios (Fig. 3A). T cell activation, indicated by CD44⁺CD69⁺ T cell expression, increased with higher APC ratios, particularly in co-cultures of splenic APCs with T cells compared to that in whole splenocytes and lymphocytes (Fig. 3B-D). Untreated co-cultures showed no increase in T cell activation (Fig. 3B-D), demonstrating that OTW extract stimulates splenic APCs, enhancing T cell activation.

Soluble factors from OTW extract-stimulated splenic antigen-presenting cells mediate T cell activation

To determine whether T cell activation was induced by soluble factors from OTW extract-stimulated APCs, LN T cells were treated with supernatants from OTW extract-treated splenic APCs (conditional medium stimulation) (Fig. 4A). CD44⁺CD69⁺ expression in LN T cells increased with a conditional medium at various dilutions for 16 h. CaCO3 and OGW extract treatments showed no effect (Fig. 4B-C). Using a transwell system to prevent cell-cell contact (Fig. 4D), we confirmed that T cell activation was significantly enhanced in OTW extract-treated groups, indicating that soluble factors from APCs stimulated with OTW extract promote T cell activation (Fig. 4E-F). We performed a cytokine array to determine which soluble factor caused T cell activation. Cytokine analysis results identified several upregulated cytokines and chemokines, including interleukin (IL)-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, KC, JE, MIP-1α, MIP-1β, RANTES, MIP-2, GM-CSF, IL-12p70, IL-27, and M-CSF in OTW extract-stimulated APCs compared to other groups (Fig. 5A-C). These data suggested that OTW extract-stimulated APCs secrete multiple cytokines and chemokines contributing to T cell activation.

Enhanced T cell activation mediated by OTW extract-primed splenic antigen-presenting cells

Next, we examined T cell activation when OTW extract-primed splenic APCs were co-cultured with T cells (Fig. 6A). Primed APCs significantly increased T cell activation compared to those in the untreated, CaCO3, and OGW extract groups (Fig. 6B-C). To elucidate the mechanism, we assessed activation markers on APCs. Upregulation of CD80, a co-stimulatory molecule essential for T cell activation, was observed in OTW extract-stimulated APCs, particularly in DCs (CD11c⁺CD11b⁺ in F4/80⁻H57⁻B220⁻), compared to other controls (Fig. 6D). The expression of MHC I (Figure S4) and MHC II (Fig. 6D) remained largely unchanged, with the exception of B cells. These findings support that OTW extract-activated splenic APCs, particularly DCs, enhance T cell activation via co-stimulatory interactions.

OTW extract augments antitumor responses of CD8+ T cells

In our previous experiments, we found that OTW extract could enhance T cell activation via APCs activation. Therefore, we hypothesized that OTW extract induces optimal CD8+ T cells activation in a tumor model. To gain further insight into the effects of OTW extract in CD8+ T cells, we assessed the anti-tumor activity of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells using OT-I mice. OT-I cells were stimulated with the ovalbumin peptide (OVA257–264, SIINFEKL) in a dose-dependent manner alongside CaCO3, OGW extract, and OTW extract, and their effector cytokine profiles were investigated in vitro. OTW extract produced more IFNγ than OT-I cells co-stimulated with CaCO3 and OGW extract (Fig. 7A-B). As OTW extract enhanced cytokine production, we determined the in vivo antitumor activity of OT-I cells stimulated with OTW extract using a solid tumor model with E.G7-OVA (Fig. 7C). While OGW-treated OT-I cells indeed showed an antitumor effect compared with the medium control, OTW-treated OT-I significantly exhibited higher efficacy than OGW-treated OT-I in adoptive transfer model (Fig. 7D-F). In addition, the frequency and numbers of OTW extract-primed OT-I cells increased and persisted in draining lymph nodes (dLN) and Sp compared to those in CaCO3 and OGW extract -primed OT-I cells. No significant difference was observed for Tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TILs) because the tumors had already completely regressed (Fig. 8A). Furthermore, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), representative immunosuppressive cells, were reduced in the Sp of the activated OTW extract-primed OT-I cell group (Fig. 8B). Notably, when activated OTW extract-primed OT-I cells were transplanted into E.G7-OVA-bearing mice, IFNγ and TNF-α expression in dLN, Sp, and TILs significantly increased compared to when activated CaCO3 or OGW extract-primed OT-I cells were transferred (Fig. 8C). These data indicated that OT-I cells are functionally empowered by OTW extract, leading to enhanced tumor rejection. To further assess whether OTW-driven soluble factors can enhance OT-I function in an antigen-independent manner, we performed additional adoptive-transfer experiments using OT-I cells primed under the following conditions: (i) SIINFEKL alone, (ii) OTW-conditioned medium (OTW-CM) alone (no SIINFEKL), and (iii) OTW-CM plus SIINFEKL, alongside the media control. Notably, OT-I cells primed with OTW-CM alone showed a statistically significant antitumor effect compared with the media control, indicating that OTW-CM can confer antigen-independent functional enhancement. However, antitumor efficacy was consistently greater when OT-I cells were primed in the presence of cognate antigen, supporting the conclusion that TCR-dependent stimulation is required for optimal activation/efficacy (Figure S5).

Discussion

Traditional medicine highlights oysters for their medicinal properties, including tonic effects, calming properties, and bone, skin, and immune health support [1–3, 5–7, 14]. Oyster-derived compounds are widely used in the food and medicine [4]. OPs protect intestinal health, and oyster shell extracts, primarily composed of CaCO3, have anti-inflammatory properties and are used as antacids in East Asia [8–10]. Given their substantial medicinal properties, oysters influence key health aspects such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and osteogenic effects linked to the immune system [5–7, 11, 15, 16]. However, the exact mechanisms by which oysters affect the immune system remain unclear, warranting further investigation. Therefore, this study hypothesized that OTW extract, derived from oyster shells, modulates the immune system. First, splenocytes from WT mice treated with OTW extract significantly increased activation of T cells (CD44+CD69+), including CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, compared to the untreated, CaCO3, and OGW extract groups. T cell activation via OTW extract was time- and dose-dependent, demonstrating its effect on immune cell activation in the Sp. These results suggest that OTW extract influences T cell activation, prompting further evaluation in LN, another secondary lymphoid organ. Contrary to expectations, OTW extract failed to activate LN T cells, indicating that other cell populations present in the Sp may mediate OTW extract-induced T cell activation. Co-culture of splenic APCs and T cells confirmed that DCs and macrophages contribute to OTW extract-mediated T cell activation in Sp. In addition, we examined whether OTW extract-mediated T cell activation was induced even without direct cell-cell interaction with APCs by treating splenic APCs with OTW extract and then treating the obtained supernatant to T cells. Consequently, T cells were activated by factors such as cytokines and chemokines produced by APCs in response to OTW extract. Our data showed that OTW extract activates T cells directly and indirectly by activating APCs.

Treating splenocytes with CaCO3, the main component of oyster shell [9, 10], did not induce APCs-mediated T cell activation, unlike OTW extract. This suggested that other components present in OTW extract, aside from CaCO3, contribute to this effect. Possible components include CaSO4, CaPO4, keratin, and amino acids, although their individual effects were not separately validated in this study. Furthermore, we conducted ingredient analysis to understand which components are responsible; however, owing to a lack of references, specific identification remains challenging. Future research is needed to explore the effects of other OTW extract components on APCs-mediated T cell activation.

APCs (excluding B cells) were isolated and their culture supernatant from OTW extract treatment was applied to T cells to assess activation. This approach confirmed that factors secreted via OTW extract-stimulated APCs contribute to T cell activation. Cytokine profiling of these supernatants revealed that OTW extract treatment induced distinct cytokine secretion profiles compared to other groups. Specifically, OTW extract-stimulated APCs showed upregulation of activation (GM-CSF, IL-12p70, IL-27, M-CSF) [17–20] and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) [21–24]. Pro-inflammatory chemokines (KC, JE, MIP-1α, MIP-1β, RANTES, MIP-2) [25–29] were also upregulated. This upregulation suggested that OTW extract enhances inflammation, which is critical for initiating innate immune responses via APCs activation.

The innate immune system initiates an early inflammatory response to defend against pathogens, beginning with local immune cell activation of neutrophils, macrophages, and DCs. These activated cells secrete cytokines and chemokines, amplifying local inflammation and recruiting adaptive immune cells to the site. This cascade strengthens initial immune defense and facilitates the transition to adaptive immunity, aiding in inflammation modulation and resolution. Thus, OTW extract-stimulated APCs, through their secreted cytokines and chemokines, likely play a crucial role in enhancing this innate-to-adaptive immune transition.

Normal inflammatory responses are crucial protective immunity because they can induce optimized adaptive immunity. Adjuvants that act as boosters are used to increase the effectiveness of vaccines [30–32] and methods that induce appropriate inflammatory responses to activate T cells in disease models [33, 34]. Key signaling pathways involved in this inflammatory response include NF-κB, JAK-STAT, MAPK, NLRP3-inflammasome, TLR, and PI3K-Akt [35–39]. Our cytokine array results confirmed that factors secreted in large quantities by APCs due to OTW extract stimulation are closely related to NF-κB and JAK-STAT signaling. In particular, activation of these signaling pathways in APCs is important for inducing T cell activation and antigen response. Accordingly, our results showed that OTW extract-stimulated APCs activate NF-κB and JAK-STAT signaling associated with inflammation, leading to the secretion of chemokines such as KC, JE, MIP-1α, MIP-1β, RANTES, and MIP-2 to recruit T cells. OTW extract-stimulated APCs also secrete cytokines (IL-6, IL-1α, IL-1β, and TNF-α) to activate T cells [40, 41]. These results suggested that OTW extract can be used as a booster to induce optimal T cell activation.

However, inflammation induced by OTW extract could potentially be attributed to residual external contaminants not entirely eliminated during oyster shell extraction. To mitigate this, Ostrea Gigas shell powder was extracted by reacting it in distilled water at 100 ℃ for 3 h, followed by filtration through a 53 μm sieve. The final extract powder was obtained via freeze-drying, with each step carefully designed to minimize potential contamination, using high-temperature treatment, filtration, vacuum evaporation, and freeze-drying, all of which effectively reduce pathogen presence [42]. Given these extensive purification steps, the likelihood of residual pathogens in the OTW extract is minimal; it is improbable that contaminants contributed to the observed inflammation.

Additionally, the historical use of oyster shells in traditional medicine suggests they are generally regarded as safe. Thus, it is unlikely that OTW extract would exhibit toxicity in vivo [5–11, 15, 16]. The observed inflammatory response is likely due to the inherent properties of OTW extract rather than any extraneous factors introduced during extraction.

Optimal T cell activation plays a crucial role in combating various diseases [43, 44]. In the tumor microenvironment, various immunosuppressive environments are established, allowing cancer cells to escape immune surveillance. Tumors employ multiple immune evasion mechanisms. Key limitations in cancer therapy include impaired APCs activation, poor T cell recruitment, and suboptimal T cell activation to kill tumors [45–49]. Current studies aim to address these challenges. Many cancer therapies combine agents such as cytokines or TLR agonists to enhance therapeutic efficacy by promoting the optimal activation of dysfunctional cytotoxic CD8+ T cells [50–55].

In this study, we found that OTW extract increased the production of cytokines that can induce T cell recruitment and activation by activating APCs, and also activates T cells via direct cell-cell interaction. Therefore, OTW extract could potentially serve as an adjuvant in cancer therapy by inducing optimal T cell activation via APCs activation. The effects of CaCO3, OGW extract, and OTW extract on antitumor activity of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells were evaluated in vitro using OT-I mice. The production of effector cytokines, such as IFNγ and TNF-α, by OT-I cells in response to SIINFEKL was significantly increased in OTW extract-treated groups, indicating that OTW extract can enhance the antigen-specific activity of OT-I cells, consistent with previous findings. To further evaluate the in vivo anti-tumor activity of OTW extract, we used a solid tumor model with E.G7-OVA. Activated OTW-primed OT-I cells exhibited superior anti-tumor effects than groups primed with CaCO3 or OGW extract. These effects included enhanced tumor growth suppression, an increased frequency of transferred effector OT-I cells, and elevated production of effector cytokines such as IFNγ and TNF-α. Regarding the apparent discrepancy between in vitro assays and the in vivo tumor outcome, we note that the in vitro readouts primarily capture acute effector activation under simplified conditions, whereas tumor control in vivo depends on additional determinants such as T cell trafficking/infiltration, persistence, expansion, and the cytokine/chemokine milieu. In this context, our cytokine/chemokine array (Fig. 5) shows that OGW induces a distinct soluble-factor signature compared with CaCO₃, with higher levels of IL-1α, JE/CCL2 (MCP-1), MIP-1, MIP-2 (CXCL2), and RANTES (CCL5). Notably, OTW induces these factors at substantially higher levels than OGW, which aligns with the significantly greater antitumor efficacy of OTW-treated OT-I in vivo. Although IFNγ production by OGW- and CaCO₃-treated OT-I appeared comparable in vitro assay, these differences in the surrounding chemokine/cytokine environment may contribute to more effective antitumor activity in vivo. These results demonstrated that OTW extract effectively induces optimal CD8+ T cells activation in a tumor model.

Although OTW also affected CD4⁺ T cell responses, this study focused on defining the mechanisms and antitumor relevance of OTW-driven CD8⁺ T cell immunity. A detailed analysis of CD4⁺ T cell subsets and their contribution will be addressed in future studies.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that OTW extract, an oyster shell extract traditionally used in Oriental medicine, serves as a potent T cell activator, playing a crucial role in the protective response of the immune system. OTW extract induces APCs activation, subsequently activating T cells via direct interactions with activated APCs and indirect pathways mediated via soluble factors produced by these activated APCs (Fig. 9). This dual activation effectively bridges innate and adaptive immunity. Optimizing T cell activation is essential for addressing challenges in treating diseases such as cancer and infections. Thus, our research highlights the potential of OTW extract-induced T cell activation via APCs as an immune enhancer, demonstrating a novel immune-boosting effect of oyster shell extracts validated by traditional medicine. This elucidation of OTW’s cellular mechanisms suggests promising therapeutic applications in modern medicine.

Proposed working model for OTW extract-mediated enhancement of CD8⁺ T cell immunity. OTW extract stimulates antigen-presenting cells (APCs), as evidenced by the upregulation of the costimulatory molecule CD80. Once activated, these APCs facilitate CD8⁺ T cell priming and activation via two primary mechanisms: (1) the secretion of immunostimulatory cytokines and (2) direct cell-to-cell contact. Ultimately, this APC-driven cascade results in robust CD8⁺ T cell activation and augmented effector functions.
