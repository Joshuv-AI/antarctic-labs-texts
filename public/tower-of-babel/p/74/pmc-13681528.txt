Polyfunctional varicella-zoster virus–reactive memory CD4+ T cells reside in human bone marrow
Authors: Zixu Wang, Simon Reinke, Yu Shen, Xiangyi Deng, Tobias Alexander, Anne Claußnitzer, Sebastian Hardt, Luis Becker, Koji Tokoyoda, Carsten Perka, Andreas Radbruch, Jun Dong
Published: 2026

The bone marrow (BM) is increasingly recognized as providing niches for the long-term maintenance of antigen-experienced memory T cells (Tm) [1]. Although most studies of human antiviral memory of the adaptive immune system rely exclusively on cells of peripheral blood (PB), prior work has demonstrated that BM harbors substantial populations of long-lived Tm, including those specific for measles, mumps, and rubella, cytomegalovirus, and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) [2]. These BM-resident Tm can be rapidly mobilized by a secondary challenge to participate in effective systemic recall responses [3]. As a molecular basis of memory, Tm exhibit stable tissue-specific epigenetic signatures of recall function and tissue-maintenance [4].

Whether the BM also harbors varicella-zoster virus (VZV)-specific Tm has remained unclear. VZV is a latent human herpesvirus that establishes lifelong latency and whose control relies primarily on CD4+ T cell-mediated immunity [5]. So far, VZV-specific CD4+ Tm are enriched among skin-resident Tm, as compared to circulating Tm [6]. Their residency in other tissues has not been analyzed, particularly not in the BM. To address this, we performed a paired analysis of Tm from PB and BM from 19 healthy individuals, aged 51–84 years (Table S1), assessing the quantity, functionality, and differentiation state of VZV-reactive CD4+ Tm (Figure 1A, detailed methods in Supplementary data).

VZV-reactive CD4+ Tm were detected by stimulating paired BM and PB mononuclear cells with the immunodominant VZV glycoprotein E (gE) peptide pool in the presence of CD28 costimulation. Antigen-reactive cells were defined according to their expression of CD154 (CD40 ligand) and one or more of the effector cytokines interleukin-2 (IL-2), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), as assessed by intracellular cytokine staining (Figure S1A, Tables S2–S3). Net responses were calculated by subtracting the CD28 costimulation-alone values for each sample, thereby minimizing the influence of donor baseline activity (Figure 1B). To better reflect the in vivo abundance of Tm cells, we adjusted the flow cytometry frequencies based on published estimates of T cell numbers in each compartment [7]. This enabled a more realistic comparison of the pool size of reactive memory T cells in BM and PB.

Compared with PB, BM contained significantly higher absolute numbers of VZV-specific CD154+CD4+ Tm (Figure 1C, Table S4A), while their frequencies were comparable between compartments (Figure S1B–S1F, Table S4B). Similarly, the absolute numbers of total cytokine-producing cells (i.e., cells producing any cytokine) and individual cytokine-producing cells (i.e., those expressing only one of the three tested cytokines) among CD154+ VZV-reactive CD4+ Tm were significantly higher in BM than in PB (Figure 1D–1E, Table S4A). Analysis of Tm expressing one, two, or three of the cytokines IL-2, IFN-γ, and TNF-α showed that most of these VZV-reactive CD4+ Tm subsets, except those producing IL-2 alone or IL-2 and TNF-α, differed between PB and BM in absolute numbers (Figure 1F, Table S4A). Interestingly, IFN-γ- and TNF-α-producing VZV-reactive Tm were the only subsets significantly higher in both frequency and absolute numbers in BM. Polyfunctional Tm, defined as cells producing two or three of the cytokines IL-2, IFN-γ, and TNF-α, were thus significantly enriched in BM (Figure 1G, Table S4A). This selective enrichment of IFN-γ-producing and polyreactive VZV-reactive Tm in BM parallels the situation in the skin, where such Tm are also more frequent than in PB. Together, these data identify BM as a major reservoir of VZV-specific CD4+ Tm, characterized by higher frequencies of Tm expressing IFN-γ+ and TNF-α+ relative to PB, and similar to the Tm of skin. Conceptually, this pattern is consistent with BM maintaining polyfunctional effector Tm for rapid responses to systemic VZV reactivation, whereas skin harbors such cells for local immune protection.

To further characterize VZV-specific CD4+ Tm in BM and PB, we analyzed CCR7 expression to distinguish central memory (TCM) from effector memory (TEM) cells (Figure 1H). In both PB and BM, most VZV-specific CD4+ Tm were CCR7+, indicating a TCM phenotype (Figure 1I, Table S4C). Although the relative frequencies of TCM and TEM were comparable between PB and BM, absolute numbers of both subsets were significantly higher in BM (Figure 1J, Table S4C). Thus, BM, like PB, harbors both the flexible (TCM) and effector (TEM) compartments of VZV-specific memory, consistent with observations in resolved acute and persistent viral infections [8].

Consistent with prior reports, we detected VZV-specific T cell responses in BM and PB [9]. Although individual variability exists, our estimates indicate that even at similar frequencies of reactive Tm cells in PB and BM, those of BM constitute a significantly larger population [10]. Nevertheless, this estimation approach cannot precisely reflect the exact cell number in individuals. Future studies incorporating more refined quantitative calculations and detection technologies will be necessary to achieve more accurate assessments. Meanwhile, although gE is an immunodominant structure in VZV immune responses, it does not fully represent the antigenic immune responses. Future studies using matrix peptide pools may allow a more complete characterization of VZV-specific T cell responses.

As data were collected from all participants at a single time point, this study precludes direct assessment of Tm migration or lineage relationships between BM and PB. Longitudinal studies integrating epigenetic profiling, single-cell transcriptomics, and T cell receptor (TCR) clonal tracking will be essential to determine the extent to which VZV-reactive Tm are mobilized from the BM into the circulation upon reactivation, and in particular, in relation to the pathogenesis of shingles. These findings may have potential clinical implications for understanding the long-term immune control of VZV and for improving strategies aimed at preventing VZV reactivation.

In summary, our findings identify BM as a major reservoir of VZV-reactive CD4+ Tm in adults. Compared with PB, BM contains substantially higher absolute numbers of antigen-reactive cells across functional subsets, particularly Tm producing IFN-γ and TNF-α, representing polyfunctional cells. Together with prior studies identifying the skin as a major site of tissue-resident VZV-specific memory, our data support a compartmentalized model of T cell memory to VZV, in which skin-resident Tm protect the skin, while those Tm in the BM provide systemic long-term memory.
