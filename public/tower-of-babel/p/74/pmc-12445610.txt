Prevalence of urogenital schistosomiasis among school-age children in Otamokun, Oyo State, Nigeria
Authors: Taiwo M Jaiyeola, Folahanmi T Akinsolu, Zaidat A Musa, Tunbosun A Olowolafe, Victoria O Iwu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The only child with moderate schistosomiasis infection was a male.•Mitochondrial cytochrome oxidase subunit 1 gene sequences were used to identify the specific Schistosoma spp.•DNA was not amplified in some of the urine samples with Schistosoma haematobium eggs.•There was a reduced prevalence of schistosomiasis after molecular characterization was carried out on the urine samples positive after microscopy.Keywords: S. haematobium; Schistosomiasis; PCR, DNA

Introduction

Schistosomiasis, a neglected tropical disease (NTD), is ranked second after malaria among the serious parasitic diseases in Nigeria, causing significant morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. It is most widely spread across the world’s tropical regions, mainly sub-Saharan Africa and also in the Caribbean, South America, the Middle East, and some countries in West Asia [2]. Globally, about 779 million people are reported to be at risk of schistosomiasis, with 207 million infected with the disease [3]. Africa alone accounts for more than 85% of all infections, and Nigeria has the greatest number of persons infected with or at risk of schistosomiasis (29 million people are infected, of which 16 million are children and around 101 million people are at risk) [4]. Urogenital schistosomiasis caused by Schistosoma haematobium is endemic in Nigeria, with substantial transmission in most states of the federation [5].

Furthermore, to control the disease effectively, accurate, precise, reliable, sensitive, and specific diagnostic methods are required for the accurate identification of Schistosoma spp [6]. This is important because decisions on treatment approach to adopt, assessment of morbidity, preventive chemotherapy, and other control actions depend on accurate estimation of the disease burden and intensity [7], but there are limitations to some of the diagnostic techniques [8]. Microscopy, although rigorous and time-consuming, is a cheap and easy procedure and the gold standard for schistosomiasis diagnosis [8]. It has been used widely in epidemiological studies to assess schistosomiasis burden in many endemic settings, but its inadequate sensitivity and dependence on the timing of sample collection, especially with low-intensity infections, often lead to sub-optimal results [9]. Antibody assays, on the other hand, are sensitive but are unable to differentiate the history of exposure from active infection and are not easy to carry out under field conditions, whereas antigen-based assays have been found applicable under field conditions but are less sensitive for S. haematobium infections [9]. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) based techniques, because of their high sensitivity and specificity, have been effectively used to identify many parasitic DNA, including S. haematobium [[9], [10], [11]]. Advancements in DNA isolation procedures and PCR technology, especially real-time PCR, make DNA amplification a credible substitute to urine microscopy [11]. Therefore, this study assessed the prevalence of S. haematobium in Otamokun, Nigeria, using a PCR assay to identify DNA of S. haematobium after urine microscopy was carried out.

Materials and methods

Study design

A community-based cross-sectional design was conducted between July and September 2023 in Otamokun, Ogo-Oluwa local government area (LGA), Oyo State, Nigeria, to detect S. haematobium eggs and DNA in the urine samples of school-age children (SAC).

Study setting and study population

Ogo-Oluwa LGA, Oyo State, in Southwest Nigeria, was purposively chosen for the study because it was one of the four LGAs reported to have a moderate prevalence of schistosomiasis in Oyo State during the last epidemiological mapping of Schistosomiasis and Soil Transmitted Helminthiasis in Nigeria [12]. The LGA currently has 10 political wards: Ajaawa I, Ajaawa II, Ayede, Ayetoro, Idewure, Lagbedu, Iwo-Ate, Odo-oba, Opete, and Otamokun. Otamokun was purposively chosen for the study because of its high schistosomiasis endemicity during the epidemiological mapping [13]. Otamokun is located in the rural area of Ogo-Oluwa with geographical coordinates 7° 57′ 0" North, 4° 11′ 0" East [14]. Agriculture is the major economic activity of the community inhabitants—cattle and goat farming, and food crops farming. The entire area is well drained by seasonal rivers, including rivers Amuro, Igbo, and Owo and their tributaries. The Amuro River is the largest of all and it flows around the community. The rivers are used mainly for agricultural purposes, including irrigation during the dry season, cattle grazing, and watering. Children also engage in recreational activities such as swimming, bathing, fishing, and fetching water, especially during the dry season. Otamokun has wet and dry seasons and most rains fall from March to October annually.

The last mass drug administration of praziquantel for schistosomiasis in this community was three years before the data collection. Infected cases were reported to the Ogo-Oluwa LGA primary health care coordinator and the Oyo State NTD coordinator, and drugs were provided for the infected children according to the national guidelines for reporting and treating NTDs. SAC aged 5-17 years old who had stayed in the community for more than 6 months and had not taken any Schistosoma treatment in the 6 months before the study were surveyed.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

The formula n = Z2p (1-p)/e2 yielded an estimated total sample size of 165, where n represents the sample size, Z = 1.96 is the standard normal variate at 5% type 1 error, p = 9.5% is the expected proportion in the population based on previous study [12], and e = 0.05 represents the absolute error or precision [15]. A multi-stage sampling method was used to select the children. In the first stage, Ogo-Oluwa LGA, one of the four LGAs with moderate schistosomiasis endemicity in Oyo State, was purposively chosen. At stage two, the Otamokun ward with a high level of schistosomiasis endemicity was chosen. In the third stage, households with eligible respondents in the study community were mapped. In the fourth stage, using the list of eligible households as a sampling frame, households were selected using systematic sampling, and every fourth household was selected. In the fifth stage, one child was selected per household to make up the sample size.

Data collection instruments and data collection

Relevant socio-demographic characteristics were collected from each child using a pre-tested semi-structured questionnaire. Each child produced mid-stream urine in a labeled 30 ml clean dry screw cap container between 10:00 am and 2:00 pm, the period when schistosome egg secretion is known to be at the peak in infected individuals. Urine samples were transported in cold boxes to the laboratory at Our Lady of Apostles Catholic Hospital, Oluyoro Oke Offa, Ibadan, Nigeria. All samples positive for S. haematobium after urine microscopy were stored in a refrigerator and used for molecular assay. Written informed consent was obtained from parents/guardians, and verbal assent was obtained from the children.

Data management and analysis

Microscopy

All 165 urine samples were subjected to urine microscopy. Ten milliliters of each urine sample was centrifuged at 3000-4000 x g for about 10 minutes, and the sediments were examined for S. haematobium eggs under 10x and 40x objective lenses and classified as light (1-9 eggs/10 ml sample) and moderate infections (≥10 to <50 eggs/10 ml sample) [16].

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted using the Qiagen QIAmp mini-kit (Cat. No./ID: 56304; Qiagen Sciences, MD). The DNA was extracted by cutting one quadrant of the 2 cm disc in half. The resulting segment was then cut into 2-3 mm squares and put into a 1.5 ml Eppendorf tube filled with 600 µl of nuclease-free water. This was shaken on a rotator at room temperature for 12 hours after being incubated for 10 minutes at 95°C. After centrifuging the tube for 10 minutes at 3000 rpm, the supernatant was transferred to a sterile 1.5 ml tube [17]. The Qiagen QIAmp mini-kit (Cat. No./ID: 56304; Qiagen Sciences, MD) was used to precipitate and concentrate the DNA following the manufacturer’s instructions, Qiagen 2016 [18]. The purity and concentration of the DNA were quantified using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis [19].

Polymerase chain reaction

A PCR targeting the Schistosoma-specific mitochondrial cytochrome oxidase subunit 1 (COX1) gene sequences of S. haematobium was performed for the identification of the specific Schistosoma spp. with a size of 500-650 bp [9]. The PCR mix comprised 12.5 µl of Taq 2X master mix from New England Biolabs (M0270), 1 µl each of 10 µM of cytochrome oxidase 1 gene forward (5′-TTT TTT GGT CAT CCT GAG GTG TAT-3′) and reverse primer (5′-TGA TAA TCA ATG ACC CTG CAA TAA-3′), 2 µl of DNA template, and then made up with 8.5 µl nuclease-free water. The cycling parameters consisted of an initial denaturation at 94°C for 5 minutes, followed by 36 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 30 seconds, annealing at 58°C for 30 seconds, and elongation at 72°C for 45 seconds. Followed by a final elongation step at 72°C for 7 minutes and hold temperature at 10°C forever. The primer used has been reported to be specific for S. haematobium to minimize the risk of undesired amplification [9]. PCR product was analyzed using gel electrophoresis to confirm amplification of the DNA, verify the product size, detect non-specific products, and assess the quality of the PCR product for sequencing [20,21].

DNA sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

The amplified fragments were sequenced using a Genetic Analyzer 3130xl sequencer from Applied Biosystems using the manufacturer’s manual, whereas the sequencing kit used was the Big Dye Terminator v3.1 cycle sequencing kit. The amplified sequences were purified using ethanol/EDTA/sodium acetate precipitation. Sanger sequencing was used and Bio Edit software and Molecular Evolutionary Genetic Analysis 11 (MEGA 11) were used for all genetic analysis. DNA sequences were identified using National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) BLAST, and errors were edited using the MEGA software [22]. The evolutionary history was inferred using the Neighbor-Joining method [23]. The evolutionary distances were computed using the Maximum Composite Likelihood method and are in the units of the number of base substitutions per site. Reference sequences were selected from the NCBI database based on the published sequences. All the sequences from the study area have been deposited in the NCBI database (Table 1).

Results

The mean age of the SAC surveyed was 9.81 ± 2.93 years, of which 87 (52.7%) were males and 66 (two-fifths, 40%) were in the 9-12 age bracket. Urine microscopy showed an overall prevalence of 7.3% (12 of 165) for S. haematobium eggs in the study population, with more males (seven, 4.2%) than females (five, 3.0%) affected. The majority (11, 91.7%) of the infected SAC had light infection (<10 eggs/10 ml urine sample), and one SAC, who was a male, had a moderate infection. Half of the affected children were between 13 and 16 years old.

After urine microscopy, all 12 samples containing S. haematobium eggs were assayed for S. haematobium DNA. There was amplification in nine of the 12 urine samples, and six of the sequences aligned with S. haematobium in the NCBI database (Figure 1). The three other samples that were amplified did not match any known organism in the NCBI database.

Phylogenetic tree

The phylogenetic relationship between the isolates from the study area showed that the isolates from the study community are related to the aligned sequences from the NCBI database (Figure 2). Isolates 5 and 7 are closely related to each other and also related to SH haplotype H4 cytochrome oxidase MT579447.1:478-599 (Senegal), MT579428.1:446-640 (Senegal), PP537995 (Cameroon), MK253576 (Tanzania), MK253576 (Malawi), and S. haematobium and Schistosoma bovis hybrid MT579443.1:437-615 (Senegal). Isolate 3 is closely related to SH isolates MT579449.1:437-615 (Senegal) and MT579447.1:437-615 (Senegal). Isolate 1 is related to both MT579449.1:437-615 (Senegal) and isolate 9. Isolate 9 is also related to SH isolates MT579421.1:343-520 (Senegal) and MT579449.1:437-615 (Senegal). Related isolates were from Northern Senegal, Cameroon, Tanzania, and Malawi.

Sequence similarity and NCBI BLAST analysis

From Table 1, BLAST analysis of the amplified mitochondrial COX1 gene sequences against the NCBI gene bank database revealed a high pairwise similarity to previously deposited S. haematobium isolates. The query sequences obtained from urine samples >1, >3, >5, >7, >8, and >9 showed percent identities ranging from 92.35-100%. Five of the sequences displayed 98.3% identity to existing S. haematobium mitochondrial COX1 sequences (Accession Nos. PQ373998, PQ373999, PQ374000, PQ374002, PQ374003), with two isolates (>1 and >8) showing complete identity (100%). Even the least similar sequence (>7) demonstrated 92.35% similarity to S. haematobium isolate BK17_BB7.

Discussion

The observed schistosomiasis prevalence of 7.3% based on urine microscopy among SAC in Otamokun, Oyo, Nigeria, represents a relatively low level of transmission compared with previous reports from other parts of Nigeria. Albeit, schistosomiasis remains a public health concern in the community studied. Several studies conducted among similar populations have documented significantly higher prevalence rates. For instance, a study in Ebonyi State reported a prevalence of 38.5% [24], whereas in Bauchi State, a higher prevalence of 58% was reported [25]. The low prevalence in the present study, despite mass drug administration with praziquantel in the community carried out three years before the data collection, may be indicative of improved access to safe water and reduced water contact behaviors.

However, when molecular characterization using PCR targeting the COX1 gene was performed, only a subset of microscopy-positive samples was confirmed as S. haematobium, resulting in a further reduction in confirmed prevalence. This discrepancy highlights a limitation of microscopy, which, while useful in field settings, may detect non-viable eggs or particles that resemble schistosome ova, leading to an overestimation of true infection rates. Similar findings have been reported in Southwest Nigeria, where several microscopy-positive samples did not yield amplifiable Schistosoma DNA, suggesting either degraded material or misidentification [26]. Another study across eight states in Nigeria also reported inconsistencies between microscopy and molecular diagnostics and detected the presence of hybrid species and potential zoonotic schistosomes in the regions [27]. These results are also in line with studies from other parts of Africa. For example, a study reported that molecular analysis in Senegal uncovered hybrid S. haematobium–S. bovis infections, which would have gone undetected with microscopy alone [28]. The ability of molecular tools to discriminate between species, identify hybrids, and confirm viable infections makes them indispensable for accurate surveillance, especially in low-prevalence or post-control settings.

Furthermore, this study used the mitochondrial gene (COX1) to construct a phylogenetic tree that provided insight into the molecular epidemiology of the S. haematobium parasite from the study area. The isolates were closely related to Schistosoma strains isolated from Northern Senegal, Cameroon, Tanzania, and Malawi. A similar study in Kebbi State, Nigeria, reported sequences phylogenetically closer to sequences from Kenya, Tanzania, and Malawi [29].

The BLAST analysis of mitochondrial COX1 gene sequences obtained from urine samples in this study revealed a high degree of genetic similarity with existing S. haematobium sequences in the NCBI GenBank database. This strongly supports the molecular identification of the parasite as S. haematobium, which is consistent with its endemic presence in the study area.

Two isolates showed 100% pairwise similarity with publicly available S. haematobium sequences, suggesting that these samples represent circulating strains that are virtually identical to previously characterized isolates. The remaining sequences demonstrated ≥92.35% identity, indicating close relatedness and minimal genetic divergence. Given that COX1 is a highly conserved mitochondrial marker often used for barcoding and molecular taxonomy of schistosomes, even slight variations observed may reflect natural intraspecific genetic diversity rather than taxonomic differences [30]. These variations could also represent different haplotypes circulating in the local parasite population.

One limitation of this study is that molecular characterization of S. haematobium was restricted to microscopy-positive urine samples. This approach may have excluded low-intensity or pre-patent infections detectable only by molecular methods. Comprehensive DNA analysis of all samples, irrespective of microscopy results, would provide a more accurate assessment of infection dynamics and genetic diversity.

In conclusion, S. haematobium is still prevalent among SAC in Otamokun, Oyo State, Nigeria, with a prevalence of 7.3% based on urine microscopy, whereas molecular confirmation using DNA sequencing matched only six out of the 12 microscopy-positive samples to S. haematobium in the NCBI gene bank. This discrepancy highlights the limitations of relying solely on microscopy for diagnosis and the potential challenges of molecular identification, including DNA quality, amplification specificity, and limited references for local strains. These findings emphasize the need for integrating sensitive molecular tools into schistosomiasis surveillance and the importance of enhancing genetic databases with region-specific parasite sequences to improve diagnostic accuracy and inform control strategies.
