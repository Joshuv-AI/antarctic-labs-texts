Erudit AI SaaS: an artificial intelligence tool based on RoBERTa for classifying employee engagement
Authors: Claudia García-Navarro, Manuel Pulido-Martos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEngagement has been defined since its inception by several currents, mainly two different ones such as those of Kahn (1990) and Schaufeli (2002). Based on these two main currents, self-report instruments for its measurement have been increasing over time. However, in addition to the limitations of the instrument itself (low reliability, low use, etc.), these instruments also have limitations inherent to self-report measures in general (social desirability, correction time, etc.). To solve this problem, and taking into account that the way of communicating through corporate tools in companies (slack, Gmail, Microsoft teams, etc.,) has increased considerably, this study proposes the validation of an artificial intelligence tool (Erudit AI SaaS) that allows to know the level of engagement of employees by analyzing the text they write in their corporate tools.MethodsTo test whether the tool is really assessing the level of engagement we compared the self-report instruments UWES-9 and JRA with the Erudit AI SaaS. Thirty-five employees were selected as participants for the study and the instruments were administered on five different occasions. To determine convergent and divergent validity, bivariate correlations were performed, in the case of reliability, this was calculated using intraclass correlation indexes.ResultsThe results showed good results for convergent validity between the Erudit AI SaaS and the self-report instruments UWES-9 and JRA. Divergent validity also yielded good results between the Erudit AI SaaS and MBI. The reliability, however, was moderate.ConclusionsThese findings provide preliminary evidence supporting the construct validity of Erudit AI SaaS for estimating employee engagement through textual data extracted from corporate communication platforms. However, while initial validity and reliability are encouraging, further research with larger samples and more robust statistical analyses is needed to generalize the results and strengthen the tool’s psychometric foundation.

Introduction

Engagement has been viewed as a state of active and emotional commitment that the employee develops towards their work and the organization. It has been linked to the degree to which employees are willing to go beyond their job duties to contribute to the success of the organization [1]. It has also been considered as a barometer of employee well-being, the organizational climate, and an indicator of the effectiveness in the application of human resources policies [2]. From a broader perspective, organizational engagement can refer to the degree of loyalty and connection that customers or stakeholders feel towards a brand or a company [3]. Engagement has been linked to positive consequences for organizations, such as talent management and retention, which ultimately improves financial results [4], as it increases productivity [5], protects against burnout [6] and the intention to leave the organization [7] and, in addition, allows for cost savings [8]. It also manages to explain an additional percentage of variance in external rotation levels beyond what is already explained, among others, by performance [6], thus gaining important attention among academics and professionals [9]. The current organizational environment, with changing market dynamics, constant technological advances, fierce competition, and unprecedented levels of customer interaction, presents an opportunity for companies and organizations to preserve and promote employee engagement, thereby aiding in the achievement of a greater competitive advantage [10].

The conceptual approach to engagement has been in constant evolution since the origins of the construct. Kahn [11] defined engagement for the first time, characterizing it as a deep physical, emotional and cognitive connection to work. This initial approach placed special emphasis on the role of the individual in expressing his or her role at work. Rothbard [12] complemented Kahn’s approach by orienting engagement in terms of attention and absorption in work, highlighting the importance of focus and concentration, as well as complete immersion in work tasks. Following a similar line, Shirom [13] and Saks [7] highlighted the same physical, emotional, and cognitive components in engagement. However, Saks goes further by introducing the idea of psychological presence in specific organizational roles, suggesting that engagement can vary depending on the role a person has in the organization.

Another approach completely different from those mentioned so far was that of Maslach and Leiter [14] who defined engagement as the antithesis of burnout. This approach allowed engagement to be understood not only in terms of its presence, but also in relation to the absence of certain negative states. Schaufeli et al. [15] advanced Maslach and Leiter’s conceptualization, introducing the components of vigor, dedication, and absorption, which expanded the understanding of engagement [15].

Other authors have enriched these perspectives by introducing ethical and moral dimensions [16], personal (involvement and satisfaction) [17], affective components [18], or by categorizing engagement as a trait, state, and behavior [19]. This conceptual diversity has led to a wide range of measurement instruments.

From the original proposal of Kahn [11] and the different approaches or conceptions of engagement reviewed, multiple instruments for measuring the construct emerge. The Work Engagement Survey [12] assesses attention and absorption at work. In developing the instrument, Rothbard tries to capture how employees were mentally present and focused on their work tasks. Shirom [13] developed the Shirom-Melamed Vigor Measure (SMVM) with the intention of evaluating the physical, emotional, and cognitive energy components of employees at work, and their willingness to invest effort, based on Kahn’s [11] vigor dimension. May et al. [20] created the Engagement Scale to assess broader aspects of engagement, such as security, availability, and integrity in the work role. The intention was to measure personal and work alignment and its impact on work engagement. The instruments Job and Organization Engagement Scales [7] and Employee Engagement Scale (EES) [21] emerged as efforts to provide a more complete assessment of engagement, integrating elements of both work and organization. The Job Engagement Scale [22] aims to evaluate physical energy, cognitive dedication, and emotional absorption, seeking to highlight personal application in work tasks. Finally, the JRA Employee Engagement Scale [23] was developed to offer a more practical and applicable measure so that organizations can easily evaluate and understand the level of engagement of their employees, that is, unlike other scales, places a greater emphasis on the employee’s engagement with the organization and their alignment with the objectives and values. However, all these scales have been used on very few occasions, most of them have only been studied once, so it would be necessary to further research them to obtain a higher level of knowledge and achieve their development and improvement [20, 24].

Likewise, other instruments have emerged based on approaches such as Britt’s (Soldier Engagement [16]), Harter et al.’s (Gallup Workplace Audit [25]), Macey and Schneider’s (Engagement Surveys [26, 27]), or Soane et al.’s (ISA Engagement Scale [18]). However, their use has been limited due to their low availability and the lack of strong empirical evidence regarding their validity and reliability, which has constrained their applicability across different contexts [28].

In contrast, regarding burnout, Maslach and Leiter developed the widely used Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) [29]. This instrument assesses three burnout dimensions: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal efficacy. From this perspective, Schaufeli et al. proposed the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES) [15], conceived as a positive counterpart to burnout. Instead of assessing emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal efficacy, the UWES measures vigor, dedication, and absorption, offering a more positive view of workplace well-being. This is the most recognized and widely used engagement tool to date [30, 31]. Considering the authors’ definition, which examines engagement through these three components (vigor, dedication, and absorption), the scale was created. However, like many self-report instruments, the UWES has psychometric limitations. While some studies support the original theoretical structure [2], there is controversy surrounding its construct validity [31–33]. Authors like Mills et al. [34] question the scale’s development, suggesting that the vigor dimension includes content related to perseverance. Nevertheless, despite the numerous proposals surrounding engagement, the approaches developed by Kahn and Schaufeli remain the most widely studied over the years, along with the self-report instruments derived from them [22, 33]. Approaches and measurement instruments for engagement

In addition to the noted limitations associated with specific measures of engagement, these measures also present those problems that are common to self-report instruments. Self-report measures have limitations related to the frequency of administration, as these instruments are usually administered annually and this does not provide information about the current reality of employees. Although abbreviated versions have been created to solve the problem; their contribution has not been as expected, since, it has reduced the interest in the instruments by participants and also increased the time and economic cost of their administration [35]. Credibility also affects the results, as responses are directed and biased and may not provide the whole truth about the item asked, due to social desirability [36]. Regarding representativeness, although these methods are quantitative and allow obtaining a direct score of the assessed construct, if the response rate of a self-report instrument is low, the result may not be representative of all participants [37]. Looking at the limitations associated with self-report instruments, such as insufficient frequency of application, questionable credibility, and lack of representativeness [35–37], there is a need to consider more flexible and reliable assessment alternatives for psychological constructs.

Analysis of networking technologies

Until now, many psychological constructs have been evaluated through closed response options, in the case of Likert-type scales, seeking their standardization through the application of psychometric techniques that allow identifying their validity and reliability, among other properties. These have been called “traditional numerical ratings” or “closed-ended questions,” and they require respondents to make judgments from a list of predetermined questions [38]. In order to obtain less biased and valuable information from self-report instruments, Speer et al. [39] within these measures includes open comment boxes, from which narratives of answers to open questions are obtained. The resulting narratives allow for rich and contextualized perspectives, which can produce ideas beyond the rigidity of traditional survey items [40]. In addition to open questions in surveys, attempts have also been made to obtain information through text from social networks, and other open media, such as blogs, forums, chat or email [39] where you can extract text content that employees write. By analyzing the content of the messages expressed through these media, relevant information can be obtained about how employees communicate with their colleagues and with the general public, what topics are important to them, how they feel about their work and their organization, and how they relate to customers and other stakeholders [41]. However, collecting and analyzing all this information is an expensive job that would take a lot of time and resources. In order to overcome these limitations of self-report instruments, and taking into account all the information that can be extracted from the text that employees write in open media, other techniques have emerged in recent years, such as artificial intelligence (AI) and/or text mining to obtain information about employees in their workplace [42–44].

The use of artificial intelligence in psychology

AI is a branch of computing that was born in the 1950s with the aim of emulating human intelligence through computing systems, that is, creating machines capable of performing complex cognitive tasks that usually would require human intelligence [45]. AI uses algorithms and models to allow machines to learn from data, make decisions, recognize patterns, and predict outcomes. A notable application field of AI is Natural Language Processing (NLP). NLP is a subdiscipline of AI and computational linguistics that focuses on how computers can effectively interact with human language [46]. Like AI, NLP was born in the 1950s, inspired by the idea of machine translation [47]. The goal of NLP is to design algorithms and models that allow machines to understand and generate text, answer questions, translate languages, extract information, and perform other complex linguistic tasks, promoting more fluid human-machine communication and the automation of tasks [48].

In relation to psychology, AI is partly inspired by the study of human cognition, and many AI models are based on psychological theories about how humans think and learn [49]. Cognitive psychology has contributed to the creation of machine learning (ML) models and the development of AI systems that attempt to simulate human cognitive processes, such as decision making, problem solving, emotion recognition, understanding human feelings, and even replication of the human mind [50]. In the case of NLP, language is a central phenomenon in psychology, directly related to communication through physiological indicators such as human language text [50]. Advances in psycholinguistics have been instrumental in the development of NLP models that can better analyze and understand text [51]. In addition, AI and NLP are used in psychology for automated text analysis, such as therapy transcripts to identify key themes, assess treatment efficacy, or analyze language patterns in people with psychological conditions [52].

Thanks to recent technological advances, researchers have been able to apply language-based ML models as alternatives to self-reports to assess constructs such as personality [53]. These studies have emphasized the diagnostic value of language, which has skyrocketed as computer tools for text analysis have become more accessible. In the last decade, more than 100 studies have linked language use to a wide range of psychological correlates [54]. Some early work found that word use was a stable individual difference with several modest but reliable correlations with self-reports of personality [55]. Many of these early findings have since been replicated in multiple studies [56, 57]. Several studies have used a similar approach (comparing word use with self-reports or behavioral assessments) and have yielded a body of evidence linking language to personality [58]. Furthermore, studies around personality have made it possible to evaluate its psychometric characteristics (reliability and validity) in various investigations [53, 59].

Use of artificial intelligence in organizational psychology

The use of language has also been gaining weight in the psychology of organizations, since organizational communication scholars have emphasized the value of content analysis based on the assumption that the content analysis of texts and discourses provides a replicable methodology to access deep individual or collective structures, such as values, intentions, work attitudes and cognitions [41]. That is, ensuring that content analysis is applicable to a wide range of organizational phenomena.

In recent years, there has been an increase in the application of AI and NLP to explore psychological constructs due to their ability to process massive volumes of data, discover complex patterns, facilitate the management and analysis of data from various sources, such as emails, survey results, performance appraisals, and corporate social networks [60, 61]. To analyze content extracted from free text, NLP algorithms have been developed to automatically score employee text content based on what they want to assess, e.g., job attitudes and perceptions [39]. This means that employee text can be automatically analyzed to identify patterns and trends in work attitudes. Thus, recent advances in NLP have opened the door to more effective assessment of narrative text. For example, emails and interactions on corporate social networks can be analyzed to identify signs of burnout [62] or assess job satisfaction [63]. Results from surveys and performance appraisals, on the other hand, can be used to predict turnover [64] or job satisfaction [65]. In addition, these methods allow assessing organizational culture or climate [8] by analyzing communication and interactions among employees.

However, in a research context, unlike with personality, there is a paucity of empirical research on psychometric evaluations of large linguistic models, for example, on the validity and reliability of the use of psychometric tests in AI [66] and ML tools in organizational contexts [67]. Although there is not yet much psychometric research around these techniques, they have already been used in several fields, including psychology, health, education, and work [68–70]. The use of AI and NLP for the assessment of these constructs has been based primarily on the latent semantic encoding model and Bidirectional Encoder Representations of Transformers (BERT). BERT is an NLP technique that has been shown to be effective in the assessment of psychological constructs [71]. BERT is based on deep learning and uses a neural network to process natural language [72]. Specifically, the use of BERT in the assessment of psychological constructs has included areas of clinical psychology [73], personality [74], and work, specifically burnout and turnover [75] and engagement [39].

Related work

One of the most recent studies on the use of ML models to analyze engagement was conducted by Núñez-Sánchez et al. [76] They applied a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) classifier to predict employee engagement levels based on 14 personal, job-related, and organizational variables. The authors identified six key factors as drivers of engagement, thereby demonstrating the potential of artificial neural networks to capture complex, non-linear relationships among variables. This highlights their usefulness as predictive diagnostic tools in organizational settings. However, there are still few studies using language models such as BERT to assess employee engagement, despite existing research consistently addressing the methodological limitations of self-report instruments and proposing natural language analysis, generated by employees themselves, as a more reliable and less biased alternative. The first study to propose this line of research was conducted by Tanaka et al. [77], whose objective was to predict engagement levels based on conversations in messaging platforms such as Slack or Microsoft Teams. They used a regression model to estimate engagement from the frequency and content of messages. Although they found a relationship between interaction frequency and engagement, they did not obtain conclusive results regarding message content, leading them to suggest the future use of models like BERT, known for their effectiveness in NLP tasks. Along similar lines, Speer et al. [39] applied NLP techniques and BERT to analyze open-ended responses in employee surveys. Their model was trained to identify linguistic patterns related to workplace attitudes such as job satisfaction and perceptions of leadership. The results showed that the model could accurately predict these attitudes solely through language analysis, also assessing the emotional tone (positive or negative) of the discourse. For their part, Liu et al. [78] developed a BERT-CNN model in an academic context to detect cognitive and emotional engagement in student forums within MOOCs. After manually labeling the discussions, they trained their model to classify them automatically, observing performance that outperformed other text classification models.

Despite the growing relevance of engagement in organizational settings, its assessment still relies almost exclusively on self-report instruments, which present significant methodological limitations: low frequency of application, social desirability bias, high time and resource costs, and ongoing concerns regarding their validity and reliability. Moreover, many of these instruments have been scarcely used and lack a solid empirical foundation to support their generalization.

In this context, we propose the study of Erudit AI, an AI- and NLP-based tool designed to classify levels of engagement through the analysis of text generated by employees within corporate digital platforms. This proposal aligns with an innovative approach aimed at overcoming the limitations of self-report measures by offering a more continuous and potentially bias-free assessment, through the analysis of spontaneous language produced in real workplace settings. We believe this tool represents a significant step forward toward more agile, automatable, and representative methods for assessing employees’ current state within their work environment.

Building on this premise, the present study aims to empirically validate the Erudit AI model by obtaining indicators of validity and reliability. Unlike previous studies that have been limited to general predictions of attitudes or emotions, this research introduces a rigorous methodological framework that directly compares the model’s performance with previously validated self-report instruments. The goal is not only to demonstrate the technical feasibility of using AI to assess engagement, but also to establish its psychometric soundness as a psychological assessment tool.

The general objective of this research is to describe and validate an AI model capable of classifying the level of engagement (high, medium, low) according to the cut-off points established by Schaufeli et al. [30] that an employee can present in their work environment taking into account the language content of the text they write in their corporate tools.

Specific objectives.

Analyze the convergent validity between self-report engagement measurement instruments and the Erudit AI SaaS.

Analyze the divergent validity between self-report burnout measurement instruments and the Erudit AI SaaS.

Analyze the reliability of the Erudit AI SaaS.

Methods

Materials

Participants

To calculate the minimum sample size, G*Power 3.1 was used. The configuration was based on the following parameters: an alpha level of 0.05, statistical power of 80%, and an expected effect size of approximately 0.50. The results indicated that a sample of 21 participants would be sufficient.

The sample consisted of 35 individuals currently employed at two companies in the technology sector. Recruitment was carried out in collaboration with the developer of the tool (Erudit AI), which provided access to both its internal staff and those of an external client company. Three inclusion criteria were established: (1) being actively employed at the time of the study, (2) being between 18 and 65 years old, and (3) actively using corporate tools compatible with the Erudit AI platform (Slack, Teams, Gmail).

The companies studied were ultimately two companies in the technology sector. The size of the workforces was 46 employees in one case and 26 in the other. At the beginning of the data collection, and in order to have a baseline level of the constructs assessed, the engagement scales were applied in addition to short-answer questions on sociodemographic variables (age, gender, position in the company, etc.). At this first stage, both engagement scales were answered by a total of 51 employees. Once the first measure had been established, in the following four weeks only the two scales were administered on the same form. The number of participations from the first measure was reduced (Table 2).

The gender distribution was very even (18 males and 17 females), the mean age of the subjects was 31.05 (SD = 6.14). All employees belonged to the technology sector with 14% in developer and sales positions, 17% in marketing positions, 22% in AI and product positions, 0.05% in human resources positions. The work mode was divided between hybrid (14.29%) and remote (85.71%).

Procedure

Data collection was carried out in phases, involving different teams and multiple steps. First, the HR team at Erudit AI sent an email to all employees, both from Erudit AI and the external company, informing them of the voluntary nature of the study and providing details on what would be assessed and for what purpose, along with the informed consent form approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Jaén (Ref. SEPT.22/5.PRY). Employees who agreed to participate signed the informed consent. The HR team received 71 signed forms and compiled a list of the email addresses of those willing to participate. This list was shared with the technical team at Erudit AI, which generated 71 anonymous alphanumeric codes. These codes were randomly assigned to each participant who had signed the consent and sent individually via email. The alphanumeric code met all security requirements (it was longer than 12 characters, included uppercase and lowercase letters as well as numbers, was generated randomly, and did not follow any recognizable pattern such as 001, 002, etc.).

Once all participants had received their codes, the HR team at Erudit AI sent them a link containing four sections: The first with sociodemographic variables (age, sex, position held in the company, etc.) and the next two containing engagement self-report instruments. To complete the sections, participants were required to enter their alphanumeric code. These responses, accessible only to the HR team, were automatically collected through Google Forms, without recording email addresses, using only the corresponding alphanumeric code. Finally, the Technical Team at Erudit AI sent the HR team each of the engagement scores generated by the Erudit AI SaaS platform, matched to the assigned alphanumeric codes, for subsequent conversion and comparison with the self-report responses.

Missing data treatment

To preserve data quality, different procedures were applied depending on the type of instrument. For the self-report instruments (UWES-9, JRA, MBI-HSS), it was mandatory to answer all items in order to submit the questionnaire. Therefore, no missing data were present in the collected samples, as incomplete responses were not recorded in Google Forms. Regarding the texts analyzed by Erudit AI, records lacking sufficient linguistic content for semantic analysis (e.g., empty responses or messages unrelated to the work context) were excluded. Additionally, a neutrality filter was applied prior to engagement classification, discarding texts that were not relevant to the construct.

Instruments

Utrecht work engagement scale

(UWES-9) [30]. It consists of nine items and includes three subscales: three items assess vigor, three items assess dedication, and three items assess absorption. It is a Likert-type scale with a seven-point frequency scale ranging from 0 (“never”) to 6 (“always”). In a sample of employees, internal consistency values were adequate for items in the three subscales (alpha values of 0.79, 0.89 and 0.72 for vigor, dedication and absorption, respectively; [30]). The UWES-9 instrument has shown high levels of internal consistency (α > 0.80), confirmatory factorial validity, and robustness across samples from various countries and sectors. This makes it a methodological standard for comparing new tools designed to measure engagement. Its abbreviated version (9 items) allows for more efficient administration without sacrificing psychometric properties, making it particularly suitable for studies in workplace settings [30, 31].

Employee engagement [23]

It consists of six items, with two items each for the cognitive, emotional and behavioral dimensions of engagement. The items are rated on a five-point Likert scale, from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”), with “neutral” as the midpoint and a “don’t know” option, which are coded as missing values. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.85. The tripartite structure of the JRA assesses cognitive, emotional, and behavioral aspects of engagement, offering a complementary perspective to that provided by the UWES-9 [24]. Thus, the combined use of the UWES-9 and the JRA allows for the capture of both affective states related to performance and the core psychological components involved in the expression of the work role, providing a more comprehensive and robust understanding of the construct.

The scales used (UWES-9 and JRA) do not include a specific time period in their items; they evaluate engagement in terms of frequency or general agreement, thus reflecting the usual level perceived by each participant.

Maslach burnout inventory – Human services survey

(MBI-HSS) [29]. It consists of 22 items distributed in three key subdimensions: depersonalization where five items are included, personal accomplishment where eight items are included and emotional exhaustion where nine items are included. The items are rated on a seven-point Likert-type scale, from 0 (“strongly disagree”) to 6 (“strongly agree”). Cronbach’s alpha is 0.80.

Erudit AI SaaS

This is an AI software as a service that uses a BERT language model to transform the text written by employees of other client companies into an engagement score from 0 to 100. To understand how this process is carried out, the following sections explain in detail how this tool is built. In order to build a reliably labeled and psychometrically valid dataset to train the RoBERTa engagement classification model, a structured manual annotation protocol was implemented.

Text selection and pre-filtering. The texts used for labeling were extracted from real corporate tools (Slack, Gmail, Microsoft Teams) via the Erudit AI SaaS platform, ensuring ecologically valid and contextually relevant conditions. Before labeling, each text underwent an automated neutrality filter designed to exclude messages that did not provide useful information for engagement analysis (e.g., generic messages, greetings, automated templates, or socially-oriented phrases unrelated to work). This filter was developed based on a specific neutrality guideline. Only the texts that passed this filter were forwarded for manual annotation.

Annotation guidelines. To perform the probabilistic distribution of engagement, in-depth research on the construct was conducted by reading the most recognized handbooks in the world of engagement [79, 80], meta-analyses [81, 82] and systematic or critical reviews [82, 83]. We then proceeded to compile the instruments used for assessment [15, 23]. Taking into account the different conceptual approaches to engagement reviewed in the introduction, it was decided to choose the instruments related to the most relevant approaches. For Kahn’s perspective or approach [11], the JRA Employee Engagement Scale [23] was chosen, and for Schaufeli et al.‘s [15] proposal, the UWES-9 was used. Before proceeding with text annotation in the tool, annotators were required to review the annotation guidelines, although they were allowed to keep them on hand for reference during the task. The annotation manual was based on the definition of engagement drawn from the two predominant theoretical approaches in the literature [11, 15]. Each engagement category was classified as high, medium, or low according to the following definitions: low: expressions of disinterest, fatigue, lack of enthusiasm, rejection, or criticism toward the task or organization; medium: neutral or ambiguous content, without clear signs of positive or negative engagement; high: expressions of enthusiasm, energy, enjoyment of work, offering help, or commitment to the task or team. In addition, consensus-based definitions were established for each category, along with real annotated examples justified at each level, inclusion and exclusion criteria (for example, congratulating a colleague on their birthday was considered a neutral act), and documentation of special or ambiguous cases, such as irony, emojis, or culturally specific expressions. These guidelines were iteratively reviewed, and all decisions made during disagreement resolution were documented to inform future versions. Methods

Data collection and preparation

Once all the documentation of the guides was completed, taking into account the classification described above, the texts were labeled. However, prior to the labeling of engagement data, a neutrality screening is performed. All texts that are chosen for subsequent labeling are passed through a filter considered to be neutral, which has been created with a guide similar to that of engagement, but in this case detecting which texts may be non-neutral. Those texts that are not valid for the metric are discarded and, on the contrary, those that are related to the engagement metric are subsequently tagged.

The first dataset where the data that have passed the neutrality filter are labeled is called “Quality Cycle (QC)”. Erudit AI has datasets on psychological constructs, including engagement, of more than 5000 examples from real data (data from companies that are currently using the Erudit AI SaaS). The labeled dataset is included in a previously trained model (in our case RoBERTa, robustly optimized BERT pre-training approach, which will be explained later), and a fit of the data with the model is performed. To check whether the tool works or not, a dataset called “Validation set” is created with real data that the model has not previously analyzed. The aim is to check how accurately the model classifies engagement.

Labeling process

Once the neutrality filter was passed, 450 texts were included in the CAT tool. Each annotator accessed the tool using their own account. The dataset with all the texts appeared there. Each text had three options to be selected (high, medium, and low). Once one of the three options was marked, the system moved on to the next text. This tool does not allow users to see the annotations made by the other person. Once the annotators completed the task independently and asynchronously, the tool itself returned the agreement percentage for the entire dataset.

Interjudge reliability (Cohen’s Kappa)

To determine inter-rater reliability, Cohen’s kappa coefficient [84] is used. If the datasets show κ ≥ 0.80, the dataset is ready to be analyzed by the RoBERTa model. If the overall dataset score is below κ 0.80, a consensus meeting between annotators must be held. During the meeting, all discrepancies must be addressed, always with the annotation guidelines present. All conflicting cases were reviewed in joint meetings, where each one was discussed until a consensus decision was reached. The decisions made in these sessions were recorded in the CAT tool, and κ was recalculated until it exceeded 0.80. Finally, everything agreed upon during the consensus meeting was integrated as new example cases in the updated version of the manual, thus feeding back into the process.

The architecture of Erudit’s classification model

This section describes the architecture of the Erudit AI model for the multiclass engagement classification task. For this purpose, a classifier based on RoBERTa is proposed. This is a variant of the BERT model that has been trained on a large text corpus to perform natural language tasks.

BERT

It is a pre-trained natural language model based on the Transformer architecture. The idea behind BERT is to train a natural language model on a large amount of unlabeled data to learn a general language representation that can be adapted to specific tasks, such as text classification or text generation. BERT uses a bidirectional pre-training approach, meaning that the model learns to predict missing words on both the left and right sides of a given word in a sentence. This allows the model to have a more complete understanding of the context in which a particular word is used. In addition, BERT uses a technique called attention mask to predict missing words in a sentence. During training, some words in a sentence are randomly hidden and the model must predict the missing words from the context of the sentence. This technique helps the model to learn and understand the structure and meaning of language at a deeper level.

In summary, BERT is a pre-trained natural language model based on the Transformer architecture, which uses a bidirectional pre-training approach and an attention mask technique to learn a general language representation that can be adapted to specific NLP tasks.

RoBERTa

RoBERTa is a pre-trained natural language model that is based on the Transformer architecture, and trained using a large amount of unlabeled text data. The training process consists of two main stages: pre-training and finetuning.

In the pre-training stage, RoBERTa is trained using a “fill-in-the-blank” task, in which a word is hidden in a sentence and the model must predict the missing word from the context. This is known as “attention mask pre-training” and allows the model to learn to understand the structure and meaning of language. In the second stage, the model is finetuned (finetuning) for a specific task, in this case, the task is to classify the level of engagement of the texts. Finetuning consists of adjusting the weights and biases of the previously trained model to fit the specific task to be performed. During this process, the model adjusts its weights to suit this task and produces more accurate and useful results.

Therefore, for finetuning, the dataset of labeled texts with their corresponding level of engagement must be input. This dataset is used as input for the model and will be provided with feedback in terms of the accuracy of its predictions. From this feedback, the model will adjust its weights and biases to produce better predictions.

The finetuning process is performed in several epochs, during which the model processes the entire dataset several times and adjusts its weights and biases based on the feedback obtained. After a given number of epochs, the model stops and is considered to have been finetuned. Once the model has been finetuned, it is used to classify the engagement level of new texts that have not been analyzed before.

In this specific fine-tuning process, the RoBERTa model was configured as follows. The pretrained roberta-base version was used, implemented with the HuggingFace Transformers library in PyTorch. During fine-tuning, the model was trained for 4 epochs with a learning rate of 2e-5, a batch size of 16, and a maximum sequence length of 128 tokens. AdamW was used as the optimizer, and CrossEntropyLoss as the loss function. 20% of the dataset was reserved for validation, and early stopping was applied to prevent overfitting. Texts were tokenized using RobertaTokenizer and training was performed on a GPU.

Transformation of text into scores

The texts that the model has not analyzed before are real employee data from Erudit AI’s client companies. In order to obtain this data, the Erudit AI SaaS is installed in the corporate tools of the client companies. The engagement results can be viewed in a private and personal dashboard for each company. For this information to appear on the dashboard, the client companies connect their corporate tools (Slack, Teams, Gmail, etc…) to Erudit AI SaaS. The texts they write in these tools are collected by this SaaS. These texts, completely new to the model are analyzed and transformed by the RoBERTa model through a process called “encoding” in order to convert the text into a numerical representation which is then used by a neural network (this is one of its output layers specialized in classification).

Once the text is encoded, the numerical score is obtained, and then the neural network, which only processes numerical data, is in charge of classifying that score depending on the percentage it obtains of belonging to low, medium or high levels of engagement. This process is repeated with all the messages and finally an average is made with all the messages and their probability of belonging to one of the three levels. After performing these calculations, the model returns the output with an engagement score that users can view on the Erudit AI SaaS dashboard as shown in Fig. 1.

Data analysis

The analyses were performed using IBM SPPS v. 24.0 statistical software for Windows. Due to the small sample size (< 50), the Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check the fit of the data to a normal distribution. Given that the data conformed to normality, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated to analyze the relationships between the different forms of measurement. To analyze construct validity, we used the “multitrait-multimethod method”, since we used different forms of engagement measurement that are not based only on self-report measures. To determine the convergent validity of the Erudit AI SaaS, as explained above, bivariate correlation analyses were carried out. Discriminant validity is determined by analyzing the bivariate correlations between the assessments with the self-report measures of burnout and the Erudit AI SaaS over a 30-day period of difference (Initial measure vs. Week 3). Finally, in the case of reliability, a test-retest was performed, comparing by means of Jamovi’s reliability analysis the ICC, the results of the initial Erudit measure and the first week of all subjects.

Analyzing the convergent validity between self-report engagement measurement instruments and the Erudit AI SaaS

The results of the bivariate correlations between the UWES-9 instrument and the Erudit AI SaaS revealed consistent patterns of association between the scores of the two instruments across all weeks. In the analysis of convergent validity, most of the correlation coefficients show large effect sizes according to Cohen’s [84] criteria. Specifically, 13 out of the 15 significant correlations between the self-report measures (UWES-9 and JRA) and the Erudit AI tool yielded r values equal to or greater than 0.50. Moreover, the 95% confidence intervals for all significant correlations did not cross the null value (r = 0). Only in two specific cases did the confidence intervals include the null value: the correlation between JRA and Erudit in Week 1 (r = .285, 95% CI [–0.053, 0.565]) and, to a lesser extent, the correlation between UWES-9 and Erudit in Week 1 (r = .405, 95% CI [0.084, 0.651]), whose lower bound approaches zero.

The results of the bivariate correlations between the total score with the JRA instrument and that obtained with the Erudit AI SaaS revealed consistent patterns of association between the scores of the two instruments in four of the five weeks evaluated. In particular, positive and statistically significant correlation coefficients were observed between JRA and Erudit AI SaaS, with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.451 − 0.710. At week 1, no significant correlation was found between the two instruments (r = .285, p = .097).

These correlations indicate a statistically significant and positive relationship between the scores of the two instruments, providing preliminary evidence consistent with the convergent validity of the proposed instrument in relation to the already validated instruments. Overall, these associations support the idea that the instrument assesses constructs similar to those measured by the related measures.

Discussion

Since its inception, engagement has been defined and evaluated from two different perspectives. On the one hand, those perspectives related to the proposal of Kahn [11] and its later variants focused on defining the concept as something physical, emotional and cognitive and; on the other hand, those related to the proposal of Schaufeli et al. [15] considering engagement to be composed of three components: vigor, dedication, and absorption and describe it as a positive manifestation of workplace well-being. Both streams of research have had a great impact, although the scales that have been derived from each of them for the assessment of the construct have been very different, since the one created by Schaufeli et al. [15] called UWES and its subsequent versions such as the one used in this study (UWES-9) have been widely used, translated into more than 60 languages and validated in 17 countries [86], than those related to Kahn’s proposal. In any case, none of the instruments has been free of controversy, raising criticisms about them and generating new versions [27].

In order to address some of the issues associated with these types of instruments, alternative assessment methods related to the collection of free and unstructured text have been proposed recently. All that has been proposed so far have been methods related to text mining [37, 87], AI, NLP or BERT [69, 77]. However, although some studies have attempted to validate these methods [59] psychometrically as in psychology, so far, no such method has been found to be psychometrically validated in the same way as self-report instruments to assess engagement. For this reason, Erudit AI proposes an AI tool that aims to classify the level of engagement presented by employees only using the text they write in their corporate tools, by integrating a SaaS in these tools. In order to verify that this SaaS based on an AI model, classifies engagement adequately, as well as the self-report instruments, an analysis of the properties of the tool is carried out based on the establishment of convergent, divergent and reliability validity.

Given that engagement has been evaluated from the two currents reviewed, it was decided to create an AI model based on both currents, in order to check whether the instrument classified engagement as a construct more related to vigor, dedication, and absorption and as the antithesis of burnout or rather related to physical, emotional and cognitive aspects as indicated by Kahn [11], or whether, on the contrary, having taken into account both currents in the creation of the guidelines, the tool measures engagement globally, without taking into account the distinction between the two. The results suggest that the scores generated by the Erudit AI tool show a degree of alignment with the theoretical dimensions of both frameworks, as evidenced by the positive correlations observed during the five-week evaluation against the UWES-9 and JRA instruments. In the case of the UWES-9 instrument, in addition to positive results during all weeks, it also presents statistically significant results. However, the JRA tool, in spite of presenting positive results, does not present significant results in all weeks. This leads us to believe that the Erudit AI tool has a tendency to classify texts more reliably following the proposal of Schaufeli et al. [15]. Moreover, this could also be affirmed considering that this proposal qualifies engagement as the antithesis of burnout, and this is confirmed by the results obtained with the MBI-HSS instrument [29], since these are negative and statistically significant when evaluated in both weeks with 30 days of difference between them.

In the case of the JRA scale and the Erudit AI instrument, although the relationships are also positive and significant, it is not so in all weeks of evaluation. This may be due to the fact that in the construction of the Erudit AI tool, texts were labeled with a greater relationship with the three dimensions of engagement proposed by Schaufeli et al. [15] than with the complete JRA scale. Another reason may have been that since the UWES-9 has 9 items and the JRA only 6, the labeled texts were mostly from the stream from which the UWES-9 is derived, and therefore, since more data were labeled in this way, the coefficients are higher with this scale. However, the lack of significance in one week could suggest that there are differences or fluctuations in the relationship between the instruments in that particular period, which could be the result of temporal variability, individual characteristics of the participants, or other factors not identified in the present study. Therefore, we should raise the need to consider the stability of the relationship between the instruments over time and conduct further analyses to understand the possible reasons behind this exception.

As for divergent validity, also assessed by correlation analysis, but in this case between the Erudit AI tool and the total score of the MBI-HSS instrument, the correlations were negative and significant. Also, the fact that there is consistency of negative and significant correlations over a 30-day period might suggest that the Erudit IA instrument maintains its ability to classify engagement and distinguish it clearly from burnout at different times. This is in line with the study by Schaufeli et al. [30] who state that like burnout, engagement is a chronic and not a transient state.

In the case of reliability, the results indicate that there is moderate consistency over time in the within-subject measurements, which means that the patterns are consistent regarding the instrument’s reliability. However, even though they are moderate, the scores do not reach a good level. This could be due to the fact that the sample consists of only 35 subjects. Another explanation could be related to how the Erudit AI instrument is constructed. Although we have found convergent validity in both instruments, it is more significant with the UWES-9 instrument than with the JRA instrument, which leads us to believe that the tool better captures engagement in terms of vigor, dedication, and absorption. This could explain why the reliability is lower in the Erudit AI instrument, as UWES-9 (α = 0.77 – 0.85) has lower reliability compared to JRA (α = 0.85) [24]. Another possible explanation is that engagement is being measured more as a state than as a trait, and therefore, its daily variability in engagement levels varies substantially. As Bakker [88] indicates, engagement fluctuates daily based on changes in work and personal resources [88]. Taking this into account, it would not make sense to compare baseline scores with weeks spaced further apart in time (such as weeks 2, 3, and 4), as engagement varies depending on factors at work, such as the demands employees face and the resources available to them [89].

In terms of psychometric properties, there is scarce empirical research investigating the reliability and validity of AI and ML tools in organizational contexts [67]. This is the first study that evaluates the psychometric characteristics of an AI model to classify engagement. These preliminary results suggest that the Erudit AI SaaS model may show promising potential for classifying engagement in a manner comparable to traditional self-report instruments. However, these findings must be interpreted within the constraints of a small, non-random sample, which substantially limits the external validity of the results. Therefore, replication with larger and more heterogeneous samples is needed to confirm its external validity. Given the limited sample size and the exploratory nature of the study, the findings should be interpreted with caution. While the results support the tool’s convergent and divergent validity, it cannot be conclusively stated that the model is equivalent to traditional instruments without more extensive validation.

Although based on these results this tool could represent a significant advancement over the limitations of traditional self-report measures, the use of AI to assess employee engagement presents important ethical and organizational challenges that must be carefully considered prior to implementation. First, the capture and analysis of employees’ spontaneous communications, even when done anonymously, raises questions about the authenticity of the consent given and the extent to which workers truly understand the implications of continuous monitoring [90]. Moreover, the contextual nature of language poses a challenge for AI models: ironic, emotional, or culturally loaded phrases can be misinterpreted, affecting the validity of inferences about a person’s emotional state or level of engagement [91]. Another critical risk is the potential for bias in the training data or within the model itself, which could perpetuate inequalities or mislabel certain groups based on their communication style, educational background, or language proficiency [92]. Finally, there is a risk that organizations may use these tools not as mechanisms to improve employee well-being, but rather as instruments of control or surveillance, which could undermine trust and increase employee disengagement [93]. These challenges highlight the need for clear regulation, ongoing ethical oversight, and a deep reflection on the purposes and limits of AI use in organizational settings issues that could be addressed in future studies.

In addition to addressing the ethical implications, studies based on these models could also be conducted to assess other psychological constructs; for example, within the workplace, new models could be developed to classify levels of burnout. Considering that this is a concept closely related to engagement, it could contribute to obtain clearer evidence on the validity of the engagement instrument, since it would allow validating Erudit’s engagement model with another similar AI model of burnout. It could be investigated whether this tool could also be used as a predictor of other psychological constructs. Knowing the level of employee engagement could anticipate the levels of other psychological constructs such as performance or turnover rate. Knowing this data in advance would allow companies to save costs.

Another possible research avenue would be to relate engagement to other constructs not directly related to psychology but rather to the specific work context. For instance, AI models could be created to establish the levels of dimensions such as employee recognition, workplace satisfaction, workload, etc. This way, high or low levels of engagement could be quantitatively explained, and conversely, these levels could predict engagement levels before they are reflected in the SaaS.

In addition to considering the BERT language model and its versions that we have worked with in this article, it would also be interesting to create the same AI tool using other AI and language models, such as LLM (Large Language Model), to compare their effectiveness and determine which model performs better in classifying engagement levels.

Limitations

Although the present study provides preliminary evidence on the feasibility of using AI to assess engagement through natural language, it must be explicitly acknowledged that the small sample size (N = 35) represents a significant limitation in terms of external validity. Given that the sample was small and non-random, the results cannot be confidently generalized to other populations, sectors, or organizational contexts. While this type of limitation is common in exploratory and initial validation studies, it should be interpreted with caution, particularly when considering the applicability of the tool in broader or more diverse settings. For future research, it may be worth considering the development of ML models explicitly based on latent structures that reflect the theoretical dimensions of engagement. This approach would allow models (such as RoBERTa) to be trained to directly estimate latent dimensions of engagement previously defined through factor analysis, enabling the rigorous application of tests such as AVE (Average Variance Extracted), HTMT (Heterotrait-Monotrait Ratio), and latent-level analyses. This integration between ML and psychometrics would support validation not only in terms of model accuracy, but also in terms of theoretical consistency and the ability to discriminate between related constructs.

In addition, the inclusion of employees from the company that developed the instrument (Erudit AI) may have introduced several potential sources of bias. Participants may have had greater familiarity with the tool, higher motivation to support the project, or increased awareness of the study objectives, which could have influenced both their self-report responses and their communication behavior. Although anonymity procedures were implemented, this contextual factor should be considered when interpreting the strength of the observed associations. Future research should prioritize fully independent samples to minimize potential expectancy or allegiance effects.

On the other hand, the datasets used in the study contain texts that may be influenced by biases related to ideology, race, age, gender, and other factors. These biases can introduce distortions into the results of the analysis, affecting the objectivity and validity of the conclusions drawn from the textual data. Although quality and coding criteria were applied, linguistic data may reflect cultural, ideological, or communicative patterns linked to variables such as age, gender, educational level, or role within the organization. This presents a significant risk, as the model could learn to associate certain expression styles or linguistic structures with specific levels of engagement, systematically penalizing employees who do not conform to those patterns. As a result, the system could reproduce existing inequalities rather than provide a neutral assessment, affecting both the model’s validity and the fairness of its practical applications. If undetected and uncorrected, this type of bias compromises the objectivity of the analysis and limits the generalizability of the results to more diverse work environments.

Given that AI technology is evolving rapidly, the models used in the study could become obsolete in a short time. In particular, advances in NLP, such as the development of larger and more context-sensitive models (e.g., GPT, LLaMA, or future versions of BERT-based architectures) could significantly outperform current tools like RoBERTa in interpreting nuanced or context-dependent language. While these improvements may lead to more accurate engagement classifications, they also pose a challenge for the generalization and reproducibility of the findings in this study. If future models are trained on different datasets, use different tokenization strategies, or produce updated semantic representations, the same texts could yield different engagement scores. This would hinder the comparability of results over time and across systems, compromising the stability and standardization of AI-based psychological assessment methods.

The study has moderate reliability, so it would be important to continue working on improving and modifying the instrument in order to achieve higher reliability in future studies.

Finally, if the study uses specific AI models, it’s important to consider that the performance and applicability of these models may vary depending on the context and the specific task for which they were trained. The results may not be extrapolatable to other domains or tasks.

Conclusion

This study provides preliminary evidence on the feasibility of using AI models, specifically Erudit AI SaaS, as a tool showing preliminary evidence of construct validity for estimating work engagement through natural language analysis. Among its main contributions is evidence consistent with convergent and divergent validity in relation to established psychometric instruments (UWES-9 and MBI-HSS), as well as moderate reliability in engagement evaluation. The study also highlights the potential of this technology to deliver more frequent, automated, and less biased measurements than traditional self-report instruments, representing a potentially innovative approach within organizational research contexts.

From an applied perspective, the study’s findings may have significant implications for human resources professionals and organizational experts. The preliminary evidence regarding Erudit AI SaaS suggests its potential as a complementary tool for monitoring engagement through automated language analysis, offering a more agile and continuous addition to traditional self-report methods. This may contribute to new ways in which organizations explore the emotional and motivational state of their employees, allowing for earlier detection of signs of disengagement or burnout, and facilitating the proactive implementation of intervention strategies. Furthermore, by integrating with digital platforms already used in the workplace (such as Slack or Gmail), this technology can operate in the background without disrupting daily work routines, significantly reducing administrative burden and improving operational efficiency. For HR professionals, this could support more informed decision-making processes pending further validation regarding well-being, organizational climate, or talent management based on dynamic and contextual data. However, its adoption must be accompanied by a critical understanding of its limitations and the establishment of ethical and technical frameworks that ensure fair, transparent, and human-centered use.

However, although the results provide preliminary support for convergent and divergent validity of Erudit AI SaaS and suggest promising potential for its use in assessing work engagement, it is important to emphasize that this remains preliminary evidence. The moderate reliability and small sample size limit the generalizability of the findings and highlight the need to replicate the study with larger and more diverse populations. Additionally, the use of AI models to interpret natural language for psychological purposes presents significant ethical, technical, and contextual challenges, such as the risk of data bias, misinterpretation of nuanced language, or potential loss of employee trust if these tools are perceived as mechanisms of control. Therefore, while Erudit AI SaaS represents an innovative step toward more agile and continuous evaluation systems, its implementation in organizational contexts must be accompanied by a critical analysis of its limitations, as well as the establishment of solid ethical and theoretical frameworks.
