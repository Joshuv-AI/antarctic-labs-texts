Impact of Neutropenia on Clinical Outcomes in Critically Ill Patients with Acute Respiratory Failure
Authors: Hwan Il Kim, Seong Mi Moon, Hyo Seok Oh, Ryoung-Eun Ko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionNeutropenia is a common clinical condition in critically ill patients and may be associated with poor outcomes, particularly in the context of acute respiratory failure (ARF). However, evidence on its prognostic impact remains inconsistent, and there are a lack of data, particularly in noncancer and immunocompetent populations. This study evaluates the association between neutropenia and mortality in critically ill patients with ARF and identifies risk factors for mortality among patients with neutropenia.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, 2412 adult patients with ARF admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) of a tertiary center between January 2019 and December 2023 were analyzed. Neutropenia was defined as an absolute neutrophil count < 1.5 × 10⁹/L at ICU admission. The primary outcome was hospital mortality; secondary outcomes included ICU mortality. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the association between neutropenia and outcomes. Subgroup analyses and risk factor assessments were conducted among patients with neutropenia.ResultsAmong the 2412 patients, 411 (17.0%) had neutropenia at ICU admission. Compared with their counterparts without neutropenia, patients with neutropenia had higher ICU mortality (48.0% versus 18.9%, P < 0.001) and hospital mortality (60.1% versus 28.3%, P = 0.007). Neutropenia remained independently associated with increased ICU (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] 1.48; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.20–1.83) and hospital mortality (adjusted HR 1.27; 95% CI 1.07–1.52). The association was more pronounced in patients without cancer (adjusted HR 3.08) than in patients with cancer (adjusted HR 1.48; P for interaction < 0.001). Among patients with neutropenia, sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score was an independent predictor of hospital mortality (adjusted HR 1.15; 95% CI 1.11–1.20; P < 0.001).ConclusionsNeutropenia at ICU admission is independently associated with increased mortality in patients with ARF, particularly among those without cancer. SOFA score is a strong prognostic indicator among patients with neutropenia. These findings highlight the need for improved risk stratification, and suggest that patients with neutropenia may benefit from specific management strategies, which should be investigated in future studies.

Introduction

Neutrophils are the predominant type of granulocyte, accounting for approximately 40–70% of circulating white blood cells, and serve as frontline components of innate immunity [1]. They exert chemotactic, phagocytic, and bactericidal functions; migrate across the vascular endothelium into tissues; and play key roles in pathogen clearance and the regulation of inflammation [2, 3]. Through phagocytosis, neutrophils eliminate invading microorganisms and remove cellular debris, including apoptotic cells and remnants of red blood cells [4, 5].

Neutropenia, defined as an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) < 1.5 × 10⁹ cells/L [6–9], is not uncommon in the general population [10] and has been found in 8.6% of patients with sepsis and 28% of hematological patients admitted to intensive care units (ICUs) [6]. It may arise from diverse pathophysiological mechanisms, including sepsis-induced bone marrow suppression, drug toxicity, autoimmune diseases, hematologic malignancies, immune dysregulation, or bone marrow failure [7, 9, 11–14]. While neutropenia can be incidentally detected [11], it has important clinical implications, particularly in the ICU setting.

Previous studies regarding leukopenia suggested poor clinical outcomes in pneumonia, sepsis, and pulmonary embolism [15–17]. In particular, the diagnosis of systemic inflammatory response syndrome, including leukopenia—typically defined leukocyte count < 4000/µL—is associated with increased risk of mortality in critically ill patients [15]. Although leukopenia has been suggested as a marker of organ dysfunctions, the relevance of low neutrophil count was not evaluated sufficiently in these studies.

In terms of neutropenia, depletion of neutrophils compromises host defenses and is closely associated with increased susceptibility to severe infections, such as pneumonia and sepsis, which are major causes of acute respiratory failure (ARF) in critically ill patients [18–21]. In neutropenic states, the inflammatory response may be diminished or atypical, potentially delaying recognition and treatment and contributing to adverse clinical outcomes [7]. Given that ARF has multifactorial causes, ranging from infection and systemic inflammation to medication-related toxicity and comorbid conditions [22], neutropenia may interact with these processes and worsen prognosis [13, 23].

Despite the clinical significance of neutropenia in critically ill patients, the relationship between neutropenia and hospital mortality is subject to debate [13, 24–28]. While several studies have reported that neutropenia at ICU admission was not associated with mortality among immunocompromised patients [13, 24, 27], others found that neutropenia was an independent predictor of mortality [25, 26, 28]. Prior research has largely focused on patients with cancer, particularly those with chemotherapy-induced neutropenic fever, or recipients of hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation. Other studies have restricted their cohorts to patients with severe neutropenia (ANC < 0.5 × 10⁹ cells/L) [29], and overlooked mild-to-moderate neutropenia, which is more commonly observed in real-world ICU practice. As a result, the relevance of these findings to broader, immunocompetent critically ill populations remains limited.

The present study aimed to evaluate the impact of neutropenia (ANC < 1.5 × 10⁹ cells/L) on hospital mortality in critically ill patients with ARF. We also sought to identify clinical factors associated with mortality among patients with neutropenia.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This retrospective cohort study included 3990 adult patients (≥ 18 years) with ARF who were admitted to a medical or cardiac ICU at a tertiary referral center in South Korea between January 2019 and December 2023. All consecutive adult ICU admissions requiring respiratory support were considered. Patients were eligible if they received invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) through either an endotracheal tube or a tracheostomy for more than 12 h, regardless of whether they fulfilled specific ARF criteria, as prolonged IMV itself indicates clinically significant respiratory failure. In addition, patients who required advanced respiratory support without IMV—including high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) or noninvasive ventilation using bilevel positive airway pressure or continuous positive airway pressure with an oronasal or facial mask—were included if they demonstrated ARF, as characterized by either a partial pressure of arterial oxygen (PaO2)/fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) ratio below 300 or a peripheral capillary oxygen saturation (SpO2)/FiO2 ratio below 315 for more than 1 h [30, 31]. All eligible patients must have received a respiratory support intervention within 3 days of ICU admission. An ANC was required at the time of ICU admission for inclusion in the study.

Patients were excluded if they had a documented do not resuscitate (DNR) order before or during ICU care (n = 415), were admitted postoperatively (n = 211), or presented with conditions such as simple congestive heart failure (n = 342), cardiogenic shock (n = 308), or cardiac arrest (n = 302), to minimize heterogeneity related to cardiogenic causes of respiratory failure. A total of 2412 patients with ARF were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Patients with ARF of cardiovascular origin were excluded because their condition is predominantly due to pulmonary edema, which is often rapidly reversible with volume management. Management strategies for these patients—including interventions related to ischemic heart disease—differ substantially from those for sepsis or pneumonia, which could confound the assessment of the independent effect of neutropenia on mortality.Fig. 1Flowchart of the study population. Flowchart of the study population. ICU intensive care unit

Neutropenia was defined as an ANC < 1500/μL, in accordance with definition from an expert panel of the French Intensive Care Society and related professional societies [6, 9]. Although the risk of infection increases with more profound neutropenia (ANC < 1.0 × 10⁹ cells/L or < 0.5 × 10⁹ cells/L), we adopted the broader consensus definition to preserve statistical power in subgroup analyses. Patients were categorized into two groups on the basis of the presence (n = 411) or absence (n = 2001) of neutropenia at ICU admission (Fig. 1).

Data Collection and Covariates

Data were extracted from the institutional clinical data warehouse, DARWIN-C, including clinical and laboratory variables. Baseline data encompassed age, sex, body mass index, comorbidities, and the cause of respiratory failure. Sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) scores were recorded on ICU admission. Gender identity was not collected. In accordance with the Sex and Gender Equity in Research (SAGER) guidelines, descriptive characteristics were disaggregated by sex. Laboratory data were collected on day 0. Comorbidities assessed included chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), heart failure, ischemic heart disease, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, stroke, osteoporosis, and malignancies (hematologic and solid tumors).

Data on early ICU management within the first 3 days included corticosteroid use, respiratory support modalities, the application of continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) and venovenous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO), vasoactive agents, and daily fluid balance.

Study Outcomes

The primary outcome was hospital mortality. The secondary outcome was ICU mortality. Outcomes were followed from the time of ICU admission until hospital death or discharge, or until 30 June 2024, whichever occurred first.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and analyzed using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test.

Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to compare hospital survival rates between two patient groups, with the time to event defined as the interval from ICU admission to in-hospital death. The log-rank test was used to assess statistical significance.

Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for hospital mortality risk in patients with neutropenia compared with those without neutropenia. Multivariable analysis was adjusted for age, reason for acute respiratory failure, SOFA score, hematologic or oncologic malignancies, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, stroke, chronic kidney disease, heart failure, and COPD.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the consistency of the association between neutropenia and hospital mortality across the strata of SOFA scores, respiratory support modality, corticosteroid use, and the presence of malignancy. In patients with neutropenia, univariable and multivariable Cox models were used to identify risk factors for hospital mortality.

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with a two-sided P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethical Approval and Consent to Participate

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review board of Samsung Medical Center (IRB no. 2023–08-003). The requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Among the 2412 patients with ARF admitted to the ICU and included in this study, 411 (17.0%) had neutropenia at the time of ICU admission. Patients with neutropenia were younger (64.0 [IQR, 53.0–71.0] versus 66.0 [IQR, 55.0–75.0] years, P < 0.001] and presented more severe illness on admission, as indicated by higher SOFA scores (10.0 [IQR, 7.0–13.0] versus 6.0 [IQR, 4.0–9.0], P < 0.001]. Overall, 62.5% were male; sex distribution was similar between patients with and without neutropenia (male 62.3% versus 62.5%; P = 0.974).

The most common cause of ARF in patients with neutropenia was related to sepsis (56.4%), while pulmonary causes (44.1%) were predominant in patients without neutropenia. Comorbidities such as COPD, heart failure, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and stroke were more common in patients without neutropenia (all P < 0.05), whereas diabetes mellitus and hematologic/oncologic malignancies were more prevalent among patients with neutropenia (all P < 0.001).

Patients with neutropenia also exhibited greater systemic inflammation and organ dysfunction, as measured by elevated levels of C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, international normalized ratio, Clinical Outcomes

Over a median follow-up period of 18.0 days (IQR, 1.0–697.0 days), neutropenia at ICU admission was associated with significantly higher mortality. ICU mortality was 48.0% in patients with neutropenia compared with 18.9% in patients without neutropenia (P < 0.001), and hospital mortality was 60.1% versus 28.3%, respectively (P = 0.007) (Table 3). Even after adjusting for potential confounders, neutropenia remained associated with increased ICU mortality (adjusted HR 1.48; 95% CI 1.20–1.83) and hospital mortality (adjusted HR 1.27; 95% CI 1.07–1.52). Subgroup Analysis

Discussion

This study demonstrated that patients with ARF and neutropenia admitted to the ICU had significantly high mortality rates—48.0% for ICU mortality and 60.1% for hospital mortality. Patients with neutropenia had a 1.48-fold increased risk of ICU mortality and 1.27-fold higher risk of hospital mortality compared with patients without neutropenia. These patients also presented with more severe illness, as evidenced by higher rates of organ failure and increased need for corticosteroids, HFNC, CRRT, vasoactive agents, and a positive net fluid balance. The association between neutropenia and mortality was particularly pronounced among patients without cancer. In patients with neutropenia, higher SOFA scores remained independently associated with increased hospital mortality after adjusting for confounders.

Previous research has primarily focused on chemotherapy-induced febrile neutropenia [25–27, 32, 33]. A population-based study in Denmark reported a 1.44-fold increased risk of all-cause mortality in patients with cancer and neutropenia [26]. Our findings align with those studies, indicating a 1.48-fold higher ICU mortality risk in patients with neutropenia. The discrepancies among studies may stem from differences in ICU settings, patient populations (which are often skewed toward cancer or immunocompromised patients), and the absence of subgroup analyses. Despite these variations, the high mortality rate (up to 60–80%) observed in patients with neutropenia in our study is consistent with those reported previously [32, 33] and highlights the need for improved management strategies.

We observed a stronger association between neutropenia and hospital mortality in patients without cancer (adjusted HR 3.08) compared with patients with cancer (adjusted HR 1.48). Most prior studies focused on patients with cancer [13, 24–26], producing little data on the impact of neutropenia in noncancer populations. Several mechanisms may explain this finding. First, severe neutropenia in patients without cancer may reflect bone marrow suppression due to sepsis-related multiorgan failure [34]. Second, autoimmune diseases and their treatments can induce neutropenia and increase vulnerability to infections [35]. Third, viral infections such as coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) may lead to neutropenia and acute respiratory distress syndrome, both of which are associated with high mortality [36]. Fourth, neutropenia may indicate an inadequate immune response to infection, contributing to worse outcomes [37]. A large multicenter study in Australia and New Zealand also reported the highest mortality in patients with neutropenia without malignancy, although the absence of a non-neutropenic control group limited its interpretation [38]. To the best of our knowledge, ours is the first study to directly compare outcomes between patients with neutropenia and patients without cancer and without neutropenia with ARF and highlight the clinical significance of neutropenia in patients without cancer.

In this study, among patients with neutropenia, the SOFA score emerged as an independent predictor of hospital mortality. This aligns with prior studies demonstrating a strong relationship between SOFA scores and mortality in neutropenic populations [38, 39]. Originally developed to assess organ dysfunction in critically ill patients, the SOFA score was later incorporated into the Sepsis-3 criteria as a clinical tool for sepsis identification. Now, the SOFA score has been validated as a reliable predictor of mortality in ICU patients [33, 39]. As the SOFA score reflects multiorgan dysfunction, it may serve as a useful prognostic tool in patients with neutropenia.

Unexpectedly, patients with neutropenia had higher SOFA scores than those without neutropenia (10.0 versus 6.0), yet a lower proportion received IMV (47.8% versus 57.0%). Although patients with a documented DNR order were excluded at enrollment, some may have discussed DNR decisions or hesitated to undergo invasive treatment, which could explain this paradox and suggests that treatment limitation or withdrawal decisions may have influenced the results.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center, limiting its generalizability, particularly across different ethnicities and healthcare systems. Second, variations in the management of neutropenia were not captured. Third, we did not systematically evaluate the etiology of neutropenia or the use of immunosuppressants in patients without cancer. The lack of specific classification regarding the etiology of neutropenia makes it difficult to distinguish between chronic neutropenia due to underlying diseases and secondary neutropenia occurring during acute exacerbations. Fourth, detailed data on causative pathogens, adequacy of empiric therapy, and time to effective therapy were not available, which may have influenced outcomes and limits further interpretation of our findings. Fifth, the duration of neutropenia prior to ICU admission was not available, which may influence disease severity, bone marrow recovery, and infection risk. Sixth, the potential role of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor was not assessed. Seventh, the severity of ARF based on PaO₂/FiO₂ ratios was not included. However, other indicators such as respiratory support modalities, SOFA scores, vasoactive drug use, and net fluid balance were used to reflect disease severity. Eighth, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II/III or IV scores, though valuable for assessing severity in critically ill patients, were not available; we therefore used the SOFA score, which, despite its limitations, is a widely used and validated measure. Finally, the number of patients without cancer with neutropenia was relatively small, comprising only 15% of the neutropenic cohort, which limits the generalizability of these subgroup findings and warrants cautious interpretation.

Conclusions

Patients with ARF and neutropenia exhibited significantly higher ICU and hospital mortality compared with patients without neutropenia. This association was especially pronounced in patients without cancer. The SOFA score independently predicted hospital mortality among patients with neutropenia. These findings underscore the clinical impact of neutropenia in critically ill patients and suggest that specific management approaches may benefit this vulnerable population, which should be investigated in future research.
