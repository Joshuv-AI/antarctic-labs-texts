Identification and validation of prognostic genes associated with m6A-regulated programmed cell death in acute lymphoblastic leukemia
Authors: Jiarui zhang, Meiru Yan, Juan Liu, Sijia Ma, Haipeng Hu, Man Xu, Min He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the prognostic significance of m6A-related programmed cell death (PCD) genes in acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). Transcriptomic data from the TCGA-ALL and GSE48558 datasets were analyzed to identify m6A-related PCD genes (corgenes), which were then intersected with 3,063 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), resulting in 23 candidate genes. Univariate Cox regression and LASSO regression analyses identified CFLAR and CDK4 as key prognostic genes, facilitating the construction of a high-accuracy risk prediction model. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed significant pathway differences between the high-risk group (HRG) and the low-risk group (LRG), including olfactory transduction, circadian rhythm, and protein export (adj.P < 0.05). Sensitivity analysis of 60 chemotherapy agents indicated that Bicalutamide was more effective in LRG, while ATRA, CCT018159, PHA-665752, and PLX4720 demonstrated greater efficacy in HRG. RT-qPCR validation confirmed upregulation of CDK4 and downregulation of CFLAR in ALL samples (P < 0.05). This study offers important theoretical support for the prognostic evaluation and personalized treatment strategies in ALL.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21986-4.Keywords: Acute lymphoblastic leukemia, N6-methyladenosine, Programmed cell death, Prognostic genes, Risk modelSubject terms: Cancer, Oncology

Introduction

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is a hematologic malignancy characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation of immature lymphoid cells. It is the most common cancer in children and a leading cause of death from disease in individuals under 19 years old, as well as a significant contributor to cancer-related mortality in adults. Recent advances in combined chemotherapy, allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and molecular targeted therapies have substantially improved the survival rates of patients with ALL1–3, particularly among children aged 1 to 14 years and adolescents and young adults aged 15 to 39 years4. Despite these advances, approximately 10–15% of patients experience relapse, with over 80% of this subgroup developing resistance to intensified chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or allogeneic stem cell transplants, eventually succumbing to the disease. While genetic factors likely contribute to chemoresistance5, a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying this phenomenon is crucial for future efforts to manage relapse and improve patient outcomes. The current risk stratification system does not consistently predict recurrence in all patients, highlighting the need for additional biomarkers that can enhance prognostic accuracy and facilitate the implementation of more effective treatment strategies.

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is a prevalent, abundant, and evolutionarily conserved cotranscriptional modification found in eukaryotic RNAs, particularly in higher eukaryotes6. m6A RNA modification plays a pivotal role in various physiological and pathological conditions, particularly in the development and progression of human cancers7. The modification of RNAs with m6A is also crucial in hematopoietic development, a vital process for the maturation of the blood system8. Research by Liu et al. demonstrated that leukemia cell resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy is linked to upregulation of FTO, an m6A regulator, which results in reduced m6A levels within the cells9. The role of m6A as a key modulator in acute myelogenous leukemia (AML) progression has been well established. YTHDF1, which regulates m6A-modified mRNA expression, is a critical factor in AML progression and serves as a major risk marker for disease deterioration10. Additionally, the overexpression of ALKBH5 in AML has been associated with poor prognosis in patients11

Programmed cell death (PCD) is an orchestrated process by which cells in an organism systematically terminate their life according to a defined program. PCD is critical for normal development, tissue homeostasis, immune response, cancer regulation, and various pathological conditions. Recent studies have identified several types of PCD, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, autophagy, necroptosis, cuproptosis, disulfidptosis, parthanatos, entotic cell death, NETosis, lysosome-dependent cell death, alkaliptosis, oxeiptosis, and zinc-dependent cell death12,13. Dysregulation of the PI3K/AKT/mTORC1 and NOTCH1 pathways affects autophagy regulation in B-cell ALL (B-ALL). Glucocorticoids, a cornerstone of acute B-ALL treatment, can induce autophagy14. While glucocorticoids are commonly used in B-ALL to trigger autophagy and promote cell death, in ETV6-RUNX1-positive cells, autophagy paradoxically promotes cell proliferation and drug resistance, complicating treatment outcomes15. In pediatric ALL, remission failure and relapse are associated with an imbalanced Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, which is critical for regulating anti-apoptotic and pro-apoptotic functions16. Recent studies have highlighted the role of m6A modification in regulating PCD gene expression, influencing cancer development and progression17. While both m6A modification and PCD are involved in ALL progression, their specific interactions in ALL pathogenesis remain poorly understood and require further investigation. Understanding the mechanisms of m6A modification and PCD in ALL is expected to provide new insights into disease pathogenesis, facilitating the identification of critical risk factors and therapeutic targets for ALL.

In this study, transcriptomic data from patients with ALL were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA-ALL) and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository (GSE48558). Differential expression analysis was conducted to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with ALL. The overlap of these gene sets led to the identification of candidate genes. Subsequently, univariate Cox and LASSO regression analyses were employed to identify prognostic genes associated with m6A and PCD in ALL. A prognostic risk model was constructed to predict patient survival. To explore potential mechanisms differentiating high- and low-risk groups, differential expression analysis, functional enrichment analysis, and inter-group relationship analysis were performed. Furthermore, the association between prognostic genes and enriched pathways was investigated, alongside the molecular regulatory networks involving these genes. These findings provide novel opportunities for predicting clinical prognosis and managing ALL.

Materials and methods

Data source

The transcriptome dataset GSE48558 related to ALL was retrieved from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/)18. This dataset, constructed on the GPL6244 platform, includes 82 cell samples from patients with ALL (ALL group) and 42 cell samples from healthy controls (control group). Additionally, the datasets TARGET-ALL-P1, TARGET-ALL-P2, and TARGET-ALL-P3, corresponding to ALL stage I, II, and III samples, were retrieved from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and combined into the TCGA-ALL dataset, which contains 648 bone marrow cell samples from patients with ALL. Data were accessed on January 8, 2025. For these 648 ALL samples, after excluding those with incomplete survival data, 194 samples with complete survival time and status were retained. Of these, approximately 70% (n = 137) were designated for the training set, and the remaining 30% (n = 57) were allocated to the testing set. In addition, 30 m6A-related genes (m6A-RGs) were obtained from the literature (PMID: 35,164,788)19 and (PMID: 31,100,245)20 (Supplementary Table 1). By integrating 18 types of genes related to PCD, 1,547 PCD-related genes (PRGs) were identified (PMID: 37,660,060)21 (Supplementary Table 2).

Acquisition of candidate genes

To identify m6A-related PCD genes, the R package “psych” (v 2.4.3)22 was employed. Spearman correlation analysis was performed on all samples in the TCGA-ALL dataset to assess the relationship between PRGs and m6A-RGs. Genes with |correlation (r)|> 0.7 and P < 0.05 were considered m6A-related PRGs and were defined as related genes (corgenes). Subsequently, the R package “limma” (v 3.58.1)23 was used to conduct differential analysis between the ALL and control groups in the GSE48558 dataset. DEGs between the two groups were identified based on the criteria of |log2 fold change (FC)|> 1 and adj.P < 0.05. The R package “ggplot2” (v 3.5.1)24 was used to generate volcano plots and heatmaps of DEGs. Finally, the R package “ggvenn” (v 0.1.10) (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=ggvenn) was applied to intersect the DEGs with corgenes. Through this intersection, genes related to m6A-regulated PCD in ALL were identified and defined as candidate genes.

The implementation of functional enrichment analysis along with the establishment of the PPI network of candidate genes

To further explore the biological functions of the candidate genes, the R package “clusterProfiler” (v 4.8.3)25 was used to conduct Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)26–28 enrichment analyses (adj.P < 0.05). The candidate genes were then input into the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) to investigate protein–protein interactions (PPI). Genes with an interaction score ≥ 0.4 were selected to build the PPI network, which was visualized using the R package “ggraph” (v 2.2.1) (https://libraries.io/cran/ggraph).​

Uncovering prognostic genes in ALL

Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed using the R package “survival” (v 3.7–0)29 on candidate genes in 137 ALL samples from the training set. The goal was to identify genes associated with overall survival (OS) in patients with ALL (P < 0.05, hazard ratio (HR) ≠ 1). To visualize the results, the R package “forestplot” (v 3.1.3)30 was utilized. Following this, the proportional hazards (PH) assumption test was conducted on the screened genes using the “survival” package (v 3.7–0). Genes with P > 0.05 passed this test, while those with P < 0.05 were excluded. For the remaining genes, LASSO regression analysis was performed using the “glmnet” package (v 4.1–8) (https://glmnet.stanford.edu). The optimal lambda value was determined to identify the prognostic genes. Next, to assess the value of prognostic genes in ALL diagnosis, single-gene ROC curves were plotted using the R package “pROC” (v 1.18.5)31 in the TCGA dataset, with ALL samples as the outcome variable. To assess survival differences between high- and low-expression groups, patients in the TCGA dataset were divided into high- and low-expression groups based on the median gene expression. Subsequently, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were plotted using the R package “survival” (v 3.7–0).

A risk model was then constructed based on the prognostic genes within the training dataset, following the formula (where β represents the coefficient and X indicates the sample expression level of each gene).

Subsequently, based on the optimal cut-off value of the risk score, ALL samples were categorized into the low-risk group (LRG) and the high-risk group (HRG). This study used the surv_cutpoint function from the “survminer” package (v 0.4.9)32 to determine the optimal grouping threshold by maximizing the log-rank statistic. The function iterates through all possible cut-off points and selects the one that maximizes the difference in survival curves, effectively distinguishing the survival status of patients in different risk groups. The differences in the distribution of risk scores and survival status between HRG and LRG were assessed. The R package “survival” (v 3.7–0) was used to generate Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival curves for both groups. The log-rank test was applied to evaluate the differences in OS between the two groups (P < 0.05). To assess the prognostic predictive ability of the risk model for ALL, the “survivalROC” (v 1.0.3.1) R package33 was used to generate ROC curves for 1-, 2-, and 3-year OS time points and calculate the area under the curve (AUC), with AUC > 0.6 considered indicative of predictive value. Additionally, the “pheatmap” (v 1.0.12) R package34 was used to generate a heatmap to visually display the expression of prognostic genes between the two risk groups. To further evaluate the accuracy and general applicability of the risk model, validation procedures were conducted on the testing set.​

Construction and validation of nomogram

To further validate the predictive performance of the constructed risk model, a nomogram was constructed using the R package “rms” (v 6.8–1)35 in the training set, combining clinical characteristics (gender and age) and risk scores. Each factor was assigned a score (Points), and the total score (Total Points) was obtained by summing the scores of all factors. Based on Total Points, the probability of patient death at different time points (1 year, 2 years, 3 years) was predicted. The higher the Total Points, the greater the probability of death. Finally, the predictive value of the nomogram was assessed using calibration curves and ROC curves. Additionally, for the 194 samples in the TCGA dataset that included survival information, Spearman correlation analysis was performed using the R package “psych” (v 2.4.3) to calculate the correlation coefficients between prognostic genes and clinical characteristics. The R package “ggplot2” (v 3.5.1) was then used to plot correlation heatmap.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)

GSEA was performed on HRG and LRG using the R package “clusterProfiler” (v 4.8.3), identifying pathway differences between the two groups. Prior to this, differential expression analysis of HRG and LRG samples in the training set was conducted using the R package “DESeq2” (v 1.42.0)36. Log2FC values for DEGs were calculated, and the gene list was sorted in descending order based on these values. The c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.symbols.gmt file was retrieved from MSigDB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb) for the subsequent GSEA analysis (adj.P < 0.05). The top five pathways, ranked by adj.P, were visualized using the R package “enrichplot” (v 1.14.2)37. For identifying pathways associated with prognostic genes, patients with ALL were classified into the high-expression group (HEG) and the low-expression group (LEG) based on the median expression value of prognostic genes. GSEA between HEG and LEG was then performed using “clusterProfiler” (v 4.8.3) R package (adj.P < 0.05).

Drug sensitivity analysis

To calculate the 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50) values for 138 chemotherapeutic drugs on ALL samples, the R package “pRRophetic” (v 0.5)38 was used. These drugs were sourced from the GDSC database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/). The Wilcoxon test was then applied to assess the differences in IC50 values between the two risk groups in the training set (P < 0.05).

Drug prediction and molecular docking

To explore potential drugs for ALL, we predicted drugs or molecular compounds interacting with prognostic genes using the DGidb database (https://dgidb.org/) and visualized the results with Cytoscape to construct a drug-prognostic gene network. We then selected drugs with the highest binding scores and 3D structures for molecular docking with prognostic genes. Protein (3D structure) data were downloaded from the PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/), and molecular ligands (3D structure) were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Both protein and ligand structures were uploaded to the CB-Dock2 platform (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/php/index.php) for molecular docking, and binding free energy was calculated. Smaller binding energy indicates stronger binding, with a binding energy ≤ -5.0 kcal/mol signifying a strong binding relationship.

Development of molecular regulatory network

To elucidate the molecular regulatory mechanisms related to prognostic genes, transcription factor (TF) information was obtained from the TRRUST database (https://www.grnpedia.org/trrust/). A TF-mRNA network centered around the prognostic genes was constructed using the “Cytoscape” (v 3.10.2)39 software. Additionally, the Regnetwork (http://www.zpliulab.cn/RegNetwork/home) and miRWalk (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de/) databases were used to predict mRNA-miRNA pairs based on prognostic genes. The miRNAs predicted by both databases were intersected to identify key miRNAs, and the “Cytoscape” software was again utilized to construct the miRNA-mRNA regulatory network.​

RT-qPCR

Blood samples were collected from 5 patients with ALL and 5 healthy controls at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University for RT-qPCR analysis. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (XJTU). Total RNA was extracted from the 10 blood samples using TRIzol reagent (Ambion, USA), and RNA concentration was measured using a NanoPhotometer N50. Reverse transcription to synthesize cDNA was performed with the SureScript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit and the S1000TM Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, USA). The primer sequences used in qPCR are provided in Supplementary Table 3. Relative quantification of prognostic genes was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method. Statistical analysis of RT-qPCR data and graphical representation were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (P < 0.05).

Statistical analysis

All bioinformatics analyses were performed using R software (v 4.3.1). The Wilcoxon test was applied for comparisons among different groups, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. For RT-qPCR analysis, inter-group comparisons were performed using the t-test (P < 0.05).

Results

Determination of candidate genes

Correlation analysis between PRGs and m6A-RGs was initially performed, resulting in the identification of 155 m6A-regulated PRGs, referred to as corgenes (Supplementary Table 4, Fig. 1A). In the GSE48558 dataset, 3,063 DEGs were detected between the ALL and control groups. Among these, 1,583 genes were upregulated, while 1,480 genes were downregulated (Supplementary Table 5, Fig. 1B-C). An intersection of the 3,063 DEGs and 155 corgenes yielded 23 candidate genes (Supplementary Table 6, Fig. 1D).

(B-C) Differential expression analysis from the GSE48558 dataset, highlighting 3,063 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between ALL and control groups, including 1,583 up-regulated and 1,480 down-regulated genes. (D) Intersection of these DEGs with the 155 corgenes, leading to the identification of 23 candidate genes.

GO and KEGG analyses and PPI network for candidate genes

GO analysis revealed significant enrichment of these 23 candidate genes in 132 GO terms (adj.P < 0.05). Specifically, this enrichment was observed in 113 biological processes (BP), 6 cellular components (CC), and 13 molecular functions (MF) (Supplementary Tables 7, Supplementary Tables 8, Supplementary Tables 9). Key BP terms included intrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway, regulation of epithelial cell proliferation, and regulation of apoptotic signaling pathway. In terms of CC, genes were notably enriched in the chromosome telomeric region, chromosomal region, and serine/threonine protein kinase complex. Furthermore, the genes were associated with MF terms such as protein serine/threonine kinase activity, protein serine kinase activity, and ligase activity (Fig. 2A). KEGG pathway analysis identified 24 pathways related to these candidate genes (adj.P < 0.05), with prominent pathways including the cell cycle, hepatitis C, and p53 signaling (Supplementary Table 10), Fig. 2B). A PPI network was constructed for the candidate genes, revealing that 16 genes had 32 protein-level interactions (interaction score ≥ 0.4). Notably, CASP8, CHEK2, and CDK4 exhibited strong interactions with other genes (Fig. 2C).

Identification of CFLAR and CDK4 as prognostic genes

In the training set comprising 137 ALL samples, univariate Cox regression analysis identified 3 genes—CFLAR, CDK4, and PRKDC—as prognostic markers for ALL. All 3 genes were associated with increased risk (HR > 1, P < 0.05) (Fig. 3A). The PH assumption test confirmed that these genes met the assumption criteria (P > 0.05) (Fig. 3B). LASSO regression analysis was subsequently performed, and with the optimal lambda (λ) value set at log(lambda.min) = -2.6596, CFLAR and CDK4 were retained as the prognostic genes (Fig. 3C-D). In the TCGA dataset, patients were divided into high- and low-expression groups based on the median gene expression levels (CFLAR: 4.41645, CDK4: 6.73615). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1A, the Log-rank test revealed a significant difference in survival rates between the two groups (p < 0.05), with lower survival rates observed in the high-expression group. Furthermore, the AUC values for prognostic genes in the ROC analysis were all greater than 0.9 (Supplementary Fig. 1B), indicating that CFLAR and CDK4 have high diagnostic accuracy and may serve as potential diagnostic biomarkers for ALL.

Development and evaluation of risk model with superior predictive power based on 2 prognostic genes

A risk model was developed based on the two prognostic genes, CFLAR and CDK4. Risk scores for each ALL sample in the training set were calculated using this model. By applying the optimal cut-off value of 0.1757819738, patients with ALL were categorized into HRG (n = 118) and LRG (n = 19). The distribution of risk scores and survival status between the two groups revealed a significant increase in the mortality rate as the risk score rose (Fig. 4A-B). The KM survival curve demonstrated that patients in LRG had a notably higher survival probability (P = 0.0026) (Fig. 4C). The ROC curve of the risk model indicated AUC > 0.6 for 1-, 2-, and 3-year survival, confirming the effectiveness of the model (Fig. 4D). Additionally, the expressions of CFLAR and CDK4 were significantly elevated in HRG (Fig. 4E). To validate the model, it was further tested in an independent set. Using the optimal cut-off value of 0.3833457 for the testing set, patients were classified into HRG (n = 24) and LRG (n = 33). The distribution of risk scores and survival status (Fig. 5A-B), KM survival curve (P = 0.0009) (Fig. 5C), ROC curve (AUC > 0.6) (Fig. 5D), and the expressions of prognostic genes (Fig. 5E) all confirmed the model’s generalizability. These results provide a useful reference for the clinical prognostic assessment of ALL.

The predictive performance of the nomogram constructed using gender, age, and risk scores was good

Subsequently, a nomogram was constructed based on clinical characteristics (gender and age) and risk scores (Fig. 6A). The calibration curve showed that the nomogram closely aligned with the reference line when predicting 1-, 2-, and 3-year mortality probabilities, with the actual curve approaching the ideal curve, indicating high predictive accuracy (Fig. 6B). Further analysis of the ROC curves of the nomogram revealed AUC values of 0.72, 0.66, and 0.64 for 1, 2, and 3 years, respectively, all exceeding 0.60, confirming the nomogram’s good predictive performance (Fig. 6C). Moreover, Spearman correlation analysis was performed on prognostic genes and clinical characteristics, and the results showed that prognostic genes were most highly correlated with risk scores (Fig. 6D).

Correlation analysis between prognostic genes and enriched pathways

GSEA identified significant differences in KEGG pathways between HRG and LRG. The key pathways involved olfactory transduction, circadian rhythm in mammals, and protein export. Notably, olfactory transduction was enriched in HRG, while the other pathways were enriched in LRG (adj.P < 0.05) (Fig. 7A). Further investigation into the enrichment pathways associated with CFLAR and CDK4 revealed that 38 KEGG pathways exhibited significant differences between HEG and LEG for CFLAR (adj.P < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 11). Among the top 5 enriched pathways, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction was primarily enriched in HEG, while pathways such as systemic lupus erythematosus, ribosome, Parkinson’s disease, and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) were enriched in LEG (Fig. 7B). This suggests that the low expression of CFLAR influences ALL progression through regulation of these pathways, excluding cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction. Regarding CDK4, 56 KEGG pathways displayed significant enrichment between HEG and LEG (adj.P < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 12). The top 5 enriched pathways, including DNA replication, proteasome, nucleotide excision repair, olfactory transduction, and cytosolic DNA sensing, were notably enriched in HEG (Fig. 7C). This suggests that high expression of CDK4 may positively regulate these pathways, thus influencing ALL progression.

Analysis of the differences in the sensitivity of chemotherapeutic drugs under different risk groups

The IC50 values of 138 chemotherapeutic drugs were used as indicators of drug sensitivity. Analysis revealed significant differences in the IC50 values of 60 drugs when comparing HRG and LRG (P < 0.05). Specifically, 37 drugs showed a notable reduction in IC50 values in HRG, indicating greater sensitivity to these drugs. Conversely, 23 drugs exhibited a significant reduction in IC50 values in LRG, suggesting that LRG was more sensitive to these medications (Supplementary Table 13, Fig. 8A). The top 5 drugs with the most pronounced differences were further analyzed. Bicalutamide displayed higher sensitivity in LRG, while ATRA, CCT018159, PHA.665752, and PLX4720 showed enhanced sensitivity in HRG (P < 0.001) (Fig. 8B).

Establishment of TF-mRNA and miRNA-mRNA regulatory networks​

It was predicted that CFLAR was regulated by 11 TFs, and CDK4 by 12 TFs. Notably, four TFs—MYC, NFKB1, STAT3, and RELA—regulated both prognostic genes (Supplementary Table 14). The constructed TF-mRNA regulatory network comprised 2 prognostic genes, 19 TFs, and 23 TF-mRNA interaction pairs (Fig. 9A). Furthermore, 89 microRNAs (miRNAs) associated with the two prognostic genes were predicted, with 71 miRNAs linked to CFLAR and 20 to CDK4. Notably, both hsa-miR-27b-3p and hsa-miR-148b-3p were involved in regulating both genes (Supplementary Table 15). The miRNA-mRNA regulatory network included 2 prognostic genes, 89 miRNAs, and 94 miRNA-mRNA interaction pairs (Fig. 9B).

Validation of prognostic genes in clinical samples​

This study revealed distinct expression patterns of the two prognostic genes in the GSE48558 dataset, particularly between the ALL cohort and control individuals. In the ALL group, CFLAR expression was significantly downregulated (P < 0.0001), while CDK4 expression was markedly upregulated (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 10A). To further validate these findings in clinical blood specimens, RT-qPCR analysis was performed. RT-qPCR results confirmed that CFLAR expression was significantly lower in ALL samples compared to controls (P = 0.0302) (Fig. 10B). Conversely, CDK4 expression was significantly higher in ALL samples than in controls (P = 0.0031) (Fig. 10C). These experimental validation results closely aligned with the bioinformatics analysis, strongly confirming the reliability of the computational findings.​

Validation of Prognostic Gene Expression in Clinical Samples. (A) Analysis of the GSE48558 dataset revealed significant expression differences between the ALL cohort and controls, with CFLAR significantly downregulated (P < 0.0001) and CDK4 markedly upregulated in ALL patients (P < 0.0001). (B) RT-qPCR analysis confirmed significantly lower CFLAR expression in ALL samples compared to controls (P = 0.0302). (C) CDK4 expression was significantly higher in ALL samples than in controls (P = 0.0031).

Discussion

ALL is an aggressive blood cancer primarily affecting immature lymphocytes, leading to their abnormal proliferation in the bone marrow and potential spread to the bloodstream and other organs. Recent studies have highlighted the significant role of m6A modification in PCD processes, influencing leukemia cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival40. This study focused on examining the relationship between m6A-RGs and PRGs using data from publicly available repositories such as TCGA and GEO. Machine learning algorithms were employed to identify prognostic genes, develop a risk assessment model, and conduct pathway enrichment analysis and drug sensitivity evaluations on HRG and LRG. Additionally, the expression patterns of prognostic genes were validated using clinical samples.

Cellular CASP8 and FADD-like apoptosis regulator (CFLAR), also known as c-FLIP, serves as a key negative regulator of apoptosis in mammalian cells41. CFLAR contains two death effector domains at its N-terminal, while its extended version, CFLAR, features an additional pseudo-caspase domain. In this domain, the critical active-site cysteine residue, essential for caspase’s proteolytic function, is substituted with a tyrosine residue42. CFLAR is vital for key cellular processes such as inflammation and apoptosis, and is being explored as a promising therapeutic target in various cancers43–45. m6A modification inhibits the activity of RIPK1 by regulating the Atf3-CFLAR-cFLIP axis, preventing excessive death of intestinal epithelial cells and thereby maintaining intestinal homeostasis46. Additionally, studies have found that m6A modification indirectly affects the expression of cFLIP protein by regulating the transcription of the CFLAR gene and mRNA stability, thereby influencing cell death mechanisms47. Previous studies have observed elevated CFLAR protein expression in lung metastatic osteosarcoma compared to primary tumors48. In leukemia, high CFLAR expression has been linked to poorer survival outcomes49. Increased CFLAR levels promote apoptosis evasion in AML cells with reduced CUX1 TF. CUX1 deficiency relieves CFLAR promoter repression, resulting in heightened CFLAR expression and leukemia cell survival. Through CRISPR/Cas9 screening, CFLAR has been identified as a specific vulnerability in CUX1-deficient AML, which can be targeted with IAP antagonists in both murine and human AML cells50. In the present study, CFLAR was identified as a risk factor for ALL in HRG. However, direct studies elucidating the molecular mechanisms between CFLAR and ALL remain limited. Given that apoptotic defects are commonly associated with poor outcomes in ALL and CFLAR is integral to apoptosis regulation, further investigation of CFLAR’s molecular mechanisms may uncover new therapeutic targets for ALL.

CDK4 is a holoenzyme composed of a cyclin regulatory subunit and a cyclin-dependent kinase catalytic subunit. CDKs are serine/threonine kinases, with catalytic domains consisting of approximately 300 amino acids. In their underphosphorylated and monomeric state, CDKs remain inactive51. CDK4 activation requires interaction with cyclin partners and phosphorylation of a conserved threonine (Thr) residue in the T-loop by CAK1/CDK752. CDK4 binds to D-type cyclins, driving cell progression through the G1 phase by inducing hyperphosphorylation of the retinoblastoma (RB) protein and its associated proteins (p107 and p130). This activation promotes E2F transcription, facilitating entry into the S phase for DNA replication53. Various mechanisms regulate CDK4 activity. Growth factors stimulate the activation of the cyclin D family, which in turn activates CDK4. Conversely, the INK4 protein family (e.g., p16INK4A) inhibits CDK4 activity through specific binding, thereby influencing cell proliferation54. Deregulated cellular proliferation is a hallmark of human cancers, including breast cancer, where the cyclin D-CDK4/6 axis is often hyperactivated. Inhibition of this axis has emerged as a promising therapeutic approach, as preclinical studies demonstrate its significant role in driving tumor cell proliferation52. In ALL, CDK4 and CDK6 are central to the core cell cycle machinery, with deletions of CDKN2A/B and RB1 frequently observed. These deletions are linked to the CDK4/cyclin D pathway, promoting cancer cell proliferation and survival55,56. Notably, cyclin D3 has been identified as a key player in the initiation and progression of T-cell ALL, and targeting the CDK4/6-cyclin D complex has shown efficacy in suppressing T-ALL proliferation and inducing cell death in animal models57. Furthermore, m6A modification regulates METTL1 through METTL3, indirectly affecting m7G modification of the CDK4 gene, thereby stabilizing CDK4 protein and promoting cell proliferation58. Additionally, m6A modification acts together with DMDRMR and IGF2BP3 to stabilize CDK4 gene expression, promote clear cell renal cell carcinoma cell proliferation, and thereby drive tumor growth and metastasis59. Our study supports these findings, showing that high expression of CDK4 promotes leukemia progression and increases risk in HRG, aligning with current literature. This reinforces the critical role of CDK4 in leukemia and suggests its potential as an important biomarker for assessing disease risk and guiding treatment strategies. In conclusion, CDK4 plays a central role in ALL development, highlighting its potential as a target for novel therapeutic interventions in ALL.

GSEA enrichment analysis revealed that CFLAR potentially impacts ALL by modulating pathways associated with ribosomes, systemic lupus erythematosus, cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, OXPHOS, and Parkinson’s disease. Similarly, CDK4 may influence ALL by modulating pathways linked to DNA replication, base excision repair, RNA polymerase, N-glycan biosynthesis, and glutathione metabolism. These findings provide valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms involved in the pathogenesis of ALL. By integrating gene-disease-pathway associations, a deeper understanding of the specific functions of these enriched pathways in ALL, the involvement of relevant genes, and their impact on the initiation and progression of the disease can be gained. This research thus offers new perspectives for the diagnosis and therapeutic intervention of ALL.

The OXPHOS pathway plays a pivotal role in the characteristics of leukemia cells, especially in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), where mitochondrial OXPHOS is essential for cell survival60,61. ZDHHC21 palmitoyltransferase is a key regulator of OXPHOS hyperactivity in AML cells, catalyzing the palmitoylation of mitochondrial adenylate kinase 2 (AK2) and enhancing OXPHOS in leukemic blasts. This process contributes to the maintenance of stemness, leukemia stem cells (LSCs), and the inhibition of myeloid differentiation in AML cells62. The aberrant activation of the OXPHOS pathway is critical for sustaining the malignant phenotype of leukemia cells. Through the analysis of these prognostic gene enrichment pathways, valuable insights into the pathogenesis of ALL have been gained, alongside potential targets and strategies for diagnosis and treatment.

Analysis of the IC50 values of 138 chemotherapy drugs in both high-risk and low-risk groups revealed significant differences in the IC50 values of 60 drugs. Notably, the top five drugs with the most significant differences were identified: bicalutamide showed higher sensitivity in the low-risk group, whereas ATRA, CCT018159, PHA.665752, and PLX4720 exhibited greater sensitivity in the high-risk group.

All-trans-retinoic acid (ATRA) has demonstrated efficacy in cancer prevention, treatment of skin disorders, and the management of acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL). ATRA primarily exerts its pharmacological effects through retinoid X receptors (RXRs) and retinoic acid receptors (RARs), leading to the transcriptional activation of differentiation-related genes and the modulation of autophagy via mTOR inhibition, thereby exerting anti-APL effects63. This highlights ATRA’s ability to impede the malignant properties of leukemia cells by regulating crucial cellular processes such as differentiation and autophagy. While ATRA-induced differentiation has revolutionized the treatment of APL from a fatal to a highly treatable hematological condition, resistance to ATRA in high-risk patients with APL remains a significant clinical challenge. Our investigation revealed heightened sensitivity to ATRA in the high-risk ALL subgroup, suggesting potential shared biological features between ALL and APL cells, thereby facilitating ATRA’s efficacy. Bicalutamide, a non-steroidal anti-androgen medication, is primarily used in the treatment of prostate cancer and is not typically associated with ALL treatment64. CCT018159 is a Heat Shock Protein 90 (HSP90) inhibitor, which has been investigated in cancer treatment due to its ability to disrupt multiple signaling pathways essential for cancer cell growth and survival65. In the context of ALL, inhibiting HSP90 could potentially destabilize oncogenic proteins, affecting leukemia cell viability. PHA-665752 selectively blocks the c-Met receptor tyrosine kinase, a pathway critical for cell proliferation, survival, and metastasis. In ALL, inhibiting c-Met could be beneficial if the disease involves aberrant activation of this pathway, contributing to leukemogenesis or therapy resistance66. PLX4720 is a selective inhibitor of the BRAF V600E mutation, primarily relevant in cancers with this mutation, such as melanoma67. Its potential relevance to ALL would depend on the presence of BRAF mutations within leukemia cells, which are less common in ALL compared to other cancers68. Each of these compounds targets specific pathways, and their potential use in ALL treatment would depend on the molecular characteristics of the leukemia and the presence of actionable targets within these pathways. Clinical trials and further research are necessary to establish their efficacy and safety in ALL.

This study identified two prognostic genes, CFLAR and CDK4, associated with m6A and PCD in ALL. The findings suggest that these genes are closely linked to the initiation and progression of ALL, particularly through their roles in regulating key pathways such as ribosome biogenesis, OXPHOS, Parkinson’s disease, and DNA replication. The risk model constructed based on these genes has shown certain predictive value in predicting the prognosis of ALL, providing a new theoretical basis for further research on the role of these genes in the progression of ALL. However, the study is limited by the insufficient exploration of the precise molecular mechanisms of these genes in ALL, resulting in a gap in understanding their exact biological functions. Additionally, while the risk model proves to be a valuable prognostic tool, its practical utility in clinical settings and effectiveness in guiding therapeutic interventions require substantial validation through evidence-based research. Moreover, the study does not examine potential interactions between these genes and other molecular pathways in ALL, which could provide further insights into their roles. Finally, due to the small sample size of qRT-PCR, the results may be biased, thereby reducing the statistical power of the analysis. Therefore, future research should focus on elucidating the detailed molecular functions of CFLAR and CDK4 in ALL, exploring their potential therapeutic applications, and validating their clinical relevance. This includes comprehensive studies to assess their interactions with existing treatments and their impact on clinical outcomes. Furthermore, the accuracy of the research conclusions can be further improved by increasing the sample size. By addressing these aspects, future investigations can enhance the clinical applicability of these findings and contribute to more effective management strategies for ALL.
