Efficacy of preoxygenation by spontaneous tidal volume breathing for 3 minutes in pediatric patients aged 0–6 years: a prospective observational study
Authors: Ornin Chintabanyat, Panjan Sanmuk, Taniga Kiatchai, Naiyana Aroonpruksakul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective preoxygenation is critical for preventing hypoxemia during airway management, particularly in infants and young children who are physiologically more vulnerable. Although the standard practice of administering 100% oxygen via face mask for at least 3 minutes is adapted from adult protocols, supporting evidence in young children remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of standard 3-minute tidal volume breathing preoxygenation in achieving a fractional expired oxygen concentration (FeO2) of 0.9 in children aged 0–6 years.MethodsThis prospective observational study included children aged 0–6 years undergoing elective surgery under general anesthesia. Exclusion criteria included baseline oxygen saturation <95%, preoperative oxygen supplementation, pulmonary disease, and overweight status. Sedation was administered using with intravenous fentanyl and midazolam. The primary outcomes were the proportion of patients achieving FeO2 ≥0.9 within 3 minutes of preoxygenation. Secondary outcomes included the proportion of participants achieving FeO2 ≥0.9 at other time points, and the median FeO2 values at 1, 2, and 3 minutes. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age group (0–2 vs. 3–6 years) and face mask compliance.ResultsA total of 126 patients were included in the analysis. The FeO2 of 0.9 was reached by 31.0%, 80.2%, and 91.3% of participants at 1, 2, and 3 minutes of preoxygenation, respectively. The median time to achieve FeO2 ≥0.9 was 80.0 seconds (95% confidence interval: 74.3–85.7). There was no statistically significant difference in time to reach the target FeO2 between age groups (P=0.10). However, children with good face mask compliance achieved the target significantly faster than those with fair compliance (median time: 80.0 vs. 100.0 seconds, P=0.009).ConclusionsStandard preoxygenation with 100% oxygen for 3 minutes is effective in achieving an FeO2 ≥0.9 in 91.3% of children under six years of age. Although age did not significantly influence the time to reach the target FeO2, better face mask compliance was associated with more rapid oxygenation.Keywords: Pediatric anesthesia, airway management, oxygen inhalation therapy, patient safety, preoxygenation

Highlight box

Key findings

• Following preoxygenation with tidal volume breathing for 3 minutes, the median time to achieve a fractional expired oxygen concentration (FeO2) ≥0.9 was 80.0 seconds (95% confidence interval: 74.3–85.7) in children aged 0–6 years.

• The target FeO2 was achieved in 31.0%, 80.2%, and 91.3% of participants at 1, 2, and 3 minutes, respectively.

What is known and what is new?

• The most commonly used preoxygenation technique prior to intubation involves tidal volume breathing of 100% oxygen for 3 minutes. In adult populations, the proportion of individuals achieving an FeO2 of 0.9 with this technique ranged from 44% to 79%, while previous pediatric study involving children aged 6–15 years reported a success rate of 79%.

• This study demonstrates that standard preoxygenation with 100% oxygen for 3 minutes is effective in achieving an FeO2 ≥0.9 in 91.3% of children under six years of age. No statistically significant difference in time to reach FeO2 ≥0.9 was observed between younger (0–2 years) and older (3–6 years) age groups (P=0.10). However, children with good face mask compliance reached the target FeO2 significantly faster than those with fair compliance (median time: 80.0 vs. 100.0 seconds, P=0.009).

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Preoxygenation in young children presents unique challenges, primarily due to limited cooperation with tight-fitting face masks. This study highlights the importance of appropriate sedation in enhancing mask tolerance and improving the success of preoxygenation in poorly cooperative pediatric patients.

Introduction

Endotracheal intubation is a critical airway procedure that carries the risks of hypoxia due to the apneic period inherent to the process. In standard practice, oxygen reserves are typically maximized by administering positive pressure ventilation after the patient loses consciousness and before the apneic phase during intubation. However, in specific clinical scenarios—such as when positive airway pressure increases the risk of aspiration or when difficulties in ventilation and intubation are anticipated—preoxygenation becomes essential to maximize oxygen reserves. Preoxygenation is performed while the patient is still awake, using spontaneous ventilation with a fractional inspired oxygen concentration (FiO2) 1.0, either prior to the induction of general anesthesia or before the administration of sedative agents in emergency or critical care settings (1,2). The adequacy of preoxygenation in achieving sufficient oxygen reserves is best assessed by measuring the fractional expired oxygen concentration (FeO2), with a target value exceeding 0.9 (3-6).

Current preoxygenation techniques used in pediatrics are largely extrapolated from methods that have been studied in adult patients. Traditional methods include tidal volume breathing for at least 3 minutes (3-5,7,8), or performing eight deep vital capacity breaths within one minute (9), both utilizing 100% oxygen delivered via a face mask and a standard anesthesia breathing system. Alternative techniques, such as noninvasive ventilation and high-flow nasal cannula, have also been reported (1,2,10,11). The most widely adopted approach involves breathing 100% oxygen through a face mask for 3 minutes (3-6,12). However, studies have reported the mean FeO2 ranging between 0.86 and 0.89 in adults using this method (4-7). The proportion of patients achieving FeO2 levels of ≥0.9 after 3 minutes of preoxygenation varies, with most studies reporting rates between 44% and 54% (4-6), while one study observed a higher rate of 77.5% (7). Extending the preoxygenation duration from three to five minutes has been shown to increase the proportion of patients reaching an FeO2 of 0.9, from 50% to 70% (5). Early evidence on preoxygenation in pediatric patients was demonstrated in infants under four months of age, where administration of 100% oxygen at 5 L/min via face mask for 3 minutes significantly reduced the incidence of hypoxemia during lumbar puncture—from 92% to 11% (13).

For pediatric studies, Butler et al. investigated children aged 1–12 years, all spontaneously breathing through a tightly sealed face mask with a 6 L/min oxygen flow via an anesthesia circle system. All participants achieved an FeO2 ≥0.9 within 80 seconds (14). Chiron et al. compared three preoxygenation techniques in children aged 6–15 years: 3 minutes of spontaneous tidal breathing, four deep vital capacity breaths, and eight deep vital capacity breaths. The proportions of patients achieving FeO2 ≥0.9 were 79%, 11%, and 68%, respectively (15). Edmunds et al. studied preoxygenation before rapid sequence induction in pediatric patients aged 0–18 years {median age [interquartile range (IQR)]: 1.6 (0.3–5.6) years}. The median (IQR) FeO2 after 3 minutes of tidal volume breathing was reported as 0.90 (0.88–0.92) (16).

Although previous studies have described preoxygenation techniques in children and adolescents, evidence on achieving effective preoxygenation in infants and young children—particularly in those with limited ability to cooperate—remains limited. At birth, the number of terminal air sacs is approximately one-tenth that of fully developed lungs. While the number of alveoli reaches adult levels by around 18 months of age, both morphological and physiological maturation of the lungs continues throughout the first decade of life (17). This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of preoxygenation using 100% oxygen during 3 minutes of spontaneous ventilation in children aged 0–6 years. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2025-277/rc).

Methods

This prospective observational study was conducted at the Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand, between March 2023 and May 2024. The study protocol was prospectively registered with the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (https://www.thaiclinicaltrials.org/show/TCTR20230309007). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Siriraj Institutional Review Board (certificate of approval No. Si 137/2023) and informed consent was obtained from parents/legal guardians of the patients.

Pediatric patients aged 0–6 years scheduled for elective surgery under general anesthesia with either endotracheal intubation or laryngeal mask airway, and classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–II, were included in the study. Exclusion criteria were: baseline pulse oximetry <95% or requirement for preoperative oxygen supplementation, known congenital heart disease, pulmonary disease, anticipated difficult airway, facial abnormalities impeding proper mask fitting, body weight for age >95th percentile or <5th percentile, and known allergy to any medication used in the study protocol.

All participants were admitted 1 day prior to surgery and received intravenous fluid according to institutional practice. Upon arrival in the operating theatre, standard monitoring—including electrocardiogram, non-invasive blood pressure, and pulse oximetry (SpO2)—were applied. Intravenous fentanyl (1 µg/kg) and midazolam (0.1 mg/kg) were administered 1–3 minutes before the initiation of preoxygenation. The anesthetic machine and circle breathing circuit (semiclosed and semiopen breathing system) were flushed with 100% for 30 seconds prior to preoxygenation. The level of sedation was assessed using the Pediatric Sedation State Scale (PSSS), which ranges from 0 to 5 (18). Preoxygenation was initiated when the participant achieved a sedation level of PSSS 2–4. Participants with a PSSS score of 5 (requiring forceful immobilization), 0–1 (deeply asleep and requiring airway intervention), or SpO2 <92% were withdrawn from the study.

After sedation, participants breathed spontaneously for 3 minutes through a facemask. The tight-fitting anesthesia facemask was applied with oxygen delivered at a flow rate of 6 L/min, while adjustable pressure-limiting (APL) valve remained fully open. Air leak was minimized by observing adequate reservoir bag movement and confirming the presence of end-tidal carbon dioxide waveform. The primary endpoint was defined as completion of 3-minute preoxygenation period with achievement of an FeO2 ≥0.9, after which anesthesia was induced per routine practice. If an FeO2 of 0.9 was not achieved within 3 minutes, preoxygenation was continued until the target was reached. If the target FeO2 remained below 0.9 at 6 minutes, the study was terminated and anesthesia induction proceeded as per standard protocol.

Data collection

FeO2 was measured using a General Electric (GE) anesthetic machine equipped with the CarescapeTM Respiratory Modules E-sCAiO (General Electric Company, Cincinnati, OH, USA). This GE differential paramagnetic sensor has a measurement range of 0–100 vol%, an accuracy of +/− (1 vol% + 2% of reading), and a rise time of less than 260 milliseconds. Data collection was performed using recordings obtained during the preoxygenation period. SpO2 and FeO2 values were extracted from the recordings at 10-second interval. The primary outcome was the proportion of participants who achieved an FeO2 ≥0.9 following 3 minutes of preoxygenation. Secondary outcomes included the proportion of participants achieving FeO2 ≥0.9 at other time points, and the median FeO2 values at 1, 2, and 3 minutes of preoxygenation.

Sample size calculation

Edmunds et al. reported a median FeO2 of 0.90 (IQR, 0.88–0.92) following 3 minutes of preoxygenation with spontaneous ventilation in children 0–18 years (16). Based on this, we estimated that 50% of participants in our study would achieve an FeO2 ≥0.9. Sample size was calculated using the n4Studies application (version 1.4.2), assuming an expected proportion of 0.50, two-sided alpha of 0.05, absolute error of 0.125, and power of 80%, resulting in a required sample size of 62 participants. According to Aroonpruksakul et al. (19), successful preoxygenation after sedation was achieved in 92.2% of children aged 0–7 years undergoing rapid sequence induction. In our study, we aimed to assess preoxygenation efficacy across two age groups (0–2 and 3–6 years). Allowing for a 20% dropout rate, the calculated total sample size was 156 participants (78 per group).

Statistical analysis

Demographic data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or number (percentage). The proportion of participants who achieved an FeO2 of 0.9 were presented as number (percentage), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated using the Wilson-Score method. Time to achieve an FeO2 of 0.9 was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves and compared using the log-rank test. FeO2 values at different time points (1, 2, and 3 minutes) were presented as median with IQR and compared using the related-samples Friedman’s test with pairwise comparisons. If FeO2 data were missing at 1, 2, or 3 minutes, the most recent previously recorded FeO2 value was carried forward for analysis. Subgroup analyses were performed based on age and face mask compliance. Age was categorized into younger (0–2 years) and older (3–6 years) groups. Compliance was classified as good (PSSS score 2–3) and fair (PSSS score 4). Difference in FeO2 between subgroups were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 156 participants were initially enrolled in the study. However, 30 participants were withdrawn, mostly due to a PSSS of 5, leaving 126 participants for the final analysis. Of these, 60 participants were aged 0–2 years, and 66 were aged 3–6 years, as described in the participant selection flow diagram (Figure 1). The overall age was 3.1±2.0 years. The majority of participants were classified as ASA classification I (71.4%) and underwent superficial surgical procedures (88.1%). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of sex, ASA classification, or procedure type (Table 1).

Subgroup analysis by age groups revealed no statistically significant differences in the time to achieve an FeO2 of 0.9 (P=0.10, Figure 4A). The median time (95% CI) to achieve an FeO2 of 0.9 was 70.0 seconds (59.2–80.8) for the 0–2-year age group and 80.0 seconds (70.6–89.4) for the 3–6-year age group. Subgroup analysis based on compliance showed that 83 participants (65.9%) had good compliance (PSSS 2–3), while 43 participants (34.1%) had fair compliance (PSSS 4). Participants with good compliance achieved an FeO2 of 0.9 more rapidly than those with fair compliance (P=0.009). The median time (95% CI) to achieve an FeO2 of 0.9 was 80.0 seconds (73.7–86.3) for good compliance and 100.0 seconds (81.9–118.1) for fair compliance (Figure 4B). Discussion

Overall, 91.3% of pediatric patients achieved an FeO2 of 0.9 within 3 minutes of preoxygenation using spontaneous ventilation, with a median time to reach the target FeO2 of 80 seconds. This outcome closely aligns with findings from a previous study employing the same preoxygenation technique. Chiron et al. reported an average time of 79±33 seconds to achieve an FeO2 of 0.9 following 3 minutes of spontaneous ventilation (15). However, our study, which focused on children aged 0–6 years, demonstrated a higher proportion of patients (91.3%) reaching the target FeO2 compared to 79% in Chiron et al.’s findings, which involved children aged 6–15 years. The lower proportion observed in Chiron et al.’s study may be attributed to older participants having a larger functional residual capacity (FRC), requiring more time to reach an FeO2 of 0.9. Our results also confirmed this trend (Table 2), as younger patients (aged 0–2 years) achieved an FeO2 of 0.9 more rapidly and in greater numbers than older patients (aged 3–6 years). In contrast, Butler et al. reported that all 25 children in their study, aged 1 to 12 years, reached an FeO2 of 0.9 within 80 seconds (14). Despite the wider age range in Butler et al.’s study, all participants achieved the target faster than those in our study.

The preoxygenation process can be influenced by various factors, particularly the inspired oxygen concentration, which is affected by anesthetic system used, oxygen flow rate, presence of leaks (20,21) and type of breathing—tidal volume or deep breathing. Additional factors include the duration of breathing, and the alveolar ventilation-to-FRC ratio (22). Roveri et al. investigated three-minute preoxygenation in children aged 6–12 years using a bag-valve mask (BVM) with and without positive-end expiratory pressure, reporting mean FeO2 values of 0.646 and 0.675, respectively (23). These values are lower than those observed in our study, which may be attributed to differences in both equipment and pediatric respiratory physiology. The BVM has been reported to inconsistently achieve FiO2 >0.85 across different models (24), whereas our study employed an anesthesia circle circuit, which provides more consistent oxygen delivery. Additionally, young children have a lower FRC per kilogram of body weight and a higher respiratory rate compared to adolescents and adults (17). Consequently, their alveolar ventilation-to-FRC ratio increased, allowing FeO2 to rise more rapidly during preoxygenation. Nimmagadda et al. highlighted the significance of a tight-fitting mask during tidal volume breathing for 5 minutes. Using oxygen flow rate of 5, 7, and 10 L/min with anesthesia circle circuit, they reported similar FeO2 values of 0.86, 0.88, and 0.88, respectively, suggesting that increasing the fresh gas flow from 5 to 10 L/min does not enhance preoxygenation (8). In this study, preoxygenation was optimized by providing all patients with an oxygen flow rate of 6 L/min via a semi-closed anesthesia breathing system. Patients were sedated to ensure cooperation and a tightly fitted mask while maintaining normal tidal volume breathing for at least 3 minutes. To keep FiO2 close to 1.0, air entrainment was minimized and confirmed by the detection of end-tidal carbon dioxide during spontaneous breathing.

The main challenge in pediatric preoxygenation is ensuring cooperation for a tightly fitted mask fit during spontaneous ventilation. This issue is particularly pronounced among younger children, especially those under 2 years of age, who often experience heightened anxiety and discomfort that makes the tight mask fit intolerable. Despite the administration of midazolam and fentanyl intravenously as part of our study protocol, 13 out of 79 participants (16.5%) in the 0–2-year age group were withdrawn from the study due to sedation failure (PSSS =5). A previous study also reported adequate preoxygenation prior to rapid sequence induction in 59 out of 64 participants (92.2%) within the age group of 0–7 years (19). In our analysis, participants with good compliance achieved the target FeO2 more rapidly (P=0.009, Figure 4B). Although the proportion of participants with good compliance reaching the target was higher than those with fair compliance (94% vs. 86%), this difference was not statistically significant. We advocate for the use of sedation to improve the effectiveness of preoxygenation in young children. With the sedative dose used in this study, only 65.9% of patients demonstrated good compliance, defined as tolerating mask application with minimal facial movement. Overall, 30 out of 156 participants (22.4%) were withdrawn from the study due to a PSSS of 5, indicating complete refusal of the mask. Further research is warranted to investigate optimal pharmacologic agents, dosing strategies, or non-pharmacological techniques to improve mask acceptance in this young population. However, one patient experienced desaturation due to upper airway obstruction, which was associated with undetected preoperative snoring.

This study also reported proportion of participants who achieved FeO2 0.9 at different time points (1, 2, and 3 minutes). In cases where patients could not tolerate preoxygenation for the full 3 minutes, shorter durations were still proved effective. Notably, 80.2% of participants achieved an FeO2 of 0.9 within 2 minutes. Similarly, Morrison et al. reported that 89.6% of pediatric patients reached an FeO2 of 0.9 within 100 seconds (25). In their study, the median time to achieve an FeO2 of 0.9 for children under 5 years old was approximately 40–50 seconds. However, their results may overestimate effectiveness, as patients who failed to reach an FeO2 of 0.9 within 2 minutes were excluded from the analysis. In emergency situations or with completely uncooperative children, even 1 minute of preoxygenation yielded measurable benefits. In our study, the median FeO2 after 1 minute was 0.86 (IQR, 0.77, 0.90), approaching the preoxygenation target and contributing to the prolongation of safe apneic time during intubation. Overall, we emphasize the critical importance of achieving effective preoxygenation, particularly in high-risk populations susceptible to rapid desaturation. This is especially relevant for patients undergoing rapid sequence induction, including young infants. In such cases, ensuring optimal preoxygenation is essential to maximize oxygen reserves and improve patient safety.

Although short-term administration of 100% oxygen during preoxygenation is a standard practice to increase safety by prolonging the apneic period during intubation, routine preoxygenation is not recommended in healthy pediatric patients without respiratory compromise or aspiration risk (e.g., a full stomach). In such cases, preoxygenation may unnecessarily increase anxiety and may contribute to atelectasis (26,27). This study has several limitations. First, preoxygenation was conducted following intravenous sedation in young children, which likely resulted in greater compliance compared to non-sedated pediatric populations. Consequently, preoxygenation in non-sedated children may be less effective than reported in this study. Second, this study specifically focused on infants and toddlers under the age of 7 years, and as such, the findings cannot be generalized to pediatric population of all ages. Third, preoxygenation in this study was carried out using a circle circuit and anesthesia machine. The FeO2 levels achieved may differ when using other equipment such as non-rebreathing mask or BVM. Future research should explore preoxygenation strategies prior to intubation in settings where anesthesia machines are not readily available.

Conclusions

Preoxygenation with spontaneous tidal breathing for 3 minutes effectively achieves an FeO2 ≥0.9 in 91.3% of young pediatric patients. While age did not significantly impact the time required to reach the target FeO2, better compliance was associated with faster preoxygenation. These findings underscore the importance of ensuring an optimal mask fit and encouraging patient cooperation to maximize the effectiveness of preoxygenation in young children.
