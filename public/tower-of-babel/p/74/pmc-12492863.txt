A rare case of supernumerary and ectopic parathyroid adenoma in the parotid gland: diagnostic and surgical challenges

Introduction

Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is the third most common endocrine disorder and the leading cause of hypercalcemia in outpatient settings. It results from elevated levels of parathyroid hormone (PTH), which disrupts calcium homeostasis. The sporadic form of PHPT accounts for 90–95% of cases, whereas hereditary forms—most notably multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN-1)—constitute the remaining 5–10%, with MEN-1 responsible for over 80% of hereditary cases. The estimated incidence is approximately 20 cases per 100,000 individuals per year, with a prevalence between 0.1% and 0.4%. PHPT is most frequently diagnosed in women over the age of 55, typically in the seventh decade of life [1].

Approximately 85–90% of PHPT cases are caused by solitary benign parathyroid adenomas. In contrast, multiple adenomas or parathyroid hyperplasia account for 15–20%, while parathyroid carcinoma remains rare, comprising only about 1% of cases. Recent evidence supports the classification of double adenomas (DAs) as a distinct clinical entity rather than a variant of four-gland hyperplasia. These have been identified in up to 15% of PHPT cases [2, 3].

Ectopic parathyroid adenomas occur in an estimated 6–16% of patients, while those arising from supernumerary glands are reported in approximately 5% of cases. The co-occurrence of ectopic and supernumerary adenomas is exceedingly rare, with a prevalence of around 0.69% [4].

These atypical presentations are primarily attributed to abnormal embryological migration of the parathyroid glands. In adults, the typical anatomical configuration comprises four glands—two superior and two inferior. The superior glands originate from the fourth branchial pouch and migrate to the posterior aspect of the upper thyroid pole by the seventh week of gestation. In contrast, the inferior glands arise from the third branchial pouch and descend alongside the thymus to the lower thyroid pole, crossing the superior glands during this process. While ectopic parathyroid tissue is most often found in locations such as the mediastinum or retropharyngeal space, its occurrence in the parotid gland is exceedingly rare, with only a limited number of cases documented in the literature [5–7].

The clinical presentation of PHPT varies widely, ranging from overtly symptomatic forms with complications such as pathological fractures, fibrous osteitis, and nephrolithiasis, to asymptomatic cases discovered incidentally through routine biochemical screening [8].

Given its prevalence and potential for serious complications, timely recognition and effective management of PHPT are critical to improving patient outcomes. This report describes a rare case of PHPT associated with double parathyroid adenomas, one of which was both ectopic and supernumerary.

Case report

A 64-year-old Italian woman was referred to our endocrine surgery outpatient clinic with suspected primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT). She reported symptoms of asthenia, diffuse joint pain, recurrent nephrolithiasis, and clinically significant osteopenia with coexisting osteoporosis. Her medical history included well-controlled arterial hypertension, dyslipidemia, hemorrhoidal disease, and documented food allergies. Physical examination revealed no abnormalities in the head and neck region, and both chest and abdominal evaluations were unremarkable.

Initial laboratory investigations demonstrated hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, vitamin D deficiency, and markedly elevated PTH levels (276 pg/mL). A thyroid ultrasound failed to identify pathological parathyroid tissue. Subsequently, a 99mTc-MIBI parathyroid scintigraphy was performed, which excluded functioning parathyroid tissue in typical anatomical locations. However, mild radiotracer uptake was observed in the right parotid region and the left submandibular area (Fig. 1A-B).

Preoperative Imaging Findings Suggestive of Ectopic Parathyroid Tissue. (A) 99mTc-MIBI parathyroid scintigraphy showing mild radiotracer uptake in the right parotid region (arrow). (B) Additional uptake observed in the left submandibular region, initially raising suspicion for pathological parathyroid tissue

To further investigate these atypical findings, a contrast-enhanced neck MRI was conducted. The imaging ruled out pathological tissue in the submandibular region but suggested a well-circumscribed adenoma measuring 10 mm in diameter within the right parotid gland. This adenoma was located in the superolateral portion of the glandular body, appeared hypointense on T1-weighted sequences, and exhibited marked contrast enhancement following administration of a paramagnetic contrast agent.

Given the unusual location and imaging characteristics, suspicion arose for ectopic parathyroid tissue. The patient was subsequently referred for an 18 F-fluorocholine PET/CT scan to confirm the diagnosis and guide surgical planning. The scan demonstrated radiotracer uptake consistent with two hyperfunctioning adenomas: (1) a nodular area of increased radiotracer uptake located posteroinferior to the lower third of the right thyroid lobe, adjacent to the thyroid parenchyma—undetected by prior imaging; and (2) focal fluorocholine accumulation corresponding to a hyperdense 7 mm nodule in the right parotid region.

Multidisciplinary surgical approach to double parathyroid adenomas

Following radiological confirmation of double parathyroid adenomas—one of which was l a supernumerary ectopic adenoma—the patient underwent a combined surgical procedure involving both the Endocrine Surgery and Maxillofacial Surgery Departments.

A preoperative baseline intact parathyroid hormone (PTH) level was obtained, measuring 202.3 pg/mL. The first stage of the operation, conducted by the endocrine surgery team, involved cervical exploration. All four parathyroid glands were identified in their typical anatomical locations. The right inferior gland displayed pathological characteristics consistent with an adenoma and was subsequently excised (Fig. 2A-B).

(B) Gross appearance of the excised right inferior adenoma, confirming its pathological features. (C) Intraoperative image of the superficial portion of the right parotid gland before enucleoresection. (D) Enucleated supernumerary ectopic parathyroid adenoma located within the right parotid gland, consistent with a supernumerary gland. (E) Histological section (Hematoxylin and Eosin stain, original magnification 100×) of the ectopic parathyroid adenoma. The lesion consists of a well-circumscribed nodular proliferation of chief cells with a follicular growth pattern (lower left), surrounded by unremarkable parotid gland parenchyma (upper and right sides of the field), confirming its ectopic intraparotid location

Intraoperative monitoring of intact PTH levels, measured 20 min after the excision of the right inferior parathyroid adenoma, demonstrated a reduction of over 30%, with levels decreasing to 133 pg/mL—supporting the successful removal of hyperfunctioning tissue.

In the second stage of the procedure, the maxillofacial surgery team performed an enucleoresection of the upper pole of the right parotid gland, targeting the superficially located ectopic adenoma (Fig. 2C-D). Intraoperative neurostimulation confirmed the integrity and normal function of the facial nerve throughout the dissection.

A subsequent PTH measurement taken 20 min after the removal of the second adenoma showed a further decline to 67.8 pg/mL, confirming biochemical remission. The surgery proceeded without any intraoperative or postoperative complications.

Postoperatively, the patient’s serum calcium levels normalized, and she was discharged on the first postoperative day in stable condition. Histopathological examination confirmed the presence of two distinct parathyroid adenomas, one of which was located within ectopic and supernumerary parathyroid tissue. At long-term follow-up, the patient reported complete resolution of symptoms and significant improvement in overall clinical status.

Histological examination of the right inferior parathyroid gland revealed an adenoma characterized by a predominantly follicular growth pattern. The ectopic adenoma excised from the right parotid gland displayed a nodular, well-encapsulated proliferation of chief cells, also consistent with a parathyroid adenoma (Fig. 2E). The surrounding salivary parenchyma appeared unremarkable, with no significant histopathological alterations. Two nearby lymph nodes exhibited reactive hyperplasia, and no malignant features were identified. These findings confirmed the diagnosis of a supernumerary parathyroid adenoma in an ectopic intraparotid location. Although the histomorphological features were consistent with a parathyroid adenoma and the biochemical findings (elevated serum PTH and hypercalcemia) provided strong diagnostic confirmation, the absence of immunohistochemical staining for PTH or GATA3 represents a limitation. Such markers would have further reinforced diagnostic certainty, particularly given the rarity of an intraparotid location. Nevertheless, the integration of clinical, biochemical, imaging, and histopathological evidence provides a reliable basis for the diagnosis in this case.

Accurate preoperative imaging is essential for selecting the most appropriate surgical strategy in patients with primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT), particularly in cases involving multiglandular disease or ectopic adenomas. The primary goal of imaging is to localize hyperfunctioning parathyroid tissue with high precision.

Currently, the most commonly employed modalities include neck ultrasound (US) and [99mTc] Tc-sestamibi (MIBI) scintigraphy. Neck US is non-invasive, cost-effective, and widely available, with a sensitivity of up to 85%, although this varies significantly (60–95%) depending on gland size and operator expertise. Its diagnostic yield decreases markedly in multiglandular disease (approximately 35%) and is particularly limited in identifying ectopic glands or those obscured by thyroid nodules. Moreover, normal-sized parathyroid glands are often not visualized at all.

[99mTc]Tc-MIBI scintigraphy is another widely used technique that relies on differential uptake in mitochondria-rich oxyphil cells within hyperfunctioning glands. Its sensitivity ranges from 69 to 91%, offering the advantage of detecting ectopic or intrathyroidal parathyroid tissue, which US may miss. However, it cannot reliably identify normal glands due to their small size and is subject to false positives. Tracer uptake can also occur in non-parathyroidal tissues such as follicular or Hürthle cell thyroid neoplasms, thyroiditis, and reactive lymph nodes, which may complicate interpretation [4, 9].

More recently, [18 F]fluorocholine positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) has emerged as a highly sensitive imaging modality, with a reported sensitivity of up to 96%. This technique offers superior spatial resolution and functional imaging capability, particularly in detecting ectopic or supernumerary glands. It also exposes patients to lower radiation doses compared to MIBI scintigraphy. Some authors advocate for its use as a first-line investigation, especially in complex cases or when conventional imaging is inconclusive. However, due to its higher cost and limited availability, others recommend reserving it for second-line evaluation [10].

Despite this debate, [18 F]fluorocholine PET/CT has demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance in challenging scenarios, such as reoperative neck surgery, multiglandular disease, and rare ectopic locations. Its ability to provide both anatomical and functional detail makes it an invaluable adjunct in the surgical planning of PHPT.

Given the critical role of precise localization in surgical planning, the identification of ectopic parathyroid tissue remains challenging. To date, reports of intraparotid parathyroid tissue are exceedingly scarce. Our structured review of PubMed and Google Scholar identified only one previously documented case, described by Gregoire-Ferriol et al., in which heterotopic parathyroid tissue was discovered within the parotid gland in association with a pleomorphic adenoma, and notably without biochemical evidence of primary hyperparathyroidism. In contrast, our case represents the first reported instance of a functioning supernumerary intraparotid parathyroid adenoma causing PHPT.

Conclusion

This case highlights the critical role of [18 F] fluorocholine PET/CT in the diagnostic workup and surgical management of primary hyperparathyroidism, particularly when parathyroid tissue is located in ectopic or supernumerary sites. The integration of this advanced imaging modality enabled precise localization of both adenomas, one of which resided in the rare parotid region, and facilitated a successful, tailored, multidisciplinary surgical approach.

By enhancing diagnostic accuracy, [18 F]fluorocholine PET/CT supports informed surgical decision-making, reduces the risk of missed adenomas, and contributes to improved patient outcomes. Its incorporation into the diagnostic algorithm is especially beneficial in anatomically complex or recurrent cases, reinforcing its value as a powerful tool in the modern management of parathyroid disorders.

While immunohistochemical confirmation (e.g., PTH or GATA3 staining) would have provided additional support to the diagnosis, the combination of biochemical, imaging, intraoperative, and histopathological findings in our case offered reliable confirmation of this rare intraparotid parathyroid adenoma.
