Cetrorelix suppresses the dominant follicle and synchronizes follicular waves and ovulation in cattle†
Authors: Dylan R Farmer, Carlos E P Leonardi, Gregg P Adams, Jennifer Campbell, John L O Oliveira, Jaswant Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo determine the effects of a GnRH antagonist on ovarian function in cattle, we tested the hypotheses that cetrorelix will (1) cause regression of the extant dominant follicle by altering gonadotrophin secretion and (2) induce a new follicular wave emergence at a consistent time after treatment. In Experiment 1, heifers were given 1.5 mg cetrorelix im on Days 1–2 (Day 0 = wave emergence), Days 3–4, and Days 6–7, or normal saline (Control, n = 8 per group). The dominant follicle was smaller and regressed earlier in groups treated with cetrorelix on Days 1–2 or Days 3–4 than in Controls (P = 0.01). Plasma LH concentrations were lower (P = 0.04) for 3 days after treatment, and the pre-wave surge in FSH occurred earlier in groups treated with cetrorelix on Days 1–2 or Days 3–4 than Controls (P = 0.01). Corpus luteum diameter was smaller (P = 0.01) in the cetrorelix groups, but luteal vascularity, life-span, or progesterone production was not affected. In Experiment 2, heifers were treated with a single 3 mg dose of cetrorelix on Day 1, 3, or 6, or normal saline (Control, n = 8 per group), given prostaglandin F2a and inseminated. Wave emergence occurred synchronously 3.6 ± 0.3 days after cetrorelix treatment, ovulations occurred 12.4 ± 0.3 days later, and 13/23 heifers became pregnant. In Experiment 3, cetrorelix given at random days of the cycle induced a new wave 3.4 ± 0.3 days later and wave emergence was more synchronous than in saline-treated cows (Barlett test P = 0.04). Results supported hypotheses and provide rationale for the development of new cetrorelix-based protocols for breeding management in cattle.Keywords: cetrorelix, dominant follicle, follicular waves, Fsh, Gnrh antagonist, Lh, ovulation, synchronization, wave emergence

Introduction

Ovarian follicles in cattle develop in a wave-like pattern [1]. Periodic surges in circulating concentrations of follicle stimulating hormone (FSH) initiate the growth of a cohort of small follicles (wave emergence) which grow at a similar rate for the first 2–3 days [2]. The cohort produce inhibin and other factors that suppress circulating FSH concentrations, resulting in the regression or cessation of growth of all (subordinate follicles) but one (dominant) follicle [3–5]. Selective growth of the dominant follicle in the face of declining FSH concentrations is enabled by the acquisition of LH receptors [6–8]. After selection, estradiol production by the dominant follicle becomes the primary FSH suppressor, thus preventing emergence of the next follicular wave [1, 9]. The fate of the dominant follicle is mediated by modulations in the pulsatile secretion of LH. The suppressive effect of progesterone on LH secretion during the luteal phase results in regression of the dominant follicle [10], whereas declining progesterone coupled with rising plasma estradiol concentrations after luteolysis results in an increase in LH pulse-frequency, and ultimately the pre-ovulatory LH surge and ovulation.

Effective fixed-time artificial insemination (FTAI) protocols require the synchronization of follicular growth, luteolysis, and ovulation to enable insemination by appointment; thus eliminating the need for the detection of estrus. Treatment with prostaglandin F2α (PGF) induces luteolysis in cattle [11–13]; however, the ensuing ovulation will occur over a 6-day period depending on the status of the dominant follicle at the time of treatment [14]. Hence, modern synchronization protocols have focussed on inducing a new follicular wave to refine ovulation synchronization in a large group of animals to within hours, and thereby, enable pre-scheduled (or fixed-time) artificial insemination.

Early synchronization protocols for FTAI were based on the use of GnRH analogs to synchronize follicular wave emergence by inducing ovulation of an existing dominant follicle [15–17]. However, the effectiveness of the protocol hinged on the presence of an ovulatory follicle at the time of GnRH treatment [18]. Therefore, a pre-synchronization step was implemented [18], and the protocol is used world-wide in dairy cattle. The advantage of pre-synchronization is a more consistent progesterone concentration but results in a longer and more labour-intensive protocol with increased animal handlings [19]. GnRH-based protocols continue to be refined, and several newer studies question the need for an initial pre-synchronization when the protocol is used in beef cattle [20, 21]. At about the same time when GnRH protocols were being explored, a protocol based on the use of estradiol and progesterone was developed in 1995 to induce regression of the extant dominant follicle with subsequent emergence of the next wave [22–25]; i.e., was not dependent on the presence of a large ovulatory follicle. The steroid-based protocol has since become the de-facto protocol for FTAI across South America. More than 11.5 million beef and dairy cows are artificially inseminated each year [26] in Brazil alone utilizing this protocol. However, due to increasing consumer sensitivity to possible deleterious effects of estrogens in food and in the environment [27, 28], use of estrogens in food-producing animals was first banned in the European Union in 2003 [29], followed by New Zealand and Australia [29], Canada and the US [30], and most recently in some South American countries [31].

In the present study, the effects of a GnRH antagonist, cetrorelix acetate, were examined to determine the potential of a non-steroidal method for elective control of ovarian function in cattle. GnRH antagonists competitively bind to GnRH receptors in gonadotrophs of the adenohypophysis, thereby suppressing the secretion of gonadotrophins [32, 33] as well as temporarily down-regulating hypophyseal GnRH receptors and its mRNA [34, 35]. In comparison to GnRH sequence (pGlu1-His2-Trp3-Ser4-Tyr5-Gly6-Leu7-Arg8-Pro9-Gly10-NH2), 5 amino acids at position 1, 2, 3, 6 and 10 are replaced by modified D-amino acids in Cetrorelix (D-Nal1-D-Phe2-D-Pal3-Ser4-Tyr5-D-Cit6-Leu7-Arg8-Pro9-D-Ala10) that facilitate competitive inhibition, downregulation of LHRH membrane receptors, stability at pH 7.0, as well as resistance to degrading enzymes (chymotrypsin, pronase, subtilisin) and oxidative attack by H2O2 [34, 36, 37]. Cetrorelix is categorized as a third generation GnRH antagonist and has been used in assisted reproductive therapy in women to inhibit premature ovulation by delaying the LH surge. Compared to previous antagonists, third generation GnRH antagonists have a substitution of D-Cit at position 6 which increases solubility receptor affinity, while inducing little or no anaphylactic response [38, 39]. Cetrorelix was selected over other antagonists because it is fast-acting; peak plasma concentrations were reached within 1–2 hr in women after a single subcutaneous treatment, and the median terminal half-life ranged from 5 to 10 hr [40]. A dose of 10 μg/kg body weight of cetrorelix suppressed LH pulse-frequency in cattle for 1 day while a dose of 100 μg/kg body weight reduced LH-pulse frequency for 7 days [41], however, the effects on dominant follicle and wave emergence were not examined. In a previous study in cattle [42], cetrorelix inhibited the LH-releasing effects of kisspeptin. An incidental finding of this study was the regression of the preovulatory follicle in all heifers.

To answer the question “can a GnRH antagonist be used to precisely control ovarian function?” experiments were done using a bovine model to determine the effects of cetrorelix on follicular, luteal, and hormonal dynamics. In Experiment 1, we tested the hypothesis that cetrorelix treatment over 2 days will inhibit gonadotrophin release sufficiently to suppress the extant dominant follicle during prescribed viable phases, but not sufficiently to alter the form and function of the corpus luteum (CL). With affirmative results, the objective of Experiment 2 was to determine the efficacy of a single dose of cetrorelix to synchronize ovarian function among animals sufficiently to enable the use of artificial insemination. Experiment 3 was done to test the synchrony of wave emergence when cetrorelix treatment is given at random (unknown) days of the cycle in postpartum cows.

Experiment 1—effect of a 2-day cetrorelix protocol on ovarian function

Angus x Hereford heifers (Bos taurus, n = 32), 13–14 months of age (13.3 ± 0.3 months) and weighing >300 kg (384.3 ± 6.0 kg) were chosen from a herd of 71 heifers maintained in outdoor corrals. An initial transrectal ultrasound examination was performed to detect the presence of a CL for selecting heifers that were pubertal and cycling [43]. The selection criteria was based on uniform age and body weight by eliminating the youngest and lightest from the group. Heifers were fed alfalfa/grass hay and barley to gain approximately 1.0 kg per head per day and had water ad libitum during the experimental period.

The ovaries of heifers were examined daily by transrectal ultrasonography using a 5–10 MHz linear-array transrectal transducer (MyLab Alpha, Esoate North America Inc., Fishers, IN, US) and standardized settings (power 100%, B-mode gain 60, color-flow Doppler gain 45%) to monitor follicular and luteal dynamics during the study period. The day of follicle wave emergence was determined retrospectively as the day when the dominant follicle was first detected at 4–5 mm with a concurrent increase in the number of 2–4 mm follicles. Dominant follicle acquires ovulatory capacity at 9–10 mm size [44, 45], therefore, ovulation was defined as the sudden disappearance of a large follicle between successive ultrasound examinations followed by detection of a CL at that location in the ovary. The dominant follicle was identified retrospectively as the largest follicle of the follicular wave [46], and luteal form and function was assessed by diameter, vascular flow, and plasma progesterone concentrations. The onset of follicle and CL regression was defined as the day before two consecutive decreases in their diameter were recorded.

To facilitate experimental logistics, the experiment was done in two replicates (n = 16 heifers per replicate). Heifers at an unknown day of the estrous cycle, but with a CL, were given two doses of a PGF analog (500 μg cloprostenol, Estrumate, Merck Animal Health, Desoto, KS, USA) im at an interval of 12 hr to induce luteolysis and ovulation. Ovarian ultrasonography was done daily for one interovulatory interval (IOI, days between consecutive ovulations), and again on Days 1, 3, 7, and 10 of the next IOI. During each ultrasound examination, the diameter and location of follicles ≥2 mm as well as the CL were hand-drawn, digital cine-loops (10–20 s duration) of each ovary were recorded, and color-flow Doppler imaging was used to grade the degree of CL vascularity (Supplementary Figure 1). Follicles were individually identified and tracked retrospectively from the day of ovulation. One person (D. Farmer) evaluated all ultrasound videos and ovarian maps.

When ovulation was first detected (Day 0, also taken as the day of emergence of the first follicular wave [47]), each heifer was assigned to one of four treatment groups using a systematic random sampling procedure (Figure 1A). Heifers (n = 8 per group) were given two doses of cetrorelix (1.5 mg per treatment) im 24 hr apart starting on Day 1 (Cetro1–2; early growing phase dominant follicle), Day 3 (Cetro3–4; mid-growing phase dominant follicle), and Day 6 (Cetro6–7; early static phase dominant follicle), or a similar volume of normal saline im [Control; heifers randomized to be treated on Days 1–2 (n = 3), 3–4 (n = 2) or 6–7 (n = 3)]. The dose of cetrorelix and route of administration were selected based on the results of a previous study in llamas [48], approximating a dose of 10 μg/kg body weight. Considering the range of body weights of heifers in Experiment 1, a total dose of 3 mg cetrorelix was equivalent to 5.9 to 9.3 μg cetrorelix per kg body weight. Cetrorelix acetate (U6114GD1903/PE3150, manufactured by GenScript in May 2021; amino acid sequence: D-Nal1-D-Phe2-D-Pal3-Ser4-Tyr5-D-Cit6-Leu7-Arg8-Pro9-D-Ala10) was stored at −20°C and was compounded from lyophilized power under aseptic conditions in a biosafety cabinet. Cetrorelix acetate (50 mg vial) was first dissolved in 100 μl DMSO and the solution was added slowly to 100 ml of 5% D-mannitol in ultra-pure water to prepare a solution containing 0.5 mg cetrorelix per ml. The solution was filtered through a 0.2 μm syringe filter, packaged in treatment vials and stored at 4°C for a maximum of one month before use. An aqueous solution of cetrorelix at neutral pH is stable at room temperature for 21 days and resistant to H2O2 oxidative attack [37].

Daily blood samples for hormone analysis were collected in heparinized tubes (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) by jugular venipuncture from Day 0 to Day 13, as well as Days 1, 3, 7, and 10 of the next IOI. Frequent blood sampling was done every 30 min for 120 min beginning immediately before the second cetrorelix treatment. Analogous samples were collected from Control heifers on Days 2, 4, and 7 (n = 8 on each day) for comparison. Within 30 min of each blood collection, samples were centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min and the plasma was stored at −20°C until assay.

Hormone analyses

Plasma LH and FSH concentrations in Experiment 1 were measured at the Animal and Dairy Sciences laboratory at the University of Wisconsin-Madison using a validated radioimmunoassay [50, 51] with some modifications [52]. The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation for LH, respectively, were 9.2% and 12.1% for diestrus samples, and 7.3% and 13.8% for estrus samples. The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation for FSH, respectively, were 5.3% and 5.6% for diestrus samples, and 3.2% and 3.0% for estrus samples. Progesterone concentrations were measured using a commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (cat. no. ab108670, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). The assay was performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were 8.7% and 10.3%, respectively.

Experiment 2—effect of a single-dose cetrorelix protocol for synchronization and insemination

Sexually mature Braford (3/8 Brahman × 5/8 Hereford) heifers (Bos indicus x Bos taurus), 24 months of age (n = 32) were selected from a herd of 110 heifers maintained on pasture with water and salt ad libitum during the experimental period. Only post-pubertal heifers (i.e., with the presence of a CL) were included in the experiment; body weights were not recorded.

The design was similar to Experiment 1 except that treatment groups were given a single dose of 3 mg cetrorelix im (Figure 1B). Heifers at an unknown day of estrous cycle, but with a CL, were given two doses of PGF (500 μg cloprostenol, Ciosin, MSD, Friesoythe, Germany) im at an interval of 12 hr to induce luteolysis and ovulation. Heifers were examined daily by transrectal ultrasonography with a 7.5 MHz linear-array transducer (Mindray Z50, Mindray Medical International Limited, Shenzhen, China) as described in Experiment 1. When ovulation was detected, heifers were assigned randomly to one of four treatment groups (n = 8 per group) and given cetrorelix on Day 1 (Cetro1; Day 0 = day of ovulation and emergence of the first follicular wave), Day 3 (Cetro3) or Day 6 (Cetro6), or normal saline [Control; heifers randomized to be treated on Days 1 (n = 3), 3 (n = 2), or 6 (n = 3)]. Gray-scale and color-flow Doppler ultrasonography cine-loops of both ovaries were recorded daily from Day 0 until the next ovulation. Two luteolytic doses of PGF (Ciosin, MSD) were given 12 hr apart to cetrorelix-treated heifers 9 days after cetrorelix treatment, and tail paint was applied and observed at 12 hrs intervals to confirm detection of estrus. Artificial insemination (AI) using frozen semen was performed by one person (C. Leonardi) 12 hr after the detection of estrus, if estrus was not detected then heifers were not inseminated. Only one AI was performed per heifer and frozen semen from one bull was used for all AI. Pregnancy diagnosis was performed by ultrasonography 30 days after artificial insemination by confirming the presence of an embryo with a heartbeat.

Vascularity assessment of the corpus luteum

In Experiments 1 and 2, luteal blood flow was recorded by color-flow Doppler imaging using standardized machine settings. After each experiment, CL cine-loops were anonymized, and the degree of vascularity was assessed based on a previous study [53] and scored subjectively into one of three grades: Grade 1 (lowest vascularity) similar to CL blood flow 48 hr post PGF treatment, Grade 2 (medium vascularity) similar to CL blood flow 24 hr post PGF treatment, and Grade 3 (highest vascularity) similar to a healthy CL during diestrus (Supplementary Figure 1).

Experiment 3—effect of a single-dose cetrorelix protocol at random days of the cycle

Lactating (suckled) Braford cows (Bos indicus x Bos taurus) between 45 and 75 days post-partum and 3 to 8 years of age (n = 16) were maintained on pasture with water and salt ad libitum; body weights were not recorded. The reproductive status of the cows (i.e., cycling or noncycling, day of the ovulatory cycle, day of wave emergence) was not known at the start of experiment. An initial ultrasound examination was done on the first day of the experiment to record the size of the largest follicle in each ovary and the presence or absence of a functional CL (>10 mm with a vascularity score of 3 based on Supplementary Figure 1). Cows were randomized so that the proportion of cows with a functional CL was the same between Cetro and Control groups (n = 4 with CL and n = 4 without CL per group). Cows in the Cetro group were given 3 mg cetrorelix im and those in the Control group were given an equivalent volume of normal saline (6 ml) im. A progesterone-releasing device (CIDR containing 1.9 g progesterone, Zoetis Ltd., Campinas, Brazil) was placed intravaginally to prevent ovulations during the observation period. The device was removed 8 days later and cows were given a luteolytic dose of PGF (Ciosin, MSD) to collect data for the purpose of another experiment. Cows were examined daily by transrectal ultrasonography with a 7.5 MHz linear-array transducer (Mindray Z50, Mindray Medical International Limited, Shenzhen, China) as described in Experiment 1 from the day of treatment for 9 days. The day of wave emergence was determined retrospectively as in Experiment 1.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Analysis System software package (learning edition, version 9.4 SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Single point measurements (e.g., largest diameter of dominant follicle, interval from cetrorelix treatment to next wave emergence or ovulation) were compared by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), Kruskal-Wallis Test, T-test, or T-test with unequal variances (Welch test) as appropriate. Close clustering of the timing of follicle wave emergence within groups (i.e., less spread) was defined as “synchrony of wave emergence” and was assessed by examining the magnitude of variance among treatment groups by Barlett test of homogeneity of variance. The dataset that passed the Barlett test of homogeneity of variance and normality of residuals (Kolmogorov–Smirnov test), ANOVA was performed to detect differences in means among groups. Proportional data were analyzed using Chi-square or Fisher exact test. If main effects or their interaction of an ANOVA test were significant (P ≤ 0.05), Tukey post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed. P-values between 0.05 to 0.1 were considered a tendency for a difference. All values are reported as mean ± SEM.

Repeated measures data (e.g., daily diameter of the dominant follicle or CL, daily plasma concentration of FSH and LH) were analyzed using the linear mixed models procedure (Proc MIXED) specifying fixed factors (Treatment and Day), random factor (Replicate), repeated factor (Day) and subject (animal ID). The initial analysis included 12 covariance structures [(SIMPLE, AR(1), ARH(1), CS, CSH, SP(POW), TOEP, TOEP(1), ANTE(1), UN, UN(1), or HF)] and the model with the lowest corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc) was selected for the final analysis. If main effects or their interaction differed (P ≤ 0.05), Tukey post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed. All values are reported as mean ± SEM. For comparison of repeated measures among groups, data were centralized to the day of wave emergence or the day of treatment, as required.

Vascularity grades were treated as nominal data (it was assumed the distance between categories was unequal but ranking order from lowest to highest was accurate) and analyzed by non-parametric and generalized linear mixed model procedure (Proc Glimmix) by specifying fixed factors (Treatment and Day), random factor (Replicate) and subject (animal ID). All values are reported as median.

Experiment 1—effect of a 2-day cetrorelix protocol on ovarian function

Follicular dynamics

Experiment 1 was designed to compare ovarian function when heifers were treated with cetrorelix during the early growing (pre-selection) phase (Cetro1–2), mid-growing (selection) phase (Cetro3–4) or early static phase (Cetro6–7) of the dominant follicle of Wave 1 (Table 1). One heifer from Cetro1–2 group was removed from the study due to failure to accurately identify the day of ovulation (Day 0). The proportion of 3-wave IOI vs. 2-wave IOI was different among treatment groups (P = 0.01); Cetro6–7 had the greatest proportion of 2-wave IOI (n = 7/8; P = 0.01), and Cetro1–2 had the greatest proportion of 3-wave IOI (n = 6/7). Specific data regarding 2-wave and 3-wave IOI are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Changes in number of ≥2 mm follicles over days during the IOI or during the post-treatment waves did not differ between the groups (Supplementary Figure 2). Plasma concentrations of gonadotrophins

Plasma LH concentrations were lower (P < 0.04) for 3 days following cetrorelix treatment (groups combined) than in the Control group, but no treatment or treatment-by-day interaction was detected in plasma FSH concentrations (Figure 3J, K). In direct group comparisons with the Control group, treatment-by-day interactions were detected in plasma LH concentration for each cetrorelix group, attributed to a decrease (P < 0.05) within the first or second day after treatment (Figure 3A, D, G). Conversely, treatment-by-day interactions in plasma FSH concentrations (P < 0.01) were attributed to an earlier rise in the Cetro1–2 and Cetro3–4 groups vs the Control group (Figure 3B, E). From frequent plasma sampling (every 30 min for 120 min from the time of second treatment) maximum, minimum, and mean LH concentrations were more than two-fold lower (P < 0.01) in cetrorelix groups compared to the Control group (Figure 4G). FSH concentrations in frequent plasma samples did not differ between the cetrorelix groups and the Control group. Form and function of the corpus luteum

One heifer (Cetro1–2) was removed from CL analysis because a luteal cyst was present before the start of the study that may have affected the CL present during the study period. The day-to-day diameter of the CL was smaller in Cetro6–7, Cetro3–4, and Cetro 1–2 groups than in Controls (P < 0.01, Supplementary Figure 5A). However, vascularity grades of the CL did not differ between the Control group and treatment groups (Supplementary Figure 5B). No effect of treatment or treatment-by-day interaction was detected for the diameter profile of the CL of the following IOI (data not shown). Plasma progesterone concentrations did not differ between the Control group and cetrorelix-treated heifers groups for 7 days following treatment (Figure 3) or on Day 10 of the post-treatment IOI (P = 0.51).

Experiment 2—effect of a single-dose cetrorelix protocol for synchronization and insemination

Follicular dynamics

Two heifers (one each from Control and Cetro3) were removed because they became lame during the study. A treatment-by-day interaction in the day-to-day diameter of the dominant follicle of Wave 1 was attributed to a progressively smaller profile in Cetro3 and Cetro1 groups than in the Control group (P < 0.01, Figure 5A); Cetro6 and the Control group did not differ. The maximum diameter of the dominant follicle was smaller (P < 0.01) and occurred earlier (P < 0.01) in the Cetro1 and Cetro3 groups than the Control group. No effect of treatment or interaction was detected on the dynamics of the dominant follicle of next wave after treatment (Figure 5A).

On the day of PGF treatment (9 days after cetrorelix treatment), the dominant follicle was 11.5 ± 0.5 mm and did not differ among cetrorelix groups.

Luteal dynamics

The day-to-day diameter of the CL did not differ among groups until the time of PGF treatment, nor did the CL vascularity score (Supplementary Figure 6A, B).

Ovulation and pregnancy rates

A greater proportion of heifers ovulated in response to PGF in the Cetro3 and Cetro6 groups (n = 6/7, n = 8/8, respectively) than the Cetro1 group (n = 4/8, Fisher exact test P = 0.04). All heifers in the Control Group (n = 7/7) ovulated within 23 days, which aligns with the expected length for 3-wave interovulatory intervals [54, 55]. There was no difference in the natural ovulation rate in the Control group vs. cetrorelix-treated heifers (combined n = 18/23, P = 0.30) but results should be interpreted cautiously due to low number of heifers in the Control group. The interval between PGF treatment and ovulation was similar among cetrorelix groups (Cetro1, Cetro3, and Cetro6 combined = 3.4 ± 0.3 days; P = 0.11). Pregnancy rate per AI were also similar (Control n = 3/5 vs. cetrorelix n = 13/21).

Comparison of results of Experiments 1 and 2

The two experiments were done at separate locations but provide an opportunity to retrospectively examine the combined data from cetrorelix groups (i.e., excluding the Control groups). For the purposes of this comparison, the first day of cetrorelix treatment in Experiment 1 was used as the group reference point. Overall, the number of cetrorelix treatments did not alter the maximum diameter of the extant dominant follicle among analogous groups (Cetro1–2 vs. Cetro1, P = 0.46; Cetro3–4 vs. Cetro3, P = 0.86; Cetro6–7 vs. Cetro6, P = 0.88). Compared to two treatments, a single larger dose of cetrorelix resulted in a shorter interval to new wave emergence when given on Day 1 (P < 0.01) and Day 3 (P < 0.01), but not on Day 6 (P = 0.29). However, the synchrony was similar between multiple vs. single treatments (Barlett test, P = 0.19).

Discussion

The aim of the study was to determine the effects of the GnRH antagonist, cetrorelix acetate, on dominant follicle regression, the pattern of gonadotrophin secretion, and the emergence of a new follicular wave in cattle. Results documented a progressive decrease in size and shortening of the life-plan of the growing phase dominant follicles after treatment. It is noteworthy that 2 doses of cetrorelix given either during the growing or early static phase of the dominant follicle lowered plasma LH concentrations for 3 days. The effect of cetrorelix treatment on circulating FSH concentrations was indirect. The suppression of LH caused the premature regression of the extant dominant follicle which subsequently hastened the rebound in plasma FSH concentrations to elicit the early emergence of a new follicular wave. A new follicular wave emerged between 3.6 and 5.8 days after the first cetrorelix treatment in Experiment 1. The results of Experiment 2 were consistent with Experiment 1: a single dose of cetrorelix induced regression of the extant growing phase dominant follicles in heifers, and new wave emergence occurred synchronously 3.6 days after cetrorelix treatment. Importantly, a single 3 mg dose of cetrorelix given to postpartum cows on random days of the cycle (Experiment 3) also elicited new wave emergence synchronously 3.4 days later. To maximize the chances of conception, we opted to time artificial insemination based on detection of estrus followed by ultrasonographic confirmation of ovulation. This approach allowed to ascertain whether cetrorelix has any deleterious effect on oocyte competence and to optimize timing for the development of a cetrorelix-based FTAI protocol. Future studies with a greater number of animals are warranted to determine the pregnancy rate of such estrogen-free, simple, and short protocol.

Around the time of selection, the dominant follicle acquires LH receptors [8] and begins to utilize LH to maintain its growth and support estradiol secretion in a low FSH milieu [5, 8]. We anticipated that a dose of 3 mg of cetrorelix would be sufficient to cause regression of the growing (pre-and post-selection) and static phase dominant follicles by depriving them of LH support required to maintain functional dominance. Cetrorelix competitively binds to the gonadotrophs in the adenohypophysis to induce a temporary suppression of LH secretion [33] for several days following treatment. In an early study in cattle, a dose of 10 μg/kg body weight of cetrorelix suppressed LH pulse-frequency for 1 day (4.7 pulses/6 hr to 1.4 pulses/6 hr), while a dose of 100 μg/kg body weight suppressed LH for 7 days [41]. However, the effects on the dominant follicle and wave emergence were not examined in that study. Similarly, an early study in llamas (an induced-ovulator species), cetrorelix prevented the preovulatory LH surge characteristically triggered by the ovulation-inducing effect of seminal NGF [48]. However, the effect of cetrorelix on follicular wave emergence was not examined. Most recently, in our study on alpacas (another induced ovulator), results suggest that cetrorelix treatment caused the demise of the growing and static phase dominant follicle and was associated with synchronous emergence of the next wave 8 days later (41). These results served as a prelude to the present study in cattle (a spontaneous ovulator) where, by design, we included dominant follicles at three specific phases of their functional status (Experiment 1 and 2) and at unknown stages of development (Experiment 3) to allow us to critically examine the effect on synchrony of wave emergence. Based on results of Experiment 1, cetrorelix suppressed the growth and shortened the lifespan of the dominant follicle when given during the pre- and post-selection phases. As expected, the diameter profile of the dominant follicle was not affected by treatment during the static phase because the dominant follicle had already reached its maximum diameter. Treatment during the static phase was also coincident with the loss of functional dominance since the day of emergence of the next wave was similar to the Control group. It is noteworthy that the next follicular wave emergence was less variable (more synchronous) than in the Control group when cetrorelix treatment was initiated without previous knowledge of the status of dominant follicle or CL (Experiment 3). It is important to highlight the importance and impact of this observation for practical and clinical purpose to develop effective breeding management program. Interestingly, when we considered the day of second wave emergence as the starting point, the dispersion in the day of emergence of next spontaneous wave (Control group) and cetrorelix-induced waves were similar.

The binding of cetrorelix to receptors in the adenohypophysis apparently did not directly influence the secretion of FSH in the present study. Maximum, minimum, and mean plasma FSH concentrations did not change within 24 hr of cetrorelix treatment. These findings are consistent with those of a previous study in which the secretory pattern of FSH was unaffected by a dose of 100 μg/kg of cetrorelix [41]. Surprisingly, another GnRH antagonist, acyline, when given in the pre-selection period (largest follicle at >5.5 mm size), led to higher FSH after treatment [56]. Follicular wave emergence is a result of a surge in circulating concentrations of FSH which peaks on the day before each wave emerges [1, 2, 9, 57]. The post-surge decline in FSH, attributed to products from both subordinate and dominant follicles, results ultimately in the FSH nadir. The rebound in FSH leading to next surge has been attributed largely to the demise of functional dominance of the extant dominant follicle [1, 3]. Hence, treatment that affects the dominant follicle may be expected to affect FSH; i.e., cetrorelix-induced suppression of the dominant follicle shortens functional dominance and hastens the next FSH surge and emergence of the next follicular wave. Results of the present study are consistent with this model since plasma FSH concentrations rebounded sooner when cetrorelix treatments were given during the growing phase of the dominant follicle.

While two treatments of cetrorelix (Experiment 1) was associated with a smaller CL diameter profile, it did not affect CL vascularity, the interovulatory interval, or plasma progesterone concentrations for 7 days after treatment. No effect on CL form or function was detected after a single treatment of cetrorelix (Experiment 2), but higher doses of cetrorelix (100 μg/kg; 10× higher than this study) have been shown to lower overall progesterone production despite no change in the onset of luteolysis or the duration of the estrous cycle [41]. It is noteworthy from our results that a dose of 3 mg cetrorelix is suitable for clinical applications wherein it is critical to maintain CL function.

In Experiment 1, we opted to start with a conservative approach to give 2 doses of cetrorelix at 24 hr interval for prolonging the duration of suppression, to account for the short half-life of cetrorelix [40], and lower dose based on body weight compared to our previous cattle and alpaca studies [42, 49]. It is understandable that the duration from cetrorelix treatment to wave emergence differed by >1.5 days between Experiment 1 (3 mg split in two treatments; 5.8–5.9 days) and Experiment 2 (single injection of 3 mg; 3.4–4 days) when treatment was initiated during early phase of wave (Days 1–4). It is interesting to note that interval from treatment to wave emergence differed between the early (growing dominant follicle; 5.8–5.9 days) and late phases (static dominant follicle, 3.8 day) when 3 mg cetrorelix was split into two dose (Experiment 1) but not when 3 mg dose was given as a single injection (Experiment 2, 3.4–4 days). Importantly, the mean interval from single cetrorelix treatment to wave emergence was identical between heifers (Experiment 2) and postpartum cows (Experiment 3). The reason for difference in single vs double treatment is perhaps related to shorter half-life of cetrorelix, longer suppression during the growing phase of wave (when cetrorelix injections were given twice) and potential emergence of new follicular wave due to natural demise of dominant follicle (around Day 6–8 of wave in both experiments) independent of cetrorelix-induced demise. It is interesting to note that the pre-selection dominant follicle continue to grow for 2 days (Cetro1–2 and Cetro1 groups) before slowing compared to immediate effect on diameter when treatment was given at the time of follicle selection (Cetro3–4 and Cetro3 groups). Our results of slower dominant follicle growth after selection are consistent with earlier studies using single or multiple injections of another GnRH antagonist, acyline [56, 58]. We recorded the selection of the dominant follicle (growth to 10 mm size) when treatments were given during pre-selection period in contrast to single or multiple treatments with acyline leading to no growth beyond 8 mm size of the largest follicle. Observed differences may be related to the differences in half-life, dose, dynamics of tissue release, and duration of LH suppression.

GnRH-based FTAI protocols are available for dairy and beef cattle but initial adaptation in beef industry was limited in Americas and Europe because of simplicity and efficacy of estradiol-based protocols. Sustained efforts to adapt GnRH-based protocols for beef industry are being made [20, 21] and estimated 7% beef cows and 15% heifers in USA were artificial inseminated in 2017 [59], presumably using GnRH-based protocols, compared to >95% dairy cattle. Despite a ban on the use of estradiol in food-producing animals in the majority of beef-exporting countries [29], estradiol-based synchronization is a predominant protocol for FTAI in South American countries. In Brazil alone, more than 10 million FTAI protocols are done every year using an estradiol-based protocol. In an early study involving a single 5 mg dose of estradiol 17b on Days 3, 6, or 9 concurrent with a progestogen ear implant [60], we found that treatment terminated the growth of the dominant follicle when given during the growing phase and thereby influenced emergence of the next follicular wave (occurring 4.3 ± 0.2 days after treatment). Using a similar approach in the present study involving a single dose of cetrorelix, we found the same effect, and likely for the same reason: because of a suppressive effect on circulating LH concentrations.

In conclusion, a total dose of 3 mg cetrorelix, given as a single or split treatment, induced regression of the dominant follicle in the early-growing and mid-growing phases. The emergence of the next follicular wave occurred at a consistent interval 3.5 days after a single dose cetrorelix treatment in both heifers and postpartum cows, irrespective of the status of the dominant follicle at the time of treatment. There were no residual effects of a 3 mg dose of cetrorelix on follicular dynamics of subsequent waves or luteal function, and the pregnancy rate was similar between saline- and cetrorelix-treated heifers. Results supported the stated hypothesis that cetrorelix treatment will inhibit LH release sufficiently to suppress the extant dominant follicle and the predictable response to cetrorelix treatment makes it an attractive method for use in pre-scheduled (fixed-time) artificial insemination where synchrony of ovulation to within 12 h is paramount.
