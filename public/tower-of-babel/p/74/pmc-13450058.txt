Hot Injection Synthesis of Ultrathin Bi2Se3 Nanosheets With Controllable Dimensions
Authors: Jara F. Vliem, Hester Noordmans, Daniel Vanmaekelbergh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn this work, a synthesis method for colloidal nanoplatelets (NPLs) of the topological insulator Bi2Se3 is presented. This method is based on the hot injection of a bismuth precursor into a selenium solution to achieve precise control over both the thickness and lateral dimensions of the NPLs. Through time‐resolved aliquot studies, three growth stages are identified: (i) an initial nucleation stage where “baby platelets” are formed, already having the final platelet thickness, (ii) a rapid, reaction‐limited lateral growth phase, and (iii) a second lateral growth phase slowed down by precursor depletion. By adapting the reactivity of the bismuth precursor in the nucleation stage, the NPL thickness can be tuned from 7 to 1 quintuple layers, largely independent of lateral size. The NPL lateral dimensions can be altered by adjusting the growth time in stage (ii) or (iii), by adapting the injection temperature, or by injecting additional precursor once the slow growth stage has been reached. This synthesis method, combined with mechanistic insights, can be used to synthesize tailored platelets for future studies on topological insulators.

Introduction

Bismuth selenide, which consists of 0.96nm thick [1] (Se‐Bi‐Se‐Bi‐Se) quintuple layers (QLs) stacked by van der Waals forces, was originally investigated for its thermoelectric properties [2, 3, 4, 5]. Its discovery as a topological insulator (TI) renewed interest in the material [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. As a TI, Bi2Se3 has protected surface states that show spin‐momentum locking, which is why a significant amount of research has been devoted to the synthesis of extended Bi2Se3 sheets [12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. When the thickness of these sheets is decreased below 6 QLs, Bi2Se3 undergoes a topological phase transition as its top and bottom surface states hybridize and become gapped. This phase transition may result in a quantum spin Hall insulator with helical edge states [6, 11, 17, 18, 19]. Investigating the topological phase transition in Bi2Se3 crystals, and the resulting changes in their electronic and optical properties, requires a synthesis method that offers precise control over the crystal thickness at the nanometer scale. Furthermore, adjusting the crystal lateral size is important to suit different experimental characterization techniques and to provide suitable geometries for fabrication of prototype electronic devices. For example, smaller sub‐micrometer crystals are ideal for scanning tunneling microscopy, while laterally larger crystals are necessary for transport measurements. An ideal synthesis method must therefore provide independent control over both thickness and lateral size. Colloidal synthesis methods fulfill these requirements, as they can provide free‐standing crystals with a wide variety of shapes and sizes [20, 21, 22, 23]. However, existing colloidal synthesis methods for Bi2Se3 (summarized in Table S1) remain limited in several ways. For example, they often fail to provide independent control over both lateral size and thickness [24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29], produce platelets that are well within the micrometer range [13, 30], do not achieve the ultrathin regime below 6nm [30, 31], or suffer from poor colloidal stability and particle clustering [32]. In the few cases that thinner platelets have been produced, these are often flower‐like structures instead of well‐defined platelets [33, 34, 35].

To address these challenges, we developed a hot‐injection synthesis method for the production of free‐standing, monocrystalline Bi2Se3 nanoplatelets (NPLs) with tunable lateral dimensions and controlled thickness down to a single QL. Our focus is on NPLs that can be considered as nearly two‐dimensional, hence with sufficiently large lateral dimensions. Such platelets are very useful for studying the electronic and optical properties in the transition from a three‐dimensional to a two‐dimensional topological insulator. Our approach also enables real‐time monitoring of NPL formation, providing new insights into the growth mechanisms of 2D van der Waals materials. While previous studies suggest that oriented attachment may influence Bi2Se3 NPL growth, we find that a zipping mechanism around the platelet edge is more likely [31, 32, 36, 37, 38]. Our synthesis method has already proven useful for investigating the topological phase transitions and optical properties of 2D Bi2Se3 [39, 40], and it will enable further investigations into its electronic, optical, and topological properties.

Results and Discussion

Hot Injection Synthesis of Bi2Se3 Nanoplatelets

The synthesis of Bi2Se3 NPLs is achieved through a hot injection method as described in the experimental section and as shown in Figure 1A. Compared to a one‐pot heating method [41], this approach provides significantly improved dimensional control as shown in Figure S1. In short, the selenium precursor is prepared by dissolving sodium selenite (Na2SeO3) in ethylene glycol (EG) in the presence of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), which primarily acts as a shape‐directing agent (Figures S2 and S3). The solution is heated to 190°C, resulting in a color change from colorless to dark red (Figure S4). The bismuth precursor is prepared by heating Bi(NO3)3·5H2O in EG on a 200°C heatiuntil a white precipitate is formed, during which NO2 is released from the mixture as a brown gas (Figure S5). The thermal decomposition of bismuth nitrate in the presence of water may form various bismuth oxynitrates (e.g., BiONO3), which are typically white solids. These oxynitrates can subsequently decompose upon further heating to yield bismuth(III) oxide (Bi2O3), as shown in Equation (3), or elemental bismuth. Injecting the bismuth precursor mixture into the 190 selenium solution initiates the growth of Bi2Se3 NPLs. After 10 min of growth, monodisperse crystalline nanocrystals have formed, which exhibit well‐defined hexagonal shapes. The platelets have a thickness of 4.0 (3.0, 5.0) QL (median and interquartile range, denoted Median(Q25, Q75)), and a lateral size of 262 (227, 302) nm (Figure 1B–D and Figure S6). The NPLs show slight lateral confinement effects, as found from their optical properties which are discussed in the Supporting Information. The NPL thickness was determined by stacking the NPLs and counting the QLs of edge‐on particles using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). As detailed in the Methods section, this approach proved reliable and more robust than atomic force microscopy.

Monitoring the Growth of Bi2Se3 Nanoplatelets

To understand the NPL growth, the formation of Bi2Se3 NPLs was monitored by extracting aliquots from the reaction mixture starting at the moment the Bi precursor is injected. TEM images of these aliquots (unwashed) are shown in Figure 2A, and a replica synthesis is shown in the SI, Figure S7. Large, elongated selenium‐rich crystals and small bismuth‐rich crystals are visible right after injection (see also Figure S8). The bismuth‐rich crystals do not have a shape consistent with NPL formation, nor the correct composition. Hence, these structures are likely remnants of the solid precipitate present in the bismuth precursor solution, instead of early‐stage NPLs. The selenium‐rich structures are consumed within one minute, while bismuth‐rich particles remain detectable after 10 min. This is consistent with the use of a slight surplus of bismuth to selenium during the reaction, i.e. a 2.14:3.00 molar ratio.

From the TEM images, the thickness and lateral size of the formed platelets were measured as described in the methods section. The results, given as the median value with error bars indicating the interquartile range (25th to 75th percentiles), are shown in Figure 2B–D. The platelet thickness is already determined 1 min after injection, while the lateral size (Figure 2B,C) grows from approximately 50 to 230nm within the first 10 min before slowing down considerably. We divided the reaction into three stages for clarity: (i) the nucleation stage, which occurs on very short timescales, (ii) the rapid growth stage, from 1–10 min, and (iii) the slow growth stage, which occurs after 10 min. These stages are depicted in Figure 3, of which the colors coincide with those in the graphs of Figure 2B,C.

Because the thickness remains constant throughout the reaction, it must be determined very early in the reaction, i.e. before 1 min when we can already observe small NPLs in the reaction mixture. We call this early stage the nucleation stage. We propose that during this stage, nucleation occurs with the formation of Bi2Se3 clusters of a specific volume. These clusters continuously restructure and develop a 2D shape of a given thickness that allows for further lateral growth [38, 45, 46]. We will refer to these 2D clusters as “baby platelets”.

During the rapid growth stage, subsequent lateral growth of the baby platelets occurs. Notably, a linear increase in diameter over time is observed during this stage, which corresponds to a quadratic increase in area (see Figure 2C). Such quadratic growth aligns with the 2D growth mechanism previously described for CdSe NPLs, where nucleation and lateral growth on side facets are energetically favored over growth on the top and bottom surfaces [38]. Due to a lower nucleation barrier, nucleation on the side facet is quickly completed along the rim in a “zipping” mechanism. We propose that a similar growth mechanism occurs for the Bi2Se3 NPLs, in contrast to the oriented attachment mechanism typically cited in the literature [31, 32, 36, 37, 38]. Literature evidence for oriented attachment is scarce and primarily based on TEM analysis of aliquots, while we do not observe oriented attachment in our own TEM aliquot studies. Moreover, the NPL edges remain sharp throughout the reaction, contradicting what would be expected from oriented attachment. Hence, our proposed zipping mechanism appears more plausible than the continuous nucleation and attachment across the entire NPL circumference as required for oriented attachment. In the Supporting Information, we show that a rate‐determining nucleation and zipping mechanism on the edge (Figure 3 ii, iii) is consistent with the quadratic increase in area shown in Figure 2C.

In the final slow growth stage of the reaction (t>10 min), lateral growth slows considerably, which is likely due to selenium depletion. By injecting additional precursor, growth can be resumed, vide infra.

Controlling Thickness and Lateral Dimensions

Because the final thickness of the NPLs is established at the onset of the reaction, tuning this parameter requires control over the nucleation stage. We achieve this through the bismuth precursor. As shown in Figure S9, the NPL thickness can be tuned by altering the degree of thermal decomposition of

Bi(NO3)3·5H2O prior to its injection into the Se precursor. While Bi(NO3)3·5H2O is soluble in EG, its decomposition products (Bi2O3 or Bi) form less soluble crystals that likely react more slowly. Consequently, the initial precursor composition influences both the nucleation and subsequent growth stages. To investigate this hypothesis, Figure 4A,B compare the outcomes of using pure Bi2O3 vs. Bi(NO3)3·5H2O in EG. Corresponding STEM‐EDX measurements of these platelets are provided in the Figure S10. Figure 4A shows that using only Bi2O3 yields thin, small NPLs of 2–3 QL thickness and approximately 100nm lateral size. Neither thickness nor lateral size increases significantly within the observed timeframe. This suggests that a slow growth regime is already reached after 1 min, implying that rapid growth occurs exclusively at t<1 min. In contrast, when using dissolved Bi(NO3)3·5H2O (Figure 4B), the NPL lateral size increases from around 30nm to 40nm during the first ∼5 min, accompanied by a slight thickness increase from 5 QL to 6 QL. After 5 min, the slow growth stage is reached and the lateral size increases more slowly, while the thickness remains around 6 QL.

Before injection into the Se precursor, the Bi2O3 was heated in ethylene glycol for 1 h at 200°C to improve the shape of the platelets, see Figure S12. From the comparison above, it appears that the growth in vertical vs. lateral directions is decided at the very start of the reaction, i.e. during the nucleation stage where baby platelets are formed. The use of Bi2O3 limits the thickness of the baby platelets and, therefore, promotes the growth of thin platelets. Due to their thinness, the platelets show a relatively fast lateral extension. When using dissolved

Bi(NO3)3·5H2O salt instead, formation of thicker baby platelets (4–6 QL) occurs in the nucleation stage. This leads to slower lateral growth since many Bi2Se3 units are required per unit length in the lateral direction. Furthermore, the nucleation of a new unit that initiates the zip growth mechanism at the edge may be more complex and slower for thicker platelets. We tentatively attribute the differences in NPL growth to the effective bismuth concentration during the nucleation stage. This concentration may be lower when using Bi2O3, which releases ions more slowly than the readily soluble Bi(NO3)3·5H2O. As a result, thinner baby platelets are formed in the Bi2O3‐based synthesis, possibly passivated by PVP before significant growth in the thickness direction can occur.

We can use the difference in reactivity to tune the NPL thickness by systematically changing the ratio of Bi(NO3)3·5H2O to Bi2O3 in the bismuth precursor. The results are presented in Figure 5A,B, which shows that the thickness of the platelets can be altered without affecting their lateral size. Platelets of thicknesses between 1–7 QL can be prepared with a near constant median lateral size of approximately 60nm. Figure 5A shows that the Bi(NO3)3·5H2O/ Bi2O3 ratio in the bismuth precursor also affects the NPL shape. This shape dependence is summarized in Figure 5C. A higher Bi(NO3)3·5H2O/Bi2O3 ratio (likely coinciding with a higher active bismuth concentration at the nucleation stage) often yields truncated triangular particles. A lower ratio, corresponding to bismuth‐poor conditions, results in rounded NPLs. Because Bi2Se3 has a trigonal axis perpendicular to the (0001) surface, an abundance of precursor material in the case of Bi(NO3)3·5H2O reveals the underlying three‐fold symmetry (see Figure S11), while the slow dissolution of Bi2O3 suppresses anisotropic growth and yields rounded particles. The rounded shape of the nanoplatelets formed with Bi2O3 can be improved by heating the bismuth precursor for >5 min, which results in smaller and more finely dispersed precursor material. The resulting NPLs have a more pronounced hexagonal shape (Figure S12).

To corroborate that the solubility and reactivity of the bismuth precursor play an important role in the two‐step nucleation process that determines the thickness and shape of the NPLs, we performed syntheses with solid bismuth powder as precursor. When using this precursor, very thin and malformed platelets are formed (Figure S13). We also performed syntheses with a lower overall concentration of bismuth by reducing the amount of Bi(NO3)3·5H2O in the bismuth precursor. In this case, slightly thinner, smaller, and rounded NPLs are obtained, i.e. 97 (80, 126) nm and 2(2, 3) QL when the amount of bismuth precursor is halved, see Figure S14. When a bismuth surplus is used instead, the platelets grow to their typical size. This shows that the characteristics of the bismuth precursor, specifically its solubility and concentration, directly influence the monomer availability during nucleation and growth, thereby determining the thickness, lateral size, and shape of the NPLs.

With respect to device applications, larger Bi2Se3 platelets with finite dimensions in the 0.1–1μm range and with controlled shapes would simplify the fabrication of complex device structures [13, 47, 48, 49]. We found that the NPL lateral size can be increased in a controlled manner by using either the reduction of the selenium precursor (see Supporting Information), or by injecting additional precursor material after the slow growth stage is reached (see also Zhuang et al.) [30] For the additional precursor injections, a 1:1 molar ratio of bismuth nitrate and sodium selenite reduced with ascorbic acid was injected after the slow growth stage had been reached. As shown in Figure 6, this significantly increases the platelets' median lateral size from 149 (137, 163) nm to 245 (223, 265) nm, with only a minor increase in median thickness from 3 (3, 3) QL to 4 (3.5, 5) QL. Hence, this simple method allows the lateral size of NPLs to be increased in a single synthesis, avoiding the need for extra washing or separate procedures as described in other reports [30].

The synthesis method described here has already proven useful for investigating topological phase transitions and optical properties of 2D Bi2Se3 [39, 40]. This research can be extended by examining the interaction between opposite edge states, which could induce a dimensional crossover from a 2D to a 1D topological insulator [50, 51]. Alternatively, these NPLs can be used to study the physics resulting from moiré patterns of overlapping crystals [52, 53, 54]. Unlike epitaxial films, platelets naturally overlap and form various twist angles upon deposition. This is shown in Figure 7, where three overlapping NPLs show various moiré patterns depending on their twist angle. Such moiré superlattices introduce a long‐range periodic potential that can change the material's electronic properties, which forms the basis for twistronics [52, 54, 55]. The interaction between the topological surface states of 3D topological insulators and moiré potentials is currently being explored [52, 53, 56].

Conclusions

In conclusion, we systematically investigated how the bismuth precursor formulation and concentration influence the morphology of Bi2Se3 nanoplatelets synthesized via a hot‐injection method. We have found (1) that the thickness of the NPLs is defined at the start of the reaction, (2) that the NPLs likely grow via a zipping mechanism, (3) that we can use the bismuth precursor reactivity at the start of the reaction to influence NPL nucleation, and (4) that this combined knowledge can be used to tune the NPLs in lateral size and thickness. Specifically, the ratio of Bi2O3 to Bi(NO3)3·5H2O in the bismuth precursor is important for the NPL shape and thickness. Bi(NO3)3·5H2O‐rich conditions favor the formation of thicker, truncated triangular NPLs, whereas Bi2O3‐rich conditions yield ultra‐thin 1‐2 QL hexagonal or circular NPLs. This difference is determined in the early stage of the reaction, where precursor “baby platelets” form in a two‐step nucleation process. The formation and morphology of the baby platelets is likely dependent on the concentration and delivery of Bi3+. Subsequent growth of the baby platelets occurs via rapid lateral expansion at the NPL edges, followed by a slower lateral growth stage due to precursor depletion. We demonstrate that the lateral size of the NPLs can be precisely controlled by manipulating the selenium reduction process during heating, by adapting the growth time, or by injecting additional precursor after the fast growth stage has been completed. The current interest in the electronic topological properties [6, 11, 17, 18, 39], proximity effects resulting in superconductivity [58, 59] and remarkable polarized photoluminescence of 2D and 3D Bi2Se3 [40, 60, 61, 62, 63] makes this procedure particularly relevant, as it provides a reproducible method for creating the platelets required for these studies.

Experimental Methods

Chemicals

Na2SeO3 (99% Aldrich), polyvinylpyrrolidone (Mw = 10,000 and 40,000, Aldrich), ethylene glycol (anhydrous, 99.8%, Aldrich), Bi(NO3)3·5H2O (99.999%, Aldrich), Bi2O3 (>99%, J. T. Baker), Bi (99.960%, J. T. Baker), L‐ascorbic acid C6H8O6 (99%, Aldrich), ethanol (anhydrous 99.8%, VWR chemicals), acetonitrile (anhydrous, 99.8%, Aldrich), acetone (anhydrous 99.8%, VWR chemicals).

Synthesis Procedures

Standard Synthesis

The synthesis of Bi2Se3 nanoplatelets is performed under nitrogen atmosphere using either a Schlenk line or a glovebox. The standard Schlenk line procedure is detailed here. The glovebox method, although nearly identical, is only used when aliquots must be extracted from the reaction mixture, vide infra. As shown in Figure S15, nitrogen atmosphere is required to obtain well‐defined platelets. Importantly, the bismuth precursor is injected into a heated selenium mixture instead of heating the bismuth and selenium compounds together as described in other reports [41]. The separation of precursors greatly improves the quality of the final platelets as shown in Figure S1.

During a standard synthesis, 0.05 g of sodium selenite, 0.22 g polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP10 or PVP40) and 9.5 mL ethylene glycol are mixed in a 50 mL roundbottom flask. The reaction mixture is degassed at the schlenkline for 15 min at room temperature. After degassing, the colorless solution is heated to 50°C to dissolve all sodium selenite, followed by heating to 190°C under N2. Heating to 190°C induces a gradual color change from yellow at 120°C, to orange (140°C), and finally dark red (180°C–190°C). Once the mixture reaches 190°C, a bismuth precursor injection solution is injected. This solution is prepared by dissolving 0.2g Bi(NO3)3·5H2O in 1mL ethylene glycol under N2, and placing this solution on a heating plate pre‐heated to 200°C up to 30 s after its color changed to turbid white. This change typically occurs around 140°C–150°C. The temperature of the precursor does not increase beyond that point as bismuth nitrate starts to decompose and react. Important to note: the decomposition process generates a brown gas, causing pressure to build up in closed vials. To safely mitigate this pressure while preventing solvent evaporation, the bismuth precursor can be prepared in a round‐bottom flask equipped with a Vigreux column and connected to a Schlenk line.

Once prepared, 0.5mL of the bismuth precursor is injected into the selenium mixture. After leaving the NPLs to grow for 10 min at 190°C, the reaction mixture is cooled to room temperature using a water bath. The black product is transferred to a scintillation vial in a glovebox and washed by addition of acetonitrile and ethanol, followed by centrifugation. The clear supernatant is discarded and the black precipitate is redispersed in 10mL ethanol. To prevent degradation, the samples are stored in a glovebox under N2 atmosphere.

Note that PVP will readily absorb water from the air during storage, which might affect the final reaction product. PVP10 has been used for most syntheses described in this work, but PVP40 yields the same results (see Figure S16) and appears more reliable, especially for the synthesis of NPLs prepared with dissolved bismuth nitrate as bismuth precursor.

Alternative Bi Precursors

Instead of Bi(NO3)3·5H2O, Bi or Bi2O3 can be used as bismuth source as long as the molar amount of bismuth in the precursor is the same as for the standard procedure. When using Bi2O3, the precursor can be heated at 200°C for several minutes before injection to improve the shape of the nanoplatelets. This turns the initial yellow dispersion turbid white. For syntheses in which a mixture of Bi(NO3)3·5H2O and Bi2O3 was used, the total molar amount of bismuth in the precursor was kept the same as for the standard procedure. The percentages quoted in this work are defined as the molar percentage of bismuth originating from Bi(NO3)3·5H2O compared to that originating from Bi2O3. Before injecting this bismuth mixture into the Se solution, it was heated for 3 min at 100°C to improve its homogeneity.

Aliquot Syntheses

When aliquots needed to be taken during the reaction, the synthesis was conducted in a glovebox under N2 atmosphere instead of using a Schlenk line. This precaution prevents oxygen from entering the reaction flask through the punctured septum. The degassing step was replaced by stirring at room temperature for 15 min, and the reaction mixture was cooled by removing the heating source. All other steps were unaltered compared to the standard procedure.

Multiple Precursor Injections

The lateral size of the NPLs can be extended by injecting additional precursor into the reaction mixture after the slow growth stage has been reached [30]. First, a bismuth injection solution (0.04 M Bi(NO3)3·5H2O in EG), a selenium injection solution (0.06 M Na2SeO3 in EG), and an ascorbic acid solution (0.045 M ascorbic acid in EG) are prepared. The bismuth and ascorbic acid dissolve easily in EG, but the selenium solution must be heated under N2 at 100°C until all Na2SeO3 dissolves. While these solutions are prepared, the standard procedure for NPL preparation is followed. After 10 min of NPL growth, 1mL of the ascorbic acid solution is injected, which decreases the temperature of the reaction mixture. Once the temperature has returned to 180°C–190°C, 2mL of the Bi and Se injection solutions are injected simultaneously and drop‐wise while keeping the temperature between 180°C–190°C. After complete injection, the platelets are allowed to grow for 15 min at 190°C. These steps may be repeated multiple times to grow larger platelets.

Characterization

Electron Microscopy

A Talos F200X (S)TEM operating at 200 kV was used for imaging and for (STEM‐)EDX analysis. An aberration‐corrected Thermo Scientific Spectra 300 (S)TEM operating at 300 kV was used for high resolution imaging. TEM samples were prepared by drop‐casting a diluted dispersion of NPLs on 200 mesh Formvar/carbon‐coated Cu TEM grids, 400 mesh ultra‐thin (3 nm) carbon coated Cu TEM grids (only for high resolution measurements), or 200 mesh Quantifoil holey carbon Cu TEM grids (for high resolution side view of NPLs). Prior to high resolution measurements, the TEM samples were treated with ethanol and activated carbon according to a procedure reported by Li et al. [64] The TEM samples were further cleaned by holding the grids in vapors of boiling acetone.

Due to the extreme thinness of the nanoplatelets, bulk structural analysis via powder X‐ray diffraction (XRD) yields significantly broadened peaks that are heavily biased by preferred orientation. Additionally, obtaining high signal‐to‐noise XRD patterns requires pooling multiple batches of NPLs, which is impractical for screening the ∼100 synthesis variations reported in this paper. Hence, phase and structural characterization were performed via Selected Area Electron Diffraction (SAED), High‐Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HRTEM), and STEM‐EDX. These techniques provide the crystallinity, structure, and elemental composition directly from drop‐cast samples.

Thickness Determination from TEM and AFM

Due to the PVP surfactants, the small thickness of the NPLs, and stacking of the NPLs, thickness determination with atomic force microscopy (AFM) often proved unsuccessful. The NPL thickness was therefore mainly determined by stacking the NPLs with acetonitrile before imaging a drop‐cast sample with TEM. From the stacked edge‐on NPLs, the thickness was obtained by counting individual quintuple layers of on average ∼80 platelets per sample. This method yields consistent trends and thicknesses between samples. For samples where stacking was not possible due to the large lateral size of the platelets, AFM measurements were carried out using a JPK Nanowizard II operating in intermittent‐contact mode in ambient atmosphere. In these cases, it is explicitly stated in the text that AFM was used. Bruker OTESPA‐R3 (26 N/m) or MikroMasch ultrasharp SPM tips (CSC37/No Al, 0.3 N/m) were used to acquire images with typical set‐points of 0.8 V. Samples were prepared by drop‐casting or spin‐coating 20 μL of a diluted NPL dispersion onto a freshly cleaved mica surface.

Note that AFM measurements often overestimate nanoparticle thickness because of the surfactant layer on the particle's surface. Because a PVP surfactant layer is typically about 1nm thick [65, 66, 67, 68], the combined top and bottom coverage adds approximately 2nm to the overall measured NPL thickness. In Figure S17, we have shown the differences between our AFM and TEM data for three different samples, showing that the median NPL thickness measured with AFM is about 2nm larger than the median obtained from TEM measurements. Therefore, we expect the TEM data to yield a more accurate thickness, which is another reason for our choice in using TEM instead of AFM.

Lateral Size Determination

The lateral size of the nanoplatelets was obtained by measuring the maximal diameter of hexagonal platelets, the diameter of rounded platelets, and the largest corner‐to‐corner distance of truncated triangular platelets. On average, ∼110 nanoplatelets were measured for each sample.

Absorption Spectra

UV–vis absorption spectra were obtained using a PerkinElmer Lambda 950 Spectrophotometer. Samples were diluted in ethanol and measured in a quartz cuvette with a path length of 1cm.

Data Visualisation and Reproducibility

To ensure the reliability of our synthesis method, all major trends regarding thickness and lateral size (i.e. especially those in Figures 2, 4, 5, and Figure S16) were verified independently. Following an initial series of syntheses, a second researcher repeated randomly selected data points to confirm the initial observations. We found that the trends were highly reproducible, as shown in Figure S16 and in the comparison between Figures S9 and S7. Note that in the figures showing trends, we plotted data from a single batch of NPLs for each data point. We chose to show the data this way for easier visualization and to avoid visual clutter due to the close overlap among repeated trials. All given data points for the thickness and lateral size trends represent the median value, with error bars indicating the interquartile range (25th to 75th percentiles).
