Revealing the genetic diversity and population structure in lentil (Lens culinaris) germplasm using inter-primer binding site (iPBS)-retrotransposon markers
Authors: Sarmad Ali Qureshi, Meliha Feryal Sarıkaya, Muhammad Azhar Nadeem, Amjad Ali, Parnaz Mortazavi, Mehmet Bedir, Muhammed Tatar, Aigul Madenova, Balnur Kabylbekova, Fawad Ali, Muhammad Tanveer Altaf, Sagi Soltanbekov, Sati Tokibayev, Muhammad Kashif Ilyas, Kağan Kökten, Faheem Shahzad Baloch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLentil (L. culinaris ssp. culinaris) is a climate-resilient legume crop that plays a key role in global food security and sustainable agriculture. Understanding genetic diversity and population structure is essential for conserving genetic resources, enhancing breeding strategies, and developing improved, high-performing cultivars.ResultsThe genetic diversity and population structure of 96 lentil genotypes were evaluated using 15 iPBS-retrotransposon markers, which produce a total of 346 scorable bands. Among these primers, iPBS-2376 yielded the highest number of bands (33). The polymorphism information content (PIC) values ranged from 0.17 to 0.38, indicating a broad range of marker informativeness. Genetic diversity analysis revealed a mean gene diversity (h) of 0.29, a Shannon information index (I) of 0.44, and an effective number of alleles (Ne) of 1.48. The maximum genetic distance (0.88) was observed between genotypes G25 (Pakistan25) and G94 (Cagil), reflecting this genetic divergence. Analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) revealed that 82% of the total variation occurred within populations. The STRUCTURE analysis and neighbor-joining (NJ) clustering consistently grouped the genotypes into two distinct clusters, largely corresponding to their geographic origins. Similarly, Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) further confirmed this separation based on geographic distribution.ConclusionsThese results demonstrate considerable genetic diversity within the lentil germplasm. This suggests that these accessions can be employed in breeding programs to design improved lentil cultivars with enhanced resilience, higher crop output, and broader adaptability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07416-7.

Background

Lentil (L. culinaris ssp. culinaris) is an annual, herbaceous, and self-pollinating diploid plant (2n = 2x = 14). It was first domesticated in Syria and Türkiye before 8500 BC [1]. Today, it is cultivated at approximately 5.6 million hectares worldwide, yielding 7.07 million tonnes. The world’s leading lentil producers are Australia (1.84 M tonnes), Canada (1.67 M tonnes), India (1.56 M tonnes), and Türkiye (0.47 M tonnes) [2]. As a nutrient-rich legume, it offers high protein content (24.6%), complex carbohydrates (including slowly-digestible starch and low crude fiber), essential minerals, vitamins, and considerable energy value [3]. Furthermore, lentils promote sustainable agriculture by fixing nitrogen, reducing fertilizer demand for cereals in crop rotations [4]. Despite its importance as a key source of income and nutrition, the crop is increasingly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change, which threatens its cultivation. Shahzad et al. [5] emphasizes that climate change endangers global food and nutritional security through abiotic and biotic stresses. To counter this challenge, genetic diversity needs to be exploited in breeding programs to develop stress-tolerant and high-yielding lentil cultivars [6].

The profound understanding of genetic diversity and population structure is highly important to optimize the effective use of plant genetic resources for conservation and breeding programs [7, 8]. Initially, morphological and isozyme markers were employed for assessing lentil genetic diversity [9]. However, these markers are limited by their susceptibility to environmental influences [10]. PCR-based markers were developed to address these limitations, as they provide reliable accuracy, consistent replicability, and can detect genetic relationships irrespective without being influenced by environmental factors [11, 12]. Among molecular markers, retrotransposons are unique genetic elements known for their high replication and mobility, making up a significant part of the genomes (50–90%) in many eukaryotic organisms [12, 13]. Kalendar et al. [13] pioneered the application of iPBS-retrotransposons as a useful genetic marker in plants and animals. The iPBS-retrotransposon marker system is a new, powerful, robust, and highly efficient approach, which is more advanced compared to other retrotransposon-based methods such as Inter-retrotransposon Amplified Polymorphism (IRAP) and Retrotransposon Microsatellite Amplified Polymorphism (REMAP) [13]. However, due to their universal nature, iPBS-retrotransposon markers have emerged as a key tool for assessing genetic diversity in recent years [14–16].

Several PCR-based markers have been utilized in the evaluation of lentil genetic diversity, namely random amplified polymorphic DNA (RAPD) [17], amplified fragment length polymorphism (AFLP) [18], simple sequence repeat (SSR) [19], inter simple sequence repeat (ISSR) [20], and start codon targeted (SCoT) [21]. However, the exploration of genetic diversity in lentil using iPBS-retrotransposon markers is limited. iPBS markers were chosen over other systems such as SSRs or ISSRs due to their high genome coverage, ability to detect retrotransposon-based variation, and greater efficiency in uncovering novel polymorphisms, which is especially valuable given the narrow genetic base of cultivated lentil. To date, only one study by Baloch et al. [22] has employed iPBS-retrotransposon markers to evaluate diversity among Lens species. In their study, Baloch et al. [22] used 10 iPBS retrotransposon and ISSR markers to determine genetic divergence of 50 accessions from six wild and one cultivated Lens species. Out of these, nine L. culinairs accessions were collected from Türkiye. They reported polymorphism rate of 51.92% in Lens culinaris using iPBS-retrotransposon markers. These findings point towards the need for further exploration of genetic diversity within cultivated lentil using iPBS-retrotransposon markers. In contrast, the present study employs more iPBS markers to assess a larger and more geographically diverse set of 96 accessions collected from Pakistan, India, Iran and several other countries. Our study aims to analyze the genetic diversity and population structure of 96 lentil (Lens culinaris) accessions using iPBS-retrotransposon markers, which will assist in the selection of diverse genotypes for breeding programs and climate adaptability.

Materials and methods

Plant material and DNA isolation

The lentil germplasm set analyzed in this study consisted of 96 accessions, including 92 genotypes provided by the Plant Genetic Resources Institute, Islamabad, Pakistan, and 4 commercially released cultivars from Türkiye were used as controls. The genotypes were selected to capture broad genetic diversity and represent a range of geographic origins, including Pakistan, India, Iran, Türkiye, and several Mediterranean and South American countries. The four control cultivars were chosen based on their regional adaptation, agronomic significance, and common use as standard checks in breeding trials in Türkiye. The detailed geographic information of these accessions is available in Supplementary Tables 1 and can also be found in our previous study [23]. Plant materials were grown in trays at the Sivas University of Science and Technology. Fresh leaf samples were collected 20 days after sowing, and DNA was extracted using the CTAB method of Doyle and Doyle [24], with modifications based on recent protocols [25]. DNA concentration and quality were assessed using two complementary methods: (1) visual inspection via 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis to confirm integrity, and (2) spectrophotometric measurement using a NanoDrop instrument (DS-11 FX series, DeNovix Inc., USA). Based on these quantifications, working DNA solutions were standardized to a uniform concentration of 5 ng/µL for PCR amplifications.

PCR amplification

A total of 35 iPBS-retrotransposon primers originally developed by Kalendar et al. [13], were utilized to screen for polymorphisms across eight randomly selected lentil genotypes. Out of the 35 iPBS primers initially tested, only 15 were selected for final analysis based on their ability to produce reproducible, clear, and polymorphic amplification patterns across all genotypes, while the remaining primers were excluded due to weak amplification, low polymorphism, or poor reproducibility. The primers sequences and annealing temperatures employed in this work are presented in Table 1. The following ingredients were used in the 10 µL PCR amplification reaction: 0.2 mM of each dNTP (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), 1X PCR buffer (Thermo Scientific), 0.2 U of Taq DNA polymerase (Thermo Scientific), 0.6 mM primers for 18-nt sequences or 1 mM for 12–13-nt sequences, and 25 ng of template DNA. Thirty cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 50–52 °C (primer-dependent) for 1 min, extension at 72 °C for 1 min, and final elongation at 72 °C for 5 min comprised the thermal cycling conditions for PCR amplification. The separation of amplified products was achieved on a 2% agarose gel (w/v) in 0.5× TBE buffer over a run time of 150 min. Agarose gel was visualized using ethidium bromide staining in Imager Gel Doc XR + system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). A 100 bp + DNA ladder was used as a reference marker. Statistical Analysis

Only clear and unambiguous bands were scored; faint, unclear, or missing bands were excluded from the binary data matrix to ensure scoring accuracy. A binary scoring approach was used, where scores of 0 corresponded to the absence of bands and 1 for the presence of bands. Genetic diversity metrics, including Shannon’s information index (I), effective number of alleles (Ne), gene diversity (h), and Nei’s genetic distance, were calculated using POPGENE software version 1.32 [26]. The PIC (Polymorphism Information Content) was calculated using the formula: PIC = 2f(1 − f). To analyze molecular variance and visualize genetic relationships, AMOVA and PCoA were conducted using GenAlEx version 6.5 [27]. The neighbor-joining analysis was performed with R statistical software version 4.4.2 [28]. The population structure of lentil germplasm was assessed through Bayesian inference using STRUCTURE version 2.3.4 (Stanford, CA, USA) [29]. The optimal number of genetic clusters (K) was estimated based on the approach outlined by Evanno et al. [30], involving ten independent runs for each K value. Each run included a burn-in period of 50,000 steps followed by 50,000 MCMC (Markov Chain Monte Carlo) iterations. Structure Selector was used to assess the results and identify the optimal population.

Results

Marker efficiency and genetic diversity parameters

In this investigation, fifteen polymorphic iPBS-retrotransposon markers successfully resulted in 346 scorable amplification bands. Among all the primers tested, iPBS-2376 recorded the highest number of bands (33), while iPBS-2399 yielded the fewest (11). On average, each primer produced 21.93 bands. Out of 346 bands generated, 329 were polymorphic. Primer 2376 produced five monomorphic bands. The study recorded an average polymorphism rate of 95.25%.

Genetic diversity indices revealed substantial variation among markers (Table 2). The Polymorphism Information Content (PIC) ranged between 0.17 (Primer 2376) and 0.38 (Primer 2392), and the overall average PIC value of all primers was 0.29. The effective number of alleles (Ne), as estimated by diversity indices, ranged from 1.25 to 1.68, corresponding to primers 2376 and 2399, respectively, with an average of 1.48. Gene diversity (h) ranged from 0.17 to 0.38 for primer 2376 and 2392, with an average of 0.29. Genetic distance and Population-based diversity

The mean gene distance using iPBS-retrotransposon markers for lentil germplasm was calculated as 0.39. The highest genetic distance (0.88) was observed between genotypes G25 (Pakistan25) and G (Cagil), whereas the lowest genetic distance (0.09) was noted between G21 (Pakistan21) and G22 (Pakistan22). AMOVA showed that the genetic variation within the population (82%) was higher than among the population (18%) (Table 3). The PhiPT value of 0.183 indicated a moderate level of genetic differentiation among populations, and this differentiation was statistically significant (p = 0.001), confirming the presence of population structure in the studied germplasm. Population based genetic diversity indices were calculated (Table 4). Pakistan had the highest value of Na (1.882), Ne (1.514) and He (0.300), uHe (0.303) and I (0.450) among all populations, including the highest percentage of polymorphism (90.46%), showing a higher level of diversity.

The Bayesian-based structure analysis also confirmed this by revealing two distinct populations among the germplasm being investigated (Fig. 3). Population A clustered 48 genotypes, while population B clustered 21 genotypes, according to a membership coefficient of ≥ 70%. Discussion

Assessing genetic diversity and population structure is fundamental for crop quality enhancement and the development of future breeding strategies [31]. Retrotransposon-based markers, particularly iPBS markers have emerged as powerful tool for assessing genetic diversity due to their abundance and widespread distribution across plant genome. In lentil, Ty3-gypsy retrotransposons like Ogre make up a large part of the genome and are unevenly distributed, making them useful for developing polymorphic genetic markers [32]. These markers have been successfully used to construct genetic linkage map and explore genetic structures. Despite the genetic bottleneck in cultivated lentil (L. culinaris), identifying remaining variation is vital for breeding under climate stress. Retrotransposon-based markers have proven effective in detecting genomic regions linked to stress responses [32], and their use supports marker-assisted breeding programs aimed at developing climate-resilient varieties [33]. Our study evaluated the genetic diversity and population structure of 96 lentil genotypes using iPBS-retrotransposon markers system. In present study, iPBS-retrotransposon markers demonstrated a significant level of polymorphism, producing 329 polymorphic bands out of 346. This study detected a polymorphism rate of 95.25%, which was higher than previous reports such as the 51.92% observed by Baloch et al. [22] in L. culinaris using iPBS-retrotransposon markers. This polymorphism rate also exceeded 76% reported by Kalbiyeva et al. [34] using ISSR markers in lentil, demonstrating the greater effectiveness of iPBS markers to reveal genetic diversity between lentil genotypes. The current research generated an average of 23.1 bands per primer and 21.93 polymorphic bands per primer, which exceeded many of the previous studies. In lentil diversity analysis with iPBS-retrotransposon, Baloch et al. [22] observed an average of 15.0 polymorphic bands per primer. Sharma et al. [35] reported 3.55 bands per primer in a study on lentils using SSR markers. Uçer et al. [14] reported a maximum of 21.25 bands and 20.33 polymorphic bands for each primer in a study on Fig (Ficus carica L.) using iPBS-retrotransposon marker. Sadık et al. [36] evaluated sugar-beet with iPBS-retrotransposon markers and detected an average of 6.8 polymorphic bands per primer.

The PIC value is crucial for assessing the effectiveness of polymorphic loci in genetic diversity and determining the markers discriminatory power. Al-Mousa et al. [37] demonstrated PIC values ranging from 0.15 to 0.39 in lentils using ISSR markers. While in the present study, the PIC values ranged from 0.17 to 0.38. The presence of genetic variation in this lentil germplasm was evaluated using multiple diversity indices (Table 2). The mean number of effective alleles (Ne) (1.48) closely aligns with the findings of Baran et al. [38] who reported a value of (1.481) while analyzing genetic diversity in 128 cotton genotypes using iPBS markers. The average Shannon’s information index (I = 0.44) and Nei’s genetic diversity (h = 0.29) observed in this study were relatively higher compared to values reported in previous studies on different crops using iPBS-retrotransposon markers [39, 40]. The mean genetic distance among lentil germplasm was 0.39, which varied from the maximum (0.88) between G25 (Pakistan25) and G94 (Cagil) to the minimum (0.09) between G21 (Pakistan21) and G22 (Pakistan22). Thus, the diverse genotypes obtained, including Pakistan25 and Cagil, could be valuable for enhancing genetic diversity in lentil crop improvement.

The AMOVA analysis showed that 82% of the genetic variance was within populations and merely 18% was among populations (Table 3). This finding suggests considerable intra-population diversity, where individual accessions within the same population differ genetically. Population genetic diversity analysis revealed high disparity between the populations. Genotypes originated from Pakistan had the highest genetic diversity, as seen from the highest Na (1.882), Ne (1.514), He (0.300), uHe (0.303), and I (0.450) values, and highest polymorphism percentage (90.46%) (Table 4). The high level of diversity observed in this population makes it a promising genetic resource for breeding programs or conservation initiatives.

The genetic diversity and population structure of the lentil germplasm were analyzed using STRUCTURE, PCoA, and neighbor-joining analyses. STRUCTURE analysis, a robust clustering method validated in earlier studies [14, 38], divided the 96 genotypes into the two major populations A and B, based on a ≥ 70% membership coefficient (Fig. 3). Population A had 48 genotypes predominantly from Pakistan but also from India, Iran, Turkey, Brazil, and Egypt, indicating shared ancestry and possible historical gene flow. This grouping supports the known domestication history of lentil in South Asian and the influence of ancient seed exchange networks among Mediterranean and South Asia regions [41, 47]. The genetic relatedness observed among these accessions is consistent with the known domestication history of lentil, which originated in Southwestern Asia around 7000 BC, with L. culinaris subsp. orientalis is identified as the wild progenitor of the cultivated subspecies culinaris [42–44]. The distribution of wild relatives, particularly in Southwest and Central Asia and the Mediterranean, further supports the possibility of historical gene flow between these regions, reinforcing the genetic clustering observed in population A [45, 46]. Population B contained 21 genotypes, 13 of which belonged to Pakistan, as well as accessions from Iran, India, and Lebanon. The presence of Pakistani genotypes across both population A and B highlights the substantial intra-regional genetic diversity present within Pakistan’s lentil germplasm. This diversity likely arises from the country’s wide range of agro-ecological zones and historical germplasm introductions from adjacent regions such as Southwest and Central Asia, which have long been part of lentil domestication and diffusion corridor [43, 44]. The clustering of Indian, Iranian, and Lebanese accessions with Pakistani lines suggests historical gene flow or shared ancestry, possibly due to the overlapping presence of L. culinaris wild relatives across these regions, contributing to regional diversification through both natural and human-mediated selection. The identification of several admixed individuals in the STRUCTURE analysis further reflects interpopulation gene flow and incomplete genetic differentiation, which is a recognized pattern in self-pollinated crops like lentil that exhibit limited but cumulative recombination events over time [47]. The presence of 28 admixed genotypes likely reflects historical introgression and limited hybridization events due to human-mediated seed exchange among regions, rather than seed contamination. Moreover, the STRUCTURE-based groupings were supported by the Nei’s genetic distance matrix, which showed that genetically divergent accessions such as G25 (Pakistan25) and G94 (Cagil) fell into separate clusters, whereas genetically similar accessions such as G21 (Pakistan21) and G22 (Pakistan22) grouped closely together. This convergence of results from both population structure and pairwise distance metrics strengthens the reliability of genetic clustering and reflects the evolutionary and geographical forces shaping lentil diversity in these regions. Two major groups were identified via neighbor-joining analysis: A and B (Fig. 4). More fine-grained genetic structure was indicated by the division of group A into subgroups A1 (78 genotypes) and A2 (13 genotypes), which included 91 genotypes from Pakistan, India, Iran, Turkey, Brazil, Lebanon, Cyprus, and Egypt. Genetic variation in Group A suggests historical gene flow or shared ancestry between regions. Group B contained only 5 genotypes, all from Pakistan, and may be diverged due to unique genetic features, local adaptation, or isolation. The PCoA confirmed the genetic structuring of the 96 lentil accessions based on iPBS-retrotransposon data (Fig. 5). As shown in Fig. 5a, genotypes are grouped into distinct clusters, while Fig. 5b illustrates clearer separation based on their geographical origin. In the case of neighbor-joining clustering and PCoA, the differences are likely due to their lower resolution capabilities compared with model-based STRUCTURE analysis.

Molecular markers, particularly iPBS-retrotransposons, have proven to be powerful tools for uncovering hidden genetic diversity and elucidating population structure in lentil germplasm. Their efficiency in detecting polymorphism and genetic differentiation provides a reliable foundation for breeding strategies aimed at climate resilience and higher productivity [48]. The use of such markers is essential for broadening the genetic base, conserving valuable resources, and accelerating the development of improved cultivars. Ultimately, molecular marker-based approaches contribute significantly to sustainable agriculture and global food security by enabling precise selection of diverse parental lines for future crop improvement [23, 49, 50].

Conclusion

The iPBS-retrotransposon marker system demonstrated high efficiency in revealing considerable genetic diversity and population STRUCTURE among 96 lentil genotypes. The high polymorphism rate (95.25%), diverse PIC values (0.17 to 0.38) and, significant intra-population variation underscore the robustness of this marker system for genotyping lentil germplasm. STRUCTURE and neighbor-joining analysis consistently clustered the genotypes into two distinct populations, confirming geographic differentiation and gene flow. These results provide a valuable foundation for marker-assisted breeding, enabling the identification and selection of genetically diverse genotypes for developing climate-resilient and disease-resistant lentil cultivars. Specifically, genetically distant accessions such as Pakistan25 and Cagil may serve as key parental lines to broaden the genetic base and enhance adaptability under environmental stresses.
