The Effect of 6 Months of Dietary Counselling on Sleep Outcomes Among Patients With Cardiovascular Diseases: A Randomised Controlled Trial
Authors: Auke J C F Verkaar, Iris van Damme, Eva R van Veldhuisen, H Myrthe Boss, Johanna M Geleijnse, Renate M Winkels
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSleep problems are highly prevalent among patients with cardiovascular diseases (CVD). We examined the effect of dietary counselling on sleep outcomes among patients with CVD. We performed secondary analyses within a previously conducted six‐month randomised controlled trial among patients with CVD. Within the trial, the intervention group received dietary counselling aimed to increase adherence to the Dutch dietary guidelines and the control group received usual care. Diet quality was assessed with an index, in which higher scores (range 0–160) indicated better adherence. Sleep outcomes were assessed with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. An overall score > 5 was classified as a poor sleep quality. Among the 112 patients with CVD included in these analyses (68.9 ± 9.2 years; 74% male; 27.7 ± 4.0 kg/m2), 32% had a poor sleep quality at baseline. Diet quality seemed to improve more in the intervention group (from 104.7 ± 16.5 to 117.0 ± 16.1) than in the control group (from 102.1 ± 22.2 to 107.7 ± 19.7). The intervention did not result in statistically significant differential changes over time between the intervention (n = 57) and control group (n = 55) for sleep quality (0.2 points on overall PSQI; 95% CI −0.6, 1.0), sleep duration (9.0 min; 95% CI −15.8, 33.8), sleep onset latency (3.8 min; 95% CI −3.5, 11.1), or sleep efficiency (−0.2%; 95% CI −4.0, 3.7). To conclude, counselling on a healthy diet did not result in better sleep outcomes among patients with CVD. Improvements in diet quality within this study might have been insufficient to affect sleep, or improving diet quality might not affect sleep among patients with CVD.Keywords: cardiovascular diseases, diet quality, intervention, nutrition, sleep

Introduction

Sleep problems including insomnia and a short sleep duration are associated with a higher risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) (Sofi et al. 2014; Covassin and Singh 2016). Among patients with established cardiovascular diseases, sleep problems are also highly prevalent (Hayes et al. 2009; Madsen et al. 2019). To illustrate, 45% of a Norwegian population with coronary heart disease reported insomnia complaints (Frøjd et al. 2021). Furthermore, a cohort study showed that 43% of patients with heart failure reported sleep problems after diagnosis, and in 30% of those patients sleep problems were still present 12 months after diagnosis (Johansson et al. 2015). Having sleep problems is associated with an increased risk of recurrent cardiovascular events in women with coronary heart disease (Leineweber et al. 2003) and with an increased risk of hospitalisation in people with heart failure (Johansson et al. 2015).

Although sleep problems are highly prevalent among patients with CVD and are associated with worse prognosis, research on how sleep could be improved among patients with CVD is limited. Two previous studies reported improvements in sleep quality after cardiac rehabilitation programmes (Lodi Rizzini et al. 2022; Duarte Freitas et al. 2011). Those programmes focused mostly on exercise but also included some dietary advice (Lodi Rizzini et al. 2022) or dietary monitoring (Duarte Freitas et al. 2011). However, those studies did not include a control group and can therefore not differentiate whether the observed change in sleep quality was due to the cardiac rehabilitation programme or whether it reflected natural change over time.

There are observational studies in other study populations without CVD that indicate that healthier dietary patterns are associated with beneficial sleep outcomes (Arab et al. 2024; Godos et al. 2021). However, intervention studies assessing whether better diet quality would result in beneficial sleep outcomes are lacking. Published intervention studies often focus on single foods, dietary supplements, macronutrient alterations, or energy restriction rather than on overall diet quality (Burrows et al. 2020; Binks et al. 2020). For example, literature shows that alcohol can directly decrease sleep onset latency while disrupting sleep quality later in the night (Ebrahim et al. 2013). Dietary intake may also indirectly improve sleep through weight loss. For instance, sleep quality drastically improved in people with obesity who lost substantial weight after bariatric surgery (Toor et al. 2012). Next to weight loss, inflammation is suggested to play a role in the relation between diet and sleep as a healthy diet can have anti‐inflammatory properties (Ricker and Haas 2017). Among patients with CVD, lower levels of melatonin are observed during the night (Brugger et al. 1995). These reduced levels of melatonin are possibly caused by accumulation of inflammatory macrophages and fibrosis in the superior cervical ganglia (Ziegler et al. 2023). This leads to denervation of pineal gland‐innervating neurons (Ziegler et al. 2023). As the pineal gland secretes melatonin (Brzezinski 1997), this process could explain the reduced melatonin levels among patients with CVD. Due to this relation between inflammation and melatonin secretion, it is suggested that anti‐inflammatory interventions could potentially prevent damage to the ganglionic structure and function (Ziegler et al. 2023). As a healthy diet has anti‐inflammatory properties (Ricker and Haas 2017), it could be argued that a healthy diet can thereby contribute to preventing a decline in sleep quality or even improve sleep outcomes among patients with CVD.

Overall, there are indications that diet or dietary components may affect sleep. However, randomised trials examining the effect of dietary counselling aimed to improve diet quality on sleep outcomes among patients with CVD, a population often experiencing sleep disturbances, are lacking. Therefore, we examined the effect of 6 months of dietary counselling on subjective sleep outcomes among patients with CVD, compared to a control group receiving usual care.

Methods

Study Design

This study involves an analysis of secondary outcomes of a six‐month two‐arm parallel randomised controlled trial including patients with CVD. The parent study aimed to assess whether counselling by a dietitian improved cardiovascular risk in patients with CVD compared to usual care (van Damme et al. 2024). The trial is registered at clinicaltrials.gov as NCT05071092 and at the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP; https://trialsearch.who.int/) under number NL‐OMON49536. Data were collected between June 2021 and November 2023. A Medical Ethics Committee approved the study protocol and all participants provided written informed consent.

Participants

Participants were recruited from two general hospitals in the Netherlands (Hospital Gelderse Vallei in Ede and Rijnstate Hospital in Arnhem). Participants were eligible if they were 18 years or older and had a diagnosis of CVD, which comprised acute coronary disease syndrome, angina pectoris, coronary revascularization, transient ischemic attack (TIA) or stroke, symptomatic aortic iliofemoral atherosclerosis, aortic aneurysm, intermittent claudication or peripheral revascularization. Exclusion criteria were use of diabetes medication, having chronic kidney disease (≥stage 4), participating in other research of which the outcomes could interfere with this trial, or not being able to speak and understand Dutch. As sleep was added later as a secondary outcome of this trial, the first 12 participants did not receive the sleep quality questionnaire and are excluded from the current analysis.

Randomisation and Blinding

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the group receiving dietary counselling or to the control group. We performed a randomisation procedure stratified for hospital site to guarantee balance of hospital sites across groups with permuted blocks of varying block sizes (4, 6, 8) using Castor Electronic Data Capture (Castor EDC, Amsterdam, the Netherlands). Due to the nature of the intervention, it was not possible to blind participants, dietitians, and researchers during the intervention.

Intervention

Participants in the intervention group were referred to a dietitian. Patients received up to 5 h of dietary counselling across seven sessions within a six‐month period. Scheduling was flexible and was based on patient preference, resulting in individual variation in session timing and frequency of sessions. The dietitians were instructed to counsel participants to increase adherence to the Dutch dietary guidelines (Kromhout et al. 2016). The Dutch dietary guidelines advise to increase intake of vegetables, fruit, whole grain products, legumes, nuts, dairy, fish, and tea; to replace butter, hard margarines, and cooking fats by soft margarine, liquid cooking fat, and vegetable oils; to replace unfiltered coffee by filtered coffee; and to reduce intake of salt, red meat, processed meat, sugar‐sweetened beverages and fruit juices, and alcohol. Together with the dietitian, participants discussed what would be suitable small changes that could be frequently made in order for these changes to become a habit. The control group did not receive any information or counselling about diet as part of the study, only usual care. No advice or information was given about sleep to the intervention or control group. The intervention is described in more detail elsewhere (van Damme et al. 2024).

Data Collection

Sleep Outcomes

Sleep quality was assessed with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), which was self‐administered at baseline and after 6 months. The PSQI is a validated questionnaire to assess habitual sleep during the past month and consists of 19 items examining seven components of sleep: sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime functioning (Buysse et al. 1989). The PSQI consists of a combination of open‐ended items (e.g., for sleep onset latency and sleep duration) and items with a 4‐point Likert scale (e.g., for sleep quality and use of sleep medication). For each of the components of the PSQI, a score from 0 to 3 is given. Together, the sum of these scores leads to an overall score ranging from 0 to 21, with lower scores indicating better sleep quality. An overall PSQI > 5 is widely accepted as an indication of poor sleep quality (Buysse et al. 1989). Besides examining the overall PSQI, we examined the effect of the intervention on the following continuous subcomponents of the PSQI: sleep duration, sleep onset latency, and sleep efficiency. Sleep efficiency was calculated by dividing the reported sleep duration by the time in bed * 100%. Implausible values for sleep outcomes (sleep duration ≥ 18 h or sleep duration ≥ 15 h in combination with a ≥ 2 h difference with time in bed; and sleep onset latency ≥ 300 min) were handled as missing data. Sleep efficiency > 100% was truncated to 100%.

Diet Quality

Diet quality was assessed at baseline and after 6 months with a brief food frequency questionnaire (FFQ), the Eetscore FFQ (De Rijk et al. 2021). The Eetscore FFQ was previously validated against a full‐length FFQ (De Rijk et al. 2021) and three‐day food records (Heusschen et al. 2022) and was considered an acceptable tool to rank participants. This Eetscore FFQ is developed to assess the Dutch Healthy Diet Index, a diet quality score which indicates level of adherence to the Dutch dietary guidelines (Kromhout et al. 2016). This diet quality score ranges from 0 to 160, with a higher score indicating better adherence. More information about the score is described elsewhere (Looman et al. 2017). We also retrieved data on alcohol intake from this brief FFQ. Frequency (days per week) and amount (glasses per day) of alcohol consumption were collected for weekdays and weekend days separately. We report diet quality and alcohol consumption before and after the intervention.

Baseline Characteristics

Body weight, height, and waist circumference were measured by the research staff at baseline and 6 months at the research facility of the hospitals. BMI was calculated based on body weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared. Weight was also measured 3 months after baseline. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure were measured on the non‐dominant arm using an automatic device (Microlife WatchBP home A) while the participant was seated, and an average of three measurements was calculated. Age, sex, and personal characteristics such as smoking habits, education level, and employment status were self‐reported by the participant. Physical activity was estimated by asking how many days per week participants were physically active for at least 30 min, including the following activities: biking, gardening, hands‐on tasks and projects, or sports. Information about the type of CVD and time since the first cardiovascular diagnosis were retrieved from electronic patient records. Data on use of sleep medication and experiencing symptoms of sleep disordered breathing was based on questions from the PSQI. Participants were classified as ‘experiencing symptoms of sleep disordered breathing’ if they reported frequent breathing pauses (at least one night per week) or if they snored loudly for three nights or more per week in combination with reporting occasional breathing pauses (less than once a week) (Fogelholm et al. 2007).

Data Analyses

R statistical software version 4.4.1 was used and for all analyses a two‐sided alpha of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The data analyses plan was finalised before starting data analyses. Researchers were blinded during data analyses (except during the per protocol analyses).

Main Analyses

We tested the difference in subjective sleep quality assessed with the PSQI between the intervention and control group after 6 months using Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) with the PSQI at baseline and hospital site included as covariates. Hospital site was included in the model because this factor was used for the stratified randomisation (Kahan and Morris 2012). Main analysis was conducted on an intention‐to‐treat basis. The same procedure was used to examine changes in the PSQI subcomponents: sleep duration, sleep onset latency and sleep efficiency. All outcomes were included in the models as continuous variables. Assumptions of linearity, normality of residuals, homoscedasticity of residuals, absence of multicollinearity and absence of influential outliers were checked and no noticeable violations were observed. Missing values for baseline PSQI (n = 21, 18.8%) and/or PSQI after 6 months (n = 35, 31.3%) were imputed using multiple imputations by chained equations using the MICE package for R software (m = 10 imputations, iterations = 10) (Van Buuren and Groothuis‐Oudshoorn 2011). For 8.9% of the participants, both baseline and PSQI after the intervention period were missing. Reasons for missing data included participants dropping out of the study, one or more questions of the PSQI were missing, or implausible values for sleep duration. Data were assumed to be missing at random. Missing observations for diet quality score and alcohol consumption at baseline and after the intervention were imputed as well. Results were pooled using Rubin's rules (Van Buuren and Groothuis‐Oudshoorn 2011). The difference in diet quality score and alcohol consumption between groups was assessed using the same method as described for the sleep outcomes.

Sensitivity Analyses

To assess if a ceiling effect limited the possibility to improve on sleep outcomes, we performed a sensitivity analyses only including participants who reported a poor sleep quality at baseline (PSQI > 5). Additionally, we performed two per‐protocol analyses. For the first per‐protocol analyses, we only included participants in the intervention group who completed five or more sessions with the dietitian. This threshold was based on the distribution of session attendance in our study population (median number of attended sessions = 5) to distinguish between higher versus lower engagement while maintaining an acceptable sample size for analysis. For the second per‐protocol analyses, we included participants in the intervention group who improved their diet quality score ≥ 0.5 SD compared to baseline and participants in the control group who did not improve their diet quality score > 0.5 SD compared to baseline. Last, a sensitivity analyses was performed for the main analyses in which we only included participants with observed PSQI data at baseline and after 6 months (complete case analyses).

Mediation Analyses

To investigate whether body weight mediated the effect of the intervention on sleep outcomes, we performed pre‐defined mediation analyses using the mediation package in R (version 4.5.0) (Tingley et al. 2014). Bias‐corrected bootstrapping (5000 samples) was performed to obtain 95% confidence intervals. For a visual overview and details of the mediation analyses, see Supplementary Methods 1 and Supplementary Figure 1. Presence and relevance of mediation through weight at 3 months was based on the effect size of the indirect effect (path a × b). Missing values for weight at 3 months (n = 16, 14.3%) and confounders were imputed in the same way as missing values for outcomes.

Sample Size Calculations

The sample size for the trial was calculated based on the primary outcome (van Damme et al. 2024). An additional sample size calculation, to see if this trial would provide sufficient power for these secondary analyses, resulted in an estimated required sample size of 88. This means our sample size of 112 participants was sufficient to perform these analyses. The sample size estimation was based on a two‐sided alpha of 0.05 and power of 80% using the approach from Borm et al. (Borm et al. 2007). Furthermore, the estimated mean difference in PSQI between groups was −1.5 and the estimated standard deviation was 3.4. These were derived from various previous intervention studies such as cognitive behavioural therapy (Redeker et al. 2022), an educational program (Chang et al. 2016), or exercise interventions (Suna et al. 2015) among people with heart failure or people participating in cardiac rehabilitation (Lodi Rizzini et al. 2022; Duarte Freitas et al. 2011). For full calculation see Supplementary Methods 2.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 124 patients with CVD were included in the trial, of which 112 were invited to fill in the sleep questionnaire. Of these 112 patients, 55 were randomised to the control group and 57 to the group receiving dietary counselling (Figure 1).

Most patients were men (74%), the average age was 68.9 years (SD 9.2), the mean BMI was 27.7 kg/m2 (SD 4.0), and this study included almost no current smokers (3%) (Table 1). A quarter of the population reported drinking 0 glasses of alcohol per week and 36% reported drinking 1 up to 7 glasses per week (Table 1). Furthermore, the majority of the study population had coronary artery disease (63%) and the median time since first diagnosis was 5 years (IQR 2.0, 10.0). Approximately one third of the study population had a poor sleep quality (32%) (Table 1). Baseline characteristics appeared similar across groups, with the exception of the number of participants with coronary artery disease which appeared slightly higher in the control group (73%) compared to the intervention group (53%; Table 1). Change in Dietary Intake and Alcohol Consumption

The median number of dietary counselling sessions received by participants in the intervention group was 5 (IQR 4, 7); 60% of the participants in the intervention group went to five or more sessions. Diet quality in the intervention group increased from 104.7 (SD 16.5) at baseline to 117.0 (SD 16.1) after the six‐month intervention. Diet quality in the control group changed from 102.1 at baseline (SD 22.2) to 107.7 (SD 19.7) at 6 months (adjusted mean difference between groups: 7.7; 95% CI 1.2, 14.2; Table 2). The change in alcohol consumption over time did not differ between the intervention and control group (adjusted mean difference between groups: −0.1; 95% CI −1.3, 1.0; Table 2). Results were comparable when exploring the diet quality scores and alcohol intake before and after 6 months only in participants with complete dietary data (data not shown).

Sleep Outcomes

The change in PSQI over time did not differ statistically significantly between the intervention and control group (adjusted mean difference between groups: 0.2 points; 95% CI −0.6, 1.0; Table 3). Likewise, there were no statistically significant differential changes over time between the intervention and control group for mean sleep duration (adjusted mean difference between groups: 9.0 min; 95% CI −15.8, 33.8), sleep onset latency (adjusted mean difference between groups: 3.8 min; 95% CI −3.5, 11.1), and sleep efficiency (adjusted mean difference between groups: −0.2%; 95% CI −4.0, 3.7; Table 3). Results for the sensitivity analyses including only patients with a poor sleep quality were similar to the results of the primary analyses (Table 3). Results for both per‐protocol analyses (Table 3) and the complete case analyses (Supplementary Table 2) were also similar to the results of the intention‐to‐treat analyses.

Mediation Analyses

Weight at 3 months did not differ between the intervention and control group (path a; −0.1 kg; 95% CI −1.4, 1.1, Table 4). Discussion

Among 112 patients with CVD, 6 months of counselling by a dietitian to improve diet quality did not result in changes in sleep outcomes compared to the control group receiving usual care. Even among patients with a poor sleep quality at baseline or within the per‐protocol analyses, there was no indication that dietary counselling aimed to improve diet quality had an effect on sleep outcomes.

As this study was, to our best knowledge, the first study specifically examining the effect of dietary counselling on sleep outcomes among patients with CVD, comparison with previous studies is difficult. Even within other study populations, most interventions examining the effect of diet on sleep outcomes examine the effects of specific foods, dietary supplements, macronutrient alterations, or energy restriction instead of focusing on overall diet quality (Burrows et al. 2020; Binks et al. 2020). To still show some comparison, our results seem to be similar to results from a trial focusing on diet quality in combination with energy restriction among 49 Finnish men with insomnia symptoms (Tan et al. 2016). That trial observed no effect of 6 months of dietary counselling aimed to improve adherence to the Finnish Nutrition recommendations, in combination with calorie restriction for overweight participants, compared to the usual lifestyle on subjective sleep outcomes (Tan et al. 2016). Although that trial deviates on several characteristics such as study population and the focus on energy restriction, their results are similar to our findings: neither in their population of males with insomnia symptoms, nor in our population of patients with CVD was an effect on subjective sleep outcomes observed.

One of the possible reasons for the lack of effect in our trial could be that the achieved improvements in diet quality were not sufficiently large to affect sleep outcomes. With the intervention group increasing their mean diet quality score from 104.7 (SD 16.5) to 117.0 (SD 16.1), the improvement in diet quality was modest. Improvements in diet quality were hypothesized to improve sleep outcomes for example due to a higher anti‐inflammatory potential of the diet, through weight loss, or due to a lower intake of specific sleep impairing dietary components such as alcohol. Considering the anti‐inflammatory potential of the diet, a longitudinal observational study found that people who improved the anti‐inflammatory potential of their diet over 3 months were less time awake after sleep onset and had a better sleep efficiency (Wirth et al. 2020). However, it is possible that the moderate dietary improvements observed in our intervention group did not lead to substantial improvements in anti‐inflammatory processes and therefore did not lead to improvements in sleep outcomes. Second, we hypothesized that dietary counselling, even though focussing on diet quality and not energy restriction explicitly, could lead to weight loss and thereby lead to an improvement in sleep outcomes. However, dietary counselling had no effect on weight at 3 months in our trial (−0.1 kg, 95% CI −1.4, 1.1), which may explain the lack of an effect on sleep outcomes. Last, alcohol is a lifestyle component that is well‐known for its overall negative effects on sleep quality (Ebrahim et al. 2013). The dietary counselling aimed to increase adherence to the Dutch dietary guidelines, which recommend to not drink alcohol, or when consuming alcohol to limit intake to one glass per day. There was no differential change in alcohol intake between intervention and control group. The absence of an effect on alcohol consumption may be a possible reason for the lack of an effect on sleep outcomes. Of note, the majority of our study population already adhered to this recommendation at baseline by drinking no alcohol (25%) or one to seven glasses per week (36%). Due to this high adherence at baseline, the magnitude of change that could be achieved also was limited.

Our study population had a better sleep quality than expected among patients with CVD. In comparison, among 240 patients with a recent (within 6 months before inclusion) revascularization, hospital admission for heart failure or heart valve surgery in a 1‐arm cardiac rehabilitation program in Spain, 66% had a poor sleep quality (Lodi Rizzini et al. 2022), while in our study only 32% had a poor sleep quality. Possibly the better sleep quality in our study population can be attributed to the longer time after first CVD diagnosis (approximately 5 years). This theory is supported by a previous cohort study that demonstrated that sleep quality improved in patients with heart failure after 1 year of follow‐up compared to directly after hospitalisation (Johansson et al. 2015). The relatively low number of patients with poor sleep quality in our study could in theory have led to a ceiling effect as there was only limited room for improvement. However, as we also observed no differences between the intervention and control group in our sensitivity analyses including poor sleepers at baseline, dietary counselling aimed to improve diet quality might not be sufficient to improve sleep among patients with CVD, even when having a poor sleep quality at baseline.

Although the use of subjective assessment of sleep in this study is valid and meaningful, research using objective measurements of sleep could provide additional insights in the relationship between dietary intake and sleep. Within this study, we fully relied on subjective assessments of sleep outcomes using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). Although it is well known that there is a discrepancy between subjective and objective estimates of sleep (Lauderdale et al. 2008), we assume measurement errors in sleep assessment to be similar between groups due to randomisation and because sleep was not part of the intervention programme. Furthermore, subjective sleep quality is a meaningful and clinically relevant outcome as it is more strongly associated with, for example, psychological well‐being than objective measurements of sleep (Jackowska et al. 2016). That being said, combining objective and subjective assessments of sleep could give a more holistic insight in the effect of dietary counselling aimed to improve diet quality on sleep outcomes.

This study is strengthened by the randomised controlled study design and the real‐world setting using data of patients with CVD in free‐living conditions. However, the relatively high percentage of missing data and the possible selection of relatively healthy and highly motivated participants can be considered limitations of this study. Due to the real‐world setting of our trial, we encountered a high percentage of participants who dropped out or had missing data. It demonstrates that, in practice, referring patients to a dietitian without strict measures to ensure retention can lead to high numbers of drop‐out and missing data. However, we addressed this by using multiple imputations, which is an often recommended method to handle missing data in clinical trials as it decreases the risk of bias due to drop‐outs and can increase power (Little et al. 2012). Furthermore, as demonstrated in an earlier publication, response rates to participation in our study were low (van Damme et al. 2024), which may have resulted in the selection of a highly motivated population that was particularly interested in lifestyle or health. Indicators of this potential selection of relatively healthy participants are the low number of current smokers (3%) and the slightly lower BMI (27.7 kg/m2) in our study population compared to almost 20% of current smokers and a mean BMI of 29.3 kg/m2 in a cohort study including 12,484 patients with coronary heart disease from 27 European countries (De Bacquer et al. 2022). The possible selection of a relatively healthy or health‐conscious population can limit the generalizability to other patients with CVD, thereby threatening external validity. Possibly, in a more representative sample of patients with CVD, greater improvements in diet quality might be possible, potentially leading to more profound improvements in sleep outcomes.

Future studies into the effect of diet quality on sleep could use a more controlled setting or could examine the effect of chrono‐nutrition on sleep. Fully controlled dietary trials could give a more conclusive answer to the question of whether dietary intake or specific components of diet can have an effect on sleep in patients with CVD. However, the often short duration and research setting of fully controlled trials will not reflect the real‐world effect as demonstrated in this study. Future studies could also explore the effect of chrono‐nutrition on sleep by examining factors such as the timing and regularity of dietary intake. Furthermore, studies examining sleep in patients with CVD could select for patients with sleep complaints as these patients might benefit most from such interventions.

Conclusion

To conclude, dietary counselling offered by a dietitian aimed to improve diet quality does not appear to result in improved subjective sleep quality among Dutch patients with CVD. Given the modest improvement in diet quality in our intervention, it is difficult to determine whether the lack of effect was due to insufficient changes in diet quality or if changes in diet quality itself are not effective for improving sleep outcomes.
