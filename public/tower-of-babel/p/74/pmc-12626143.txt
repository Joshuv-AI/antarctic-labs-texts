Hysteroscopy as an Alternative to Curettage in Persistent Pregnancy of Unknown Location
Authors: Ishai Levin, Aviad Cohen, Yael Shulman, Yossi Tsur, Yaron Gil, Shikma Bar-On
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study evaluates the effectiveness of hysteroscopy in managing persistent pregnancy of unknown location (PUL) as an alternative to traditional curettage. Six patients diagnosed with PUL underwent hysteroscopy, four of them by the vaginoscopic approach and without anesthesia, and two under general anesthesia. The objective of the procedure was to locate the pregnancy, treat it, or take a tissue sample in cases of PUL where imaging studies were nonconclusive. Results showed that in five patients, hysteroscopy successfully identified and removed a gestational mass, leading to complete resolution of beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (ß-hCG) levels within 24 days. One patient was diagnosed with extrauterine pregnancy due to the absence of intrauterine gestational mass in hysteroscopy and a plateauing level of ß-hCG a day after the procedure. The findings indicate that hysteroscopy may offer a safe and more precise alternative to traditional blind curettage.

INTRODUCTION

Pregnancy of unknown location (PUL) presents diagnostic and treatment challenges, as it involves a positive pregnancy test without a visible intrauterine or extrauterine gestational sac on ultrasound. Traditional management often includes uterine curettage to obtain tissue for histopathological examination, which helps to differentiate a nonviable intrauterine pregnancy from ectopic pregnancy. Uterine sampling with pipelle endometrial biopsy or curettage is routinely used for the demonstration of chorionic villi in cases where beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (ß-hCG) levels have been rising slowly or plateauing.[123] In fact, algorithms incorporating such diagnostic curettage for trophoblast demonstration are routinely used worldwide to aid in the diagnosis and treatment of such pregnancies.[4]

In patients who have persisting PUL (plateauing levels of ß-hCG without clear ultrasound evidence of intra- or extrauterine pregnancy [EUP]) and who are at risk of an EUP, definitive treatment should be administered. Barnhart et al.[5] recently demonstrated that the active management of patients with persistent PUL is superior to expectant management and that these patients have higher rates of pregnancy resolution.

However, curettage for PUL has several limitations. The sensitivity of curettage in detecting chorionic villi, indicative of a nonviable intrauterine pregnancy, is only approximately 70%[4] which might lead to potential misdiagnosis and inappropriate management. Moreover, uterine curettage carries inherent risks, including uterine perforation, infection, and the development of intrauterine adhesions. These complications may have long-term implications for reproductive health.

In this study, we suggest hysteroscopic management rather than standard uterine evacuation with curettage in patients with persistent PUL. The use of endoscopic treatment rather than blind curettage offers several advantages. Hysteroscopy allows direct visualization of the uterine cavity, enabling the immediate and accurate identification of gestational tissue to distinguish between intrauterine pregnancy and EUP. By providing a clear visualization of the uterine cavity, hysteroscopy can help avoid unnecessary treatments, such as methotrexate administration or repeat curettage. Hysteroscopic evacuation of retained products of conception (RPOC) has also been demonstrated to cause less endometrial injury and intrauterine adhesion compared with curettage.[6] Unlike curettage, hysteroscopy can be performed in the office setting without anesthesia. This reduces the risks associated with general anesthesia and allows for quicker recovery times.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This case series was conducted in the Tel Aviv Sourasky Medical Center, a university-affiliated tertiary referral center in Tel Aviv, between April 2023 and April 2024. The Tel Aviv Sourasky Medical Center institutional review board approved this prospective study of endoscopic evaluation of nonviable pregnancies (0087-20-TLV) and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. We included 6 patients with PUL according to the standard diagnostic criteria of a positive urine pregnancy test combined with the inability to locate an intrauterine or an EUP by transvaginal ultrasonography and the inability to rule out an ectopic pregnancy. We used our previously described observation protocol for EUP. Briefly, patients with EUP or PUL are hospitalized and observed with daily monitoring of ß-hCG for a maximum of 5 days. As much as longer follow-up may prove beneficial with respect to prognosis and location of the pregnancy, we used our local protocol for maximal 5-day follow-up before deciding on treatment. This protocol was previously presented in our work.[78] Patients are discharged if spontaneous resolution with declining ß-hCG levels occurs, and treatment with methotrexate (MTX) or surgery is offered if ß-hCG levels increase or plateau. In cases of persistent PULs (plateau in ß-hCG levels and location of the pregnancy cannot be accurately determined by ultrasound), uterine curettage or treatment with MTX is administered as per protocol.[9] In the six patients recruited to our study, hysteroscopy was performed as an alternative to curettage. All procedures were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Office procedures were performed without anesthesia, and operative room procedures were performed under general anesthesia. We used narrow-caliber hysteroscopes. Illumination was provided by a high-intensity cold-light source via a fiberoptic lead. The uterine cavity was distended with normal saline using the Storz Endomat Select hysteroscopy fluid-management system set to a pressure of approximately 100 mmHg. The vaginoscopic approach was used in all cases. Extraction of pregnancy tissue was performed by means of grasping forceps. One to eight months following the procedure, we performed a short telephone questionnaire, asking about pain during the procedure and satisfaction from the procedure, immediate and late complications, ß-hCG follow-up and future plans for pregnancy.

Following the procedure, five of the patients showed a sharp decrease in ß-hCG levels on the day after the procedure and a complete ß-hCG resolution by a maximum of 24 days [Table 1]. All five patients were eventually diagnosed with intrauterine pregnancies based on the final pathology reports, which revealed trophoblastic tissue and/or chorionic villi in all cases. The sixth patient was diagnosed with EUP by the exclusion of intrauterine gestational mass on hysteroscopy and a plateauing level of ß-hCG 1 day after the procedure (from 194 mIU/mL on the day of the procedure to 223 mIU/mL the day after the procedure). She was therefore treated with MTX with a complete ß-hCG resolution 8 days after the procedure [Table 1]. The diagnosis of EUP was confirmed by the final pathology report, which revealed nongestational endometrium with reactive changes.

Upon a short telephone questionnaire, which was performed between 1 and 8 months after the procedure, all six patients expressed high levels of satisfaction with the treatment and reported quick recovery and no need for analgesics. All four patients who underwent the procedure without anesthesia expressed only mild transient discomfort at the time of the procedure. All six patients continued follow-up as recommended upon discharge, and none of the patients had to return to the emergency room for any medical reason following the procedure.

Following the procedure, four of the six patients have conceived again. Three of them are pregnant at the time of writing this manuscript, and one of them delivered 11 months after the procedure. One patient has continued ART treatment with no success yet while 1 patient has not attempted to conceive since the procedure. The postprocedural reproductive outcomes are summarized in Table 1.

DISCUSSION

Pregnancies of unknown location present clinical dilemmas in diagnosis and treatment. Changes in levels of ß-hCG are not always diagnostic in terms of pregnancy evolution while ultrasound studies are sometimes less than informative.[10] Moreover, some patients present with a small intrauterine sac or a pseudosac that only adds to the confusion and impairs a correct diagnosis. A few diagnostic algorithms have been developed, incorporating history, laboratory work, imaging, and even mathematical models to predict the evolution and location of PULs.[11121314] Magnetic resonance imaging should be considered and reserved for select cases of PUL where ultrasound is nondiagnostic and where further imaging will impact management.

Protocols for evaluation of PUL in hemodynamically stable patients with plateauing levels of ß-hCG and suspected abnormal intrauterine pregnancy call for uterine evacuation followed by ß-hCG follow-up. Patients with risk factors or clinical suspicion for ectopic pregnancy are treated with MTX for presumed ectopic pregnancy. Endometrial biopsy and uterine curettage are invasive modalities for the diagnosis of suspected abnormal intrauterine pregnancy. Uterine curettage is also useful as a treatment modality in PULs that turn out to be nonevolving intrauterine pregnancies. Pipelle biopsy, being a random sample of the endometrium, is less accurate, whereas curettage has the disadvantage of necessitating anesthesia.

In this study, we suggest the use of hysteroscopy as an alternative to curettage in cases of PULs where protocol calls for evacuation of the uterus. Hysteroscopy is an elegant technique; it is quick and safe, and it has significant advantages compared with blind curettage or biopsy. It offers an additional advantage in cases where a pseudosac of ectopic pregnancy cannot be clearly distinguished from unviable intrauterine sac. Treatment wise, it offers prompt resolution of a nonevolving intrauterine pregnancy. Following uterine evacuation by curettage or hysteroscopy, it is crucial to follow up with ß-hCG levels until they are undetectable and pathology reports. We upheld this principle in all six cases in our study due to the significant risk of missed diagnosis of ectopic pregnancy.

Prior studies have compared hysteroscopic management to traditional curettage for similar indications, including RPOC and first-trimester abortion. A systematic review that compared hysteroscopic resection to traditional curettage for RPOC demonstrated a lower risk of intrauterine adhesions, a lower risk of incomplete tissue evacuation, and shorter time to conception in the hysteroscopic resection group.[6]

Our group performed a prospective study on hysteroscopy treatment on 15 patients diagnosed with early spontaneous miscarriage at gestational age of <12 weeks. The study concluded that hysteroscopic evacuation of first-trimester miscarriage by a standard resectoscope is a safe and feasible technique.[15]

Similarly, Weinberg et al. used the TruClear (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) tissue removal system to study the feasibility of hysteroscopic morcellation for uterine evacuation of 10 cases of early miscarriage and concluded that this technique was safe and feasible for the treatment of first-trimester spontaneous abortion.[16] A recent randomized trial for the treatment of early miscarriage by Meshaal et al. compared hysteroscopic-guided uterine evacuation to blind suction D and C and ultrasound-guided D and C followed by gentle sharp curettage. Those authors divided 315 patients into 3 equal groups and showed a significantly shorter time to conceive for women who underwent hysteroscopic management compared to those who underwent blind or ultrasound-guided suction D and C.[17]

We believe that this surgical option for PUL is safe and offers a quick view of the uterus in order to help locate the pregnancy. It also has the potential to minimize uterine damage and the formation of intrauterine adhesions. Previous research has demonstrated that surgeons’ opinions regarding the pathology of retained trophoblast hold good discriminatory ability in terms of the final pathology.[18] We carried out office hysteroscopy using the vaginoscopic approach without anesthesia in four patients. The two other patients opted to undergo the procedure with general anesthesia, but it is our belief that this is a simple procedure that can be carried out without anesthesia by vaginoscopy in most cases and thereby the risks associated with general anesthesia are reduced. Despite the great advantages of hysteroscopy, it is important to also take into consideration the specific limitations of hysteroscopy, including the need for certain expertise and the higher costs of the procedure compared to traditional curettage. The specific complications of operative hysteroscopy such as fluid overload and electrolyte imbalance are expected to be extremely rare in these procedures considering the short procedure time and the fact that most procedures can be completed in an office setting.

The main limitation of our study is its small sample size. Moreover, the absence of a control group undergoing standard curettage during the same period for the same indication makes it difficult to assess the comparative effectiveness of hysteroscopy.

CONCLUSION

This article introduces the use of hysteroscopy as a diagnostic and therapeutic alternative to curettage in the management of persistent PUL. The results suggest that hysteroscopy may offer a safe and more precise alternative to traditional blind curettage.

This is a small-scale, early-bird demonstration of the advantages of this technique. Larger-scale studies will be required to elaborate our findings and may bring about a paradigm shift in clinical practice.
