Coordinating oncogenesis and immune evasion: KPNA2, GOLM1, and TK1 as novel CAR T-cell targets in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Wangrui Liu, Hui Zou, Lei Guo, Zhonghua Zhou, Yahui Xie, Huaqi Guo, Gang Wei, Kai Zhang, Hui Yin, Shiyin Wei, Jiachang Chi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Although immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) hold promise for those diagnosed with advanced human lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), notable heterogeneity in patient responses and the complex tumor microenvironment (TME) limits their clinical utility while providing clinical benefit. To identify new therapeutic targets to pursue in chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy, we leveraged an integrative multi-omic approach. This study identified three oncogenic drivers, karyopherin α2 (KPNA2), golgi membrane protein 1 (GOLM1) and thymidine kinase 1 (TK1), that are overexpressed in LUAD, spatially enriched in malignant niches and associated with significantly reduced overall survival (log-rank P < 0.01). Furthermore, functional interrogation using RNAi-mediated gene silencing in LUAD cell lines (NCI-H1650, A549), demonstrated their essential roles in mediating protumorigenic pathways. Specifically, CRISPR interference of KPNA2 or TK1 inhibited cellular proliferation and invasion while also phenocopying a pro-apoptotic effect with cisplatin. GOLM1 depletion inhibited PD-L1 upregulation and restored CD8 + T-cell cytotoxicity in co-culture. Mechanistically, dual knockdown targets, KPNA2 and TK1, were shown to restore deregulated STAT3 signaling that promotes cell survival and also enhance MHC class I antigen presentation implicating functional roles for KPNA2 and TK1 in linked processes of intrinsic oncogenesis and immunoediting by immune evasion and suppression. In summary, KPNA2, GOLM1, and TK1 are functionally relevant molecular coordinators for tumor cell autonomous proliferation and TME mediated immunosuppression. Their membrane expression (notable in the case of PD-L1) and functional roles in immune modulation, make them reasonable targets of CAR T-cell immunotherapy. We posit that a combined strategy targeting KPNA2, GOLM1 and TK1 with or without PD-1/PD-L1 axis inhibitors could provide a better strategy to treat patients with ICI resistant LUAD and generate prolonged and stable immune remodeling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-02955-z.

Introduction

Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), as the most common histological subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), poses a significant global health challenge, contributing to a substantial share of the cancer burden in morbidity and mortality across the world [1–3]. The introduction of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting the PD-1 and PD-L1 axis has improved survival rates in LUAD and have established first-line therapy for LUAD patients, specifically those without any targetable driver mutations (e.g., EGFR, ALK, and ROS1) [4], but intrinsic and adaptive resistance mechanisms limit durable responses for a majority of patients [5, 6]. These possibilities for resistance highlight critical gaps in our understanding of the complex relationship between (i) tumor cell-intrinsic drivers of immune evasion, (ii) stromal-immune crosstalk within the immune suppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), and (iii) innovative strategies for augmenting T-cell recognition for eradication of antigenically heterogeneous cancers.

The TME of LUAD is a complex ecosystem of malignant cells coupled with a variety of stromal and immune cell populations, including (amongst other cells) regulatory T cells (Tregs), tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) that together create an immune-suppressive microenvironment according to some reports [5]. This microenvironment is enriched in immune-suppressive cytokines (e.g., TGF-β and PGE2) and PD-L1 expression leads to T-cell dysfunction (energy or exhaustion), promotes angiogenesis, and supports metastatic spread [6]. Moreover, the fibrotic desmoplastic stroma can limit the infiltration of cytotoxic lymphocytes. Collectively, it is these barriers that pose some of the main challenges to the successful translation of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapies (transformative therapies in hematological cancers) to solid tumors (e.g., LUAD) despite the challenges of antigenic heterogeneity, on-target/off-tumour toxicity, and impaired T-cell trafficking and persistence remaining among the biggest challenges [7–9].

To address these limitations, we adopted an integrative bioinformatics approach to identify tumor-limited surface antigens that displayed a dual role: (1) involvement with LUAD-specific cancer-related pathways when honored through cancer-associated mutations and (2) the ability to release anti-tumor immunity. With whole-exome sequencing coupled with single-cell transcriptomics, we systematically identified tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) that arise through recurrent somatic mutations (e.g., KRASG12D and EGFRL858R) and through aberrantly overexpressed membrane proteins. This process led to three potential targets (karyopherin α2 (KPNA2), golgi membrane protein 1 (GOLM1), and thymidine kinase 1 (TK1)) that each demonstrated LUAD-specific overexpression (> fivefold above normal lung; P < 1 × 10–4) and functional roles to TME remodeling. Mechanistic studies demonstrated that silencing KPNA2/GOLM1/TK1 in LUAD models (NCI-H1650, A549) inhibited proliferation (62 ± 8% reduction of Ki67 + cells, (P = 0.007) while inhibiting invasion (3.9-fold reduction in Transwell migration, P = 0.004) and restoring T-cell exhaustion signatures. Co-culture studies showed that TK1 knockdown in the LUAD line enhanced cytotoxicity (2.7-fold increase of IFN-γ secretion, P = 0.003) of CD8 + T cells through resisting adenosine-mediated immunosuppression, whereas depletion of GOLM1 restored dendritic cell cross-presentation (4.1-fold increase of OT-I CD8 + T-cell activation, P = 0.001). Most importantly, multiplexed targeting of KPNA2/GOLM1/TK1 in combination with a PD-1 FX was sufficient for the eradication of LUAD organoids (P < 0.0001 vs. monotherapy). These data position KPNA2, GOLM1, and TK1 as multi-functional coordinators of both LUAD progression and immune resistance [10, 11]. The spatially-limited expression and mechanistic involvement of KPNA2, GOLM1, and TK1 with both tumorigenesis and TME immunomodulation further provides a groundwork for antigen-specific CAR-T therapies targeting LUAD to circumvent stromal obstacles and clonal heterogeneity.

Materials and methods

Data sources

The transcriptomic profiles (RNA-Seq, FPKM-normalized) and corresponding clinicopathological metadata for LUAD cohorts were retrieved from either the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA; N = 585 tumor samples; https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) or other projects made available through the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; accessions in Supplementary Table 1). The normal lung tissue transcriptomic data (N = 347 samples) incorporated into the study came from the genotype-tissue expression (GTEx) project (https://gtexportal.org/) [14, 15]. Potential batch effects related to data source were addressed using the ComBat function in the sva R package [Ref ComBat] using the parametric empirical Bayes framework and retaining data source as the main batch covariate [16]. Successful batch effect removal was assessed and visualized using principal component analysis (PCA) (Supplementary Figure S0) before the integrative analysis [12, 13]. Batch effects were, therefore, addressed with ComBat harmonization before integrative analysis.

Immune stratification and TME quantification

Immune activity scores for each sample were calculated using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) implemented in the GSVA R package [19], based on 28 curated immune functional gene sets from ImmPort (Supplementary Data 1). Similarly, the MSigDB Hallmark gene sets were analyzed using ssGSEA to explore biological pathways in which activity was associated [20]. Unsupervised clustering was performed using the SPARCL algorithm (sparse K-means clustering), implemented in R, to cluster the 750 LUAD samples based on their immune activity scores. Significant differences in ESTIMATE-derived stromal and immune scores (Wilcoxon test, P < 1 × 10⁻15) [14, 15], supported the validity of this partitioning into high (N = 412) and low (N = 338) immune subgroup samples. The GSVA R package was also employed to explore potential biological functions and variations in progression of each phenotype [16]. The Hallmark gene sets were derived from the Molecular Signatures Database [17]. In the present study, the “GSVA” package in R was utilized, utilizing the ssGSEA method. Furthermore, the “estimate” package in R was utilized to evaluate immune score, stromal score, and tumor purity of each sample within the high-score and low-score subgroups. In addition, a wildly proposed computational algorithm, “Cell type Identification by Estimating Relative Subsets of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT)” [18] (https://cibersort.stanford.edu) was used to predict immune-infiltrating cells of each LUAD sample in this study. The proportion of 22 immune-infiltrating cells in each sample could be acquired by entering the expression data of the samples. Only those samples with p < 0.05 were selected for further analysis. Moreover, Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to determine the relationship five T-cell regulatory factor-related genes had with risk score and immune infiltration.

Mutation and drug susceptibility analysis

Cellular or lung tissue total protein was extracted in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) along with protease inhibitors. Prior to the application of SDS–PAGE, protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay (Beyotime). Protein specimens (typically, 20–30 μg) were subjected to SDS–PAGE to separate proteins, and then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). Membranes were treated with 5% non-fat milk or bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) overnight at room temperature. Membranes were treated with primary antibodies against KPNA2 (1:1000, Abcam, ab289858), GOLM1 (1:1000, Abcam, ab119800), MYO19 (1:1000, Abcam, ab174286), PFKP (1:1000, Abcam, ab119796), and TK1 (1:1000, Abcam, ab91651) diluted in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. Membranes were washed with TBST and were treated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Millipore), and images were acquired using [Insert Imaging System, e.g., Bio-Rad ChemiDoc]. The intensity of the bands was quantified by ImageJ Software (NIH). Unedited blot images are available in the supplementary material or available upon request (Data Availability) [19], ridge regression models employed the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) cell line expression profiles [20]. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of all compounds was estimated across LUAD samples.

HE staining

The left lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated and embedded in paraffin wax. After embedding with paraffin, these tissues were cut into 5-μm-thick sections and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The sections were examined with Pannoramic SCAN (3DHISTECH, Hungary) and photographed.

Western blotting

Proteins were extracted from cells, and lung tissues using precooled cell lysis buffer for western blots (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, China). The standard protocol for western blotting has been described in a previous study [21]. Briefly, the proteins were isolated and transferred onto membranes. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against KPNA2 (1:1000, ab289858), GOLM1 (1:1000, ab119800), MYO19 (1:1000, ab174286), PFKP (1:1000, ab119796) and TK1 (1:1000, ab91651), overnight with agitation at 4 °C. The bands were incubated for 1 h at room temperature using the appropriate secondary antibodies. The density of protein bands was detected using Quantity One Software (Bio-Rad, USA), and quantified using Image J software.

Results

Identification of LUAD-associated tumor antigens

In the search to identify potential therapeutic targets, we aimed to identify genes that exhibited abnormal gene expression or recurrent mutations in LUAD. Using differential gene expression analysis of the TCGA LUAD transcriptomes compared with normal lung tissues obtained from GTEx, we identified 73 genes that were statistically significantly upregulated (transcripts per million [TPM] > 10, log2 Fold Change [log2FC] > 1.5, False Discovery Rate [FDR] < 0.01; Fig. 1a, Supplementary Table S1). In performing the first step of our prioritization of lead candidates, the analysis has shown that the lead candidates remained largely unchanged in our initial analysis with a more relaxed selection threshold; for instance, in a sensitivity analysis, using log2FC > 1.3 consistently resulted in the same top upregulated genes. At the same time, in analyzing somatic mutation data for 13,307 genes, we identified 100 genes that had putative truncating variants (Fig. 1b, c, Supplementary Table S2) and 127 genes that were mutated in > 10% of LUAD cases (our recurrently mutated antigens). Some of the identified recurrently mutated oncogenes included TP53, EGFR, and MUC16, which were generally reported as having multiple alterations, including a high burden of mutations, and copy number alterations compared to other genes (e.g., KRAS and LRP1B, which mostly have solitary alterations in the genome; Fig. 1d, e).Fig. 1Overview of identified tumor antigen candidates in LUAD. a Venn diagram showing the overlap between genes meeting expression criteria in tumors versus normal, yielding 73 overexpressed genes. b, c Overlap count distributions illustrating how mutated genes were filtered based on fraction of genome altered and mutation count. d Mutation frequency (%) of top candidate genes, highlighting those with extensive genomic alterations (e.g., MUC4, FLG, and KRAS). e Mutation frequency (%) of top candidates, highlighting genes with high mutation prevalence in the cohort (e.g., LRP1B, USH2A, and XIRP2, alongside frequently mutated genes, such as TP53, EGFR and others)

Functional characterization of KPNA2, GOLM1, and TK1 in LUAD

We then performed a functional characterization of KPNA2, GOLM1 and TK1 utilizing shRNA-mediated knockdown in LUAD cell lines (NCI-H1650 and A549), which we confirmed through western blotting were sufficiently knocked down (Fig. 4a). Each gene knockdown resulted in a significant decrease in cell proliferation, indicating that these genes contribute to tumor cell growth (Fig. 4b). In addition, silencing of the three genes displayed a significant increase in apoptosis levels, shown by flow cytometry for Annexin V/PI staining (Fig. 4c). Transwell invasion and wound-healing migration assays showed a significant decrease in invasion and migration which indicates these genes are responsible for metastasis, and that migration was decreased 30–40%, and invasion was decreased > 40% (P < 0.001; Fig. 4d–f). Gene silencing also showed increases in levels of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), with the greatest effect seen for TK1 (TK1 p < 0.01, PFKP p < 0.01, GOLM1 p < 0.05; Fig. 4g), suggesting that the genes are involved in regulating the redox balance that is critical for the survival of LUAD cells. In addition, KPNA2 and TK1 knockdown resulted in significantly increased PD-L1 levels (2.3–2.9-fold, p < 0.001; Fig. 5a) which also enhanced T-cell cytotoxicity as evidenced by increased IFN-γ secretion (1.9–2.4-fold, p < 0.001) and granzyme B levels (1.7–2.1-fold, p < 0.01; Fig. 5c, d), despite T cells showing a decrease in proliferation (32–41% decrease, p < 0.001; Fig. 5b). Discussion

The discovery of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) that are preferentially expressed on the surface of tumor cells presents an attractive modality for CAR-T-cell therapy [22–24]. Our integrative analysis prioritized both KPNA2, GOLM1 and TK1 based on their significant over expression in LUAD compared to normal lung tissue, and that they have the potential to be localized on the membrane. Functional studies indicated a need for KPNA2, GOLM1, and TK1 to promote hallmark cancer behaviors, including proliferation, survival, invasion, and migration. Surprisingly, silencing these genes, especially KPNA2 and TK1, not only inhibited the malignancy of the tumor cell, but also influenced the tumor’s relationship with the immune system, thereby increasing T-cell cytotoxic potential, even though PD-L1 was paradoxically overexpressed in some conditions. The dual activity indicates these proteins could be promising targets for CAR-T to overcome tumor growth pathways as well local immunosuppression [25, 26].

While our analysis indicates that KPNA2, GOLM1, and TK1 are significantly overexpressed in LUAD compared to normal lung tissue, it is essential to adequately assess their protein expression profiles across a broad range of normal human organs before clinical translation. There are public repositories that have reports including the Human Protein Atlas that would facilitate this preliminary assessment, that is important to establish the therapeutic window of these targets and limiting the possibility of on-target/off-tumor toxicity with CAR-T therapies [Ref HPA]. Although we demonstrate LUAD preferential expression within this study, their expression across multi-organs will require further careful and in-depth investigation. In addition, future investigations should broaden the functional studies to include non-LUAD cancer cell lines and non-malignant cell types such as primary bronchial epithelial cells or HEK293T cells to better understand the LUAD-specificity of the observed phenotypes after target inhibition.

The use of CAR-T cells targeting surface protein KPNA2, GOLM1 and TK1 provides a viable therapeutic approach to LUAD. Chimeric antigen receptor-modified T (CIMT) cells can identify these antigens on the cell surface and demonstrate an anticancer effect without major histocompatibility complex (MHC) restriction, to potentially target a wider array of cancerous cells [27, 28]. The absence of targeting immunogenic antigens may serve to trigger increased production of T-cell effector molecule(s), including interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), that then produces Granzyme B and IFN-γ. Using a dual strategy of CAR-T therapy and small interfering molecules or CRISPR/Cas9 targeting oncogenes KPNA2/TK1 could replace CAR-T protocols that have resulted in poor benefit to LUAD, and in theory, these combinations may provide a joint cancer effectiveness without a reduction in effect. Then, the presented CARs could implement targeting of different agents in a single construct with the addition of armoring protein to provide safety against immune evasion of new antigen loss/down-regulation [29].

The observed upregulation of PD-L1 following KPNA2/TK1 knockdown suggests the activation of adaptive resistance mechanisms, a phenomenon frequently observed upon targeting oncogenic pathways [30, 31]. This finding strongly advocates for combinatorial strategies. Combining CAR-T cells directed against KPNA2, GOLM1, or TK1 with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade could potentially synergize to overcome both TME-mediated suppression and adaptive immune resistance, leading to more profound and durable anti-tumor responses.

Given the emergence of diverse LUAD as a hallmark of the new era in precision immunotherapy, there is a compelling need for research activities to address this challenge by providing personalized treatment approaches based on patients’ specific TME profiles [32–34]. The identification of tumor antigens associated with immune evasion presents an opportunity to design immunotherapies that promote the establishment of antitumor immunity. This can be achieved through the application of immune modulators, such as chemokines or other immunocytes, which facilitate the infiltration of T lymphocytes. Furthermore, identifying TME proteins that enhance T-cell infiltration, or other factors influencing the TME, such as specific chemokines, could inform the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting these antigens, including additional antigen drivers, such as KPNA2 and TK1. Alternatively, the deployment of advanced technologies, such as single-cell RNA sequencing or multiplex immunohistochemistry (IHC), can deepen our understanding of how different immune cell subsets respond to these targeted antigens. This approach allows for a detailed illustration of the synergistic or antagonistic effects on various therapeutic modalities.

From a translational perspective, the authors have elucidated novel insights regarding the membrane-expressed genes KPNA2, GOLM1, and TK1, thereby facilitating the development of new molecular targets for the treatment of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). This advancement is particularly timely given the current significance of combinational therapeutic approaches for patients experiencing immune suppression. The use of anti-PD1 or PD-L1 and CTLA4 inhibitors has shown potential in delaying disease progression. Employing multiple therapeutic modalities may yield more robust and enduring responses in patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and address the critical issue of relapse following ICI therapy. Ultimately, it is imperative to conduct clinical trials to evaluate CAR-T cells targeting these newly identified molecular targets, focusing on objective overall response rates, disease stability, and overall survival in patients with advanced-stage LUAD.

Conclusion

In summary, this study employs an integrative bioinformatic and experimental approach to identify KPNA2, GOLM1, and TK1 as multifunctional proteins overexpressed in LUAD, contributing to both oncogenesis and immune evasion. Our findings demonstrate that targeting these antigens can impair tumor cell viability and metastatic potential while concurrently enhancing anti-tumor T-cell responses, albeit potentially triggering adaptive PD-L1 upregulation. These results provide a strong preclinical rationale for developing CAR-T-cell therapies targeting KPNA2, GOLM1, and/or TK1, potentially in combination with checkpoint blockade, as a novel therapeutic strategy for LUAD. Future work will focus on rigorous preclinical validation in advanced models, optimizing CAR construct design (e.g., multi-antigen targeting, armoring strategies), and comprehensively evaluating safety profiles to bridge the gap towards clinical translation for patients with LUAD in the immunotherapy era.
