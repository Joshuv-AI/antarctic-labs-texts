Methionine restriction alleviates diabetes-associated cognitive impairment via activation of FGF21
Authors: Yuyu Zhang, Yajie Wang, Yiju Li, Jingxi Pang, Annika Höhn, Weixuan Dong, Rui Gao, Yan Liu, Da Wang, Yongbo She, Rui Guo, Zhigang Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlucose metabolism disturbances may result in diabetes-associated cognitive decline (DACI). Methionine restriction (MR) diet has emerged as a potential dietary strategy for managing glucose homeostasis. However, the effects and underlying mechanisms of MR on DACI have not been fully elucidated. Here, we found that a 13-week MR (0.17 % methionine, w/w) intervention starting at 8 weeks of age improved peripheral insulin sensitivity in male db/db mice, a model for type 2 diabetes. Notably, MR significantly improved working as well as long-term memory in db/db mice, accompanied by increased PSD-95 level and reduced neuroinflammatory factors, malondialdehyde (MDA), and 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG). We speculate that this effect may be mediated by MR activating hepatic fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) and the brain FGFR1/AMPK/GLUT4 signaling pathway to enhance brain glucose metabolism. To further delineate the mechanism, we used intracerebroventricular injection of adeno-associated virus to specifically knock down FGFR1 in the brain to verify the role of FGFR1 in MR-mediated DACI. It was found that the positive effects of MR on DACI were offset, reflected in decreased cognitive function, impaired synaptic plasticity, upregulated neuroinflammation, and balanced enzymes regulating reactive oxygen species (Sod1, Sod2, Nox4). Of note, the FGFR1/AMPK/GLUT4 signaling pathway and brain glucose metabolism were inhibited. In summary, our study demonstrated that MR increased peripheral insulin sensitivity, activated brain FGFR1/AMPK/GLUT4 signaling through FGF21, maintained normal glucose metabolism and redox balance in the brain, and thereby alleviated DACI. These results provide new insights into the effects of MR diet on cognitive dysfunction caused by impaired brain energy metabolism.Keywords: Methionine restriction, Diabetes-associated cognitive impairment, Fibroblast growth factor 21, Glucose metabolism, Glucose transporter proteins, AMP-Activated protein kinase

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a metabolic disorder characterized by chronic hyperglycemia resulting from peripheral insulin resistance. As a major global health issue [1], T2DM significantly increases the risk of various complications, including cardiovascular diseases, nephropathy, and neuropathy. Among these, a growing body of evidence highlights diabetes-associated cognitive impairment (DACI) as a significant, yet underappreciated complication with profound implications for patients' cognitive function and overall quality of life [2]. The pathophysiology of DACI is multifactorial, involving both peripheral and central mechanisms. Central hyperglycemia in DACI disrupts the normal function of glucose transporter proteins (GLUTs), particularly GLUT1 and GLUT4 [3], which are essential for glucose uptake in neurons and glial cells. This disruption in glucose homeostasis contributes to neuronal dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation, ultimately leading to cognitive decline [4]. Therefore, understanding the perturbations in peripheral and central glucose metabolism is crucial for identifying potential therapeutic targets to prevent or mitigate cognitive decline in diabetic patients.

Patients with T2DM are often suggested to consider lifestyle modifications, particularly the dietary changes [5]. The reduced diabetic incidence in vegan populations may be attributed to the lower methionine content in vegetarian diets compared to omnivorous diets, which contributes to increased insulin sensitivity [6]. Methionine restriction diet (MR) characterized by low methionine intake can be achieved through predominantly plant-based diets, such as vegan and vegetarian diets [7]. MR has emerged as a promising dietary intervention with a number of benefits on metabolic health. Studies have shown that limiting methionine intake improves peripheral insulin sensitivity and enhances glycemic control [8], which helps to alleviate the condition commonly observed in metabolic disorders like obesity and T2DM [9]. The beneficial effects of MR on peripheral insulin sensitivity are accompanied by reductions in systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, further contributing to overall improved metabolic perturbations.

Intriguingly, the metabolic improvements induced by MR are not solely related to peripheral tissues. Emerging evidence suggests that MR also exerts protective effects on the central nervous system. MR has been shown to improve working memory and spatial memory in obese mice induced by a high-fat diet (HFD) [10]. MR alters brain lipid composition [11], which may enhance neuronal function. Of note, MR activates brain fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) and downstream antioxidant signaling to alleviate cognitive decline in aged mice [12]. FGF21 can be induced by nutritional stress that regulates glucose and lipid metabolism [13], which may contribute to the activation of AMPK [14]. Moreover, FGF21 has promising neuroprotective effects. It can enhance hippocampal synaptic plasticity, alleviate cognitive impairment in HFD-induced obese rodents [15], and protect the brains of d-galactose-induced aged mice from oxidative stress and neuroinflammation [16]. Loss of FGF21 in the central nervous system (CNS) or astrocytes leads to dysregulated glucose metabolism and impaired cognitive memory in mice [17]. However, how MR affects brain glucose metabolism through FGF21 and thus alleviates DACI has not been fully explored.

In this study, we aimed to elucidate the role and mechanism of MR in alleviating DACI. We hypothesized that MR could mitigate DACI by improving glucose metabolism. In db/db mice, MR reduced insulin resistance, enhanced cognitive function, and improved brain glucose metabolism by activating hepatic FGF21 and the brain FGFR1/AMPK/GLUT4 pathway. Knocking down brain FGFR1 reversed these cognitive benefits, suggesting that MR's regulation of FGF21 could be a promising strategy for DACI intervention. Our study provides new insights into the effects of MR diet on cognitive dysfunction caused by impaired brain energy metabolism.

Materials and methods

Animal experiments

The 7-week-old male C57BLKS/JGpt-Leprem2Cd/Gpt (db/db) mice and m/m littermates were purchased from Gempharmatech Co., Ltd. (China) and housed individually under standard conditions in a temperature (23 ± 1 °C)- and humidity (40 %)-controlled animal room with 12-h light/dark cycle in Northwest A&F University Experimental Animal Center. After a one-week acclimation period, db/db mice and m/m mice were fed either a normal diet (ND, 0.86 % methionine, w/w) or a methionine-restricted diet (MR, 0.17 % methionine, w/w) without cystine for 13 weeks. All diets were prepared and obtained from Trophic Animal Feed High-Tech Co., Ltd. (China) and the feed formula detailed in Table S1. During the final week, insulin tolerance tests, [18F]-FDG PET scans, and behavioral tests were performed. The methionine dose used in the feed formula is based on previous studies [18]. The db/db mice and m/m mice were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 6–8/group): (a) m/m + ND: m/m mice fed a normal diet, (b) m/m + MR: m/m mice fed a methionine-restricted diet, (c) db/db + ND: db/db mice fed a normal diet, (d) db/db + MR: db/db mice fed a methionine-restricted diet.

To further elucidate the role of brain FGFR1 in MR-mediated cognitive function in db/db mice, intracerebroventricular (i.c.v.) injection of adeno-associated virus type/mutant (AAV2/9) expressing FGFR1 shRNA was used to knock down FGFR1 in the brain [19]. The db/db mice were randomly divided into two groups and i.c.v. injections into the lateral ventricles (stereotaxic coordinates: 0.3 mm posterior to bregma, 1.0 mm lateral from the midline, 2.5 mm from the skull surface) of both sides with a total volume of 3 μL of pAAV-U6-shRNA (FGFR1)-CMV-EGFP-WPRE or pAAV-U6-shRNA (negative control, NC)-CMV-EGFP-WPRE. After two weeks of recovery, the db/db mice were randomly divided into 3 groups (n = 7–11/group) and were fed a normal diet or a methionine-restricted diet for 5 weeks: (a) db/db + Sh-NC + ND: db/db were i.c.v. injected with Sh-NC and fed a normal diet, (b) db/db + Sh-NC + MR: db/db were i.c.v. injected with Sh-NC and fed a methionine-restricted diet, (c) db/db + Sh-FGFR1+MR: db/db were i.c.v. injected with Sh-FGFR1 and fed a methionine-restricted diet. The pAAV-U6-shRNA (FGFR1)-CMV-EGFP-WPRE and pAAV-U6-shRNA (NC)-CMV-EGFP-WPRE were provided by OBiO Technology Co., Ltd. (China). Detailed FGFR1-shRNA and negative scrambled sequences are shown in Table S2.

The mice were fasted for 12 h before sacrifice. The mice were sacrificed under anesthesia, and every effort was made to minimize pain throughout all experiments. All procedures for the mouse studies followed the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals (eighth edition, ISBN-10: 0-309-15396-4) and were conducted under protocols approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Northwest A&F University (XN2023-0717).

Fasting glucose concentration and insulin tolerance test (ITT)

For the fasting glucose concentration test, mice were fasted for 12 h beforehand. For ITT, mice were fasted for 6 h beforehand as previously mentioned [20]. Fasting blood glucose levels (0 min) were measured before insulin injection (0.75 U/kg, Sigma Aldrich, USA), and subsequent blood glucose levels were recorded at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min post-injection using the OneTouch® SelectSimple™ glucometer (LifeScan Inc., China).

Barnes maze

The Barnes maze tests spatial memory in animals using a circular platform with 20 equidistant holes, one of which hides an escape box. The platform is divided into four quadrants, each marked with different colors and shapes. The day before the experiment, mice were acclimated to the escape box for 4 min. During the training period (days 1–4), each mouse started under a box in the maze center for 30 s before exploring for 3 min. If the mouse did not find the escape box within 3 min, it was manually guided to it and stayed there for 1 min. On the test day (day 5), the escape box was removed, and the mouse explored the platform for 5 min. The escape latency, or time to first explore the target hole, was recorded. The maze and escape box were cleaned with 70 % ethanol after each trial, and the maze was rotated. All parameters were recorded using SuperMaze software (Shanghai Xinruan Information Technology Co., Ltd, China).

Novel object recognition (NOR)

The novel object recognition test assesses long-term learning and memory in mice [21]. On the first day, mice acclimated to a 40 × 40 × 40 cm open field for 5 min. On the second day, two familiar objects were placed centrally, and mice explored them for 5 min. On the third day, one familiar object was replaced with a novel one, and exploration was recorded for 5 min. The preference index was calculated as (novel object exploration time)/(total exploration time), and the discrimination index as (novel object exploration time - familiar object exploration time)/(total exploration time). The open field was cleaned with 70 % ethanol after each trial, and data were recorded using SuperMaze software (Shanghai Xinruan Information Technology Co., Ltd, China).

[18F]-FDG PET

After 12 weeks of MR treatment, three mice from each of the db/db + ND and db/db + MR groups underwent PET scans before sacrifice. Following a 12-h fast, mice were injected with [18F]-FDG through the tail vein and anesthetized with 2 % isoflurane in a 65 %/35 % nitrous oxide/oxygen gas mixture. The injection dose was based on the body weight of each mouse. After 30 min, mice were scanned for 20 min using a PET/SPECT/CT systems (Madic Technology Co., Ltd., China). All analyses were conducted using PMOD (V3.5, PMOD Technologies) using tracer-specific templates for spatial normalization.

Real Time-qPCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from tissues using TRIzol (AG21102, ACCURATE BIOTECHNOLOGY(HUNAN）CO.,LTD，ChangSha，China) and RNA purity was assessed with a NanoDrop OneC-UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (840–317400, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA). The PrimeScript RT Master Mix Reverse Transcription Kit (Shandong Sparkjade Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China) was used to reverse 1 μg RNA into cDNA. mRNA levels were quantified with LightCycler® 96 System (Roche, Swiss). 2-△△Ct was used to calculate relative gene expression changes. The primer sequences are listed in Table S3.

Western blot

The total tissue proteins from 6 mice per group were extracted using kits (PL001, ZhongHuiHeCai Biotechnology, China). The protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (A55864, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) before equal amounts of protein were loaded and separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). For liver protein, the total proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane using a wet transfer apparatus. Membranes were blocked with 5 % (w/v) skim milk powder for 2 h and incubated with primary antibodies, rabbit anti-FGF21 (Abcam, ab171941, 1:1000), mouse anti-β-actin (Santa Cruz, sc-47778, 1:2000) at 4 °C overnight. The secondary antibody was incubated at room temperature for 2 h (1:5000). The immunoreactive bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent and scanned using a ChemiDoc XRS imaging system (ChemiDoc™ XRS+, Bio-Rad). Quantification of the Western blot results was analyzed by Image J analysis software (National Institutes of Health, Scion Corporation, USA).

The protein expressions in the cortex and hippocampus were detected using Protein Simple Jess system (Protein Simple, USA). Briefly, the protein samples were prepared after dilution with a sample buffer. The protein samples were mixed with a master mix and heated at 95 °C for 5 min to denature the samples. Subsequently, 3 μL of denatured protein, chemiluminescent substrate, primary antibody, and HRP-conjugated secondary antibody were added to the corresponding wells of the assay plate. A biotinylated ladder box (12–230 kDa) was integrated into each assay. The plates and capillaries were then placed on the Jess machine for automated protein electrophoresis, blocking, antibody incubation, and signal detection. The results were analyzed by Compass software (Protein Simple, USA). The primary antibodies used for Jess simple Western analysis were: rabbit anti-phospho-AMPKα (Cell Signaling Technology, #2535, 1:50), rabbit anti-AMPKα (Cell Signaling Technology, #5831, 1:50), rabbit anti-GLUT4 (Abcam, ab33780, 1:50), rabbit anti-FGFR1 (Cell Signaling Technology, #9740, 1:50), rabbit anti-PSD-95 (Abcam, ab18258, 1:50), rabbit anti-Syn (Cell Signaling Technology, #36406, 1:50), mouse anti-β-actin (Santa Cruz, sc-47778, 1:50).

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry

Histological brain and liver sections were fixed with 4 % (v/v) paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. The sections were then rehydrated in xylene and ethanol through a series of decreasing concentration gradients (100 %, 90 %, 80 %, and 70 % ethanol) for 5 min each, followed by washing in PBS (pH 7.4) five times, with 4-min intervals between washes. Histopathological staining was performed using H&E. For immunohistochemistry, sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (IBA-1, Abcam, ab178847, 1:100; NEUN, Abcam, ab104224, 1:200; GLUT4, Abcam, ab33780, 1:100; 8-OHdG, Abcam, ab48508, 1:200; PSD-95, Abcam, ab18258, 1:200; FGF21, Abcam, ab171941, 1:250) diluted in 0.01 M PBS. Subsequently, the sections were washed 5 times with PBS. For immunofluorescence, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated to IgG biotin, CY3, or Alexa Fluor 488 for 2 h at room temperature. The sections were washed 3 times and then covered with mounting medium containing 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Sigma-Aldrich, F6057). For immunohistochemistry, sections were placed in 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution containing 0.03 % H2O2 for 5–8 min. Hematoxylin was added dropwise to the sections to stain cell nuclei. Finally, the sections were sealed. Microscopic images were obtained using a fluorescence microscope (DM5000 B, Leica, Germany). Images were quantitatively analyzed using Image J analysis software (National Institutes of Health, Scion Corporation, USA).

Malondialdehyde (MDA) measurement

The level of MDA (BC0025) in the cortex were quantified using commercial kits from Solarbio (Beijing, China). The quantification was performed utilizing the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method as previously described [22], which is widely recognized for its sensitivity and specificity in detecting lipid peroxidation.

ELISA assays

Serum was collected after sacrifice for ELISA assays. The plasma insulin (MB-3315A) and FGF21 (XL-EM1943) were measured by the ELISA kits (Jiangsu Meibiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China) and (Shanghai Xinle Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated as (fasting insulin concentration (mU/L) × fasting glucose concentration (mg/dL) × 0.05551)/22.5.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 7.0 (GraphPad Software Inc, USA) was used for the data analysis. All data are reported as mean ± SEM. Two-way ANOVA analysis or one-way ANOVA was used to compare differences between groups with Tukey's test for multiple comparisons. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Effects of MR on peripheral insulin sensitivity and FGF21 signaling pathway in db/db mice

Eight-week-old male db/db mice and their m/m littermates were subjected to either a normal diet (0.86 % methionine, w/w, ND) or a methionine-restricted diet (0.17 % methionine, w/w, MR) for a duration of 13 weeks, as illustrated in Fig. 1A. The average weekly food and water intake of every db/db mice was significantly higher than that of m/m mice (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1B and C). The body weights from weeks 0–13 are presented in Fig. 1D. MR significantly increased the average weekly water intake (p = 0.0831) and reduced body weight gain (p < 0.05) in m/m mice; however, these changes were not observed in db/db mice (Fig. 1C and E). To investigate the effect of MR on peripheral insulin sensitivity in db/db mice, ITT was performed following the MR. The alterations in blood glucose levels within 120 min after insulin injection are shown in Fig. 1E. As expected, the area under the curve was higher in db/db mice compared to m/m mice (p < 0.01); however, this effect was significantly mitigated by MR in db/db mice (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1F). Additionally, db/db mice exhibited higher fasting glucose concentration and HOMA-IR values compared to m/m mice (p < 0.01); MR significantly reduced fasting glucose concentration and HOMA-IR values in db/db mice (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1G and I). MR also reduced fasting insulin concentration in m/m mice (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1H). GLUT4 in subcutaneous white adipose tissue (sWAT) and gastrocnemius muscle is crucial for glucose uptake into peripheral tissues [23]. Our data demonstrated that MR reversed the marked reduction in GLUT4 expression observed in the sWAT and gastrocnemius muscle of db/db mice (p < 0.01) (Fig. S1A).

Effects of MR on cognitive function and synaptic plasticity in db/db mice

In this study, we employed the Barnes maze and novel object recognition tests to assess the influence of MR on cognitive function in db/db mice. In the Barnes maze experiment, following four days of training (Fig. 2B), db/db mice exhibited a significantly longer escape latency to locate the target hole compared to m/m mice on the fifth test day (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). MR notably decreased the escape latency in db/db mice (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). The representative tracks of the test day are depicted in Fig. 2A. In addition, MR significantly increased the preference index (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2E) and discrimination index (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2F) of db/db mice in the novel object recognition test. The representative tracks are depicted in Fig. 2D. Further, neuronal morphological changes in db/db mice brain tissues were assessed using H&E staining. Severe nuclear condensation and neuronal shrinkage were observed in the hippocampal CA1 region (Fig. 2G) and the cortex (Fig. S2A) of db/db mice brains, while MR alleviated these neuronal morphological changes. Postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD-95) plays a crucial role in synaptic transmission and synaptic plasticity, thereby influencing learning and memory function [24]. Immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. 2G, Fig. S2A) revealed that the expression of PSD-95 protein in the CA1 and cortex of db/db mice was significantly lower compared to m/m mice (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2H and I). MR markedly elevated the PSD-95 protein level in the CA1 and cortex of db/db mice (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2H and I).

Effects of MR on neuroinflammation and oxidative stress in db/db mice

Neurons have the capability to release ATP to mitigate the excessive activation of microglia [25], which in turn reduces inflammatory response. In comparison to the m/m mice, the db/db mice exhibited a significant increase in the number of activated microglia cells within the cortex (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3C, Fig. S3A). MR markedly reduced the number of activated microglia cells in CA1 (p < 0.05) and cortex of db/db mice (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3A–C). Additionally, the mRNA expression level of the proinflammatory cytokine Tnf-α were significantly upregulated in db/db mice compared to m/m mice (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3D). MR significantly reduced the mRNA expression levels of both Tnf-α (p < 0.01) and IL-1β (p < 0.05) in db/db mice (Fig. 3D). MR also elicited an upregulation in the mRNA expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 in m/m mice (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3D). The level of MDA, a lipid peroxidation marker, in db/db mice was significantly higher than that in m/m mice (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3E). MR markedly decreased cortex MDA level in both m/m (p < 0.01) and db/db mice (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3E). Compared to m/m mice, db/db mice had a greater number of 8-OHdG positive cells in the hippocampal CA1 (p < 0.05) and cortex (p < 0.01), which was significantly attenuated by MR treatment (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3F–H, Fig. S3B).

Effects of MR on glucose uptake and FGFR1/AMPK/GLUT4 signaling pathway in the brain of db/db mice

[18F]-FDG PET CT scans reveal reduced glucose uptake and metabolism in the brains of diabetic patients in previous studies [26]. Similarly, we found that MR significantly increased FDG uptake in the brains of db/db mice, particularly in the hippocampus, cortex, and amygdala regions (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4A–C). Glucose transporters in the brain are responsible for maintaining the balance of glucose transport within the brain. The mRNA expression of Glut1 in the brains of db/db mice was significantly downregulated compared to m/m mice (p < 0.01), while there was no significant difference in Glut4 mRNA expression between m/m + ND and db/db + ND groups (p > 0.05) (Fig. 4D). MR upregulated the mRNA expression of Glut4 in db/db mice (p < 0.05) and decreased the mRNA expression of Glut1 in m/m mice (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, our results showed that the number of NEUN and GLUT4 positive cells in the hippocampal CA1 region and cortex of db/db mice was significantly lower compared to m/m mice (p < 0.05), while MR reversed this effect (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4E–G, Fig. S4A). GLUT4 is upregulated by activated AMPK, which enhances glucose utilization and subsequently improves neuronal activity and homeostasis [27]. Consistently, Western blot analysis (Fig. 4H) demonstrated that the protein expression levels of GLUT4 (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4I) and p-AMPKα (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4J) were downregulated in the brains of db/db mice compared to m/m mice. Following MR intervention, the protein levels of GLUT4 (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4I) and p-AMPKα (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4J) were significantly increased in the brains of db/db mice. FGF21 directly activates the AMPK pathway by FGFR1 signaling [28]. Notably, the protein expression level of FGFR1 (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4K) was downregulated in the brains of db/db mice compared to m/m mice. Following MR intervention, the protein level of FGFR1 (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4K) was significantly increased in the brains of db/db mice.

These results indicate that MR may alleviate glucose homeostasis in db/db mice, likely attribute to the activation of FGFR1/AMPK/GLUT4 signaling pathway in brain.

Effects of MR on FGF21 signaling pathway in db/db mice

FGF21 is a crucial regulator for the metabolism of specific amino acid restriction, particularly through its role in insulin sensitivity [29]. Hence, we further investigated the influence of MR on the FGF21 signaling pathway in db/db mice. Compared to m/m mice, db/db mice exhibited elevated serum FGF21 level (p < 0.01); however, MR significantly reduced serum FGF21 level in db/db mice (p < 0.05) to the level of m/m mice (p > 0.05) (Fig. 5A). To elucidate this phenomenon, we examined the expression of FGF21 co-receptors (β-Klotho, Fgfr1) in its target tissues, including the liver, sWAT, and gastrocnemius muscle. It was observed that compared to m/m mice, db/db mice had lower expression levels of β-Klotho and Fgfr1 genes in sWAT (p < 0.01) (Fig. S5A). Following MR intervention, the expression levels of β-Klotho (p < 0.01) and Fgfr1 (p < 0.01) in db/db mice were significantly elevated in sWAT (Fig. S5A). No changes in β-Klotho and Fgfr1 mRNA expression were observed in muscle among groups (p > 0.05) (Fig. S5B). Serum FGF21 is mainly derived from the liver [30]. The upstream genes (Gcn2, Atf4, Pparα) of hepatic FGF21 were detected [31,32]. There were no statistically significant differences observed in the expression levels of the Gcn2 gene among groups (p > 0.05). The mRNA expression of Atf4 (p < 0.05) and Pparα (p < 0.01) genes in db/db mice were significantly reduced compared to those in m/m mice. Following MR intervention, the expression of Atf4 and Pparα (p < 0.05) genes in db/db mice was significantly upregulated compared to db/db mice treated with ND. MR upregulated the expression of Fgf21 in m/m (p < 0.01) and db/db (p < 0.05) mice (Fig. 5B). Additionally, the expression of FGF21 co-receptors (β-Klotho, Fgfr1) in the liver were assessed. It was observed that compared to m/m mice, db/db mice had lower expression levels of β-Klotho and Fgfr1 genes in liver (p < 0.01) (Fig. 5B). Following MR intervention, the expression levels of β-Klotho (p < 0.01) and Fgfr1 (p < 0.05) in the liver of db/db mice were significantly elevated (Fig. 5B). In line with the findings from Fgf21 gene transcription, immunohistochemistry (Fig. 5C and D) and Western blot (Fig. 5E and F, Fig. S5C) analyses consistently showed that MR enhanced the expression of FGF21 in the livers of both m/m and db/db mice (p < 0.05). Notably, MR significantly increased the cortex FGF21 level in m/m (p < 0.01) and db/db mice (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5G). No significant differences were found in brain Fgf21 and β-Klotho mRNA expression levels among the four groups (p > 0.05) (Fig. 5H). However, the expression level of the Fgfr1 gene was significantly lower in db/db mice compared to m/m mice (p < 0.01). MR notably reduced the Fgfr1 mRNA expression level in m/m mice (p < 0.01) but significantly increased it in db/db mice (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5H).

The role of brain FGFR1 in MR mitigating cognitive impairment in db/db mice

To investigate the regulating role of brain FGFR1 in MR-mediated cognitive improvement in db/db mice, we employed the AAV2/9-mediated delivery system to specifically knockdown FGFR1 expression in the brain. After i.c.v. injection, db/db mice underwent a two-week recovery period before being divided into three groups. These groups were then subjected to either a ND or a MR for a duration of 4 weeks. At the end of the fourth week of dietary intervention, ITT and behavioral tests were conducted. The experimental scheme is shown in Fig. 6A. Knockdown of brain FGFR1 did not significantly impact food intake, water intake, nor body weight gain across the groups (p > 0.05) (Figs. S6A–C).

The db/db + Sh-NC + MR group performed better than db/db + Sh-NC + ND group in the Barnes maze (Fig. 6F and H), particularly evidenced by a significant reduction in escape latency on the fifth test day (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6E). In contrast, knocking down FGFR1 in the brains of db/db mice fed with MR exhibited a significant increase in escape latency on the fifth test day compared to the db/db + Sh-NC + MR group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 6E). Additionally, the db/db + Sh-NC + MR group also performed better than db/db + Sh-NC + ND group in the novel object recognition (Fig. 6G), which was demonstrated by significantly higher discrimination and preference indices in the db/db + Sh-NC + MR group compared to the db/db + Sh-NC + ND group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6I and J). Conversely, knocking down FGFR1 in the brains of db/db mice fed with MR resulted in significantly lower discrimination and preference indices than the db/db + Sh-NC + MR group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6I and J).

These findings indicate that knockdown of brain FGFR1 may not modulate the MR-mediated improvement of peripheral insulin sensitivity in db/db mice. In contrast, knockdown of brain FGFR1 may abolish, at least in part, the beneficial effects of MR on cognitive function in db/db mice.

The role of brain FGFR1 in MR mitigating synaptic damage, neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress in db/db mice

Neuronal morphological changes in FGFR1-treated db/db mice brain tissues were assessed using H&E staining. As shown in Fig. S7A, severe nuclear condensation and neuronal shrinkage were observed in the hippocampal CA1, DG, and CA3 regions in the brains of db/db + Sh-NC + ND group, while MR alleviated these phenomena. Notably, the CA1 and DG of the db/db + Sh-FGFR1+MR group exhibited intense nuclear staining, and numerous deformed cellular morphologies were observed in the CA3 region. Synaptic function depends on the healthy morphology of brain cells. The expression levels of PSD-95 and Syn proteins in the brains of the db/db + Sh-NC + MR group were significantly higher than those in the db/db + Sh-NC + ND group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 7A–C). Whereas, compared to the db/db + Sh-NC + MR group, the expression levels of PSD-95 and Syn proteins were significantly reduced in the hippocampus of db/db + Sh-FGFR1+MR group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 7A–C). Compared to db/db + Sh-NC + ND group, the mRNA expression levels of Tnf-α and IL-1β were significantly downregulated (p < 0.05), while IL-10 was significantly upregulated in the db/db + Sh-NC + MR group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 7D). After FGFR1 knockdown, Tnf-α (p < 0.01), IL-6 (p < 0.01), IL-1β (p < 0.05), and iNos (p < 0.05) were significantly upregulated in db + Sh-NC mice fed with MR (Fig. 7D). Superoxide dismutases (Sod1, Sod2) are enzymes which catalyze the conversion of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in intracellular [33]. While NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) is a primary source of ROS, which has been established in the context of brain damage. Our results showed that db/db + Sh-NC + MR group had significantly higher Sod1 and lower Nox4 mRNA expression levels compared to db/db + Sh-NC + ND group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 7E). After Sh-FGFR1 injection, the mRNA expression levels of Sod1 (p < 0.05) and Sod2 (p < 0.01) were down-regulated and Nox4 was up-regulated (p < 0.05) respectively in db/db mice fed with MR (Fig. 7E).

The role of brain FGFR1 in modulating the AMPK/GLUT4 signaling pathway in response to MR

To further understand whether FGFR1 knockdown in the brain of db/db mice affects downstream signaling and to what extent, we examined the AMPK/GLUT4 signaling pathway. Consistent with our previous results, MR upregulated Fgfr1 mRNA expression in the brains of db/db + Sh-NC mice (p < 0.01) (Fig. 8A). As expected, Fgfr1 mRNA expression in the db/db + Sh-FGFR1+MR group was significantly lower than in the db/db + Sh-NC + MR group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 8A). Furthermore, Glut1 (p < 0.05) and Glut4 (p = 0.0678) mRNA expression were significantly upregulated in the db/db + Sh-NC + MR group compared to the db/db + Sh-NC + ND group (Fig. 8B). Compared to the db/db + Sh-NC + MR group, the expression levels of Glut1 and Glut4 mRNA were significantly downregulated in the db/db + Sh-FGFR1+MR group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 8B). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that the number of NEUN and GLUT4-positive cells in hippocampus was significantly higher in the db/db + Sh-NC + MR group compared to the db/db + Sh-NC + ND group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 8C and D, Fig. S8A). However, this effect was reversed following FGFR1 knockdown (p < 0.05) (Fig. 8C and D). Consistent with the RT-qPCR results, FGFR1 protein expression level in the db/db + Sh-NC + MR group were significantly higher than those in the db/db + Sh-NC + ND group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 8E and F). The treatment of Sh-FGFR1 resulted in significantly lower FGFR1 protein levels in the brains of db/db mice fed with MR compared to the db/db + Sh-NC + MR group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 8E and F). Notably, compared to the db/db + Sh-NC + ND group, the protein levels of p-AMPKα (p < 0.05) and GLUT4 (p < 0.01) were markedly elevated in the db/db + Sh-NC + MR group (Fig. 8E–G&H). Whereas, the expression levels of p-AMPKα (p < 0.05) and GLUT4 (p < 0.01) proteins were significantly reduced in the db/db + Sh-FGFR1+MR group compared to the db/db + Sh-NC + MR group (Fig. 8E–G&H).

Discussion

In this study, we found that MR improved peripheral insulin sensitivity, cognitive function, and glucose metabolism in db/db mice. These effects were linked to increased PSD-95 expression, reduced neuroinflammation and oxidative stress in the brain, and activation of the FGFR1/AMPK/GLUT4 pathway in the brain and the ATF4/PPARα/FGF21 pathway in the liver. Knocking down the FGF21 co-receptor FGFR1 in the brain diminished MR's neuroprotective effects and inhibited the activation of the FGFR1/AMPK/GLUT4 pathway. Our results further suggest that MR may effectively address DACI through the FGFR1/AMPK/GLUT4 pathway.

MR can improve insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues and regulate hyperglycemia [34]. Consistent with previous findings, db/db mice exhibited insulin resistance whereas MR alleviated this condition and enhanced the uptake of blood glucose into peripheral tissues (Fig. 1, Fig. S1). FGF21 is a potent regulator of the metabolic effects mediated by MR, mainly through improving insulin sensitivity [35]. Specific protein restriction can increase FGF21 levels in rodents and humans via activation of hepatic GCN2, ATF4, PPARα [[36], [37], [38]]. Our study found that MR activated the FGF21 signaling pathway in the liver of both m/m and db/db mice, corroborating previous research findings (Fig. 5). However, MR did not modulate GCN2 mRNA levels in the livers of both m/m and db/db mice (Fig. 5), suggesting that GCN2 may not influence MR's ability to enhance hepatic FGF21 transcription and release or improve insulin sensitivity [39]. Circulating FGF21 is mainly derived from the liver [40] and targets several tissues, mainly adipose tissue [41] but also the liver, muscle and brain [42]. Despite the multiple metabolic benefits of FGF21, increased circulating FGF21 levels are often seen in obesity and diabetes, indicating a state of “FGF21 resistance” [43]. Consistently, our study also found that serum FGF21 levels were higher in db/db mice than m/m mice. This was also accompanied by decreased expression of the FGF21 co-receptor β-Klotho and Fgfr1 in liver and sWAT (Fig. 5, Fig. S5), which were also indicators of FGF21 resistance. However, MR alleviated FGF21 resistance in db/db mice by increased expressions of β-Klotho and Fgfr1 in liver and sWAT and reduced the circulating FGF21 levels (Fig. 5, Fig. S5), suggesting that MR enhanced the sensitivity and utilization of FGF21 in target tissues in db/db mice. Consistent with our findings, increased serum FGF21 levels are inversely correlated with whole-body insulin sensitivity in humans [44].

The central nervous system (CNS) is one of the important targets of FGF21 by crossing over the blood brain barrier (BBB) [45], which makes FGF21 have potential neuroprotective effects. FGF21 has been shown to mitigate cognitive impairment in rodents induced by a high-fat diet, enhance hippocampal synaptic plasticity [15], and especially reduce oxidative stress [46] and neuroinflammation [47]. Dysregulated FGF21 signaling in the CNS results in impaired brain glucose metabolism and memory formation [17]. Consistent with previous studies, cognitive deficits and impaired brain glucose metabolism were alleviated in db/db mice with activated FGF21 signaling in brain induced by MR (Fig. 2, Fig. 4). Alterations in neuronal energy metabolism can impact the physiological activities of microglia [48]. Glucose enters brain from circulation mainly through GLUT1 transport across BBB and subsequently taken up by neurons via GLUT4 [49]. Neurons further convert glucose into ATP and release to mitigate the excessive activation of microglia, which in turn reduces inflammatory response [50]. Consistent with the [18F]-FDG PET data (Fig. 4), we speculate that MR may augment glucose uptake and utilization in brain neurons by upregulating the expression and translocation of GLUT4. The increased inflammatory response in the brains of diabetic mice is characterized by the overactivation of microglia [51] and the upregulation of inflammatory factors such as TNF-α [52]. Consistent with previous studies, our findings revealed that MR alleviated neuroinflammation in db/db mice (Fig. 3). Additionally, properly functioning microglia safeguard synaptic plasticity from oxidative stress by decreasing the production of reactive oxygen species and clearing dysfunctional tissue components [53]. Our results indicated that MR reduced the levels of MDA and the number of cells expressing 8-OHdG, both of which were elevated in db/db mice (Fig. 3). This reduction may explain the beneficial effects of MR on synaptic function in db/db mice (Fig. 2). A limitation of our study is the insufficient exploration of the relationship between peripheral FGF21 signals, such as FGF21 resistance, and cognitive function.

FGFR1 is mainly expressed in neurons and essential for long-term memory and neurogenesis [54]. MR significantly increased the levels of FGF21 without affecting its mRNA expression in the cortex of db/db mice (Fig. 5). This suggests that MR may enhance the transfer of circulating FGF21 to the brain, consistent with the observed reduction in circulating FGF21 levels in the db/db + MR group (Fig. 5). Moreover, the downregulated FGFR1 expression levels in the brain of db/db mice were reversed following MR (Fig. 5), similar to the changes observed in peripheral tissues (Fig. S1). This indicates that MR enhances the sensitivity of the db/db brain to FGF21 by upregulating FGFR1. There is also strong evidence indicate that endocrine FGF21 directly activates the AMPK pathway via FGFR1 signaling, thereby triggering AMPK signaling in its target tissues [28]. AMPK regulates glucose and lipid metabolism, and its abnormal activity is observed to disrupt these processes in the brains of T2DM [55]. AMPK activation can enhance the expression of GLUT4 or GLUT1, thereby increasing glucose utilization and leading to the production of substantial amounts of ATP, which improves neuronal activity and homeostasis [27]. Here, we found that AMPK and its downstream signaling pathways, GLUT4, were impaired in the brains of db/db mice (Fig. 4). MR reversed this impairment, which may explain the observed improvements in glucose metabolism and cell morphology in the brains of db/db mice (Fig. 2, Fig. 4). Notably, loss of β-Klotho expression in specific brain regions blocks FGF21's ability to respond to nutrient signaling [56]. Although we did not observe the effect of MR on brain β-Klotho in db/db mice, the role of brain β-Klotho in cognitive improvement through nutritional signals remains to be elucidated.

Our results demonstrated that although specific knocking down brain FGFR1 did not significantly affect the peripheral metabolic benefits and FGF21 signaling induced by MR, it interfered with the MR-mediated improvement in cognitive function in db/db mice (Fig. 6, Fig. S5). This indicates that the effect of knocking down brain FGFR1 is specific to the brain instead of periphery. Consequently, the improvement in cognitive function achieved by MR, whether through indirect enhancement of peripheral glucose metabolism or direct effects on the brain, necessitates FGFR1 signaling. Additionally, even with MR treatment, synaptic plasticity, anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidant effects in db/db mice remained impaired following FGFR1/AMPK/GLUT4 inhibition (Fig. 7, Fig. 8). These results may be attributable to the impact of FGFR1 gene variation on energy metabolism [57] and its disruption of the inflammation and redox state. Inhibition of AMPK can directly result in excessive ROS production by diminishing the regulation of mitochondrial efficiency [55]. SOD1 and SOD2 facilitate the conversion of intracellular ROS [33]. While NOX4 is a primary source of ROS, which has been confirmed in the hippocampal microglia of db/db mice under high glucose conditions [57]. NOX4 promotes glial cell ferroptosis via oxidative stress-induced lipid peroxidation [58] and exposure of saturated fatty acids to cytoplasmic ROS leads to reactive aldehydes, such as MDA [59]. ROS also attack DNA and cause DNA peroxidation in diabetic brains [60]. These findings elucidate that MR mitigates ROS production via the FGFR1/AMPK/GLUT4 pathway, thereby decreasing lipid peroxidation and DNA oxidative damage in the brains of db/db mice (Fig. 3). Overall, these results suggest that FGFR1 activation by MR is crucial for the initiation of downstream cascade signaling and biological processes.

Overall, our findings suggest that MR provides neuroprotection against DACI by activating the FGFR1/AMPK/GLUT4 pathway via FGF21, improving brain glucose metabolism, reducing neuroinflammation, and restoring synaptic plasticity. While effective for DACI, MR offers limited benefits in mice with normal glucose metabolism, emphasizing the need for careful subject selection.
