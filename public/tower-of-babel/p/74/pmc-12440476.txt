Association between Life’s Crucial 9 and sarcopenia: The mediating role of testosterone partially
Authors: Bikai You, Zhangxin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sarcopenia, an age-related decline in skeletal muscle mass and function, is influenced by modifiable lifestyle factors. Life’s Crucial 9 (LC9), a composite cardiovascular health metric, may mitigate sarcopenia risk, potentially mediated by testosterone. However, population-level evidence and mechanistic insights remain limited. This cross-sectional study analyzed 5726 adults from the 2011 to 2016 the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. Sarcopenia was diagnosed using sex-specific thresholds for appendicular skeletal muscle mass index. LC9 scores (0–100) were calculated across 9 domains. Multivariable logistic regression evaluated LC9–sarcopenia associations, while mediation and restricted cubic spline analyses assessed testosterone’s role and nonlinear relationships. Higher LC9 scores were inversely associated with sarcopenia prevalence (fully adjusted odds ratio per unit increase: 0.96, 95% confidence interval: 0.95–0.97). Participants with ideal LC9 adherence had 82% lower risk (odds ratio: 0.18, 95% confidence interval: 0.10–0.32) versus low adherence. The association was partially mediated by testosterone, which accounted for a significant 9.85% of the total effect. A nonlinear threshold effect emerged, with risk reduction accelerating at LC9 scores > 73.3. Subgroup analyses confirmed consistent associations across demographics and comorbidities. Our findings suggest that adherence to LC9 is associated with a lower risk of sarcopenia, partially mediated through testosterone homeostasis. Early improvement in LC9 metrics, especially in individuals with poor baseline cardiovascular health, may help mitigate age-related muscle decline, highlighting the potential value of integrated lifestyle interventions. Further longitudinal studies are needed to confirm causality and elucidate the long-term relationship between LC9 optimization and sarcopenia prevention.

1. Introduction

Sarcopenia, an age-related syndrome characterized by progressive deterioration of skeletal muscle mass, strength, and functional capacity, has emerged as a pivotal geriatric health concern. Its escalating prevalence in aging populations and intricate associations with chronic low-grade inflammation, metabolic dyshomeostasis, and adipose–muscle axis dysregulation underscore its multifactorial pathogenesis.[1] Although genetic susceptibility and biological aging constitute intrinsic risk factors, accumulating evidence underscores the critical role of modifiable lifestyle factors in modulating sarcopenia progression.[2]

The American Heart Association’s Life’s Essential 8 framework,[3] recently expanded to Life’s Crucial 9 (LC9) through the incorporation of sleep health metrics, offers a standardized approach to cardiovascular risk mitigation. This composite index integrates 9 domains: dietary quality, physical activity patterns, nicotine exposure, body composition, lipid profiles, glycemic control, blood pressure management, sleep health, and psychological stress regulation.[4] Importantly, emerging research reveals that these metrics demonstrate pleiotropic biological effects extending beyond cardiometabolic protection. Recent mediation analyses have identified systemic inflammatory mediators and endocrine pathways as critical intermediaries linking LC9 to diverse clinical outcomes, including psoriasis,[5] overactive bladder,[6] and infertility.[7]

Testosterone, a key anabolic hormone, serves as a central regulator of muscle protein synthesis and neuromuscular integrity.[8] Clinical studies consistently demonstrate that low serum testosterone levels independently predict accelerated muscle loss and sarcopenia progression, particularly in older males.[9] Mechanistically, specific components of LC9 (notably physical activity optimization, adiposity reduction, and stress mitigation) exert direct influences on testosterone metabolism. For example, visceral adiposity enhances aromatase-driven testosterone-to-estrogen conversion, while chronic psychological stress triggers cortisol-mediated suppression of gonadal steroidogenesis.[10] Given that diet and exercise directly affect body composition, physical function, and metabolic outcomes, large-scale clinical trials are essential to evaluate the effectiveness and tolerability of hormone replacement in treating sarcopenia.[11] A growing body of evidence is aimed at elucidating the sex-specific differences in hormonal, inflammatory pathways, and molecular mechanisms associated with sarcopenia.[12]

Despite robust mechanistic plausibility, 2 critical knowledge gaps remain unresolved. First, no population-level study has comprehensively evaluated the aggregate effect of LC9 adherence on sarcopenia risk. Second, the quantitative contribution of testosterone as a potential mediator between lifestyle factors and musculoskeletal health outcomes remains uncharacterized. To address these gaps, we hypothesize that optimal adherence to LC9 metrics may attenuate sarcopenia susceptibility, with testosterone homeostasis serving as a key intermediary mechanism (a paradigm analogous to atherogenic indices mediating metabolic-bladder interactions).

2. Methods

2.1. Data source and population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is an ongoing nationally representative cross-sectional survey designed to assess the health and nutritional status of the U.S. population. This study utilized data from the 2011 to 2016 cycles, participants were excluded based on the following criteria: Missing demographic information (n = 7084); incomplete sarcopenia diagnostic data (n = 9728); missing LC9-related data (n = 7292); other covariate deficiencies (n = 72). After applying these exclusion criteria, 5726 participants were retained for final analysis (Fig. 1).

Flowchart for inclusion of study participants from NHANES 2021 to 2016. NHANES = The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey.

2.2. Definition of sarcopenia and serum total testosterone measurements

The diagnosis of sarcopenia was established according to sex-specific thresholds proposed by the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health Sarcopenia Project.[13] This consensus framework defines sarcopenia using the adjusted skeletal muscle mass by body mass index (ASMI), calculated as adjusted skeletal muscle mass (measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry) divided by height squared (kg/m²). The diagnostic of sarcopenia thresholds are: men: ASMI ≤ 0.789 kg/m²; women: ASMI ≤ 0.512 kg/m². This method has been widely applied to assess the relationship between sarcopenia and adverse health outcomes such as obstructive sleep apnea and overactive bladder.[14,15] The quantification of serum total testosterone was performed using isotope dilution liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry, a gold-standard methodology developed and validated by the CDC’s Clinical Chemistry Branch.[16,17]

2.3. Calculation of the LC9 score

The LC9 score comprises 9 core components: dietary quality, physical activity level, nicotine exposure status, sleep health, body mass index (BMI), lipid profile, blood glucose level, blood pressure status, and mental health. Each cardiovascular health (CVH) component was standardized to a 0 to 100 points scale using validated criteria established in prior studies (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/P962).[4,18] The composite LC9 score was calculated as the arithmetic mean of these standardized component scores, providing a comprehensive assessment of overall CVH status. Furthermore, referring to the classification of LE8, we have divided CVH into 3 levels (low, moderate, and ideal CVH).[19]

2.4. Assessment of covariates

In this study, we adjusted for potential confounding factors based on previously established methodologies.[20,21] The covariates included: sociodemographic characteristics: age, sex, and race/ethnicity (Mexican American, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and other races); socioeconomic status: poverty-income ratio (PIR) and education level (<high school, high school, and more than high school); lifestyle factors: alcohol consumption (never, former, mild, moderate, and heavy); dietary factors: total energy intake and protein consumption, and comorbidities: chronic kidney disease (CKD). Notably, smoking status, hypertension, diabetes, and BMI were excluded from adjustment as these variables are integral components of the LC9.

2.5. Statistical analysis

A descriptive statistic was stratified by participants’ disease status. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard error and compared between groups using Student t tests. Categorical variables are reported as frequency counts (n) with percentages (%), with group comparisons performed using Chi-square tests. To examine the association between LC9 and sarcopenia risk, we constructed 3 weighted multivariable logistic regression models to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs): crude model 1: unadjusted; model 1: adjusted for demographic factors (age, sex, race/ethnicity, and PIR); model 2: further adjusted for alcohol consumption, CKD, total protein intake, and total energy intake. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential effect modifications. Additionally, given testosterone’s established role in sarcopenia pathogenesis, we performed mediation analysis to assess its potential mediating effect in the LC9–sarcopenia relationship. Furthermore, the nonlinear relationship between serum LC9 score and sarcopenia risk was investigated using restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression models.

All analyses were conducted using R Statistical Software (Version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with survey weights applied in accordance with NHANES analytic guidelines.

3.2. Correlation between LC9 score and sarcopenia

Multivariable logistic regression models consistently demonstrated an inverse association between LC9 scores and sarcopenia prevalence across all models. In the fully adjusted model, each score increase in LC9 score was associated with a 4% reduced risk of sarcopenia (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.95–0.97, P < .001) (Table 2). When LC9 was categorized into tertiles, moderate CVH with OR = 0.58 (95% CI: 0.38–0.89), ideal CVH with OR = 0.18 (95% CI: 0.10–0.32), compared with low CVH, indicating progressively lower sarcopenia risk with higher CVH status (Table 3).

3.3. Subgroup analysis and mediation effect

Stratified analyses were conducted across sex, hypertension status, diabetes status, dyslipidemia, alcohol consumption, smoking status, and CKD status. As depicted in Figure 2, LC9 maintained significant inverse associations with sarcopenia risk in all subgroups (P-interaction > .05 for all).

3.4. Mediation effect and the RCS analysis

Mediation analysis revealed that testosterone partially explained the LC9–sarcopenia association. The total effect of LC9 on sarcopenia was β = −0.05 (P < .001), with testosterone mediating 9.85% of this relationship (Fig. 3). RCS analysis demonstrated a statistically significant nonlinear relationship between circulating LC9 levels and sarcopenia risk (P-nonlinear < .001). Notably, the dose–response curve exhibited a threshold effect, with sarcopenia risk showing a sharp decline when LC9 score surpassed 73.3 (Fig. 4).

4. Discussion

Our study demonstrates a significant inverse association between adherence to LC9 metrics and sarcopenia risk, with testosterone partially mediating this relationship (proportion mediated: 9.85%). Each unit increase in LC9 score correlated with a marked reduction in sarcopenia risk, particularly among individuals with suboptimal baseline CVH. Notably, a nonlinear threshold effect was observed, where sarcopenia risk declined sharply once the LC9 score exceeded 73.3, suggesting that even modest improvements in LC9 adherence may yield clinically meaningful benefits.

The association between LC9 and a lower risk of sarcopenia may be explained by its synergistic association with testosterone homeostasis and anti-inflammatory pathways. Testosterone mitigates sarcopenia risk through multiple mechanisms: binding to androgen receptors in muscle cells to activate protein synthesis via DAAM2-mediated nuclear actin polymerization and RNA polymerase II recruitment, while suppressing proteolytic pathways.[22] enhancing satellite cell proliferation and differentiation, critical for muscle repair and hypertrophy.[23] reducing visceral adiposity and systemic inflammation, thereby counteracting mitochondrial dysfunction and chronic inflammation (key drivers of muscle wasting). LC9 components synergistically support these processes.[24] For instance, nutrient-dense diets rich in zinc,[25] leucine,[26] bioactive peptides,[27] and anti-inflammatory compounds promote testosterone synthesis and muscle protein turnover via different signaling. A diet rich in anti-inflammatory nutrients, as advocated by LC9, combined with the anti-inflammatory effects of testosterone, can synergistically reduce inflammation and lower sarcopenia risk. Concurrently, regular exercise (particularly resistance training) induces acute testosterone release and sustains muscle anabolism through adipokine–myokine crosstalk (e.g., elevated spexin and follistatin).[28] By optimizing body composition, LC9 adherence reduces aromatase-driven testosterone-to-estrogen conversion and cortisol-mediated suppression of steroidogenesis, further preserving muscle mass. Physical activity not only enhances muscle strength and function but also positively influences testosterone levels.[29] Exercise-induced muscle damage and repair processes trigger the release of testosterone and other anabolic hormones, promoting muscle growth and repair. Studies indicate that even one resistance training session, when maintained consistently (>10 weeks), can enhance muscle strength and attenuate age-related declines in muscle function in older adults, thereby supporting the preservation of muscle mass and reducing risks associated with sarcopenia and frailty.[30,31] These increases correlated with reductions in body fat mass, improvements in metabolic syndrome parameters, and gains in muscle mass, highlighting the role of exercise-induced adipokine–myokine crosstalk in enhancing metabolic balance and skeletal muscle health. For instance, in elderly individuals, testosterone replacement therapy has been shown to increase muscle volume and strength, highlighting its anabolic effects on skeletal muscles.[32]

Subgroup analyses revealed consistent inverse LC9–sarcopenia associations across sex, metabolic comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes), and renal function, though effects were amplified in males and individuals with cardiometabolic disorders. This enhanced protection may reflect greater baseline susceptibility to lifestyle-related inflammation and hormonal dysregulation in these populations. For example, males exhibit higher androgen receptor density, while metabolic dysfunction exacerbates inflammation-driven muscle catabolism. Our findings underscore LC9’s potential as a scalable, non-pharmacological intervention for sarcopenia prevention, particularly in high-risk subgroups.

In our study, participants with sarcopenia exhibited a higher rate of nicotine exposure. This finding is consistent with existing epidemiological evidence. As summarized by Yuan and Larsson,[33] smoking with nicotine exposure has been identified as a risk factor for sarcopenia. The potential mechanisms are likely multifaceted. First, smoking is a strong inducer of systemic inflammation, and a chronic inflammatory state can accelerate muscle loss by promoting muscle protein breakdown and inhibiting synthesis. Second, various toxic substances in tobacco may have direct toxic effects on muscle cells, impairing mitochondrial function and causing muscular oxidative stress. Furthermore, smokers are often more prone to other unhealthy lifestyle habits, which further exacerbates the risk of muscle loss. Nevertheless, this highlights the potential necessity of incorporating smoking cessation into comprehensive strategies for the prevention and management of sarcopenia.

Unlike previous studies focusing on isolated lifestyle factors, this work establishes LC9 as a holistic framework for sarcopenia mitigation. While prior investigations linked physical activity or diet quality to muscle health, our mediation analysis elucidates testosterone’s intermediary role (a novel mechanistic insight). However, we acknowledge the possibility of reverse causality, as preexisting sarcopenia may itself lead to a lower LC9 score by limiting an individual’s capacity for physical activity and potentially influencing other lifestyle behaviors. Future longitudinal studies are needed to better delineate the temporal relationship between LC9 and sarcopenia onset.

Our findings on the association between LC9 and sarcopenia risk are consistent with a growing body of literature demonstrating the pleiotropic health benefits of optimal CVH metrics. Notably, recent studies have extended the influence of LC9 beyond cardiometabolic diseases to include inflammatory and immune-mediated conditions. For example, Liu et al[5] reported a significant inverse relationship between LC9 score and psoriasis risk and identified the Systemic Inflammation Response Index as a significant mediator, accounting for 7.02% of the association. Similarly, in the context of severe abdominal aortic calcification, higher LC9 scores were associated with a 26% reduction in prevalence per 10-point increase, with Systemic Inflammation Response Index mediating 5.8% of this protective relationship.[34] While these studies collectively highlight inflammation as a common pathway linking LC9 to diverse health outcomes, our study provides novel insights into the specific mechanisms underlying sarcopenia. Beyond inflammatory pathways, we identified testosterone homeostasis as a distinct and significant mediator in the LC9–sarcopenia relationship. This suggests that while anti-inflammatory effects represent a common biological mechanism across LC9-related health benefits, endocrine pathways may play a more prominent role in musculoskeletal aging compared to other conditions. Furthermore, our finding of sex-specific effects in the testosterone-sarcopenia relationship adds another layer of complexity not explored in previous LC9 studies.

Our study possesses several strengths that enhance its validity and clinical relevance. First, it leverages a nationally representative sample from NHANES, ensuring the generalizability of the findings to the broader U.S. population. Second, the use of a comprehensive and standardized LC9 score allows for a detailed assessment of overall cardiovascular and lifestyle health. Third, the mediation analysis provides deeper insights into the biological pathways linking LC9 to sarcopenia, particularly the role of testosterone. These findings hold significant clinical implications, as they emphasize the importance of integrated lifestyle interventions in preventing and managing sarcopenia. Early optimization of LC9 metrics, especially in high-risk populations, could serve as a cost-effective and noninvasive strategy to combat age-related muscle decline and improve quality of life in older adults.

However, our study also has certain limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of a temporal relationship between LC9 adherence and sarcopenia development. Longitudinal studies are needed to confirm whether improvements in LC9 metrics over time can lead to a reduction in sarcopenia incidence. Additionally, testosterone levels were measured only once and may not reflect long-term hormonal status. Future research could benefit from repeated measurements to capture the dynamic changes in testosterone levels. Furthermore, residual confounding factors that were not accounted for in our analysis might influence the observed associations.

In conclusion, our study provides novel evidence on the association between LC9 adherence and sarcopenia risk, elucidating the mediating role of testosterone. These findings underscore the potential of LC9 as a comprehensive lifestyle intervention framework for sarcopenia prevention and management. Further research is warranted to explore the long-term effects of LC9 optimization on sarcopenia progression and to identify the most effective interventions for different population subgroups.

5. Conclusion

Our findings suggest that adherence to LC9 is associated with a lower risk of sarcopenia, partially mediated through testosterone homeostasis. Early improvement in LC9 metrics, especially in individuals with poor baseline CVH, may help mitigate age-related muscle decline, highlighting the potential value of integrated lifestyle interventions. Further longitudinal studies are needed to confirm causality and elucidate the long-term relationship between LC9 optimization and sarcopenia prevention.
