Transcriptomic dynamics of cardiac remodeling after myocardial infarction
Authors: Ardo Sanjaya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyocardial infarction (MI) initiates a cascade of biological changes ending in cardiac remodeling and heart failure. Recent studies have characterized transcriptional programs in post-infarct remodeling, focusing on discrete timepoints. This study adopted a complementary approach by constructing a temporal map of gene expression changes from minutes to weeks post-myocardial infarction. Two mouse RNA-sequencing datasets were integrated, covering post-infarction timepoints from 10 min to 28 days. Expression data were analyzed using clustering, functional enrichment, and network-based hub gene analysis to define distinct gene expression modules. Nine clusters were identified, including immune activation, extracellular matrix remodeling, mitochondrial dysfunction, and circadian rhythm disruption. Immune signatures peaked between 1 and 72 h post-MI, fibrotic remodeling and cell proliferation dominated the 3–7-day window, and fibrosis still extended to the 28-day mark. Mitochondrial dysfunction persisted into the 28th day, along with circadian rhythm and metabolic dysfunction. These trajectories highlight distinct pathways and the potential therapeutic windows across infarction. This research offers a timeline for temporally guided therapies in MI. Identifying distinct pathways and hub genes to provide a molecular basis for future studies targeting inflammation, fibrosis, metabolism, and chronobiology. Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI), driven primarily by ischemic injury to the myocardium following coronary artery occlusion, remains a leading cause of mortality and morbidity worldwide. Despite advances in reperfusion strategies and pharmacological interventions, MI is a major contributor to the global burden of heart failure (HF)1. HF is defined as a chronic, progressive syndrome and is now recognized as a public health problem. In the United States alone, HF affects approximately 6 million individuals, with projections estimating a prevalence of 8.5% among adults aged 65 and older by 20302. Long-term survival after HF diagnosis remains poor, with five-year mortality rates approaching 50%2. Therefore, understanding the dynamic events underpinning the process from MI to cardiac remodelling and heart failure development is essential to identify new therapeutic targets.

Cardiac remodeling after MI is a multifaceted and temporally orchestrated process characterized by several biological events, including cardiomyocyte death, immune activation, fibroblast expansion, and extracellular matrix reorganization3,4. This complex progression is typically divided into three overlapping yet distinct phases: an acute inflammatory phase occurring within minutes to hours post-infarction3,5, a reparative or proliferative phase over subsequent days4,6, and a chronic remodeling phase that unfolds over weeks, leading to scar maturation and possible maladaptive hypertrophy7.

Understanding the temporal dynamics of these phases is crucial for therapeutic timing. Interventions that indiscriminately modulate inflammation or fibrosis may have opposing outcomes depending on the healing phase. For instance, while early inflammation is necessary for debris clearance and initiation of repair, excessive or prolonged inflammation has been linked to adverse remodeling5,8. These implied that targeted immunomodulation within a specific temporal window could improve healing and reduce heart failure risk9. For example, the drug Colchicine has now been recommended by the American Heart Association and ESC guidelines due to its potential to reduce cardiovascular events post-MI10,11. However, the timing of administration may be an important factor, as several trials have shown different results regarding its effectiveness depending on administration time12. Therefore, research that aims to profile these changes to myocardial tissue post-infarction offers important information for time-sensitive interventions.

Transcriptomic profiling using RNA sequencing enables high-resolution tracking of gene expression dynamics, revealing regulatory networks that govern inflammation, repair, and remodeling. Recent studies have employed this approach to map early and late transcriptional responses in murine models of MI13,14. Other research has utilized single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics post myocardial infarction15,16. However, these studies focused their analyses on specific biological questions, such as immune response, and are of a limited timespan. This study integrates transcriptomic analysis using two complementary mouse datasets to address these limitations. The combined dataset covered from 10 min to 28 days post-infarction. Raw RNA-seq reads were reprocessed, and batch correction was applied to integrate the datasets. Key transcriptional modules and their changes across the temporal landscape were characterized through clustering, functional enrichment, and network analysis. Hub genes specific to certain phases were also identified, which may be considered potential targets for treatment. These findings provide a temporal map of post-MI remodeling and highlight candidate molecular targets for therapeutic intervention aligned with the distinct phases of cardiac injury and repair.

Results

This study performed a time-series analysis integrating two publicly available datasets to characterize the temporal transcriptomic changes following myocardial infarction. The two datasets allowed us to capture the evolution of myocardial infarction from early to chronic with data at 10 min, 1 h, 6 h, 24 h, 72 h, 7 days, and 28 days post-ligation. Both datasets were generated using bulk RNA-sequencing of left ventricular tissue from C57BL/6 mice subjected to ligation of the left anterior descending coronary artery, with corresponding sham controls. The sample distribution is shown in Fig. 1a. The FASTQ files were directly processed to ensure minimal differences in data analysis between the two datasets. Principal component analysis demonstrated batch effects between datasets, as shown by the clear separation of the two control groups (Fig. 1b). Batch effects between datasets were corrected using the ComBat algorithm from the sva package. Principal component analysis confirmed effective correction of batch effects through the close position of the two control groups from each dataset (Fig. 1b).

Figure 1c presents volcano plots depicting significantly altered genes (adjusted p-value < 0.05, |log₂ fold change| > 1) at each time point, with upregulated genes in red and downregulated genes in blue.

The earliest transcriptional changes were detected as soon as 10 min post-ligation, involving genes such as Bmal1, Perp1, and Cox6a2. These genes are involved in inflammation, endoplasmic reticulum proteins, and oxidative phosphorylation, indicating rapid activation of stress-responsive and mitochondrial pathways. These responses intensified by 1 and 6 h, with a substantial rise in pro-inflammatory and apoptotic marker genes (Fos, Egr1, Junb, Ccl3, Cxcl2). By 24 and 72 h, transcriptional remodeling was evident, with upregulation of immune-related genes and emerging signatures of extracellular matrix remodeling (Spp1, Serpine1, Chil3). Notably, the 72-hour and 7-day timepoints exhibited the greatest percentage of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the analyzed transcriptome (Fig. 1d), suggesting a transcriptional peak. At 28 days, although the number of DEGs decreased, the level did not reach the baseline level, highlighting ongoing remodeling even after chronic exposure.

To dissect temporal patterns of gene expression dynamics across the myocardial infarction time course, clustering was applied using the Mfuzz algorithm on standardized expression profiles17. This approach grouped co-regulated genes based on their temporal expression trajectories. It revealed nine distinct clusters (Fig. 2a), each representing a characteristic transcriptional signature associated with specific phases of cardiac injury and remodeling (Supplementary Materials 2). The clusters revealed several characteristic expression patterns, including early to intermediate responses (Clusters 3, 4, and 9), featuring genes that rapidly responded post-myocardial infarction. A separate group of clusters (Clusters 1, 5, 6, 7, and 8) reflected late responses, with expression patterns peaking during the 72-h to 7-day window. In contrast, Clusters 2 and 4 exhibited late suppression, characterized by a sustained downregulation of gene expression. These temporally distinct patterns were also supported by DEG analysis (Fig. 2b). The findings highlight the complex and phase-specific regulatory landscape governing the cardiac transcriptome following ischemic injury.

In contrast, Clusters 1, 5, 6, and 8 exhibited late-phase activation, with peak expression between 72 h and 7 days post-infarction. Cluster 1 and 6 was enriched in myeloid leukocyte activation and immune response signaling phagocytic pathways (1) and complement activation (6), suggesting initial activation of innate immune activity 6 h post infarction and extending to 7 to 28 days post infarction. Cluster 5 included genes involved in extracellular matrix organization, signifying remodeling and fibrosis, which increased at 72 h and peaked at 7 days post-infarction. Cluster 8 reflected a process associated with cellular proliferation with enrichment in chromosome segregation and nuclear division. These processes start to increase 24 h post-infarction and peak at 7 days before gradually decreasing at 28 days, reflecting the peak of cell proliferation and regeneration at 72 h post-infarction.

Clusters 4 and 7 represent two clusters with a biphasic pattern, with an initial increase and subsequent decrease for Cluster 4 and the reverse for Cluster 7. Cluster 4 showed an initial increase in post-infarction and a progressive decline in expression, peaking at 7 days. This cluster is enriched for genes related to muscle cell development, suggesting a suppression of regenerative or developmental muscle programs. However, cluster 8, enriched in genes associated with cell proliferation, showed significant upregulation during the same period. The combined context and timing imply that the increase in mitotic gene expression is more likely associated with fibrotic remodeling than true muscle regeneration. Cluster 7 was enriched for genes involved in circadian rhythm regulation, which have recently been linked to myocardial infarction. In the analysis, these genes showed an initial decline in expression following infarction, followed by a progressive increase during the 7- to 28-day period. However, the trend remained ongoing at 28 days, making it unclear whether these genes had reached their peak expression. This sustained elevation may reflect delayed or compensatory reactivation of circadian regulatory pathways during cardiac remodeling.

Cluster 2 is the only cluster that showed predominantly decreased expression after an infarction. This cluster was enriched for genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation and the citric acid cycle, which showed a marked downregulation beginning as early as 6 h post-infarction and reaching a minimum at day 7. This pattern likely reflects impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism in cardiomyocytes following ischemic injury.

To prioritize key regulatory genes involved in myocardial infarction (MI), a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the core genes from each cluster (Supplementary Materials 4). Network analysis was performed to identify highly connected nodes. Hub genes are the top 5 genes with the highest degree from each cluster. These genes were selected to represent the changes each cluster experienced over time and possible treatment targets for interventions during myocardial infarction. Additionally, genes from each cluster were further analyzed for their associated transcription factors using DoRothEA18 with the mouse regulome (Supplementary Materials 5), providing insights into the transcriptional regulation underlying each cluster.

The earliest transcriptional changes, observed between 10 min and 6 h post-infarction, were predominantly associated with Clusters 3 and 4 (Fig. 3a and b). These include early innate immune response genes and those involved in muscle organ development, reflecting an immediate injury response. By 24 h, a more robust inflammatory program was evident, marked by elevated expression of genes in Cluster 9, including Tnf, Mmp9, and several cell adhesion molecules (Icam1, Cd44, Itgam), indicating active leukocyte recruitment to the infarcted tissue. At 72 h, the transcriptional profile shifted toward proliferative and fibrotic processes. Genes associated with cell cycle regulation (Ccna2, Cdk1, Cdc20) showed peak expression, while pro-fibrotic markers such as Col1a2, Dcn, and Bgn became increasingly upregulated. The early neutrophil response and developmental signals from Clusters 3 and 4 had essentially returned to baseline. Interestingly, genes associated with circadian rhythm (Per1, Nr1d1, Cry2, Dbp) showed a pronounced decline at 72 h, followed by a progressive rebound that exceeded baseline levels by 28 days. At 7 and 28 days, the fibrotic program from Cluster 5 remained persistently upregulated. Another notable finding is among genes in Cluster 6: Adipoq, C3, Cyp2e1, Dcn, and Lep. This cluster may reflect a pattern of metabolic dysregulation, complement activation, and extracellular matrix expansion, which might represent maladaptive remodeling. By 28 days, most inflammatory and proliferative signals had returned to baseline, reflecting the return to baseline after infarction. However, fibrosis, circadian-related genes, and several metabolic genes remain elevated, potentially indicating ongoing tissue remodeling, chronobiological imbalance, and metabolic dysfunction.

Discussion

This study integrated two time-series transcriptomic datasets from 10 min to 28 days post-MI, allowing a high-resolution temporal analysis of myocardial infarction. However, a limitation of this reanalysis is the lack of detailed anatomical information for the myocardial samples in both original studies (GSE153494 and GSE236374), meaning it is unclear whether the samples originated from the infarct core, border zone, or remote myocardium, each of which follows distinct transcriptional trajectories after MI16,19. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as most representative of infarct-zone remodeling.

Clustering revealed nine distinct temporal clusters corresponding to immediate early responses, innate immune activation, leukocyte chemotaxis, fibrotic remodeling, mitochondrial suppression, and circadian rhythm dysregulation. These clusters described the biological transitions from acute injury response to chronic adaptation. Through network analysis, hub genes within each cluster were identified. These genes showed temporally regulated activity, highlighting candidate regulators that may serve as phase-specific therapeutic targets. These results helped extend the classic discussion of MI healing from inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling with a much deeper insight.

The immediate early response to myocardial infarction is reflected by Clusters 3 and 4, which show transcriptional activation as early as 10 min post-infarction (Fig. 3a and 3b). Notably, genes such as Fos and Jun, classic immediate early genes, were rapidly upregulated, consistent with their established role in responding to cellular stress such as ischemia20. These transcription factors are known to mediate cardioprotective responses by attenuating apoptosis during early ischemic insult21,22, a phenomenon reported as early as the 1990 s, supporting the validity of the time-course analysis. Further enrichment of their transcription factors (Supplementary Materials 5) also revealed enrichment factors associated with acute injury, survival response, and stress such as Stat3, Jun, Fos, Rela, and Nfkb1 for cluster 3 and Elk1 for cluster 4, further solidifying our findings.

An interesting finding is the early induction of pro-inflammatory chemokines, including Cxcl2 and Ccl3, which play key roles in neutrophil recruitment. This supports the concept that neutrophil infiltration is among the earliest events in myocardial injury. Neutrophils have been implicated in exacerbating tissue damage by releasing reactive oxygen species (ROS), matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), and pro-apoptotic cytokines23. Experimental studies have shown that early inhibition of neutrophils within 15 min to 1 h post-infarction can significantly reduce myocardial damage24–26, although clinical translation remains limited. This analysis refines this window by showing that neutrophil-associated transcriptional signatures remain elevated from at least 1 h to 72 h post-infarction. This suggests neutrophils may contribute to the initial injury and the sustained inflammatory environment. Therefore, therapeutics that modulate neutrophil activity may remain effective, offering a broader intervention window for clinical application.

During the intermediate phase of MI, approximately 24 to 72 h post-infarction, transcriptomic signatures shifted, characterized by immune cell activation and chemotaxis (Clusters 1 and 9) and stromal remodeling (Cluster 5). Cluster 1 encompassed genes central to leukocyte function and immune signaling, with hub genes including Actb, Fcgr3, Itgb2, Ptprc, and Tyrobp. The upregulation of these genes reflects a robust activation of the innate immune response, promoting leukocyte recruitment, adhesion, and functional engagement within the infarcted myocardium. The results were further supported by the enrichment of transcription factors associated with inflammation, such as Spi1, Tbx21, Pax5, and Batf. In parallel, Cluster 9 highlighted increased expression of chemokines and adhesion molecules such as Tnf, Mmp9, Icam1, and Cd44, which mediate leukocyte chemotaxis and transmigration into the inflamed tissue. Enrichment of their transcription factors also revealed the involvement of inflammatory (Nfkb1, Cebpb, and Bhlhe40) and fibrosis (Smad3 and Smad4) transcription factors. These findings indicate that the heart remains actively inflamed throughout this phase, consistent with previous studies3,9. While immune activity is essential for clearance and the initiation of tissue repair, prolonged or excessive inflammation has been strongly linked to adverse cardiac remodeling and progressive functional decline5,8. Therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating this inflammatory response, such as colchicine, have shown potential clinical benefit. The COLCOT trial has highlighted that colchicine’s efficacy may be time-dependent, with earlier administration (within the first 3 days post-MI) yielding more favorable outcomes27. However, the evidence remains mixed with COVERT-MI28 and CLEAR SYNERGY29 trials showing non-significant benefits.

Parallel with immune activation, the emergence of transcriptional signatures associated with stromal remodeling and cell proliferation, primarily reflected by Clusters 5 and 8, was observed. Cluster 5 was enriched for genes involved in extracellular matrix (ECM) organization and collagen biosynthesis, including Col1a2, Dcn, and Bgn, markers of activated fibroblasts. The upregulation of these genes as early as 72 h post-infarction marks the onset of scar formation. Simultaneously, Cluster 8 displayed a strong proliferative signature characterized by cell cycle regulators such as Ccna2, Cdk1, and Cdc20 and by enrichment of transcription factors (Supplementary Materials 5) associated with cell proliferation, such ﻿as﻿ E2f4, E2f1, Foxm1, and E2f2. Considering the post-mitotic nature of adult cardiomyocytes30.this mitotic activity likely originates from expanding fibroblast and immune cell populations rather than cardiomyocytes. Notably, prior studies in murine models of acute myocardial infarction have demonstrated that ECM deposition and fibrosis contribute to maladaptive remodeling of myocardial tissue31–33, while inhibition of fibrosis can confer significant improvements in cardiac function34–36. These studies also highlight the relatively broad therapeutic window for antifibrotic intervention, with efficacy observed even when treatment is initiated up to 5 days after infarction onset34–36. These findings support the observation that fibrosis-related gene expression remains elevated from 72 h to 28 days post-infarction. Therapeutic targeting of fibrotic remodeling remains feasible throughout this period, although earlier intervention may offer superior outcomes by limiting early ECM deposition.

As the infarcted myocardium progresses into the late phase of remodeling (7 to 28 days post-infarction), transcriptional signatures associated with fibrosis persist. These findings are supported by previous studies that identified fibrotic changes and cardiac remodeling to extend even beyond 28 days37–39. In addition to fibrosis, several transcriptomic changes concerning metabolism and circadian rhythms were observed. These changes suggest transitioning from acute inflammation to a more chronic structural stabilization through fibrosis and long-term functional adaptation.

Cluster 2 exhibited sustained downregulation of genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial energy metabolism, including key citric acid cycle and electron transport chain components. These transcriptional changes were detectable as early as 6 h post-infarction, reached their lowest expression at 7 days, and remained markedly suppressed through 28 days. This trajectory likely reflects persistent mitochondrial dysfunction in surviving cardiomyocytes, an increasingly recognized contributor to pathological cardiac remodeling40. Mitochondrial dysfunction has become a research focus due to its dual role in both ischemic injury and reperfusion-associated damage41,42. The data suggest that mitochondrial gene dysregulation emerges early and may also exacerbate myocardial damage during reperfusion through excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and disrupted ATP generation. The early transcriptional suppression of multiple mitochondrial genes underscores the potential of mitochondrial impairment as a primary driver of cardiomyocyte death.

This mitochondrial dysfunction presents a promising but underexplored therapeutic target. Recent reviews have emphasized the importance of mitochondrial dynamics in cardiac injury and repair41,42. However, there are translational studies specifically targeting mitochondrial function as an intervention in post-infarction heart failure43,44. Therapies to preserve mitochondrial integrity or modulate electron transport chain activity could reduce infarct size, mitigate reperfusion injury, and enhance functional recovery. Future investigations should prioritize mitochondria-directed strategies as part of a multiphasic approach to MI management, particularly given the temporal persistence of this transcriptional signature, which may promote the development of heart failure.

Cluster 7 captured an interesting transcriptional signature associated with circadian rhythm regulation. Genes such as Per1, Nr1d1, and Cry2 were initially downregulated following infarction, but showed a rebound expression from day 7, with levels exceeding baseline by day 28. Enrichment of this cluster revealed the Clock gene as the main transcription factor involved. Disruption of circadian gene networks has been increasingly implicated in the pathogenesis of myocardial infarction and subsequent heart failure. Epidemiological and preclinical studies have demonstrated that the timing of infarction relative to the circadian cycle influences infarct size, inflammatory response, and survival outcomes45,46. Targeting PER2, a core circadian regulator, has been shown to confer cardioprotection in murine models45. Additionally, dysfunction of BMAL1 or CLOCK exacerbates myocardial damage and promotes maladaptive remodeling45,47. The late reactivation of circadian genes observed in the analysis may reflect an attempt to restore signaling in the injured myocardium. However, this shift could also indicate maladaptive compensation that promotes dysfunction. These findings highlight a relatively underexplored48 but potentially important signaling of post-MI remodeling. Future studies aimed at characterizing the role of circadian gene regulation in ischemic injury may open novel therapeutic avenues to improve recovery and prevent heart failure progression.

Cluster 6 revealed late-phase upregulation of genes involved in complement activation and humoral immune signaling, including C3, Adipoq, Dcn, and Lep. C3 activation has been shown to exacerbate myocardial damage post-infarction49, although preclinical studies targeting C3 have shown variable outcomes50,51. The upregulation of Adipoq and Lep also points to a metabolic function during this period. Myocardial leptin overexpression has been linked to worsened remodeling and ventricular dysfunction52. These genes highlight a distinct late-phase transcriptional program, possibly reflecting chronic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation. These findings underscore the need to investigate late-stage mechanisms of cardiac remodeling further and identify therapeutic opportunities beyond the acute injury window.

The time-course transcriptomic analysis outlines the evolving molecular landscape of the myocardium following infarction. Myocardial injury initiates a cascade of transcriptional signature clusters in distinct but overlapping waves (Fig. 3b). Early response genes activate within minutes, followed by innate immune activation and neutrophil chemotaxis. These are succeeded by increased transcriptomic signatures of cell proliferation and fibrotic remodeling by 72 h, while oxidative phosphorylation genes become suppressed. From day 7 onward, sustained fibrosis, persistent oxidative phosphorylation suppression, and increased transcriptomic signatures of circadian and complement-related pathways were identified. This timeline highlighted the importance of aligning therapeutic interventions with the evolving molecular landscape of myocardial infarction. Immune modulation may be most effective within the first 24–72 h. However, antifibrotic and metabolic interventions could remain effective even in the chronic phase. Importantly, these findings reveal that myocardial infarction is not a static injury but a dynamic process where effective treatment must be adjusted to the evolving landscape of the infarcted heart.

Study limitations

The current study has several limitations. This study provides a high-resolution view of temporal transcriptional changes post-myocardial infarction. However, the analysis was based on bulk RNA-sequencing data, which do not fully capture the cellular heterogeneity in myocardial tissue. Additionally, the use of animal models may not directly translate to humans. These issues could be addressed through single-cell sequencing in future work. Second, the analysis focused on transcriptional changes and did not include other layers such as proteomics or epigenetic regulation, which may also influence post-MI remodeling. Finally, functional validation in preclinical models and translational studies in human patients will be essential to confirm the utility of timing these interventions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides a transcriptomic timeline of cardiac remodeling following myocardial infarction. By integrating multiple datasets and identifying temporally distinct gene expression clusters, this study highlights key biological processes defining each infarction phase. The results offer a foundation as a therapeutic window for intervention strategies. Further research is warranted to translate these transcriptomic signatures into actionable interventions aimed at preventing the progression from myocardial injury to heart failure.

Methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

Raw FASTQ files for both the early- and late-stage MI time courses were retrieved from GEO (accession numbers GSE15349413 and GSE23637414. GSE153494 comprises left ventricular samples collected at 10 min, 1 h, 6 h, 24 h, and 72 h post-LAD ligation (n = 3 per timepoint), along with matched controls. GSE236374 comprises left ventricular samples collected 7 and 28 days post-LAD ligation (n = 3 per timepoint), along with sham controls. All downloaded reads underwent initial quality control with FastQC (0.12.1). The reads were then quasi-mapped to the mouse transcriptome using Salmon (v1.10.1)53. Transcript-level TPMs and estimated counts were generated for each sample and were imported into R54 using tximport (v1.30.0)55. Gene annotations were obtained from Ensembl (release 114)56.

Normalization, batch Correction, and principal component analysis

Raw counts were normalized to account for differences in sequencing depth and composition, and batch effects from the two datasets were corrected to remove technical variation. To normalize for library size and variance, raw gene-level counts were used in DESeq2 (v1.42.0)57 with the formula ~ dataset + timepoint. To correct for batch effects between the datasets, the ComBat function from the sva package was applied (v3.50.0)58 on the VST-transformed counts, specifying dataset as the batch variable and preserving “timepoint” as the biological covariate. Successful batch removal was confirmed by inspecting PCA plots of the ComBat-corrected VST values. These batch-corrected values are used for subsequent time series analysis.

Differential gene expression analysis

Differential expression was assessed for each MI timepoint versus controls using DESeq2, controlling for the dataset covariate. Genes with adjusted p value < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| > 1 were deemed differentially expressed for that timepoint. The DEGs were compiled across the seven MI timepoints: 10 min, 1 h, 6 h, 24 h, 72 h, 7 days, and 28 days.

Time-course clustering

The ComBat-corrected VST values of the union DEGs were used as input for time-course clustering using Mfuzz (v2.62.0)17. An optimal cluster number of nine was determined by examining minimum centroid distances and cluster separation metrics. A sensitivity analysis with 9 and 11 clusters (both showing good separation metrics) was conducted, but 9 clusters provided a better overall representation of the temporal expression patterns. The 3000 most variable genes were used as input for the clustering algorithm. Each gene was assigned a membership value for each cluster, and genes with membership ≥ 0.7 were designated core members. Each cluster’s average trajectory was plotted over all time points to display its expression dynamics.

Functional enrichment analysis

Core members of each cluster were submitted to clusterProfiler (v4.10.0)59 for Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Process, KEGG pathway, and Reactome enrichment analyses60. Terms with adjusted p < 0.01 were retained. Enrichment results were recorded per cluster.

Protein–protein interaction networks and Hub-Gene identification

For each cluster, core member genes were submitted to the STRING database (v12.0) using the default settings61. The resulting interaction edges were imported into R and modeled using igraph (v1.5.1)62. Within each cluster-specific network, hub genes were defined as the five nodes with the highest degree/connections. Heatmaps of hub-gene expression across all timepoints were visualized using pheatmap (v1.0.12). Transcription factor enrichment analysis was performed using the DoRothEA framework with its curated mouse regulome18.
