Neonatal steroid exposure and body composition in preterm infants at discharge
Authors: Megan K Kraemer, Tricia J Johnson, Katherine A Bell, Delaney C Mulcahy, Leah A Cerwinske, Kousiki Patra, Bradley M Appelhans, Jieun David, Mandy Brown Belfort, Quyen M Diep, Aloka L Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCorticosteroids are frequently used in very preterm (VPT) infants. Corticosteroids can increase adiposity and decrease lean mass, with unclear effects in VPT infants. We hypothesized that corticosteroid exposure in VPT infants would be associated with increased fat mass (FM), %FM, and decreased fat-free mass (FFM).MethodsThis prospective study included 62 infants <32 weeks’ gestational age (GA) or <1500 grams birth weight (BW). Body composition was obtained using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) at neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) discharge. We estimated FFM from BIA, calculated FM and %FM, and determined z-scores for postmenstrual age and sex. Body composition indices in steroid-exposed and non-steroid-exposed subjects were compared using Mann-Whitney U tests.ResultsThe mean GA was 28 ± 2.5 weeks, and the mean BW was 1089±401grams. Fourteen infants (22.5%) had steroid exposure, which was associated with significantly higher FM, %FM, and FFM. However, no significant differences in FM, %FM, and FFM z-scores were observed.ConclusionSteroid exposure was associated with differences in the body composition of VPT infants at NICU discharge. However, after adjusting for post-menstrual age and sex using z-scores, the difference was no longer significant, likely reflecting the longer hospitalization and later postmenstrual age at discharge in the steroid-exposed cohort.Impact Corticosteroids are regularly utilized in neonatal intensive care units, and the impact of these medications on body composition in preterm neonates is unclear.Our study found no significant difference in fat mass (FM), %FM, or fat-free mass (FFM) z-scores between very preterm (VPT) infants with and without steroid exposure.Our study highlights the feasibility of using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) to assess body composition in the NICU.

Introduction

Nutrition and postnatal growth are critical components in the management of very preterm (VPT, gestational age [GA] <32 weeks) and very low birth weight (VLBW, birth weight <1500 g) infants. There has been growing interest in delineating the effects of gains in fat mass (FM) and fat-free mass (FFM) among VPT infants. FFM accretion reflects the growth of the brain, muscle, bone, and other tissues that are closely related to long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes of infants. FM represents the accumulation of fat, which may not contribute to these beneficial health outcomes.1–7 Furthermore, increased FM accretion in infancy may be associated with negative effects, including higher blood pressures at age 4 years and alterations in infants’ temperaments.2,8 Consequently, distinguishing FM from FFM accrual and trying to enhance the latter has become a recent focus of neonatal nutrition research. A growing body of literature has evaluated the impact of various nutrition practices on FM and FFM patterns in VLBW infants.9–15 Yet studies assessing the sequela of medications, specifically corticosteroids, on body composition are lacking. This is relevant given the frequent use of postnatal systemic corticosteroids in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) for multiple disease processes including hypotensive shock, adrenal insufficiency and prevention or treatment of bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD).16

The potential for corticosteroids to impact body composition among preterm infants is biologically plausible. Many, but not all, observational studies and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in adults and children found that steroid exposure is associated with increased FM and decreased FFM.17–19 However, subjects in the childhood studies had an average age of 9.1 ± 4.0 years, with the youngest study participants being 4 years old, and there are no studies of VPT infants. Yet, there is strong evidence that corticosteroids negatively impact growth in this high-risk patient population, specifically, slower weight gain and decreased head circumference and linear growth.20–27 Consequently, these findings suggest corticosteroid exposure may have a negative impact on FFM accretion and contribute to perturbations in adiposity. Importantly, corticosteroids are typically administered during a critical period when growth perturbations can permanently impact long-term outcomes.1,2,4–8 Basic anthropometrics, including body mass index (BMI), do not represent adiposity accurately in VPT infants; therefore, it is important to use a methodology designed specifically to discriminate FM from FFM.28–32 Bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) has recently been validated for this purpose in the NICU setting.33

The objective of our study was to assess the association between corticosteroids and FM and FFM accretion of VPT/VLBW infants using BIA. We hypothesized that VPT/VLBW infants with a history of systemic corticosteroid exposure during their NICU hospitalization would have greater FM and %FM and less FFM at NICU discharge compared to VPT/VLBW infants without a history of steroid exposure.

Methods

Study Design/Subjects

This was a prospective observational cohort study evaluating body composition in VPT/VLBW infants. Subjects were recruited between October 2022 and December 2023 in the Rush University Medical Center NICU through two prospective cohort studies that were combined to create the analytic cohort (Fig. 1). The first prospective cohort study was the ReDiMOM Follow-Up Study. This study enrolled infants from the Reducing Disparity in Mother’s Own Milk Trial (ReDiMOM) RCT (NCT04540575) and followed them through the first two years of life. Body composition measurements were obtained prior to NICU discharge and at NICU follow-up clinic visits.34,35 For the second prospective cohort study (steroid-specific cohort study), VLBW or VPT infants not enrolled in the ReDiMOM RCT were approached for enrollment once the infant was nearing NICU discharge, defined as oral intake ≥ 50% of the full daily enteral feeding volume and stability in either room air or low-flow nasal cannula (i.e., home-going nasal cannula). Infant eligibility criteria for inclusion in the analytic cohort for this study included birth GA ≤ 31 6/7 weeks or BW < 1500 grams, and admission to our NICU within 48 hours of life. Exclusion criteria included major congenital gastrointestinal anomalies (e.g., gastroschisis, omphalocele, tracheoesophageal fistula, congenital diaphragmatic hernia), history of spontaneous intestinal perforation or necrotizing enterocolitis (Bell’s Stage ≥ 2), complex congenital heart disease, major chromosomal abnormalities, or non-English speaking families due to their low prevalence in our NICU. Both prospective cohort studies were approved by the Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from the parent/guardian at the time of enrollment.

Maternal characteristics

The following were obtained from the electronic medical record (EMR): maternal age, maternal insurance (public versus private), complete antenatal steroid defined as receiving ≥2 doses of betamethasone (dexamethasone was not utilized at our center), and histologic chorioamnionitis based on pathology results. Maternal race and ethnicity (non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, non-Hispanic white, other racial groups) and BMI (kg/m2) were obtained from the EMR.

Infant characteristics

The following were obtained from the EMR: infant’s GA at birth, sex, SNAPPE-2, and level of respiratory support on day of life (DOL7). The SNAPPE-2 illness severity score was calculated based on the first 12 hours of life.36,37 For infants without an arterial blood gas in the first 12 hours of life, the presumed value of the PaO2/FiO2 score was 0 as most infants with severe hypoxemia have arterial lines in place in our NICU. The level of respiratory support on DOL7 was obtained via the EMR. BPD severity was assigned at 36 weeks postmenstrual age (PMA) based on the diagnostic criteria proposed by Jensen and colleagues.38

Nutritional Intake

Duration of parenteral nutrition (PN, days), daily enteral feedings, and supplementation with liquid protein or medium chain triglyceride oil through DOL 28 were obtained from the EMR. Using daily enteral feeding volume (mL) of mother’s own milk (MOM), donor milk (DM), and formula, we calculated the daily percent of MOM and then calculated the average of these values to characterize the infants’ exposure to MOM during DOL 1-28. Our institution’s feeding guideline for VPT infants recommends prioritization of early MOM administration as soon as colostrum is available. At 48 hours, if there is insufficient MOM, DM feedings are initiated. In the rare case that parents refuse DM, preterm formula is utilized. Infants receive trophic volume feeds for 2-5 days, based on BW. Subsequently, enteral feeds are advanced by 30 mL/kg/day daily. When the enteral feeding volume reaches 70 mL/kg/day, feedings are fortified to 22 kcal/oz using a bovine human milk fortifier (HMF) and then to 24 kcal/oz the following day. Infants demonstrating feeding intolerance with bovine HMF on two separate occasions are transitioned to a human milk-derived fortifier. For infants receiving formula, the caloric content is increased to 24 kcal/oz after the infant has tolerated 140 mL/kg/day of enteral feeds. Liquid protein and medium-chain triglyceride oil are utilized as-needed with input from the NICU dieticians based on the infant’s growth trajectory and blood urea nitrogen level.39,40 Infants receiving DM transition to preterm formula (24 kcal/oz) over three days starting at 33 weeks PMA. In preparation for discharge, infants transition to unfortified MOM and/or transitional formula (22 kcal/oz) when demonstrating adequate volume intake with on-demand bottle and/or breast feeding.

Steroid Exposure

Steroid exposure during the NICU hospitalization was obtained from the EMR, including the type of systemic corticosteroid (hydrocortisone, dexamethasone, or prednisone). Systemic steroid exposure was coded as any/none and as cumulative dosing equivalence in milligrams of hydrocortisone.41 The primary analysis categorized systemic steroid exposure as a dichotomous variable.

Anthropometrics

Birth and discharge anthropometrics (weight, length, and head circumference) were obtained from the EMR. Standard procedures were in place for obtaining these measurements in the NICU: weight is collected daily, and length and head circumference are collected weekly and at discharge. Paper measuring tapes were used for birth length measurements, and length boards were used at NICU discharge. Length measurements were completed with two staff members assisting when feasible. Head circumferences were obtained using paper measuring tapes. Fenton growth curves or World Health Organization growth curves (if PMA exceeded 50 weeks) were used to determine anthropometric z-scores.42

Body composition outcomes

The primary outcomes were body composition variables (FM, %FM, FFM expressed as z-scores) at NICU discharge. We utilized bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) to assess FM and FFM due to the advantages of this method in the NICU population, including the ability to analyze patients on supplemental oxygen, the portability of the machine allowing for analysis without the need to transport patients, and the affordability of this modality over air displacement plethysmography (ADP).43 BIA is widely used in older children and has recently been validated against ADP for preterm infants in the NICU.33 BIA measures the body’s resistance to a weak electrical current that is applied through electrodes placed on the limbs.44 Because current flows easily through hydrated tissue like lean mass, but poorly through anhydrous tissue like fat, resistance is indirectly proportional to FFM. Resistance can be entered into a prediction equation to calculate absolute FFM. In our study, BIA was performed within 72 hours preceding NICU discharge using the BodyStat Quadscan 4000 (BODYSTAT, Isle of Man, United Kingdom) to measure resistance at 50 kHz utilizing a standardized procedure adapted from Bell et al. 33 To improve precision, the BIA measurement was repeated four times within 10 minutes per infant per encounter. The median resistance value from the four BIA measurements was then input into the Lingwood birth equation, along with the infant’s weight and length, to calculate FFM for each infant.33,45 FM was calculated by subtracting the FFM from the infant’s body weight at time of measurement. %FM was then calculated by dividing the FM by the infant’s body weight and multiplying by 100. FM, %FM, and FFM z-scores for PMA and sex were calculated based on the Norris et al reference values.46

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics included mean ± SD, median [25th percentile, 75th percentile], and n (%). Maternal and infant characteristics were compared between steroid-exposed and non-exposed groups using Chi-square tests for categorical variables and Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables with skewed distribution. Body composition outcomes in steroid-exposed and non-steroid-exposed subjects were compared using Mann-Whitney U tests.

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our results. In addition to adjusting for PMA at NICU discharge using z-score reference values, we performed a linear regression analysis to assess the association between body composition outcomes and steroid exposure while adjusting for birth GA, sex, duration of PN, and PMA at discharge. Because body composition calculated from BIA data can be influenced by the choice of prediction equation, we also completed a sensitivity analysis using the 1992 Kushner47 equation using a hydration constant of 0.8.47,48 A potential limitation of using BIA is the lack of a prediction equation without bias, which can result in non-sensical value of FM < 0 kg.33 Therefore, we additionally conducted a sensitivity analysis removing infants with values < 0 kg from analyses. Finally, a secondary analysis using linear regression evaluated the association between cumulative dose of corticosteroids as a continuous variable and body composition outcomes.

Exploratory matched analysis was undertaken to attempt to account for the significant differences in demographics and baseline illness severity among steroid exposed and non-exposed infants. For this analysis, we compared the 14 steroid exposed infants to 14 non-steroid-exposed infants matched for GA ± 1.6 weeks and BW ± 335 g.

Results

Infants were enrolled between October 2022 and December 2023, with 51 enrolled in the ReDiMOM Follow-Up study and 12 enrolled in the steroid-specific cohort study. Of the 63 subjects, 62 completed BIA measurement at NICU discharge (1 infant without steroid exposure was discharged prior to body composition measurements being obtained) (Table 1). Of these, 14 (23%) were exposed to steroids during their NICU hospitalization. These 14 subjects received varying types of steroids (43% only hydrocortisone; 21% only dexamethasone; 21% hydrocortisone and dexamethasone; 15% hydrocortisone, dexamethasone, and prednisone) (Supplemental Table S1). The average cumulative dose was 136 mg equivalents of hydrocortisone, with doses ranging from 19 mg to 769 mg equivalents of hydrocortisone. Indications for steroids included facilitation of weaning respiratory support (79%) or perioperative (inguinal hernia repair, catheter closure of patent ductus arteriosus, or gastric tube placement) at the discretion of the anesthesiologist (21%).

Infants with steroid exposure were significantly more preterm (25.7 ± 1.9 weeks vs. 29.0 ± 2.2 weeks, p < 0.001) and had higher SNAPPE-2 scores (38.6 vs. 17.6, p = 0.001) than those without steroid exposure (Table 1). Similarly, infants with steroid exposure were significantly smaller at birth than those without steroid exposure (760 ± 228 g vs. 1184 ± 391 g, p < 0.001); however, there were no significant differences in birth anthropometric z-scores (Table 2). Infants with steroid exposure were more likely to require invasive ventilation on DOL 7 and had significantly greater severity of BPD compared to infants without steroid exposure. There were no significant differences between infants with steroid exposure compared to those without steroid exposure with respect to maternal age, race and ethnicity, insurance, BMI maternal antenatal steroids, chorioamnionitis, or infant sex.

There was no difference in MOM exposure over the first 28 days or type of fortifier between groups; however, steroid exposure was associated with longer PN duration and greater use of dietary modulators (79% vs. 35%, p = 0.011) (Table 1). Infants with steroid exposure had longer length of stay (LOS) with older PMA at discharge (45.3 weeks vs. 39.7 weeks, p < 0.001), but no significant differences in discharge anthropometric z-scores were noted.

Bivariate analysis demonstrated that steroid exposure was associated with significantly higher FM (0.803 kg vs. 0.294 kg, p < 0.001), higher %FM (16.47% vs. 9.04%, p < 0.001), and higher FFM (3.965 kg vs. 2.932 kg, p < 0.001) at NICU discharge (Table 3). However, there were no significant differences between groups in FM z-scores, %FM z-scores, or FFM z-scores (Table 3; Fig. 2). After adjusting for the previously listed infant characteristics in a linear regression model, steroid exposure was not significantly associated with FM, %FM, or FFM. Secondary analysis did not reveal any statistically significant associations between cumulative dose of corticosteroids (in mg equivalents of hydrocortisone) and z-scores for %FM, FM, or FFM (Fig. 3). Sensitivity analyses utilizing the 1992 Kushner47 equation to calculate FFM49–51 also demonstrated significantly higher FM, %FM, and FFM in the steroid-exposed infants, but this significance did not persist once data were converted to z-scores. Sensitivity analysis, removing five infants with FM values < 0 kg yielded results that were consistent with the overall findings.

The secondary matched analysis of 14 non-steroid-exposed infants with the 14 steroid-exposed infants yielded similar results for body composition metrics. FM, %FM, and FFM were all higher at discharge for steroid-exposed infants, but z-scores were similar between groups. These results are summarized in Supplemental Tables S2–4. Length of stay, PMA at discharge, birth length, BPD severity, discharge weight, and length remained significantly different baseline characteristics between the groups. However, birth gestational age, birth weight, birth head circumference, SNAPPE-II, PN exposure, and discharge head circumference were no longer significantly different between the cohorts.

Discussion

To our knowledge, our study is the first to assess body composition outcomes after corticosteroid exposure in a high-risk VPT infant population despite the widespread use of corticosteroids for the prevention and/or treatment of BPD.16,52–55 Prior literature describing the relationship between corticosteroids and body composition has primarily focused on adults and older children with a notable paucity of research for VPT and VLBW infants.17–19 Alternatively, studies evaluating the impact of corticosteroids of growth outcomes in VPT and VLBW infants primarily focus on weight gain, linear growth or head circumference trajectories. Therefore, we chose to focus our study on body composition outcomes rather than the more traditionally studied anthropometrics. Our study contributes to the growing body of literature focusing on body composition of preterm infants and highlights the need for further research evaluating the impact of corticosteroids on FM and FFM accretion in this high-risk population.

Although our bivariate analysis demonstrated significantly greater FM, %FM, and FFM in the steroid-exposed infants, these differences likely reflected the different clinical characteristics of the groups, including higher PMA at discharge among steroid-exposed infants (45.3 weeks versus 39.7 weeks discharge PMA). In fact, we found that z-scores for body composition metrics, which compare infants to their expected size for PMA, were similar between groups, suggesting that corticosteroids may not be directly associated with changes in short-term body composition in VPT/VLBW infants.

We found a significant difference in the provision of dietary modulators (medium-chain triglyceride oil, liquid protein), with more infants in the steroid-exposed cohort receiving these. Medium-chain triglyceride oil and liquid protein are initiated in our institution based on a collaborative decision between the medical and dietitian teams when an infant is not meeting their weight or linear growth goal trajectories, has a low BUN39,40 or is on exclusive DM feeding, as DM is associated with slower weight, length and head circumference growth in VLBW infants compared to formula.56 We found similar percent intake of MOM during the first month of life between the two groups, suggesting the need for modulars was not related to differences in quantity of DM feeds. It is plausible that the postnatal corticosteroid exposure stunted somatic growth in treated infants, as previous articles have noted,20–23,57 and therefore, more infants were prescribed modulars to improve growth. Given that our data did not demonstrate significant differences in z-scores at discharge across all anthropometrics and body composition outcomes, it is possible that the additional nutrition provided through these modulars assisted in overcoming any negative impact on growth that would have otherwise been apparent.

A strength of this study is that we assessed body composition rather than relying only on anthropometrics. We utilized BIA, which has been recently validated for VPT/VLBW infants in the NICU.33 While ADP has been used more commonly than BIA to measure body composition in the NICU, ADP is limited by cost, inability to move the machine easily, and the need for clinically stable patients, as the infants cannot require supplemental oxygen or continuous infusions when undergoing analysis. In this study, eight infants were discharged on home oxygen (5/14 steroid-exposed and 3/48 non-steroid-exposed), and BIA permitted their inclusion despite their ongoing respiratory support requirements.

BIA has previously been used to evaluate body composition in multiple studies, including infants.58,59 However, there are limited data on the validity of BIA measurements in the infant population, especially VPT/VLBW infants. A recently published study by Bell et al compared BIA-derived metrics with ADP body composition measurements and found that body composition measurements calculated using the birth Lingwood equation demonstrated acceptable agreement with ADP-derived metrics.33 An additional strength of our study is that our sensitivity analysis using an alternate equation to Lingwood45 (Kushner47 equation) to calculate body composition from BIA data returned similar results, suggesting our findings were not unduly influenced by the choice of prediction equation.

A potential limitation of this study is that we applied z-score norms that were developed using ADP for our BIA body composition results.46 Ideally, we would have utilized z-scores specifically developed with BIA, however, no reference values currently exist for body composition measurements obtained via BIA in VPT/VLBW infants. Additonally, BIA – when used with the currently published equations for calculating neonatal body composition – has been shown to overestimate FFM.33 Despite this limitation, the use of the same z-score references for both groups likely minimized the impact on our comparison between steroid exposed and non-exposed infants. However, this methodological discrepancy does preclude direct comparison of our calculated FFM and FFM z-score values with those from other studies using ADP-derived values. Finally, our results highlight the importance of utilizing z-scores when comparing growth and body composition outcomes, given the significant differences in PMA at discharge between the two groups in this study.

An additional limitation of this observational study is the potential for residual confounding by indication with regard to the use of postnatal corticosteroids in infants with higher illness acuity and the impact of clinical illness on FM and FFM accretion independent of steroid use. Prior studies have demonstrated that critical illness is associated with poorer growth outcomes, including basic anthropometric markers (weight, length, head circumference) and with less FFM accretion.60,61 However, the significant differences between groups in multiple baseline characteristics (GA, BW, SNAPPE-2 score) and the small sample size limited the feasibility of meaningful multivariate analyses. The matched analysis accounting for GA and BW differences reassuringly showed similar results to the primary analysis, but is limited by its small sample size.

Given the small number of subjects with a history of steroid exposure, we could not stratify based on the type or dosage (in mg/kg) of corticosteroid received. Our institution did not have a standardized steroid guideline in place at the time of the study, which contributed to variation in PMA and chronological age of the patients at the time of steroid treatment as well as the dosage and type of steroid used (Supplemental Table S1). However, we attempted to account for this practice variation by calculating each infant’s cumulative steroid dose in equivalents of mg of hydrocortisone.41 This was important, because studies of older children and adults suggest that shorter courses or low doses of steroids may not impact body composition.18,62 We found no clear association between cumulative steroid dosing and body composition z-scores, suggesting that at the doses received by our cohort, factors other than steroid exposure may be more influential in body composition development in VPT/VLBW infants.

Our study contributes to the growing body of literature examining body composition in preterm infants by assessing what, if any, association exists between this regularly utilized class of medications and FM and FFM accretion. We found no significant differences in FM, %FM, or FFM z-scores, accounting for sex and PMA at NICU discharge, between infants with a history of steroid exposure and those without steroid exposure. However, our study was a single-center study with a small sample size, which limits generalizability. We did not perform sample size calculations, and thus, our study may be underpowered to detect small but potentially clinically significant differences in body composition metrics. These limitations highlight the need for future research to examine a larger population, ideally from multiple centers, to better account for variations in nutritional protocols, clinical practice, and demographics, and enable adjustment for potential confounders. Prospective cohort studies and RCTs of postnatal steroids in VPT/VLBW infants should measure body composition before, during, and after corticosteroid treatment to determine the immediate and longer-term changes in body composition with use of these medications. Furthermore, future studies should collect weekly longitudinal anthropometric data to delineate any changes associated temporally with steroid administration. Additionally, multiple methods for assessing body composition (e.g., BIA and skin fold thickness, BIA and ADP, etc.) should be compared to further validate the resulting FFM, FM and %FM values. Until further data regarding body composition are available from larger cohorts, clinicians should continue to closely monitor anthropometric growth parameters among VPT/VLBW infants treated with systemic corticosteroids, and these infants should be considered at high nutritional risk and followed closely to ensure adequate nutritional support.
