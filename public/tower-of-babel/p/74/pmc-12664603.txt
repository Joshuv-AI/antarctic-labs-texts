PROCA11 early activation fuels prostate cancer neuroendocrine traits by regulating cell survival and migration
Authors: Rocco Cipolla, Marc Gabriel, Ugo Szachnowski, Zhimin Wang, Martina Romani, Giorgia Ianese Regin, Micaela Piemontese, Virginie Firlej, Marina Pinskaya, Antonin Morillon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryTumor plasticity drives the emergence of castration-resistant and neuroendocrine prostate cancer following androgen-deprivation therapy (ADT). Non-genetic determinants, including long non-coding (lnc)RNAs, preceding the acquisition of neuroendocrine traits, can disrupt androgen dependence and promote resistance. However, the precise transcriptome dynamics prior to therapy-induced neuroendocrine trans-differentiation remains unknown. Using a prostate cancer model mimicking the onset of castration-resistant and neuroendocrine phenotypes, we uncovered rapid lncRNA changes, with PROCA11 emerging as the most prominent. PROCA11 is highly expressed in advanced adenocarcinoma and neuroendocrine prostate cancer tumors. This predominantly nuclear lncRNA is rapidly activated upon hormone depletion and persists in neuroendocrine-like cells. PROCA11 promotes cell survival, proliferation, and motility by reducing apoptosis and enhancing adhesion, contributing to neuroendocrine traits. PROCA11 binds to the cell-cycle regulator CCAR2/DBC1 and to CTNNA1, controlling cell adhesion and cytoskeleton. We suggest that PROCA11 arises early during tumor plasticity and controls the development of castration-resistant and neuroendocrine phenotypes in prostate cancer.Subject areas: Biological sciences, Cancer, Epigenetics, Genomics

Introduction

Initiation and progression of prostate cancer depend on androgens and on the androgen-inducible transcription factor (TF) - androgen receptor (AR). Hence, androgen deprivation therapies (ADTs) in combination with AR antagonists are used as a first-line treatment for prostate cancer. However, patients often relapse and develop castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC), dropping the 5-year survival rate to 30%.1 Furthermore, up to 20% of CRPC patients undergo histological transformation from adenocarcinoma to neuroendocrine (NE) subtype, also known as therapy-induced NE prostate cancer (tNEPC), which is considered lethal.2,3,4 CRPC is characterized by high heterogeneity in tissue morphology and cellular and molecular features. Indeed, emerging single-cell and bulk transcriptomic studies of post-ADT tumors revealed a complex combination of cell types for some retaining properties of prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD) with still active AR signaling, and some progressively acquiring androgen independence (AI) and/or undergoing NE trans-differentiation giving rise to tNEPC.5,6,7,8,9 However, these trajectories are not linear and prevailing, reflecting high lineage plasticity of advanced prostate cancer tumors due to multiple genetic alterations and activation of numerous reprogramming TFs (ONECUT2, BRN2, SOX2, OCT4, and ASCL1) and chromatin modifiers (EZH2 and SWI/SNF) in response to inhibition of the AR signaling.10,11,12,13,14 These models propose that AI is acquired at early onset of the NE, resulting from the high plasticity of the tumor. In this respect, non-genetic events have been proposed to control the NE trans-differentiation, including some long non-coding (lnc)RNAs.

Indeed, massive RNA-sequencing revealed thousands of lncRNAs, highly expressed in CRPC, and some in NEPC tumors.15,16 Among them, MIAT, lncRNA-p21, LINC00261, and H19 were described as regulators of the NE trans-differentiation in prostate cancer.17,18,19,20 These lncRNAs were shown to contribute to tumor plasticity and cancer progression modulating gene expression patterns mostly at the transcriptional level. MIAT, a myocardial infarction associated transcript, and H19 are upregulated in NEPC and proposed to interact with and regulate the activity of PRC2 reshaping H3K27me and H3K4me3 at gene promoters toward NE trans-differentiation.19,21 Another study revealed that the treatment of CRPC patients with the AR antagonist - enzalutamide - induces the expression of lncRNA-p21 enabling switching the EZH2 methyltransferase activity from the histone H3K27 to a non-histone substrate, the STAT3 TF.18 Finally, LINC00261 is an example of an evolutionarily conserved lncRNA, which contributes to proliferation and metastasis in NEPC through two distinct nuclear and cytoplasmic mechanisms.20 However, although these unique examples illustrate the role of lncRNAs in acquisition of NE traits, they were all characterized in fixed contexts as misregulated RNA subtypes in established tumor states in tissues or patient-derived xenografts. None of them addressed the dynamic response resulting or controlling the transition toward NE and the androgen-independent proliferation and relapse, leaving open the precise identification of the tumor plasticity sequential events that bypass the chemical castration in the onset of CRPC and NEPC.

Our rationale relied on deep monitoring of the whole non-annotated transcriptome changes en route to breaking the androgen addiction. In this respect, we took advantage of the unique and powerful dynamic prostate cancer LNCaP cell system, recapitulating the CRPC and NEPC onset,22 to identify the lncRNAs early induced in response to androgen deprivation. Total RNA-sequencing, followed by differential expression analysis of de novo scallop-assembled transcripts and multiple filters, isolated 15 unannotated lncRNAs, including the top candidate PROCA11, which is highly expressed in high-risk prostate cancer tumors, ADT-treated CRPC is associated with the emergence of NEPC traits. This majorly nuclear lncRNA, presenting three main isoforms, is lowly expressed or absent in prostate normal tissues and in hormone-dependent cells but activated following AR knock-down or release of AR from PROCA11 promoter upon androgen deprivation. Activation of PROCA11 transcription precedes those of NE genes and persists at high levels through NE trans-differentiation. Gain- and loss-of-function experiments demonstrated that PROCA11 promotes cell proliferation, regulating a balance between cAMP and RAS signaling as well as synaptic and metabolic activities. In vitro, PROCA11 RNA pull-down revealed proteins involved in adherent junctions, cytoskeleton organization, signal transduction, and replication. Among them, the CCAR2 cell cycle regulator and the CTNNA1 cell adhesion protein were validated for binding to PROCA11 by RNA-immunoprecipitation. Supporting the functional connection between PROCA11 and its protein partners CCAR2 and CTNNA1, we demonstrated that the lncRNA exerts not only an anti-apoptotic effect in response to androgen deprivation, but also a reduction in cell migration, suggesting enhanced adherent junctions and alterations in cytoskeletal dynamics. We propose that, in early response to androgen deprivation, PROCA11 is rapidly activated and contributes to cellular plasticity by promoting survival and proliferation along with remodeling of cell morphology and adhesion. This may foster the onset of AI and NE phenotype.

Results

Characterization of the dynamic transcriptomic atlas revealed PROCA11, a lncRNA rapidly induced by androgen deprivation in prostate cancer cells

To characterize transcriptome changes underlying cell trans-differentiation in response to androgen deprivation, we took advantage of a dynamic in vitro cell model recapitulating early response to ADT and progressive acquisition of NE-like and AI traits.22 In this system, first, adenocarcinoma LNCaP cells are grown in the presence of dihydrotestosterone (DHT) mimicking primary epithelial androgen-dependent luminal tumors (AD-cells). Then, they are subjected to androgen deprivation which induces immediate growth arrest and progressive change in cell morphology and properties toward the NE-like phenotype (NE-like cells) with weaker but persisting AR in the nucleus (Figures 1A, S1A, and S1B). We performed a total stranded RNA-sequencing of these cell populations at different time points of transition and quantified gene expression using a custom transcriptome annotation including the GENCODE release 32 and scallop-assembled transcripts from intergenic and antisense regions and regions overlapping annotated non-coding sense exonic transcripts (see STAR Methods). To focus on the earliest changes induced by androgen deprivation, we further performed a differential expression analysis comparing two conditions: AD-cells actively proliferating in the presence of DHT and growth-arrested NE-like cells cultured for 15 days and 1 month in hormone-depleted media (NE-15d/1m) (Figure 1A). In total, 964 lncRNAs were significantly induced by androgen deprivation (adj. p-value ≤0.05, Figure 1B; Table S1). Further selection of the most robust lncRNA candidates was done through a three-steps filtering: (i) normalized read counts above 95th percentile in NE-15d together with (ii) a significant increase of expression in high-risk versus low-risk prostate tumors of prostatectomy origin (adj. p-value ≤0.05),23 and (iii) co-expression with protein-coding genes functionally linked to neurogenesis (Pearson’s r ≥ 0.99) (Figure 1B). This allowed isolation of two already annotated (FAM66D and LPP-AS2) and thirteen unannotated lncRNAs with the topmost up-regulated candidate, named PROstate CAncer lncRNA 11(PROCA11) (Figures 1C and 1D). Molecular cloning and sequencing identified three major isoforms of PROCA11, differing in inclusion of the second and third exons, hereafter referred to as PROCA11-V1, V2, and V3, respectively (Figure 1D).

Since PROCA11 expression was low in AD-cells but strongly stimulated in NE-like cells, we hypothesized and tested whether its transcription can be regulated by AR, using CUT&RUN profiling. The results revealed the presence of AR at the PROCA11 promoter, enclosing an in silico-predicted androgen response element (ARE),24 in AD-cells growing in the presence of androgen. The AR peak was dramatically decreased upon androgen deprivation concomitantly with the increase in PROCA11 levels (Figure 1D). To understand the relationship between AR and PROCA11 expression, we performed RNAi-mediated depletion of AR in AD-cells. It resulted in a 30% increase of PROCA11 levels in contrast to the prostate-specific antigen-encoding KLK3 gene, a known target activated by AR (Figure 2A).

Next, to determine whether the increase in PROCA11 levels upon androgen deprivation results from increased transcriptional activity rather than changes in RNA stability, we measured the levels of nascent, yet unspliced PROCA11 transcripts.25 For this purpose, nascent transcripts were isolated from the chromatin fraction of cells treated with alpha-amanitin to block transcription. Further quantification of intronic sequences revealed a substantial increase in nascent RNA, supporting the notion of enhanced PROCA11 transcription in response to androgen deprivation (Figure 2B). This response was very rapid, even preceding the detection of the characteristic NE marker ENO2 (Figure 2C). Finally, we assessed a subcellular localization of PROCA11 by single-molecule RNA-FISH using a pool of probes complementary to exons common to all three isoforms (n = 21) and to the second and third exons (n = 3) (Table S14; Figure 1D). In cells growing in the presence of DHT, we detected on average 15 molecules of PROCA11 per cell increasing up to 2.5-fold upon hormone withdrawal for 72 h (NE-72h) (Figures 2D and 2E). In both conditions, up to 80% of PROCA11 transcripts were localized in the nucleus (Figure 2F). Albeit subcellular fractionation and quantification of specific PROCA11 isoforms revealed their uneven distribution with V3 being more abundant in the nucleus than V1 and V2 (Figure 2G).

In conclusion, androgen deprivation of adenocarcinoma cells induces drastic transcriptomic changes with the most significant increase in levels of the unannotated lncRNA PROCA11. The PROCA11 gene is composed of seven exons that are spliced in three variants, all showing mostly nuclear residence but distinct distribution of the isoforms between the nucleus and the cytoplasm. Androgen deprivation leads to a release of AR from the PROCA11 promoter and burst of its transcription very early in the onset of the NE phenotype. Therefore, one may speculate that PROCA11 contributes to acquisition of castration resistance as part of CRPC and NEPC transcriptomes.

PROCA11 expression is elevated in ADT-treated adenocarcinoma, CRPC and NEPC tumors

To get insights into the clinical relevance of PROCA11 lncRNA, we explored its expression in numerous publicly available clinical datasets. First, the genotype-tissue expression query revealed rather low PROCA11 expression in normal tissues except for the ovarian tissues26 (Figures S2A and S2B). Then, lncRNA levels were assessed in cancer tissues across The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cohorts. As expected, PROCA11 was markedly elevated in PRAD, but also notably high in glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) (Figure 3A). Significantly, the higher lncRNA abundance in intermediate- and high-risk prognosis specimens than in low-risk tumors of TCGA-PRAD reinforced our hypothesis of a possible association between PROCA11 and advanced malignancy (Figure 3B).

Next, PROCA11 expression was interrogated in metastatic CRPC with 41 tumors classified as castration-resistant adenocarcinoma with still active AR signaling (AdPC) and 23 tumors of NEPC with inactive AR.2,6 Remarkably, PROCA11 abundance was comparable in both subgroups though slightly lower in AdPC (p-value >0.05) (Figure 3C). This observation additionally confirmed that PROCA11 can be expressed in AR-positive tumors and is not restricted to the pure NE phenotype. We decided to explore more deeply the relationship between PROCA11, AR, and NE gene signatures in another clinical study of post-ADT metastatic CRPC tumors. They were subclassified based on the expression of AR and NE genes into the following groups: high AR expression (ARPC, AR+/NE-), low AR expression (ARLPC, ARlow/NE-), small cell or NE histology with NE gene expression but lacking AR (SCNPC, AR-/NE+), concomitant expression of both AR and NE genes representing double-positive amphicrine phenotypes (AMPC, AR+/NE+), and double-negative (DNPC, AR−/NE−).8 Remarkably, PROCA11 expression was the highest in SCNPC, but still detectable in all tumor subtypes, though notably low in ARLPC and DNPC (Figure 3D). This observation supported again the PROCA11 expression in AR-positive cells but also suggested implication of other factors or stimuli most likely provided by NE cells. However, it remains difficult to conclude if elevated PROCA11 expression is a consequence or a cause of the onset of the NE phenotype. At the very least, the lack of correlation with genetic alterations in TP53, RB1, and PTEN (|r| < 0.1), all frequently mutated in CRPC and NEPC tumors, suggests that PROCA11 upregulation may occur independently of these common genetic drivers (Figures S3A and S3B). Our attention was drawn to the observation of a bimodal distribution of the lncRNA in ARPC and AMPC tumors with still active AR signaling reaching in some tumors the mean expression levels comparable to AR-negative SCNPC (Figure 3D). Hence, we undertook an unbiased search for a transcriptomic pattern discriminating the top PROCA11 high (above 75th percentile) and low (below 25th percentile) tumors of ARPC and AMPC subtypes together with the SCNPC tumors highly expressing the lncRNA (Figure S3C). First, we compared tumors that were untreated or treated with AR pathway inhibitors, targeting either the AR itself (enzalutamide) or the androgen biosynthesis (abiraterone), and found no significant association between the treatment administration and PROCA11 levels (χ2 = 0.104, p = 0.7469) (Figure S3D). Second, we compared transcriptomes of tumors with high versus low expression of PROCA11. Differential expression (q-value ≤0.05) and KEGG pathway enrichment (FDR ≤0.05) analyses across these specimens revealed that high PROCA11 levels are correlated with elevated expression of genes associated with synaptic transmission, hormone biosynthesis, ECM interaction, and cell adhesion (Figure 3E; Tables S2 and S3). Furthermore, in addition to PROCA11, among the genes most discriminative between high and low PROCA11 tumors were the NE marker NCAM1/CD56 and neuron-specific CNTNAP5, NRXN1, and NRXN3, all part of the neurexin family of genes with functions in cell adhesion and intercellular communication in the nervous system (Table S3). Comparison of gene expression patterns revealed that tumors with elevated PROCA11 levels are associated with significantly higher expression of NE genes but lower expression of luminal genes (q-value ≤0.05) (Figure 3F). Notably, the expression pattern of basal genes also tended to increase in cells with high PROCA11 levels, suggesting a possible role or association of this lncRNA with the emergence of a distinct mixed phenotype characterized by elevated cellular plasticity. Still, the lack of a single-cell resolution in this study did not allow us to conclude if this is relevant to a population of cells with hybrid identity or high cell-to-cell heterogeneity or both.

Overall, these results provide evidence of high expression of PROCA11 in advanced prostate cancer tumors and low levels in all normal tissues, with the notable exception of ovaries. Notably, culminating in androgen-independent NEPC, PROCA11 expression is not confined to this subtype but is also present in cells with still active AR signaling and, particularly, in tumors with high cellular and phenotypic heterogeneity. Its co-expression with adherent junction, ECM interaction genes but also basal and NE genes supports a potential role of the lncRNA in the onset of NE phenotype and presumably proliferation and motility.

PROCA11 is expressed in an ADT-naive tumor of the mixed adenocarcinoma and NE phenotype

As pointed out above, the high heterogeneity of tumors treated by ADT might mask the distinct transcriptomes of the multiple cell populations found in the CRPC context. Nevertheless, in the absence of publicly available single-cell transcriptomic data of ADT-tumors and to get insights into the cellular origins of PROCA11, we took advantage of two unique and high quality scRNA-sequencing datasets from the MURAL collection of ADT-naive patient-derived xenografts that recapitulate the diversity of tumor cell identities.9 Indeed, one specimen represented a mixed pathology with AR- and NE-positive cells (224R, AR+/NE+) and the other corresponded to a homogenous adenocarcinoma with active AR signaling and negative for NE genes (287R, AR+/NE−). A uniform manifold approximation and projection analysis separated 224R into two adenocarcinoma (AD) clusters (2 and 3) and five NE clusters (0, 1, 4–6), and 287R into three adenocarcinoma clusters, comparably with the literature9 (Figures 4A and S4A). Remarkably, PROCA11 was detectable only in the 224R mixed tumor, specifically, at high levels in one of the AD clusters, cluster 2, and parsimoniously in cells of all five NE clusters (Figure 4B). The 287R tumor showed low or no presence of the lncRNA (Figure S4B). Hence, we concluded that PROCA11 can be expressed in ADT-naive tumors, and as expected in NE cells, but unexpectedly also in AD-cells with still active AR signaling. This result suggests that high lncRNA abundance in AD-cells might reflect the presence of an active signal originating from the microenvironment created by NE cells, absent from the 224R adenocarcinoma-exclusive tumor. Nevertheless, gene ontology enrichment analysis revealed, in AD-cells expressing PROCA11 (cluster 2), up-regulation of genes involved in cell cycle and division, while in cells devoid of the lncRNA, higher expression of genes with functions in translation (30 genes of RPL and RPS family), cell cycle control, and apoptotic signaling (q-value ≤0.05) (Figure 4C; Tables S4 and S5). Seurat clustering based on cell cycle-specific gene expression patterns revealed increased proportion of cluster 2 cells in the G2/M phase pointing to increased proliferation (p-value = 1.75 × 10−7, Fisher exact test) (Figure 4D).

In conclusion, single-cell transcriptomic analyses revealed PROCA11 expression in an ADT-naive tumor of the mixed AD and NE phenotype and notably in both AR-positive and AR-negative cells. The low or undetectable expression of PROCA11 in the pure adenocarcinoma and its elevated expression in the mixed AD/NE tumor reinforce our hypothesis that the lncRNA expression could rely on a microenvironment promoted by NE cells. In any case, even in AD-cells, high PROCA11 levels are associated with cell cycle control, suggesting a general role of the lncRNA in cell proliferation.

PROCA11 regulates cell proliferation and NE features through regulation of apoptosis, adhesion, and hormone metabolism genes

Since PROCA11 is expressed, even at low level, in androgen-dependent cells with still active AR signaling, we hypothesized that it might influence proliferation of AD-cells. We thus performed gain- and loss-of-function studies in cells growing in the presence and in the absence of androgens, to determine if alterations in lncRNA expression change cancer-related phenotypes. First, we sub-cloned the three major lncRNA variants into a lentiviral construct under the control of the constitutive CytoMegaloVirus (CMV) promoter and transduced them into LNCaP (oeP11) to overexpress each of three variants (oeV1, oeV2, and oeV3, respectively) or control gene (Figure S5A). To decrease PROCA11 levels, we used a siPOOL strategy transfecting LNCaP cells growing in the presence or the absence of androgen with a mix of thirty siRNAs targeting exons common to all PROCA11 isoforms (siP11) or non-targeting controls (siSCR). PROCA11 depletion by siP11 was efficient, achieving 80%–90% decrease in cells overexpressing PROCA11 variants though less robust (no more than 50%) in physiological conditions (Figure S5B). To follow cell proliferation, we used an xCELLigence real-time cell analysis based on continuous measurements of electrical impedance. AD-cells and oeV1/V2/V3 cells overexpressing PROCA11 were seeded on plates, transfected or not with siRNAs and monitored for 260 hours. Stable and constitutive PROCA11 overexpression resulted in faster proliferation rates for all three lncRNA isoforms (Figure 5A). Reverse, transient PROCA11 depletion in AD-cells and oeV1/V2/V3-cells significantly reduced proliferation (Figure 5B). An increase in proliferation was confirmed in two additional prostate cancer cell lines, 22Rv1 and PC3, ectopically overexpressing each of the PROCA11 isoforms (Figure S5C). Both cell lines are androgen-independent for growth and do not express PROCA11 endogenously (Figure S5A). Notably, 22Rv1 cells are AR-positive, while PC3 cells are AR-negative. The observation that PROCA11 overexpression promotes proliferation across all cell lines suggests that its pro-proliferative effect is independent of AR signaling.

Proliferation depends on the progression of a population of cells through the cell cycle. Hence, we assessed it in cells using flow cytometry and propidium iodide staining of DNA. PROCA11 depletion resulted in up to 10% increase in the proportion of cells in G1 over S and G2/M phases (Figure 5C). In addition, high PROCA11 levels were systematically associated with an elevated number of mitotic cells (2%–3% G2/M gain over G1/S population of cells, Figure 5C), consistent with an increased proliferation when PROCA11 is overexpressed.

To get insights into the role of PROCA11 in NE trans-differentiation, we left cells growing in the absence of androgen and at the same time performed transient siRNA-mediated depletion of the lncRNA (Figure S5D). After 48 h of post-transfection, we assessed apoptosis by flow cytometric analysis of cells stained with Annexin V and propidium iodide (Figure 5D). Remarkably, PROCA11 depletion resulted in an increase in the proportion of Annexin V-positive cells. This finding suggested that PROCA11 may have an anti-apoptotic role, enabling cells to survive in response to androgen deprivation.

Since PROCA11 expression coincides with the upregulation of NE genes and given that the NE trans-differentiation is often accompanied by cytoskeleton remodeling, which in turn drives cell adhesion and migration,29 we performed a wound healing assay in cells overexpressing each of the three PROCA11 isoforms (Figures 5E and S5E). This analysis revealed that elevated expression of all three lncRNA isoforms is associated with reduction of cell migration (Figure 5E), suggesting that PROCA11 may contribute to the modulation of migratory behavior.

Finally, to explain the changes in cellular phenotypes induced by depletion of PROCA11, we performed RNA-sequencing of cells treated by siRNAs in the presence and absence of androgens. Differential expression analysis in the androgen-dependent context identified 40 genes that were expressed 1.5-fold higher in cells with elevated PROCA11 levels (siSCR condition) and 43 genes that were expressed 1.5-fold higher in cells with low PROCA11 levels (siP11 condition) (q-value ≤0.05) (Table S6). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that high PROCA11 levels are associated with increased expression of genes involved in hormone biosynthesis (Figure 5F), including the UGT2B family of UDP glucuronosyltransferases (UGT2B11, UGT2B15, UGT2B17, and UGT2B18) and cytochrome P450 enzymes participating in the metabolism of androgens (Table S7). Notably, UGT2B15 and UGT2B17 have substrate specificity for testosterone and DHT, and their function constitutes an important mean to inactivate and control ligand levels available for AR activation.30,31,32 Consistent with our observations in clinical CRPC samples (Figure 3G), we found that cells expressing PROCA11 at high levels show also elevated expression of genes involved in focal adhesion and ECM interactions such as laminins and integrins (Table S7).

In the context of NE trans-differentiation, most significantly deregulated genes were associated with key cancer-related and signaling pathways (PI3K-Akt and TP53), focal adhesion, cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, and actin cytoskeleton dynamics (q-value ≤0.05, Figure 5G; Tables S8 and S9). Notably, EPHA4 (ephrin type-A receptor 4) was the most upregulated gene in cells with decreased PROCA11 levels. In prostate cancer, EPHA4 is known to promote cell migration.33,34 These two findings support the observation that PROCA11 reduces cell migration, potentially by upregulating adhesion-associated genes, in the presence and absence of androgens.

Collectively, these results suggest that PROCA11 promotes proliferation in the presence of androgens and may play a role in regulating cell cycle progression. It also reduces cell migratory capacity, consistent with the upregulation of genes involved in cell adhesion and cytoskeletal organization. All three isoforms appear to contribute similarly to the lncRNA-induced phenotypic changes. PROCA11 depletion altered the expression of genes related to cell adhesion and hormone metabolism, mirroring patterns observed in CRPC tumors. The interconnection between these pathways may facilitate cellular reprogramming toward androgen-independent proliferation. Remarkably, our findings also highlight an anti-apoptotic function for PROCA11 in the early response to androgen deprivation, suggesting that it acts as a key mediator of tumor plasticity at the onset of therapy resistance.

PROCA11 interacts with proteins regulating cell proliferation and adhesion

Many lncRNAs have been recognized for their functional roles mediated through interactions with proteins. Thus, to further elucidate the PROCA11 mode of action in cells, we carried out an extensive search for potential protein interactors. RNA affinity purification coupled with mass spectrometry was performed, wherein in vitro transcribed biotin-labeled PROCA11 isoforms and a control XEF1 mRNA were incubated with lysates of AD-cells cultured in the presence of DHT (Figure S6A). Our rationale was to isolate all proteins robustly associated with all types of transcripts (control and PROCA11) (adj. p-value ≤0.05) but directly excluding ribosomal proteins assigned by HUGO gene nomenclature committee (Table S10). Among these 337 identified proteins, 42 contained RNA-binding domain (RBD), or RNA recognition motif or double-stranded RBD (dsRBD) (Table S11). Additional 163 proteins showed the RBP2GO score35 above 15 and so were identified in other studies as interacting with RNA or making part of a ribonucleoprotein complex (Table S12) (Figure 6A). Proteins associated with PROCA11 were further subjected to STRING analysis, revealing high enrichment in some nucleus-restricted proteins as replication factor C (RFC2/3/4/5), or nuclear and cytoplasmic complexes as catenin complexes (CDH1, CTNND1/A1/B1), and cell junctions (CTNNA1/B1) and GTPase complexes (GNAI2/3, GNAS, GNB2/3, RRAS, and RRAS2) (FDR<0.05) (Figure 6B; Table S13). They make part of the physical subnetwork including pathways of apelin signaling and neurotransmitters synapse, DNA replication and repair, and adherent junctions (Figure 6C).

Among PROCA11-interacting proteins, we focused on two candidates: cell cycle and apoptosis regulator 2 - CCAR2 (aka DBC1), and CTNNA1 encoding ⍺-catenin. First, CCAR2 has been reported to function as a co-regulator of multiple chromatin modifying and TFs including p53 and the AR V7 variant.36,37,38 Importantly, CCAR2 contains an RNA-binding S1-like domain important for its activities in DNA repair and cell cycle control.39 Second, ⍺-catenin makes part of adherent junctions and through its binding to actin fibers it plays an important role in cell adhesion, ECM interaction and organization of cytoskeleton.40 Notably, it doesn’t contain a canonical RNA-interacting domain; however, it was reported to bind RNA in one high-throughput study.41 To validate the proteomic experiment, we performed immunoprecipitation of CCAR2 and ⍺-catenin in AD-cells physiologically expressing PROCA11 at relatively low levels (Figure S6B) followed by reverse transcription and quantification of immunoprecipitated RNAs relative to TElomerase RNA Component (TERC), a nuclear noncoding RNA of the telomerase complex. It showed a significant enrichment of PROCA11 but no other irrelevant housekeeping noncoding RNAs such as U1 or TERC (Figures 6D and 6E). Hence, we confirmed that PROCA11 interacts with CCAR2 and ⍺-catenin. Although, this association was not restricted only to the lncRNA since other mRNAs were immunoprecipitated with the two proteins but to a lower extent.

Finally, considering the anti-apoptotic role of PROCA11 under androgen-deprived conditions, we examined the relationship between PROCA11 and CCAR2 expression. Western blot analysis revealed that PROCA11 overexpression results in a 30%–50% reduction in CCAR2 levels in total protein extracts (Figure 6F). Immunofluorescence analysis further revealed that this reduction is most pronounced in the nucleus, the main subcellular location of CCAR2. Notably, the strongest effect was observed in cells overexpressing the V3 isoform, which is more enriched in the nucleus than V1 and V2 (Figure 2, Figure 6G and 2G).

Taken together, proteomic analyses revealed PROCA11 interaction with ⍺-catenin and CCAR2, linking PROCA11 activity to regulation of cell adhesion, cytoskeleton organization, and proliferation (reviewed by Kim et al. and Kobielak et al.39,40). Moreover, the inverse correlation in expression between PROCA11 and CCAR2 in androgen-deprived cells further supports a functional link between them. This finding provides the first indication of a potential regulatory role for PROCA11.

Discussion

Emerging examples suggest that lncRNAs intervene in fine-tuning regulation of many cellular processes modulating cell identity and fate.42 In cancer, lncRNAs are proposed to influence tumor progression and response to therapies.43,44 However, very few examples of cancer-related lncRNAs have been documented within their dynamic activation contexts resulting from the tumor plasticity. This is even more acute for prostate tumors in response to hormone treatment. In this work, we report the first early and rapidly ADT-induced lncRNA with pro-proliferative and anti-apoptotic functions enabling cells to adapt better to androgen-deprivation and contribute to the emergence of NE phenotype.

Transcriptome profiling of the dynamic in vitro LNCaP system revealed PROCA11 as the most activated in response to androgen deprivation. Importantly, PROCA11 levels are elevated in tumors of high Gleason grades, ADT-treated adenocarcinoma, and NEPC tumors. Therapies targeting AR signaling, such as ADT, affect the plasticity of prostate cancer cells by inhibiting the capacity of AR to activate transcription of proliferation and pro-survival genes. On the other hand, ADT results in a release of AR and activation of genes involved in stem-cell fate, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, or NE trans-differentiation.45,46 Similarly, androgen ablation in the adenocarcinoma cell line induces, otherwise low, transcription of PROCA11. It coincides with the AR release from the lncRNA promoter and persists in cells undergoing the NE trans-differentiation. PROCA11 expression is also stimulated by the depletion of AR in the presence of androgens. Together, these findings strongly suggest that the lncRNA transcription is maintained at basal level in androgen-dependent cells being repressed by AR but strongly activated by another TF upon androgen withdrawal followed by AR release. In silico search for TF binding sites identified response elements of the basal TBP subunit of TFIID, but also of the AP-1 complex members (JUN and FOS), some other ligand-dependent TFs (GR and RAR/RXR), differentiation TFs (POU2F2, LEF1, and FOXA1) and others (ESR1- and USF2). All these TFs are upregulated in response to androgen deprivation and may orchestrate an unexplored transcriptional program through NE trans-differentiation. In perspective, it will be important to determine which TF takes over in early response to androgen deprivation enabling a repression-to-activation switch or the maintenance of AR-transactivated genes expression as it was shown for the ERɑ TF in AR-negative cells and prostate cancer tumors.47

The absence or low expression of PROCA11 in normal prostate tissues and its spiking in NE and ADT-treated prostate tumors suggest a critical role for this lncRNA in the disease progression. ADT induces very heterogeneous changes in cells, some with still active AR signaling but also AR-negative cells acquiring stem cell-like or NE features.8 In all studied clinical cases, elevated PROCA11 levels were associated with co-expression of NE signature. However, scRNA-sequencing also revealed PROCA11 presence in the ADT-naive tumor of the mixed adenocarcinoma and NE phenotypes, but not in the pure homogeneous adenocarcinoma. Notably, it was expressed in AR-positive adenocarcinoma cells presenting more proliferation. One can also speculate that a particular NE microenvironment contributes to PROCA11 expression in the presence of active AR signaling. NE cells secrete a variety of peptides and proteins with endocrine and paracrine effects, modulating properties of luminal cells to regulate prostatic growth and function (reviewed in48). Therefore, in heterogeneous mixed tumors, the NE cells’ secretome can activate a specific response, remodeling the transcriptome of adjacent adenocarcinoma cells stimulating PROCA11 expression and endowing them with NE properties (Figure 6H). Single-cell and spatial resolution studies will shed light on the intricate relationship between PROCA11, the AR signaling, and the onset of CRPC and NEPC phenotypes in tumors.

PROCA11 expression in AR-positive tumor cells was associated with increased proliferation. Moreover, perturbation of PROCA11 expression through gain- and loss-of-function approaches led to changes in cell proliferation and cell cycle progression. Functional association analysis revealed that attenuation of PROCA11 expression is concomitant with downregulation of genes involved in androgen biogenesis but upregulation of Ras and cAMP pathways. Activation of the Ras signaling in ADT-treated prostate cancer was reported to reduce the androgen requirement of LNCaP cells for growth.49 In a similar way, activation of the cAMP pathway was reported to promote AR translocation into the nucleus in conditions of low androgen levels, also stimulating AR signaling and survival.50 Thus, activation of these two pathways in response to PROCA11 depletion may converge to sensitize AR activity to sub-physiological levels of androgen. In the brain, cAMP function is tightly linked to synapse formation and neuron plasticity (reviewed in Shahoha et al51). In PROCA11-depleted cells, activation of the cAMP signaling pathway coincides with increased expression of genes involved in synaptic activity. Both in the absence and presence of androgens, alterations in PROCA11 expression are associated with perturbations in the expression of genes governing cytoskeletal organization, cell adhesion, and motility. These pathways are known to play critical roles in the acquisition and maintenance of the NE phenotype. Moreover, PROCA11 contributes to cell survival following androgen withdrawal by reducing apoptosis. All these findings point to the role of PROCA11 in regulation of cell plasticity, probably enabling the emergence or maintenance of the NE phenotype in conditions of perturbed androgen supply. This regulatory mechanism implies an intricate crosstalk between PROCA11 and AR and possible feedback loops involving metabolic and neurogenic signaling.

The proteomic study through the high-throughput RNA pull-down revealed PROCA11 capacity to bind proteins with activities in cell adhesion and cytoskeleton organization, replication and apelin signaling. Among them, CCAR2 and ⍺-catenin were further validated by RIP for the interaction with PROCA11 in the androgen-dependent context. The nuclear CCAR2 has been already shown to bind MALAT1. This lncRNA impedes CCAR2-mediated inhibition of the SIRT1 deacetylase and consequently perturbs the downstream p53 pathway increasing cell proliferation.52 It is tempting to propose a similar mode of action for PROCA11 (Figure 6H). In line with this hypothesis, high levels of PROCA11 were associated with increased proliferation in adenocarcinoma cells of the mixed AD/NE tumor (scRNA-seq, Figure 4D) and in androgen-dependent cells (Figure 5C). Moreover, overexpression of PROCA11 in androgen-deprived cells results in a decrease of CCAR2 levels additionally supporting a possible role of the lncRNA as a decoy. Also, considering the diversity of functions and importance of the S1 RNA-binding domain reported for some CCAR2 activities, one may hypothesize other scenarios where PROCA11 comes in a play. Since ⍺-catenin lacks a canonical RBD, it is possible that RNA interacts with adherent junctions and cytoskeletal complexes, modulating cytoskeletal tension and junctional dynamics while playing an architectural role in their stabilization (Figure 6H). This possibility is particularly compelling in the context of NE trans-differentiation, where the disassembly and subsequent reorganization of cell-cell contacts are key features of the process. The link between PROCA11 and cell adhesion was supported by transcriptomic changes and altered migratory behavior observed not only under androgen-dependent conditions, but also during the early stages of transition to a NE phenotype (Figure 5).

In addition to CCAR2 and ⍺-catenin, many other proteins were identified as possible interactors of PROCA11. Intriguingly, among them was a group of GTPases playing important roles in signal transduction and many cellular processes. Other candidates belonged to the RFC family of replication factors. Similar to ⍺-catenin, they do not contain a canonical RBD but were identified in several high throughput studies as directly interacting with RNAs or making part of ribonucleoprotein complexes.53,54,55,56,57 Following the new paradigm of riboregulation and architectural role of RNAs in proteins’ activities, it will be important to further investigate the relevance of our findings and how PROCA11 can influence them in the context of CRPC and NEPC onset.

This study discovered an unannotated AR-repressed lncRNA PROCA11 associated with castration-resistant and NE phenotypes in prostate cancer. Its expression in CRPC context is most likely stimulated though paracrine signaling of NE populations of cells and maintained high even in the AR-positive CRPC cells. We propose that PROCA11 plays a role in the plasticity of prostate cancer cells contributing to the reinforcement of adherent junctions, but also to the cell cycle regulation, through architectural or scaffold functions. Such activity may amplify the NE phenotype, potentially reinforcing its own expression through feedback signaling in neighboring androgen-dependent cells, and may promote subsequent androgen independence and increased proliferation (Figure 6H). Still, it remains to decipher precise molecular mechanisms of how PROCA11 promotes the development of a NE phenotype and cell survival following hormone withdrawal.

Limitations of the study

There are three main limitations to note. First, induced PROCA11 expression in response to androgen deprivation is hard to generalize beyond LNCaP cells because of death or limited proliferation of other androgen-dependent cell lines, such as VCaP, in the absence of androgens restricting any further study. Second, research into PROCA11 in NE cells is needed, but is hampered not only by the lack of suitable models but also by the insufficiency of single-cell analyses of clinical samples. Finally, more studies on the PROCA11 lncRNA interactions with proteins, using orthogonal techniques such as RNA pull-down or CLIP, are recommended to better understand the dynamics and functional significance of these associations.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

The LNCaP cell line was purchased from ATCC (CRL-1740), cultured at 37°C in a humid 5% CO2 atmosphere in either the RPMI 1640+GlutaMAX medium (Life Technologies) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 2 nM DHT (AD-cells, or +DHT) or the phenol red-free RPMI 1664 medium supplemented with 10% dextran charcoal-stripped FBS (CSS) (NE- and AI-cells). HEK293T was purchased from ATCC (CRL-3216) and cultured in DMEM + GlutaMAX (Life Technologies) supplemented with 10% FBS. The 22Rv1 and PC3 cell lines were purchased from ATCC (CRL-2505, CRL-1435) and cultured in 10% FBS complemented RPMI 1640 media. Cells were systematically tested and free of mycoplasma. Cell lines are listed in Table S14.

Plasmids

The PROCA11 cDNA was obtained by reverse transcription and PCR with primers complementary to 5′ and 3′ extremities of the gene, V1, V2 and V3 amplicons were resolved in the 1% agarose gel and purified using the gel purification kit (Qiagen). Each cDNA was cloned into pDONR201 (Invitrogen) and further sub-cloned into pLenti CMV Blast DEST (706-1) (Addgene, #17451)59 using the Gateway system (Thermo Fisher). Constructs were validated by Sanger sequencing. Plasmids are listed in Table S14.

Method details

Transfection and lentiviral transduction

Transfections were performed at 50–70% of cell confluency using the JetPRIME transfection kit (Polyplus) following the manufacturer’s protocol: (i) LNCAP with siPOOL (siTOOLs Biotech) targeting PROCA11 (siP11), AR (siAR) or non-targeting scramble (siSCR) at a final concentration of 30 nM. Further experiments (RNA-seq, Apoptosis assay) were performed 48 h post-transfection; (ii) HEK293T with 1.3 μg of psPAX2 (Addgene, 12260), 0.8 μg of pVSVG (Addgene, 36399)59 and 0.8 μg of the lentiviral plasmid hosting the cDNA of GFP lacking the first ATG (CTR) or PROCA11 variants (V1, V2, V3). 48 h post-transfection, viral supernatant was collected, filtered and added to cells at 70% of confluency at 0.5 MOI for 24 h. Transduced cells were sub-cultured in the medium supplemented with Blasticidin S (Thermo Fisher) at 10 μg/mL for four passages prior to use. The expression of PROCA11 isoforms was validated by RT-qPCR using primer sets specific to each transcript (Table S14).

Subcellular fractionation

The protocol is described in details in Pinskaya et al.25 for 107 cells. All steps were performed on ice with ice-cold buffers supplemented with 20 U/μL SUPERase-IN (Thermo Fisher) for RNA extraction and 0.1 mM AEBSF (SIGMA) for protein extraction. For nascent transcription experiments, α-amanitin was added in the HLB buffer at 25 μM concentration. RNA extraction from all fractions was performed using Qiazol at 65°C for 5 min with regular mixing and then purified with miRNeasy kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For protein extractions, the chromatin fraction was sonicated for 4 min (30s-on/30s-off, “high power”) on the Bioruptor Plus (Diagenode). Proteins’ content was quantified with the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher).

RNA extraction, reverse transcription, RNA-seq library preparation and sequencing

Total RNA extraction was performed directly from cell cultures with miRNeasy kit according to the manufacture’ instructions including the DNase treatment step (Qiagen). Reverse transcription was performed on 500 ng of RNA with random primers and SuperScript II Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher). RNA-seq libraries were prepared from 500 ng of RNA using the TrueSeq stranded total RNA-seq protocol with the ribo-zero depletion of rRNAs and were sequenced on HiSeq (Illumina).

Quantitative PCR

cDNA was diluted 10–50 times and used for the real-time PCR with LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master (SIGMA) and primer sets specific to exon-exon junction, intron or exon regions of each transcript of interest, using the absolute quantification approach. The RPL11, POLR2F or ACTB genes were used as reference genes for relative abundance calculations. Error bars represent standard deviations in relative quantity of cDNA prepared from at least three independent experiments. Primers sequences are listed in the Table S14.

Cell cycle analysis

In total, 2 × 105 cells were recovered after trypsinization, washed twice in PBS, resuspended in 500 μL of ice-cold 70% EtOH and incubated at 4°C for 30 min. Subsequently, cell pellets were treated with 50 μL of RNase A solution (100 μg/mL) for 5 min at room temperature and stained in 300 μL of 50 μg/mL propidium iodide solution for the next 30 min. Cells were processed using the NovoCyte flow cytometer (Agilent). Data was acquired for 104 single cells per condition and analyzed by the FlowJo software (v10.9.0).

Proliferation

Cells were seeded (8000 cells/well) in a 96-well RTCA E-plate (Agilent) in 4 replicates per condition. Following a short spin at 130 x g for 1 min, cells were transfected with siSCR or siP11 as described above. Electrical impedance was measured for 260 intervals of 1 h using the RTCA xCELLIgence device (Agilent). Cell index slope and doubling time were retrieved from the software and used for statistical analysis in R.

Wound healing assay

Cell suspensions containing 2 × 106 cells were seeded to each well of the six-well plate and left growing to 95–100% of confluence. After scratching, each wound was imaged twice at time t = 0 and t = 144 h post-scratch with The ZOE Fluorescent Cell Imager with a total of 30–48 high-field units per condition. The invaded area was calculated as a ratio of migrated cell surface area to total surface area with a MatLab-based image automated analysis software “HTM wound healing tool”.61 Results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean, with statistical analysis performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test.

Apoptosis assay

Cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at 30% of confluence, transfected with siSCR or siP11 at 40%. The assay was performed 48 h post-transfection following trypsinization and wash in phosphate buffered saline (PBS without Ca2+) at room temperature and using the Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit APC (eBioscience). Annexin V-APC and PI binding were detected with a LSRFortessa X-20 flow cytometer. The flow cytometry results were analyzed by the FlowJo software to calculate the % of Annexin V and PI positive cells for each of three replicates, mean and SD values.

RNA-seq analysis

Read alignment files were used as input for running the Scallop v0.10.5 transcriptome assembler with default options.64 Assemblies were merged and mapped to the reference annotation Gencode Release 32 (GRCh38.p13) by the Cuffmerge v1.0.0 tool.65 All non-annotated transcripts with class code “u” (intergenic, n = 4120 transcripts, n = 2582 genes), “x” (antisense, n = 2837 transcripts, n = 1629 genes) and code “j” (sense exonic overlap, n = 35 transcripts and n = 1 gene) restricted only to noncoding genes were retrieved and added to GRCh38.p13 to make a new custom transcriptome assembly. Reads were mapped using Tophat v2.0.4 (max mismatches allowed = 3)66,72 The uniquely mapped reads were counted at the gene level using featureCounts v2.0.067 and the custom assembly build and, then, processed by DESeq2 v1.26.072 using the DESeq R package.62,68 Luminal, basal, NE gene signatures were retrieved from,27 ARGs were retrieved from.73

Correlation expression matrix and gene ontology

A correlation matrix was built between each lncRNA and the totality of differentially expressed PCGs (n = 6982) obtained from all the time points of hormonal deprivation (15 days, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months) using the pcor() in R. Highly correlated PCGs (|pearson r| ≥ 0.99) were selected for the gene ontology analysis with the R package clusterProfiler v3.14.3 (fun = enrichGO, ont = “BP”, pvalueCutoff = 0.2). PROCA lncRNAs (n = 15) make part of the functional group [nerv | neur | axon | synap].69

Public bulk and single-cell RNA-seq datasets query

Read counts from TCGA were retrieved by TANRIC.58 Tumor classification as low-, intermediate or high-risk group was performed according to Gleason and TMN scores as previously described.23 Expression in normal tissues was obtained from the GTEx Portal via the SRA cloud data delivery service, by using the dbGaP eRA credentials for accession number phs000424.v9.p2, and the google cloud platform for storage on 12/15/2022 (https://gtexportal.org/home/).26 CRPC RNA-seq datasets were accessed through the NCBI GEO data repository under the number GSE126078,8 and through dbGaP under the number phs001666.v1.p1.6 ScRNA-seq datasets of PDX 224R and 287R MURAL collection were downloaded from the Sequence Read Archive database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/) under the BioProject accession PRJNA675382 and analyzed as described in Risbridger et al.9 Distribution of cells among phases of the cell cycle was assessed using Seurat clustering toolkit.28

SmiFISH probes

Probes design and procedure were carried out following the instructions of Tsanov et al.74 SmiFISH probes are listed in Table S14 and their position is showed in Figure 1D.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were seeded in DHT-containing or hormone-depleted media at the density of 40 × 104 cells per well, left growing for 78 h (AR) and 96h (CCAR2), then fixed with 4% Formaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Following permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 and blocking with 1% BSA, cells were stained with anti-AR (ab108341, Abcam) at 1:200 dilution, or anti-CCAR2 antibody (ab215852, Abcam) at 1:500 dilution for 1 h, washed three times with PBS, and stained with the mix of Phalloïdin Alexa Fluor plus 647 (A22287, Thermo Fisher) at 1:400 dilution and either anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (A11008, Thermo Fisher) or Anti-Rabbit, Alexa Fluor 594 (A11037, Thermo Fisher) at 1:300 dilution for 45 min at room temperature in the dark. After three washes with PBS, the cells were treated with DAPI for 20 min at room temperature, in the dark. After three washes, the coverslips were mounted with ProLong Glass Antifade Mountant (P36980, Thermo Fisher) following the manufacture’s procedure and then processed for imaging ProLong Glass Antifade Mountant (P36980, Thermo Fisher) following the manufacturer’s procedure and then processed for imaging.

Microscopy image acquisition and analysis

For smiFISH and AR-IF, images were acquired as stacks over the cells’ height (z) with an interval of 0.3 μm with a 40x (AR-IF) or 60× (smiFISH) objective on a Zeiss AxioVision microscope equipped with an ApoTome module. Image analysis was carried using the ImageJ2 Fiji package.71 Stacks, spaced out by 0.3 μm, were merged using maximum intensity Z-projection. Two regions of interest per cell defined total and nuclear areas. PROCA11 foci were counted using find maxima and the threshold = 80. Cytoplasmic counts were obtained by subtracting the total with nuclear counts.

For CCAR2 immunofluorescence, images were taken as stacks over the cells’ height (z) with an interval of 0.5 μm with a 63× oil immersion objective on a Zeiss LSM 880 NLO two-photon microscope. Three-dimensional image analysis was performed in Imaris (Oxford Instruments) using a surface segmentation module. Results are presented as the ratio of total CCAR2 signal intensity to nuclear volume. A nonparametric Welch’s t test was used to assess statistical significance between each of the two conditions.

CUT & RUN

The experiment was performed in two replicates following the protocol from Henikoff lab75 with modifications described in Jarroux et al.76 DNA fragments were purified with NEB Monarch PCR & DNA purification kit following the protocol enrichment for short DNA fragments and was quantified by Qubit HS DNA kit. Antibodies are listed in Table S14.

CUT & RUN libraries preparation and sequencing

CUT & RUN libraries were prepared using the TruSeq ChIP-Sample Prep Kit-Set A and PCR Box (Illumina) from 5 ng of DNA with 10 PCR cycles for amplification following the manufacturer’s procedures. Concentration and quality were assessed by Qubit with dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher) and High Sensitivity DNA Analysis Chip Kit (Agilent Technologies). Libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq – S1 device in a pair-end 100 bp read length mode.

CUT & RUN analysis

Reads were first aligned to human genome version GRCh38 using bowtie (version 2.4.1),63 with options -I 10 -X 700 --no-mixed. Reads with MAPQ value ≥30 were kept for further analysis. To equilibrate datasets, the larger samples were randomly down-sampled. For AR, peaks were called relative to the IgG control using MACS2 version 2.2.670 and options: -f BAMPE -g hs -B --keep-dup all. For further analysis, common peaks between conditions were also merged into peak regions. The merged peaks were assigned to closest genes and the shortest distance between each peak and the closest transcription start site (TSS) coordinates were calculated using the Gencode v32 annotation. The AR binding site within the PROCA11 region was inferred using PROMO.24

Gene features analysis

Gene ontology (GO) biological process, KEGG pathways and cellular compartment terms enrichment analyses were performed using DAVID.77,78 The gene enrichment score was calculated as –log10(p-value). STRING was performed only for physical subnetwork with the highest confidence parameter (0.9), with disconnected nodes hidden. COMPARTMENTS and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were filtered for interactions with a strength above 0.7 and FDR below 0.05.79

In vitro transcription

Transcripts were produced using the T7 and SP6 RNA polymerase kit (Ambion) and linearized pCRBlunt II-TOPO plasmids containing PROCA11 (V1, V2, or V3) or XEF1 cDNA sequences under the control of the T7 or SP6 promoter. Biotin labeling was performed during in vitro transcription by incorporating Biotin-16-UTP at a 1:40 M ratio relative to unmodified UTP, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Each RNA sample was then denatured at 65°C for 10 min and allowed to cool to room temperature for at least 30 min in refolding buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8; 1 mM EDTA; 50 mM NaCl).

RNA affinity purification

In total, for four replicates, 4 × 107 cells were collected trypsinization, washed twice in PBS and incubated in the lysis buffer (1M HEPES-KOH pH7.5, 2.5M KCl, 0.5M EDTA pH8, 0.5 NaF) for 30 min on ice. The lysates were centrifuged twice at 16,000 x g for 20 min at 4°C to recover clarified lysate. Proteins quantity was assessed with the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher) and 2 mg of the proteins’ extract per pull-down was precleared using 50 μL of Dynabeads MyOne C1 (Thermo Fisher) for 2 h, then incubated with 100 pmol of refolded RNA for additional 1 h, and finally, with 50 μL of beads for 3 h more at 4°C on a rotating wheel. Following 3 washes in F150 buffer (5 mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% (v/v) Tween, 0.5 mM DTT, 1x Complete EDTA-free protease inhibitor), beads with precipitated RNA-protein complexes were incubated overnight at 37°C with 20 μL of 50 mM NH4HCO3 buffer containing 1 μg of sequencing-grade trypsin. The digested peptides were loaded and desalted on Evotips provided by Evosep (Odense, Denmark) according to manufacturer’s procedure.

LC-MS/MS acquisition and data analysis

Samples were analyzed on a timsTOF Pro 2 mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) coupled to an Evosep One system (Evosep, Odense, Denmark) operating with the 30SPD method developed by the manufacturer. Briefly, the method is based on a 44-min gradient and a total cycle time of 48 min with a C18 analytical column (0.15 × 150 mm, 1.9 μm beads, EV-1106) equilibrated at 40°C and operated at a flow rate of 500 nL/min. H2O/0.1% FA was used as Solvent A and ACN/0.1% FA as Solvent B. The timsTOF Pro 2 was operated in PASEF mode over a 1.3 s cycle time.80 Mass spectra for MS and MS/MS scans were recorded between 100 and 1700 m/z.

MS raw files were processed using PEAKS Online 11 (build 1.9, Bioinformatics Solutions Inc.). Data were searched against the SwissProt Homo Sapiens database (downloaded January 2023, 20405 entries). Parent mass tolerance was set to 20 ppm, with fragment mass tolerance to 0.05 Da. Specific tryptic cleavage was selected and a maximum of 2 missed cleavages was authorized. For identification, the following post-translational modifications were included: Acetylation (Protein N-term), Oxidation (M), Deamidation (NQ) as variables and Half of a disulfide bridge (C) as fixed. Identifications were filtered based on a 1% FDR threshold at both peptide and protein group levels. Label free quantification was performed using the PEAKS Online 11 quantification modules, allowing a mass tolerance of 10 ppm, a CCS error tolerance of 0.02 and a 0.5-min retention time shift tolerance for match between runs. Protein abundance was inferred using the top N peptide method and TIC was used for normalization. Normalized data is available at Table S9.

RNA immunoprecipitation

In total, 107 cells were collected by trypsinization, washed with the ice-cold PBS, lysed, and pre-cleared as described in Feng et al.81 Precleared lysates (2 mg) were incubated overnight with IgG (Ab37415, Abcam) or CCAR2 (ab215852, Abcam) or CTNNA1 (ab51032, Abcam) antibody (Table S14) on a wheel at 4°C and precipitated for 1 h on Dynabeads Protein A (Thermo Fisher). Following three washes beads were collected and treated with Qiazol for RNA extraction following the manufacturer’s procedure. The RNA enrichment was quantified relative to IgG, and the irrelevant housekeeping ncRNA – TERC. The efficiency of immunoprecipitation was assessed by the western blot (Figure S6B).
