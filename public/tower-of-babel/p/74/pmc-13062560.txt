Antimicrobial susceptibility of Escherichia coli isolated from wild deer (Cervus nippon yesoensis) and fox (Vulpes vulpes schrencki) in Hokkaido: a pilot study on sustainable antimicrobial resistance monitoring
Authors: Yui KAWABATA, Akira FUKUDA, Rin SATO, Hirokazu KOUGUCHI, Chie NAKAJIMA, Yasuhiko SUZUKI, Kohei MAKITA, Tetsuo ASAI, Masaru USUI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In Japan, sustainable national antimicrobial resistance (AMR) monitoring has been established in both human and veterinary fields. However, AMR monitoring in the environment, including wildlife, remains limited. This study aimed to assess the possibility of sustainable monitoring using wild deer transported to slaughterhouses and wild foxes surveyed for Echinococcus multilocularis. Escherichia coli, a common AMR indicator bacterium, was isolated from rectal swabs of deer and foxes using Chromagar ECC, with and without antimicrobials (cefotaxime (CTX) or nalidixic acid (NA). Antimicrobial susceptibility was determined, and β-lactamase genes were detected using polymerase chain reaction. Plasmids carrying β-lactamase genes were analyzed by whole-genome sequencing. A total of 104 E. coli strains were isolated from (103/120, 85.8%) deer and 61 strains from (56/99, 56.6%) foxes using medium without antimicrobials. Using a medium containing CTX and NA, two and three strains were isolated, respectively, exclusively from foxes. All deer strains were susceptible to the tested antimicrobials. However, 0–11.5% of fox strains from medium without antimicrobials (n=61) demonstrated resistance. Three strains from foxes had β-lactamase genes in plasmid. Finally, this study demonstrates the feasibility of sustainable AMR monitoring using wild deer transported to a slaughterhouse and wild foxes surveyed for E. multilocularis.

INTRODUCTION

Antimicrobials are used to treat bacterial infections in humans and animals and as feed additives in livestock. However, their use imposes selective pressure, resulting in the emergence and spread of antimicrobial-resistant bacteria (ARB) and antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) [2, 11]. This poses a global concern because it complicates the treatment of bacterial infections in both humans and animals.

In 2015, the World Health Organization published a global action plan [34]. In response, the Japanese government established the National Action Plan on antimicrobial resistance (AMR) for 2016–2020 and 2023–2027 [26, 27]. ARB and ARGs are detected in humans, livestock, and environment (wildlife) [4], indicating that human activities contribute to their spread. Therefore, the action plan states that AMR should be addressed using a One Health approach based on sustainable AMR monitoring results. Sustainable AMR monitoring in humans and animals is being conducted through the Japan Nosocomial Infections Surveillance [29], Japan Surveillance for Infection Prevention and Healthcare Epidemiology [13], and Japanese Veterinary Antimicrobial Resistance Monitoring System [31]. Although several studies have reported AMR in Japanese wildlife [4, 5, 12, 24, 30], no continuous monitoring has been conducted to date—likely because of the challenges in consistently collecting wildlife samples. Wildlife acts as reservoirs for ARBs and ARGs, and their movement could potentially spread these to livestock and the environment.

To enable continuous wildlife sampling, we used wild deer (Cervus nippon yesoensis) and foxes (Vulpes vulpes schrencki). Wild deer living in Hokkaido are hunted and consumed as game meat and are regularly brought to slaughterhouses. Wild foxes in Hokkaido are known to carry Echinococcus multilocularis, which can cause disease in humans. Therefore, a certain number of foxes are sampled annually to survey E. multilocularis carriage [23].

To enable sustainable AMR monitoring in wildlife, a pilot assessment was conducted by isolating Escherichia coli—used as an AMR indicator bacterium—from wild deer brought to the slaughterhouse and foxes collected during Echinococcus surveys. The goal of this study is to collect a consistent and sufficient number of samples to enable continuous monitoring of AMR in wildlife.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample collection

A total of 120 deer rectal swabs were collected using seed swabs (Eiken Chemical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at the Urausu Town Gibier Processing Center in June, September, and December 2022 and March 2023 (Supplementary Table 1). The deer were transported across various regions across Hokkaido. All samples were transported and stored at 4°C until isolation.

Additionally, 99 fox rectal swabs were collected using seed swabs (Eiken Chemical Co., Ltd.) in Hokkaido in March 2023. For the Echinococcus survey, foxes were collected across Hokkaido (Supplementary Table 2). The foxes were captured in 26 municipalities in Hokkaido between April 2022 and March 2023 during an annual surveillance program conducted by the Hokkaido Institute of Public Health, Japan. Because the samples may be contaminated with E. multilocularis, they were stored at −80°C to inactivate the parasite eggs before bacterial isolation [10].

This study used wild deer slaughtered for meat, so no permit was required. Fox used in this study were the same as those used in the Echinococcus survey, so no permit was required. The deer used were those within two hours of being hunted. It was not possible to know that the post-mortem interval, as hunters stored and transport their fox carcasses in different ways.

Isolation and identification of E. coli

E. coli was isolated from rectal swab samples using CHROMagarTMECC (Kanto Chemical Co., Inc., Tokyo, Japan) with and without cefotaxime (CTX) (1 µg/mL; Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan) or nalidixic acid (NA) (32 µg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and incubated overnight at 37°C. The swab was directly applied to the medium. CTX or NA was used to screen for potential extended-spectrum β-lactamase production or quinolone resistance. These antimicrobials are medically significant. For each sample, a maximum of nine colonies (three each from the medium supplemented with and without CTX or NA) were selected for further analyses. Bacterial identification was conducted using matrix-assisted laser desorption-ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry with the Bruker MALDI Biotyper system (Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA, USA) [22]. For strains not identified by the above methods, an E. coli-specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed to identify E. coli, as described previously [32] (Supplementary Table 3).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) were determined using a microbroth dilution method with an Eiken Frozen plate (Eiken Chemical Co., Ltd.), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The following 12 antimicrobial agents were tested: ampicillin (ABPC), cefazolin (CEZ), CTX, meropenem (MEPM), gentamicin (GM), kanamycin (KM), tetracycline (TC), NA, ciprofloxacin (CPFX), colistin (CL), chloramphenicol (CP), and sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim (ST). Resistance breakpoints were defined according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines [8]. E. coli ATCC25922 and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC27853 were used as quality control strains. We excluded clones in which strains isolated from the same sample exhibited similar antimicrobial susceptibility test results. However, isolates demonstrating a difference of three or more dilution steps in the MIC during antimicrobial susceptibility test were not considered a clone.

Detection of β-lactamase genes

In CEZ-resistant strains, β-lactamase genes were detected using PCR and sequencing. β-lactam-resistant genes (blaTEM, blaSHV, blaCTX-M-1group, blaCTX-M-2group, and blaCTX-M-9group) were detected by PCR using primers described previously [3, 9, 16] (Supplementary Table 4). The bla gene subtype was determined by sequence analysis, as described below. PCR products were purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Inc., Brea, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The mixture was prepared in a volume of 14 µL containing the following components: sterile distilled water, PCR products, and primers. Subsequently, Sanger sequencing was performed using the service of FASMAC (https://fasmac.co.jp/dnaseq). The primers used were the same as those used for PCR. The results were analyzed using Geneious (https://www.geneious.com/) and ResFinder [36]. To select the isolates to be subjected to whole-genome sequencing (WGS), β-lactamase typing was performed by sanger sequencing.

WGS and bioinformatics analysis

WGS was performed for the three E. coli strains harboring the β-lactamase gene. Genomic DNA was extracted from overnight cultures using phenol-chloroform and the QIAQuick® PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen N.V., Venlo, Netherlands). The DNA concentration was determined using a Qubit fluorometer. Sequencing was performed using a MiSeq (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and short-read sequences were assembled using SPAdes v3.15.5 [20].

Genomic DNA for long-read sequencing was extracted using a Genomic-tip 20/G and Genomic DNA Buffer Set (QIAGEN). The library was prepared using the Rapid Barcoding Sequencing Kit SQK-RBK004 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Long-read sequencing was performed using a MinION system with a Flow Cell (Oxford Nanopore Technologies).

Long-read sequence data were analyzed using the following methods—adapter trimming was performed using Porechop v0.2.4 (https://github.com/rrwick/Porechop). Quality filtering was performed using Filtolong v0.2.1 (minimum length: 1,000 bp; minimum mean quality: 9 or 10) (https://github.com/rrwick/Filtlong). Error correction was performed using short-read sequencing data with LoRDEC v0.9 [21]. De novo assembly was performed using Unicycler v0.5.1 [33]. All bioinformatics analyses were conducted using the Galaxy platform.

ARGs were detected using ResFinder and ABRicate (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate) [36]. Sequence types (STs) were determined by multilocus sequence typing (MLST) using PubMLST [14]. Plasmid replicon types were detected using PlasmidFinder v2.0 or 2.1 [6, 7]. These circular plasmids were compared with similar sequences using BLAST and visualized using the BLAST Ring Image Generator [1]. Plasmid sequences were annotated using DFAST [25].

WGS and characterization of plasmid carrying β-lactamase gene

WGS was performed on three E. coli strains harboring β-lactamase genes (F29-2, F95-1, and FC5-1), and the data were analyzed. The MLST analysis revealed that the F29-2 (Accession No. DRR906919), F95-1 (Accession No. DRR906920), and FC5-1 (Accession No. DRR905083) strains belonged to sequence types ST10, ST68, and ST162, respectively (Table 3).

Short- and long-read sequence analyses revealed that F29-2 had one circular chromosome and six plasmids. Detection of ARGs using ResFinder and ABRicate revealed that two plasmids (F29-2_L and F29-2_S) harbored β-lactamase gene (Table 3). F95-1 had one circular chromosome and one circular plasmid (pF95-1) that carried the β-lactamase gene. FC5-1 had one circular chromosome and four circular plasmids. Detection of ARGs using ResFinder and ABRicate revealed that two plasmids (FC5-1_L and FC5-1_S) harbored β-lactamase gene.

F29-2_L (152929 bp; Accession No. LC877974) harbored aph(6)-Id, blaTEM-1B, sul1, sul2, tet(A), and dfrA5. F29-2_S (61187 bp; Accession No. LC877975) harbored aph(6)-Id, blaTEM-1A, floR, qnrS1, sul2, and tet(A). pF95-1 (81003 bp; Accession No. LC877976) harbored blaCTX-M-55and qnrS1. FC5-1_S (139280 bp; Accession No. LC877977) harbored aph(6)-Id, blaTEM-1B, sul2, tet(A), and dfrA14. FC5-1_L (298733 bp; Accession No. LC877978) harbored aac(3)-IId, aph(6)-Id, aph(3’)-Ia, blaCTX-M-55, blaTEM-1B, mph(A), lnu(F), floR, qnrS1, arr-3, sul3, tet(A), and dfrA14. Detection of plasmid replicons using PlasmidFinder revealed that F29-2_L, F29-2_S, pF95-1, FC5-1_S, and FC5-1_L were classified into incompatibility (Inc.) groups FII, Inc.X1, Inc.FII, Inc.FIC, and Inc.HI2, respectively (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

In this study, E. coli was isolated from 85.8 and 56.6% of deer and fox rectal swabs, respectively, using a medium without antimicrobials. E. coli is commonly used as an indicator for AMR monitoring [5]. The rectal swabs of wild foxes were frozen at −80°C to inactivate E. multilocularis eggs. However, E. coli was isolated from over half of the wild fox samples. Deer living in urban areas can acquire ARB/ARGs [5], and foxes harbor ARB/ARGs [4]. Therefore, these animals can be used as indicators. Additionally, AMR monitoring in livestock is conducted using samples collected during meat hygiene inspections—this method can be applied to deer at gibier processing centers. However, foxes are difficult to capture. Therefore, an annual epidemiological survey of E. multilocularis prevalence in foxes in Hokkaido can be useful [23]. In this study, we collected 120 deer rectal swabs and 99 fox rectal swabs over a one-year period. Previous studies have shown that 237 deer samples were collected from 2016 to 2019, and 242 deer samples were collected from 2013 to 2017 [5, 24] Therefore, the method of this study allows for a relatively large number of samples to be collected continuously. These results indicated that wild deer and fox samples can be used for sustainable AMR monitoring of E. coli in wildlife. We don’t conclude that number of samples is enough for AMR monitoring in wildlife in this study. Further studies are needed to reveal the optimal and sustainable sample size.

Antimicrobial-resistant E. coli strains were isolated only from the fox samples. AMR studies in deer in Japan have demonstrated a low resistance rate [4, 24]. Therefore, our results indicated that the antimicrobial resistance rate in deer was remains low. In contrast, the resistance rates in foxes ranged from 0% for MEPM and KM to 11.5% for ABPC and TC. The rate of antimicrobial-resistant E. coli strains isolated from wild animals is higher in omnivores than that in herbivores [5]. This study demonstrated similar results, indicating a difference in the proportion of ARB between foxes (omnivores) and deer (herbivores) in Hokkaido. These results highlight the significance of considering species differences and comparing data from multiple animals, which may help avoid under- or over-estimating the spread of ARB in the environment.

An antimicrobial-resistant E. coli carrying β-lactamase genes (blaTEM and blaCTX-M) was isolated from the fox samples. The blaCTX-M-55 was detected in F95-1and FC5-1, but the MLST types of the E. coli strains were different. Furthermore, the plasmid replicons of two plasmids (pF95-1 and FC5-1_L) were also different. These results suggested that it is unlikely that specific ST or specific plasmids are prevalent in foxes living in Hokkaido. However, these results also demonstrated that these ARGs are carried by wild foxes in Hokkaido, Japan. These β-lactamase genes have been detected in humans, animals, and environment in Japan [4, 15, 19, 37]. Resistance rates vary based on habitat, and ARB/ARGs are more likely to be isolated from urban and agricultural areas than that from mountainous areas [5, 30]. In Hokkaido, foxes lived in forests and urban areas and their fringes [17, 28]. Therefore, measures should be implemented to prevent the spread of ARB/ARGs among wild animals, such as banning the feeding of wild animals and restricting their access to farms.

F29-2_S carried β-lactamase and plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance genes. A blast homology search revealed that the F29-2_S plasmid was highly similar to the plasmid of E. coli isolated from a urinary catheter in Hokkaido [18]. The other plasmids (F29-2_L, pF95-1, FC5-1_S, and FC5-1_L) carrying the β-lactamase gene were similar to plasmids in bacteria isolated from humans, animals, and environment in Japanese prefectures other than Hokkaido. In this study, we observed that highly homologous plasmids were isolated from the same prefecture, implying the possibility of ARGs circulation between humans and wildlife. However, the regional coverage of plasmid obtained between humans and wild animals was 79%, and direct transmission of plasmid could not be demonstrated in this study. Transmission of plasmids harboring ARGs between farm animals and wild weasels captured near animal facilities has been reported, suggesting transmission from livestock to wild animals [35]. However, transmission from wild animals to humans or livestock is unknown. Therefore, continuous AMR monitoring in wildlife is necessary to assess public health risks. Additionally, measures are required to prevent AMR transmission to humans and wildlife.

ARB/ARGs are spreading in humans, animals, and environment—necessitating countermeasures based on the One Health approach. There is less research and monitoring data on AMR in the environment than on AMR in humans and animals. This study indicates that continuous AMR monitoring using wild deer and foxes is possible in Hokkaido and will be useful for future environmental studies. Additionally, we demonstrated that plasmids carrying the β-lactamase gene are carried by wild foxes in Hokkaido, Japan. It is crucial to continuously monitor AMR in wildlife and implement measures to prevent ARG circulation between humans, livestock, and wildlife.
