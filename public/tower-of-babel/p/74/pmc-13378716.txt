Influenza vaccine awareness and uptake patterns among women of reproductive age in Pudong New Area: A community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Peipei Liu, Yanfang Min, Chunmei Cai, Yating Ren, Zhongyang Hu, Yakang Shen, Cuanhui Niu, Xiao Liu, Jing Wang, Shumin Chen, Qi Zhang, Yong Wang, Min Ji, Ke Zhou, Xiaowen Zhang, Hua Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe World Health Organization prioritizes pregnant women for influenza vaccination; in China, flu vaccination during pregnancy has not been widely adopted. This gap is due to a lack of targeted policies and limited public health promotion. We aimed to describe knowledge and attitudes toward seasonal influenza vaccination among women of reproductive age (15–49 y old) (WRA), to inform evidence-based policy interventions. A cross-sectional survey assessed WRA’s influenza and vaccine awareness, vaccination route preference, and influencing factors, analyzed via chi-square tests and multivariate logistic regression. The knowledge score among WRA in Shanghai Pudong was 3.77 ± 1.23, and the influenza vaccine knowledge score was 3.55 ± 1.66; the pass rates were 60.61% and 56.46%, respectively. Healthcare workers had the highest cognitive levels. Multivariate analysis indicated that education level and being a healthcare professional were predictors of the influenza vaccine pass rate. In particular, the preference for injection decreased sharply from 41.25% among non-pregnant women to 13.31% in the hypothetical pregnancy scenario. In comparison, nasal spray preference rose from 10.60% to 23.17%, and unwillingness increased from 10.19% to 35.58% (P < .05). Pregnant women preferred the nasal spray at 41.67%, a rate significantly higher than that of non-pregnant women (10.60%, P < .05). When receiving influenza vaccine recommendations, 87.63% of participants were more inclined to follow the advice of obstetricians and gynecologists. While WRA in Pudong New Area, Shanghai, demonstrates a relatively high level of attention to influenza and influenza vaccines, key gaps still exist. Strengthening obstetricians’ guidance, promoting non-injectable influenza vaccines, and delivering targeted health education are important strategies to improve vaccination uptake in this population.

Introduction

Seasonal influenza is an acute respiratory infection caused by influenza viruses and imposes a substantial disease burden worldwide.1,2 During its seasonal wave of infection, influenza leads to a large number of cases and deaths worldwide.3 The Global Influenza Surveillance and Response System (GISRS) reported that, even in mild years, influenza can result in 290,000 to 650,000 fatalities and 3 million to 5 million severe cases worldwide, and cause greater social and economic damage during epidemic.4,5 Studies have demonstrated that vaccination can significantly reduce the risks of influenza-related outpatient visits, hospitalizations, and deaths.6–11

International studies have indicated that pregnant women have a higher morbidity and mortality of influenza than the general population, with evidence showing that they account for a significant proportion of influenza-related death cases.12 Similar protective effects of influenza vaccines have also been confirmed in other high-risk groups, such as patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.13 A study showed that receiving an influenza vaccine during pregnancy reduced the risk of influenza hospitalization by an average of 40%.14 Clinical studies have shown that influenza vaccination during pregnancy not only substantially reduces the risk of influenza in expectant mothers but also provides passive immunity to the fetus by transferring maternal antibodies across the placenta in response to the vaccine.15 Influenza vaccination during the U.S 2023–2024 season was similarly effective against influenza-associated emergency department and urgent care encounters in pregnant and non-pregnant women.16

The World Health Organization (WHO) and numerous national public health agencies, including those in Australia and the United States, recommend influenza vaccination for pregnant women, a recommendation supported by studies on vaccination rates and healthcare provider attitudes.2,17–19 In China, the actual influenza vaccination coverage rate (VCR) among pregnant women remains critically low at 1.5% from 2000 to 2024.6 A 2024 survey reported that only 6.9% of pregnant women were considering vaccination, primarily due to safety concerns and a lack of knowledge.20 In contrast, markedly higher maternal influenza vaccination coverage is documented across developed Western settings. For pregnant women, influenza vaccination VCR range from 51.9%(18–24 y) to 64.4%(25–34 y) in the United States.18 During the 2016–2017 influenza season, the average VCR among women in European Union countries was 25%.21 Nevertheless, such favorable coverage levels are not universal across low- and middle-income countries worldwide. By comparison, studies in India showed that vaccination uptake was nearly zero due to insufficient knowledge and misconceptions among pregnant women and healthcare providers.22,23 It is well established from global investigations that influenza vaccination coverage among pregnant women varies widely across regions and health systems. Research has shown a positive association between female sex and influenza vaccination rates among those aged 18–49. WRA are vital to health protection.24 However, the WRA has not been identified as a key target group in relevant policies.

To address these gaps in the literature, the cross-sectional study aims to evaluate knowledge of influenza and influenza vaccines, and the willingness to receive influenza vaccination among WRA, which provides evidence for preconception and prenatal interventions to improve influenza vaccination coverage among WRA in urban China.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The study protocol received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board at Jiang Zhen Community Health Service Center, Pudong New Area, Shanghai. Written informed consent was acquired from all participants before data collection. All personally identifiable information was anonymized to ensure participant confidentiality.

Study design

A cross-sectional survey was conducted at 20 Community Health Service Centers (CHSCs) in Pudong New Area, Shanghai, China. Twenty CHSCs were selected based on their classification as urban (six), urban-rural fringe (seven), or suburban (eight), out of a total of 46 CHSCs available. The survey assessed vaccination status during the 2024–2025 influenza season, influenza vaccine awareness, and administration routes among WRA, as well as the reasons for receiving or not receiving the influenza vaccine. Data collected from Dec 2024 to Feb 2025 via an electronic questionnaire. Community investigators, trained by professional investigators, then conducted simple random sampling of 50–70 WRA at each CHSC, completed interviews, and collected questionnaires. The inclusion criteria were: 1) females aged 15–49 y; 2) living in Pudong New Area for ≥6 months; 3) having a fixed residence and contact number; 4) able to independently complete the questionnaire with informed consent. The exclusion criteria were: 1) community residents who lived in Pudong New Area of Shanghai for no more than 6 months; 2) WRA who declined to participate in the survey after being informed of the informed consent; 3) WRA who had a cognitive disorder or couldn’t answer.

The sample size calculation was based on influenza VCR among pregnant women and non-pregnant women of childbearing age worldwide. For WRA, the influenza VCR is 36.7%–37% in the United States,25 20%–26.4% in South Korea,26 and 40.6% overall in Japan.27 A VCR of 40.6% was used as the expected rate for WRA in this study.

The following sample size calculation formula for a cross-sectional study was used to estimate the required sample size:n=Zα/22×π×1−πd2

The overall expected VCR (π) was calculated to be 40.60%. With a 95% confidence level (corresponding to Zα/2), a margin of error (d) of 0.034, and a response rate of 80%, the calculated sample size (n) was 1200.

Data sources

We used a standardized questionnaire to collect data, including three sets of questions: (1) socio-demographic characteristics, including age, gender, occupation, educational attainment, and average monthly household income; (2) the knowledge of influenza and influenza vaccines; (3) willingness to vaccinate and preferred route of influenza vaccines. Written and informed consent was obtained from all participants before the interviews. The survey was conducted face-to-face by CHSC healthcare workers.

A pilot survey of 50 WRA was conducted to assess and refine the questionnaire. The pilot test yielded an effective response rate of 90%, with Cronbach’s α = 0.82 for the influenza knowledge module and 0.78 for the vaccine knowledge module, indicating satisfactory internal consistency. The formal questionnaire further demonstrated good reliability, with Cronbach’s α = 0.74 for influenza-related knowledge and 0.69 for influenza vaccine-related knowledge. These reliability metrics, along with alignment with standardized health literacy scoring criteria, supported the instrument’s reliability and validity for formal data collection.

Based on the scoring methods of relevant studies, influenza and influenza vaccine knowledge were assessed using a structured scoring system comprising 12 questions, with six focused on influenza-related knowledge and six on influenza vaccine-related knowledge. Each question was scored on a one-point scale, yielding a maximum score of six for influenza knowledge, six for vaccine knowledge, and 12 for the combined total. For single-choice questions, one point was awarded for correct answers, while incorrect responses or “don’t know” selections received zero points. For multiple-choice questions with n correct options, each correct selection was assigned one/n points, and the total score was summed. “Awareness” was defined as a correct answer to single-choice questions or a score of ≥0.6 on multiple-choice questions, and awareness rates were calculated using this criterion. A passing score was set at ≥3.6 for both influenza-related knowledge and influenza vaccine-related knowledge (corresponding to 60% of the maximum score of six for each domain), consistent with the threshold commonly used in domestic and international studies on health knowledge assessment.28 Scores below this threshold were considered failing; total scores and pass rates were computed using these benchmarks.

Statistical analysis

All the analyses were conducted in R, version 3.10. Descriptive analyses summarized the participants’ demographic characteristics.

Frequencies (percentages) summarized categorical variables; means (standard deviations) and medians (interquartile ranges, IQR) summarized quantitative variables. Factors influencing responses were analyzed using χ2 tests and logistic regression analysis. Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify independent factors associated with the influenza vaccine knowledge pass rate. The dependent variable was defined as passing the influenza vaccine knowledge test (≥3.6 points, coded as 1 = yes, 0 = no). Independent variables included age, education level, occupation, household registration, and household income, selected based on prior literature and clinical relevance. Potential confounders were identified and adjusted based on existing evidence and biological plausibility. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF); all VIF values were below 1.5, indicating no severe multicollinearity. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer – Lemeshow test, and the model showed acceptable fit (P > .05).

Results

This study included four key analyses among WRA: influenza and vaccine knowledge were evaluated; factors associated with knowledge levels were explored; preferences for vaccination routes were examined; and trusted sources of vaccination advice were identified.

Multivariate analysis confirmed that educational level was significantly associated with influenza-related knowledge. Participants with an associate or bachelor’s degree had a higher pass rate (63.77%, 653/1024) than those with a high school education or below (influenza: 63.77% vs 46.58%, OR = 1.76, 95%CI: 1.28–2.40, P < .001; vaccine: 57.32% vs 34.70%, OR = 2.26, 95%CI: 1.64–3.12, P < .001) (Table 3). Occupation was independently associated with vaccine-related knowledge. The pass rate among medical staff (75.00%) was significantly higher than among non-medical staff (52.27%, OR = 2.34, 95%CI: 1.31–4.18, P = .005). A marginally significant difference in influenza knowledge was also observed between medical staff (76.56% vs 59.77%, OR = 1.90, 95%CI: 1.05–3.45, P = .033) (Table 3). Household registration status was associated only with vaccine knowledge: WRA with Shanghai household registration had a higher pass rate (58.90%) than those without Shanghai registration (47.31%; OR = 1.95, 95%CI: 1.13–2.17, P = .04). However, no significant difference was observed in influenza knowledge (61.06% vs 58.85%, P = .17). Age and household income showed no significant association with the pass rates of either knowledge type (Table 3).

Willingness to accept influenza vaccine administration methods among WRA with different physiological statuses

We surveyed influenza vaccination preferences among pregnant and non-pregnant women and further explored vaccination intentions among non-pregnant women in a hypothetical pregnancy scenario. Among non-pregnant women (n = 1217), injection was the most preferred option (41.25%, 502/1217), followed by acceptance of both injection and nasal spray (38.00%, 462/1217), nasal spray (10.60%, 129/1217), and unwillingness (10.19%, 124/1217) (Table 4). In contrast, pregnant women (n = 60) exhibited a distinct preference pattern: nasal spray was the most preferred choice (41.67%, 25/60), followed by unwillingness (31.67%, 19/60), injection only (18.33%, 11/60), and acceptance of both methods (8.33%, 5/60) (Table 4). In a hypothetical pregnancy scenario, non-pregnant women reported 35.58% (433/1217) unwillingness to receive an influenza vaccination, which was considerably higher than in the non-pregnant scenario (10.19%, 124/1217). Preference for injections decreased sharply from 41.25% (502/1217) to 13.31% (162/1217) (Table 4). The preference for nasal spray (23.17%, 282/1217) (Table 4). Influenza vaccine recommendation-seeking preferences among WRA

Most participants (87.63%) prioritized advice from obstetricians. Preference rates for other sources were lower: 44.09% for other clinicians, 32.11% for community family doctors, 46.91% for the expert consensus recommendations, and 26.5% for national health policy guidance. Among 64 medical staff, 89.06% also preferred advice from obstetricians, consistent with other WRA’s preferences. However, medical staff showed a lower preference for community family doctors (46.88%), followed by 62.5% for clinicians, 54.69% for expert consensus, and 34.38% for national health policy guidance.

Participants demonstrated moderate knowledge of influenza and influenza vaccines. Higher educational attainment and healthcare employment were associated with better vaccine-related knowledge. Pregnancy status was strongly linked to a preference for nasal spray vaccination and lower acceptance of injectable influenza vaccines. Most importantly, obstetricians represented the most trusted source of guidance for influenza vaccination.

Discussion

This study focused on WRA to assess their current knowledge of influenza and influenza vaccines, potential influencing factors, and their willingness to receive different vaccination methods across different physiological states. Their opinions and preferred guidance on influenza vaccination during pregnancy, as a key health-protective measure, were also explored.

Compared with similar domestic studies, our study observed relatively high levels of knowledge about influenza and vaccines among participants. Educational attainment was a consistent predictor of both influenza and vaccine knowledge, in line with prior literature. In our study, awareness rates for influenza symptoms (68.91%), transmission routes (67.66%), preventive measures (90.05%), vaccination timing (69.46%), and the necessity of annual vaccination (55.52%) were all higher than those reported in a survey of pregnant women in Suzhou (63.74%, 62.23%, 72.39%, 6.97%, 19.18%, respectively).28 Moreover, knowledge scores for influenza (3.77 ± 1.23) and influenza vaccine knowledge scores (3.55 ± 1.66) were both higher than those reported in the Guangzhou study.29 This difference may be attributed to Shanghai’s strong economic development, abundant healthcare resources, and sustained investment in public health education. Pudong New Area, in particular, has implemented targeted health promotion activities, such as community lectures, digital health communication, and routine vaccination education, since 2020, which likely contributed to the higher influenza-related knowledge awareness observed in this study.

Despite this relatively good overall awareness, notable knowledge gaps persisted. Only 15.82% of participants could name specific influenza virus types, 36.34% understood the health risks influenza poses to pregnant women, and 34.53% could identify different influenza vaccine types. Our study also found that educational level and healthcare professionals were key factors associated with knowledge, consistent with previous findings from an Italian study on pregnant and postpartum women.30 These specific knowledge gaps should be prioritized in health education interventions to improve influenza vaccination among WRA in Shanghai.

Table 3 showed a clear association between pregnancy status and influenza vaccine hesitancy. Among non-pregnant WRA, 10.19% reported unwillingness to vaccinate. This proportion rose to 35.58% in the hypothetical pregnancy scenario, closely aligning with the 31.67% hesitancy rate observed among currently pregnant women. This trend suggests that WRA hold latent concerns about the safety of influenza vaccination during pregnancy. These trends are consistent with recent regional and international research. A 2023–2024 study on willingness to receive influenza vaccination during pregnancy in Shanghai found that only 10.90% of participants were willing to be vaccinated,31 and a study on the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) survey in Minhang District, Shanghai, reported an even lower proportion of 6.9%.20 Beyond China, fluctuating maternal vaccination coverage has been well-documented in the United States despite established vaccination promotion strategies. Uncertainty about fetal safety remains a persistent, primary deterrent for pregnant individuals across.32–35 Overall, low levels of knowledge, safety concerns, and insufficient policy promotion are key barriers to influenza vaccination among pregnant women in China.36 These results highlight the importance of shifting intervention timing from the “pregnancy period” to the “pre-conception period.” Currently, health education programs in China primarily focus on pregnant women.37 However, a 10-y follow-up study in the United States (2010–2020) showed that advancing interventions to the pre-conception period increased subsequent pregnancy vaccination coverage by 23.5%.38 Combined with our results, targeted interventions for pre-conception WRA (e.g., integrating influenza vaccine safety information into pre-conception health counseling and community family planning seminars) can alleviate latent concerns before pregnancy, preventing the sharp decline in vaccination willingness observed in the hypothetical scenario.

The results of Table 3 also indicated that pregnancy status strongly shaped preferences for the vaccination route. Preferences for vaccination methods differed by physiological status: pregnant women preferred nasal spray vaccines, and non-pregnant women in the hypothetical pregnancy scenario showed reduced preference for injectable vaccines and increased preference for nasal spray vaccines. This pattern is supported by multiple studies, which have found that fear of side effects, needles, or injection pain, and concerns about contracting influenza are among the prevalent factors associated with non-injectable vaccination.39–45 This trend may be explained by the fact that pregnant women often show heightened sensitivity to physical discomfort and infection risks, which is associated with physiological and psychological changes during pregnancy and with lower acceptability of invasive injections. On the other hand, nasal spray vaccines are characterized by non-invasiveness and mucosal immunization: they avoid skin puncture, reduce pain perception, and use an administration route consistent with the natural transmission pathway of influenza viruses, which is associated with enhanced psychological acceptability among pregnant women. A study by Blondel et al. revealed that non-injectable vaccines are more favored by pregnant women due to lower pain and higher perceived safety.46 Additionally, an Indian study found that offering nasal spray vaccines increased vaccination willingness among women planning pregnancy by 18.3%.47 Therefore, expanding access to safe, pregnancy-approved nasal spray influenza vaccines critical to overcoming two major barriers: fear of injections and safety concerns. This strategy has already been piloted in parts of sub-Saharan Africa, resulting in a 15.2% rise in vaccination coverage,48 demonstrating its practical applicability.

This survey also revealed that 87.63% of participants prioritized obstetricians’ advice above all other sources, with the level of trust significantly surpassing that of other clinicians, expert consensus, or national guidelines. This observation is supported by international evidence: a population-based survey in Geneva identified obstetricians as critical drivers of maternal influenza vaccine uptake,46 a U.S. study revealed that proactive counseling from obstetricians increased vaccine acceptance by 37.8%,35 and other U.S.vaccination administrative data further corroborate this association.49 These studies collectively demonstrate that obstetrician recommendations serve as a pivotal determinant of vaccine willingness among WRA. Given their influential role, obstetricians should serve as core messengers for preconception vaccine safety. Specialized training can strengthen their communication skills on this topic. During routine consultations, they should proactively explain the scientific mechanism by which influenza vaccines transfer antibodies across the placenta to protect the fetus. For example, they can cite evidence that vaccination during pregnancy reduces the risk of influenza in infants under six months by 40%.14 They can adopt the communication strategy used in the Indian study, where “doctors explain fetal development safety in conjunction with ultrasound examinations”,41 to address underlying safety concerns at the primary care level. This approach is consistent with the global consensus that “clinician guidance is a key predictor of vaccine acceptance”,31 forming an intervention loop of “evidence dissemination-trust building-willingness enhancement”; particularly, the hypothetical scenario among non-pregnant women reflected the same hesitancy observed among pregnant women, indicating concerns about vaccination safety during pregnancy. Overall, pregnancy status, whether actual or hypothetical, affects vaccination method preferences, offering valuable insights for the development of targeted public health strategies.

This study has several limitations. First, as a cross-sectional study, this investigation captured only a snapshot of influenza vaccine knowledge, attitudes, and willingness among WRA at a single time point (December 2024–February 2025). It cannot establish causal relationships between variables. Secondly, this study was conducted exclusively in Pudong New Area, Shanghai, a highly urbanized and resource‑abundant region. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings may be limited, and the results may not be directly applicable to rural or less developed areas in China. Thirdly, the relatively small sample size of pregnant women may limit the statistical power of related analyses.

Conclusion

This study addressed the gap in understanding influenza vaccine literacy and vaccination awareness among WRA in Pudong New Area, Shanghai, and showed a medium level of vaccine literacy and suboptimal willingness to be vaccinated. Educational level, employment in healthcare, and pregnancy status were significantly associated factors; pregnant individuals showed a clear preference for nasal-spray vaccines and high trust in obstetricians. Therefore, we recommend that obstetricians receive targeted communication training to deliver consistent, evidence-based information about the influenza vaccine during preconception and prenatal care. Integrating influenza vaccine education into routine preconception counseling can reduce latent safety concerns before pregnancy and increase vaccination willingness. Expanding access to needle-free nasal spray vaccines and strengthening obstetrician-led counseling should become core public health strategies to improve maternal influenza vaccination in China.
