Pediatric Hereditary Polyposis Syndromes: Diagnosis, Surveillance, and Management Across Evolving Guidelines
Authors: Hussein Khalifeh, Xiaoyi Zhang, Brett J. Hoskins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePediatric hereditary polyposis syndromes require lifelong surveillance and multidisciplinary management, yet pediatric care remains variable despite multiple evolving guideline frameworks. This review synthesizes current society recommendations and contextualizes their implementation within pediatric clinical practice.MethodsA narrative review was performed using current literature and major hereditary polyposis guideline frameworks selected for their pediatric relevance and clinical applicability, with emphasis on pediatric considerations in familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP), attenuated FAP, juvenile polyposis syndrome (JPS), Peutz-Jeghers syndrome (PJS), and rare or emerging polyposis syndromes.ResultsThe reviewed guidelines are broadly concordant in supporting syndrome-directed surveillance, but differ in pediatric specificity, age of initiation, surveillance intervals, and emphasis on extracolonic manifestations. The accompanying tables synthesize recommendations across major societies and highlight areas of concordance and variability for FAP, JPS, PJS, and less common syndromes. Key pediatric distinctions include hepatoblastoma screening in APC-associated disease, hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia evaluation in SMAD4-associated JPS, and early small-bowel surveillance in PJS to prevent intussusception. Persistent implementation gaps include management of genotype-negative disease, timing of surgical referral, use of advanced therapeutic endoscopy, and transition to adult care.ConclusionPediatric hereditary polyposis syndrome management may benefit from an integrated synthesis of society recommendations, individualized by phenotype, genotype, polyp burden, available expertise, and local resources. Consolidated guideline-based tables may serve as practical reference tools that facilitate more consistent pediatric care, although their impact on clinical implementation, consistency of care, and patient outcomes remains to be established.

Introduction

Pediatric hereditary polyposis syndromes (HPS) are a heterogeneous group of inherited disorders marked by gastrointestinal polyp formation and elevated lifetime risks of gastrointestinal and extraintestinal malignancy. Although individually uncommon, these conditions carry substantial clinical significance because they often present during childhood or adolescence, require repeated endoscopic surveillance, and may necessitate major surgical intervention before adulthood [1, 2]. The major syndromes encountered in pediatric practice include familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) and attenuated FAP (AFAP), juvenile polyposis syndrome (JPS), and Peutz-Jeghers syndrome (PJS), with rarer but increasingly recognized entities such as MUTYH-associated polyposis (MAP), constitutional mismatch repair deficiency (CMMRD; also referred to as biallelic mismatch repair deficiency [BMMRD]), Lynch syndrome, PTEN hamartoma tumor syndrome (PHTS), polymerase proofreading-associated polyposis (PPAP), AXIN2-associated polyposis, and GREM1-related hereditary mixed polyposis syndrome [2, 3].

Management of HPS in children differs in important respects from adult hereditary cancer practice, particularly in surveillance goals and timing of intervention, while requiring consideration of developmental status, anesthesia and procedural risks, family-based decision-making, and long-term continuity of care [4–6]. In children, clinical priorities often include prevention of complications such as bleeding, anemia, prolapse, protein-losing enteropathy, or intussusception, with long-term cancer prevention becoming increasingly important through adolescence and adulthood [1, 7]. Because cancer risk in childhood is typically lower than in adulthood, surveillance strategies must balance long-term cancer prevention against procedural burden, growth, psychosocial development, and quality of life [4, 8].

Syndrome-specific extracolonic manifestations, including hepatoblastoma in APC-associated disease, hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT) in SMAD4-associated JPS, and intussusception in PJS, can more commonly present in childhood and may influence both diagnosis and surveillance strategies [7, 9, 10]. In addition, the transition from pediatric to adult care represents a uniquely vulnerable period requiring deliberate longitudinal planning to maintain continuity of surveillance and management [5, 6].

This topic is timely and clinically relevant, reflecting ongoing developments in pediatric surveillance, genetics, and multidisciplinary management. A recent survey demonstrated that most pediatric gastroenterologists and surgeons manage only 1–5 children with HPS, and that care remains highly variable despite available guidance, with heterogeneity in surveillance practices, surgical referral thresholds, and documentation of disease burden [11]. An accompanying commentary emphasized the need for standardization and continued education in pediatric hereditary polyposis care [12]. Recent updates from European Society for Paediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN), the American Association for Cancer Research (AACR) Childhood Cancer Predisposition Working Group, and European hereditary tumor societies further reinforce the need for an updated pediatric-focused synthesis [3, 4, 8, 13, 14]. However, existing guidelines do not fully address key challenges in real-world pediatric implementation, including access to advanced endoscopic therapy, management of genotype-negative disease, and coordination of multidisciplinary and transitional care. This review aims to summarize current guidelines, emphasizes variability in real-world pediatric care, and identifies areas where existing guidelines lack pediatric-specific clarity for further study.

Methods

This narrative review synthesized current recommendations for pediatric HPS from major international society guidelines and key review articles. Guidelines were identified through targeted searches of PubMed and official society websites. Eligible documents included contemporary evidence-based guidelines or position statements addressing surveillance, diagnosis, or management of hereditary gastrointestinal polyposis syndromes with pediatric relevance. The principal frameworks selected for comparison were those from ESPGHAN, AACR, National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons (ASCRS), American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE), and the US Multi-Society Task Force (USMSTF) because of their broad international use, pediatric applicability, or influence on clinical practice. Recommendations were compared qualitatively with emphasis on surveillance initiation, surveillance intervals, extracolonic screening, and pediatric-specific management considerations. This review was intended as a narrative synthesis rather than a formal systematic review or guideline appraisal.

Comparison of Major Guideline Frameworks

Although recommendations from ESPGHAN, AACR, NCCN, ASCRS, ASGE, and the USMSTF are broadly concordant, they differ in emphasis, intended audience, and degree of pediatric specificity. ESPGHAN provides the most explicitly pediatric-focused recommendations for adenomatous polyposis, particularly in relation to timing of lower gastrointestinal surveillance and family-centered counseling in children and adolescents [4, 13, 14]. AACR guidance is especially valuable for childhood cancer predisposition syndromes and extraintestinal surveillance considerations, including hepatoblastoma risk in APC-associated disease and pediatric tumor surveillance across inherited cancer syndromes [8, 9]. By contrast, NCCN and other adult-oriented hereditary cancer frameworks are highly useful for longitudinal cancer risk management but may be less detailed in pediatric procedural timing and other childhood-specific issues. Recommendations across major societies are broadly aligned, but important differences remain in scope, clinical priorities, and implementation. Pediatric-focused guidelines place particular emphasis on age-appropriate surveillance initiation, developmental considerations, family-centered decision-making, and balancing procedural burden with long-term cancer prevention, whereas adult hereditary cancer guidelines focus more extensively on lifelong cancer risk reduction, post-surgical surveillance, and long-term management [4, 8, 15]. Highlighting these variations is clinically useful, as pediatric gastroenterologists, surgeons, and hereditary cancer specialists may reference different society statements in their practice, contributing to differences in management [11]. Importantly, these differences do not necessarily represent disagreement regarding optimal management. Rather, they often reflect differences in intended patient populations, healthcare systems, resource availability, regional practice patterns, and access to specialized expertise. Accordingly, implementation should be individualized based on local expertise, multidisciplinary resources, patient-specific factors, and available healthcare infrastructure while remaining consistent with the underlying principles of syndrome-directed surveillance. The strength of evidence supporting individual recommendations also varies, with many pediatric recommendations based on observational studies or expert consensus because high-quality prospective pediatric data remain limited.

These distinctions are illustrated by several syndrome-specific recommendations. In APC-associated polyposis, ESPGHAN provides explicit pediatric guidance on the age to begin lower gastrointestinal surveillance, whereas adult-oriented guidance focuses more heavily on timing of colectomy and long-term management [4, 15, 16]. In JPS, genotype-specific recommendations increasingly emphasize upper gastrointestinal surveillance and HHT evaluation for SMAD4 carriers [7, 17]. In PJS, pediatric guidance places strong emphasis on early small-bowel surveillance to prevent intussusception, not solely neoplasia [7, 8].

Familial Adenomatous Polyposis (FAP) and Attenuated FAP

Genetics and Pathophysiology

FAP is caused by pathogenic variants in APC and remains the most common HPS encountered in pediatric practice. Classic FAP is generally characterized by development of hundreds to thousands of colorectal adenomas (Fig. 1), whereas AFAP is associated with fewer adenomas, often approximately 10–99, and later onset of neoplasia [2, 4]. Genotype–phenotype correlations in APC are particularly clinically relevant in children because variant location may influence adenoma burden, age at presentation, desmoid risk, and the likelihood of attenuated versus classic disease, thereby helping guide counseling and procedural planning even if surveillance must still be individualized [2, 18]. Pathogenic variants in the 5′ and 3′ ends of APC and in portions of exon 9 are more often associated with attenuated phenotypes, while variants in other regions are linked to classic FAP [2].Fig. 1Diffuse colorectal adenomatous polyposis in familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP)

An important practical point is that de novo APC mutations occur in up to 30% of patients with FAP, so the absence of a family history does not exclude the diagnosis [2, 4]. Clinicians should therefore maintain suspicion for FAP in children with multiple adenomas or suggestive extracolonic findings even without an affected family member and should consider referral for genetic evaluation when the phenotype is concerning.

Clinical Presentation in Children

Colorectal adenomas in FAP often begin developing in the first decade of life, with approximately half of affected individuals demonstrating adenomas by age 15 and the great majority by adulthood [1, 4]. Presenting symptoms in children may include rectal bleeding, iron deficiency anemia, abdominal pain, diarrhea, or nonspecific gastrointestinal complaints, although many are diagnosed through predictive testing and surveillance after identification of an affected parent [1, 16].

Although colorectal cancer risk in childhood is low, it is not negligible. Reported risk is less than 0.2% before age 15 and approximately 1.3% before age 20, increasing sharply thereafter if colectomy is not performed [4, 15]. This relatively low early pediatric cancer risk supports structured surveillance during childhood rather than automatic early colectomy solely on the basis of genotype.

Extraintestinal Manifestations

Extraintestinal manifestations are a hallmark of APC-associated disease and may precede gastrointestinal symptoms. These include congenital hypertrophy of the retinal pigment epithelium (CHRPE), dental abnormalities, osteomas, epidermoid cysts, desmoid tumors, thyroid cancer, and upper gastrointestinal polyposis, especially fundic gland polyps and duodenal adenomas [1, 2, 16]. Thyroid cancer, including the cribriform-morular variant, is an important extracolonic malignancy in FAP [1, 16].

Hepatoblastoma risk is particularly relevant in infancy and early childhood. Although uncommon overall, its concentration in young children with APC pathogenic variants has led some pediatric guidelines to recommend abdominal ultrasound and alpha-fetoprotein surveillance every 6 months through approximately age 5 when a pathogenic APC variant is known early in life [8, 9]. Desmoid tumors remain one of the most challenging extracolonic manifestations because they can be triggered or worsened by abdominal surgery and can produce major morbidity despite lack of metastatic potential. APC variants between codons 1445 and 1580 have been associated with increased desmoid risk, making genotype potentially relevant to operative timing and counseling [18].

Genetic Testing and Screening Strategies

In families with known APC-associated FAP, predictive genetic testing is generally considered around the age at which colorectal surveillance would begin, typically around 10–15 years, although testing may occur earlier when results would change management, such as when hepatoblastoma screening is being considered in infancy or early childhood [4, 8, 9]. Genetic counseling before and after testing is essential and should be revisited as pediatric patients transition to adult care to address inheritance patterns, reproductive considerations, evolving disease knowledge, and increasing patient autonomy in medical decision-making.

Regarding lower gastrointestinal screening, both colonoscopy and flexible sigmoidoscopy have been used historically. Because adenomas usually begin distally, sigmoidoscopy was previously accepted in some settings, but colonoscopy provides full colonic assessment and is increasingly favored for comprehensive pediatric surveillance, especially as management decisions depend on total adenoma burden and rectal involvement [4, 15].

Surveillance Protocols

Most major guidelines recommend initiating colorectal surveillance for classic FAP between ages 10 and 14 years, with intervals of every 1–2 years depending on findings and polyp burden [4, 8, 15]. If no polyps are identified, intervals may be extended modestly in selected patients, but lifelong surveillance remains necessary.

Upper gastrointestinal surveillance is generally initiated later than colorectal surveillance because duodenal and gastric neoplasia usually emerge after adolescence. However, timing should be guided by symptoms and family history. When upper tract disease is present, surveillance intervals are guided by duodenal severity, commonly using Spigelman staging in older adolescents and adults [3, 4]. Extraintestinal surveillance may also include hepatoblastoma screening in early childhood and consideration of thyroid surveillance, though practice varies [8, 9].

Management Approaches

Management is determined by phenotype rather than age alone. Endoscopic polypectomy can control limited disease in selected children, but colectomy becomes necessary when adenoma burden is endoscopically unmanageable, when high-grade dysplasia or carcinoma is present, or when there is rapid progression in number or size of lesions [4, 15]. Surgical timing should therefore be tailored to disease burden and progression, with some adolescents able to defer surgery under close surveillance, while others require earlier intervention [19].

The major surgical options are total abdominal colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis and proctocolectomy with ileal pouch-anal anastomosis (IPAA). The former preserves the rectum and may be preferred when rectal disease is limited, whereas the latter is favored for extensive rectal polyposis or advanced rectal neoplasia [15]. In a recent U.S. pediatric multicenter cohort, the median age at colectomy was 14 years, and IPAA was the predominant operation, highlighting that many children ultimately require major colorectal surgery during adolescence despite increasingly individualized, surveillance-guided timing of intervention [20].

Chemoprevention remains controversial. Sulindac, celecoxib, and combination approaches such as sulindac plus erlotinib have reduced adenoma burden in trials, but no regimen has demonstrated replacement of surgery or proven cancer prevention, and toxicity remains a concern [21–23]. Concerns for cardiovascular toxicity in particular have limited the use of celecoxib [24]. For children, these strategies remain adjunctive and individualized rather than standard of care.

Juvenile Polyposis Syndrome (JPS)

Genetics and Molecular Features

JPS is an autosomal dominant hamartomatous polyposis syndrome caused most commonly by pathogenic variants in BMPR1A and SMAD4, although only about half of clinically diagnosed patients have an identifiable pathogenic variant on current genetic testing [1, 17]. This incomplete detection rate is clinically important because it means that a negative genetic test does not exclude JPS when phenotypic criteria are met.

Genotype–phenotype correlations in JPS are among the most clinically useful in hereditary polyposis. The distinction between SMAD4- and BMPR1A-associated disease is particularly important because SMAD4 carriers more often exhibit gastric polyposis, gastric cancer risk, and HHT overlap, with potentially more complex extracolonic surveillance needs, whereas BMPR1A-associated disease more commonly presents as predominantly colonic juvenile polyposis without the same degree of gastric or vascular involvement [10, 17, 25, 26]. Children without identified mutations may still meet clinical criteria and require surveillance, though available data suggest they may have a lower subsequent polyp burden than disease-causing variant-positive patients [17].

Clinical Presentation

JPS is generally diagnosed when a child has multiple juvenile polyps (Fig. 2), often defined clinically as five or more juvenile polyps in the colorectum, juvenile polyps throughout the gastrointestinal tract, or any number of juvenile polyps with an affected family history [7]. Presenting symptoms include rectal bleeding, anemia, abdominal pain, diarrhea, prolapse, and protein-losing enteropathy, especially with diffuse gastric involvement [1, 27]. Polyps often appear in childhood or adolescence, although age at diagnosis is variable and may be earlier in pathogenic variant-negative patients identified due to presenting symptoms [17].Fig. 2Pedunculated juvenile polyp in juvenile polyposis syndrome (JPS)

Cancer Risks

JPS carries substantial lifetime gastrointestinal cancer risk. Colorectal cancer risk has been estimated in the range of approximately 40–50%, and gastric cancer risk is particularly elevated in SMAD4-associated disease, with reported rates up to approximately 21–30% in some cohorts [10, 26, 27]. Cancer generally develops later than childhood, but these adult risks justify pediatric surveillance to reduce cumulative polyp burden and identify high-risk phenotypes early.

SMAD4-Associated HHT Overlap

The overlap of SMAD4-associated JPS and HHT is one of the most distinctive and clinically important features of pediatric JPS care. A meaningful proportion of SMAD4 carriers demonstrate HHT features, including epistaxis, mucocutaneous telangiectasias, pulmonary and cerebral arteriovenous malformations, and cardiovascular manifestations such as aortopathy and valvular abnormalities [10, 25]. Because some vascular manifestations can arise in childhood and can be clinically significant, SMAD4 carriers should undergo appropriate HHT evaluation at diagnosis and coordinated multidisciplinary follow-up thereafter [7, 25].

Surveillance and Management

Pediatric surveillance typically begins between ages 12 and 15 years, or earlier if symptoms are present, using colonoscopy and upper endoscopy at intervals of approximately 1–3 years depending on findings and polyp burden [7, 8]. Upper endoscopy is especially important in SMAD4 carriers because of gastric disease risk. By contrast, routine upper endoscopy may be less necessary in BMPR1A carriers without symptoms or unexplained anemia [7].

Endoscopic polypectomy is the first-line management strategy. Surgical intervention, including colectomy or gastrectomy, is reserved for uncontrolled bleeding, severe anemia, protein-losing enteropathy, cancer, dysplasia, or polyp burden that cannot be managed endoscopically [7, 27]. For mutation-negative patients and at-risk relatives without an identified familial variant, surveillance should be guided by phenotype and family history rather than by genetic diagnosis alone [2, 17].

Peutz-Jeghers Syndrome (PJS)

Genetics and Diagnosis

PJS is caused by pathogenic variants in STK11/LKB1 and is diagnosed clinically through a combination of hamartomatous polyps (Fig. 3), characteristic mucocutaneous pigmentation, and family history [2, 7]. Pediatric Manifestations

Pediatric PJS is notable for its characteristic hyperpigmented macules on the lips, buccal mucosa, and digits, as well as for small-bowel-predominant gastrointestinal polyposis [7]. Polyps may also occur in the colon and stomach. The most important pediatric complications are recurrent abdominal pain, intussusception, obstruction, and gastrointestinal bleeding. Many children with PJS experience urgent or emergent small-bowel complications before adulthood, historically leading to repeated laparotomies [1, 7].

For PJS, pediatric management is shaped not only by future cancer risk but also by the substantial childhood risk of small-bowel polyp-related complications, especially intussusception, which distinguishes pediatric surveillance priorities from those in adults [7, 8].

Cancer Risks

Lifetime gastrointestinal and extraintestinal cancer risks in PJS are substantial, including colorectal, gastric, small bowel, pancreatic, breast, gynecologic, and testicular cancers [7]. Reported lifetime risks include approximately 39% for colorectal cancer and 29% for gastric cancer in pooled estimates. In pediatric practice, however, acute bowel complications are often more immediately relevant than near-term malignancy, so surveillance aims both to prevent emergency presentations and to establish lifelong risk management [7].

Surveillance Strategies

Pediatric surveillance is generally recommended to begin around age 8, with colonoscopy, upper endoscopy, and small-bowel evaluation using video capsule endoscopy or magnetic resonance enterography, or earlier if symptoms are present [7, 8]. Polyps larger than approximately 10 mm should generally be removed because of the risk of intussusception and bleeding [7]. Balloon-assisted enteroscopy (BAE) has become increasingly important for therapeutic small-bowel polypectomy and may reduce the need for laparotomy when available in experienced centers [28–30]. In adulthood, surveillance expands to include extracolonic cancer screening, particularly for pancreatic, breast, and gynecologic malignancies, distinguishing long-term management from pediatric priorities focused on prevention of small-bowel complications [7].

Emerging and Rare Polyposis Syndromes

MUTYH-Associated Polyposis (MAP)

MAP is an autosomal recessive syndrome caused by biallelic MUTYH pathogenic variants. Its phenotype overlaps with AFAP, often with tens rather than hundreds of adenomas and onset in late adolescence or adulthood [3, 15]. Although less common in pediatric presentation, it should be considered in children or adolescents with adenomatous oligopolyposis, especially when family history is absent because of recessive inheritance.

Constitutional Mismatch Repair Deficiency (CMMRD)

CMMRD, also referred to as biallelic mismatch repair deficiency (BMMRD), is a rare autosomal recessive cancer predisposition syndrome caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in mismatch repair genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, or PMS2). It is characterized by extremely early-onset malignancies, often arising in the first decade of life, including hematologic cancers, brain tumors, and gastrointestinal neoplasia, frequently accompanied by multiple colorectal adenomas or polyposis. Patients may also exhibit café-au-lait macules and other features overlapping with neurofibromatosis type 1, reflecting the underlying hypermutator state. Distinguishing CMMRD from Lynch syndrome is critical, as disease penetrance is substantially higher and surveillance begins in early childhood, including colonoscopy as early as age 3–6 years, brain MRI, and hematologic monitoring [31, 32].

Lynch Syndrome

Lynch syndrome, caused by heterozygous or monoallelic pathogenic variants in mismatch repair genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, or EPCAM), is the most common hereditary colorectal cancer syndrome but typically presents in adulthood rather than childhood. In contrast to CMMRD, which results from biallelic mutations and is associated with extremely early-onset malignancy, Lynch syndrome is characterized by later-onset colorectal and extracolonic cancers, with surveillance generally initiated in early adulthood. Pediatric presentation is uncommon but should be considered in adolescents with early-onset colorectal cancer, advanced adenomas, or a strong family history of Lynch-associated malignancies. Distinguishing Lynch syndrome from CMMRD is critical, as surveillance intensity, timing, and cancer risk differ substantially between these conditions [33, 34].

Polymerase Proofreading-Associated Polyposis (PPAP)

PPAP is caused by pathogenic variants in the exonuclease domains of POLE and POLD1. It is usually characterized by oligopolyposis or adenomatous polyposis and increased colorectal cancer risk, with reported cases in adolescence. Associated extracolonic risks include endometrial cancer for POLD1 carriers and possible brain tumors for POLE carriers [35].

Other Rare Syndromes

Other rare syndromes increasingly captured on multigene panels include AXIN2-associated polyposis, often associated with oligodontia, and GREM1-related hereditary mixed polyposis syndrome, which features mixed histology colorectal polyps and increased cancer risk [36, 37]. PHTS, caused by PTEN pathogenic variants, may include juvenile-type gastrointestinal polyps but is more often recognized in childhood because of macrocephaly, developmental concerns, autism spectrum disorder, and mucocutaneous findings; routine colonoscopy generally begins later in life rather than in childhood [38–40].

Although these syndromes are less commonly encountered in pediatric practice, they remain important considerations in the evaluation of children with atypical polyposis phenotypes or early-onset malignancy. Given the limited pediatric-specific evidence base, recommendations should be interpreted cautiously, as management often relies on extrapolation from adult cohorts or expert consensus rather than robust pediatric data. Practical Approach to Diagnosis and Genetic Testing

Clinical Recognition and Diagnostic Approach

HPS should be suspected in any child with multiple colorectal polyps, hamartomatous polyps, early-onset adenomas, recurrent large polyps, suggestive extracolonic findings, or a family history of polyposis or early gastrointestinal cancer [2, 7]. Initial evaluation should incorporate polyp number, histologic type, distribution, family history, and extraintestinal findings. Polyp histology is especially informative: adenomatous polyposis suggests FAP, MAP, or PPAP; juvenile polyps in multiplicity suggest JPS or PHTS; and small-bowel hamartomatous polyps with mucocutaneous pigmentation suggest PJS.

Distinguishing a solitary juvenile polyp from syndromic disease is particularly important, as most isolated juvenile polyps are benign and nonhereditary, whereas recurrent or numerous juvenile polyps, upper gastrointestinal involvement, persistent anemia, or a positive family history should prompt genetic evaluation [1, 28]. Extraintestinal features such as macrocephaly, developmental differences, mucocutaneous pigmentation, oligodontia, osteomas, desmoid tumors, CHRPE, or HHT-related manifestations may further refine the differential diagnosis and guide targeted evaluation [10, 18, 36].

Genetic Testing Strategies

Current consensus favors multigene panel testing for most children with suspected HPS rather than narrow single-gene testing, except when phenotype is highly specific and a known familial variant is already established [2, 41, 42]. Genetic counseling is essential pre- and post-testing.

Variants of uncertain significance should not direct management, and surveillance should instead be guided by phenotype, family history, and longitudinal endoscopic findings. This is particularly relevant for children with convincing clinical polyposis but no identifiable pathogenic variant, including those with JPS, where genotype-negative disease is common. Periodic re-evaluation and broader sequencing may be appropriate when clinical suspicion remains high, particularly in children with atypical features or mixed phenotypes [2, 17, 41]. In such cases, longitudinal phenotype evolution and polyp burden trajectory often guide management more strongly than genetic findings alone.

Genotype–Phenotype Correlations

Genotype–phenotype information has growing clinical utility. In APC-associated disease, mutation location may help anticipate severity and desmoid risk [18]. In JPS, distinguishing SMAD4 from BMPR1A is especially useful because it informs expectations regarding gastric disease, HHT overlap, and upper gastrointestinal surveillance needs [10, 17]. Similar correlations for rarer syndromes remain less well developed and are an area of ongoing study.

Multidisciplinary Management

Team Composition and Coordination

Optimal pediatric HPS care requires coordinated multidisciplinary management involving pediatric gastroenterology, colorectal surgery, medical genetics, oncology, pathology, radiology, psychology, and specialized nursing or care coordination [5, 43]. Referral to specialized centers is particularly valuable for children with heavy polyp burden, in need of advanced endoscopic therapy, with suspected small-bowel disease, or consideration of prophylactic surgery.

Transition to adult care should begin early and proceed as a structured developmental process rather than a single transfer event. Transition planning should include education regarding lifelong surveillance, operative history or planning, genotype-specific risks, reproductive counseling, and coordination with adult hereditary cancer specialists [5, 6].

Endoscopic Management

Endoscopy in pediatric HPS extends beyond diagnosis and surveillance and increasingly includes therapeutic interventions aimed at delaying surgery, reducing symptom burden, and preventing acute and long-term complications. In FAP and JPS, endoscopic polypectomy may control disease for prolonged periods when lesions remain limited in number and size, while in PJS, advanced enteroscopy techniques are particularly important for removing clinically significant small-bowel polyps and preventing recurrent intussusception or laparotomy [7, 28, 30].

The growing use of endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR), video capsule endoscopy, and BAE highlights the need for referral pathways to centers with pediatric therapeutic endoscopy expertise, specialized anesthesia support, and multidisciplinary backup when polyp burden becomes complex or diffuse [28, 29, 43, 44]. Because access to these advanced techniques varies substantially across institutions, resource availability may directly influence thresholds for surgical referral and contribute to differences in clinical outcomes.

Surgical Considerations

Surgical decision-making in pediatric polyposis should be framed as a balance between oncologic risk reduction, residual bowel function, rectal disease burden, developmental readiness, and expected adherence to postoperative surveillance. In FAP, the choice between ileorectal anastomosis and IPAA is influenced not only by rectal polyp burden but also by anticipated quality of life, stool frequency, fertility considerations, and the family’s capacity for long-term surveillance of retained rectal or pouch mucosa [15, 20]. Similarly, in JPS, colectomy is generally reserved for endoscopically unmanageable disease, severe symptoms, or neoplasia, reinforcing that operative intervention in children should be individualized rather than triggered by age alone [7, 19, 27]. This variability aligns with observed differences in surgical referral thresholds across providers [11].

Psychosocial and Family-Centered Considerations

The management of pediatric HPS extends beyond surveillance intervals and procedural planning. Predictive testing in minors, repeated endoscopic procedures, anticipation of future surgery, and awareness of familial cancer risk may all contribute to anxiety for both patients and caregivers, particularly during adolescence when autonomy is developing but family-based decision-making remains central [5, 6]. Counseling should therefore address not only inheritance and cancer risk, but also school functioning, body image, bowel-function expectations after surgery, readiness for transition, and strategies for communicating genetic risk within the family. These issues are especially relevant in syndromes such as FAP and PJS, where lifelong surveillance often begins in childhood and intensifies during the transition to adult care [5].

Transition of Care

Transition should be approached as a structured process rather than a discrete transfer event. Best practice includes beginning transition discussions in early adolescence, documenting genotype and syndrome-specific surveillance needs, summarizing prior endoscopic and surgical history, clarifying residual anatomy, and identifying adult gastroenterology, hereditary cancer, and colorectal surgery teams who will assume care [5]. This is particularly important in FAP, JPS, and PJS, where missed surveillance during young adulthood may coincide with increasing adenoma burden, rising cancer risk, or loss to follow-up after pediatric-centered care ends [5, 6]. Practical elements of transition should include a written care summary documenting genotype, cumulative polyp burden, prior surgical interventions, residual anatomy, and current surveillance intervals, as well as early identification of adult providers with expertise in hereditary gastrointestinal cancer syndromes. Re-engagement with genetic counseling during transition may also help address inheritance, reproductive considerations, family cascade testing, and evolving genotype–phenotype knowledge.

Current Controversies and Knowledge Gaps

Practice Variation

Despite multiple guidelines, real-world care remains inconsistent. Kurowski and colleagues demonstrated broad variation in surveillance practices, colectomy referral thresholds, and guideline use among pediatric gastroenterologists and surgeons [11]. This heterogeneity suggests that publication of guidelines alone has not achieved standardization and supports the need for pediatric-specific protocols, care pathways, and multidisciplinary decision-support tools [12].

Areas Requiring Further Research

Several areas remain incompletely defined. These include the optimal surveillance strategy for mutation-negative JPS, the efficacy and safety of chemoprevention in children with FAP, long-term functional and oncologic outcomes of different pediatric surgical approaches, genotype–phenotype correlations for rare variants, and best practices for transition of care [5, 17, 23, 45]. Pediatric data remain limited for many HPS, and many recommendations are extrapolated from adult cohorts or based primarily on expert consensus rather than high-quality prospective pediatric studies. Consequently, the strength of evidence supporting individual recommendations varies across syndromes and guideline frameworks, particularly regarding surveillance intervals, management of genotype-negative disease, and optimal timing of intervention.

Limitations of This Review

This review has several limitations. First, it is a narrative review rather than a systematic review; although major contemporary guideline frameworks and key publications were intentionally selected, a formal systematic search strategy, predefined study selection process, and formal appraisal of guideline quality were not performed. Second, recommendations were synthesized qualitatively without formal assessment of the strength or certainty of evidence supporting individual guideline statements. Finally, the pediatric evidence base remains limited for many HPS, with numerous recommendations derived from observational studies, adult cohorts, or expert consensus. These limitations should be considered when interpreting areas of guideline concordance and variability.

Future Directions

Emerging Therapies

Future research priorities in pediatric HPS include development of prospective pediatric natural-history cohorts, improved genotype–phenotype risk stratification, identification of biomarkers that predict aggressive disease, and evaluation of how advanced endoscopic strategies may delay or reduce the need for surgery. Additional priorities include better characterization of genotype-negative polyposis, safer and more effective chemoprevention strategies for APC-associated disease, and studies addressing quality of life, adherence, and transition outcomes in adolescents and young adults with lifelong surveillance needs [2, 7, 45]. Further work is needed to define optimal multidisciplinary care models and evaluate comparative effectiveness of endoscopic and surgical management strategies. As precision medicine evolves, integration of broader germline testing, variant reinterpretation, and syndrome-specific risk modeling may further personalize management, but pediatric evidence remains limited and should be prioritized in future multicenter collaborative studies [8, 41].

Practical Guidelines and Recommendations

Summary Points for Clinical Practice

For pediatric clinicians, several practical principles emerge from current evidence. Children with multiple adenomas, multiple juvenile polyps, suggestive extracolonic findings, or a concerning family history should undergo genetics evaluation and syndrome-directed surveillance. Multigene panel testing is generally preferred. FAP surveillance typically begins at ages 10–14 years, JPS at 12–15 years, and PJS small bowel and endoscopic surveillance around age 8, with earlier evaluation for symptoms (Table 1). Management should be individualized by phenotype, genotype, and endoscopic burden, and care should ideally occur within multidisciplinary centers [4, 7, 8, 13, 14].

Clinical Algorithms and Tables

The tables provided synthesize surveillance recommendations across major guidelines and consolidate key clinical features of both common and less frequently encountered syndromes. By aligning age of initiation, surveillance intervals, and syndrome-specific considerations in a single format, they are intended to support rapid clinical reference and highlight areas of concordance and variability across existing frameworks, which may contribute to differences in clinical practice. In addition, the inclusion of emerging and rare syndromes aims to address gaps not consistently covered in current guidelines and to facilitate practical decision-making in pediatric clinical settings.

Conclusion

Pediatric HPS require individualized, phenotype-driven management that accounts for developmental stage, procedural burden, and lifelong cancer risk. While recent guidelines provide a valuable framework, important gaps remain in pediatric-specific evidence and real-world implementation. This review synthesizes current society recommendations to provide a practical pediatric-focused reference; however, whether integrated guideline summaries or consolidated tables improve guideline implementation, consistency of care, or patient outcomes has not been formally evaluated. Future studies should evaluate whether standardized care pathways, expanded access to specialized endoscopic expertise, and multidisciplinary management improve the consistency and quality of care across diverse healthcare settings.
