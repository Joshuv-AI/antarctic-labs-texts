Prospective study to evaluate the effectiveness of guideline-based therapy for chronic cough and refractory chronic cough: PROCOUGH
Authors: Mustafaa Wahab, Elena Kum, Nermin Diab, Danica Brister, Ana Oliveira, Wafa Hassan, Sue Beaudin, Catie Stevens, Jennifer Wattie, Lesley Wiltshire, Karen Howie, Kieran Killian, Kimberley Holt, Gordon Guyatt, Roma Sehmi, Gail M. Gauvreau, Jaclyn A. Smith, Paul M. O'Byrne, Imran Satia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimChronic cough is a common and burdensome condition with limited licensed therapies. Current guidelines recommend identifying treatable traits and using off-label therapies based largely on small clinical trials, but the real-world effectiveness of this approach remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of guideline-based therapies on objective and patient-reported outcomes in patients with chronic cough attending a tertiary cough clinic.MethodsPROCOUGH was a prospective, single-centre, observational cohort study conducted at McMaster University Medical Centre (December 2021 to May 2025). Adults with chronic cough (>8 weeks) underwent comprehensive assessment, including lung function testing, airway inflammation measures, sputum cytology and 24-h cough monitoring (VitaloJAK™), alongside patient-reported outcomes. Patients were treated according to European Respiratory Society guideline recommendations, with all assessments repeated after a treatment trial. The primary outcome was change in 24-h cough frequency. Secondary outcomes included Leicester Cough Questionnaire (LCQ) and Cough severity visual analogue scale (CS-VAS). Responder analyses were based on ≥30% cough frequency reduction, ≥30 mm CS-VAS improvement and ≥1.3-point LCQ increase.Results100 patients completed follow-up (mean±sd age 58.1±14.0 years; 58% female; median cough duration 7 years). Treatment resulted in a 54% reduction in 24-h cough frequency (95% CI 42.0–62.9%), a 20.2-mm decrease in CS-VAS (95% CI −26.2– −14.2) and a 3.4-point increase in LCQ (95% CI 2.6–4.2). Individually, 56%, 36% and 64% met cough frequency, CS-VAS and LCQ responder criteria, respectively, but only 14% met all three. Despite treatment, 71% reported insufficient cough control.ConclusionGuideline-based therapy improves objective and subjective cough outcomes; however, most patients fail to achieve satisfactory symptom control, highlighting a substantial unmet need for effective treatments for refractory unexplained chronic cough.

Introduction

Chronic cough, defined as a cough persisting for more than 8 weeks is a common and troublesome symptom, with an estimated global prevalence of approximately 10%; regional variations range between 2–18% [1, 2]. It is the leading cause for referral to a specialist [3]. Chronic cough has a profound impact on patients’ physical, psychological and social well-being [4, 5]. Patients suffer with embarrassing and frustrating symptoms such as sleep disturbances, urinary incontinence, syncope, anxiety and experience social withdrawal [5]. There are many drivers of chronic cough including asthma, non-asthmatic eosinophilic bronchitis (NAEB), post-viral cough, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and upper airway cough syndrome (UACS), [6, 7]. Appropriate evaluation can reveal an identifiable cause or treatable trait, which when addressed, leads to partial or complete resolution and termed explained cough, though the true proportion achieving full resolution remains unclear. However, robust evidence from randomised controlled trials for interventions targeting these conditions in improving chronic cough is still lacking. When cough persists despite treating an underlying condition, it is classified as refractory chronic cough (RCC). If no cause is identified, this is termed unexplained chronic cough (UCC) [8]. Both RCC and UCC are thought to reflect similar underlying neurophysiological mechanisms involving airway and central sensitisation. These terms are often used synonymously as refractory unexplained chronic cough (RUCC) [8–12].

International guidelines, such as those from the European Respiratory Society (ERS) [13], the American College of Chest Physicians [14] and the British Thoracic Society [15] recommend that clinicians first identify and treat conditions or “treatable traits” before classifying chronic cough as RUCC. If targeting of treatable traits is insufficient to improve cough or quality of life, these guidelines recommend centrally acting neuromodulators (e.g. opioids, pregabalin, gabapentin and amitriptyline) and nonpharmacological cough control therapy. These pharmacological agents are not licensed for chronic cough and are prescribed off-label; they carry potential risks of adverse effects such as constipation, sedation, unsteadiness and dizziness and in the case of opioids, the potential for misuse and addiction [16–20].

Few prospective studies have reported the burden and prevalence of RUCC in secondary or tertiary care. Previous estimates of RUCC, largely derived from retrospective database analyses, vary widely [2, 5, 21]. These discrepancies are likely due to differences in clinician bias, diagnostic definitions, referral patterns and the availability of specialised testing. Furthermore, most assessments of treatment response have relied on unvalidated or subjective tools, limiting their interpretability. No prospective studies have comprehensively evaluated the effectiveness of guideline-based treatments for chronic cough or RUCC on objective cough frequency.

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of current guideline-based therapies on changes in objective 24-h cough frequency and patient-reported outcomes after undergoing standardised investigation protocols, predefined diagnostic criteria and a guideline-based treatment algorithm. The secondary objective was to explore responder group analyses and investigate the proportion of patients who had a sufficient treatment response warranting no additional treatment.

Methods

Study design

The PROCOUGH study was a prospective, single-centre, investigator-initiated observational cohort study conducted at McMaster University Medical Centre between December 2021 and May 2025. Recruitment took longer than anticipated due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic during the first year of the study, and limited laboratory capacity that constrained enrolment. During this period, our clinical research facility was closed to new patient recruitment and clinics were conducted predominantly virtually, substantially reducing our ability to enrol participants. The study consisted of four visits evaluating adult patients referred for chronic cough. Comprehensive assessments were completed at baseline and post-treatment follow-up between 2–8 weeks later (figure 1). The protocol received approval from the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (no. 11231) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. All participants provided written informed consent.

Study design overview. LCQ: Leicester Cough Questionnaire; CS-VAS: cough severity visual analogue scale; BDR: bronchodilator reversibility; FENO: fractional exhaled nitric oxide; ICS: inhaled corticosteroid; LABA: long-acting β-agonist; PPI: proton-pump inhibitor.

Study participants

Individuals were eligible if they were adults (aged 18–80 years) referred to a cough specialist primarily for their chronic cough, defined as a cough lasting more than 8 weeks, presented a normal chest radiograph and had no evidence of airflow obstruction (forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC)≥0.7). Exclusion criteria included current or recent (within 6 months) smoking with a ≥20 pack-year history, active respiratory infection or asthma exacerbation within the past month and significant underlying pulmonary diseases such as interstitial lung disease, bronchiectasis, emphysema or severe uncontrolled asthma. Patients using centrally acting medications (e.g. amitriptyline, pregabalin, gabapentin or opioids) within 2 weeks of screening were excluded unless these could be safely discontinued prior to visit 1. Full details of the inclusion and exclusion criteria are available in the supplementary material.

Study assessments

At visit 1, after consent, participants underwent a detailed medical evaluation, including full medical history and examination, followed by baseline assessments: spirometry with bronchodilator reversibility, allergen skin prick testing to common aero allergens (house dust mite, cat, dog, horse, feather, mould, tree mix, grass and ragweed), Leicester Cough Questionnaire (LCQ), Cough Severity Visual Analogue Scale (CS-VAS) and 24-h cough monitoring using the VitaloJAK™ cough monitor.

At visit 2, 24 h later, participants returned for blood sampling for serum IgE and complete blood cell count, fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FENO) testing (NIOX), methacholine challenge test (2-min tidal breathing method up to 16 mg·mL−1 using the Wright nebuliser) and sputum induction for differential cell count. Physicians used results from clinical investigations obtained at visits 1 and 2 to make informed, shared decisions with patients at the end of visit 2 to deliver targeted treatment.

Following completion of treatment, participants returned for visit 3 to repeat questionnaires (LCQ and CS-VAS) and 24-h cough monitoring to evaluate treatment response. Visit 4 occurred on the following day and mirrored the physiological assessments of visit 2, including FENO measurement, spirometry, blood sampling, sputum induction and processing for differential cell count.

Comprehensive details of the study procedures are available in the supplementary material.

Clinical treatment pathways

A study specific treatment algorithm was developed based on the current ERS clinical practice guidelines for chronic cough and RCC [13] but also included methacholine challenge, FENO and sputum differential cell count (supplementary figure S1). Patients with an identifiable underlying cause (e.g. asthma, NAEB, GERD or UACS) received targeted therapy initiated at visit 2. Treatment pathways were individualised based on diagnostic findings. Treatment options include inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) with or without a long-acting β-2 agonist (LABA), proton-pump inhibitor (PPI), intranasal corticosteroids (INCS) or azithromycin.

If patients had already trialled guideline-based therapies prior to study entry in clinical practice, but the cough had persisted, or all investigations were normal, then patients were labelled as “RUCC” and were offered either neuromodulator therapy; morphine 5 mg twice daily for 2 weeks, pregabalin up to 150 mg twice daily, gabapentin up to 300 mg three times daily or amitriptyline up to 25 mg nightly or virtual cough control therapy (CCT). CCT was delivered virtually by a trained physiotherapist and speech-language pathologist over five individualised 45–60-min sessions.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was change in 24-h cough frequency (coughs per hour; coughs·h−1) measured using the VitaloJAK cough monitor at baseline and after completion of treatment. Secondary outcomes included changes in quality of life as measure by the LCQ and cough severity measured by the CS-VAS on a 0–100 mm visual analogue scale. The “responder” criterion threshold was based on current estimates of minimally important differences (MID) of ≥30% change in 24-h cough frequency [22] and a difference of ≥30 mm on the CS-VAS [23] and ≥1.3 on the LCQ [24]. At the end of the treatment period, patients also answered a cough-control question assessing subjective clinical improvement: “Is your cough sufficiently better that you feel no further treatment is needed?” Response options were dichotomised as “yes/probably yes” or “no/probably no”.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and baseline data were presented as mean±sd or as median and interquartile range (IQR). Normality assumptions for all continuous outcomes were evaluated using Shapiro–Wilk testing and Q–Q plot inspection. Descriptive analyses revealed that cough frequency data were non-normally distributed, as commonly observed in chronic cough studies [25, 26]. Therefore, all cough frequency end-points were log-transformed, with geometric mean and log sd calculated and shown as % change from baseline. All patients who completed at least one post-treatment assessment were included in a modified intention-to-treat (mITT) analysis. We performed a complete case analysis, including participants with both baseline and follow-up assessments. Subgroup analysis of the effects of individual treatments was also performed. The individual treatments were grouped as ICS/LABA, gabapentinoids (pregabalin or gabapentin), morphine, CCT, “other” and “observation”. Two patients on amitriptyline were included in the gabapentinoids group. Four patients were classified in the “other” group: two received azithromycin, one received a PPI and one received INCS. Although treatment was offered to all patients, five elected to forego treatments and were managed by observation alone. Adverse events were monitored for the duration of the study. Independent t-tests were used to compare baseline demographic characteristics between groups where normality assumptions were met. Within-subject treatment effects, including subgroup analyses, were assessed using paired t-tests for normally distributed data. For outcomes that were non-normally distributed, appropriate transformations (e.g. log10 transformation for cough frequency) were applied prior to parametric testing, or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used where normality could not be achieved. A two-sided p-value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

No formal sample size calculation was performed prior to study initiation, as this study was designed as a pragmatic, real-world evaluation of guideline-based therapy across multiple treatable traits and estimates of within-patient variability in objective cough frequency were not available at the time for this population. Recruitment was therefore guided by feasibility considerations. Post hoc analysis demonstrated that the sd of the change in log10 24-h cough frequency was 0.48 and the sd at visit 1 was 0.43 and post-treatment was 0.57. Based on this observed variability, a sample size of 100 patients provides approximately 90% power to detect a clinically meaningful 30% reduction in cough frequency using a paired two-sided test at the 0.05 significance level. We investigated the proportion of patients who had a response based on MID thresholds of LCQ, CS-VAS, cough frequency and the proportion who met all three MID thresholds. Data were analysed using SPSS v.28.0 (IBM Corp., NY, USA).

Results

Participants

A total of 108 participants were enrolled in the study. Two participants were excluded due to screen failures, six participants were lost to follow-up (three receiving ICS+LABA, two receiving gabapentinoids and one managed by observation), resulting in 100 participants who returned for follow-up and completed the study (supplementary figure S2). Six participants had missing cough data, resulting in cough frequency analyses being performed on 94 participants with complete pre- and post-treatment measurements. The distribution of treatable traits/conditions within the cohort was as follows: asthma (n=31), NAEB (n=16), neutrophilic bronchitis (n=2), GERD (n=1), chronic rhinosinusitis (n=1) and cough hypersensitivity (n=49). Of the 100 participants included in the analysis, 58% were female with a mean age of 58.1±14.0 years and 63% had never smoked. The median cough duration was 7 years (IQR 3–15).

Baseline demographic and cough characteristics were comparable across treatment groups (table 1). Clinicians chose initial treatment based on treatable traits identified in visit 1 and 2, which is reflected in differences in baseline characteristics by treatment selected. FENO was the highest at 28.0 ppb (IQR 16.0–53.0) in patients treated with ICS+LABA, including the greatest proportion with a positive methacholine challenge and lower provocative dose that causes 20% fall. Sputum eosinophil % also differed across treatments (p=0.038). Other parameters, including BMI, smoking status and baseline cough measures, did not differ between groups.

Changes in objective and subjective cough outcomes

After treatment, across the cohort, 24-h cough frequency improved by 53.6% (95% CI 42.0–62.9%), p<0.0001. Patient-reported CS-VAS improved after treatment by 19.5 mm (95% CI −26.2– −14.2), p<0.0001. LCQ improved by 3.4 (95% CI 2.6–4.2), p<0.0001 (figure 2).

Effects of individual treatments on cough outcomes

Subgroup analyses by treatment type demonstrated within-group improvements across all three outcomes (figure 3). For the patients who received them, ICS-LABA, gabapentinoids, morphine, CCT and “other” (azithromycin, PPIs or INCS) all resulted in significant decrease in 24-h cough frequency. Reductions in 24-h cough frequency ranged from 15% to 72% (figure 3a). Corresponding improvements in cough-specific quality of life were observed, with mean LCQ increases ranging from 2.7 to 6.1 points (figure 3b), and changes in CS-VAS ranging from −47.7 mm to −3.2 mm (figure 3c). When comparing across treatment groups, no significant differences were detected in 24-h cough frequency (p=0.82). However, statistically significant differences were observed for LCQ scores (p=0.028) and CS-VAS (p<0.001), indicating heterogeneity in patient-reported outcomes between treatment modalities.

Responder analyses

The proportion of patients that met each responder group criteria are shown in figure 4. Across the three responder definitions, baseline characteristics were similar between responders and nonresponders (supplementary table S1). The proportion of patients meeting each criterion varied; using a ≥30% reduction in 24-h cough frequency, 56% met the responder criterion. In contrast, 36% achieved a ≥30-mm improvement in CS-VAS, and these responders had higher baseline VAS (p=0.002) and lower LCQ scores (p=0.016) than nonresponders. For the LCQ responder definition (≥1.3-point increase), 64% met the criterion, again demonstrating lower baseline LCQ (p=0.018) and higher baseline VAS scores (p=0.287) than nonresponders. When all three responder thresholds were combined into a composite outcome, only 14% met criteria. This group was older (p=0.048), had longer cough duration (p=0.025), lower FEV1 % predicted (p=0.015), higher % bronchial epithelial cell (p<0.001), lower baseline LCQ scores (p=0.028), higher baseline VAS scores (p=0.073) and higher baseline cough frequency than nonresponders (24-h; p=0.029, awake; p=0.026, night-time; p=0.043).

Patient-reported cough control

Overall, 29% of participants reported that their cough was sufficiently controlled, whereas 71% indicated that their cough remained inadequately controlled to warrant further treatment. The proportion of participants who reported cough control differed across treatment groups: ICS+LABA (11 of 41 patients; 26.8%), gabapentinoids (8 of 21 patients; 38.1%), morphine (5 of 18 patients; 27.8%), CCT (3 of 11 patients; 27.3%), other treatments (2 of 4 patients; 50%) and observation (0 of 5 patients), where no patients reported cough control.

Adverse events

There were no reported serious adverse events (AEs). Overall, 31% of patients reported ≥1 AE during treatment. No discontinuations were reported due to AEs. Total AE rates differed across treatment groups, with the highest proportions observed among those receiving gabapentinoids (47.6%) and opioids (39%), followed by ICS+LABA (27.5%), observation (20%) and CCT (18%) (supplementary table S2). Treatment-related AEs were reported in multiple groups. In the ICS+LABA group, the most common events were headaches and/or migraines (10%) and throat irritation (8%). In the neuromodulator group, reported events included fatigue (24%), anxiety (14%), amnesia (14%), dizziness (10%) and weight gain (10%). In the opioid group, constipation (28%), fatigue (11%) and headache (6%) were observed.

Discussion

In this prospective observational study, we demonstrated that guideline-based treatments of chronic cough were associated with improvements in both objective and subjective outcomes, including 24-h cough frequency, cough-specific quality of life (LCQ) and cough severity (CS-VAS). To our knowledge, this is the first study in a specialised secondary care setting to show the impact of guideline-recommended treatments using validated outcome measures. However, although each outcome showed measurable improvement, these treatments led to sufficient control of cough in 29% only. Although responder analyses revealed that the proportion of patients meeting each criterion varied substantially, only 14% met all three MID criteria, and hence highlighting the poor alignment of these criteria within this patient group and clinical setting. Nevertheless, these data do reinforce the value of a systematic treatable traits-based approach to guide individualised therapy, but also highlight the need for the development of more effective therapies for cough.

We acknowledge this study was conducted in a clinical research facility with investigations that may not be available in all centres or tests which are routinely recommended by ERS clinical practice guidelines for all participants with chronic cough. We recognise that some of these investigations may be more resource-intensive in routine practice or costly, such as sputum induction and processing, methacholine challenge and FENO. We used sputum induction to diagnose NAEB, as reliance on blood eosinophils alone maybe inadequate to reflect airway inflammation. Our results underscore the value of measuring inflammatory biomarkers such as sputum induction and FENO in distinguishing patients whose cough is driven by airway inflammation from those more likely to benefit from therapies targeting cough hypersensitivity syndrome. Hence, a major benefit of these assessments of inflammation and bronchial hyperresponsiveness, is identifying a negative or normal tests to rule out underlying conditions which may require inhaled steroid with or without bronchodilators, and the potential for using centrally acting medications to suppress coughing. Using this approach, approximately 45% of patients were identified as having a treatable trait, while the remaining 55% were diagnosed with RUCC and subsequently managed with therapies targeting cough hypersensitivity.

Although 24-h cough monitoring is currently not available in routine clinical practice, it is valuable and feasible to complete simple subjective cough patient-reported outcomes in clinic, as patients with more severe symptoms on the CS-VAS and LCQ may have the greatest response. Furthermore, the CS-VAS or a 0–10 numerical rating scale remains a quick, simple and highly practical tool for clinical use, with ease of administration and high patient acceptability, making it valuable for routine monitoring and suitable to use alongside more comprehensive assessments.

Completing these assessments raises an important question: what measure of response threshold should we be using to evaluate if treatment has been effective in these patients? In chronic cough literature, the following definitions of MID have been used: a ≥30% reduction in 24-h cough frequency [22], a ≥30-mm improvement on the VAS [23] or a ≥1.3-point increase in the LCQ total score [24], but not all three have been used together as a composite. These MIDs were largely derived from randomised controlled trials in RUCC and may have limited generalisability across broader chronic cough populations. Moreover, the LCQ MID itself has been increasingly debated, with recent studies proposing higher thresholds such as 2.3 points [27, 28] and even 3.3–4.1 points in post hoc analyses [29]. Furthermore, the CS-VAS MID remains controversial, with some studies suggesting that the MID may be as low as 17 mm [28]. The definition of the MID is “the smallest difference in score in the outcome of interest that informed patients or informed proxies perceive as important, either beneficial or harmful, and which would lead the patient or clinician to consider a change in the management” [30] and so it is reasonable to question whether the commonly used thresholds in chronic cough truly meet this definition, particularly the requirement that they meaningfully influence treatment decisions. In other words, do these MIDs genuinely lead clinicians to alter management or are they simply statistical benchmarks that may not translate into real-world clinical change? Importantly, these tools were developed for use in clinical trials and have not been tested or validated to guide routine clinical care. Each MID reflects only a single aspect of treatment benefit, objective cough frequency, perceptual severity or cough-specific quality of life and therefore may not fully capture a patient's overall improvement. So, in our study we combined the three most commonly used thresholds into a single composite “objective and subjective responder” definition, requiring patients to meet all three criteria. Using this approach, we found that although 56%, 36% and 64% of patients met the cough frequency, VAS and LCQ responder definitions individually, only 14% satisfied all three simultaneously, underscoring how response rates vary depending on the metric used and highlighting the need for an integrated approach that incorporates both objective and patient-reported outcomes. In our study, we also examined the proportion of patients who perceived their cough to be well controlled following treatment. Notably, only 29% of participants reported adequate cough control. This highlights an important distinction: improvement according to responder criteria does not mean patient-perceived control of chronic cough. These findings suggest the need to consider “controlled cough” as a complementary clinical construct, analogous to the responder concept but capturing a different dimension of therapeutic benefit. More research is required to anchor each of the subjective and objective outcomes on to patient-reported global rating of change to also understand how meaningful an objective and subjective improvements are perceived by patients.

Overall, AEs were reported in 31% of participants. Although treatment-related AEs are commonly observed with opioid and neuromodulator therapies, the incidence in our study was relatively low compared with previous studies [16, 20, 31]. This likely reflects the short treatment duration and limited follow-up period, which may have reduced the likelihood of capturing delayed or cumulative adverse effects. Consequently, the long-term safety and tolerability of these therapies cannot be inferred from our findings. Larger studies with extended follow-up are needed to more accurately evaluate the true adverse event profile associated with these treatment options.

This study has limitations. First, it was conducted at a single centre, which may limit the generalisability of our findings to other healthcare settings or patient populations. In addition, as participants were recruited from specialist clinics, the cohort likely represents patients with more severe or treatment-resistant cough, introducing potential selection bias. While objective cough frequency monitoring is not routinely accessible in all clinical settings, its use in specialist centres provides a complimentary and feasible end-point to measure; future multicentre or registry-based studies would help determine whether these findings extend to broader clinic environments. Furthermore, there are efforts being made to develop accurate fully automated cough monitoring systems which have sufficient accuracy to be used in clinical practice, so the concept of objective cough monitoring being used in the clinic should not completely disregarded. Second, the treatments provided were heterogeneous and tailored to individual phenotypes or treatable traits, and the study was not powered to draw conclusions about the efficacy of specific interventions. Third, this was as an open-label observational study without a placebo arm. The observed 54% reduction in 24-h cough frequency is similar to the placebo responses up to a 57% reduction reported in double-blind randomised controls trials of RCC [25]. Without blinding, improvements may partly reflect contextual factors like regression to the mean, day-to-day variability [32], the Hawthorne effect [33] and the placebo effect [34], which is the specific neurobiological effect due to expectation and hope of therapeutic benefit, and the true treatment effect [35, 36]. Fourth, our results reflect outcomes after a single round of treatment; however, in clinical practice, patients often require multiple sequential treatment trials over 3–6 months when initial therapy fails. Conversely, early responders may not remain stable over time simply because they improved initially. This highlights the need to evaluate the natural history and long-term trajectory of chronic cough, including treatment durability and patterns of relapse or response loss. Fifth, the absence of long-term follow-up prevents assessment of the durability and safety of neuromodulator or other treatment effects over time.

Conclusion

This study highlights the effectiveness of guideline-based therapies on chronic cough and RCC and demonstrates that a treatable traits driven approach improved cough frequency, severity and quality of life, yet full symptom control was achieved in only 29% of patients. The discrepancies across responder definitions and their weak alignment with patient-perceived control underscore the limitations of single MIDs and the need for multidimensional outcome measures. Moreover, effective treatment options remain a large unmet need for patients with persistent RUCC.
