Extraction of Phenolic Compounds with the Highly Efficient Novel Coextractants Cyclohexanone and Methyl Isobutyl Ketone
Authors: Gary Q. Yang, Weibin Cai, Tiankun Liu, Yujun Wang, Liangxia Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Extractant selection is the most important aspect for the extraction treatment of phenolic wastewater from the coal chemical industry. Highly efficient extractants are urgently needed. To our knowledge, we identified for the first time one set of coextractants, cyclohexanone and methyl isobutyl ketone (MIBK), after spending plenty of time and energy for the extraction of phenolic compounds, including polyhydroxyphenol, from wastewater. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy of the solvents before and after extraction shows that intermolecular hydrogen bonding between the molecules of the extractants and phenols is established, contributing to the high performance. The effects of pH, temperature, and salt concentration on the extractant performance were then studied. The extraction favors acidic and neutral pH values. Increasing the temperature has a negative influence on the extraction. The salt effect by putting inorganic salt can obviously enhance the extractant performance. Compared with single extractants such as MIBK and diisopropyl ether (DIPE), coextractants have significantly improved recovery rates of resorcinol and phloroglucinol. To learn if this set of coextractants also has good performance in practical extraction processes for the treatment of industrial wastewater, three-stage countercurrent extraction experiments were carried out with these coextractants for the extraction of phenolic compounds from coal chemical wastewater. Cascade experiments were used to simulate and verify the countercurrent extraction process in actual industry. According to the results, it is observed that this set of coextractants also has good performance in practical extraction processes for the treatment of industrial wastewater.

Introduction

Phenols are oxygen-containing derivatives of aromatic hydrocarbons. The weakly acidic organic components of phenols are highly toxic and difficult to degrade. In the petrochemical industry, dyestuff industry, organic pesticide industry, and especially the coal chemical industry, phenolic wastewater is produced in large quantities. Direct discharge of wastewater will cause serious environmental problems and is unacceptable. Many countries, including China, have identified phenolic wastewater as a priority pollutant and have conducted in-depth research on its treatment. −

The solvent extraction method is extensively used in the treatment of high-concentration phenolic wastewater. The process of treating coal chemical industry wastewater involves a series of steps, including the evaporation of ammonia, the removal of carbon dioxide, and the extraction of phenols, followed by biochemical treatment. − People have even developed novel materials, such as low-cost fibers and adjustable hyper-cross-linked poly­(ionic liquid), for the treatment of such wastewater. In order to reduce the toxicity of the wastewater to activated sludge and enhance the effects of the biochemical treatment, the concentration of phenols, including volatile and nonvolatile ones, after the phenol extraction step should be less than 500 mg/L. However, currently most extraction processes obtain treated water with a concentration of phenols above 500 mg/L. In the extraction process, the selection of extractants is of paramount importance, as its performance determines the energy and material consumption of the extraction process. ,, Researchers have conducted extensive research on various organics, including hydrocarbons (benzene, toluene, cyclohexane), − alcohols (octanol, 2-butanol, tert-butanol), − ethers (diisopropyl ether (DIPE), tert-amyl methyl ether (TAME)), , esters (isoamyl acetate, dimethyl carbonate), , ketones (methyl isobutyl ketone (MIBK), methyl n-butyl ketone (MBK), mesityl oxide) − and complexes (tributyl phosphate (TBP), tri­octyl­phos­phine oxide (TOPO)). − Among them, MIBK and DIPE are the two most frequently employed extractants to extract phenol in industry; however, their efficacy in extracting polyhydroxyphenols needs further improvements. In the course of our research, we have identified cyclohexanone as a highly efficient extractant capable of extracting phenolics (including polyhydroxyphenols) from wastewater. ,

Due to the high mutual solubility between the two extractants of a coextractant system, their solubility in water is significantly reduced, beneficial to the wastewater treatment process. Some of the extractants will be dissolved in water, which should be recovered later with additional processes. Therefore, their solubility in water should not be large to reduce recovery costs. As a result, people use coextractants containing more than one extractant to improve the extraction performance. ,− Extraction systems of coextractants are widely used. For example, Ruan et al. performed experimental and microscopic analyses for the separation of isopropanol–water using a system of coextractants, hydrophobic deep eutectic solvents, and obtained good results. In general, ketones can form good coextractants with alcohols, but alcohols with a low molecular weight have a large solubility in water, and those with a high molecular weight usually have a stimulating odor, limiting the industrial application of such coextractants.

Cyclohexanone has a higher diffusion coefficient than DIPE and MIBK for the extraction of phenol. However, the difference in the density of cyclohexanone and water is too small, and its solubility in water is too large, limiting its industrial application. Higher density differences facilitate the operation of the extraction tower and lead to better extraction performance. A suitable component that can form a system of coextractants with cyclohexanone should at least have high mutual solubility between it and cyclohexanone and low solubility in water.

In this study, based on our previous work, we first identified one set of coextractants, cyclohexanone & MIBK, that is highly efficient for the extraction of phenolic compounds from wastewater, after spending plenty of time and energy. The molecular-level mechanisms leading to the high performance of the coextractants were explored. Herein, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy and electrostatic potential (ESP) analysis , were used to study the reasons leading to the high performance. The influences of the extractant component volume ratio, temperature, pH value, and salt concentration on the performance of the coextractants were investigated. It is necessary to study whether this set of coextractants is also highly efficient in practical extraction processes to handle industrial phenolic wastewater. As a result, countercurrent extraction experiments were carried out for the extraction of phenolic compounds in coal chemical wastewater.

Simulation and Experimental Methods

The COSMO-RS Method

COSMO-RS is the abbreviation for “conductor-like screening model for realistic solvents”, and the COSMO-RS method is a statistical thermodynamic method for calculating thermodynamic properties of (mixed) fluids based on quantum chemical calculations. − The COSMO-RS method can be used to perform electrostatic potential (ESP) analysis to evaluate the molecular interaction between coextractants and phenolic compounds. We implemented the COSMO-RS method with COSMOthermX version 18.0, using the Becke–Perdew (BP) functional for density functional theory calculations with a triple valence plus polarization function (TZVP) basis set (BP_TZVP_18 parametrization). The molecular structures of cyclohexanone, MIBK, DIPE, phenol, resorcinol, and phloroglucinol were created with the structure editor of the simulation software. Then, quantum chemistry calculations using density functional theory (DFT) were done based on the created structures to compute the charge densities of the atoms of the molecules, from which the screening charge density (SCD) distribution was obtained to calculate the distribution profile of σ (SCD), p(σ).

Experimental Methods

Table shows the suppliers, mass fraction purities, and CAS registry numbers of the chemicals used. All extraction experiments were carried out in a conical flask of 100 mL. Unless otherwise noted, the volume ratio of organic phase and aqueous phase was 1:2. (It is observed according to experimental results that the inflection point of the curve is 0.5, so it is taken as the volume ratio of the organic phase to the aqueous phase. Using this ratio, not only was a high extraction efficiency maintained, but a low operation cost was also achieved.) The experiments were done at 30 °C. The two phases were stirred at a speed of 200 rpm for 120 min in a bath at a constant temperature. Then, the mixture stood still for at least 12 h. The lower aqueous phase was taken with a disposable syringe, and the concentration of the phenols was measured. The pH value of the solution was determined by a pH meter (Mettler-Toledo FiveEasy Plus).

The concentration of phenol in the aqueous phase was measured with a UV spectrophotometer (LabTech UV9100A) by the 4-aminoantipyrine spectrophotometric method (510 nm). A 1 mL portion of phenol was added in a breaker. Water was added to it so that the concentration of phenol was between 0.1 and 5 mg/L. This range would make the test results more accurate. The NH3/NH4Cl buffer solution was added to it, and the pH of the system was adjusted to about 10. Using a 510 nm wavelength light, the absorbance will be proportional to the concentration of phenol, so the concentration of phenol can be measured based on the absorbance with the aid of the standard curve.

The concentrations of resorcinol and phloroglucinol were analyzed by a high performance liquid chromatograph (HPLC, Shimadzu LC-20A), equipped with an Agilent ZORBAX Eclipse XDB-C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm). Table shows the chromatographic conditions of the HPLC. The bromine method was used to determine the concentrations of total phenols (including phenol, resorcinol, and phloroglucinol) and volatile phenols (only phenol) of coal chemical wastewater. The concentrations of volatile phenols and total phenols were all determined by the bromination method (EPA320.1). In this method, an excessive brominating agent is added to the system, and it reacts with phenols; then, the unreacted brominating agent is determined by the iodometric method (EPA345.1). The concentrations of phenols can be calculated. When volatile phenols are determined, the sample needs to be distilled with the method described in EPA420.1, and then the content of phenol in the distillate is determined. In contrast, when nonvolatile phenols are determined, the samples need not be distilled so that, in addition to volatile phenols, the nonvolatile phenols can also react with bromine. The COD value of the wastewater was determined by the potassium dichromate oxidation method (EPA410.4).

Results and Discussion

The performance of the coextractant is mainly determined by its components. Therefore, the choice of extractant components has special significance.

Figure shows the influence of the coextractant component volume ratio (V 1/V 2) on the phenol distribution coefficient for the two sets of coextractants: cyclohexanone & DIPE and cyclohexanone & MIBK. With the increase of the volume ratio of cyclohexanone in the coextractants, the distribution coefficients for both sets of coextractants increase because cyclohexanone shows stronger interactions with phenols than MIBK and DIPE according to the ESP analysis with the COSMO-RS method. When the volume fraction of cyclohexanone is 0.1, the distribution coefficients for the two sets of coextractants cyclohexanone & DIPE and cyclohexanone & MIBK are 37.4 and 81.5, respectively. When the volume fraction of cyclohexanone is 0.3, the distribution coefficients for the two sets of coextractants increase to 51.9 and 91.4, respectively. Compared to the single extractants DIPE and MIBK, the performance of the coextractants is better even when the coextractants only contains small fractions of cyclohexanone. According to the results above, the cyclohexanone & MIBK set of coextractants has much better performance than the other one. The optimum volume fraction of cyclohexanone is 0.1.

In the practical application of extraction, it is very important to select the appropriate volume ratio between the oil phase and the aqueous phase to improve the extraction efficiency and lower the operation cost. Figure showed the influence of the phase volume ratio between the oil phase and the aqueous phase on the phenol extraction efficiency at 30 °C. When the ratio is 1, the extraction efficiency for this set of coextractants, cyclohexanone & MIBK, is 98.7%, and the concentration of phenol in the aqueous solution after extraction decreases to 25.5 mg/L. The high performance of the added cyclohexanone caused the extraction efficiency of this coextractant to decrease slightly with decreasing phase volume ratio. As the ratio decreases to 0.2, the extraction efficiency for this set of coextractants, cyclohexanone & MIBK, decreases only slightly to 90.8%. It is observed that the inflection point of the curve is 0.5, so it is taken as the volume ratio of the organic phase to the aqueous phase. Using this ratio, not only is a high extraction efficiency maintained, but a low operation cost is also achieved.

From Figure , water has continuous peaks at the σ ranges of both −0.018 to 0.01 e/Å2 and 0.01 to 0.02 e/Å2, indicating that water can be both a hydrogen bond donor and a hydrogen bond acceptor. The strong negative charge peaks of cyclohexanone, MIBK, and DIPE at the range of 0.008 to 0.021 e/Å2 indicate that they can be hydrogen bond acceptors. The strong positive charge peak of the phenols at the range of −0.023 to −0.008 e/Å2 demonstrates their potential to be hydrogen bond donors. In Figure , the small-area peak at 0.018 e/Å2of DIPE corresponds to an oxygen atom, which has only weak potential to be a hydrogen bond acceptor. According to the peak areas, the ability of cyclohexanone to be a hydrogen bond acceptor is better than that of MIBK and DIPE. In Figure , the phenols and the extractants both have continuous peaks at −0.008 to 0.008 e/Å2, which correspond to nonpolar functional groups. The electron-rich π system of the molecules of the phenols can be an electron donor, while the π* orbit of the carbonyl group of the molecules of cyclohexanone can be an electron acceptor. π–π interactions can be established, and the cyclic structure of cyclohexanone is nearly parallel to the plane of the benzene ring, contributing to higher stability of the hydrogen bonds. Such a configuration surely leads to larger nonbonding interactions of nonpolar portions, contributing to extraction. The introduction of hydroxyl groups in their molecules weakens the nonpolarity of phloroglucinol and resorcinol, but on the contrary, their interactions with water increase.

Figure shows the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of the extractants before and after extraction. Before extraction, due to the existence of CO, coextractants cyclohexanone & MIBK exhibit the characteristic absorption peaks at 1714.3 and 1718.6 cm–1. For the coextractants cyclohexanone & MIBK, after the extraction of phenol, resorcinol, and phloroglucinol, the characteristic absorption peak shifts from 1718.6 cm–1 to 1711.8, 1701.2, and 1698.1 cm–1, respectively. The characteristic absorption peaks of CO all move to lower wavenumbers after extraction, due to the formation of the intermolecular hydrogen bond CO···HO, causing a significant change in the electron cloud density distribution around the carbonyl group. The O and C atoms of the carbonyl group of cyclohexanone have negative and positive charges, respectively, and the electrostatic interactions between two cyclohexanone molecules will therefore cause their self-association. The magnitude of such interactions is much smaller than that of the CO···HO bonding interactions, so their contribution to peak shifts is much smaller than that of the CO···HO bonding interactions. Hydrogen bonds between the coextractants and phenols improve the distribution coefficient of phenolic compounds.

The influence of pH on the distribution coefficient of phenol is shown in Figure . When the pH value of the extractant–phenol​–water solution is less than 8, it has little effect on the distribution coefficient of phenol, which is large at around 81.7 and nearly remains constant. When pH > 8, the distribution coefficient decreases dramatically. Phenol is weakly acidic. When pH < 8, phenol in water mainly exists in molecular form. In contrast, when the pH > 8, phenol molecules are ionized in aqueous solution. According to ESP analysis, the extractants can only combine with phenol in molecular form, so lower pH values see much better extraction performance of the coextractants. Wastewater usually contains ammonia, which is removed with a distillation column. After its removal, the pH value decreases from 10 to around 8.

The ionization balance is as follows: 5 PhOH↔kaPhO−+H+ The extraction equilibrium can be expressed as follows: 6 PhOH↔DPhOHorg Then, the expression that extracts the equilibrium distribution coefficient is obtained: 7 D=[PhOH]org[PhOH]+[PhO−]=Da1+10pH−pka where D a represents the distribution coefficient for the strongly acidic condition (ignoring the presence of PhO–) and k a stands for the dissociation constant of phenol (k a = 10–9.98).

When the pH value reaches 10, the number of molecular phenol and dissociated phenol in aqueous solution is close to 1:1. The calculated value of D is about 50% lower than the value for pH values lower than 8, consistent with the experimental value. With the increase of pH value, more phenol molecules dissociate to PhO–, resulting in a further decrease in the distribution coefficient of phenol. It is well-known that industrial coal gasification wastewater contains high concentrations of ammonia, which makes the pH value larger than 8 and close to 10. Therefore, it is necessary to remove ammonia before the extraction process.

Figure shows the distribution coefficient as a function of temperature. It is observed that lg­(D) increases linearly with 1000/T, indicating that the extraction of phenol is an exothermic process and elevated temperature is not favored for the extraction process. According to Figure , the value of ΔH° of eq for the coextractants cyclohexanone & MIBK is −11.25 kJ/mol, which matches the magnitude of hydrogen bond energy. It is also observed that the hydrogen bond between the extractant and phenol can be destroyed due to the increase of temperature, as the distribution coefficients for both sets of coextractants show an obvious decrease.

Temperature (T) influence on the distribution coefficient of phenol.

The residual temperature of coal gasification wastewater is generally in the range of 50–80 °C, and it is generally lowered to about 50 °C for extraction due to the low energy consumption, high phenol distribution coefficient, and low solvent volatilization loss at this temperature. People use a distillation column to remove ammonia in wastewater, and the pH value decreases from 10 to around 8. The wastewater from the distillation tower will exchange heat with the incoming water. The temperature of the wastewater will then become around 50 °C. Lowering the wastewater temperature further is not economically suitable due to high energy consumption.

Figure shows the distribution coefficient of phenol as a function of the concentration of the salt Na2SO4. As the salt concentration increases, the distribution coefficient gradually increases. Inorganic salt ions attract a part of water molecules, reducing the content of water molecules, thereby increasing the relative concentration of phenol in the aqueous solution and leading to an increase in the distribution coefficient. Furthermore, salt can also facilitate the separation of the organic and aqueous phases and shorten the liquid–liquid equilibrium time.

Influence of salt concentration on distribution coefficient.

In addition to phenol, the content of nonvolatile polyhydroxyphenols in coal gasification effluents is also quite high, most of which are binary and ternary phenols. The most effective method for removing high concentrations of phenolic compounds is the extraction method. We took resorcinol and phloroglucinol, which are more difficult to extract than other binary or ternary phenols, such as catechol and 1,2,4-trihydroxybenzene, as representatives for extraction experiments. Therefore, if this set of coextractants shows good performance for the extraction of resorcinol and phloroglucinol, it will also perform well to extract other binary or ternary phenols.

The distribution coefficient decreases dramatically with the increase in temperature from 25 to 50 °C. The increase in temperature breaks the hydrogen bonds between the polyhydroxyphenol and the coextractants. It is essential to learn whether this set of coextractants also has high performance in practical extraction processes for the treatment of industrial wastewater. Using this set of coextractants, countercurrent extraction experiments were carried out for the extraction of phenolic compounds from coal chemical wastewater. Multistage countercurrent extraction is widely used in large-scale industrial processes. In the laboratory, a cascade experiment is used to simulate and verify the countercurrent extraction process in actual industry. The stability of MIBK is good. The process to recover MIBK has already been employed successfully in industry. At 95 °C, cyclohexanone can form an azeotrope with water. From the top and bottom of the cyclohexanone recovery tower, cyclohexanone (97.7% of the total amount of cyclohexanone) and an azeotrope of cyclohexanone and water and phenols (2.3% of the total amount of cyclohexanone) were obtained, respectively. To separate the azeotrope, hypnone is added as an extractant to it, and the extractive distillation process is employed. Though an additional process is needed to recover cyclohexanone, only 2.3% of the total amount of cyclohexanone is recovered with the process. Thus, the corresponding amount of energy consumption is low, and its contribution to the total amount of energy consumption is small. In total, the energy consumption for cyclohexanone is 50% higher than that for MIBK, but the former’s performance for extracting polyhydroxyphenols is better than the latter. Thus, the coextractant system removed more phenolic compounds from the wastewater and offers people cleaner treated water, contributing more to the environment.

Conclusions

The extraction method is widely used for the treatment of phenolic wastewater from the coal chemical industry. Highly efficient extractants are urgently needed. We are the first to identify one set of highly efficient coextractants, cyclohexanone & MIBK, for the extraction of phenolic compounds from phenolic wastewater. The molecular-level mechanisms leading to the high performance of the coextractants were explored. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy of the extractants before and after extraction shows that intermolecular hydrogen bonding between extractants and phenols is established, leading to high performance of the coextractants. The influences of pH, temperature, and salt concentration on the extraction performance were then investigated. Lower temperatures have better extraction performance. The salt effect of Na2SO4 can obviously enhance the extraction performance. Compared with single extractants such as MIBK and diisopropyl ether (DIPE), coextractants have significantly improved recovery rates of resorcinol and phloroglucinol. Using this set of coextractants, countercurrent extraction experiments were performed for the extraction of phenolic compounds from coal chemical wastewater. When this set of coextractants, cyclohexanone & MIBK, was used, the concentration of total phenols in the wastewater decreased from 5915 to 281 mg/L. After spending plenty of time and energy, we have successfully identified high performance coextractants for the extraction of phenolic compounds, including polyhydroxyphenol.
