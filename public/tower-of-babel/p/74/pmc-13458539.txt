Extreme mass-independent fractionation of mercury isotopes driven by atmospheric plasma-induced chemical process
Authors: Xing Liu, Guan Wang, Yuzhe Wang, Wang Zheng, Hongtao Zheng, Pengju Xing, Zhenli Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mass-independent fractionation (MIF) of mercury (Hg) provides a powerful tracer for reconstructing Hg cycling in the Earth system. Although generally attributed to photochemistry, many natural isotope signatures remain inconsistent with the established mechanisms. Here, we present experimental evidence that atmospheric plasma-induced reduction produces odd Hg-MIF with magnitudes and patterns distinct from photochemical processes. Plasma reactions yielded far larger odd-MIF signatures than photoreduction, with Δ199Hg spanning −78‰ to +28‰, the most negative Hg-MIF across all Hg-reduction experiments. The sign of Hg-MIF reverses with solution pH, yielding strongly negative Hg-MIF at pH ≥ 3.0 but positive Hg-MIF at pH ≤ 2.0. This reversal arises from competition between plasma-generated species, where solvated electrons drive negative magnetic isotope effects (MIE) at higher pH, while hydrogen radicals produce positive MIE at low pH. These findings reveal plasma chemistry as a fundamental but previously overlooked driver of Hg isotope fractionation. Given the ubiquity of natural plasmas such as lightning and aurora, plasma processes need to be integrated into models of Hg cycling in Earth’s atmosphere and surface environments.

Introduction

Mercury (Hg) is the only toxic heavy metal that circulates globally in its elemental gaseous form, exerting widespread environmental impacts1–3. Stable Hg isotopes, particularly mass-independent fractionation (Hg-MIF, e.g., Δ199Hg)4, provide powerful tracers of Hg sources and transformation processes across Earth’s systems5–7, from modern atmospheric Hg cycling2, to ecological evolution8–10, and paleo-volcanic events11. To date, natural Hg-MIF signatures have been predominantly attributed to photochemical reactions in the atmosphere and surface environments4,12–17. However, growing evidence shows that natural isotope anomalies can not be reconciled with light-driven processes alone. For example, pronounced negative shifts in Δ199Hg observed before the evolution of terrestrial vegetation8,18 and under polar night conditions19,20, contradict photochemical expectations. Additionally, global Hg cycling models based on existing fractionation frameworks often fail to reproduce the isotopic compositions observed in natural samples2,3,5,6,21. These inconsistencies highlight the limitations of a purely photochemical perspective and emphasize the need to explore additional non-photochemical mechanisms to explain the diverse Hg-MIF signatures in natural systems.

Plasma, ubiquitous on Earth and beyond (e.g., lightning, auroras, and solar wind), represents an important yet often overlooked driver of global elemental cycling22–27. Previous studies have shown that plasma can generate bioavailable nitrogen24,26 and phosphorus25 at fluxes comparable to those of photochemistry. Moreover, plasma has been demonstrated to induce pronounced MIF in elements such as oxygen28, magnesium29, and titanium30, etc. Unlike photochemical pathways driven by photon absorption, plasma induces isotope effects through non-thermal electron impacts and symmetry-driven reactions involving energetic radicals and excited complexes31. Despite these findings, plasma-induced Hg-MIF has not been reported. Our earlier work demonstrated that reactive species produced in atmospheric plasma-liquid systems efficiently reduce Hg2+ to Hg0 via pathways distinct from photochemistry32–38. Here, we show that plasma-induced reduction, which is mediated by solvated electrons (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{e}}}}}_{({{{\rm{aq}}}})}^{-}$$\end{document}e(aq)−) and hydrogen radicals (H•(aq)) generated at the interface, produces extreme Hg-MIF signatures distinct from those produced by known photo-reduction processes. These findings identify plasma chemistry as a previously overlooked driver of Hg-MIF, offering a plausible explanation for enigmatic isotopic anomalies and providing new constraints for refining global Hg cycling models.

Results

Experimental design

To investigate Hg isotope fractionation during plasma-induced reduction, we constructed a customized atmospheric plasma reactor39 designed to generate a solution anode glow discharge (SAGD). This configuration simulates the conditions of negative cloud-to-ground lightning, which accounts for ~95% of natural discharges27. In this configuration, the Hg2+-containing solution itself acts as the anode, while a tungsten electrode serves as the cathode. A stable plasma is sustained using a high-voltage DC power supply operated at 30 mA (Fig. 1a; see Methods). This reactor permits precise control over the discharge current, solution pH, and discharge gas composition, enabling experiments under variable conditions that more closely replicate natural plasma environments. Hg isotope fractionation during plasma reduction

Our most striking finding is that plasma-induced reduction produces exceptionally large odd Hg-MIF with an isotopic signature distinct from those of photochemical pathways. In these experiments, MIF occurred exclusively in odd isotopes (199Hg and 201Hg), with no detectable MIF in even isotopes (200Hg and 204Hg; Fig. S1). The most pronounced MIF appeared in residual Hg2+ at pH 4.0, where Δ199Hg reaches -78‰ after ~90% reduction (Fig. 1b). To our knowledge, this represents the largest Hg-MIF reported for any reduction process. Between pH 3.0 and 5.0, residual Hg2+ consistently exhibits negative Δ199Hg with positive δ202Hg, indicating depletion of odd-Hg isotopes concurrent with enrichment in heavy even isotopes. This inverse relationship places our data in distinct regions of δ202Hg-Δ199Hg space (quadrants II and IV), contrasting with the first-third quadrant clustering characteristic of most photochemical systems40,41 (Fig. 1b). For clarity, we define this MIF pattern as negative MIF ((-)MIF), in contrast to the positive MIF (( + )MIF) commonly observed in photochemistry40,41. Photochemical reduction typically produces (+)MIF, whereas (-)MIF is rare and of much smaller magnitude. It has been observed only under specific conditions, such as for Hg complexed with thiol ligands (e.g., Hg(Cys)2), where Δ199Hg values are restricted to −1.3‰ to 0.89‰ (Fig. 1b)40,41. Notably, the remarkably large MIF observed here (Δ199Hg from −78‰ to +28‰) is accompanied by only modest mass-dependent fractionation (MDF) (δ202Hg from −0.81‰ to +1.6‰). The combination of exceptionally large negative odd-MIF and minimal MDF shifts demonstrates that plasma chemistry drives unprecedented and distinctive Hg-MIF.

pH dependence of Hg isotope fractionation

During plasma-induced reduction, the residue Hg2+ consistently exhibited increasing δ202Hg values under all pH conditions examined. In contrast, both the sign (±) and magnitude of odd-Hg MIF were highly pH-dependent. Under moderately acidic conditions (pH 2.3–5.0), the reaction produced pronounced (-)MIF, characterized by progressive depletion of odd isotopes in the residue Hg2+ (Fig. 2a, b). At lower pH (1.6 and 2.0), however, the residue Hg2+ became enriched in both 202Hg and 199Hg, generating (+)MIF. In addition to this sign reversal, the magnitude of MIF is markedly suppressed under strongly acidic conditions, with the Δ199Hg differences falling below 3.3‰. While such low-pH environments are rare in nature, they provide a valuable experimental window for probing the mechanism of plasma-induced Hg-MIF. All plasma reduction reactions followed pseudo-first-order kinetics, expressed as ln([Hg]/[Hg]0) = -kt, with rate coefficient (k) decreasing as pH declined (from 1.19 h-1 at pH 5.0 to 0.09 h-1 at pH 1.6; Fig. S2). Control experiments

The SAGD process, often characterized as glow-discharge electrolysis, involves not only plasma chemistry but also UV radiation and charge transfer43, potentially introducing concurrent photochemical and electrochemical effects. To isolate these contributions, we conducted rigorous control experiments. Electrochemical reduction without plasma (Fig. S3a, Supplementary Text 1) rapidly reduced Hg2+ (Fig. S2), albeit with slower kinetics than the SAGD progress. However, this pathway produces negligible Hg-MIF (Δ199Hg from -0.10 to +0.12‰) while yielding measurable MDF (δ202Hg shifts from -0.86 to 1.54‰, Fig. 2c). Furthermore, exposure to plasma-light irradiation alone, without direct contact between the plasma and the sample (Fig. S3b, Supplementary Text 2), resulted in negligible Hg reduction and consequently no isotope fractionation. Together, these control experiments demonstrate that the anomalously large odd-MIF signatures arise specifically from plasma-induced reduction, rather than conventional electrochemical or photochemical pathways.

Discussion

Large and distinct odd Hg-MIF induced by plasma reduction

One remarkable feature of our results is the exceptionally large MIF observed in odd Hg isotopes (199Hg and 201Hg) during plasma-induced reduction. The measured Δ199Hg values span an unprecedented range from -78‰ to +28‰, far exceeding the MIF signatures typically reported for photochemical reduction (Fig. 1b). To better capture the intrinsic isotope effects beyond the apparent fractionation in raw isotope ratios, we quantified enrichment factors (E199Hg) from the slope of Δ199Hg versus ln(f)5,44, where f is the fraction of remaining Hg2+. As shown in Fig. 3a, E199Hg varies strongly in both magnitude and sign (±) and exhibits a clear dependence on pH. At pH 5.0 and 4.0, E199Hg is strongly positive (+28.6‰ and +31.5‰, respectively). However, as the pH declined, E199Hg decreased sharply, from +31.5‰ at pH 4.0 to +1.5‰ at pH 2.3, and became consistently negative (~ -2.1‰) at pH 2.0 and 1.6. This strong pH dependence, which closely correlates with the significant slowdown of Hg2+ reduction kinetics at low pH (Fig. 3b and Fig. S2). In contrast, the enrichment factors for MDF (e.g., E202Hg) show no clear trend with pH and remain relatively small, typically ranging from -0.5‰ to -1.1‰. Photochemical reduction experiments generally yield much smaller E199Hg than those observed in plasma-induced reduction (Fig. 3b). Previous studies have reported that E199Hg values for photochemical Hg2+ reduction typically range from -6.73‰ to +1.03‰12,40, and from -16.23‰ to -0.08‰ for methylmercury (MeHg) photochemical reduction14. Notably, one of the most positive E199Hg reported for photochemical Hg2+ reduction (+1.03‰) occurs in the presence of cysteine at pH 3.640; however, this value remains far below the +17.2‰ observed in our plasma-induced reduction experiment at pH 3.0. In photochemical systems, both the sign and magnitude of MIF are strongly influenced by ligand identity and pH41. While slightly positive E199Hg can occur under specific conditions, most photochemical reductions of Hg2+ and MeHg exhibit clearly negative E199Hg values. Systems dominated by oxygen-containing ligands consistently produce (+)MIF (negative E199Hg value) across the tested pH range (3-7)41, where thiol-ligands can generate either (+)MIF or (-)MIF depending on both pH and redox conditions40. In contrast, our plasma-induced reduction experiments, conducted in an aqueous matrix involving only oxygen-containing ligands (OH and H2O) and no thiol or organic ligands, consistently yielded positive E199Hg values at pH ≥2.3. Moreover, even small changes in pH induced significant shifts in both the magnitude and the sign of Hg-MIF. Collectively, these results demonstrate that plasma-induced reduction processes produce significant MIF, with enrichment factors that vary with pH and differ clearly from those in photochemical systems.

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain Hg-MIF, primarily the magnetic isotope effect (MIE) and the nuclear volume effect (NVE)45,46. The ratio of Δ199Hg to Δ201Hg provides a key diagnostic for distinguishing between these mechanisms and elucidating reaction pathways. The MIE, a kinetic isotope effect, typically produces Δ199Hg/Δ201Hg ratios ranging from 0.85 to 1.46, with both the magnitude and sign of the MIF influenced by reaction conditions14. In contrast, the NVE, which arises from differences in nuclear charge radii, yields a theoretical ratio of 1.59 ± 0.05 (2SE)12,41 and generally produces much smaller anomalies (<0.4‰)47. As shown in Fig. 4, the Δ199Hg/Δ201Hg slopes obtained from the plasma-induced reduction process vary with pH. From pH 5.0 to 3.0, the slope remains nearly constant at ~0.91 (Fig. 4a) despite a roughly linear decline in reduction rate (Fig. S2). At lower pH, however, the slope first decreases to 0.72 at pH 2.3, then increases to 1.20 and 1.27 at pH 2.0 and 1.6, respectively. The Δ199Hg/Δ201Hg ratios and the large MIF magnitudes observed here (E199Hg up to +31.5‰), strongly support MIE as the primary mechanism governing MIF during the plasma reduction process. Negative MIF corresponds to Δ199Hg/Δ201Hg ratios <1.0 (0.72–0.91), whereas positive MIF is characterized by values > 1.2. At pH 2.3, the Δ199Hg/Δ201Hg ratio reaches a minimum, suggesting a transitional regime in which both (-)MIF and (+)MIF pathways are active, with the former still dominating. At lower pH, the (+)MIF pathway becomes increasingly prominent, consistent with the rising slope. This transition is further reflected in the magnitude of MIF: at pH 2.3, the absolute value of E199Hg reaches a minimum (+1.79‰), while deviations from this pH in either direction significantly enhance the extent of MIF.

While a shift in Hg speciation coincides with the transition of Hg-MIF between pH 2-3 (Fig. S5), this change itself does not act as the causative factor for the Hg-MIF sign reversal. Within this pH range, the dominant hydrated Hg2+ species shift from [Hg(H2O)n]2+ (n = 1-7) at pH <3.0 to hydrolyzed [Hg(OH)2(H2O)n] at pH > 3.0 (Fig. S5)50,51. This speciation change is unlikely to govern the MIF sign reversal, as demonstrated by solution cathode glow discharge (SCGD) experiments, which employ an opposite polarity to our SAGD system and consistently yield positive MIF (Fig. S6, Supplementary Text 4). Therefore, changes in Hg speciation are unlikely to govern the MIF sign reversal. To further test the role of H•(aq), we introduced H2 (50 ~ 100 mL/min) into the Ar discharge, which promotes the generation of aqueous H•(aq)52,53. Under both pH 2.3 (normally yielding (-)MIF) and pH 2.0 (normally yielding (+)MIF), H2 addition accelerated Hg2+ reduction and altered isotopic signatures (Fig. 5c/f). At pH 2.0, increasing H2 flow enhanced the magnitude of (+)MIF, with E199Hg shifting from -1.92‰ to -4.59‰ (Fig. 5g). At pH 2.3, H2 addition reversed the MIF sign from negative to positive, with E199Hg changing from +2.32‰ to -2.25‰ (Fig. 5d/g). Implications for the global Hg cycle

Photochemical reactions have long been regarded as the dominant source of odd Hg-MIF in nature. Our experiments reveal a previously unrecognized, plasma-induced pathway. Unlike photoreduction, which typically produces modest positive or only limited negative anomalies (E199Hg from -16.23‰ to +1.03‰)12,40, plasma-induced reduction produces exceptionally large negative Δ199Hg anomalies (E199Hg up to ~ +31‰) with a consistent Δ199Hg/Δ201Hg slope of 0.91 at pH ≥ 3.0. Importantly, this mechanism remains operative under more realistic atmospheric conditions. In experiments conducted under an Ar/air (1:1) plasma, the residual Hg2+ still exhibited a pronounced positive E199Hg signature (+11.5‰), albeit with slower kinetics compared to pure Ar-SAGD (reduction rate decreasing from 0.93 h–1 to 0.23 h–1; Fig. S7). These findings challenge the prevailing view that photochemistry is the sole driver of large Hg-MIF and introduce plasma chemistry as a new pathway for explaining isotopic signatures that have been difficult to reconcile with existing photochemical lab work. Such plasma-driven processes likely compete with, or even dominate, conventional photochemistry in specialized atmospheric environments.

This possibility is particularly relevant in polar regions. Significant negative Δ199Hg values (as low as -3.45‰ to -5.08‰) have been reported in snow samples collected during, or immediately following, prolonged periods of darkness19,20. Such observations are difficult to explain solely through photochemical Hg cycling because photochemical reactions are effectively suppressed during the polar night and early spring owing to the absence of sunlight. This unsolved discrepancy is further underscored by recent three-dimensional isotope models, which explicitly exclude polar regions from global simulations, acknowledging that the mechanisms governing high-latitude Hg-MIF remain poorly constrained54. Importantly, auroral processes are already known to perturb the polar atmospheric redox budget through the generation of reactive NOx and HOx, and have been shown to drive up to 8% depletion of stratospheric ozone55. In this context, plasma-induced mercury reduction may represent a plausible non-photochemical isotopic fractionation pathway in polar atmospheres. If plasma-induced reduction yields Hg0 with highly positive Δ199Hg values, the residual Hg2+ pool must inevitably inherit the pronounced (-)MIF signatures. This mechanism provides a reasonable, albeit still qualitative, framework linking plasma chemistry to the strongly negative MIF signatures observed in polar snow and ice archives. This interpretation is consistent with the recent proposal by Song et al. (2026)54, who identified plasma chemistry, specifically the missing lightning component in atmospheric isotope models, as the likely key to resolving isotopic anomalies that remain recalcitrant to photochemical explanations.

Beyond modern environments, plasma chemistry may also provide a speculative perspective on paleoenvironmental Hg records. Current models typically assume mantle-derived Hg lacks MIF (Δ199Hg = 0), attributing positive Δ199Hg in pelagic sediments to photoreduction and negative ones in marginal marine sediment settings to vegetation uptake and subsequent weathering8,11,18. However, the occurrence of negative Δ199Hg excursions in pre-Ordovician sediments, prior to the emergence of extensive terrestrial vegetation, remains difficult to reconcile within this conventional photochemical framework. Existing photochemical models require unrealistically restrictive conditions (e.g., near-total light attenuation18), and often produce Δ199Hg/Δ201Hg ratios (1.46 ~ 1.89 for thiol-ligand photoreduction or photochemical oxidation processes15,40) that differ substantially from the observed values of ~0.956,57. Our findings suggest that plasma processes, such as volcanic lightning58 during large eruptions, may represent a potential non-photochemical source of (-)MIF in pre-vegetation intervals. Large explosive eruptions are capable of generating intense lightning activity, as illustrated by the 2022 Hunga Tonga eruption59. Nevertheless, whether volcanic lightning can generate negative Hg-MIF signatures and preserve them in the geological record remains to be systematically evaluated.

To assess the potential global significance of this mechanism, we extrapolated our air-Ar experimental yields to modern lightning conditions using a flash rate of 44 ± 5 s-1 and an average energy of 6.7 GJ per flash60,61. This calculation suggests that lightning could theoretically reduce ~570 tons of Hg2+ annually, corresponding to approximately 9.5% of troposphere Hg2+ reduction fluxes (Supplementary Texts 5)54,62. Clearly, these estimates represent an upper bound derived from optimized and continuous plasma conditions. In natural environments, lightning is highly transient and spatially heterogeneous, and atmospheric Hg2+ primarily exists in particulate-bound forms rather than in the well-mixed aqueous system employed here, all of which will strongly affect the actual fluxes63. Nonetheless, plasma-induced reduction generates exceptionally high E199Hg signatures (up to +31.5‰), far exceeding and opposite in sign to those of typical photochemical reduction (down to −2.75‰)12. Consequently, even a minor contribution of plasma-induced Hg2+ reduction, whether driven by tropical lightning or auroral forcing, could measurably influence atmospheric Hg isotope signatures. Such effects may therefore necessitate modification of current global models, many of which rely on artificially adjusted source signatures (Fig. S8).

It should be acknowledged that no direct field evidence of plasma-induced Hg-MIF has been observed. This may reflect the intrinsic complexity of natural lightning processes, which likely involve both the reduction of Hg2+ and the oxidation of Hg0, leading to the partial re-oxidation of newly produced Hg0. Moreover, the highly transient nature of lightning channels, their spatial heterogeneity, and subsequent atmospheric mixing further hinder the preservation of distinct isotopic signatures. These factors likely explain why plasma-induced anomalies remain elusive despite their mechanistic plausibility demonstrated here. Nevertheless, related plasma-induced isotope effects have been documented in other systems. Laboratory simulations of dust-driven electrostatic discharges on Mars demonstrate that low-temperature plasma can induce large kinetic isotope effects across multiple element systems64,65. Similarly, sulfur MIF observed in volcanic sulfate from large eruptions66 has traditionally been attributed to stratospheric photochemistry, although plasma reactions associated with volcanic lightning may also induce similar isotope anomalies. These observations collectively support the broader possibility that plasma processes may represent an underappreciated source of MIF in natural environments.

Overall, our plasma-liquid reaction system offers a unique and versatile platform for investigating the mechanism of Hg-MIF. Unlike previous gas-phase plasma MIF studies (e.g., O, Mg, and Ti)29,30, our setup operates at a plasma-liquid interface, allowing direct separation of Hg0 products from Hg2+ reactants, enabling reproducible isotopic measurements and controlled mechanistic tests. Importantly, it also more closely mimics natural lightning-driven processes at atmospheric interfaces. It can be extended to MIF studies of other elements (e.g., S, Cd, Pb, and Zn), thereby broadening plasma-driven isotope geochemistry.

Methods

Experimental setup for plasma-induced Hg reduction

It is known that the accumulation of positive and negative charges within numerous cloud particles induces the formation of a strong electric field culminating in lightning strikes26,27. Inspired by this natural phenomenon, our work utilizes a DC glow-discharge plasma reactor to investigate the unique isotopic signatures of plasma-induced reduction processes, thereby facilitating a comparative analysis of our distinct fingerprint with that of actual lightning. The plasma-induced Hg2+ reduction is achieved at the plasma-liquid interface using the apparatus shown in Fig. 1a, which consists of a homemade SAGD plasma reactor and a trapping unit. The Hg2+ working solutions (100 μg/L) were prepared by diluting the GBW 08617 Hg standard (containing ~3% HNO3) with ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ·cm). The pH was adjusted using double-purified HNO3 and ultrapure water as required for each experimental run. The reactor, with a working volume of approximately 100 mL, employs a Pt wire immersed in the solution as the anode, while a microplasma serves as the cathode. The microplasma was initiated by temporarily reducing the distance between the tip of a tapered tungsten electrode (outer diameter, 2 mm; length, 10 cm) and the solution surface (100 μg/L Hg). The gap, or height of the discharge, was set to about 3 mm in the present study. A 5-kΩ resistor inserted between the Pt anode and the positive output of the high-voltage DC power supply (HB-Z202-200AC, Tianjin Hengbo High Voltage Power Supply Plant, China) was used to stabilize the discharge current. As shown in Fig. 1a, argon gas (100 mL/min, Ar1) was continuously supplied as both the discharge gas and the carrier gas, transporting the Hg0 produced by the plasma reaction into the trapping solution (inverse aqua regia, 40% (v/v) HNO3:HCl=2:1) for collection67. After a certain time interval, the plasma was turned off and the system was purged with Ar (Ar2, Fig. 1a) for 10 mins. Subsequently, 4 mL of reaction solution was withdrawn from the reactor and immediately mixed with 10 mL of trapping solution, and then kept in the freezer until isotopic analysis. These operations ensured that both the trapped Hg and the residual Hg2+ in the reactor had identical Hg speciation and matrix composition, thereby enabling reliable isotope measurements. Simultaneously, the trapping solution was also renewed at the time of subsampling and collected to determine the evolution of Hg isotope ratios in both reactants and products of plasma-induced reduction. In the simulated air discharge experiments, a stable plasma discharge cannot be sustained for extended periods in pure ambient air. Therefore, these experiments were performed at pH 4.0 and a current of 40 mA using a 1:1 Ar–air mixed gas atmosphere. For ease of comparison with previously reported photochemical studies, we converted this instantaneous mode into a cumulative mode during data processing. Specifically, the isotope composition of the products obtained in our experiments was recalculated to represent the corresponding values under the cumulative mode. Each experiment lasted 3 to 5 hours, depending on the reaction rate under different conditions.

All acids used in this study were purified by double sub-boiling distillation using Savillex’s DST-1000 sub-distillation system. Ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ cm, Millipore Company, America) was used for the preparation of solutions. Commercially available NaNO2 and acetone were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. High-purity argon (99.999%) and compressed air were obtained from Wuhan Iron and Steel Group Oxygen Company, Wuhan, China. Hydrogen gas was supplied by a hydrogen generator (model TP-3030C, Beijing Beifen Tianpu Instrument Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).

Mercury concentration and isotope composition measurements

Total Hg concentrations were measured using atomic fluorescence spectrometry (AFS 9700, Beijing Haiguang Co., China). Mercury isotope analysis was performed on a Nu Plasma II MC-ICPMS instrument (Nu Instruments, UK) at the State Key Laboratory of Geomicrobiology and Environmental Change. Six Hg isotopes (198Hg, 199Hg, 200Hg, 201Hg, 202Hg, and 204Hg) and two Tl isotopes (203Tl, 205Tl) were measured simultaneously. A cold vapor generation (CVG) system were used for Hg sample introduction, with a Hg concentration of 5 μg/L. For mass bias correction, a 60 μg/L Tl standard solution was introduced via pneumatic nebulization. The sample introduction flow rate was set to 1.5 mL/min, yielding a signal intensity of ~1.5 V for 202Hg. Mass bias was corrected using both internal standardization based on the measured 205Tl/203Tl ratio and the sample-standard bracketing (SSB) method with the standard.
