Evaluating and Determining the Effects of Psychotropic Drugs on Motor Vehicle Driving Performance: Key Considerations of the Japanese Guideline and Criteria for Clinical Implementation of Evidence
Authors: Tetsuo Nakabayashi, Kunihiro Iwamoto, Akiko Yamaguchi, Yuki Konishi, Momoe Saji, Reiji Yoshimura, Kousuke Kanemoto, Hirofumi Aoki, Masahiko Ando, Norio Ozaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe cross‐national differences in automobile driving precautions stated in psychotropic drug package inserts primarily reflect regulatory policies and insufficient evaluation method standardization until recent years, rather than intrinsic ethnic differences. In January 2025, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) of Japan issued the Criteria for Determining the Effects of Psychotropic Drugs on Motor Vehicle Driving Performance. These criteria were published as an appendix to an earlier guideline for evaluating these effects published in December 2022. These guidelines and criteria, developed by the authors, provide methods for evaluating the effects of psychotropic drugs. However, these are not intended to assess the fitness of an individual patient to drive. The MHLW guideline and criteria recommend a tiered approach for assessing clinically meaningful driving impairment. Adverse events affecting alertness, such as drowsiness, caused by psychotropic drugs on driving performance are more frequent during the initial treatment stages and dose escalation. Therefore, evaluating the effects of psychotropic drugs on driving performance requires careful consideration of the persistence of clinically meaningful impairment. Additionally, understanding the magnitude and duration of such effects is crucial for selecting appropriate treatments and offering effective guidance to patients. The MHLW criteria categorize the effects of psychotropic drugs on driving performance into four levels based on their magnitude and duration. Furthermore, the MHLW criteria could help to determine which clinical trials are necessary to evaluate the persistence of clinically meaningful impairment. Namely, the MHLW criteria could be used to structure the substantial evidence needed for evaluating the effects on driving performance for approval applications and clinical implementation.

Introduction

Pharmacological treatment aims to reduce symptoms and alleviate distress and impairment in social, occupational, and other important activities, and is therefore an important treatment option for patients with psychiatric disorders. Recent advancements in psychotropic drugs have minimized the associated sedative side effects, thereby improving their tolerability [1, 2, 3]. For example, compared with sedating antidepressants, nonsedating antidepressants exhibit weaker inhibition of muscarinic, histaminergic, α‐adrenergic, and dopaminergic neurotransmitter release [4, 5, 6]. In recent years, the focus of approved hypnotics has shifted from benzodiazepine receptor agonists to orexin and melatonin receptor agonists [7, 8, 9, 10]. These differences account for their improved safety profile. The tolerability of psychotropic drugs is essential for not only ensuring continued pharmacotherapy to prevent relapse or recurrence [11, 12, 13], but also maintaining the patient's social functioning. In particular, automobile driving frequently serves as an essential means of transportation in daily life [14, 15]. Therefore, examining the effects of psychotropic drugs on driving performance and providing appropriate precautions can help protect patient safety while improving social functioning through the selection of appropriate therapeutic options.

Driving requires multiple cognitive and physical functions, including arousal, sensory perception, cognition, and psychomotor skills [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. These are essential driving‐related functions [22, 23]. Therefore, psychotropic drugs often affect driving performance in ways other than just causing drowsiness, which makes it crucial to identify the affected functions and assess the clinical significance of the effects of psychotropic drugs. To ensure the efficient and consistent evaluation of the effects of psychotropic drugs on driving performance, we developed a guideline that provides evaluation methods, which was published by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of Japan (MHLW) on December 27, 2022 [24]. This guideline, similar to that of the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), recommends a tiered approach [24, 25]. However, it incorporates unique criteria tailored to Japan's regulatory and clinical context. Differences between the MHLW and FDA guidelines have been reported [26]. In addition, specific information on the design of clinical trials to assess the clinical meaningful impairment of psychotropic drugs on driving performance has been reported [27]. On August 15, 2024, the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) noted that evaluating the persistence of clinically meaningful impairment is important to determining such effects [28]. Consequently, we developed criteria to determine these effects [29].

Evaluating the extent of the effects of psychotropic drugs on driving performance is crucial to ensure that appropriate precautions are included in package inserts and that suitable guidance is provided in clinical practice. In principle, the package inserts of medications are designed based on evidence and provide precautions regarding the operation of heavy machinery such as automobiles. To support this, we developed criteria for determining these effects, included as an appendix to the MHLW guideline, which was published by the MHLW on January 31, 2025 [29]. The guideline outlines a structured approach for evaluating the effects of psychotropic drugs on driving performance. The accompanying criteria further classify these effects based on their magnitude and duration. Together, these elements form a cohesive framework to ensure accurate evaluation and informed clinical decision‐making. However, the MHLW guideline and criteria are not intended to assess the fitness of an individual patient to drive.

This paper provides an overview of the MHLW guideline and its appendix, along with the rationale behind them. It also describes what kinds of clinical trials are needed to evaluate and determine the effects of psychotropic drugs on driving performance, and this information is essential for appropriately structuring the substantial evidence for approval applications and clinical implementation.

Overview of the MHLW Guideline

Framework and Appendix of the MHLW Guideline

To provide context for the structure of the MHLW guideline and promote an understanding of the MHLW criteria, we first outline its main evaluation steps and criteria. The guideline aims to provide a systematic framework to classify and assess psychotropic drugs' effects on driving, ensuring safety and efficacy in clinical practice. It is appropriate to evaluate the profile of the investigational drug in the tiered approach and identify the proposed studies needed, rather than conducting all the studies indicated in the MHLW guideline [28]. The appendix to the MHLW guideline, which is described in the following section, further elaborates on the evaluation criteria categorized by the magnitude and duration of these effects.

The Tiered Approach

In the MHLW guideline, the basic framework of the tiered approach is as follows [24, 26].

STEP 1: Pharmacological Evaluation

Nonclinical studies will evaluate the pharmacological effects of the investigational drug and its metabolites to identify any potential impacts on driving‐related functions such as alertness, sensory perception, cognition, and psychomotor functions.

STEP 2: Pharmacodynamic Evaluation

Early‐phase clinical trials, including pharmacodynamic studies, will assess the drug's impact on driving‐related functions identified in STEP 1 with high sensitivity.

STEP 3: Evaluation of the Temporal Profile of Adverse Events in Clinical Trials

The temporal profile of adverse events affecting automobile driving will be evaluated in all clinical trials, including exploratory, confirmatory, and long‐term studies. This evaluation includes time of onset, duration, and tolerance. These driving‐related adverse events are defined through pharmacological (STEP 1) and pharmacodynamic (STEP 2) assessments.

STEP 4: Driving Study

Driving studies will be conducted if evidence indicates an effect on any driving performance domain.

Four core functional domains related to driving (alertness, sensory–perceptual functions, cognitive functions, or psychomotor functions) were selected in the MHLW guideline [24, 26]. The aim of pharmacodynamic studies in STEP 2 is to screen for the effects of the investigational drug on the functional domains identified in STEP 1 by using psychometric tests. To ensure high sensitivity in pharmacodynamic tests, it is appropriate to select those for which the relationship between alcohol and dosage has been studied, such as psychomotor vigilance, continuous performance, digit symbol substitution, and choice reaction time tests [26, 30, 31]. However, assessing the clinical significance of the pharmacodynamic study results remains challenging [32, 33]. Therefore, later in development, it is appropriate to design studies to characterize the clinical relevance of earlier findings. The main purpose of exploratory, confirmatory, and long‐term studies is to assess the efficacy and safety of the drug under development for the disease. These studies will evaluate the temporal profile of adverse events affecting automobile driving when assessing the effects of the drug on driving performance (STEP 3). This is because the adverse events caused by psychotropic drugs may occur only in the early stages of administration, such as when tolerance or habituation is established [34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39]. Additionally, previous studies have shown weak correlations between the plasma concentrations of certain types of psychotropic drugs and driving impairment [40]. Therefore, the administration period for clinical trials should be long enough to evaluate the temporal profile. In other words, this period should consider not only pharmacokinetics but also the results of pharmacodynamic studies and adverse event occurrence in previous clinical trials. It is also important to evaluate the effects of both single and multiple dosing of an investigational drug to evaluate the persistence of clinically meaningful impairment (STEP 4).

Based on the experience of the MHLW and PMDA in lifting the prohibition of driving a car, etc., for serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) such as venlafaxine, duloxetine, and milnacipran, in 2016 [41, 42], the MHLW guideline specified the cases in which a driving study is not required to reduce the burden of development. Of these SNRIs, only venlafaxine has been investigated in a driving study [43]. However, the prohibition was also lifted for duloxetine and milnacipran because selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are not prohibited when driving a car, etc., and SNRIs have not been found to be significantly different in terms of their affinity to the receptors that affect arousal pharmacologically [44]. Furthermore, clinical trials [45, 46, 47] and epidemiological studies [41, 42] have not reported any significant differences in the occurrence of adverse events affecting automobile driving between SSRIs and SNRIs. Namely, the MHLW guideline states that clinically meaningful impairment can be assessed, and a driving study is not required for the following cases: If there is no obvious effect on the functional domains related to automobile driving;If driving studies have already been conducted abroad and ethnic factors are not expected to influence the efficacy and safety of the investigated drug;If driving studies have been conducted with other drugs that do not significantly differ in affinity to receptors that pharmacologically affect arousal, and there is no significant difference between the investigated drug and those drugs in terms of the occurrence of adverse events that may affect automobile driving in clinical trials or epidemiological studies.

Evaluation of Clinical Meaningful Impairment

The purpose of assessing the effects of drugs on automobile driving performance is to predict and reduce the risk of road accidents. However, the effects of drugs on driving performance cannot be assessed based on the risk of actual automobile crashes because it would be unethical to use car crashes as an endpoint in randomized controlled trials. Therefore, driving studies using motor vehicles or driving simulators assess the clinical significance of investigational drug effects.

Subjects in driving studies are selected from the population likely to use the drug to provide information about disease‐drug interactions [48, 49, 50], including older adults. This is because providing information about disease‐drug interactions is important for clinical application [24, 25]. However, it is possible to select from healthy subjects if the differences between healthy subjects and patients are sufficiently small [36, 51, 52, 53].

The primary endpoint should be selected according to the characteristics of the investigational drug. The MHLW and FDA guidelines provide an example of the use of the standard deviation of lateral position (SDLP) to assess drug‐related drowsiness in driving studies [24, 25]. SDLP, which refers to the degree of weaving while driving, is a standardized index used as a primary endpoint in driving studies worldwide [22, 54, 55]. If the investigational drug affects functional domains other than alertness, such as sensory‐perceptual, cognitive, or psychomotor functions, then it would be appropriate to establish evaluation metrics other than SDLP as the primary endpoint. In fact, the clinically relevant cut‐off points for indicators other than the SDLP, such as braking reaction time, collision numbers, and standard deviation of speed, have not yet been determined, so interpreting the results is limited at this point. Because the purpose of the driving study is to evaluate the presence or absence of clinically meaningful impairment with higher specificity, newer psychotropic drugs with fewer sedative side effects [56, 57, 58] require positive controls to confirm assay sensitivity. The positive control will be a drug that has previously been shown to impair driving performance by significantly increasing SDLP (e.g., zopiclone [59, 60, 61, 62, 63, 64], mirtazapine [52, 65, 66, 67, 68]). In general, negative studies in the absence of demonstrated assay sensitivity without positive controls are not interpretable because false negatives cannot be ruled out. It is common to examine the difference in SDLP between placebo and investigational drug administration using a crossover design to eliminate the influence of individual driving ability [33, 69].

Furthermore, assessing the persistence of clinically meaningful impairment is also important in determining impact. The timing of multiple dosing in driving studies should consider the pharmacokinetics of the investigational drug and the tolerance or habituation observed in other clinical trials.

Special attention should be paid to the interpretation of driving study results. However, like the FDA guideline, the MHLW guideline does not describe specific statistical evaluation methods in order to maintain flexibility. Therefore, this review explains the statistical evaluation methods that have been used most frequently in recent years. Recent driving studies evaluate clinically meaningful impairment through non‐inferiority or statistical superiority to placebo. The clinical significance of the results cannot be determined based solely on statistical superiority. For this reason, non‐inferiority tests are often used in the primary analysis. If the mean difference in SDLP is not statistically significant, then it has no apparent effect on driving performance [52, 70, 71, 72, 73]. On the other hand, even if the mean difference in SDLP is statistically significant, if the upper limit of the confidence interval is below the non‐inferiority margin, that is, non‐inferiority to placebo is demonstrated, the effect is judged not to be clinically meaningful impairment [60, 61, 62, 66, 74]. Furthermore, if the upper limit of the confidence interval exceeds this margin, that is, non‐inferiority to placebo is not demonstrated, then the effect is considered to be clinically meaningful impairment [75]. When examining non‐inferiority, blood alcohol concentration (BAC) is commonly used as the benchmark. The non‐inferiority margin is set as the difference in SDLP between the investigational drug and placebo administrations at a BAC of 0.05%. Epidemiological studies have reported a dose–response relationship between BAC and the risk of road traffic crashes, with an increased risk of road traffic crashes at BAC levels of 0.05% and above [54, 76, 77, 78], and that BAC correlates with changes in SDLP from placebo [79, 80, 81, 82, 83]. These non‐inferiority margins are set based on the reliability and validity for each motor vehicle or driving simulator used in driving studies. For studies with on‐the‐road driving using motor vehicles, for example, the non‐inferiority margin is set at 2.4 cm [33, 80]. In driving studies using simulators, the non‐inferiority margin varies depending on the type of simulator and ranges from 2.6 to 9.23 cm [71, 74, 81, 83, 84]. This margin was determined through previous validation studies. Namely, driving studies evaluated using reference values set by the BAC could be used to predict the risk of traffic accidents and would be more ecologically valid than psychometric tests [32, 33, 85]. However, because the SDLP reference values using BAC as a benchmark have been validated for the mean, and reference values for individual subjects have not been established, it is possible to assess population effects [62]. Alcohol has a wide range of effects on the central nervous system, acting as an agonist of γ‐aminobutyric acid type A (GABAA) receptors, and also acting directly or indirectly on other neurotransmitter systems, such as the glutamate, dopamine, serotonin, and opioid systems [86, 87, 88, 89, 90]; therefore, BAC is biologically appropriate to use as a benchmark.

Insomnia affects driving performance [16, 17, 91]. The goal of insomnia treatment is to improve not only insomnia symptoms, but also functioning the next day [92, 93, 94]. Driving studies involving hypnotics have used symmetry analysis to examine the imbalance between functional improvement the next day and residual hypnotic effects [60, 61, 62, 71, 74]. This symmetry analysis evaluates driving performance at the individual level and gauges whether the number of subjects with an increase or decrease in SDLP is statistically significantly imbalanced. Symmetry analysis is often used as a secondary analysis to evaluate consistency with the results of the primary analysis.

Structure of the MHLW Criteria

Table 1 shows the criteria for determining the effects of psychotropic drugs on driving performance as presented in the appendix to the MHLW guideline. As mentioned above, the purpose of evaluating the effects of psychotropic drugs on driving performance is to assess the “persistence of clinically meaningful impairment” to obtain a basis for setting the precautions in the package insert. Figure 1 illustrates the classification of psychotropic drug effects on driving performance. The blue areas indicate periods of clinically significant impairment, highlighting differences in the temporal profiles of adverse effects. This classification facilitates the visualization of the magnitude and persistence of these impairments. In addition, the criteria presented in Table 1 consider that the need for pharmacodynamic and driving studies should be considered at each stage of the tiered approach. Namely, if the pharmacological effects are theoretically negligible or clearly influential in terms of affecting driving performance domains, then their assessment in the pharmacodynamic study is unnecessary. In addition, if the pharmacological effects of the investigational drug can affect driving performance, but the pharmacodynamic study, which is a screening study, shows no apparent effects on functional domains related to automobile driving, for example, then it may not be necessary to conduct a driving study. Comparison With the DRUID Categorization System

The FDA guideline does not provide the criteria for determining the effects [25]. The European Commission has mandated that the Summary of Product Characteristics indicate the degree of influence on the operation of heavy machinery, such as automobiles, using four levels [95], and the Driving under the Influence of Drugs, Alcohol and Medicines (DRUID), the European Union (EU) research project, has reevaluated the approved drugs [96, 97, 98]. A notable distinction is that the MHLW criteria emphasize longitudinal effects across the entire treatment period (Table 1), whereas the DRUID categorization system primarily addresses initial treatment phases [96]. This distinction underscores the comprehensive nature of the MHLW criteria in capturing both short‐term and persistent effects. The MHLW criteria focus on psychotropic drugs throughout the entire treatment duration, while the DRUID system applies to all medical drugs, emphasizing only the initial treatment phase.

Although non‐psychotropic drugs (e.g., hypoglycemic agents, antiallergic agents) may also affect driving performance, the MHLW guideline and its appendix were limited to psychotropic drugs, similar to the FDA guideline [25]. This is because the effects on driving performance vary depending on the characteristics of the drug, and thus should be evaluated in an appropriate manner. Furthermore, as a result, the MHLW guideline and its appendix covered the entire period of administration, taking into account the temporal profiles of adverse events of psychotropic drugs. After the DRUID evaluation results and the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration guideline were published in 2011 [22, 96], it has become common to evaluate the effects of multiple as well as single dosing in driving studies [27]. Thus, it is appropriate to include not only the magnitude, but also the duration of effects in the MHLW criteria. In addition, in the MHLW criteria, each category and study result has been contrasted because of the well‐established methods for evaluating the effects of psychotropic drugs on driving performance.

Precautions in the Package Insert

Figure 2 shows the cross‐national variation in precaution levels. This difference stems from regulatory policies and the insufficient standardization of evaluation methods until recent years [26, 99]. The policy of precautions in the package insert is also specified in the appendix to the MHLW guideline (Table 1). The appendix to the MHLW guideline, similar to the European Commission guideline [95], specifies four levels of precautions. In fact, because recent psychotropic drugs approved in Japan, the United States (US), or the EU have some central nervous system effects, there is no lack of caution in the package inserts for hypnotics (Figure 2a), antidepressants (Figure 2b), antipsychotics (Figure 2c), antiseizure medications (Figure 2d), and a mood stabilizer (Figure 2e), and some precaution is provided at three levels (see also Tables S1–S5). However, because the precautions in the package insert regarding the effects on the ability to drive and use machinery are not limited to automobile driving, the provisions in the appendix to the MHLW guideline are limited to assumptions. Namely, the package insert is expected to state that driving, etc., is not prohibited if the effect is “Category 0: No effect” or “Category 1: Mild effect”, and to be cautious about the possibility of effects in the latter case. In the case of “Category 2: Moderate”, it is assumed that driving, etc., is partially prohibited, for example, during a part of the initial period of administration. The factors specifying partial prohibition in the package insert are shown in Table 2. Partial prohibition of driving, etc., for some of the approved psychotropic drugs is often based on duration rather than circumstance (prohibition by duration: Japan 0%, US 100.0%, EU 62.5%), and the prohibition by circumstance includes not only drug effects, but also conditions related to mental disorders or other health issues [16, 100]. In the case of “Category 3: Severe”, driving a car, etc., is prohibited during administration.

Challenges and Future Directions

The MHLW guideline and its appendix detail the evaluation methods employed in the development of new psychotropic drugs. Actually, vornorexant, an orexin receptor antagonist approved in Japan in August 2025, includes precautions included in its package insert in accordance with the MHLW criteria [101, 102, 103].

Although clinical trials provide direct evidence of a drug's efficacy and safety, it may not be feasible to conduct trials aimed at reevaluating already approved drugs. Reevaluation of approved drugs requires a different approach than evaluating new drugs. For this reason, the MHLW guideline and its appendix state that evaluations of approved drugs can be used for reference [24, 26]. Iwamoto et al. [104] reviewed the effects of the approved antiseizure medications on driving performance to assess the feasibility of applying the MHLW criteria to approved drugs. The target drugs in this review included carbamazepine, valproate, lamotrigine, lacosamide, and levetiracetam, all commonly prescribed as oral monotherapy for focal or generalized seizures in adults [105, 106, 107]. Carbamazepine, valproate, lamotrigine, and lacosamide are voltage‐gated sodium channel modulators, while levetiracetam is a synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A modulator [108, 109, 110]. The findings from this review demonstrate the alignment of the MHLW criteria, particularly “Category 2: Moderate”, with real‐world observations of antiseizure medications, reinforcing the guideline's applicability to approved drugs. This review concludes that the initial administration of these five antiseizure medications might affect driving performance, but these effects appear to diminish with continued use. This conclusion was based on findings from nonrandomized driving studies with long‐term administration [111, 112], which were consistent with the epidemiological data [113, 114, 115, 116]. Considering the pharmacokinetics, carbamazepine induces cytochrome P450, leading to decreased blood levels over days of administration [117, 118], which may affect driving performance in the initial phase [119, 120].

However, the inconsistency between existing data on approved drugs and the MHLW guideline can often be attributed to differences in the study design, such as the lack of standardized driving studies or variations in the temporal profiles of adverse events. Real‐world data (RWD) and evidence (RWE) are expected to play a greater role in evaluating approved drugs compared with those under development. Additionally, conducting driving studies with patients taking certain types of psychotropic drugs is challenging. For instance, it may be clinically unacceptable to stop antiseizure medication for patients with epilepsy, even though driving studies typically employ a crossover design, which requires stopping medication (see also “Evaluation of Clinical Meaningful Impairment”). The consistency between the results of nonrandomized driving studies and epidemiological data may enable us to consider all of the effects on driving performance [104]. Furthermore, real‐world driving performance data from electronic health records or telematics systems could provide insights into the long‐term impact of psychotropic drugs on driving behavior. Therefore, depending on the sufficiency of evidence for each individual approved drug, it is essential to establish the positioning of RWD and RWE in the overall clinical data package [121, 122, 123].

Because the MHLW guideline and its appendix provide methods for evaluating the effects of psychotropic drugs on driving performance, they are not intended to assess the fitness of an individual patient to drive. Neither the MHLW guidelines nor the FDA guideline consider external ethnic factors, such as region‐specific traffic conditions, when conducting pharmacodynamic and driving studies. Regarding the evaluation of drug effects alone, the MHLW guideline and criteria appear applicable to countries other than Japan. However, the content of the precautions in the package insert should be carefully formulated based on the regulatory and clinical settings of each region.

Conclusion

The MHLW guideline and its appendix provide a comprehensive and robust framework for evaluating and categorizing the effects of psychotropic drugs on driving performance. Furthermore, the appendix to the MHLW guideline provides criteria that could be used to determine which clinical trials, including pharmacodynamic tests and driving studies, are necessary to evaluate the effects of psychotropic medications on driving performance. Namely, the MHLW criteria could be used to structure substantial evidence and clinical data packages for evaluating the effects on driving performance for approval applications. By adopting a tiered approach with clear classification criteria, this guideline ensures precise assessments that address both clinical and regulatory demands. To ensure that the clinical data package of drug development is well planned and that appropriate precautions are taken, the temporal profile of adverse events affecting automobile driving should be evaluated in all clinical trials. Additionally, the timing of multiple dosing in a driving study should consider the pharmacokinetics of the investigational drug and the tolerance or habituation observed in other clinical trials.

The cross‐national differences in the automobile driving precautions stated in the psychotropic drug package inserts primarily reflect regulatory policies and the insufficient standardization of evaluation methods, rather than ethnic differences in pharmacokinetics or pharmacodynamics. At present, unlike in the US and the EU, most Japanese package inserts for even standard psychotropic drugs stipulate that patients should not engage in the operation of heavy machinery, such as automobile driving. From this point forward, the use of the MHLW guideline and its appendix will facilitate the clinical implementation of evidence on driving performance and the package insert is expected to provide evidence‐based precautions. It is important to note that the MHLW guideline and its appendix are not intended to help determine a patient's fitness to drive in clinical practice. Other factors, such as medical conditions, can also affect driving performance in addition to medications [16, 100, 124, 125, 126, 127, 128, 129]. Therefore, even if a drug has no effect on driving performance, the safety of individual patients cannot be guaranteed. It is crucial to provide appropriate guidance on a case‐by‐case basis as well as risk communication between patients and physicians in clinical practice [98]. Moreover, future efforts should focus on incorporating RWD and RWE to enhance their applicability and effectiveness.
