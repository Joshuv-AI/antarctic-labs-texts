Radiological examination of the “Braided Lady”: A CT-based case study of an Egyptian mummy
Authors: Max J Goodman, Victoria Araj, Douglas Pierce, Andrew Nelson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Braided Lady, an ancient Egyptian mummy previously examined through radiography, was subjected to a high-resolution multidetector CT with 0.625-mm slice thickness and multiplanar and 3-dimensional reconstructions to assess mummification techniques and explore potential causes of death within a cultural and medical context. CT analysis confirmed prior findings of well-preserved osseous and soft tissue anatomy, incomplete evisceration, and the absence of excerebration, consistent with mummification practices reserved for nonroyal elites of the New Kingdom. Dental wear and pathology were observed, likely reflecting the abrasive diet of ancient Egyptians, and unique dental findings suggested participation in the ''Opening of the Mouth'' ritual. No acute skeletal trauma or disease was evident; ambiguous findings of calcifications may indicate chronic parasitic infection, but no definitive evidence was found for infectious or metabolic disease as cause of death. Despite limitations posed by tissue desiccation and lack of historical records, this multidisciplinary radiological assessment advances our understanding of ancient Egyptian funerary practices and demonstrates the value of modern noninvasive imaging in reconstructing the lives and deaths of archeological individuals.Keywords: Ancient Egyptian mummy, Mummification, Paleopathology, Opening of the mouth ritual, IMPACT radiological mummy database

Case report

We present in this case report a young adult female Egyptian mummy who, based on mummification trends, is dated from the 18th to 21st Dynasty of the Third Intermediate Period or the New Kingdom of Egypt (Fig. 1) [[1], [2], [3]]. She was discovered in a cache of mummies in Egypt and not in an individual tomb. She was brought to Canada in 1860 by Dr. James A. Douglas, a former grave-robber and physician, and then given to the Niagara Falls Museum [4]. She was later purchased by the John Carlos Museum at Emory University in 1999 before being sold to the Museum of World Treasures in Wichita, Kansas in 2006 [2]. Because of her notable and rare hairstyle that caught the attention of anthropologists and museum visitors, she earned the name the “Braided Lady” [5].

The braided lady captured at the museum of world treasures, Wichita, Kansas.

There are particular markers that indicate that the Braided Lady was of high status, although not of royalty [3]. Firstly, her arms are in an extended position over the pelvis; royalty would have had their arms crossed [6]. On physical examination, it was discovered that there remained remnants of her spleen and liver from an incomplete evisceration, and there was no evidence of postmortem craniocervical trauma to suggest excerebration [3]. Conversely, members of the royal class would have been completely excerebrated and eviscerated, and the poor would not have even been attempted [7]. Additionally, her wrappings were nonuniform and not of fine linens that royalty dressed in. Finally, she was observed to have manicured hands polished with henna, and she appeared to have been in a state of overall good health rather than malnourishment or cachexia.

The Braided Lady was first examined by egyptologist Bob Briers who wrote in 2008 that she may have died in her early to mid-20s as determined by examination of her X-rays. Unfortunately, these X-rays are no longer available to be viewed today, but it was believed at the time to have displayed mandibular molars and a partial fusion of her growth plates in the distal femur and proximal tibia [3]. Wade & Nelson then concluded that this would mean the Braided Lady would have been in her late 20s or early 30s. It is thought that she would have been the height of 4′10′′ with a curved spine from X-ray [5]. Briers took samples from the Braided Lady for further examination including material from abdominal tissue, linens, hair, and an insect exoskeleton that was removed from her hair; however, the results are not publicly known [3].

Although prior X-ray examinations provided initial insights into her anatomy, high-resolution computed tomography (CT) and 3-dimensional reconstruction offer a more detailed evaluation of skeletal and desiccated soft tissue structures. Overall, this case highlights how modern CT imaging and 3-dimensional reconstruction techniques can be applied to mummified specimens, underscoring the relevance of diagnostic radiology in interpreting preserved human remains and reconstructing their biological and cultural context.

Objective

To confirm previous conclusions made on X-ray about the Braided Lady and to provide a more detailed 3-Dimensional analysis of her so as to better understand ancient medical, cultural, and funerary practices.

Materials and methods

CT has emerged as an informative approach to noninvasive mummy analysis [6,8]. The IMPACT Radiological Mummy Database is a large-scale collaborative online database of mummy radiographs from researchers and institutions worldwide [9]. The Braided Lady was the mummy from the database we chose to analyze, as the CT results had never before been formally assessed. Axial CT images of the Braided Lady were obtained with sagittal, coronal, and 3-dimensional reconstructions on a GE LightSpeed 16 scanner at 0.625 mm slice thickness. Scanning was performed at 140 kVp without the administration of intravenous or oral contrast. The scanned data was visualized on ORS web visualization software. As part of our research team, D.P., a board-certified radiologist and fellow in abdominal imaging, was responsible for the impression of the CT scan. Utilizing this impression, the remainder of our research team were able to make interpretations on the Braided Lady’s life, culture, and possible causes of demise.

Findings

The cranial vault was intact, normally sized, and atraumatic (Fig. 2). Residual desiccated soft tissue compatible with grossly preserved cerebral matter layered in the posterior cranial fossa and extended a thin linear density through the foramen magnum and along the spinal canal, consistent with residual pachymeninges and/or spinal nervous tissue.

Intact, atraumatic cranial vault with desiccated cerebral tissue and residual meningeal or spinal nervous tissue extending through the foramen magnum (green thick arrow); both the maxillary and mandibular incisors and premolars are also flattened (yellow arrows) in this image suggesting dental wear.

The paranasal sinuses were lucent and the nasal septum was midline. There was no evidence of trans-sphenoidal postmortem trauma to suggest excerebration had been attempted via that approach. The orbits were intact with some remaining intraorbital tissue bilaterally.

Regarding dentition, the upper right incisor was fractured at the crown and the central right incisor had been displaced posteriorly into the oropharynx where it was found to be intact (Fig. 3). Both the upper right and left premolars were not present. The remaining molars and premolars were flattened with widened crowns.

Extensive periodontal disease is seen anteriorly (yellow dotted arrow) as well as a right fractured molar (blue arrow) and an intact central right incisor (red thick arrow) displaced posteriorly into the oropharynx.

The atlanto-occipital joints and all cervical, thoracic, and lumbar vertebrae and articulations were intact without curvature. No radiographic evidence of metabolic bone disease, age-advanced demineralization, or prior aggressive bone lesions was found. Desiccation had widened the sacroiliac joints and the left innominate bone was displaced posteriorly in a gravity-dependent fashion, also resulting in malalignment of the pubic symphysis (Fig. 4). Osseous structures of the pelvis were otherwise intact without evidence of fracture.

Desiccation and packing material (yellow arrows) notable within the pelvis.

Apart from the anterior translation of the left tibiotalar joint, the osseous structures of the appendicular skeleton were in excellent condition. The skeleton was atraumatic.

Within the thorax, there are densities in the upper and left mediastinum that resemble packing material and/or desiccated great vessels with probable cardiac tissue (Fig. 5). Depression of the central anterior chest wall was present related to postmortem desiccation of costochondral cartilage. Interestingly, residual fibrous pericardium and the respiratory diaphragm were seen (Fig. 6).

Desiccation present within the thorax, cardiac tissue observable.

Residual fibrous pericardium and diaphragm observable.

Within the abdominal cavity, there was desiccated intraperitoneal tissue including residual liver in its native position within the right upper quadrant (Fig. 7). Other hyperdense packing and/or desiccated abdominal organs were noted in the left upper quadrant and pelvis, but apart from a morphology reminiscent of a kidney in the left upper quadrant, there were no clear structural clues as to their identity.

Desiccated intraperitoneal tissue including residual liver (yellow arrows) in its native position as well as desiccated abdominal organs present in left upper quadrant, possibly left kidney (green thick arrow).

Discussion

Because we do not possess records of the Braided Lady’s death, we are unable to definitively establish our findings to a cause of mortality. Desiccation of the tissue, the removal of some organs for packing, and the shifting of her body from transportation created difficulty in establishing inferences of injuries or trauma antemortem as well as the pathogenesis of systemic diseases [9]. We also do not have possession of the Braided Lady’s prior X-rays, nor were we present with her body to perform physical exam findings and hence relied on previous documentation. Regardless, we will consider some differentials.

Because the Braided Lady was estimated to have lived between her 20 and 30s, it is unlikely that her death was due to natural causes. Women of higher status in ancient Egypt were afforded the ability to establish businesses beyond upkeep of the household and childbearing which put them at risk of occupational injuries [10]. While the findings of her pelvis might suggest a history of pregnancy, such an assertion is confounded by desiccation. Additionally, there was no indication that the Braided Lady was located alongside the remains of a fetus or newborn, nor were there remains in the birth canal [11]. Given her braided hair, manicured hands, and atraumatic skeleton without signs of degeneration, she unlikely engaged in harsh physical labor as well.

The dentition of ancient Egyptian mummies has been historically poor [12]. Although the Braided Lady did not on previous physical exam findings nor on imaging appear to have suffered malnutrition, her teeth suggest being worn down. Her missing teeth also suggest a history of periodontal disease [12]. These oral findings are likely explained by a diet of refined foods like bread that were contaminated by particulate matter, however we do not have the carbon and nitrogen isotopic analyses to make more specific mentions of her diet [13]. Dental abscesses were uncommon given the fibrous diet of the majority of ancient Egyptians, but the pathological incidence seems to have risen over time and across social hierarchies [14]. While it is very well possible that dental disease could have been the cause of death of the Braided Lady, it could have also made her more susceptible to other fatal etiologies.

It is noteworthy that the fractured incisor and the one found intact within her pharynx suggest that an ''Opening of the Mouth'' (OMR) ritual was performed [15]. This finding may also be accidental, but cases of OMR have been documented before and are described in rare Greek texts of Egyptian mummies. Reasons for this procedure are open to interpretation and range from being part of a magic ritual to symbolizing the body as a medium to the dead during a portion of the mummification process after evisceration and shortly prior to wrapping the mummy. Confirmation by CT reveals that the Braided Lady had an incomplete evisceration without excerebration. The New Kingdom commonly removed and dried the organs, apart from the heart, and returned them into the body wrapped in linen bundles [16]. Within the New Kingdom, variations to the extent of evisceration and transnasal excerebration were done and can range from complete to no attempt [17].

Let us consider infectious diseases, as ancient Egypt is notorious for its rather biblical plagues. It is unknown if the Braided Lady was placed in a cache because of social class reasons or if she was victim to a pandemic requiring rushed mass burials [18]. The incomplete evisceration and lack of excerebration can point to either possibility. Smallpox is believed to have existed between the 18th to 21st Dynasty and may have even killed Ramses V [19]. It is identified by pustules across the body and by molecular analysis; however, the Braided Lady is unlikely to have died from smallpox given no observed findings of skin lesions. Yersinia Pestis can also be ruled out without eschar, vesicles, or buboes found on the skin. Although X-ray suggested the Braided Lady had a curved spine, this was not corroborated on CT and does not suggest a disease such as Pott’s [20]. Parasitic infections are possible and were common in ancient Egypt due to the lack of sanitation in food and water as well as in their irrigation at the time. Without microscopic evaluation, the calcifications within the skull, intraperitoneal, and pelvic vault may be residual or evidence of Schistosomiasis to Strongyloides stercoralis to Taenia solium to Ascaris lumbricoides to others [21]. Even if these parasites are a possibility, they are predominantly chronic in nature and unlikely to cause death but could certainly exacerbate another disease. Malaria has been diagnosed before in New Kingdom mummies, given the nature of the Nile Valley as a prime location for mosquitoes [22,23]. However, malaria as well as other acute infections do not have a specific presentation without molecular or immunological techniques of identification. Further evaluation of the Braided Lady and more information regarding the cache of mummies she was found with are warranted.

To place these findings in context, it is useful to compare the Braided Lady’s features with patterns reported in previously studied Egyptian mummies. A systematic review of CT-detectable pathologies in 189 Egyptian mummies found that artificial changes such as organ removal, dental and jaw pathologies, and chronic degenerative skeletal changes were among the most commonly reported findings, with the cause of death rarely identifiable on imaging [24]. In comparison, the Braided Lady exhibits incomplete evisceration without excerebration and well-preserved soft tissue remnants, which contrast with the more typical pattern of extensive organ removal seen in many cases of her time. Additionally, her dental wear and displaced incisor suggesting OMR complement the frequent dental pathologies reported in the literature but also provide a distinctive cultural marker. These differences underscore how high-resolution CT can not only detect common paleopathological features but also reveal individual, historic, and cultural variations in mummification practices.

Overall, this case illustrates how high-resolution CT and 3-dimensional reconstruction can provide detailed study of skeletal, dental, and soft tissue features while physically minimizing the disturbing of fragile mummified remains. The Braided Lady demonstrates the important role of radiology in anthropological research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, high-resolution CT and 3-dimensional reconstruction of the Braided Lady allowed a detailed assessment that revealed incomplete evisceration without excerebration, consistent with nonroyal elite status, as well as well-preserved internal structures, dental wear, and evidence of ritual practices such as the displaced incisor associated with OMR. No acute skeletal trauma, advanced degenerative disease, or metabolic bone pathology was observed, suggesting that death was not due to major injury or overt disease, though the exact cause cannot be determined. In spite of the limitations inherent in studying ancient remains, such as tissue desiccation, displacement from transportation, and the absence of clinical history, this radiological case study provides valuable insight into the life, death, and burial practices of the Braided Lady. Ultimately, CT imaging is a valuable, noninvasive method for studying mummified remains and can provide insights into both bioanthropological and cultural aspects of ancient Egyptian life.
