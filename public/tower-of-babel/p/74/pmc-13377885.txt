Use of Plasmapheresis and Cholestyramine in the Management of Thyroid Storm in the Setting of Cardiogenic Shock and Elevated Liver Enzymes: A Case Report and Review of Literature
Authors: Mohanad Qwaider, Nour Hassouna, Mohammad Safi, Moath Elhady, Manali Pragani, Mohammad Alqadi, Qutaiba Qafisheh, Wade Jodeh, Fadi Safi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThyroid storm is a rare, life-threatening endocrine emergency, and management becomes particularly challenging in patients with concurrent cardiac and hepatic dysfunction when standard antithyroid therapy is contraindicated. The objective of this report is to describe a patient with thyroid storm complicated by cardiogenic shock and liver injury who was successfully treated using alternative hormone-lowering strategies.Case ReportA 55-year-old woman with severe ischemic cardiomyopathy presented with syncope and was found to have severe thyrotoxicosis following exposure to amiodarone. Laboratory evaluation revealed a thyroid-stimulating hormone level below 0.01 milli-international units per liter (reference range 0.49-4.67) and a free thyroxine level greater than 7.8 ng per deciliter (reference range 0.61-1.6). She developed encephalopathy, acute kidney injury, and marked liver injury. Despite these findings, blood pressure remained within the normal range. Invasive hemodynamic assessment confirmed cardiogenic shock. Antithyroid medications were withheld because of hepatic dysfunction. Management included corticosteroids, cholestyramine, supportive care, and 3 sessions of plasmapheresis. Following 3 sessions of plasmapheresis, she showed marked biochemical and clinical improvement, with normalization of liver function and recovery of renal function, allowing successful discharge.DiscussionThis case illustrates the diagnostic difficulty of thyroid storm in patients with advanced heart failure and preserved blood pressure and adds to the limited literature on alternative treatment strategies in the setting of organ dysfunction.ConclusionThis case demonstrates that plasmapheresis combined with cholestyramine may be effective in stabilizing patients with severe thyroid storm when conventional antithyroid therapies cannot be used and highlights the need for heightened clinical suspicion in atypical presentations.Key words: amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis, cardiogenic shock, cholestyramine, plasmapheresis, thyroid storm

Clinical Relevance

This case demonstrates that plasmapheresis and cholestyramine can stabilize patients with severe thyroid storm and cardiogenic shock when standard antithyroid therapy is unsafe, emphasizing the need for early recognition and alternative treatment strategies in patients with advanced cardiac and hepatic dysfunction.

Introduction

Thyroid storm (TS) is a rare but life-threatening endocrine emergency characterized by a hyperadrenergic response to severe thyrotoxicosis. Mortality remains approximately 10% to 20% despite modern therapy.1 TS reflects both markedly elevated circulating thyroid hormone levels and increased end-organ responsiveness, leading to rapid multisystem failure. Clinically, TS is classically marked by tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, and central nervous system dysfunction.2,3 As no single laboratory threshold confirms TS, diagnosis remains primarily clinical and is supported by structured tools such as the Burch–Wartofsky Point Scale (BWPS) and the Japan Thyroid Association (JTA) criteria.4 TS is often precipitated by an identifiable trigger, including infection, surgery, abrupt withdrawal of antithyroid therapy, or medication-associated thyrotoxicosis, among which amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis (AIT) represents a particularly relevant and high-risk etiology in patients with underlying cardiovascular disease.

AIT is traditionally classified into 2 phenotypes with distinct mechanisms and treatment implications. Type 1 AIT results from iodine-induced increased thyroid hormone synthesis, typically in patients with underlying autonomy such as Graves’ disease or multinodular goiter. In contrast, Type 2 AIT reflects destructive thyroiditis with release of preformed thyroid hormone from damaged follicular cells. Type 1 generally responds to thionamides, whereas Type 2 is ameliorated with glucocorticoids; mixed forms may require combined strategies and consideration of definitive therapy when clinically feasible.5

In patients with advanced heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), diagnosing TS is challenging, as the hyperdynamic physiology of thyrotoxicosis may be blunted or even replaced by a low-output, high-systemic vascular resistance (SVR) state, masking shock (despite preserved blood pressure). Literature describing TS in severe HFrEF with multiorgan failure is limited, particularly when hepatic and renal dysfunction constrain standard therapy.

Standard therapy targets multiple steps of thyroid hormone synthesis, release, and peripheral action, including thionamides, inorganic iodide, glucocorticoids, and beta-adrenergic blockade, alongside aggressive supportive care and treatment of precipitants.6 However, in critically ill patients with multiorgan failure, particularly acute hepatic injury or renal failure, components of the standard regimen may be unsafe or ineffective.7 In such settings, extracorporeal and adjunctive approaches such as therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) and bile acid sequestrants (eg, cholestyramine) may be considered as bridge therapies to stabilization or definitive treatment.6

We report a case of AIT-associated TS presenting with normotensive cardiogenic shock in severe ischemic cardiomyopathy, successfully managed with a multimodal strategy including TPE and cholestyramine when thionamides were temporarily contraindicated. This case highlights the diagnostic complexities in such scenarios and underscores the utility of plasmapheresis and cholestyramine as life-saving adjunctive therapies when conventional treatments are limited.

Case Report

A 55-year-old woman with a significant medical history, including ischemic cardiomyopathy with a severely reduced ejection fraction (10% to 15%), prior coronary artery bypass grafting, percutaneous coronary intervention, and an automatic implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (AICD) for recurrent ventricular fibrillation, uncontrolled type 2 diabetes mellitus (HbA1c 10.6%) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, presented to an outside hospital after syncope and headache. She was initially brought to an outside emergency department after experiencing sudden onset headache and syncope. She was diagnosed with subarachnoid hemorrhage and managed conservatively. She subsequently developed agitation and experienced multiple AICD shocks, later attributed to atrial arrhythmia. For this, an amiodarone infusion was started.

Considering patient’s tachyarrhythmias, thyroid studies were performed, revealing a thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) level of <0.01 mIU/L (0.49-4.67) and a free thyroxine (FT4) level of >7.8 ng/dL (0.61-1.6). These findings were consistent with severe thyrotoxicosis, raising suspicion of TS, precipitated by IV amiodarone administration. Of note, her TSH receptor-antibodies were elevated, making her course consistent with Type 1 AIT. Patient was initiated on standard TS therapy, including methimazole (MMI) loading, dexamethasone, and propranolol, and transferred to our facility for further specialized management.

Upon arrival at our intensive care unit, she was encephalopathic with a GCS of 10. Her labs were significant for severe transaminases with AST >2600 U/L (≤ 41) and ALT >1600 U/L (≤ 31), hyperammonemia, and oliguric acute kidney injury. Despite these severe systemic manifestations, her blood pressure remained within normal range, 115-132/64, with MAP 81, which was an atypical hemodynamic presentation for TS; often manifesting with hypertensive emergency. In lieu of the patient’s oliguric acute kidney injury, cold extremities, worsening liver function, and HFrEF, she was presumed to be in cardiogenic shock.

Invasive hemodynamic monitoring via right heart catheterization confirmed cardiogenic shock with a low cardiac index (1.5 L/min/m2) accompanied by elevated filling pressures (RA 30 mmHg; PCWP 45 mmHg). SVR was markedly elevated (1275 dyn s/cm5), consistent with compensatory vasoconstriction, providing a mechanistic explanation for preserved arterial pressure despite low forward flow. She required dobutamine and intermittent norepinephrine support, along with intubation and continuous renal replacement therapy for anuria.

TS was supported by formal diagnostic scoring. Based on clinical presentation: encephalopathy, severe heart failure with cardiogenic shock, atrial arrhythmia, clear precipitating event, and severe hepatic dysfunction, the BWPS was ≥75 (Table 1), consistent with TS; she also met JTA criteria for TS1. Given the complex presentation (SAH, arrhythmia, and shock), alternative etiologies for hemodynamic collapse were considered and excluded (including acute coronary syndrome and infection), as summarized in the admission timeline (Tables 2 and 3).

Management of TS

Because of marked acute hepatic injury, consistent with ischemic hepatitis and hyperammonemia, our team avoided further escalation of thionamide therapy. Propylthiouracil was considered particularly high-risk given its well-described association with severe hepatotoxicity. MMI was also withheld in the setting of fulminant transaminase elevation and multiorgan failure, pending hepatic recovery.7 In this context, TPE was selected as an adjunctive hormone-lowering strategy, consistent with guideline-supported rescue therapy for severe thyrotoxicosis/TS when conventional treatments are contraindicated or ineffective.5,8 TPE was performed every 1-2 days for a total of 3 sessions over 5 days, with planned endpoints of (1) downward trend in FT4 levels, (2) clinical stabilization of shock and mental status, and (3) improving hepatic and renal indices; the number of sessions was individualized to balance expected biochemical benefit against the potential hemodynamic risks of extracorporeal therapy in cardiogenic shock.

Concurrently, cholestyramine (4 g four times daily via nasogastric tube) was used to interrupt enterohepatic circulation of thyroid hormones and augment clearance, an approach described in TS management resources and supported by clinical reports showing accelerated decline in thyroid hormone levels when added to standard therapy.6,9 Glucocorticoids were continued (hydrocortisone stress dosing) to reduce peripheral conversion of T4 to T3 and to address potential relative adrenal insufficiency in TS. After initiation of TPE and adjunctive therapy, FT4—initially >7.8 ng/dL (>4.5× the upper limit of normal)—declined substantially, accompanied by improvement in transaminases, ammonia, and end-organ perfusion. Neurological status improved, allowing extubation on hospital day 6.

Despite a transient recurrence of ventricular tachycardia, which necessitated further AICD discharges, the patient's overall condition stabilized. Inotropic support was gradually weaned, and renal function recovered. On hospital day 12, she was stable enough to be transferred to a step-down unit. She was ultimately discharged to a skilled nursing facility for rehabilitation, with comprehensive plans for outpatient follow-up with endocrinology and cardiology specialists. Prior to discharge, she was safely initiated on oral MMI (Figure) and beta-blockers.

Discussion

This case highlights key lessons for diagnosing and treating TS when severe baseline cardiomyopathy blunts the expected “hyperdynamic” picture.

First, TSs can be easy to miss in advanced HFrEF. Marked tachycardia and systolic hypertension may be absent when cardiac reserve is exhausted, and patients instead present with nonspecific decompensation (worsening congestion, arrhythmias, encephalopathy, or hepatorenal dysfunction) that can be misattributed to heart failure or critical illness. In this context, structured criteria (e.g., BWPS and JTA) help integrate multisystem findings and support the diagnosis when individual signs are confounded; in our patient, formal scoring (Table 1) strengthened diagnostic confidence despite an atypical phenotype.4

Second, distinguish the cause of thyrotoxicosis from the trigger of storm. Chronic amiodarone exposure and thyroid studies suggested AIT, with TRAb positivity favoring Type 1 or mixed physiology—important because Type 1 often requires antithyroid therapy whereas Type 2 often requires glucocorticoids.10,11 Separately, storm is often precipitated by acute stress; here, subarachnoid hemorrhage/critical illness plausibly converted compensated thyrotoxicosis into systemic decompensation.2 The pattern of suppressed TSH with markedly elevated FT4 supported true thyrotoxicosis over non-thyroidal illness. Given the broad differential for shock, alternative contributors (ACS, sepsis, arrhythmia-mediated decompensation) should be explicitly assessed and documented alongside TS.

Third, invasive hemodynamics can reveal “masked” cardiogenic shock. Thyrotoxicosis classically lowers SVR and increases cardiac output. However, patients with severe systolic dysfunction may be unable to mount such response. Compensatory vasoconstriction can dominate, producing low cardiac index–high SVR with preserved blood pressure (“normotensive cardiogenic shock”). In our patient, right-heart catheterization firmly confirmed shock—it clarified mechanism and guided cautious support and endocrine rescue rather than relying on vital signs alone.12,13

Fourth, therapy often requires adaptation when organ failure limits standard options. When thionamides and iodide are contraindicated or ineffective, guidelines and expert reviews describe TPE as a rescue option to rapidly reduce circulating, protein-bound thyroid hormones and to serve as a bridge to recovery or definitive therapy.8 The optimal number of exchanges is not standardized; published experiences commonly report 3-6 procedures, individualized to clinical response and biochemical trends.14 In our patient, 3 sessions were selected with predefined endpoints (hemodynamic stabilization, improvement in encephalopathy, and declining FT4), while closely monitoring for volume shifts and electrolyte/coagulation disturbances—particularly relevant in cardiogenic shock and multiorgan failure. Cholestyramine was used concurrently to reduce enterohepatic recirculation of thyroid hormone and may have contributed incremental benefit as an adjunct. Given the multimodal nature of therapy, we have tempered causal language and interpret improvement as temporally associated with the combined strategy rather than attributable to a single intervention. Conclusion

This case highlights that TS can present as normotensive cardiogenic shock in patients with advanced HFrEF, and that formal diagnostic scoring combined with invasive hemodynamics may be essential to avoid missed diagnosis. When standard antithyroid therapy is constrained by hepatic and renal failure, a multimodal strategy—including glucocorticoids, cholestyramine, and consideration of TPE as a bridge—may be reasonable in carefully selected patients with close monitoring for procedure-related risks. Because this is a single-case report and multiple therapies were used, definitive efficacy of any one intervention cannot be concluded; individualized decision-making and multidisciplinary management remain critical.

For patients who are unable to metabolize or tolerate thionamides due to significant organ dysfunction, as seen in this case with severe hepatotoxicity, adjunctive interventions can be lifesaving. Plasmapheresis provides rapid removal of circulating thyroid hormones, while cholestyramine effectively interrupts their enterohepatic recirculation, both contributing to accelerated hormone clearance and clinical improvement.

Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for thyrotoxicosis in heart failure patients presenting with unexplained decompensation and arrhythmias, even when classic hyperdynamic signs are absent. This case reinforces the importance of a multidisciplinary approach and the consideration of aggressive and non-conventional hormone-lowering strategies when managing complex endocrine and cardiovascular emergencies.
