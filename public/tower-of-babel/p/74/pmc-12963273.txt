Immune reconstruction after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation: rules, mechanisms, and applications
Authors: Chen-mo Li, Qiu-yi Wang, Zhi-gang Chen, Cong-cong Wang, Yu-tong Xie, Lei Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune reconstitution (IR) is a critical factor influencing the quality of life and long-term prognosis in hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) recipients. But delayed IR post-transplant may lead to infections and disease recurrence, while impaired reconstitution is closely associated with graft-versus-host disease (GVHD). To formulate strategies that promote immune recovery, it is crucial to understand the fundamental principles and influencing factors of IR. The emergence of technologies such as single-cell sequencing enables researchers to reveal the specific patterns and mechanisms of immune reconstitution under different conditions, which has given us a better understanding of immune reconstitution than before. Current evidence indicates that delayed IR is related to several aspects, mainly the strength of the conditioning regimen, the composition of the graft, and the occurrence of GVHD. This review summarizes the main characteristics of immune reconstitution after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, combined with the latest research progress Carefully explore the influencing factors of immune reconstitution and the existing monitoring methods.Keywords: Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, Immune reconstitution after transplantation, Graft versus host disease, Graft versus leukemia

Introduction

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) is a potentially curative treatment for hematologic malignancies and inherited blood disorders. However, impaired immune reconstitution after transplantation critically influences clinical outcomes, contributing to opportunistic infections [62], disease relapse, and graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) [1]. The key challenge lies in the temporal dissociation between innate and adaptive immune recovery: neutrophils and natural killer cells typically recover within the first three weeks, whereas T- and B-cell reconstitution may require months to years. Moreover, numerical recovery often does not correspond to functional competence. Earlier studies largely focused on restoring immune cell counts, overlooking qualitative immune restoration. In recent years, the disruption of immune homeostasis has become a major research focus. High-dimensional profiling revealed that immune reconstitution encompasses not only quantitative replenishment but also complex processes such as clonal dynamics, epigenetic remodeling, and microenvironmental crosstalk [3]. Although single-cell technologies provide deep mechanistic insights, translating these findings into clinical practice remains challenging due to the gap between basic research and clinical application. The dynamics of post-transplant immune responses are shaped by several key determinants: (1) the integrity of the thymic and bone marrow niches; (2) graft composition, including stem cell source, CD34+ dose, and the balance between regulatory and effector T cells; and (3) external perturbations such as cytomegalovirus reactivation and gut microbiota dysbiosis [4]. This review summarizes recent advances in understanding immune reconstitution after allo-HSCT and systematically discusses its patterns, influencing factors, and clinical implications.

Methodology

This review is based on a literature search of PubMed and Web of Science for articles published between 2002 and 2025. Key search terms included ‘allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation’, ‘immune reconstitution’, ‘GVHD’, ‘graft-versus-leukemia’, and ‘immunotherapy’. We prioritized clinical studies, mechanistic investigations, and translational research with clear implications for clinical practice. Overview of immune reconstitution kinetics

Immune reconstitution (IR) post allo-HSCT follows a predictable yet variable timeline influenced by donor type, conditioning, and post-transplant events. Innate immunity recovers first, with neutrophils and NK cells reaching functional levels within weeks. Adaptive immunity recovers more slowly, with T-cell reconstitution occurring through two main pathways: early peripheral expansion of mature donor T cells and later thymic output of naïve T cells. B-cell recovery is often the slowest, sometimes requiring years for full humoral competence. Innate immune reconstitution

Innate immunity serves as the first line of defense post-transplant, recovering earlier than adaptive immunity and playing key roles in infection control, tumor surveillance, and modulation of GVHD. Reconstitution involves the recovery of epithelial barriers, complement, neutrophils, monocytes/macrophages, NK cells, and dendritic cells (DCs)[5]. DCs, as professional APCs, serve as a bridge between innate and adaptive immunity. Circulating DC precursors are classified into myeloid dendritic cells (mDCs) and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs). mDCs primarily induce Th1-type immune responses, whereas pDCs tend to promote Th2-type responses[7].

IR of NK cells

NK cell reconstitution occurs relatively rapidly following HSCT, typically initiating around day 14 and approaching pre‑transplant levels by day 21 (Fig. 1)[5]. This recovery follows a biphasic pattern: the CD56bright subset emerges first via IL-15 dependent peripheral expansion, while the cytotoxic CD56dim subset matures later, with phenotypic education largely completed by day 28. This divergence reflects the fact that NK cell development and functional maturation primarily take place within the bone marrow microenvironment rather than the thymus [8]. In the context of HLA-haploidentical transplantation, donor-derived NK cells exert alloreactivity through the “missing-self” recognition mechanism, which occurs when recipients lack HLA class I alleles corresponding to donor inhibitory KIR ligands. This NK cell alloreactivity contributes to several clinically beneficial effects: it enhances the GVL response by selectively targeting residual leukemic cells, promotes donor engraftment through lysis of host antigen-presenting cells and T cells, and reduces the risk of GVHD via early clearance of host dendritic cells. The latter effect may permit the administration of higher T-cell doses to support immune reconstitution without exacerbating GVHD [87]. In HLA-matched transplantation, NK cell numbers generally normalize within one month, although full functional reconstitution usually requires approximately three months [10].

IR of Innate lymphoid cells

Innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) are a distinct subset of lymphocytes originating from common lymphoid progenitors that differ functionally and developmentally from B and T cells [11]. They respond rapidly to tissue damage, infection, and inflammation by producing a range of pro-inflammatory and immunoregulatory cytokines.In the setting of allo-HSCT, ILCs have been implicated in mucosal injury induced by conditioning therapy, the pathogenesis of GVHD, and susceptibility to infections. Reconstitution of ILCs in recipients is a prolonged process, requiring up to 6 months to complete. At 3 months post-transplantation, circulating levels of ILC1 and ILC2 remain lower than normal donor values, whereas ILC3 subsets recover to normal ranges by this time point, indicating a predominant role for ILC3 in early ILC reconstitution [12].

Adaptive immune reconstruction

The establishment of adaptive immunity in humans is a continuous and lifelong process, initiated during embryonic development [13]. In early childhood, the immune system matures rapidly upon exposure to environmental antigens and vaccines [14]. Typically, antigen-specific T-cell responses peak within 1–2 weeks, generating effector cells followed by contraction and the formation of long-lived memory subsets [15]. Similarly, T-dependent B-cell responses lead to germinal center formation, affinity maturation [16], and the production of high-affinity antibodies within 2–3 weeks, along with memory B cells and long-lived plasma cells [17]. Beyond the age of sixty, the immune system undergoes progressive senescence, marked by thymic involution and diminished output of naïve T cells [18]. This results in reduced T-cell receptor diversity, a contracted naïve T-cell pool, and a diminished capacity to mount effective responses against new antigens.

T lymphocytes

CD4+/CD8+T cells

The reconstitution of T-cell subsets post-transplantation follows a "dual-timing" model (Fig. 1) [9]. The initial months after transplantation represent an early peripheral expansion phase, during which mature donor T cells from the graft rapidly replenish the lymphodepleted microenvironment via cytokine-driven proliferation [19]. This phase is dominated by CD8+ T cells and characterized by oligoclonal expansion driven by specific antigens such as CMV, leading to a marked reduction in T-cell receptor (TCR) diversity [20]. Notably, the clonal skewing established during this phase is remarkably stable and exerts a lasting influence on long-term immune memory [21]. However, this early, potent antigen-driven expansion presents a double-edged sword. Persistent antigen stimulation, such as CMV reactivation, can lead to functional exhaustion of CD8+ T cells [22]. This exhausted state is closely associated with profound cellular metabolic dysregulation and epigenetic reprogramming—a process involving disrupted mitochondrial function, a shift toward glycolytic metabolism, depletion of key metabolites, and subsequent epigenetic silencing of effector genes, ultimately locking T cells into a dysfunctional state [23]. Transcriptomic analyses at 6 and 12 months post-transplantation revealed no markedly differentially expressed genes between these time points, and compared to healthy controls, differentially expressed genes in patients were consistently underrepresented [24, 25]. Late-phase reconstitution of the T-cell compartment primarily depends on central thymic expansion—a thymic output process whereby donor-derived hematopoietic stem cells generate new naïve T cells in the thymus [26].Lymphoid progenitors from the bone marrow migrate to the thymus, undergo TCR rearrangement and positive/negative selection, and give rise to a self-tolerant and highly diverse T-cell pool [27]. In murine models of fully matched sibling transplantation, CD4 + memory T cells are mainly reconstituted via this central pathway, whereas CD8 + memory T cells expand predominantly through peripheral proliferation [28].A recently identified lymphoid subset, termed CD8Trp, was found to expand more prominently in haploidentical transplants [28]. Its transcriptomic characteristics are very similar to those of treg CD4+, suggesting a potential effect on excessive inflammation and tissue protection. It is interesting to note that the previously discovered CD8+FoxP3+Tregs share some characteristics with CD8Trp cells [30].

Regulatory T Cells

Regulatory T cells (Tregs), a functionally mature subpopulation of CD4+ T cells, are essential for suppressing immune responses and maintaining tolerance through cell-contact mechanisms and inhibitory cytokines. In patients undergoing allo-HSCT, Tregs help modulate T-cell activity, reducing the incidence of moderate-to-severe GVHD while preserving the GVL effect [33]. Tregs are functionally heterogeneous and can be categorized into distinct subsets. Thymus-derived Tregs (tTregs) exhibit stable suppressive activity [34], whereas peripherally induced Tregs (pTregs) are critical for establishing mucosal immune tolerance [35]. Further stratification based on surface markers and transcription factors—such as naïve (CD45RA+) and memory (CD45RO+) Tregs, as well as subsets expressing Helios, IRF4, or STAT3—reveals dynamic post-transplant changes in these populations. Each subset differentially influences the clinical balance between GVHD and GVL. Preclinical studies demonstrated that Tregs rely on a distinct metabolic profile to maintain their functional stability, while a set of epigenetic regulators ensures the expression of key identity genes such as FOXP3. Disruption of this regulatory axis–such as aberrant FOXP3 hypermethylation associated with gain-of-function DNMT3A mutations–has been linked to impaired Treg function in preclinical models [36]. These epigenetic alterations may represent one potential mechanism underlying Treg dysfunction in patients with chronic GVHD [38].

B lymphocytes

Reconstitution of humoral immunity mediated by B cells is a prolonged process, often requiring up to five years for complete recovery. During the initial months post-transplantation, very few B cells are detectable (Fig. 1). Although B cell counts can return to normal levels within 1–2 years, they often decline after that [39]. Clinical results confirmed that the recovery of B cells is mainly achieved through the expansion of bone marrow progenitor cells. This includes the regeneration of CD19+CD27− naive B cells and CD19+CD27+memory B cells. The recovery of CD19⁺CD27⁺ memory B cells depends on antigen exposure and CD4⁺ T cell help, hence their reconstitution generally reaches normal levels only after two years[39]. Following myeloablative allo-HSCT, CD19⁺CD27⁻ naïve B cells typically normalize within six months and exceed normal levels by 24 months. In contrast, CD19⁺CD27⁺ memory B cells begin to recover gradually only after two years. This delayed memory B cell recovery may be attributed to impaired CD4⁺ T cell reconstitution and a persistent inversion of the CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T cell ratio. Delayed B-cell reconstitution is often accompanied by epigenetic alterations and microenvironmental imbalances. Preclinical evidence suggested that mutations in hematopoietic stem cells(HSCs) may disrupt B-cell differentiation and memory formation [40]. Meanwhile, in the post-transplant bone marrow microenvironment, elevated BAFF levels and reduced April expression created a cytokine imbalance. This imbalance promoted the proliferation and activation of autoreactive B cells, which may exacerbate pathological damage in chronic GVHD [41]. Furthermore, the presence of a “rapid B-cell reconstitution” subset in some patients suggests that factors such as microbiota or cytokine profiles may influence the heterogeneity of immune recovery, offering potential clues for clinical intervention[42].

Immune reconstitution of the adaptive immune system is a phased and multi-pathway dynamic process. T-cell recovery follows a "dual-timing" model: early reconstitution relies on the peripheral expansion of mature donor T cells, whereas later recovery depends on thymic output of naïve T cells. B-cell reconstitution is the slowest, often requiring years to restore full humoral immunity. Understanding these distinct patterns is essential for developing targeted strategies to promote functional immune restoration.

Determinants of immune reconstitution

T cell reconstitution is partially dependent on thymic function; thus, factors affecting the thymus can directly or indirectly impair T cell recovery. The intensity of preconditioning and GVHD primarily contribute to delayed T cell reconstitution by inducing thymic damage(Fig. 1) [43]. Notably, GVHD itself causes severe thymic dysfunction, and immunosuppressive agents used for GVHD treatment, such as cyclosporine, may further exacerbate thymic injury [44], collectively delaying T cell reconstitution.

Thymic impairment is considered a key factor in delayed T-cell reconstitution. In preclinical models, activation of the BMP4-GPR39 axis had been shown to promote thymic regeneration and enhance T-cell output [45], suggesting a potential therapeutic target for improving thymic function in transplant recipients. Specifically, Zn2+or small-molecule agonists bound to the G-protein coupled receptor GPR39, activating the Gαq subunit and triggering downstream PLCβ-PKC signaling [46]. Animal studies indicated that GPR39 agonists can enhance thymic function in aged mice, highlighting their potential therapeutic utility [45]. Furthermore, thymic epithelial cells often undergo epigenetic dysregulation during aging, such as loss of heterochromatin and reduction in repressive histone marks [47]. These changes promote the expression of adipogenic genes while suppressing key thymic developmental factors, which may further exacerbate thymic atrophy and hinder T-cell reconstitution [45].

Different graft sources, the recovery of lymphocyte subsets post-HSCT is also different [48]. A study by Storek J et al. showed that between bone marrow transplant(BMT) and peripheral blood stemcell transplant(PBSCT), patients receiving PB grafts had faster lymphocyte reconstruction, with CD4+ T-cells being the most significant. The number of nucleated cells in UCB grafts was 10 ~ 100 times lower than that of BM. The results of Taiki Ando et al. showed that the median values of ALC, CD201 B-cell, CD4+T-cell subsets and NK cell subsets (CD3−CD56+, CD16+CD57−) were significantly higher than those of PBSCT and BMT at 100 days after UCT, while CD8+ T-cell subsets and CD3+CD56+ NK-cells were significantly less[49]. Dual transplantation of peripheral blood and bone marrow hematopoietic stem cells is also a method taken to combine the advantages of different components in the two grafts [50].

Clinical studies have revealed that adaptive IR after haploidentical transplantation is a dynamic and protocol-dependent process[2]. In the Beijing protocol, ATG damages thymic stromal cells, resulting in reduced naïve CD4+ T cells (CD45RA+) and an early dominance of memory T cells (CD45RO+). In contrast, the post-transplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy) regimen selectively depletes proliferating effector T cells while preserving resting Tregs, leading to faster recovery of CD4+ T cells [29].

A recent real-world study indicated that female recipients exhibit higher B-cell numbers in the late post-transplant phase than males, suggesting a potential role of sex hormones in B-cell development and functional recovery [39]. However, the common occurrence of premature ovarian insufficiency after allo-HSCT challenges this hypothesis [51]. Further investigations measuring hormone levels and IR parameters are required to clarify this relationship. Clinical studies have demonstr ated age significantly influences B-cell reconstitution [52]. Children demonstrate markedly faster B-lymphocyte recovery compared to adults [53]. These differences may be attributed to thymic and bone marrow function. The thymus undergoes age-related involution; although preconditioning causes damage, thymic recovery in children remains more robust than in adults. Additionally, aging alters the bone marrow microenvironment, leading to functional decline that impairs B-cell regeneration [54]. Moreover, the use of immunosuppressive agents such as ATG may further delay B cell recovery [2].

Age-related epigenetic reprogramming may affect the lymphoid differentiation potential of HSCs. The Kitlow HSC subgroup is characterized by low expression of the Kit stem cell factor receptor and the maintenance of a unique "lymphoid-primed chromatin landscape" by the transcription factor ZBTB1 [31]. This epigenetic state is marked by the opening of chromatin regions rich in binding sites for specific lymphatic transcription factors such as Runx3 and Zeb2 enhancers, this structure allows Kitlo HSC to differentiate primarily into lymphoprogenitors and strengthen its thymic homonization capacity. However, with age, the proportion of Kitlo HSC decreased significantly and chromatin accessibility shifts toward the myeloid lineage, resulting a more than 50% reduction in T cell production [32].

Immune reconstitution is regulated by multiple factors, primarily including thymic function, graft source, conditioning intensity, GVHD and its treatment, as well as patient age and sex. Thymic impairment is a central mechanism underlying delayed T-cell recovery. Different graft sources—such as bone marrow, peripheral blood, and umbilical cord blood—significantly influence the pace and pattern of lymphocyte subset recovery.

Clinical consequences– relapse, GVHD and infection

GVHD represents a leading cause of non-relapse morbidity and mortality following HSCT. It is driven by hyperactivated donor T cells that target host tissues and is classified as either acute or chronic. Acute GVHD (aGVHD) is a systemic inflammatory disorder mediated by alloreactive T cells, commonly involving the skin, gastrointestinal tract, and liver [55]. IR plays an important role in the onset, development of aGVHD. GVHD can also impede immune recovery; for instance, B-cell reconstitution within the first eight weeks post-transplant is markedly impaired in patients with aGVHD. Early recovery of CD4+T cells is associated with reduced non-relapse mortality and improved event-free and overall survival, underscoring the protective role of timely CD4+T-cell reconstitution against Agvhd [55].

Patients with chronic GVHD(cGVHD) frequently exhibit immune dysregulation, mainly because their T and B cells do not function properly [56]. Studies showed that poor ischemia and prolonged post-transplantation lymphopenia increase susceptibility to Tregs apoptosis and are associated with a higher incidence of cGVHD [57]. PTCy regimen reduces the cGVHD by promoting priority Tregs expansion [58]. Experimental reports show that more than half of patients improved symptoms of cGVHD after recovery of Tregs homeostasis by treatment with IL-2 [59], further reinforcing the role of Tregs in cGVHD. Impaired early reconstruction of naive B cells is also associated with the onset and development of cGVHD [60]. This subgroup leads to increased B-cell reactivity against antigens after a delay in recovery and may also allow better B-cell survival, which may cause a greater number of autoantibodies against cGVHD target organs. Symptoms of cGVHD did improve after the removal of B cells with rituxan, suggesting that B cells play a greater role in the development of cGVHD [61].

The imbalance of T cell subset reconstruction is the core mechanism of GVHD. The study of allo-HSCT revealed that aGVHD patients showed an imbalance between CD4+ TEM amplification and Th1/Th2, while cGVHD was characterized by CD8+ TEM cell depletion and Treg dysfunction. This imbalance stems from two-pathway competition: thymus injury forces peripheral expansion to dominate reconstruction, producing short-lived effector T cells. Notably, older children compensated for thymic atrophy by enhancing HPE, but failed to improve functional diversity. Clinical interventions need to target thymus regeneration simultaneously with subpopulation regulation (Tregs infusion).

Post-transplant relapse is a multifactorial process driven by tumor-intrinsic escape mechanisms, dysregulation of the immune microenvironment, and epigenetic alterations. Relapsing tumor cells frequently downregulate HLA class I/II expression to evade T-cell recognition and upregulate immune-checkpoint ligands such as PD-L1 and CD155[86], promoting T-cell exhaustion[64]. Mechanistically, this may result from defects in the interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) signaling pathway or epigenetic silencing of antigen presentation-related genes [63]. CD47 is highly expressed on leukemia stem cells and binds to SIRPα on macrophages, inhibiting phagocytosis and facilitating immune escape [65]. Delayed functional T cell reconstitution also contributes significantly to relapse. Concurrently, impaired functional reconstitution of adaptive immunity contributes to relapse: CD8+T cells often exhibit a terminally exhausted phenotype [66], and the T-cell receptor repertoire loses diversity while becoming enriched for clones targeting non-tumor antigens (e.g. CMV) [67]. Furthermore, diminished NK-cell cytotoxicity and deficiencies in cytokines such as IL-15 and IL-7 weaken innate and memory-cell surveillance, compromising the GVL effect [64, 68].

The quality of immune reconstitution directly influences three major clinical outcomes after transplantation: GVHD, relapse, and infection. Imbalanced recovery of T-cell subsets(such as overactivated effector T cells)is central to GVHD development. Delayed functional T-cell reconstitution, reduced NK-cell activity, and epigenetic alterations contribute to leukemia relapse. Early immune deficiency increases the risk of opportunistic infections. Therefore, monitoring and optimizing the immune reconstitution process may represent a key strategy for improving transplant outcomes.

Therapeutic strategies to modulate immune reconstitution

Graft optimization

Professor Huang Xiaojun first proposed and systematically elucidated the concept of graft optimization [69]. Initially, allogeneic graft optimization aimed not to enhance IR, but rather to improve engraftment rates and reduce the incidence of severe GVHD— though often at the cost of delayed immune recovery. T-cell depletion is now a well-established graft optimization strategy. Common approaches include CD8⁺ T-cell depletion, αβ TCR⁺ T-cell depletion [70], and CD45RA⁺ T-cell depletion, all of which have demonstrated favorable clinical benefits. Among these, TCRαβ/CD19⁺ cell depletion is considered a promising transplant strategy that facilitates rapid reconstitution of NK and γδ T cells [71]. Treg-enriched grafts have shown promise in reducing GVHD while supporting immune recovery. Multiple clinical studies demonstrated that Tregs can prevent GVHD and promote lymphoid system reconstruction without compromising the GVL effect, even in the absence of post-transplant immunosuppression [74, 88, 89].

MSCs co-transplantation

MSCs can bidirectionally regulate T cell responses [6]. They can inhibit the excessive activation and proliferation of CD4+ Th1/Th17 cells and CD8 + cytotoxic T lymphocytes [73]. MSCs also rely on cell contact-dependent mechanisms to promote the generation and expansion of Tregs, and secrete cytokines such as TGF-βand IL-10 which play a core role in maintaining immune tolerance and inhibiting GVHD [75]. These mechanisms have been supported by numerous preclinical and clinical observations.

To achieve better therapeutic effects, new engineering and pretreatment strategies are constantly emerging. Mesenchymal stem cells are modified with bispecific polyvalent peptides that simulate PSGL-1 on the surface of neutrophils to enhance their localization ability to the inflammatory site, and can inhibit excessive neutrophil infiltration [76]. Also, there is a strategy called “training immunity”, such as treating MSCs with TNF-α and IFN-γ in a certain sequence. This can enable MSCs to acquire a form of “inflammatory memory”, so that when they are stimulated again, the immune regulatory response can become stronger [77]. The application of MSCs based on their immunosuppressive and tolerogenic properties has entered the stage of clinical implementation, while further engineering and preconditioning strategies remain largely in the phase of experimental model development and validation, aimed at building more optimized therapeutic platforms in the future.

To enhance targeting precision, chimeric antigen receptor-engineered MSCs (CAR-MSCs) have been developed in preclinical studies, showing improved site-specific migration and immunomodulation in animal models[37]. While these engineered cells hold promise, their safety and efficacy in human transplant settings remain unexplored.

Donor CAR-T cell combination therapy

Donor-derived CAR-T cells demonstrate unique advantages in promoting IR and preventing relapse after allo-HSCT[72].Upon infusion, donor CAR-T cells rapidly expand, bridging the “immune window” before full T-cell reconstitution post-transplant, making them particularly suitable for high-risk patients with early relapse [78]. These cells can be manufactured either from pre-transplant cryopreserved donor lymphocytes or from fully chimeric donor-derived cells obtained post-transplant. Studies indicated that donor CAR-T cells may secrete cytokines such as IL-2 and IFN-γ, activating innate immune cells and indirectly promoting recovery of the immune ecosystem [79]. Donor-derived CD19 CAR-T therapy in patients with relapsed B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) after transplantation achieved a complete remission (CR) rate of 78.6% and a one-year overall survival (OS) rate of 56%[83].

To overcome HLA restrictions and avoid GVHD, universal off-the-shelf strategies involving knockout of TCRαβ and HLA class I/II molecules via gene editing have been developed, though early-generation products suffered from poor persistence in vivo [81]. A notable advance described a “glycan shield” strategy: using CRISPR screening to knockout the glycosylation regulator gene SPPL3, resulting in a dense layer of glycosylation on the surface of CAR-T cells. Donor-derived CAR-T cell therapy represents a promising strategy for patients with impaired IR or relapse after allo-HSCT.

Multiple strategies are currently available to modulate post-transplant immune reconstitution. These primarily include graft optimization, co-transplantation of mesenchymal stromal cells, infusion of donor-derived CAR-T/CAR-NK cells, as well as approaches targeting thymic regeneration and epigenetic modulation. The authors believe that the future direction lies in integrating multi-omics monitoring with engineered cell therapies to enable end-to-end management from risk assessment to precision intervention.

Monitoring approaches and biomarkers

Quantitative monitoring of TREC analysis

TCR excision circles (TRECs) serve as a surrogate marker for thymic-derived CD4⁺CD45RA⁺ naïve T cells and can be used to evaluate thymic output [84]. A study by Athina-Soragia Gkazi et al. demonstrated a correlation between T-cell counts and TREC levels in the peripheral blood of post-transplant patients [85]. TRECs are episomal DNA fragments generated during TCR gene rearrangement in the thymus and are retained in each newly produced naïve T cell [84]. Quantitative PCR-based measurement of TRECs provides an estimate of recent thymic emigrants, enabling indirect assessment of thymic output, evaluation of CD4+T-cell reconstitution, and monitoring of cellular immune recovery, thereby supporting prognostic evaluation and clinical management. TCR Diversity monitoring (Immune Pool)

Human adaptive immunity is mainly mediated by the specific binding of T-cell receptors(TCR) on T lymphocytes and B-cell receptors(BCR) on B lymphocytes to peptide-MHC complexes [86]. TCR diversity can be monitored through TCR immuno sequencing [85], a method that that can precisely characterize and quantify the clone types of individual T cells. Since the complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) of the TCR gene varies in length and nucleotide sequence across different T-cell clones, profiling CDR3 sequences allows assessment of TCR repertoire diversity and recovery. Jeroen W.J. van Heijst et al. were the first to perform quantitative analysis of TCR diversity in patients after allo-HSCT [80], accurately determining individual TCR clonal frequencies. Their findings supportted the value of quantitative TCR repertoire analysis in predicting prognosis and preventing infections and relapse. Since conventional spectratyping does not provide data on individual clonotypes, Athina-Soragia Gkazi et al. applied a next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based approach to profile CDR3 regions [85]. NGS not only reproduces and quantifies inferences from spectratyping but also offers novel insights into immune recovery.

CyTOF detects the accurate typing of immune cells after transplantation.

The central strength of CyTOF is its ability to perform phenotypic analyses of individual cells with very high parameters in a single test, which makes it possible to identify new subpopulations or functional states not detectable by conventional cytometry and to discover predictive characteristics related to clinical outcomes [43]. A recent sequential follow-up study of post-transplant patients showed a significant increase in circulating granzyme K (GZMK)+CD8+CCR5-expressing T cells, using CyTOF in combination with other techniques, in patients with occlusive bronchiolitis syndrome (occlusive bronchiolitis syndrome) associated with chronic GVHD. Cloned GZMK+CD8+T cells are biomarkers of the occurrence of BOS after allo-HSCT [82]. CyTOF allows simultaneous detection of cytocynes, Phosflow signaling proteins and transcription factors, allowing a direct assessment of cellular functional status [43]. Gournay et al. used CyTOF to define a subpopulation of strongly diminished T cells based on co-expression of transcription factors (T-bet, EOMES) and inhibitory receptors (PD-1, TIGIT, TIM-3) in T cells after transplantation [43]. This population was highly enriched in recurrence patients and morphological evidence of its onset even prior to recurrence provides a critical window for early intervention. The high-dimensional data generated by CyTOF can be used to build predictive models, define "ideal" IR states, and ultimately help assess clinical prognosis and customize intervention strategies.

Future perspectives

Immune reconstruction is a multidimensional and dynamic process that directly impacts patient survival and quality of life. Successful reconstruction requires not only restoring cellular numbers but also rebuilding functional diversity. Asynchronous recovery of T and B cells, impaired thymic output function, microbiota imbalance, and metabolic disorders collectively contribute to delayed reconstruction and related complications. Future research should integrate multi-omics approaches with clinical indicators to develop dynamic predictive models for early identification, risk stratification, and intervention. Novel strategies such as graft optimization, engineered mesenchymal stem cells, donor-derived CAR-T cells/CAR-NKcells, thymus regeneration, and epigenetic regulation show significant potential. The ultimate goal is to establish a comprehensive system from pre-transplant risk assessment to postoperative monitoring and precision interventions, achieving coordinated recovery of immune system quantity, function, and tolerance to comprehensively enhance transplant efficacy.
