More than an OPTION: left atrial appendage closure beyond high bleeding risk in the post-ablation setting
Authors: Marco Schiavone, Luigi Di Biase
Published: 2026

This editorial refers to “Percutaneous left atrial appendage closure following catheter ablation therapy of atrial fibrillation: outcomes stratified by bleeding risk—a sub-analysis of the OPTION study” K. Phillips et al. https://doi.org/10.1093/eupace/euag093

Over the past decade, left atrial appendage closure (LAAC) has progressively moved from a niche alternative for patients with contraindications to oral anticoagulation (OAC) to a broader therapeutic option across different clinical scenarios.1–3 The randomized OPTION trial4 marked a critical step in this evolution by evaluating LAAC as a concomitant procedure with AF ablation, demonstrating non-inferiority for thromboembolic prevention and superiority for bleeding compared with OAC. In this context, the present pre-specified sub-analysis of OPTION presented in this issue of Europace,5 provides an important additional perspective by examining outcomes according to baseline bleeding risk. The authors report the impact of HAS-BLED score on clinical outcomes in the 1600 patients enrolled in OPTION. Patients with AF undergoing catheter ablation and at increased thromboembolic risk were randomized to LAAC or continued OAC and stratified according to HAS-BLED categories (0, 1, 2, and ≥3). As expected, event rates for the composite endpoint of death, stroke, or systemic embolism increased progressively with higher HAS-BLED scores, but remained similar between treatment arms across all strata (interaction P = 0.73). In contrast, non-procedural bleeding—the primary safety endpoint—was consistently lower with LAAC, reaching statistical significance in HAS-BLED 0, 1, and ≥3 groups (all Gray’s test P < 0.05), while showing a non-significant numerical reduction in the intermediate-risk group (HAS-BLED 2; P = 0.26 when accounting for competing risk). The reduction in bleeding was largely driven by clinically relevant non-major bleeding events and appeared most pronounced in lower-risk patients, an observation specifically highlighted by the authors. However, the study was not powered for subgroup comparisons, and no significant interaction across HAS-BLED strata was demonstrated. Rather than redefining indications, these findings highlight an important concept: bleeding risk is a continuum, and the benefit of avoiding long-term anticoagulation may extend beyond the highest-risk categories.

This analysis raises a central question: how should LAAC be positioned across the heterogeneous spectrum of AF populations? First, the authors should be commended for stratifying outcomes according to HAS-BLED score. This study reinforces the concept that bleeding risk is not confined to a high-risk subgroup but progressively increases across categories. This is particularly relevant in the context of LAAC, a therapy whose primary advantage lies in reducing long-term exposure to anticoagulation. Thus, the observation that bleeding reduction with LAAC extends even to lower-risk patients is not paradoxical, but rather consistent with the cumulative nature of anticoagulation-related bleeding over time. Even patients considered ‘low risk’ at baseline may accrue clinically meaningful bleeding events during lifelong therapy.

Second, the concomitant setting of OPTION represents a uniquely favourable scenario for LAAC. Combining catheter ablation and LAAC within a single procedural pathway has important practical implications.6 This is particularly relevant in patients with long-standing persistent AF, in whom adjunctive strategies such as LAA electrical isolation may be required, thereby further supporting a combined procedural approach, as recently reported.7 Importantly, this strategy may also mitigate early complications: performing LAAC in the same session avoids staged interventions that expose patients to repeated procedural risk.

Third, the study highlights an important limitation of risk scores. While HAS-BLED effectively stratifies bleeding risk at the population level, it does not capture all dimensions of clinical vulnerability. Factors such as frailty, comorbidity burden, or anticipated adherence to anticoagulation are not fully reflected. The absence of a significant interaction across HAS-BLED strata in this analysis reinforces the idea that treatment decisions should not rely exclusively on predefined thresholds. Instead, LAAC should be considered within a broader clinical framework that integrates procedural feasibility, patient preference, and long-term risk trajectories, especially in selected procedural contexts such as post-ablation patients.

When placed within the broader landscape of contemporary trials, this interpretation becomes more informative. Similar signals emerge from CHAMPION-AF8: LAAC achieved non-inferiority for thromboembolic outcomes while significantly reducing bleeding at mid-term follow-up. Importantly, CHAMPION-AF confirms that the benefit of LAAC is not restricted to patients with contraindications to anticoagulation, but may extend to those in whom anticoagulation is otherwise considered acceptable. In both OPTION and CHAMPION-AF, the clinical value of LAAC is driven less by its ability to reduce bleeding over time. In contrast, the CLOSURE-AF trial9 offers a complementary perspective. In this study, LAAC did not demonstrate non-inferiority compared with best medical therapy. However, this finding should not be viewed as contradictory, but rather as reflective of a different clinical context. The population enrolled in CLOSURE-AF was characterized by a higher overall clinical risk profile, with a greater burden of comorbidities and competing risks, which are likely to influence both early and long-term outcomes (baseline HAS-BLED in the LAAC groups CLOSURE-AF 3.1 ± 0.9; CHAMPION-AF 1.3 ± 0.8, OPTION 1.2 ± 0.8). In this setting, the upfront procedural risk of LAAC—including periprocedural complications—may offset part of the expected long-term benefit. Moreover, in CHAMPION-AF, the primary safety endpoint focused on non-procedure-related bleeding and therefore did not capture early procedural events. As a result, differences in endpoint definitions should be carefully considered when interpreting the net clinical benefit across trials, especially in higher-risk populations where early hazards may weigh more heavily. This is further supported by the higher rates of procedural major bleeding observed in CLOSURE-AF compared with OPTION, which may partly reflect differences in device selection and procedural factors across studies.

The OPTION trial reflects a fundamentally different population. Patients undergoing catheter ablation represent a selected group in whom oral anticoagulation is not only feasible but mandatory in the peri-procedural period. This implies the absence of absolute contraindications to anticoagulation and identifies a cohort with a more favourable clinical profile, in whom both ablation and adjunctive strategies can be safely considered. In this context, LAAC is evaluated primarily as a strategy to reduce long-term bleeding risk (as this subanalysis demonstrates) rather than as an alternative in patients unable to tolerate anticoagulation. LAAC is not a zero-risk intervention, and its net clinical benefit depends on the balance between early procedural risk and long-term protection from thromboembolism and bleeding. Taken together, OPTION, CHAMPION-AF, and CLOSURE-AF should be viewed not as conflicting trials, but as complementary evidence across different clinical scenarios. OPTION and CHAMPION-AF address settings in which anticoagulation is feasible and relatively well tolerated, making long-term bleeding reduction a key determinant of benefit. In contrast, CLOSURE-AF reflects a population in whom higher baseline and procedural risk modify the overall therapeutic balance.

Real-world data further support this context-dependent interpretation. Large registries such as EWOLUTION10 have demonstrated that LAAC can be performed with high procedural success in routine clinical practice, even outside highly selected trial populations. However, these data also highlight the importance of patient selection. In EWOLUTION, a cohort characterized by advanced age and substantial comorbidity burden (mean CHA2DS2-VASc ≈4.5, HAS-BLED ≈2.3), approximately one in six patients died within 2 years, with a large proportion of deaths driven by non-cardiovascular causes and therefore the long-term benefit of LAAC postulated by this OPTION sub-analysis is difficult to obtain. Instead, the prospective RECORD registry11 further supports the potential value of a combined strategy, showing that LAAO plus ablation was associated with a lower risk of cardiovascular death, stroke, or systemic embolism at 3 years compared with LAAO alone, without an increase in bleeding events. A large propensity-matched analysis with 5-year follow-up12 reported comparable rates of stroke, major bleeding, and mortality between LAAO alone and combined LAAO with ablation, suggesting that the choice of a combined strategy should be guided by procedural considerations and patient characteristics rather than an expectation of improved long-term outcomes. Observational and meta-analytic data focusing on specific high-risk populations provide additional nuance. In an individual patient-level meta-analysis including over 24 000 patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD),13 there was no significant association between ESRD and stroke/TIA incidence (HR, 1.22; 95% CI, 0.66–2.26), but major bleeding was higher on patients with ESRD (HR, 1.65; 95% CI, 1.01–2.69) after LAAC, that was also associated with an increased risk of complications. A similar pattern emerges when considering advanced age.14 In a large nationwide analysis including more than 80 000 procedures, octogenarians and even nonagenarians had no significant increase in in-hospital mortality or stroke, nor in most major complications, compared with younger patients. However, vascular complications were modestly but consistently higher (aOR ≈1.5–1.6), suggesting greater procedural vulnerability despite preserved overall safety. On the other hand, patients with AF and major gastrointestinal bleeding who underwent LAAC achieved superior outcomes, including fewer major bleeding and stroke events with improved survival in patients when compared to continued OAC.15

Taken together, the available evidence suggests that LAAC is not one thing for all AF patients. Its clinical value depends critically on context. In post-ablation patients with relatively low bleeding risk, as highlighted in this OPTION subanalysis, LAAC may serve as a strategy to reduce cumulative bleeding exposure over time within an integrated rhythm-control pathway. The challenge moving forward is not to expand LAAC indiscriminately, but to better define where it provides true incremental value. The present analysis moves the field in that direction, but also reminds us that patient selection—rather than technology alone—will ultimately determine clinical success.
