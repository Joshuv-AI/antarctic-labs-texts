Hemodialysis Effect During Ex Vivo Lung Perfusion
Authors: Kentaro Nakata, Qimeng Gao, Isaac Alderete, Ahmed Hassan, Nihal Aykun, Riley H. Kahan, Benjamin A. Hughes, Charles J. Arnold, John M. Carney, Andrew S. Barbas, Matthew G. Hartwig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProlonged lung preservation by ex vivo lung perfusion (EVLP) is an important objective in lung transplantation. Maintaining stable perfusate composition is key, and integrated dialysis is a potential method. We examined the suitability of integrated hemodialysis with EVLP to maintain perfusate homeostasis and extend perfusion time.MethodsLungs were procured from 30- to 40-kg Yorkshire pigs. The clinical-grade TransMedics OCS Lung system was used with perfusate consisting of autologous washed red blood cells and TransMedics OCS solution. Hemodialysis was added to the venous return line of the device and begun 1 hour after the start of perfusion. Physiological variables and blood gas data were monitored during perfusion, and perfusate cytokine levels were evaluated at prespecified times.ResultsHemodialysis moderately stabilized electrolyte levels and reduced lactate, but promoted the more rapid development of edema, resulting in shorter mean preservation time in (control [n = 8] vs hemodialysis [n = 6]: 24 hours vs 7.5 hours, P = .034). Using a high-sodium dialysate (n = 5) reduced edema formation but did not significantly extend lung preservation. Notably, significant upregulation of interferon-γ (P < .001) and interleukin 8 (P = .005) was observed in this group, as well as upregulation of the anti-inflammatory cytokine interleukin 10 (P = .0037).ConclusionsIntegrating hemodialysis into an EVLP circuit was detrimental to graft preservation, leading to progressive edema and reduced graft survival. Despite stabilizing circulating electrolyte concentrations, adding dialysis resulted in proinflammatory cytokine upregulation, further reflecting declining graft viability. These findings demonstrate the standard hemodialysis protocol is inadequate for perfusate reconditioning during long-term EVLP, requiring modification for this application.

In Short ▪Integrating standard hemodialysis into ex vivo lung perfusion stabilized electrolytes but accelerated pulmonary edema, significantly shortening graft preservation time compared with controls.▪High-sodium dialysate reduced edema formation but did not improve overall graft survival and was associated with increased proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokine expression.▪Conventional dialysis protocols are not directly transferable to ex vivo lung perfusion and require modification to prevent endothelial injury and preserve graft viability.

Many donor lungs experience acute injuries before transplantation, such as aspiration, warm ischemia, or trauma, compromising viability and increasing the risk of postoperative complications such as primary graft dysfunction. Consequently, lung transplantation remains limited by low donor yield. Worldwide, only 20% to 30% of organ donors ultimately become lung donors.1 Thus, there is a critical need to expand the donor pool, and ex vivo lung perfusion (EVLP) may play a key role by enabling viability assessment and potential graft rehabilitation.2

Although clinical EVLP systems typically operate for <6 hours, preclinical studies suggest that extended perfusion engages cellular repair mechanisms that enhance graft function.3 However, prolonged EVLP can lead to metabolic waste accumulation, increased vascular resistance, and declining lung compliance. Efficient waste elimination and electrolyte stabilization are essential for achieving multiday EVLP.

Previous studies of hemodialysis during EVLP have yielded mixed results. An early study using a negative-pressure system found no benefit and increased vascular resistance,4 whereas a subsequent study with a 6-hour acellular EVLP protocol showed effective lactate clearance but unclear effects on lung function and inflammation.5

In this study, we evaluated integrated hemodialysis with a clinically approved, positive-pressure ventilated OCS Lung device (TransMedics) using a perfusate supplemented with red blood cells. We hypothesized that hemodialysis would maintain perfusate homeostasis, eliminate metabolic waste, and significantly extend viable organ preservation.

Material and Methods

Animal Care and Use Statement

All experiments were conducted using procedures approved by the Duke University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and in accordance with the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.” Animals were housed in an American Association for Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care–certified animal housing facility with free access to water and food.

Ex-Vivo Lung Perfusion (EVLP) Methods

Lungs and blood were harvested from 30- to 40-kg female pigs (N = 19) and assigned to control (n = 8), hemodialysis (n = 6), or high-sodium (HiNa) dialysate (n = 5) groups. The lungs were placed on a TransMedics OCS Lung device. Perfusion was terminated when Pao2/fraction of inspired oxygen (Fio2) ratio persistently dropped <300. Complete methods for lung procurement and EVLP can be found in the Supplemental Material.

Hemodialysis Circuit

Perfusate was drawn from the pulmonary artery (PA) inflow line using a MasterFlexL/S roller pump (Cole-Parmer Instrument Co) at 200 mL/min, consistent with clinical guidelines of 5 to 7 mL/kg/min,6 through a xevonta Hi23 hemodialysis filter (B. Braun Medical Inc), with dialysate passed retrograde to perfusate flow. Dialysate inflow and waste were started at 20 mL/min and adjusted to maintain a stable fluid level in the reservoir. Two treatment arms with different dialysate compositions were used. Both dialysates consisted of 4 mEq potassium and 3 mEq calcium. One dialysis-EVLP (Di-EVLP) arm had normal sodium levels (140 mEq), and the other (HiNa-EVLP) had higher sodium levels (160 mEq).

Histologic Analysis

Hematoxylin and eosin-stained slides were graded by a blinded lung transplant pathologist. Full methods for sample collection, preservation, and analysis procedures can be found in the Supplemental Material.

Statistical Analysis

GraphPad Prism 10.1.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc) was used for statistical analysis. Values are presented as mean (SD). Analysis of variance, Mantel-Cox log-rank test, and mixed-effects modeling were used as appropriate, with P < .05 considered significant. Additional details are described in the Supplemental Material.

Physiological Effects of Hemodialysis During EVLP

The Pao2/Fio2 ratio was used as a measure of pulmonary function and was the experimental cutoff, such that a persistent decline <300 mm Hg signaled termination of the experiment. Owing to progressive loss of lungs, we confined our statistical analyses of physiological variables to the first 6 hours, during which all lungs but 1 in the Di-EVLP group are represented. Analysis of the change in Pao2/Fio2 ratio from baseline levels at 1 hour revealed a significant main effect of treatment and interaction of treatment by time (Figure 1C). Post hoc comparisons to C-EVLP, though, only revealed a significant increase in Pao2/Fio2 ratio in the Di-EVLP group at 5 hours (P = .015). Similar to the Pao2/Fio2 ratio, dynamic compliance remained stable across the 3 cohorts during perfusion. However, confining analysis to the first 6 hours of perfusion revealed a significant main effect of time on the percentage change in dynamic compliance from baseline, but no apparent differences among groups (Figure 1D).

Perfusion variables, including pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) and PA mean arterial pressure (PA-MAP) were also analyzed. PVR displayed a significant increase over the initial 6 hours of perfusion in the HiNa-EVLP group compared with C-EVLP (Figure 2A). Although a modest trend was observed in Di-EVLP, this was not significant. Hemodialysis Effects on Perfusate Osmolality and Electrolyte Concentrations

Analysis of perfusate composition over time was evaluated between groups. In the Di-EVLP group, mean osmolality was markedly reduced relative to C-EVLP (Figure 3A). This disruption appeared to be the result of progressive loss of sodium from the perfusate, likely into the dialysate. Supplementing the dialysate with high sodium (160 mEq) corrected this imbalance and recovered the osmolality of the solution (Figure 3B). Without dialysis, lactate accumulated in the perfusate of the C-EVLP group, but was attenuated in either dialysis group (Figure 3C). Cytokine Expression Analysis

Cytokines in the perfusate were analyzed at 1 hour and 6 hours, with the results expressed as log2 fold-change over this interval. Tumor necrosis factor-α displayed no apparent change among groups (Figure 4A). Interferon-γ, however, showed a robust increase in the HiNa-EVLP group only (Figure 4B). Interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-6 did not change significantly in either group from control (Supplemental Figure 1). IL-8 was significantly increased in the HiNa-EVLP group relative to control, and a nonsignificant trend observed for Di-EVLP (Figure 4C). Platelet Endothelial Cell Adhesion Molecule 1 Quantification and Histologic Scoring

The change in platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule 1(PECAM1), a protein native to vascular endothelial cells, abundance in the perfusate from 1 to 6 hours was assessed to measure vascular integrity. A modest, although nonsignificant, the increase of PECAM1 in both dialysis groups was greater than in the C-EVLP group over this interval (Supplemental Figure 2A).

Terminal biopsy specimens were also collected for histologic assessment. Alveolar hemorrhage, vascular congestion, and fibrin deposition did not exhibit any apparent differences among groups (Supplemental Figure 2B). We note, however, that the lung leukocyte infiltration score specifically in the HiNa-EVLP group was decreased relative to control (Supplemental Figure 2C).

Comment

This study evaluated the feasibility of integrating hemodialysis into EVLP using the OCS Lung platform. Although the concept is attractive for extending graft viability and controlling metabolic waste, our findings indicate that standard dialysis variables led to reduced preservation time and the development of pulmonary edema. The use of a Pao2/Fio2 ratio threshold (<300) as a functional end point standardized assessment across groups but limited longitudinal data acquisition beyond 18 to 24 hours in most animals, posing a key limitation in assessing temporal trends.

We initially hypothesized that sodium loss during dialysis disrupted osmotic balance, contributing to edema. Adjusting the dialysate sodium concentration to 160 mEq/L in the HiNa-EVLP group reduced fluid accumulation but failed to prevent graft failure, suggesting that additional factors—such as endothelial dysfunction or the removal of vasoregulatory mediators such as nitric oxide—may play central roles.7,8

Significant hemolysis was observed during perfusion, with plasma free hemoglobin >10 mg/dL by 6 hours (Supplemental Figure 3). These levels are sufficient to scavenge nitric oxide and impair vasodilation, contributing to increased pulmonary vascular resistance.7 Moreover, elevated sodium concentrations have been shown to degrade the erythrocyte glycocalyx, promote red blood cell aggregation, and increase shear stress, which may further compromise endothelial integrity.8

We also observed upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-8 and interferon-γ, particularly in the HiNa-EVLP group. These findings may reflect neutrophil activation or migration into the perfusate.9,10 Concomitant increases in IL-10 suggest a counterregulatory anti-inflammatory response. Although histologic injury scores were not significantly different, a reduction in neutrophil infiltration in the HiNa-EVLP group is consistent with the hypothesis of reverse transepithelial leukocyte migration during EVLP.10

Given the rapid rise in pulmonary vascular resistance in the dialysis groups, microbial contamination cannot be excluded despite sterile technique; future studies should include endotoxin assays and cultures. Additionally, dialyzer type and flow rates were adapted from clinical protocols and may not be optimal for EVLP.5 Finally, because this was a porcine model, these findings may not fully translate to human lung transplantation.

Although this study yielded negative results, it provides valuable mechanistic insight into the physiological stressors introduced by hemodialysis during EVLP. Our data suggest that conventional dialysis techniques, when applied without modification, may be detrimental in blood-based EVLP systems. Moving forward, dialysis-compatible perfusate formulations, endothelial-stabilizing strategies, and optimized solute flux control will be essential for achieving prolonged, stable perfusion. Filtration or replacement-based perfusate conditioning remains a promising approach, provided these challenges can be addressed.
