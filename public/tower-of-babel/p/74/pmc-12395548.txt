Glycemic Derangements With Capivasertib—From Hyperglycemia to Diabetic Ketoacidosis: A Report of 3 Cases
Authors: Sophie Nicolich-Henkin, Lauren Waters, Neha Bansal, Stanislaw Klek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCapiversatib is a pan-Ak strain transforming inhibitor used in combination with fulvestrant for the treatment of hormone receptor-positive advanced breast cancer and has a known adverse effect of hyperglycemia. We present 3 cases of severe hyperglycemia, 2 of which presented as diabetic ketoacidosis, in patients being treated with capivasertib. These cases demonstrate the severity of hyperglycemia that can result from capivasertib use, as well as highlight treatment regimens and patient outcomes.

Introduction

Capivasertib is an oral, small-molecule pan-Ak strain transforming (AKT) inhibitor. It was approved in November 2023 by the Food and Drug Administration for use in combination with fulvestrant for the treatment of adults with hormone receptor-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HR+/HER2−) locally advanced or metastatic breast cancer [1]. It is classically dosed as 400 milligrams (mg) twice daily for 4 consecutive days, followed by 3 nontreatment days, in conjunction with fulvestrant (herein termed “standard treatment cycle”), although dose reductions for adverse effects are considered [2].

AKT inhibitors work by inhibiting the serine/threonine kinase AKT, which is a downstream effector of PI3K and plays a crucial role in signal transduction [3, 4]. This pathway is frequently disrupted in different malignancies, and overactivation of the pathway contributes to carcinogenesis, proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of tumor cells [3, 4]. Consequently, many antineoplastic agents, including capivasertib, target the PI3K/AKT pathway (Fig. 1).

The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Extracellular signals bind to a receptor in the plasma membrane, which activates PI3K. PI3K activates AKT, which causes it to transfer from the cell membrane intracellularly to active downstream effector molecules. Overactivation of this pathway contributes to carcinogenesis, proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of tumor cells. PI3K inhibitors and AKT inhibitors are antineoplastic agents that can target this pathway; capivasertib works by inhibiting AKT and therefore preventing downstream tumor cell survival.

Hyperglycemia is a known adverse effect of capivasertib, documented in up to 16.3% of patients receiving treatment, with higher doses of capivasertib associated with higher rates of hyperglycemia [2, 5]. Despite this known potential consequence of treatment, capivasertib remains a novel agent, and thus firm treatment guidelines directing antihyperglycemic management are not well established. In this case series, we present 3 cases of severe insulin resistance, 2 of which presented as diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), in patients being treated with capivasertib, and discuss their individualized management and outcomes.

Case Presentation

Case 1

A 55-year-old female presented to the emergency department with nausea and vomiting. She had a medical history of type 2 diabetes mellitus managed on sitagliptin, metformin, and dapagliflozin. She also had a history of stage IV HR+/HER2− breast cancer on capivasertib 400 mg twice daily for the standard treatment cycle. She presented with glucose of 1359 mg/dL (75.5 mmol/L) [reference range 70-100 mg/dL (3.88-5.55 mmol/L)], venous blood pH of 7.12 (reference range 7.3-7.4), and a betahydroxybutyric acid of 5.5 mmol/L (99.1 mg/dL) (reference range < 0.3 mm/dL, <3.1 mg/dL), consistent with DKA. Her hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) was 13.9% (128 mmol/mol) (reference range, <5.7% <39 mmol/mol), significantly increased from the most recent HbA1c of 6.7% (50 mmol/mol) 4 months prior. Her body mass index (BMI) was 20.22 kg/m2. Capivasertib was held, and she was initiated on IV insulin infusion per our institution-based DKA protocol.

Very high insulin infusion rates were required during treatment, reaching >80 units of insulin per hour. She remained on the insulin infusion for approximately 60 hours prior to DKA resolution (Fig. 2), then was transitioned to a subcutaneous insulin regimen of basal and prandial insulin dosed at 0.4 units/kilogram (kg), significantly less than she had required on IV infusion.

Case 2

A 79-year-old female presented to the emergency department due to polyuria and ongoing “high” readings from her continuous glucose monitor (CGM). She had a medical history of type 2 diabetes on basal and prandial insulin and empagliflozin, as well as HR+/HER2− breast cancer on capivasertib 400 mg twice daily for the standard treatment cycle. Her HbA1c prior to starting capivasertib was 8.3% (67 mmol/mol). Her BMI was 32.27 kg/m2. On presentation, she had severe hyperglycemia with serum glucose 565 mg/dL (31.4 mmol/L), though not in DKA.

Capivasertib was held, and she was admitted to the hospital for severe hyperglycemia. She was initiated on basal and prandial insulin at 0.4 units/kg dosing with rapid improvement in glycemia.

Case 3

A 73-year-old female presented to the emergency department due to significant hyperglycemia in the setting of polyuria and polydipsia. She had a medical history of diet-controlled type 2 diabetes, as well as HR+/HER2− breast cancer on capivasertib 320 mg twice daily for the standard treatment cycle. She was initiated on capivasertib 7 months prior.

On presentation, she had blood glucose of 838 mg/dL (46.5 mmol/L), venous blood pH of 7.19, and a betahydroxybutyric acid of 3.8 mmol/L (39.5 mg/dL), consistent with DKA. She was treated with IV insulin infusion per our institution-based DKA protocol, with rates reaching >60 units of insulin per hour. Her HbA1c was 6.5% (48.0 mmol/mol), increased from 5.6% (38 mmol/mol) 3 months prior. Her BMI was 21.77 kg/m2. She remained on insulin infusion for approximately 72 hours before DKA resolved (Fig. 3), then was transitioned to a subcutaneous regimen of basal and prandial insulin dosed at 0.5 units/kg.

Diagnostic Assessment

Case 1

Evaluation for precipitating factors of DKA, involving thorough infectious workup, dietary review, and medication reconciliation including evaluation for recent steroid use, was unrevealing. Antibody testing for type 1 diabetes was not performed. Her severe insulin resistance was attributed to initiation of capivasertib, which had been started 3 weeks prior to presentation, with the last dose taken the evening prior to admission.

Case 2

The patient had initiated capivasertib 11 days prior to presentation. Evaluation for other precipitating factors was unrevealing. Antibody testing for type 1 diabetes was positive for zinc transporter 8 antibody; however, c-peptide was detectable and appropriately elevated for hyperglycemia. Her acute hyperglycemia was attributed to capivasertib.

Case 3

Evaluation for underlying etiologies of insulin resistance and for precipitating factors of DKA was unrevealing. Antibody testing for type 1 diabetes was not performed. Her severe insulin resistance was attributed to capivasertib.

Treatment

Case 1

Once DKA resolved, she was restarted on capivasertib at a reduced dose of 200 mg twice daily for the standard treatment cycle. On the days she received capivasertib, she received basal and prandial insulin, while on days she did not receive capivasertib, she received only basal insulin with a dose reduction of >30%.

She was discharged on mixed insulin with twice-daily dosing for ease of administration. On days she received capivasertib, she was advised to take >200% more insulin than on days she was not receiving capivasertib.

Case 2

She was discharged on basal and prandial insulin as well as a reduced dose of capivasertib of 320 mg twice daily for the standard treatment cycle. On treatment days, due to initially experiencing postprandial hyperglycemia, her doses were quickly increased to 62% more prandial insulin than nontreatment days; however, despite this increase, she was still consistently hyperglycemic, as seen on her CGM report (Fig. 4). She eventually required >400% increase in prandial insulin and >35% increase in basal insulin on treatment days compared to nontreatment days, with treatment days still affected by severe hyperglycemia.

Continuous glucose monitoring report during use of capivasertib. Patient was receiving treatment with capivasertib on Saturday, Sunday, Monday, and Tuesday and was not taking capivasertib on Wednesday, Thursday, and Friday. At the time of this download, she was taking 62% more prandial insulin on treatment days than nontreatment days. Despite this, she experienced severe hyperglycemia, mainly postprandial, on the days of taking capivasertib as well as the day following it (Wednesday), while in the next 2 days (Thursday and Friday) she remained euglycemic.

Case 3

Her insulin requirements continued to decrease after resolution of DKA. Basal and prandial insulin were discontinued, and she was monitored on only a lispro correctional scale of 1 unit of insulin for every 50 mg/dL (2.8 mmol/L) of glucose above 150 mg/dL (8.3 mmol/L), 3 times daily prior to meals. Given her low insulin requirements in conjunction with chronic kidney disease, she was discharged home on sitagliptin and advised to monitor her glucose levels. Capivasertib was reduced to 200 mg twice daily for the standard treatment cycle.

Outcome and Follow-up

Case 1

Approximately 1 month after her hospital admission, she was readmitted to the hospital for acute renal failure and died.

Case 2

About 5 months after her initial presentation, she was switched from capivasertib to trastuzumab deruxtecan by oncology. Insulin doses were reduced to less than nontreatment day dosing.

Case 3

One month after discharge, she died at another institution; records were not available.

Discussion

Given its novelty, the mechanism of action by which capivasertib causes hyperglycemia has not yet been extensively studied; however, previous studies have investigated the pathway of insulin resistance in PI3K and AKT inhibitors. Insulin binds to the insulin receptor, which leads to activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway and influences downstream effector molecules to allow for the effects of insulin [6, 7] (Fig. 5). A study of mice fed ad libitum examined the effects of a pan-AKT inhibitor. After being administered the pan-AKT inhibitor, mice were found to have a transient increase in glucose and insulin levels as well as an increase in phosphorylation of the insulin receptors in their livers [8]. However, these mice had a reduction in downstream targets of insulin, indicating that despite the noted increase in insulin levels and increase in insulin receptor activation, there was inhibition of the downstream effects of insulin activity [8].

The insulin signaling pathway. Insulin binds to the insulin receptor, which has tyrosine-kinase activity and mediates the metabolic functions of insulin. Binding of insulin stimulates the association of the receptor with downstream mediators, including insulin receptor substrate-1 and PI3K. PI3K activates AKT, which works on downstream effector molecules to allow for the downstream effects of insulin. These effects include inhibition of glycogen synthase kinase-3 GSK-3, which removes inhibition from GS, allowing for glycogen synthesis, and translocation of glucose transporter type 4 to the plasma membrane, allowing for glucose transport into cells. An AKT inhibitor, such as capivasertib, blocks the actions of AKT after activation by insulin through the insulin receptor, so despite high levels of insulin and insulin receptor activation, downstream effects are unable to be performed.

A 2021 retrospective study characterized hyperglycemia experienced by patients treated with a PI3K or AKT inhibitor. When covariates were included in a multivariable logistic regression model, only BMI ≥ 25 and HbA1c ≥ 7 were independent significant predictors of hyperglycemia [9]. This study also noted that other medications besides insulin were successful in treating PI3K or AKT inhibitor-induced hyperglycemia, including metformin, sodium glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, and sulfonylureas [9]. Given that PI3K and AKT inhibitors act on the insulin pathway downstream of the insulin receptor (Fig. 5), it is possible that medications that target insulin resistance may be more effective than exogenous insulin administration in addressing the side effect of hyperglycemia. Sodium glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, in particular, have shown robust antiglycemic responses in mice with evidence that they may increase the effectiveness of PI3K inhibitors, although caution should be undertaken with their use given the risk of euglycemic DKA [9]. Further research is necessary to determine the role of noninsulin antihyperglycemic medications in these patients. The 3 cases we present demonstrate the severity of hyperglycemia and insulin resistance that can be seen during capivasertib treatment. Notably, 2 of our reported patients presented with severe hyperglycemia within 3 weeks of capivasertib initiation, while the third patient presented with severe hyperglycemia after 7 months of treatment. This indicates that severe insulin resistance does not have a clear pattern of onset. Additionally, our 3 reported patients had BMIs of 20.22 kg/m2, 32.27 kg/m2, and 21.77 kg/m2 and pretreatment HbA1cs of 6.7% (50 mmol/mol), 8.3% (67 mmol/mol), and 5.3% (34 mmol/mol), respectively, which demonstrates that cases of severe insulin resistance with capivasertib use may occur outside of aforementioned predictors of hyperglycemia and that simply having a history of dysglycemia, regardless of HbA1c, may predict hyperglycemic response.

In contrast, a literature review revealed 2 previous case reports published of DKA during treatment with capivasertib in patients with no prior history of diabetes, suggesting more research is needed to determine predictors of severe hyperglycemia. It is noteworthy that case 2 did not present in DKA despite having the highest pretreatment HbA1c. We attribute this to glucose monitoring via CGM alerting to hyperglycemia before symptom development. Cases 1 and 3 were not engaging in glucose monitoring. Glucose monitoring may protect against DKA in patients taking capivasertib by allowing for early detection of hyperglycemia.

In this case series, it was notable that significantly higher insulin doses were required on capivasertib treatment days vs nontreatment days, prandial requirements increased more than basal requirements, and unusually high insulin drip rates over prolonged time courses were required to treat capivasertib-induced DKA. Comparatively, at the time of transition off insulin drip, at which point capivasertib had been held for multiple days, significantly lower doses of subcutaneous insulin were required. Further research is needed to determine the best treatment regimens for patients experiencing capivasertib-induced insulin resistance.
