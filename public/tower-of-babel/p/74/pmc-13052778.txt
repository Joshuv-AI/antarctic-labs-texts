Bilateral thalamic involvement in scrub typhus meningoencephalitis: a case report of an uncommon neuroimaging pattern mimicking Japanese encephalitis
Authors: Achyoot Sharan Koirala, Ujjwal Kumar Shah, Ram Bahadur Khadka, Shekhar Jaiswal, Alok Kumar Jha, Prabesh Panta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundScrub typhus is a major but under-recognised cause of acute febrile illness in Southeast Asia. Neurological involvement occurs in up to 20% of cases, but bilateral thalamic lesions, which are classically associated with Japanese encephalitis (JE), are rare in scrub typhus.Case presentationA 45-year-old woman presented during the monsoon season with high-grade fever, altered mental status and generalised tonic-clonic seizure. MRI revealed bilateral symmetric thalamic T2/FLAIR hyperintensities with diffusion restriction, initially raising strong suspicion for JE. Serological testing showed positive scrub typhus IgM/IgG and negative JE IgM. Lumbar puncture was deferred due to raised intracranial pressure. She was managed with intravenous doxycycline, ceftriaxone, acyclovir (later discontinued), hypertonic saline, corticosteroids and supportive ICU care.ConclusionThis case highlights a rare neuroimaging manifestation of scrub typhus that closely mimics JE. In endemic regions, scrub typhus should be considered in febrile neurological illness even in the absence of eschar or typical imaging findings.

Introduction

Scrub typhus, caused by Orientia tsutsugamushi, is a major cause of acute febrile illness that predominantly affects agricultural workers in endemic areas. Transmission occurs via chigger bites, the larval form of trombiculid mite, frequently during monsoon seasons.1 2 Typical features include fever, thrombocytopenia, elevated liver enzymes and an eschar that develops at the chigger bite location in about 40% of cases.3

Neurological involvement occurs in 20% of patients and includes meningitis, meningoencephalitis, encephalopathy, movement disorders and peripheral neuropathies.2 3 Atypical neurological presentations often delay diagnosis and treatment, contributing to significant morbidity.4

Bilateral thalamic lesions are seen in Japanese encephalitis (JE), a major public health concern in Nepal during the monsoon, and their presence frequently biases clinicians towards a diagnosis of JE. Thalamic involvement in scrub typhus is extremely rare. We present a case of scrub typhus meningoencephalitis with bilateral thalamic involvement.

Case presentation

A woman in her mid-40s presented during peak monsoon season, a period marked by high prevalence of JE, with a 3-day history of high-grade fever (Tmax 104°F) with chills and rigour, headache, progressive limb stiffness (upper>lower) and altered mental status. On day 3, she developed generalised tonic-clonic seizure with frothing and up-rolling of eyes. There was no vomiting, photophobia, neck stiffness, ear discharge, bowel/bladder incontinence or recent travel.

Her medical history was significant for hyperthyroidism on carbimazole for 10 years. She denied smoking, alcohol or substance use.

Examination showed pulse 92 beats per minute, BP 170/80 mm Hg, temp 103°F and SpO₂ 90% on room air. Neurological examination revealed Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) E2V2M5 (9/15), disoriented, pupils sluggishly reactive, brisk reflexes (3+) and bilateral upgoing plantar responses. Other systemic examinations were unremarkable.

Due to declining consciousness, she was intubated. During hospitalisation, she developed features suggestive of raised intracranial pressure (ICP), including deteriorating GCS and papilledema on fundus examination, for which she was managed with hypertonic saline (3% NaCl).

Laboratory findings showed leucocytosis (11.6×109/L, 89% neutrophils), normal transaminases, International Normalized Ratio (INR) 1.21, creatinine 1.5 mg/dL and Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN) 30.8 mg/dL. Scrub typhus IgM and IgG were positive, confirming active infection. Dengue serology (NS1, IgM and IgG) was negative. JE IgM was negative, reducing the likelihood of JE despite the patient’s epidemiological risk. Lumbar puncture was deferred due to elevated ICP. MRI of the brain showed symmetric T2/FLAIR hyperintensities involving both thalami (figure 1), accompanied by diffusion restriction, consistent with an acute inflammatory or infectious process and subtle leptomeningeal enhancement along the tentorium. Spine imaging was normal.

The principal differential diagnosis was JE, given the strong epidemiological overlap and the fact that bilateral thalamic lesions are characteristic of the disease; however, the diagnosis was excluded due to negative JE IgM serology. Scrub typhus meningoencephalitis was strongly supported by positive serology, although thalamic involvement is an uncommon manifestation. Deep cerebral venous thrombosis was considered but was unlikely, given the absence of venous sinus abnormalities or haemorrhagic changes on imaging. Metabolic and toxic encephalopathies were also evaluated but lacked supporting laboratory abnormalities. Artery of Percheron infarction was another possibility due to its ability to produce bilateral thalamic lesions with diffusion restriction; however, the patient’s febrile prodrome and subacute presentation made vascular aetiology less likely. Dengue is also an important differential diagnosis of encephalitis, especially in endemic regions. However, in our case, dengue serology was negative. Thus, scrub typhus meningoencephalitis with atypical bilateral thalamic involvement emerged as the most consistent diagnosis.

She initially received doxycycline 100 mg intravenous two times per day (targeted therapy), ceftriaxone 2 g intravenous two times per day (empirical bacterial coverage), acyclovir 500 mg intravenous three times a day (until HSV excluded), levetiracetam 1 g intravenous two times (for seizure control), paracetamol 1 g intravenous three times a day (for fever), 3% NaCl and dexamethasone 8 mg intravenous three times a day (for cerebral oedema) and ICU support. Acyclovir was discontinued after negative HSV serology. Inj. Doxycycline was recommended for 10 days, but due to financial difficulties, the family discharged her against medical advice to a government hospital on the third day of admission. At the time of discharge, the patient was still intubated with no signs of recovery. Further follow-up could not be done.

Discussion and conclusions

Scrub typhus frequently involves multiple organ systems, affecting hepatic, pulmonary, cardiac, renal and neurological systems, although such complications are described more frequently in other rickettsial illnesses. Neurological involvement is more often seen in scrub typhus and occurs in up to 20% of cases, with untreated mortality ranging from 7% to 30%, particularly in patients at extremes of ages or with high rickettsial load.2

Orientia tsutsugamushi primarily targets endothelial cells. CNS invasion is believed to occur through phagocyte-facilitated transport after bacterial binding to non-professional phagocytic cells like fibroblasts and endothelial cells as well as neutrophils and macrophages. The organism escapes phagosomes, proliferates in the cytoplasm and triggers activation of transcription factors, such as nuclear factor-kappa B, inducing chemokine expression and endothelial apoptosis.2 3 These mechanisms contribute to microvascular inflammation, blood–brain barrier dysfunction and subsequent neurological manifestations, including meningitis, meningoencephalitis, encephalopathy, movement disorders and peripheral neuropathies. Neurological manifestations under the spectrum of meningitis/meningoencephalitis/encephalopathy have been reported in about 14%–83% of patients with scrub typhus.2 3

This case demonstrates a rare neuroimaging pattern of scrub typhus meningoencephalitis with bilateral symmetric thalamic lesions. Such lesions are typically associated with JE, an endemic disease in Nepal during the monsoon season, making diagnostic confusion common.4 Because both diseases cocirculate in the same regions, bilateral thalamic involvement may be incorrectly attributed solely to JE, potentially delaying appropriate therapy. Thalamic involvement in scrub typhus, when described, usually appears as part of multifocal infarcts rather than isolated, symmetric bilateral lesions.3 The unusually selective thalamic involvement observed in this patient expands the known neuroradiologic spectrum of severe scrub typhus.

The mechanism for bilateral thalamic injury remains uncertain. Proposed explanations include small-vessel vasculitis of perforating branches supplying both thalami, diffuse microvascular inflammation or involvement of the artery of Percheron.3 Microvascular endothelial dysfunction caused by O. tsutsugamushi-mediated inflammation may render deep gray nuclei particularly vulnerable due to their rich vascular supply. Similar mechanisms have been implicated in other severe neurological presentations of scrub typhus, including haemorrhagic encephalitis and multifocal necrotising lesions.1 3

Diagnosis in this patient was established by positive scrub IgM/IgG, negative JE IgM and MRI findings showing T2/FLAIR hyperintensities with diffusion restriction in both thalami, features consistent with acute inflammatory pathology, helping differentiate scrub typhus from alternative causes of bilateral thalamic lesions, such as deep cerebral venous thrombosis, metabolic encephalopathies or viral meningoencephalitis.1 5 Early neuroimaging is essential for localisation, narrowing the differential and prompting timely doxycycline therapy in endemic regions.

Dengue encephalitis represents another important differential diagnosis in endemic regions when bilateral thalamic lesions are encountered. Dengue virus can cause neuronal injury with cerebral oedema and haemorrhage secondary to vascular leak, frequently involving the bilateral basal ganglia and thalamic complex and producing the characteristic neuro-radiological ‘double-doughnut’ sign. Although this sign is not pathognomonic, its recognition may assist in distinguishing dengue encephalitis from other aetiologies of acute febrile encephalopathy in the appropriate clinical and serological context and may facilitate early diagnosis of expanded dengue syndrome. However, similar bithalamic lesions have been reported in several other conditions, including Wernicke’s encephalopathy, subacute sclerosing panencephalitis, artery of Percheron infarction, deep cerebral venous sinus thrombosis and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders. In situations where cerebrospinal fluid analysis or other diagnostic tests are inconclusive or lumbar puncture is contraindicated because of raised ICP, such imaging patterns may guide clinicians towards targeted testing and management. In our patient, negative dengue serology together with positive scrub typhus IgM/IgG supported rickettsial infection as the most plausible aetiology despite overlapping neuroimaging features.6

Management included intravenous doxycycline, the drug of choice for scrub typhus, along with empiric ceftriaxone. Hypertonic saline and corticosteroids were used for raised ICP and levetiracetam for seizure control. Similar multimodal treatment approaches have been shown to improve outcomes in severe rickettsial encephalitis.4 5

This case emphasises several vital clinical lessons. First, scrub typhus must be considered in the differential diagnosis of acute encephalitis syndrome during monsoon seasons in South Asia, even in the absence of classical features such as eschar, which may be absent in up to 40% of patients.7 Second, serological testing for scrub typhus should be performed early in endemic regions, given its overlapping presentation with viral encephalitis syndromes. Third, clinicians should maintain heightened suspicion for rickettsial CNS infections when encountering bilateral thalamic lesions, despite their strong association with JE.
