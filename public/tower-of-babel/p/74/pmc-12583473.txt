Investigation of optical properties of three dimensional reflectance rulers fabricated by size-controlled monodisperse polystyrene colloidal particles
Authors: Meisam Gazmeh, Somayeh Karimi, Seyed Hassan Tavassoli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study is relevant to the design of the final activity of 3 dimensional photonic crystals (3D PCs), especially for applications where the reflectance range is a key parameter. Highly monodisperse spherical Polystyrene (PS) particles, synthesized in the size range of ∼200 to 550 nm by varying the concentration of Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate (SDS), are used to fabricate 3D PCs on glass substrates. The resulting structures show a clear size-dependent optical response due to their photonic crystal lattices, with reflectance values exceeding 80% across various particle sizes. This finding implies the successful fabrication of stable and homogeneous colored opal films with highly reproducible, sensitive, and tunable reflectance responses across a wide range of the visible spectrum. This set of samples can potentially serve in various applications, justifying their designation as reflectance rulers. In the following, the optical properties of the resulting PCs as reflectance rulers have been investigated by comparing simulated and experimental reflectance spectra. Interestingly, the sphere sizes estimated via Bragg’s law closely match those measured using Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM), validating both the substrate fabrication method and the simulation model. This consistency between the simulation predictions and the experimental observations confirms the accuracy of the assumptions used for the crystal structure.

Introduction

PCs, whether they are artificially made or naturally formed, have unique optical properties and tunable structural colors due to their periodic spatial modulation of the dielectric constant1–5. The fabricated PCs enable to control over light propagation within the crystal structure1,6. Depending on the wavelength range of the incident light, the light can be either reflected or transmitted from the crystal lattice6–8. The blocked wavelengths are reflected through Bragg diffraction, creating a photonic band gap or stop band8,9. The morphology, size, structure of crystal and refractive index of the PC, in addition to the refractive index of the surrounding medium and the angle of incident light, collectively have an essential impact on determining the wavelengths of stop band and the intensity of reflections10,11. These features make PC a suitable structure for development in various optical applications such as light harvesting, vibrational spectroscopy, imaging, and sensing10–16. To achieve the optimal performance of these optically active structures for specific applications, it is crucial to focus on the fabrication process and characterization of PCs17,18.

Colloidal crystallization is a widely method for fabricating artificially 3D structured objects featuring regularly arranged particles8,19–21. This method is known for being relatively simple, fast and cost-effectiveness compared to other techniques21. In this method, the properties of the synthesized particles such as material, shape, size, size distribution and their dispersion in the periodic structure are the main indicators to judge the quality of the fabricated PCs. In this regard, the reflectance spectra and related parameters, such as the maximum peak and its width, can indeed be accounted valuable tools for determining the quality of colloidal crystals3,4,13,22–24. Meanwhile, a great effort has been devoted to the fabrication of spherical colloidal PCs and broadening their applications, especially in the optical devices and sensors15–17,25. The spherical symmetry ensures that their stop band, reflection wavelengths and generally optical performance of PCs remain consistent regardless of crystal surface rotation under illumination at a fixed incident angle of the light.

Dielectric-based structures can be easily used to light localization at the nano-scale for developing a wide range of photonic devices16,25. These materials offer several key advantages including low invasiveness, easy production, reproducibility, versatility, recyclability and cost-effectiveness11,15,16,25–28. In addition, dielectric structures have shown unique properties such as electromagnetic resonant response in the range of visible and infrared, thermal stability, low Ohmic losses to prevent energy dissipation into heat, absence of quenching to perform the photoluminescence experiments, high quantum efficiency and ability to direct improvement of signal extraction1,13,15,29–31. Also, the surface of dielectric materials can be easily functionalized with the groups –NH2, -COO-, -PO4-3, -SH, and –OH for sensing applications13,32,33. It is noteworthy that the selection of these kind of materials for accurate measurements in especial applications is limited by some specific requirements such as a higher refractive index than the surrounding media and high thermal stability15,28,34. Polystyrene particles due to their low water absorption, rigidity, high specific surface areas, rapid diffusion, high mechanical properties, dispersive stability and cost-effectiveness in production offer an appropriate solution for fabricating high-performance colloidal-based PCs13,28,35. According to the aforementioned characteristics, spherical PS particles within the size range of approximately 200 to 550 nm exhibit strong confinement of light to a very small volume in the visible range and their tunable reflection peaks can be widely utilized as reflectance rulers effectively in the desirable applications6,13–17,36–39. Achieving a reproducible synthesis process to control particle size distribution is essential for ensuring quality and suitability for final use in various applications. The emulsion polymerization is the typical method for preparing monodisperse particles with sizes smaller than 1 μm in a single stage of the reaction8,19–21,40,41. In this method, the size of PS particles is dependent on the concentration of initiator, concentration of surfactant, and reaction temperature21,42.

The motivation for this research is to obtain a high quality fabrication method of photonic crystals using polystyrene particles of various sizes and to highlight their potential applications as reflectance rulers. Herein, we investigate the systematic preparation of a series of structurally colored films featuring predictable, tunable, and high-reflectance peaks, along with verified spatial reproducibility. The PS particles have been synthesized in a wide range of sizes from 200 to 550 nm by using the emulsion polymerization method. The influence of different concentrations of SDS on the final properties of the PS particles has been investigated to find a practical guideline for how to synthesize various sizes of monodisperse particles. Using the resulting PS particles and a drop-casting method under carefully controlled conditions, 3D PCs were fabricated on the glass substrates. Then, we used the reflectance-based optical spectroscopy for investigation of optical properties of the fabricated PCs. The reflected light was collected using an optical fiber and analyzed with a spectrometer. Given the arranged structure of PS particles on the substrate surface, reproducible rulers can be fabricated that reflect over a wide range of visible light. The experimental results show strong agreement with theoretical predictions and simulations.

Experimental

Materials

Styrene monomer [C6H5CH = CH2] and potassium persulfate (KPS) [K2S2O8] were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Sodium bicarbonate [NaHCO3] and Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate (SDS) [CH3(CH2)11OSO3Na] were obtained from Acros Organics. Sodium hydroxide [NaOH], sulfuric acid (96%) [H2SO4] and hydrogen peroxide (30%) [H2O2] were obtained from Merck. Milli-Q deionized (DI) water with a resistivity of 18 MΩcm was used in the preparation of all solutions throughout the polymerization process.

Synthesis of monodisperse colloidal PS particles

Negatively charged PS particles of various sizes were synthesized via the emulsion polymerization method20,42. In this process, styrene used as the monomer, SDS as the emulsifier, KPS as the initiator, and sodium bicarbonate as the buffer20,42,43. To remove inhibitors, the styrene monomer was purified by washing with a 10% aqueous sodium hydroxide solution followed by multiple rinses with DI water. It should be emphasized that SDS, as an anionic surfactant with acceptable solubility in water at room temperature, played a key role in stabilizing the PS particles during synthesis44. To finely control particle size, the amount of SDS was varied between 20 mg and 100 mg, while keeping all other reaction conditions and component amounts constant.

All glassware was first immersed in the freshly prepared Aqua Regia cleaning solution [HCl–HNO3 (3:1)], rinsed with DI water several times and finally oven-dried before use. A water-cooled reflux condenser was added to the second outlet of a 500 mL three-necked round-bottomed flask, which was sealed to maintain a constant reaction volume. Next, 200 mL of DI water, 125 mg of NaHCO3 and an adequate amount of SDS were added to the reaction flask. Nitrogen gas was slowly bubbled through the solution to maintain an inert atmosphere, while mechanical stirring was maintained at 300 rpm (see Fig. 1). The flask was placed in a 70 °C-oil bath to maintain the reaction temperature within ± 1 °C, monitored via a thermometer fitted to one of the flask necks. After 10 min, 250 mg of KPS (dissolved in 50 mL of DI water at 40 °C) and 25 g of prewashed styrene were added. The nucleation process started immediately, as indicated by the gradual development of a milky white appearance. The reaction was then allowed to proceed for 24 h under constant stirring. After completion, the mixture was cooled to room temperature and the resulting monodisperse PS particle suspension was stored at 4 °C. By varying the quantity of SDS while keeping other conditions constant, colloidal PS particles with diameters ranging from approximately 200 to 550 nm were successfully synthesized. Notably, the final color and optical properties of the different samples were tunable by varying the particle size, allowing the opals to exhibit distinct structural colors visible under natural light.

Preparation of PS opal films on the glass substrates

Artificial PC films were grown using the self-assembly method9,26,27. At first, 1 cm×1 cm glass substrates were pretreated to become hydrophilic through the Piranha cleaning process—immersing them for 15 min in a 3:1 mixture of concentrated sulfuric acid and hydrogen peroxide. After thorough rinsing with deionized water, the treated glasses were oven-dried. A selected volume of colloidal suspension containing PS particles of a given size was then drop-cast onto the center of each substrate and carefully spread to cover the entire surface. The samples were placed on a heater at 50 °C—below the glass transition temperature of polystyrene—and left to dry. Crystallization completed within 15 min, resulting in uniform films that exhibited bright structural colors under ambient light, depending on the particle size. This visual effect confirmed the successful formation of the periodic PC structures. Additionally, heating during the drying process enhanced the mechanical stability of the films by directing the crystallization outward rather than toward the center of the substrate9,45.

Characterizations

The size and size distribution of spheres were measured by Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS). A 3%wt solution of colloidal PS particles was prepared by dissolving them in DI water at room temperature. The solution was poured into a test tube and DLS measurement was conducted using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano Series equipped with a 633 nm He-Ne laser at a 173° scattering angle. The hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and Zeta potential were recorded to evaluate particle characteristics.

Also, the pH-dependency of the resulting aqueous PS particle suspensions —synthesized using varying concentrations of SDS without further purification— was measured using a Consort P901 pH meter.

A Hitachi S-4160 FE-SEM operating at 15 kV was used for analyzing the surface morphology of the PC films. At first, the surface of the samples was coated with a thin layer of gold to prevent charging effects during the experiment. The morphology of PS particles was then observed and their size and distribution were precisely estimated from the obtained FESEM images.

Finally, the reflection spectra of the colloidal crystal films on the glass substrates were measured using an Ocean Optics HR2000 spectrometer equipped with a Xe lamp as a white light source. In the first configuration, setup Fig. 2a was used for normal reflection measurements. In this setup, a Y-branched fiber was utilized to facilitate the normal incidence of light onto the sample surface and collect the reflected light at the same direction. In this type of fiber, the incident light exits from the central fiber and strikes the sample surface at a distance of 5 mm, forming a spot size of approximately 1 mm. The reflected light from the sample surface is then collected by the outer fibers, which are positioned 1 mm away from the central fiber at an angle of approximately 0.2 degree. To evaluate the angle dependency of the reflectance spectra, a variable-angle configuration was employed. The setup in Fig. 2b has been used to measure reflectance at angles from 10° to 50°. This setup included two optical fibers combined with objective lenses and a double motorized rotation stage with a resolution of 1°, as illustrated in Fig. 2b. Also, the light beam has been expanded to 2 mm by using a lens in both the incident and reflected light path. An optical mirror was used to measure the reference reflection spectrum corresponding to 100% reflectance. Then, without changing the conditions, the reflection spectrum of the corresponding crystal was recorded.

Numerical simulation of reflectance

The Finite-Difference Time-Domain (FDTD) method is based on discretizing both time and space, allowing for a time-domain analysis of electromagnetic wave propagation. Here, a periodic structure of closely packed polystyrene spheres with a Face-Centered Cubic (FCC) lattice was assumed within the first Brillouin zone. The refractive index of polystyrene was set to 1.59. A plane wave source with different incidence angle and a wavelength range of 400–1400 nm was used. Periodic boundary conditions (Bloch conditions) were applied in the lateral directions (x and y), and Perfectly Matched Layers (PML) were applied in the propagation direction (z axis) to simulate an infinite periodic array. Also, the number of layers was also optimized to achieve maximum reflectance. Reflectance spectra were calculated by monitoring the reflected power at the top boundary of the simulation domain. Finally, the simulation results were used to predict the spectral position of the photonic stop bands as a function of particle size. Results and discussion

Assembling the ideal structure with the most regular lattice and the minimal defect requires synthesizing PS particles that have highly uniform size and shape distribution1,14,33. The size of synthesized particles is greatly affected by the concentration of reaction reagents and the preparation procedure20,21,40,42. Here, we controlled the particle size and distribution by adjusting the amount of SDS, while keeping other conditions fixed during the synthesizing process. As expected, increasing the SDS amount reduced particle size and maintained an acceptable size distribution by effectively stabilizing small monomer droplets by SDS during polymerization19,20,46. We also measured the pH and zeta potential of each colloidal solution. The gradual pH increase with more initiator implies that SDS, as an ionic surfactant, regulates the ionic strength of the aqueous phase8,20,47. The competition between the generated protons and the increment of the surface potential of PS particles confirms that higher SDS concentrations lead to more stable particles, and also the enlarged particle size is attributed to the charge screening effect by protons. The PDI indicates particle size uniformity. According to the reported experimental conditions in Table 2, PDI could be controlled in all samples, and the monodispersed particles can be synthesized by adjusting the SDS concentration. In the following, we fabricated a series of 3D PC structures on glass substrates using the self-assembly method with PS particles ranging from ~ 200 to 550 nm in diameter. Consistent with the DLS-measured results in Table 2 showing changes in diameter with increasing SDS amount, the FESEM images also confirm a similar particle size trend across experiments. Notably, at sufficiently high SDS concentrations, the hydrodynamic diameter measured by DLS shows greater discrepancy compared to the size measured from FESEM images. This mainly goes back to the existence of more SDS in the solution, leading to an increase in the surface potential of the synthesized particles and subsequently enlarging the hydrodynamic diameter measured by DLS20,40. Overall, the results confirm that the size distribution of the synthesized PS particles could be precisely controlled in a wide range of the SDS concentrations, and the anionic SDS has the best performance for the polymerization of styrene and yielding stable particles.

As illustrated in Fig. 3a-f, the sample surfaces display shiny structural colors that change with the PS particle size. A noticeable blue shift occurs as particle size decreases. This reflection arises from the periodic PC structure, which creates a band gap in the visible light range, preventing light propagation at specific frequencies2,28. The resulting PCs exhibited size-dependent structural colors visible to the naked eye, attributed to the orderly arrangement of PS particles on the substrate, which is clearly confirmed by electron microscope images. As shown in Fig. 3, the PS spheres tend to assemble in ordered rows, with the [111] family of crystalline planes characteristic of FCC structures clearly evident in self-assembled systems on glass substrates6,25,28. The simultaneous excitation of pure [111] planes leads to diffraction effects that cause the reflectance spectra to deviate from the predictions of simple Bragg diffraction. Figure 4 shows the dependence of the reflectance spectrum on the related effective parameters by comparing the measured spectra of opals made from S7 colloidal PS particles (225 nm) with simulation results. In Fig. 4a, the strong agreement between experimental and simulated reflectance spectra is evident, however, the experimental results exhibit a decrease in peak intensity and an increase in FWHM. The reflectance intensity and photonic band gap width are influenced by the opal characteristics. Also, the interference fringes in reflectance spectra that are seen in simulations of thin films, are absent and dampened in experimental data. The observed discrepancies between experimental results and simulation likely arise from the fundamental differences between experimental and simulation data, such as light extinction caused by the sample’s opal structure, resulting from both Bragg scattering and disorder in the periodic crystal48,51,52. While, the simulation assumes an ideal defect-free structure with complete Bloch periodic boundary conditions in the periodic direction. The opal PCs fabricated in this study exhibit large-scale periodicity due to the effective fabrication method. Consequently, the reflection peaks of different samples exceed 80%, indicating a significant performance of the manufactured opals. We will explain the quality of the assembly of PS opals later.

As shown in the inset of Fig. 4b, the quality factor (Q-factor), defined as the ratio of center wavelength to FWHM, was calculated for various incidence angles3,24,53,54. The Q-factor is a dimensionless parameter used in photonic crystal structures, especially in sensing applications3,22,24,53,54. Optimization of the Q-factor is necessary to get maximal efficiency of reflectance rulers. A larger Q-factor indicates a narrower and more well-defined resonance, which enhances the ability to detect small shifts in wavelength caused by changes in the surrounding environment, such as variations in the refractive index or other optical properties of photonic crystal23. This results in greater sensitivity and resolution, both of which are essential for accurate and reliable detection in optical sensors. In addition, a high-Q resonance minimizes energy losses and improves signal-to-noise ratio, which is crucial for detecting low concentrations of analytes or subtle changes in optical properties3. Our results show that the Q-factor, similar to the FWHM, increases at higher incidence angle. Given the strong performance of the fabricated PC at normal incidence, as indicated by the consistency between experimental data and simulation predictions, the increase in Q-factor at larger angles is attributed to the influence of additional plane effects3,22. As a result, achieving a high Q factor, strong reflection intensity, and a detectable shift in peak wavelength with minimal noise is a key design consideration in developing high-performance photonic crystal-based sensors4,23.

The 33-layer of the desired PC structure (sample S7) shows the highest reflection intensity and the narrowest FWHM, indicating the greatest contribution of the inner layers in spectral enhancement. In the following, the repeatability of colloidal suspension deposition on a horizontal substrate was investigated as a function of drop-casting volume (see Fig. 5a-d). For this aim, volumes of 100, 150 and 200 µL of sample S7 were deposited onto 1 cm ×1 cm the glass substrates, placed on a heater at 50 °C, and left to dry. Then, reflectance across the resulting array was analyzed. The reflectance intensity at Bragg’s peak, recorded at nine points on each sample surface, showed the acceptable reproducibility. The relative standard deviations (RSD), indicating the precision of the measurements, were less than 4.07%, 1.83%, and 2.47% for the 100, 150 and 200 µL samples, respectively. This result is significant and confirming that the used fabrication method reliably produces high-quality opal structures. The results also show that the samples made by 100 µL PS particles exhibit lower reflectivity due to due to undesired film imperfections such as defects, voids, and structural disorder. Increasing the volume up to 150 µL improves crystal quality, achieving over 75% reflectivity and a well-ordered close-packed FCC structure from the film’s edge to its center. However, using 200 µL results in a ~ 10% drop in reflectance, likely due to defect accumulation in the thicker multilayer structure. It seems that the use of monodisperse polystyrene particles, precise control over the volume and concentration of the colloidal suspension, and maintaining a constant environmental temperature during the drying process all played a direct role in achieving uniform film formation. Consequently, a volume of 150 µL was selected for subsequent opal film fabrication.

Conclusions

This study investigated the optical response of reflectance rulers fabricated by PS particles arranged in a FCC structure on the glass substrates. To achieve this aim, a broad range of PS particle sizes was synthesized, and the fabrication process of 3D PCs was optimized. The reflectance spectra properties of the corresponding opals as reflectance rulers were studied from both simulation and experimental perspectives. Particular attention was given to the size-dependent characteristics of the spectral response. The findings highlight the critical influence of particle size distribution and crystal arrangement on the quality factor and overall performance of the opals. These insights demonstrate the potential of such reflectance rulers for precise nanoscale distance measurements in chemical and biological sensing applications.
