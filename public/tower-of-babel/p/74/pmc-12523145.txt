Dyslipidemia for delayed healing after macular hole surgery: a clinical risk analysis study
Authors: Jingwen Hui, Yuejun Zhou, Quanhong Han, Ying Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeDelayed macular hole (MH) closure poses a clinical challenge, yet few studies have examined the influence of systemic metabolic factors such as dyslipidemia on postoperative retinal repair. This study investigated risk factors associated with delayed closure after MH surgery, with a particular focus on dyslipidemia.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 432 patients who underwent MH surgery with an internal limiting membrane (ILM) flap technique at Tianjin Eye Hospital between 2015 and 2024. Clinical and biochemical parameters—including total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), age, sex, and postoperative positioning compliance—were assessed. Multivariable logistic regression, smoothed curve fitting, and threshold-effect analyses were used to identify predictors of delayed MH closure.ResultsOf the 432 patients, 60 experienced delayed MH closure. Higher TC and TG levels were significantly associated with delayed closure after adjustment for confounders. These findings suggest that lipid dysregulation may contribute to slower retinal repair.ConclusionDyslipidemia is associated with delayed MH closure, highlighting a potential role of systemic metabolic dysfunction in postoperative retinal recovery. Lipid-lowering strategies may be considered as adjunctive measures to improve surgical outcomes. Prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and explore targeted interventions.Keywords: Macular hole, Delayed healing, Dyslipidemia, Lipid metabolism, Vitrectomy, Retinal repair

Introduction

Idiopathic macular hole (MH) is a relatively prevalent retinal disorder, distinguished by a full-thickness defect within the neurosensory retina in the center of the macula or fovea [1, 2]. This disease may result in impairments of visual functions such as decreased central vision, central scotoma and metamorphopsia. The clinical progression of MH formation typically spans several weeks to several months, with females exhibiting a higher incidence rate than males [2]. Currently, the standard surgical technique for MH includes pars plana vitrectomy (PPV), internal limiting membrane (ILM) peeling, intravitreal gas tamponade, and postoperative facedown positioning [3, 4]. Through this standardized procedure, more than 90% of MHs achieve anatomical closure with improved visual function [4]. In addition, the earlier the surgical intervention is performed, the higher the closure rate. The majority of patients report a notable reduction in central scotoma, albeit with a less significant enhancement in visual acuity after surgery [5]. This may be intimately associated with the integrity of the ellipsoid zone and the external limiting membrane (ELM) of the retina, as the regeneration of the ellipsoid zone defect necessitates a long time after surgery [5].

Despite achieving a high closure rate, it remains certain that MHs demonstrate persistent difficulty in closing.The primary risk factors influencing MH healing include MH size (> 400 μm) [3, 6], duration [7, 8], ILM peeling adequacy [9], gas tamponade sufficiency [9], and postoperative positioning compliance [10]. Recent studies have indicated that dyslipidemia may indirectly affect MH healing through systemic microvascular dysfunction, endothelial damage, inflammation, and impaired angiogenesis. The diameter of the hole constitutes the most important factor influencing closure [7]. For ophthalmologists, the size of the hole is also a crucial consideration in selecting the appropriate surgical technique. Currently, to improve the healing rate of macular hole surgery, multiple surgical techniques emerged, including inverted ILM flap technique, central retinal detachment by subretinal balanced salt solution (BSS) injection, autologous tissue transplantation which involves the lens capsule, autologous neurosensory retina, and human amniotic membrane transplantation [5], as well as the utilization of autologous blood to enhance adhesion of the graft to the foveal site [11] and et al. The application of ILM techniques is prevalent in initial surgical interventions for MHs. However, it represents challenges for the refractory MHs, as the partial peeling of the ILM during the initial surgical procedure [8]. In the management of large idiopathic MHs (>400 μm), the anatomic closure rate achieved through ILM flap surgery, which stands at 98%, surpasses that of traditional ILM peeling, which is 88% [12]. It has been reported that ILM peeling could be suitable for holes less than 535 μm, ILM flap techniques might be suitable for holes between 535 and 799 μm, and other more invasive techniques such as the transplantation of human amniotic membrane or macular hydro dissection could be considered for holes larger than 800 μm in diameter [13].

However, the majority of research conducted on MHs has primarily concentrated on the treatment strategy involving their closure. Relatively few studies have focused on the factors associated with the healing time of MHs. Anatomic closure of the majority of MHs is achieved within three days following surgery, while delayed MH healing is observed in a minority of patients. The existing literature on the delayed healing of MHs is limited. Although there is no universally accepted definition, reports in the literature generally consider a macular hole that remains open within 4 weeks after surgery but later closes spontaneously to represent delayed closure [14]. Consequently, our objective was to undertake a comprehensive statistical analysis of the proportion and durations of delayed MH healing, as well as its impact on the recovery of postoperative defect structure and visual function. Furthermore, we aimed to explore the relevant influencing factors of delayed healing, the differences in visual function improvement between those with and without delayed MH healing, and potential intervention strategies.The primary outcome was the incidence of delayed MH closure (defined as closure > 2 weeks after surgery), and the secondary outcome was postoperative visual acuity improvement.

Materials and methods

Data source and study population

The participants in the research had to be between the ages of 18 and 80. Among the 432 eligible adults, as is shown in Fig. 1, we included 372 individuals with closure (anatomical MH closure on OCT within 2 weeks post-surgery) and 60 participants without closure within 2 weeks, all with complete pre- and postoperative data and ≥ 1 month of follow-up. An inverted ILM flap technique was used in all surgeries.

Flow chart of participants selection: 432 patients underwent MH surgery; 372 achieved closure within 2 weeks, 60 did not achieve closure within 2 weeks. Of these 60, 34 achieved closure within 1 month, 26 required a secondary procedure

This retrospective study collected patients from the first department of Vitreous and Retina in Tianjin Eye Hospital, focusing on individuals who were diagnosed and treated for macular holes(MH) between 2015 and 2024. Data quality and reliability were assessed through standardized data entry procedures, double-checking clinical records, and independent verification by two researchers.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients aged 18–80 years; (2) Clinically diagnosed idiopathic MH confirmed by OCT; (3) Underwent primary MH surgery with ILM flap technique; (4) Complete pre- and postoperative data and ≥ 1-month follow-up.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Ocular trauma history; (2) Other significant retinal or optic nerve diseases; (3) Severe visual impairment unrelated to MH; (4) Incomplete follow-up data.

Ethics statement

All the authors were thankful to the patients who agreed to participate in this article, which was approved by The Medical Ethics Committees of Tianjin Eye Hospital (KY-2024051). All authors had permission to access patient records and confirmed that all written consent was obtained.

Statistical analysis

The regression models were adjusted for covariates that have been previously linked to biomarkers and MH, including age, and sex. In this study, we performed data analysis using EmpowerStats software and logistic regression models to evaluate clinical outcomes. Baseline characteristics of the study population were categorized according to MH subgroups and described using statistical methods. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed through weighted linear regression models. To explore the association between biomarkers and MH, we conducted a multivariate linear regression analysis, calculating beta coefficients and 95% confidence intervals. Three models were used for multivariate testing: model 1 without adjustments, model 2 adjusted for gender, age, and model 3 adjusted for all relevant covariates. Smoothed curve fitting was performed with the same set of adjustments. A threshold effect analysis was employed to identify the relationship and potential inflection points between biomarkers and MH. The same statistical approach was applied to gender-specific subgroups. The significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To reduce variability within the dataset, we employed a weighting method during analysis.

Discussion

Delayed MH healing remains a significant clinical challenge, necessitating a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms. Our retrospective study identified elevated TC and TG levels as important systemic metabolic risk factors associated with delayed MH closure. Below, we explore potential biological pathways through which lipid metabolism might influence retinal repair and tissue regeneration.

Dyslipidemia has been widely implicated in systemic microvascular dysfunction, which could extend to retinal microcirculation. Elevated low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol contributes to oxidative stress and endothelial cell damage, disrupting retinal vascular homeostasis [15]. In contrast, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol is known to exhibit vasoprotective properties by reducing oxidative damage, enhancing endothelial nitric oxide production, and mitigating inflammatory responses [16]. A deficiency in HDL or an imbalance in lipid profiles could therefore compromise retinal repair by impairing vascular function and cellular integrity. Furthermore, cholesterol accumulation within retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells may exacerbate inflammation and promote lipid peroxidation [17], leading to delayed extracellular matrix remodeling, an essential process in MH closure. In experimental hypercholesterolemia models, increased cholesterol deposition in retinal tissues has been associated with choroidal thickening, vascular hypertrophy, and inflammatory cell infiltration, supporting the hypothesis that lipid dysregulation could delay MH healing [18].

Chronic inflammation plays a pivotal role in wound healing, including in the retina. Elevated systemic lipid levels have been shown to increase circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and C-reactive protein (CRP) [19]. These inflammatory mediators not only disrupt the blood-retinal barrier but also interfere with cellular migration and proliferation—critical steps in the closure of MHs [20]. Studies have demonstrated that dyslipidemia enhances macrophage activation and induces foam cell formation within retinal capillaries, which could contribute to a pro-inflammatory microenvironment unfavorable for tissue repair. Additionally, inflammatory cytokines modulate matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), enzymes responsible for extracellular matrix degradation and remodeling [21]. Dysregulation of MMP activity may result in inadequate ILM contraction and delayed tissue reorganization, prolonging the healing process. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is a key mediator of angiogenesis and plays a crucial role in the repair of retinal tissues [22]. Dyslipidemia has been linked to alterations in VEGF signaling, potentially impairing the ability of endothelial cells to regenerate damaged capillaries [23]. Inadequate neovascularization may reduce oxygen and nutrient supply to the foveal tissue, thereby slowing post-surgical tissue recovery. Recent findings suggest that lipid-rich environments suppress endothelial progenitor cell function, further diminishing angiogenic potential [24]. Moreover, hyperlipidemia-induced dysfunction of pericytes—supportive cells essential for capillary stability—may contribute to fragile neovascularization, leading to inefficient nutrient transport to healing retinal tissues. This impaired angiogenic response could be one of the key factors underlying prolonged MH healing in patients with high TC and TG levels.

Given the strong association between dyslipidemia and delayed MH healing, incorporating lipid-lowering strategies into the management of MH patients could be beneficial. Statins, widely used for their lipid-lowering and anti-inflammatory properties, have been shown to improve endothelial function and enhance microvascular repair [25]. Future studies should investigate whether preoperative lipid optimization can improve MH closure rates and postoperative visual outcomes. Additionally, anti-inflammatory agents targeting TNF-α and IL-6 pathways could be explored as adjunct therapies to mitigate inflammation-induced delays in MH healing. Nutritional interventions, including omega-3 fatty acids and antioxidants, may also promote a retinal environment conducive to faster tissue repair.

Further studies are needed to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms linking dyslipidemia to retinal wound healing. Prospective clinical trials investigating the impact of lipid-lowering interventions on MH surgical outcomes will be crucial. Additionally, exploring genetic predispositions related to lipid metabolism and retinal repair may help refine individualized treatment strategies.

Study strengths and limitations

Our retrospective approach and threshold effect analysis allow exploration of complex metabolic associations, yet inherent selection bias and unmeasured confounders such as diabetes and hypertension may have influenced outcomes.

This study has several strengths. First, it utilizes a comprehensive dataset of 432 patients, providing a robust analysis of factors influencing delayed macular hole (MH) healing. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on surgical techniques, this research explores the novel association between dyslipidemia (elevated TC and TG) and MH healing delay, contributing to the growing body of literature on metabolic influences in ophthalmology. Additionally, the use of advanced multivariate statistical models, including logistic regression and threshold effect analysis, strengthens the reliability of the findings by minimizing potential confounders. The study also holds significant clinical implications, suggesting that lipid-lowering interventions could be considered as adjunct therapies to optimize MH surgical outcomes.

However, this study has some limitations. As a retrospective study, it is subject to inherent selection bias and cannot establish causality between dyslipidemia and delayed MH healing. Furthermore, the lack of long-term follow-up data limits insights into prolonged postoperative outcomes, and potential confounding metabolic conditions, such as diabetes and hypertension, may not have been fully accounted for. Additionally, being a single-center study, the generalizability of the findings to broader populations is restricted. Appart from these, the statistical analysis has some limitations: pre-operative OCT biomarkers, macular hole stage, base diameter, and surrounding retinal characteristics were not considered. Whether gender and race were adjusted as risk factors for the success of macular hole surgery—specifically for fresh or chronic macular holes—remains unclear. Finally, while we hypothesize that lipid dysregulation affects inflammation and angiogenesis in MH healing, direct molecular or experimental validation was not performed, necessitating further mechanistic studies in future research.

Conclusion

This study identifies a significant association between dyslipidemia and delayed healing after macular hole surgery, highlighting the potential impact of systemic metabolic factors on retinal recovery. These findings align with previous studies linking systemic metabolic disorders to retinal healing delays. Prospective studies focusing on lipid management interventions are warranted.Understanding the mechanisms linking dyslipidemia to MH healing could provide new insights into optimizing treatment strategies and improving patient care.
