The utility of high-frequency ultrasound for identification of spermatogenesis
Authors: Nicholas A Deebel, Joshua A Halpern
Published: 2025

Prognostication in reproductive urology has always been challenging. Numerous studies have utilized physical examination findings, hormone levels, genetic testing, and other biomarkers to predict the presence of sperm production in men with nonobstructive azoospermia (NOA), the likelihood of semen parameter upgrading after varicocelectomy, and a wide range of other sperm-related outcomes. By and large, these algorithms have yielded poor accuracy and limited clinical utility. However, a recent study from Nariyoshi et al. (1) demonstrated a novel approach toward predicting the presence of spermatogenesis in men with NOA using high-frequency scrotal ultrasound (US). They found that the presence of thicker seminiferous tubules on US was associated with improved odds of successful sperm retrieval.

The current study from Nariyoshi et al. (1, 2) builds upon this prior work by using the same US-based approach to men with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (HH). Nearly 70%−90% of men with HH will have sperm in the ejaculate after adequate hormone therapy, although the timeframe for recovery of spermatogenesis is variable (3, 4, 5). In this retrospective, observational study, the investigators sought to determine if the change in appearance of the seminiferous tubules on US could reliably predict the return of sperm to the ejaculate in response to gonadotropin therapy.

A total of 10 azoospermic men with HH received human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) three times weekly with the addition of recombinant follicle-stimulating hormone (rFSH) 3 months after the start of hCG (2). Scrotal US and semen analysis were performed before treatment, 3 months after hCG therapy, 1 month after combined therapy, and then every 3 months up to 12 months. Overall, 9 men demonstrated return of sperm to the ejaculate with a median time of 4.5 months after initiating hCG with rFSH. The investigators found a significant increase in density of “thick” seminiferous tubules from baseline at 3 months hCG plus rFSH therapy, and both increased testicular volume and thick seminiferous tubule density were positively correlated with semen parameters. Perhaps most importantly, the appearance of thick seminiferous tubules very closely preceded the appearance of sperm in the ejaculate, indicating that this was a reliable marker of spermatogenesis.

The study does have limitations insofar as it is small and retrospective, which may limit generalizability (2). The investigators do not discuss the learning curve for seminiferous tubule assessment by an expert ultrasonographer nor is there a discussion of interoperator or intraoperator variability. As such, it is not clear whether US can be easily and reliably implemented for seminiferous tubule assessment in reproductive urology clinics.

Nonetheless, an US-based approach could improve care pathways in men with HH. The use of US, which is noninvasive and has limited cost, could obviate the need for regular (every 1−3 month) semen testing during gonadotropin therapy and may reduce both the cost and psychological burden for men during this timeframe. This would be similarly applicable to men with azoospermia because of exogenous androgen administration who are undergoing gonadotropin therapy. However, given the ubiquity and decreasing cost of at-home semen testing in many countries, the use of US in lieu of semen analysis for assessment of pharmacologic response may have only modest benefit. For the minority of men with HH who do not have return of sperm to the ejaculate after 1−2 years of gonadotropin therapy, US may also allow for optimal timing of sperm retrieval through detection of healthy tubules amenable to sperm extraction.

Across their two studies, Nariyoshi et al. (1, 2) demonstrate that high-frequency US is yet another tool among many in the diagnosis, treatment, and prognostication of male factor infertility. Future work is needed to clarify interobserver reliability and the precise clinical care pathways that will most benefit from its use—whether for men with NOA (to assist with preoperative or intraoperative identification of spermatogenesis in men undergoing microdissection testicular sperm extraction), men with HH (to minimize the frequency of semen testing and expedite in vitro fertilization), or perhaps even to replace testicular biopsy in men with azoospermia of unclear etiology (e.g., normal testicular volume and equivocal FSH levels). Given that high-frequency US is a noninvasive, low-risk, and low-cost ambulatory procedure that relies on technology that already resides in many reproductive urology clinics, we encourage and look forward to additional studies that may further define the role for this diagnostic approach in clinical care pathways.
