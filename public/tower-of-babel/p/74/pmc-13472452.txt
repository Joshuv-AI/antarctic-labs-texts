Reimagining periodontal education in India: The need for a modern, competency-based curriculum
Authors: Neeraj Deshpande
Published: 2026

Dental education in India stands at an important crossroads. Over the last two decades, advances in biomaterials, digital dentistry, implantology, regenerative therapies, and evidence-based clinical protocols have transformed periodontal practice worldwide. Yet, much of our undergraduate and postgraduate dental training continues to rely on traditional teaching models that prioritize procedural numbers and examination performance over true clinical competency and patient-centred outcomes.

The need for curriculum reform becomes even more significant when we recognize that the last BDS curriculum in India was introduced by the Dental Council of India in 2007, while the MDS curriculum was revised in 2017. Since then, dentistry has evolved rapidly, and expectations from graduates and specialists entering clinical practice and academia have changed considerably.

As educators and clinicians, we must ask ourselves an important question: Are we preparing students for the realities of modern periodontal practice, or merely training them to fulfil curricular requirements? The answer lies in developing a curriculum that is modern, competency-based, outcome-oriented, and globally benchmarked, while remaining practical and adaptable to Indian healthcare realities.

Globally, periodontal education has progressively shifted toward competency-based learning. Modern frameworks focus not only on theoretical knowledge and technical skills, but also on clinical decision-making, communication, ethics, interdisciplinary care, digital literacy, and evidence-based treatment planning. Increasingly, the emphasis is on what a student can independently and responsibly perform in real clinical situations.

In contrast, dental training in India often remains examination-oriented. Students may complete mandatory clinical quotas without necessarily developing confidence in diagnosis, comprehensive treatment planning, supportive periodontal care, or management of complex cases. At the postgraduate level, areas such as implantology, peri-implant disease management, digital workflows, CBCT interpretation, research methodology, and scientific communication require stronger and more structured integration.

A competency-based curriculum fundamentally changes the philosophy of education. The focus shifts from “How many procedures were completed?” to “Can the student diagnose, plan, execute, and maintain treatment predictably, ethically, and independently?” Such an approach is essential if we wish to produce clinicians who are scientifically sound, clinically competent, and globally relevant.

At the same time, although benchmarking against international educational models is important, adopting Western frameworks without adapting them to the Indian context may not be feasible. Our patient numbers, institutional diversity, resource availability, and public healthcare realities are unique. The goal should not be imitation, but intelligent adaptation.

It is encouraging to acknowledge the efforts being made by the National Dental Commission to involve educators, specialty societies, academic leaders, and stakeholders in designing a new curriculum framework. One sincerely hopes that these collective efforts will lead to meaningful reforms that strengthen dental education and ultimately improve patient care. The need of the hour is not merely a revised syllabus, but a reimagined educational philosophy—one that prioritizes competency over procedural numbers and prepares future clinicians to deliver ethical, evidence-based, and patient-centred periodontal care.
