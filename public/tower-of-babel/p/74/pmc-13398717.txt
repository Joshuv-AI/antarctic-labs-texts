Assessment of Thyroid Uptake Ratio Values in a Normal Population. Establishment of Normal Reference Values for 99mTc‐Pertechnetate Thyroid Uptake in an Australian Population
Authors: Bethany Wornes, Amalia Karahalios, Melanie Tempest
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThyroid scintigraphy with 99mTc‐pertechnetate is a nuclear medicine examination performed since the early 1970s that allows for functional assessment of the thyroid. Quantitative thyroid uptake expressed as a percentage of the injected dose is calculated in many centres as part of the examination. Our current normal institutional reference of 1%–4% was derived from a 1971 study. Uptake values may change with geographical location and over time, with international studies reporting updated normal reference values for local populations of 0.2%–2.0%, 0.2%–2.0%, 0.26%–1.64% and 0.4%–2.4%. Introduction

Thyroid scintigraphy with 99mTc‐pertechnetate is a routine nuclear medicine examination that plays a valuable role in localisation and characterisation of thyroid tissue [1]. Common clinical indications include assessment of abnormal thyroid function consistent with overt or subclinical hyperthyroidism, evaluation of multinodular goitre to determine number and function of nodules, and assessment of suitability for radioiodine therapy for thyrotoxicosis [1].

Our institution, Western Health, in Melbourne, Australia, caters for a population that is multicultural, socioeconomically diverse and among the fastest growing in Australia [2]. The Nuclear Medicine Department at our institution performs around one hundred 99mTc‐pertechnetate thyroid scans per year. Quantitative thyroid uptake expressed as a percentage of the injected dose is a useful tool in the management of patients with thyroid disorders [3]. It is routinely calculated as part of the examination at our institution and is a standard inclusion in the clinical report. The current normal reference values used at our institution of 1%–4% are based on data from a 1971 study on a population from the United States [4]. A contemporaneous study from the United Kingdom also reported an upper limit of 4% [5].

Anecdotally, we noted that uptake values in this normal range were not always associated with the clinical context, biochemical findings and qualitative assessment of the image. We manually derived the thyroid uptake ratio values calculated by the software to ensure that they were correct. Given that our manual derivation matched that produced by the software, we were circumspect about the appropriateness of the normal reference range for our population, raising the question on the validity of the normal reference range. The aim of this study was to establish reference values for 99mTc‐pertechnetate thyroid update in a Victorian, Australian patient population.

Methods

The study was approved by the Western Health Office of Research QA2020.110 and covered both retrospective and prospective data collections. The need for written informed consent was waived based on the National Health and Medical Research Council, [6] Ethical Considerations in Quality Assurance and Evaluation Activities (2014) guidelines.

Thyroid scintigraphy is performed as a routine component of parathyroid scintigraphy at our institution. A prospective study was conducted calculating thyroid uptake on anterior thyroid static images acquired following intravenous injection of 99mTc‐pertechnetate for patients undergoing parathyroid scintigraphy at our department of Nuclear Medicine from January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2021. A retrospective study for examinations performed between January 1, 2017, and January 1, 2021, was also conducted. The retrospective component was undertaken to increase the sample size and better represent the patient population in our institution. In total, 271 consecutive examinations were identified and assessed.

Imaging Protocol

All patients were scanned using a standard thyroid scintigraphy protocol. Images were acquired 15–25 min following intravenous administration of 50 MBq 99mTc‐pertechnetate. Patients were imaged with a gamma camera equipped with a low‐energy, high‐resolution, parallel‐hole collimator. The patient was positioned head first and supine. Images were acquired with a 256 × 256 matrix for a total of 300 s with a zoom of 1.00. 99mTc‐pertechnetate imaging was performed prior to intravenous injection of 99mTc‐Sestamibi for parathyroid scintigraphy.

Calculation of Thyroid Uptake

The number of counts in the thyroid was determined by drawing a region of interest around the borders of the gland. A separate smaller square region of interest was drawn over the shoulder region making sure to avoid any vascular structures for background subtraction. All counts obtained were corrected for the decay of 99mTc‐pertechnetate and acquisition time.

There were 77 parathyroid scans performed from January 2021 to December 2021. Of these, 31 met the inclusion criteria. A dose calibrator was used to measure the residual activity of the 99mTc‐pertechnetate syringe post intravenous injection. Across the 2021 cohort, an average residual activity of 0.92 MBq was established.

Participants prior to January 2021 did not have residual activity recordings, as this was not routinely measured for standard parathyroid scintigraphy protocols. To account for this, a residual of 1 MBq was derived from the average of the prospective cohort and historical thyroid residual doses.

Statistical Analysis

Distribution of the planar uptake ratio was visually inspected using a histogram. The association between sex and planar uptake ratio, and time and planar uptake ratio were visually assessed with box‐and‐whisker plots, and the association between age and planar uptake ratio was visually assessed using a scatter plot.

We fitted linear regression models to assess the association between sex and age (years) and the outcome, planar uptake ratio, with the outcome on the log scale.

The planar uptake ratio had a highly skewed distribution; hence, the reference range was calculated using the natural logarithm and then back transformed (i.e., took the exponential values) to obtain the reference range on the natural scale. We also completed a sensitivity analysis with only those participants who had a TSH blood test done in the last 6 months.

Discussion

A new reference range of 0.4%–2.4% for thyroid uptake values was established in this study. The new range is lower than the reference range of 1%–4%, which was previously used at our institution. The goal of this study was to define a thyroid uptake reference range for the Western Health patient population using patient data from our own hospital catchment. This goal was achieved by recruiting patients consecutively with specific inclusion and exclusion points from the study. The structure of this study meant that there was no additional burden placed on the patient or any alterations to care pathways. There was no additional radiation and no compromises to patient privacy.

This study was a single centre study involving both prospective and retrospective components. We acknowledge the limitations of retrospective data and encourage future prospective studies.

The prospective cohort had 31 participants; as such, the retrospective component was undertaken to recruit more participants and better represent the patient population. The small number of participants in this 2021 cohort meant that, although there was a decrease in planar uptake ratio (Table 5) compared to the retrospective cohort, there were not enough participants to draw a robust conclusion. It is speculated that several factors may have contributed to this decrease, including more accurate measurement of residual doses during 2021, tighter adherence to timing and workflow in prospective data collection, and minor procedural variations between staff members. It is also plausible that patient characteristics have shifted, given the ensuing COVID‐19 lockdowns that continued into 2021 in Victoria and the knock‐on effects of dine‐in eating habits.

The catchment that our institution caters for is multicultural, socioeconomically diverse and among the fastest growing in Australia [2]. Given the vast cultural diversity of our patient population, this study did not aim to address the individual cultural demographics that may impact thyroid evaluation using 99mTc‐pertechnetate [7]. It is thought that the reference range derived from this study reflects the characteristics of this clinical catchment. Although ethnicity was not analysed as a separate variable, the diversity of the population likely contributes to the applicability of these values within our local setting.

Historically, residual syringe activity for 99mTc‐pertechnetate was not recorded for parathyroid studies at our institution, as the primary purpose of these examinations was assessment of the parathyroid glands rather than thyroid quantification. Consequently, individual residual values were unavailable for the retrospective cohort. To address this, we applied an estimated residual activity of 1 MBq, derived from the mean measured residual in the prospective 2021 cohort (0.92 MBq), historical thyroid residual dose recordings, syringe type, and a similar workflow used across all study years. While the use of an estimated value introduces some uncertainty into the retrospective administered‐dose calculations, the estimate is considered a reasonable reflection of typical practice. Small differences in true residual activity are thought to have potentially contributed to the variation observed between the retrospective and prospective planar uptake ratios.

There were six criteria applied to both prospective and retrospective participant cohorts.

Patients who met the first five criteria and appeared to meet the sixth criteria were included despite not having undergone blood TSH testing in the 6 months pre or post scan. This presented another limitation relating to the incomplete availability of TSH measurements. While 98 (64.9%) participants had blood results confirming euthyroid, 53 (35.1%) were included based on clinical assessment and imaging findings alone. To assess the impact of this, a sensitivity analysis was performed using only participants who had a TSH measurement completed within 6 months of imaging. This analysis did not change the median uptake value or the calculated reference range, supporting the robustness of the inclusion criteria. However, the absence of biochemical confirmation for a proportion of participants introduces the potential for inaccuracy, and future studies would benefit from routine thyroid function testing for all participants.

This study provides an evidence‐based range relevant to a Victorian, Australian population. Given that sufficient iodine nutrition is required for thyroid hormone health [8], the departure from 1% to 4% reported in 1971 is hypothesised to be representative of the geographical and transitory variations in dietary iodine over time [9]. Dietary influences such as the discontinuation of iodophor as a disinfectant of milking parlours in the Australian dairy industry [10] or the mandatory introduction of iodinated salt use in commercial bread production in Australia and New Zealand in 2009 [11] are thought to have contributed to the variations in iodine deficiency and sufficiency over time.

This study, along with others from around the world [12, 13, 14, 15] demonstrated no meaningful difference in thyroid uptake ratios between biological sexes. This study found a slight negative association between age and log uptake ratios, a trend replicated in studies from Nepal, Saudi Arabia, Türkiye, Namibia and the United Kingdom [12, 13, 14, 15, 16], indicating that age, not biological sex, is a more relevant factor influencing thyroid uptake.

The findings of this study are also consistent with findings from research in other countries, mirroring the geographical deviations found by similar studies conducted around the world. Globally, an overall thyroid uptake range of 0.26%–2.9% [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18] was observed.

Thyroid hormones play a critical role in human physiology and are vital in growth, neuronal development, reproduction and energy metabolism [19]. Thyroid dysfunction conditions like hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism are common and can have profoundly adverse effects on patient health if untreated [19]. Thyroid scintigraphy is a key nuclear medicine examination that assists clinicians in the delineation of abnormal thyroid function.

The reporting of a nuclear medicine thyroid scan involves assessment of both the qualitative and quantitative properties of the imaging. While qualitative assessment, clinical context and biochemical status are important considerations when interpreting the images, thyroid uptake values provide an objective quantitative measure of function and are an established component of the clinical report. It is therefore important that the normal reference values are accurate and representative of the local population.

This study did not assess the ethnicity of participants which is known to affect thyroid development [7], and it is recommended that future larger cohort multicentre studies should explore this further. Urinary iodine measurements [8] and mandatory thyroid function blood testing could also be a consideration for future studies to ensure the iodine sufficiency of populations and to further evidence the accuracy of normal thyroid uptake ranges.

Conclusion

Our results provide a new understanding of thyroid uptake values using 99mTc‐pertechnetate within the context of an Australian population, addressing a gap in existing knowledge. There was a slight negative association between age and log uptake ratios but no difference between sexes. The calculated normal reference range in this study (0.4%–2.4%) was found to be less than reported in the 1971 study in a US population.

The thyroid uptake reference range has been updated at our institution, with the values now relevant to the local population.
