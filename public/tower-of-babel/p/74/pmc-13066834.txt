Phenotyping preserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm) using quantitative high-resolution computed tomography imaging features
Authors: Jun Hu, Zhujin Shen, Tong Su, Yu Fang, Wen Yang, Chen Chu, Xin Peng, Chao Zou, Jian He, Xiaoyan Xin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm) is characterized by decreased forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) with a preserved FEV1/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio. It is a highly prevalent and heterogeneous condition which is associated with increased risk of respiratory symptoms, developing airway limitation, and mortality. However, there are few radiological studies related to phenotyping of PRISm. We aimed to investigate the airway changes dependent on emphysema, and to evaluate the correlation between quantitative high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) parameters and pulmonary function parameters, with the purpose of exploring the possible phenotypes of PRISm based on quantitative HRCT.MethodsThis retrospective study enrolled 102 cases with PRISm from December 2020 to July 2023. All patients underwent inspiration computed tomography (CT) scanning and pulmonary function testing (PFT) within 3 months. The parenchyma and airway parameters were measured by automatic segmentation software (IntelliSpace Portal) with quantitative and semi-quantitative analysis, respectively. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the differences in demographic data, PFT characteristics, and CT parameters among groups. Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to investigate the correlation between quantitative CT parameters and lung function indices.ResultsThe parenchyma and airway parameters, including total lung capacity on CT (TLCCT) and wall area percentage (WA%), displayed significant differences along with the severity of emphysema (P=0.008; P=0.004). Most of the parenchyma parameters had mild to moderate correlations with the airway parameters, among which the correlation between TLCCT and WA% was the strongest (r=−0.64, P<0.001). The TLCCT and WA% also showed statistical correlation with percentage predicted FVC (FVC% pred) that reflected restrictive ventilatory impairment (RVI) (r=0.27, P=0.006; r=−0.34, P<0.001). Based on the degree of emphysema and bronchial wall thickness, four possible radiological phenotypes were issued. There were significant differences in TLCCT, FVC/TLCCT, lumen area (LA), and WA%, and parts of lung function parameters among the four phenotypes (all P<0.05).ConclusionsThere were differences in quantitative parenchyma and airway parameters and parts of PFT results in different morphological phenotypes based on HRCT, which may contribute to the stratification of PRISm patients by a novel radiological method.Keywords: Preserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm), high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT), quantitative analysis, radiological phenotypes

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a heterogeneous lung condition characterized by chronic respiratory symptoms and airflow obstruction (1). Although it is a preventable and treatable disease, it is still a major cause of morbidity and mortality globally, and the COPD burden is expected to increase in coming decades (2). At present, the diagnostic criterion of COPD is the forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) <0.70 after bronchodilator inhalation, mainly dependent on the pulmonary function test (PFT). Abnormal results of PFT examination will only occur when the lung function impairment exceeds 30%, and small airway restriction exceeds 75% (3). Since it is a heterogeneous condition, the early diagnosis of COPD is still a challenge. There are no obvious respiratory symptoms in some patients during the early stage, and the early diagnosis and standardized treatment of COPD patients based on PFT is difficult. It is reported that the earlier the patients receive treatment, the more the lung function improves, emphasizing the importance of early diagnosis for COPD patients (4).

There is a precursor condition of COPD, called preserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm), that is characterized by preserved ratio (FEV1/FVC ≥0.70 after bronchodilation) but impaired spirometry (FEV1 <80% of reference) (5). The prevalence of PRISm is particularly high in current and former smokers, and it is associated with increased risk of respiratory symptoms, cardiopulmonary disease, cardiovascular mortality, hospitalization, and an increased hazard of developing airway limitation (5-8). PRISm is not always a stable phenotype and can transform into both CT-normal and obstructed airflow limitation, or remain as persistent PRISm (9). Research has shown that around 20–30% of PRISm cases transitioned to airflow limitation over time, and were associated with lower baseline FEV1%, FEV1/FVC, and lower FVC/total lung capacity (TLC), higher age, current smoking, female gender, and a longer forced expiratory time (FET) in the second assessment (7,9-11). Therefore, PRISm cases should be considered “patients” and deserve care and treatment, because they already experience symptoms and/or have functional and/or structural abnormalities (5).

In recent years, quantitative high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) has played an important role in the research of PRISm and COPD, especially the phenotypes of COPD. Several studies have reported three morphological phenotypes of COPD, called A phenotype, E phenotype, and M phenotype, based on emphysema and airway changes on chest HRCT. A study showed that the three phenotypes display different clinical characteristics and responsiveness to treatment (12). Another study established that the three phenotypes show different correlations between PFT, computed tomography (CT) volume, and magnetic resonance (MR) perfusion, indicating that novel imaging methods may guide clinical management (13). Meanwhile, some of the latest studies issued nearly 10 phenotypes of COPD based on visual or quantitative CT imaging features, which have different clinical characteristics, rates of progression, and risk of mortality, providing a potentially useful tool to characterize underlying pathological processes of heterogeneity in COPD patients (14,15). These findings provided evidence for the classified management of COPD. In terms of PRISm, there are few studies related to the classification of PRISm cases based on chest CT. It has been reported that centrilobular emphysema (CLE) and paraseptal emphysema (PSE) were common in smokers with PRISm, but only CLE was associated with air-trapping, indicating different physiological roles of these emphysema subtypes (16). However, the role of airway changes in PRISm is not discussed, and the interaction of emphysema and airway changes or HRCT phenotypes of PRISm is unknown.

In our study, we performed a quantitative analysis of the lung parenchyma and bronchi in patients with PRISm to explore the interaction of emphysema and airway changes and potential phenotypes of PRISm based on chest HRCT. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-2051/rc).

Methods

Ethical approval

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, The Affiliated Hospital of Medical School, Nanjing University (No. 2022-449-02), and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Patients

This retrospective study included 102 respiratory clinic patients or inpatients from the Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, The Affiliated Hospital of Medical School, Nanjing University, from December 2020 to July 2023. All patients underwent PFTs and inspiration CT scanning. PRISm was defined as post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ≥0.70 and FEV1 <80% predicted (pred), according to the 2024 Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) (5). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) incomplete clinical data (n=13); (II) poor image quality (n=21); (III) acute lung infection, interstitial pneumonia, lung mass or surgery history, multiple bronchiectasis or diffuse emphysema (n=45); and (IV) software could not calculate (n=6) (Figure 1). Finally, 102 patients with PRISm were enrolled in this study.

Flow chart of patients enrolled in the study. CT, computed tomography; HRCT, high-resolution computed tomography; PFT, pulmonary function test.

Spirometry and clinical data collection

Following the guidelines of the American Thoracic Society (ATS) and European Respiratory Society (ERS) (17), the patients underwent PFT (MasterScreen, Jaeger, Hoechberg, Germany) within 3 months before or after CT examination. FEV1, percentage predicted FEV1 (FEV1% pred), FVC, percentage predicted FVC (FVC% pred), and FEV1/FVC were tested according to the reference values for a Chinese population. With at least three measurements, the best value was obtained. Besides, basic clinical parameters such as age, sex, smoking status, and body mass index (BMI) were evaluated in our study.

CT and quantitative parameters

CT examinations were performed by using a 64-MDCT scanner (Brilliance 64, Philips Healthcare, Cleveland, OH, USA) or a 16-MDCT scanner (Aquilion TSX-101A, Toshiba, Tokyo, Japan). After multiple respiratory training, all chest CT scans were obtained in the supine position of patient, at end inspiration. The following parameters were used: a tube voltage of 120 kVp, automatic tube current modulation (ATCM) technique, a reconstruction matrix of 512×512 pixels, a section thickness of 1.5 mm, and an interval of 1.5 mm. Both technicians and radiologists were double-blinded to the clinical indices and pulmonary function of the patients.

All CT images were evaluated using an automatic lung image analysis tool (IntelliSpace Portal 7.0, Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) (18). There were four steps: lung segmentation, lung density measurement, airway extraction, and airway measurement. Using a model-based algorithm, the lungs and lobes are segmented automatically. Then, the trachea was extracted automatically and verified. The TLC on CT (TLCCT) was calculated automatically. The FVC/TLCCT ratio was calculated as an imaging-derived parameter to potentially reflect the presence of hyperinflation (lower ratio) or a restrictive pattern (higher ratio in the context of low FVC) (10). Low-attenuation area percentage (LAA%) was defined as percent lung volumes with a threshold of −950 and −910 Hounsfield units (HU) (19,20) (Figure 2).

In terms of airway, the parameters of wall, lumen, and airway from fourth to sixth grade bronchus were obtained, and wall area percentage (WA%) was calculated further. In detail, quantitative analysis of airway was performed in the fourth, fifth, and sixth generations of the following bronchial pathways: LB1 (apicoposterior segment of the left upper lobe), LB4 (superior segment of the lingula), and LB10 (posterobasal segment of the left lower lobe), RB1 (apical segment of the right upper lobe), RB4 (lateral segment of the right middle lobe), and RB10 (posterobasal segment of the right lower lobe) (21). In each segmental pathway, airway dimensions, including WA, wall thickness, lumen area (LA), airway area, and WA%, were measured and calculated. The WA% was defined as follows: WA% = WA/(WA + LA) × 100 (12). The mean value of the measured parameters of the fourth, fifth, and sixth generations of bronchus was used for statistical analysis. All the segmentations and measurements were automatically performed by the software and were supervised by two radiologists (T.S. and J Hu with 2 and 6 years of experience, respectively).

Then, the patients were divided into a no emphysema group, mild emphysema group, and medium emphysema group by a dividing value of 0.5% and 3% of LAA−950. Based on the value of 0.5% of LAA−950 and 55% of WA%, we further divided the patients into four groups as follows: with LAA−950 ≤0.5% and WA% ≤55% (CT-normal phenotype), with LAA−950 ≤0.5% and WA% >55% (airway-dominant phenotype), with LAA−950 >0.5% and WA% ≤55% (emphysema-dominant phenotype), with LAA−950 >0.5% and WA% >55% (mixed phenotype).

Statistical analysis

All statistical data were analyzed using a commercially available software (SPSS 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed with multigroup comparisons, a least significance difference (LSD) was used for pairwise comparison, and Tamhane’s T2 nonparametric test was used when the variance was uneven. The skewed distribution data were presented as the median and interquartile range, and Mann-Whitney and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used for comparison among the three groups. A Chi-squared test was used to analyze the categorical variables. Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to investigate the correlation between quantitative CT parameters and lung function indices. Statistical significance was indicated when P<0.05.

Both LAA−950 and LAA−910 were significantly higher in the mild emphysema group and medium emphysema group than that of no emphysema group, as well as the medium emphysema group compared with the mild emphysema group. The TLCCT was increased, and the FVC/TLCCT was decreased along with the degree of emphysema. As for airway parameters, the results showed that the LA were significantly different among the three groups, as well as WA%. To be specific, the LA was higher in both the mild emphysema group and medium emphysema group than it was in the no emphysema group, and the WA% of medium emphysema group was significantly lower than that of no emphysema group.

Correlation between parenchyma and airway parameters on CT

The above results showed that most parenchyma and airway parameters displayed significant differences along with the severity of emphysema. Thus, we further analyzed the correlation between parenchyma and airway parameters.

We observed significant correlations between parenchymal and airway parameters. TLCCT showed moderate to strong negative correlation with WA% (r=−0.64, P<0.001), and positive correlation with LA (r=0.60, P<0.001). LAA−910 also correlated moderately with WA% (r=−0.59, P<0.001) and LA (r=0.50, P<0.001) (Table 2). These findings indicate that increasing emphysema and lung volume are associated with thinner airway walls and larger LAs in the PRISm cohort.

The possible phenotypes of PRISm

In line with that exhibited above, the results showed that the parameters reflecting bronchial wall thickening were much more severe along with the decrease of emphysema. Besides, some of the CT parameters showed significant correlation with PFT results. Therefore, it is worthwhile to explore the potential phenotypes of PRISm based on emphysema and bronchial wall thickening. Based on the parameters reflecting bronchial wall thickening and emphysema, four possible imaging phenotypes were issued in our study, as shown in Figure 4.

There were significant differences in sex, BMI, and smoking status among the CT-normal group, airway-dominant group, emphysema-dominant group, and mixed group. In terms of pulmonary function parameters, there were significant differences in FEV1 and FVC among the four groups; nearly the same trend was observed in FEV1% pred and FVC% pred. Both LAA−950 and LAA−910 were significantly higher in the emphysema-dominant phenotype and mixed phenotype than that of the CT-normal and airway-dominant phenotypes. The TLCCT was lowest in the airway-dominant group as compared with the emphysema-dominant and mixed phenotypes, and the FVC/TLCCT in the emphysema group was lower than that of airway-dominant phenotype and mixed phenotype (Figure 5, Table 4).

When analyzing airway parameters, the results showed that the wall thickness was higher than it was in the CT-normal and emphysema-dominant group, and the WA% was highest and the LA was lowest in the airway-dominant group, as compared with other groups. In terms of mixed phenotype, the results showed that higher wall thickness, lower LA, and higher WA% were found as compared with the emphysema-dominant phenotype, with contrary results to those of the airway-dominant phenotype (Figure 5, Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, PRISm patients were classified into groups based on the severity of emphysema, and correlation analysis between the parenchyma and airway parameters, quantitative CT parameters, and lung function parameters were performed. We aimed to explore the potential phenotypes in patients with PRISm based on the parameters of emphysema and airway obtained by quantitative analysis on chest HRCT.

In recent years, semi-quantitative or quantitative analysis on chest CT has been increasingly applied in the study of PRISm and COPD (12,13,16,22,23). In our study, the parenchyma and airway parameters were evaluated using an automatic lung image analysis tool (IntelliSpace Portal 7.0, Philips Healthcare) (18) by semi-quantitative methods. The parenchyma parameters, including LAA−950, LAA−910, and TLCCT, were automatically calculated, and the airway parameters, including WA, LA, airway area, and WA%, were obtained by semi-quantitative analysis. LAA is usually used to quantify emphysematous changes in COPD patients, and the most commonly used threshold value is −950 HU (5), which reflects the strongest correlation with pathologic changes of emphysema (24). Thus, it is the main parameter of emphysema quantification and the basis of grouping in our study. Besides, a meta-analysis has shown that LAA with a threshold value of −910 HU showed significant correlation with FEV1/FVC, DLCO, and clinical symptoms (20), which is the reason for evaluating the LAA−910 in our research. Studies have reported that TLCCT in PRISm was lower than that in healthy controls (25), and reduced FVC/TLCCT ratio in PRISm is associated with increased symptoms, radiographic emphysema and gas trapping, exacerbations, and progression to COPD (10), which is why we explore the role of TLCCT and FVC/TLCCT in different phenotypes of PRISm patients. In terms of airway, the indices of wall, lumen, and WA% have been used to differentiate PRISm cases from CT-normal or COPD cases (22,26). As a result, we also evaluated the airway parameters in cases with PRISm and the interaction with parenchyma parameters.

It has been reported that TLCCT is lower in PRISm patients than that in healthy controls (25), and our results showed that TLCCT gradually increases with emphysema severity in PRISm patients, suggesting possible phenotypes associated with emphysema in PRISm. Our results also showed that FVC/TLCCT gradually decreases with emphysema severity, which is consistent with the results of Fortis et al. (10), indicating the higher severity of emphysema in PRISm patients, the probably higher risk of progressing to COPD (10). In regard to airway parameters, we found that LA gradually increased, but WA% gradually decreased significantly with emphysema severity, which is partially consistent with Konietzke et al. (27). They found that the LA and airway diameter in the upper lung lobes gradually increased along with GOLD stages 1–4, which showed the same trend as we found in PRISm patients. They also reported that the wall thickness in the upper lung lobes gradually increased, and WA% did not show significant differences with the increase of GOLD stages. For the lower lung lobes, they found that airway diameter, wall thickness, and WA% were highest in GOLD 2, but without statistical significance of LA. These results suggested that the mechanisms of airway inflammation and remodeling in patients with PRISm are partially similar to COPD patients, and the possible airway changes in patients with PRISm in different lung lobes are worth further explored.

We also found that most of the parenchyma parameters had mild to moderate correlations with the airway parameters, among which the correlation between TLCCT and WA% was the strongest. The TLCCT was negatively correlated with WA and WA%, suggesting the possible phenotype of bronchial wall thickening with a lower TLCCT, which may be more severe in PRISm than the phenotype with normal airway wall, as reported by Zhao et al. (25). Nambu et al. reported that a threshold of −910 HU was better than that of −950 HU for correlations with physiological measurements in patients with mild COPD (19). Our results also showed that parts of airway parameters showed mild correlations with LAA−950, whereas more indices of airway exhibited stronger correlations with LAA−910, indicating that LAA−910 may be a more sensitive indice in patients with PRISm, a precursor condition of COPD. Overall, these results suggested that the possible interaction between emphysema and airway changes may play important roles in the pathogenesis in patients with PRISm.

Besides, most of the parenchyma and airway parameters display a statistically significant relationship with parts of pulmonary function results that are associated with ventilatory function. LAA−910 showed mild correlation with FEV1 and FVC, which was not observed in LAA−950, suggesting LAA−910 may be an early indicator of lung tissue destruction and is associated with lung function in PRISm. It is reported that TLCCT was correlated with FVC% pred in GOLD 0–4 stages of COPD patients (27). Our results showed that TLCCT showed mild to moderate correlation with FEV1, FVC, and FVC% pred, except for FEV1% pred and FEV1/FVC in PRISm patients, indicating that TLCCT better reflects the changes in restrictive ventilatory impairment (RVI), as compared with obstructive ventilatory impairment (OVI), and the impact of pulmonary parenchymal changes on pulmonary function is different in patients with PRISm and those with COPD. In terms of airway indices, the LA and WA% displayed mild to moderate correlation with FEV1, FVC, and FVC% pred, except for FEV1% pred and FEV1/FVC, further verifying that the airway changes in PRISm patients may also mainly affect RVI.

Based on the parameters of emphysema and airway obtained by quantitative analysis on chest CT, we further explored the potential phenotypes in patients with PRISm, and four possible phenotypes are issued in our study. In terms of the airway-dominant phenotype, the wall thickness and WA% were highest and LA was lowest among the four phenotypes. Additionally, the TLCCT and FVC% pred of the airway-dominant group were lowest and FVC/TLCCT was highest among the four phenotypes. According to the ERS/ATS update (28), RVI is defined by a low TLC and FVC% pred, which mainly results from pulmonary fibrosis (29). The latest research has reported that lower lung function and increased airway wall thickness were associated with interstitial lung abnormality (ILA) among participants with PRISm (30), indicating that the airway-dominant phenotype may be a typical but severe phenotype in PRISm patients, with a main manifestation of airway wall thickness and RVI. Whether these patients are combined with ILA is worthy of further exploration. The emphysema-dominant phenotype showed higher prevalence of smokers, lower wall thickness, lower WA%, and higher LA than that of the airway-dominant phenotype. Besides, the TLCCT was highest and FVC/TLCCT was lowest in the emphysema-dominant phenotype among the four phenotypes, and the FEV1/FVC was lowest in this phenotype but without statistical significance, suggesting that this phenotype may be at higher risk of progression to COPD (10). In terms of the mixed phenotype, the results showed that lower TLCCT, higher FVC/TLCCT, higher wall thickness, lower LA, and higher WA% were found as compared with the emphysema-dominant phenotype, with contrary results to those of the airway-dominant phenotype. It has been reported that the M phenotype in COPD patients shows severe OVI and higher rate of exacerbation or hospitalization (12,13). The above results indicated that the mixed phenotype in PRISm patients may be different to the M phenotype in COPD patients, which warrants large sample confirmation.

There are some limitations in this study. Most importantly, the number of enrolled patients was relatively small, resulting in a small sample in the four groups of phenotypes, which may reduce the confidence level of the study and requires further confirmation via a large sample size study. Second, although we have performed respiratory training for these patients, there were still differences in the degree of inhalation among different patients, which may affect the quantitative analysis results of airway parameters on chest CT. Third, the classification criteria for PRISm phenotypes lack a reference basis, since there were few previous studies exploring the phenotypes based on chest CT. As a result, we explored the possible imaging phenotypes of PRISm patients with reference to the CT phenotypes of COPD patients; large-sample studies are needed to confirm our findings in the future.

Conclusions

We confirmed that airway parameters change with the severity of emphysema in cases with PRISm, and there is a significant correlation between them. The airway and parenchyma indices are correlated with some lung function parameters. We further explored possible imaging phenotypes of PRISm based on the degree of wall thickness and emphysema, and found that different phenotypes have differences in parenchyma and airway parameters, which may affect lung function and risk of progression to COPD. This study has clinical significance because we established possible underlying phenotypes of PRISm using quantitative analysis, which may contribute to the stratification of PRISm patients depending on quantitative HRCT.
