Within-host genetic diversity of pneumococcal serotype 3 during one-year prolonged carriage in a healthy adult
Authors: Lusako L Sibale, Stephanie W Lo, Newton Kalata, Tinashe K Nyazika, Ndaona Mitole, Victoria Dyster, Alice Kusakala, Mercy Khwiya, Gift Sagawa, Joseph A Phiri, Akuzike Kalizang’oma, Todd D Swarthout, Ken Malisita, Arox W Kamng’ona, Robert S Heyderman, Stephen D Bentley, Brenda A Kwambana-Adams, Chrispin Chaguza, Kondwani C Jambo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStreptococcus pneumoniae adapts within hosts through genetic variation and genome plasticity, facilitating persistence under antibiotic and immune pressures. Here, we investigated a prolonged pneumococcal carriage episode (>337 days) in a healthy, HIV-uninfected adult in Malawi. Whole-genome sequencing of single-colony isolates and plate sweep samples confirmed persistent colonisation by a multidrug-resistant serotype 3 strain with a distinct sequence type (GPSC10-ST18362), which maintained stable predominance despite transient acquisition of other serotypes. The sequentially sampled isolates showed 2 to 11 single-nucleotide polymorphism differences, no evidence of recombination, and modest gene loss involving mobile genetic elements. The total genome size decreased from 2.06 Mb to 2.03 Mb across isolates. Intrahost single-nucleotide variants were identified in genes related to metabolism, stress response, and DNA repair, but showed no consistent signatures of positive selection. Capsular locus analysis revealed deletions consistent with GPSC10-related vaccine-escape profiles. These findings highlight the capacity of GPSC10-ST18362 to persist asymptomatically for months with limited within-host genomic diversity.Subject terms: Genetics research, Bacterial genetics, Clinical microbiology, Bacterial genomics

Introduction

Clinically relevant bacterial species, such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, have small, haploid genomes and exhibit lower mutation rates compared to RNA viruses1,2. However, due to their large population sizes and rapid growth, even these lower mutation rates can lead to significant genetic diversity during infections3. Additional diversity is generated through mechanisms such as hypermutation, recombination, and integrating mobile genetic elements4,5. Single-nucleotide variant mutations and small indels are commonly identified as the primary drivers of within-host evolution in bacterial populations, whereas recombination events and mobile genetic element insertions occur less frequently but are still significant3.

In S. pneumoniae, base substitutions occur approximately once every 15 weeks6, while recombination, though less frequent, introduces large-scale genetic changes with an average of 72 SNPs per event7. This enhanced genomic plasticity facilitates adaptation to antibiotics and vaccine escape, demonstrating the pathogen’s capacity for rapid evolution in response to selective pressures. In some cases, prolonged infection can drive within-host evolution that results in more virulent strains, as observed in pathogens such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis8.

Longitudinal carriage studies are important in elucidating potential factors that may explain the dynamics of pneumococcal colonisation, such as the carriage duration, colonisation density, multiple carriage, and clone succession driven by the within-host bacterial evolution. Understandably, most studies on pneumococcal carriage dynamics have primarily focused on children due to the reported high invasive disease burden and carriage prevalence9–13. However, the old paradigm of short pneumococcal carriage durations in adults11,13–17 appears to be shifting, with a recent longitudinal study showing carriage durations of up to 4 months in adults18. Such prolonged pneumococcal carriage in adults may constitute reservoirs for continued transmission and circulation of serotypes, especially those less targeted by the PCVs19, in high carriage prevalence and disease-burdened settings.

Adult pneumococcal carriage has historically been considered transient, though persistent carriage prevalence of vaccine escape pneumococcal serotype 3 has been reported19. Pneumococcal serotype 3 is included in PCV13 but evades host antibody-mediated clearance20,21. Its capsular polysaccharide’s distinctive structure and electrochemical properties significantly impede opsonophagocytosis22, an essential anti-bacterial mechanism against pneumococcus. Moreover, pneumococcal serotype 3 is associated with a case fatality rate of 30%–47% in critical cases23. The persistence and adaptability of serotype 3, even post-vaccine introduction, highlight serious concerns about its clonal evolution and vaccine escape.

Therefore, we investigated whether prolonged nasopharyngeal serotype 3 S. pneumoniae carriage would promote genomic diversification in an adult with long-term carriage. We used samples from an individual recruited under an adult longitudinal pneumococcal carriage study24, who had cultured-confirmed pneumococcal serotype 3 isolates in all his longitudinal samples, spanning 337 days. We conducted phenotypic and genomic analysis across all the longitudinal samples to ascertain prolonged carriage and assess within-host genetic diversity and evolution.

Results

Identification of prolonged serotype 3 carriage

Pneumococcal carriage duration is influenced by host immunity, bacterial fitness, and environmental factors, with most adult episodes considered short-lived18. Global surveillance has demonstrated a persistent pneumococcal carriage duration of an upper bound of ~258.3 days in children and adults (Supplementary Table 1). In our Malawi cohort24, the overall median carriage duration for all pneumococcal serotypes was 27 days (range 2–337 days) across 83 episodes (Supplementary Table 2), while for phenotypic serotype 3, the median duration was 29 days (range 3–337 days) across 27 episodes (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Table 2). Notably, in one healthy, HIV-uninfected, PCV-unvaccinated adult (age range of 25–35 years), serotype 3 pneumococci were consistently recovered across multiple time points spanning 337 days (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Table 3). This pneumococcal carriage episode duration of approximately a year exceeds previously documented upper bounds for serotype 3 carriage, which have generally remained under 100 days in both children and adults (Supplementary Table 4).

We further observed fluctuations of pneumococcal density during the presumed prolonged pneumococcal carriage episode, with a median carriage density of 33,500 CFU/ml (range from 1340 CFU/ml to 36,850,000 CFU/ml) (Fig. 1B), indicating potential dynamic bacterial population shifts over time. Therefore, we performed both whole plate sweep and single-colony-derived sequencing to understand the potential within-host diversity and evolution associated with this presumed prolonged carriage. Fourteen whole plate sweep samples were sequenced and used in the final analysis (Fig. 1C). In addition, we managed to sequence thirteen single-colony-derived isolates. Out of the thirteen samples sequenced, eleven passed single-colony quality control and were used in the subsequent analysis (Fig. 1C).

Genotyping reveals ST18362 lineage

Genomic data availability has allowed pneumococci to be clustered based on their genetic background, notably the Global Pneumococcal Sequencing Cluster approach (GPSC) using POPulation Partitioning Using Nucleotide Kmers (PopPUNK)25 and Public databases for molecular typing and microbial genome diversity (PubMLST)26. These clusters are essential in determining pneumococcal virulence, invasiveness, and potential vaccine escape27. All isolates obtained during the prolonged carriage episode were confirmed as S. pneumoniae serotype 3 through in silico serotyping. Using PopPUNK-based clustering, the isolates were classified as belonging to GPSC10, a lineage previously associated with high recombination potential28. Further molecular typing via PubMLST revealed a sequence type, ST18362, with a single locus variant to GPSC10-ST700.

Placement of the isolates within a global phylogeny of serotype 3 showed that they clustered closely with GPSC10-ST70029, circulating mainly in Africa based on the Global Pneumococcal Sequencing database (Fig. 2A). The GPSC10 (CC230) lineage itself is globally distributed and capable of expressing multiple serotypes (Fig. 2B). These data confirm prolonged pneumococcal carriage of a distinct sequence type, ST18362, serotype 3 sub-lineage that belongs to a highly diverse and recombinant lineage.

Antimicrobial resistance profiling of GPSC10-ST18362 isolates

The persistence of S. pneumoniae within hosts can maintain exposure to selective pressures, potentially contributing to antimicrobial resistance (AMR)30. In this study, all serotype 3 isolates belonging to GPSC10-ST18362 were resistant to tetracycline, oxacillin, and cotrimoxazole, with reduced susceptibility to benzylpenicillin. However, they remained susceptible to erythromycin and ceftriaxone and were classified as multidrug-resistant (MDR) (Supplementary Fig. 1A).

Importantly, this resistance pattern aligns with profiles observed in other GPSC10-serotype 3 isolates in the Global Pneumococcal Sequence (GPS) database. Among the dominant circulating serotype 3 lineages (GPSC12, GPSC51, GPSC10, GPSC83 and GPSC43), only GPSC10 was consistently non-susceptible to penicillin and amoxicillin using the CDC pipeline31,32 MIC prediction (Supplementary Fig. 1B). These findings confirm that GPSC10-ST18362 represents a MDR lineage consistent with known GPSC10-serotype 3 resistance profiles.

Within-host SNP variation and genome reduction

S. pneumoniae, a pathogen capable of significant genomic changes, can persist within-host environments through mutations, recombination, and genome rearrangement7,30. We therefore hypothesised that prolonged carriage would be associated with within-host genomic changes that would support its continued persistence. No evidence of recombination was detected (Fig. 3A), and we confirmed it by running Genealogies Unbiased By recomBinations In Nucleotide Sequences (Gubbins)33. This was further supported by fastGEAR34 analysis, which did not identify fine-scale recombination events. However, we observed a single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) distance among the 11 single-colony isolates ranging from 2 to 11 SNPs (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, numerous mutations were observed in genes previously identified as recombination hotspots35, including penicillin-binding protein 2 × (pbp2x) and pneumococcal surface protein A (pspA) (Fig. 3B). Pneumococcal surface protein A was associated with a high abundance of non-synonymous amino acid changes (Fig. 3C).

We further assessed potential genome rearrangement using a Bacterial Pan Genome Analysis Pipeline (Panaroo pipeline). We observed gene deletions over time, with the majority of genes deleted conferring to hypothetical proteins (Fig. 3E). However, we also observed the deletion of genes that conferred to ATP-binding cassette transporters (yusV, yfhA, fepD_2, yhfQ), Antitoxin (pezA), and Toxin (pezT) (Fig. 3E). In addition, these genes were located near a Tn5253-like ICE (defective chloramphenicol and tetracycline resistance-conferring element36) and flanked by two copies of the pezAT toxin-antitoxin system, one of which was deleted (Fig. 3D). Correspondingly, the total assembled genome size decreased from 2.06 Mb to 2.03 Mb across later isolates. All together, these findings show that prolonged carriage of GPSC10-ST18362 was associated with microevolutionary changes, including point mutations and genome reduction.

Capsular deletions and virulence gene profile

The pneumococcal capsule is a major contributor to S. pneumoniae virulence and vaccine escape37. Pneumococcal lineage recombination affects carriage duration and pneumococcal capsule size38. Recent data from Malawi shows a vaccine escape serotype 3 GPSC10-ST700, with a distinct capsular polysaccharide (cps) locus deletion associated with increased antimicrobial resistance and lower susceptibility to opsonophagocytic killing29. Global pneumococcal serotype 3 phylogeny analysis of the cps locus revealed that GPSC10-ST18362 clustered with other serotype 3 isolates within the GPSC10 lineage (Fig. 4A). Notably, it shared a similar capsular deletion pattern with GPSC10-ST700, including the absence of wzg, wzh, wzd and wze genes (Fig. 4B). These deletions were not present in the globally dominant clonal complex CC180 (GPSC12) or Netherlands 3-31, which retains a complete cps locus consistent with the PMEN31 reference (GenBank accession number, CR931634) (Fig. 4B).

Using the Mass screening of contigs for antimicrobial resistance or virulence genes (ABRicate) virulence factor database (VFDB), we screened for genes linked to colonisation and persistence. Other pneumococcal core genome virulence genes like cbpD, lytA and lytC have been associated with biofilm formation, fratricide, and adaptation39. GPSC10-ST18362 carried a suite of virulence-associated genes, including cbpD, lytA, lytC, pspA and nanA (Supplementary Fig. 2). Their detection here is not unique but may support general colonisation fitness. We did not observe any notable gene acquisitions or losses among known accessory virulence genes during the carriage episode. Overall, the GPSC10-ST18362 serotype 3 isolate exhibited a capsular deletion pattern similar to a known vaccine escape lineage and retained core virulence genes, suggesting conserved colonisation fitness without notable accessory gene changes.

Lineage persistence despite co-colonisation

Within-host competition among pneumococcal lineages is a key driver of genomic evolution, shaping colonisation dynamics and adaptation40. During multiple carriage, strains compete for resources and niches, with genetic adaptations and virulence factors influencing colonisation advantage41–43. External pressures like antibiotics and vaccines further shape these competitive interactions, which can disrupt co-colonisation and favour lineages with adaptive advantages39. Whole-plate sweep sequencing identified transient acquisition of other pneumococcal serotypes at various time points during the 337-day carriage episode (Fig. 5). However, S. pneumoniae GPSC10-ST18362 serotype 3 was detected at all sampling points and remained the predominant lineage throughout the study period. No evidence of replacement by transiently carried serotypes was observed. The high abundance of GPSC10-ST18362 across samples suggests stable persistence during prolonged colonisation in the host environment. These results document the continued detection of GPSC10-ST18362 during the entire follow-up period despite occasional co-carriage of other pneumococcal serotypes.

Intrahost SNP variants indicate genomic plasticity

Studies on pneumococcal populations have shown that carriage provides an environment for the emergence of minority variants through mutation and genetic drift within-host, shaped by host immunity, microbial competition, and antibiotic exposure44. Therefore, we hypothesised that this persistent carriage of serotype 3 GPSC10-ST18362 might be associated with an increased prevalence of minority variants reflecting its genomic plasticity. Whole plate sweeps sequencing was used to assess intrahost single-nucleotide variants (iSNVs) in GPSC10-ST18362 across the sampling period. We restricted this analysis to samples containing only a single pneumococcal lineage (GPSC10) and serotype (pneumococcal serotype 3).

Variants were annotated against the reference genome, and for summarising cumulative changes, each unique iSNV was counted once per gene or intergenic region relative to the first sample collected. The majority of unique iSNVs mapped to transposase- or repeat-associated regions, where mapping artefacts cannot be excluded. However, we observed unique iSNVs in genes associated with bacterial metabolism (adcAII, phoH, phtB), DNA synthesis and repair (carA, pcrA), and oxidative stress response (gor) (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Fig. 3A, and Supplementary Data 1). Intergenic unique iSNVs were also detected near loci involved in stress response, ion transport, and cell wall biosynthesis (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. 3B). The cumulative distribution of these unique iSNVs across the carriage episode is summarised in Fig. 6C (genic regions) and Fig. 6D (intergenic regions), highlighting random, dispersed accumulation of variants. Overall, these findings demonstrate that GPSC10-ST18362 prolonged carriage was associated with intrahost genomic diversification through low-frequency, dispersed point mutations.

Discussion

This study documents the persistent carriage of a S. pneumoniae serotype 3 strain with a distinct sequence type (GPSC10-ST18362) over 337 days in a healthy, HIV-uninfected adult. This represents one of the longest described adult carriage episodes of serotype 3 to date and contributes important insights into pneumococcal colonisation dynamics in high-burden settings.

Pneumococcal serotype 3 is associated with severe clinical outcomes, including adverse cardiovascular events and higher mortality rates in adults23,45,46. The inclusion of pneumococcal serotype 3 in high-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccines has been less effective in reducing serotype 3 disease burden compared to other vaccine-included serotypes, as evidenced by persistent serotype 3-associated parapneumonic pleural effusion/empyema, ongoing vaccine evasion, and serotype replacement in adult pneumonia cases47–49 and overall circulation in the community19. Serotype 3 capsule is not covalently linked to peptidoglycan and, in turn, facilitates the shedding of serotype 3 capsule, which in turn interferes with bacterial clearance20. Moreover, serotype 3 has the lowest surface charge (zeta potential), which plays a crucial role in evading the immune system by reducing phagocytosis and complement deposition22. In line with these findings, we identified pneumococcal carriage of serotype 3 for approximately one year in a healthy adult, highlighting the capacity of serotype 3 to persist asymptomatically in the adult nasopharynx50.

The expansion potential of GPSC10 lineages is exemplified by the spread of the MDR GPSC10 serotype 24F, which has been linked to invasive disease in France28. In our global analysis, the persistent carriage isolates belonging to ST18362 clustered within the GPSC10 lineage. According to the Global Pneumococcal Sequencing (GPS) database, GPSC10 serotype 3 isolates are predominantly found in Africa, suggesting a pattern of clonal dissemination across the continent. The ST18362 isolates were also MDR, consistent with the resistance profiles typically associated with GPSC10 strains28. Notably, ST18362 has a single locus variant to GPSC10-ST700. Moreover, the identification of new and emerging variations at the single-nucleotide level among serotype 3 has been highlighted as essential in surveillance due to clade expansion48. ST18362 harbored a capsular deletion encompassing the wzg, wzh, wzd and wze genes, mirroring the deletion seen in GPSC10-ST700, which has been linked to altered susceptibility to opsonophagocytic killing29. Given their potential impact on host interactions, the phenotypic consequences of such capsule deletions, including effects on colonisation duration and lineage expansion, warrant further investigation.

Pneumococci colonisation success is determined by a complex competitive interaction from both other bacterial species51 and other pneumococcal lineages52,53. Several factors have been associated with colonisation success or failure, including capsular thickness41,42, positive carriage status53 and competent cells fratricide43. Competent cell fratricide is mediated by bacteriocins54, as well as a combination of a putative murein hydrolase cbpD, autolysin lytA, and cell wall hydrolase lytC39,43. Co-colonisation was transiently detected from other pneumococcal serotypes during follow-up; however, GPSC10-ST18362 remained predominant throughout. This observation suggests the lineage maintained stable colonisation over time. Although the mechanisms underlying this dominance were not directly assessed, lineage-specific features such as capsule structure may have supported long-term colonisation, but further work is needed to confirm such traits.

Pneumococcal serotype 3 lineages exhibit substantial genetic diversity, with evidence of frequent horizontal gene transfer and genomic variability, as observed in multiple studies48,55. Prolonged carriage durations have been associated with increased genetic alterations and adaptive changes30,38. Whole-genome sequencing of longitudinal single-colony isolates revealed 2–11 SNP differences across the core genome, with no evidence of recombination detected by Gubbins or fastGEAR. SNP variation was concentrated in a few genes known to be recombination hotspots35, such as pbp2x and pspA, but we found no conclusive signals of recombination even at fine-scale. Pan-genome analysis showed modest gene loss, including hypothetical proteins and genes in ABC transport systems and toxin-antitoxin modules located within a Tn5253-like ICE. Overall genome size decreased from 2.06 Mb to 2.03 Mb, suggesting minor genome reduction that could reflect genome streamlining or lineage-specific plasticity. The biological significance of these genome changes and deletions warrants further functional validation.

Despite a comprehensive analysis of persistent within-host pneumococcal carriage of a serotype and lineage of global concern, the study has some limitations. Whole-plate sweep sequencing had insufficient depth to fully characterise minority variants. We did not perform concurrent household sampling, so repeated reacquisition from close contacts cannot be excluded. However, the observed sequential accumulation of minor SNP variation, modest genome reduction, and consistent detection of GPSC10-ST18362 over 337 days support prolonged within-host persistence as a more likely explanation.

In conclusion, this prolonged carriage episode of GPSC10-ST18362 reveals how serotype 3 lineages with established multidrug resistance profiles may persist asymptomatically in adults, potentially serving as reservoirs for further spread. These observations underscore the importance of high-resolution longitudinal surveillance to understand pneumococcal carriage dynamics, genomic plasticity, and the potential for serotype 3 persistence despite vaccine pressure.

Methods

Study design and recruitment

Nasopharyngeal samples were collected from asymptomatic adults in Blantyre, Malawi24. In brief, the study participants were recruited 3 days after screening and then followed up on days 7, 14, 21 and 28 for the first month, and then every month for 12 months. All participants were recruited from the ART clinics and Voluntary Counselling and Testing centres at Lighthouse-Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital and Gateway Health Centre in Blantyre. Participants were screened for pneumococcal carriage, using WHO-recommended nasopharyngeal sampling and microbiological culture methods for pneumococcal detection56. Inclusion criteria included confirmed pneumococcal carriage in adults aged 18–45 years, living with a child under 5 years old, and providing written informed consent.

Microbiological culture and density quantification

Standard microbiological culture was used to determine the presence of S. pneumoniae from the nasopharyngeal swab56. We identified S. pneumoniae by their morphology and optochin sensitivity. The bile solubility test was used on isolates with no or intermediate (zone diameter <14 mm) optochin susceptibility56. Plates showing no S. pneumoniae growth were incubated for a further 24 h before being reported as negative. A single-colony of confirmed pneumococcus was selected and grown on a new SBG plate. Growth from this second plate was used for serotyping by latex agglutination (ImmuLexTM 23-valent Pneumotest; Statens Serum Institut, Denmark). Pneumococcal density was quantified using microbiological culture serial dilutions57 on a gentamicin-sheep blood agar plate (SBG; 5% sheep blood agar, 5 μL gentamicin/mL), and results were reported as colony-forming units per millilitre (CFU/mL).

Antimicrobial resistance profiling

Antimicrobial susceptibility of pneumococcal isolates was assessed by the disk diffusion method (Oxoid, USA) for beta-lactams antibiotic (oxacillin 1 μg), MLSB antibiotic (erythromycin 15 μg), tetracycline antibiotic (tetracycline 30 μg) and Trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (co-trimoxazole 1.25–23.75 μg).

Beta-lactam antibiotic susceptibility was confirmed by benzylpenicillin Etest (bioMérieux, Marcy-I’Étoile, France) minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs). A Ceftriaxone Etest was also performed when oxacillin 1 µg zone diameter <9 mm according to European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) guidelines58. Etest strips were applied to the surface of the plates according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of the drug where the zone edge intersected the Etest strip.

Interpretation of results followed EUCAST guidelines for meningitis breakpoints58. S. pneumoniae ATCC 49619 was used as a quality control strain. MDR was defined as non-susceptibility to agents in three or more chemical classes of antibiotics59,60.

DNA extraction and quantification

For single isolate culture specimens, 10 μl of stored pneumococcal isolates were plated onto Columbia CNA agar containing 5% sheep blood (CBA). These were then incubated overnight at 37 ± 2 °C with 5% CO256. All growth was collected from the plate using 10 μl sterile plastic loops into an Eppendorf tube ready for DNA extraction. For plate sweep culture specimens, 100 μl of stored respiratory samples (nasopharyngeal swab in STGG with known positive for pneumococcus determined by standard microbiological culture56) were plated onto CBA. These were then incubated overnight at 37 ± 2 °C with 5% CO2. All growth was collected from the plate using 10 μl sterile plastic loops into an Eppendorf tube, ready for DNA extraction. QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) was used for DNA extraction following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA quantity and quality were evaluated by Invitrogen Qubit Fluorometer. Samples that had a total DNA yield of >2.5 μg were submitted for sequencing.

Whole genome sequencing

Whole genome sequencing (WGS) was performed at the Wellcome Sanger Institute on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina-HTP NovaSeq 6000 Paired-end sequencing). Specifically, the sequencing platform was NovaSeq SP, the read length was 150 bp, the plex was 384, and the expected reads per sample were 1.8 M.

Single colony analysis

Quality control of single colonies (Appendix–Supplementary Methods28) QUality ASsessment Tool (QUAST) v5.2.0 (https://github.com/ablab/quast) was used to extract assembly quality metrics. Overall sequencing depth of >20X61 total number of contigs of <500, and total length of the assembled genome size between 1.9 and 2.35 Mb were retained. To account for contamination, >90% of reads mapped to S. pneumoniae using the Kraken metagenomic classification algorithm (v1.0.0) (https://github.com/DerrickWood/kraken) and >60% mapping coverage of the reference genome (PMEN global clone Spain23F-1, accession number FM211187). Finally, to account for contamination due to multiple carriages of >1 pneumococcal strain, the number of heterozygous sites of <220 or the percentage of heterozygous sites over the total number of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) of ≤ 15% were included as previously described. SeroCall (https://github.com/knightjimr/SeroCall) was performed as a second stage to confirm multiple serotypes.

Phylogeny and recombination analysis

Genome de novo assemblies were done by Spades (v3.15.4)62, and Genome alignments were done by Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) (v 0.7.17-r1188)63. S. pneumoniae strain 475 chromosomes, complete genome, serogroup 24 [GenBank: CP046355.1] as a GPSC10 reference. Gubbins (v3.2.1)33 was used to detect recombination, and phylogenetic trees were constructed by Randomised Axelerated Maximum Likelihood (RAxML) (v8.2.13) (https://github.com/stamatak/standard-RAxML). To further explore possible fine-scale recombination events, we additionally ran fastGEAR/2016-12-1634, which identifies smaller mosaic recombination signals. We contextualised the episode to global pneumococcal lineages (n = 20,924 genomes) assigned by popPUNK (v2.6.0)25 and serotype 3 (n = 593 genomes) isolates that passed the above QC from the Global Pneumococcal Sequencing Project database (https://data.monocle.sanger.ac.uk/) (lane id’s included in the source data file). We also included serotype 3 from the parent study (n = 30 genomes) that passed the above QC. The phylogenetic trees overlaid with epidemiological data and in silico output were visualised using ggtree (v3.8.0) (https://github.com/YuLab-SMU/ggtree).

Pan-genome analysis

Prokka: Rapid prokaryotic genome annotation (v1.14.5) (https://github.com/tseemann/prokka) was used for annotation, and gene clustering analysis was done by panaroo (v1.3.3) (https://github.com/gtonkinhill/panaroo) and visualised by an interactive viewer for populations of bacterial genomes linked by a phylogeny (phandango) (https://github.com/jameshadfield/phandango). To confirm the deletion of pezA and pezT gene copies, we conducted a blast search (v2.16.0)64 using reference sequences against each assembled genome, in addition to standard annotation pipelines.

Genome lineage clustering was done by assigning GPSC using PopPUNK (v2.6.0)25. Sequence typing was done by MLSTcheck (v2.23.0) and serotyping by a k-mer-based Pipeline to identify the Serotype from Illumina NGS reads (SeroBA) (v1.0.2) (https://github.com/sanger-pathogens/seroba). Detection of recombination was done by Gubbins (v3.2.1)33, detection of pairwise snp distance was done by a Pairwise SNP distance matrix from a FASTA sequence alignment (snp-dist) (v0.7.0) (https://github.com/tseemann/snp-dists) and summary of SNPs relative to a first sample as reference sequence using snipit: summarise snps relative to your reference sequence (v1.1.2) (https://github.com/aineniamh/snipit). Detection and annotation of majority variants were done by a Bayesian haplotype-based genetic polymorphism discovery and genotyping tool (freebayes (v1.3.10)) (https://github.com/freebayes/freebayes) and genetic variant annotation, and functional effect prediction toolbox (SnpEff) (v5.2) (https://github.com/pcingola/SnpEff), respectively within a rapid haploid variant calling and core genome alignment tool (snippy) (v4.6.0) (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy). Resistance profiling was done by Mass screening of contigs for antimicrobial resistance or virulence genes (ABRicate v1.0.1) (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate), the comprehensive antibiotic resistance (CARD; http://arpcard.mcmaster.ca) database and the Pathogen Watch (v12.0.4) (https://pathogen.watch/) CDC pipeline31. Virulence genes profiling was done using the ABRicate (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate), VFDB (http://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/) and blast (v2.16.0)64 using diverse pspA sequences as ref. 65.

Capsular locus analysis

Genome mapping was done by BWA (v0.7.17-r1188)63. Serotype 3 capsular encoding region capsular locus reference used was NCBI accession: (GenBank accession number, CR931634). Tree constructed by RAxML (v8.2.13) (https://github.com/stamatak/standard-RAxML). The phylogenetic trees were visualised using ggtree (v3.8.0) (https://github.com/YuLab-SMU/ggtree). We extracted and annotated the capsular locus of serotype 3 in Geneious Prime (v2025.2.1) (https://www.geneious.com) and visualised it by Easyfig (v2.2.2) (https://github.com/mjsull/Easyfig).

Mobile genetic element analysis

We assessed the presence of MGE in all isolates from this persistent carriage using ICEfinder (v2.0) (https://bioinfo-mml.sjtu.edu.cn/ICEfinder/ICEfinder.html) and confirmed the element by annotating it using Geneious Prime (v2025.2.1) (https://www.geneious.com) and Artemis (v18.2.0) (https://sanger-pathogens.github.io/Artemis/). Tn5253 (GenBank: EU351020.1) was used as a reference to annotate the mobile genetic element and the final illustration visualised by Easyfig (v2.2.2) (https://github.com/mjsull/Easyfig).

Plate sweep analysis

Lineage deconvolution

All pneumococcal genomes that passed quality control (20,924 genomes) from the Global Pneumococcal Sequencing Project database (https://www.pneumogen.net/gps/) were included as a reference for lineage deconvolution. PopPUNK (v2.6.0)25 was used to reassign lineages to these reference genomes. Using a statistical mixture model, the mSWEEP (v2.0.0)66 algorithm used read pseudo-alignments output from Themisto (v2.1.0) (https://github.com/algbio/themisto) to quickly estimate the abundance of lineages based on the reference within a mixed sample. mGEM (v1.3.3)67 algorithm used the resulting likelihood estimates output from mSWEEP to deconvolute the mixed reads into groups based on the lineages. Shovill (v1.1.0) (https://github.com/tseemann/shovill) was used for the assembly of binned reads.

To reduce the possibility of false positives, lineages were only called if they were present at a relative abundance of greater than 1% (0.01), as described previously67. The quality control tool demix_check (https://github.com/tmaklin/coreutils_demix_check) was run on the resulting lineage-level bins, and high confidence scores of 1 and 2 were retained as reliable identification.

Serotyping, serotype abundance and antimicrobial-resistant calls

To determine serotypes, SeroCall (https://github.com/knightjimr/SeroCall) was run on raw reads samples, and SeroBA (v1.0.2) (https://github.com/sanger-pathogens/seroba) was run on each deconvoluted lineage to confirm untypable serotypes and serotypes at the sub-serogroup level. The transient lineages and serotypes were resolved to GPSC using SeroCall (https://github.com/knightjimr/SeroCall) and mSWEEP in conjunction with SeroBA/PopPUNK. This approach has been previously validated by comparing plate sweeps to a single-colony pick, demonstrating an accuracy of 97.5% (157/161)44. Resistance calling was done by ABRicate (v1.0.1), the CARD (http://arpcard.mcmaster.ca) database and Pathogen Watch (v12.0.4) (https://pathogen.watch/) CDC pipeline31.

Minority variants call

To investigate the intrahost evolution, we rigorously employed the LoFreq (v2.1.5) (https://github.com/CSB5/lofreq) variant calling pipeline on all samples, specifically those with a single identified GPSC lineage and serotype, using mSWEEP (v2.0.0)66 and SeroCall (https://github.com/knightjimr/SeroCall), respectively. Therefore, plate sweep samples with multiple lineages and serotypes were excluded (Fig. 5). We included samples with over 89% of reads mapping to S. pneumoniae using Kraken (v1.0.0) to address contamination (Supplementary Fig. 4). Therefore, the final analysis included 64% (9/14) sampling points. S. pneumoniae strain 475 chromosomes, complete genome, serogroup 24 [GenBank: CP046355.1] as a GPSC10 reference to call minority variants, annotate the variants and compute genome coverage. Reads were aligned to the reference genomes using BWA (v0.7.17-r1188)63. The Picard tools (v2.23.8) “CleanSam” function was then used to align soft clip reads to the end of contigs and to set the alignment qualities of unaligned reads to zero. The LoFreq pipeline was initially run with a coverage of 20 reads to identify a variant.

The resulting variant calls were used along with the read alignment as input to the GATK (Genome Analysis Toolkit Variant Discovery in High-Throughput Sequencing Data) (v4.6.2.0) (https://github.com/broadinstitute/gatk) BaseRecalibrator tool (v4.1.9), as suggested in the LoFreq manual (https://csb5.github.io/lofreq/commands/), to improve the estimated base quality scores. Finally, the LoFreq pipeline was run for a final time with a coverage requirement of 20 reads. The resulting genetic variant calls were only considered if their maximum allele frequency was ≤ 0.5 and minimum was ≥ 0.15. The minimum allele frequency was based on Adam Lauring’s rule of thumb68 “that the coverage should be 10 times the reciprocal of a variant’s frequency”. The variants were annotated using SnpEff (v5.2) (https://github.com/pcingola/SnpEff). Each mutation was counted once per gene or locus tag position during the carriage episode using the first sample sequenced as a reference after annotation with S. pneumoniae strain 475 chromosomes, complete genome, serogroup 24 [GenBank: CP046355.1]. Therefore, only a unique mutation to the first sample sequenced are indicated in the subsequent follow-up days. Given the moderate sequencing depth of whole plate sweep sequencing, these calls provide a qualitative view of minority variants but do not support confident inference of allele frequency dynamics over time.

Colonisation episode definition and data reporting

A pneumococcal colonisation episode was defined by either the first pneumococcal carriage at study screening or the re-acquisition of pneumococci after pneumococcal clearance. Pneumococcal clearance was defined by detecting negative cultures for any serotype at two consecutive sampling points, as previously described11,13. R statistical package ggplot2 (v3.4.3, https://ggplot2.tidyverse.org), Inkscape (open-source vector graphics) (v 1.3) (https://inkscape.org) and BioRender (a web-based tool used to create high-quality scientific illustrations) for visualisation (http://www.biorender.com/).
