Arcuate nucleus‐specific progesterone receptor knockdown in female mice is sufficient to induce PCOS‐like hyperactivity in the reproductive axis
Authors: Kelly A. Glendining, Melanie Prescott, Kyoko Potapov, Elodie Kip, Rebecca E. Campbell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMammalian reproductive function is dependent upon gonadal steroid hormone feedback within the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis. In polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), impaired progesterone (P4) negative feedback leads to hyperactive pulsatile secretion of luteinising hormone (LH) and impaired reproductive function. Although the precise upstream neuronal network modulating P4 negative feedback remains undefined, gamma‐aminobutyric acid (GABA)ergic neurons in the arcuate nucleus (ARC) have been implicated. To identify the critical location of P4 negative feedback, we knocked out progesterone receptors (PR) specifically from GABA neurons by genetic cross (VGAT‐Cre; PRfl/fl) or specifically from the arcuate nucleus by stereotaxic AAV‐Cre delivery to PRfl/fl mice and assessed HPG axis function. Females with GABA‐specific PR knockout (GABAPRKO) exhibited delayed vaginal opening and fewer estrous cycles compared to controls, but LH pulse frequency and P4 negative feedback were unaffected. In contrast, females with AAV‐Cre‐mediated knockdown of PR specifically in the ARC exhibited increased LH pulse frequency in comparison to control and AAV‐Cre off‐target animals. ARC‐specific PR knockdown also resulted in significantly decreased estrous cycle frequency, increased cycle length, reduced time spent in proestrus, and impaired P4 negative feedback, but no change in testosterone levels. These data show that reduced PR signalling in the ARC of adult female mice is sufficient to drive hyperactivity within the HPG axis and mimic some but not all reproductive features of PCOS. The subtle reproductive deficits in GABAPRKO may reflect compensatory mechanisms or the involvement of multiple neuronal phenotypes in P4 negative feedback. Together, this work supports the ARC as a critical site for P4 negative feedback regulation of the reproductive axis.

INTRODUCTION

Mammalian reproduction and fertility are dependent upon gonadal steroid hormone feedback within the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis. Pulsatile gonadotropin‐releasing hormone (GnRH) release from neurons in the hypothalamus stimulates the synthesis and secretion of the pituitary gonadotropins, including a mirrored pattern of pulsatile luteinising hormone (LH) secretion. 1 , 2 Pituitary gonadotropins promote folliculogenesis and steroid synthesis in the ovary, and estradiol (E2) and progesterone (P4) from the ovary provide classic homeostatic negative feedback to suppress GnRH/LH secretion for much of the cycle. 3 , 4

Women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) exhibit significantly increased GnRH/LH pulse frequency 5 , 6 , 7 and an impaired sensitivity of gonadal steroid hormone feedback. PCOS is a highly prevalent neuroendocrine disorder and the leading cause of anovulatory infertility in reproductive‐aged women. 8 , 9 , 10 Higher concentrations of E2 and P4 are required to decrease pulsatile LH release in PCOS patients in comparison to non‐PCOS controls. 5 , 11 , 12 , 13 Impaired negative feedback regulation of GnRH/LH secretion, in particular, is thought to underlie the pathological features of PCOS 5 , 7 , 11 , 12 , 14 , 15 which include oligo/an‐ovulation, clinical and/or biochemical hyperandrogenism, and polycystic appearance of the ovaries. 16 , 17

In support of this, preclinical models of PCOS display similarly blunted P4 negative feedback, 18 , 19 , 20 increased LH pulse frequency, and recapitulate many relevant PCOS features including impaired estrous cyclicity, ovarian dysregulation, and hyperandrogeniemia. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23

The negative feedback actions of P4 on GnRH secretion are mediated by the nuclear progesterone receptor (PR), 14 however, the precise neural location where P4 and PR exert this effect is not fully understood. Evidence from preclinical studies implicates the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus (ARC) (homologous to the infundibulum in humans), a region densely populated by steroid hormone receptors. Studies in sheep and rats have demonstrated that delivery of a PR antagonist into the ARC of ovariectomised females disrupts the ability of peripheral P4 treatments to inhibit LH pulse frequency. 24 , 25

In addition to the exact site of P4 negative feedback, the identity of the neurons responsible for mediating P4 feedback to GnRH also remains to be determined. As GnRH neurons do not express PR, 26 , 27 , 28 it is thought that a P4‐sensitive interneuron projecting to GnRH modulates GnRH activity and LH secretion in response to fluctuating P4. Kisspeptin neurons in the ARC, identified as the GnRH neuron pulse generator, 29 , 30 are the most likely candidates, as they synapse with GnRH neurons 31 and express receptors for E2 and P4. 32 While mouse models with PR knockdown in kisspeptin neurons display reduced fertility and impaired E2‐induced LH surges, 33 they display normal estrous cyclicity, and LH pulse frequency across the estrous cycle is similar to WTs. 31 , 34 Together, this indicates that PR signalling in kisspeptin neurons plays a role in E2‐mediated positive feedback but is not involved in P4 negative feedback.

Alternatively, gamma‐aminobutyric acid (GABA)ergic neurons in the ARC are also implicated in P4 negative feedback. GnRH neurons receive significant synaptic input from steroid hormone sensitive GABAergic neurons originating in the ARC. 20 Likewise, GnRH neurons express both GABAA and GABAB receptors, 35 , 36 and GABA can be excitatory to GnRH neurons through GABAA activation due to their high intracellular calcium concentration. 37 , 38 , 39 Hyperandrogenic models of PCOS with impaired P4 negative feedback and elevated LH pulse frequency exhibit increased GABAergic innervation 20 , 22 , 40 , 41 , 42 and neurotransmission 18 , 23 to GnRH neurons. 20 , 23 ARC GABA neurons express PR, but the proportion of ARC GABA neurons expressing PR is reduced in PCOS‐like models. 20 , 43 , 44 Acute activation of ARC GABA neurons stimulates LH secretion and chronic activation of ARC GABA neurons can recapitulate PCOS‐like reproductive deficits. 22 Compellingly, GABA and its metabolites are elevated in the CSF of PCOS patients compared to controls, 45 and GABAergic modulators such as valproate have been shown to impact fertility and increase the risk for PCOS. 46 These data suggest that reduced sensitivity to P4‐mediated suppression of ARC GABA neurons may drive the increased GnRH/LH drive in PCOS. 15

Here, we sought to identify the critical location of P4 steroid hormone negative feedback and determine the impact of site‐specific reductions in P4 signalling on the development of PCOS‐like features. To do this, we generated a developmental global knockout with PR exclusively deleted from all GABA neurons using a Vgat‐Cre × PRfl/fl mouse, to determine whether GABA‐specific loss of PR can reproduce reproductive features of PCOS. Second, we refined our approach by using site‐specific Adeno‐associated virus (AAV)‐mediated knock down of PR in adult PRfl/fl females to knock down PR specifically in the ARC, a highly steroid hormone‐sensitive site implicated in preclinical studies to be the site of steroid hormone feedback regulation of GnRH neurons. 24 , 25 We hypothesised that mice with PR knock down in all GABA neurons and PR knockdown in ARC will replicate some, or all, of the reproductive deficits common to PCOS.

RESULTS

Validation of the GABAPRKO mouse model

Immunohistochemistry and confocal analysis confirmed knockout of PR expression in GABAergic neurons of Vgat‐Cre tdTomato × PRfl/fl mice. There was no statistically significant difference in the total number of Vgat‐tdTomato+ cells in the ARC of GABAPRKO and WT control mice (t (4) = 1.083, p = .34; Figure 1A,B). In WT mice, >43% of total Vgat expressing tdTomato+ cells in the ARC co‐expressed PR, in comparison to GABAPRKO mice where 0% of Vgat expressing tdTomato+ cells in the ARC co‐expressed PR (t (4) = 19.15, p < .0001; Figure 1C,D). Together this indicates highly efficient Cre‐induced excision of PR in Vgat+ neurons of GABAPRKO mice, with no impact of Cre excision on total Vgat cell number in ARC.

Delayed pubertal onset and modest estrous cyclicity impairment in female GABAPRKO mice

Pubertal onset in GABAPRKO female mice was assessed by the timing of vaginal opening (VO) and first estrous. Timing of VO did not differ significantly between GABAPRKO and WT mice (t (4) = 1.083, p = .28; Figure 2A). In contrast, the timing of first estrous was significantly delayed by approximately 1.6 days in GABAPRKO mice (29.5 days, n = 14) in comparison to WT controls (27 days, n = 7; U = 18, p = .015; Figure 2B).

Estrous cyclicity of female GABAPRKO mice was monitored by vaginal cytology smears from PND60‐80. Representative cyclicity plots of WT and GABAPRKO mice are shown in Figure 2C. The number of estrous cycles/21 days was significantly reduced in GABAPRKO (U = 34, p = .042, Figure 2D), but there was no significant difference in cycle length (U = 40, p = .13; Figure 2E). The percentage of time GABAPRKO and WT spent in proestrus, estrus, or diestrus was not significantly different (P: U = 46.0, E: U = 64.5, D: U = 56.5, p > .05; Figure 2F, Table S2). Together, this indicates that a loss of PR in GABAergic neurons results in delayed puberty, and a subtle reduction in the number of estrous cycles, but no change in estrous cycle length.

Normal LH pulsatility and intact progesterone negative feedback in GABAPRKO

To assess changes in negative feedback regulation of LH pulsatility, serial blood samples were collected over 2 h. Representative LH pulse profiles for WT and GABAPRKO mice are shown in Figure 3A,B. Analysis of LH pulse dynamics showed no significant difference in LH pulse frequency (t (11) = 0.590, p = .58; Figure 3C) and no significant difference in LH pulse amplitude (p = .45; Figure 3D) between GABAPRKO and WT mice. Likewise, no significant differences were found in basal LH levels (t (11) = 0.755, p = .66; Figure 3E), mean LH levels (t (11) = 0.379, p = .71; Figure 3F), or LH AUC (t (11) = 0.084, p = .93; Figure 3G).

To investigate whether PR knockdown in GABAergic neurons impairs P4 negative feedback, LH was measured before and after OVX and following administration of P4 to assess the ability of exogenous P4 to lower a post‐castration rise in LH (Figure 3H). OVX resulted in a marked rise in serum LH levels over basal both in GABAPRKO (n = 6) and WT mice (n = 3), and a marked decrease of LH following insertion of the P4 implant, (F (2,14) = 12.78, p = .0007; Figure 3I,J, Table S3). However, there was no main effect of genotype (F (1,7) = 0.624, p = .46), or a significant interaction between genotype and treatment (F (2,14) = 0.182, p = .835). Together, these data indicate that P4 negative feedback is intact in females with global knockdown of PR in GABA neurons.

Normal ovarian morphology in GABAPRKO

Analysis of ovarian morphology revealed no significant difference in the number of pre‐ovulatory follicles of GABAPRKO and WT mice (t (16) = 1.43, p = .171; Figure 3K–M). Similarly, there was no difference in the number of corpora lutea in the ovary of GABAPRKO and WT mice (t (16) = 0.654, p = .523; Figure 3N). Examination of the cellular composition of the largest antral follicle showed no significant difference in the area composed of theca (t (14) = 0.082, p = .936; Figure 3O) or granulosa cells (t (14) = 0.358, p = .726; Figure 3O), and no difference in plasma testosterone (t (13) = 0.010, p = .992; Figure 3P). Together, this indicates that a loss of PR in GABAergic neurons results in no change in LH secretion, P4‐negative feedback, or ovarian morphological features.

Validation of AAV‐Cre‐mediated PR knockdown in ARC

To avoid the compensatory mechanisms of developmental knockout and address the importance of the ARC in P4 negative feedback, we targeted PR expression postnatally by delivering a single bilateral injection of AAV‐Cre into the ARC of adult female PRfl/fl mice (Figure 4A,B). An additional control group of PRfl/fl mice received a bilateral injection of AAV‐mCherry (no Cre) into the ARC (Figure 4B). Immunohistochemical and confocal analysis confirmed that AAV‐Cre‐mediated excision of PR was highly effective and efficient, with >99% of AAV‐Cre infected, mCherry positive cells in the ARC failing to express PR (Figure 4C). In contrast, there was extensive colocalization in control mice injected with AAV‐mCherry, with >31% of mCherry positive cells in the ARC co‐expressing PR. Quantification of the total number of PR‐immunolabelled cells in the rostral, middle, and caudal regions of ARC showed a significant but incomplete reduction of PR expression in mice that were successfully targeted with AAV‐Cre in the ARC (F* (7,23.67) = 20.58, p < .0001; Figure 4D), with a mean overall knockdown of >48%, largely targeting the rostral and mid regions of the ARC (Figures 4E, S1, and S2). The extent of PR knockdown corresponded to the degree of AAV‐Cre infection, with a significant negative correlation between mCherry+ and PR+ cell counts in mice with successful targeting of AAV‐Cre (r = −0.579, p = .049; Figure 4F). Together, this data confirms successful AAV‐Cre‐mediated knockdown of PR in the ARC of PRfl/fl mice.

Estrous cyclicity is disrupted by AAV‐Cre‐mediated PR knockdown in ARC

Estrous cyclicity was assessed by vaginal cytology over 14 days before (pre‐AAV) and after AAV injection (post‐AAV; Figure 5A). The number and length of estrous cycles were not different after AAV injection in AAV‐mCherry controls (n = 4) or AAV‐Cre with no knockdown (n = 12) (Figure 5B,C). In contrast, PRfl/fl mice with PR knockdown had a significantly reduced number of cycles (W = 75.0, p = .002, n = 12; Figure 5B), and displayed significantly longer estrous cycles after transfection (W = −43.0, p = .027; Figure 5C). The percentage time spent in proestrus (Figure 5D), estrus (Figure 5E), and diestrus (Figure 5F) did not differ significantly after AAV injection in either AAV‐mCherry or AAV‐Cre with no knockdown (p > .05). However, there was a significant reduction in the percentage of time spent in proestrus in AAV‐Cre with knockdown (W = 45.0, p = .004; Figure 5D), but no change in the percent of time spent in estrus (p > .99; Figure 5E), or diestrus (p = .43; Figure 5F). This data indicates that decreased PR signalling specifically in the ARC of adult female mice is sufficient to reduce estrous cyclicity.

AAV‐Cre‐mediated PR knockdown in ARC impacts LH pulsatility

Five weeks after PRfl/fl mice were bilaterally transfected with AAV‐Cre we performed serial blood sampling to assess LH release dynamics using ELISA. As no notable differences in estrous cyclicity were detected between AAV‐mCherry and AAV‐Cre with no knockdown groups, these were combined for subsequent comparisons of LH secretion and ovarian morphology (now referred to as AAV no knockdown). During the sampling period, five animals had no detected LH pulses as determined by PULSAR software so were excluded from analysis, including n = 3 from AAV controls and n = 2 from AAV‐Cre with knockdown. In actively pulsing animals, mice with PR knockdown (n = 10) exhibited significantly increased LH pulse frequency in comparison to AAV controls (n = 13, U = 25.5, p = .009; Figure 6A–C), but no significant difference in LH pulse amplitude (p = .68; Figure 6D), basal LH levels (p = .96; Figure 6E), mean LH (p = .60; Figure 6F), or LH AUC (p = .22; Figure 6G). Together, this data indicates that PR knock down in ARC is sufficient to induce hyperactive LH pulsatility.

AAV‐Cre‐mediated PR knockdown in ARC impacts P4 negative feedback regulation of LH

To assess whether PR knockdown in ARC impacts the ability of exogenous P4 to lower a post‐castration rise in LH, 5 weeks after PRfl/fl mice were bilaterally transfected with AAV‐Cre, LH was measured before and after OVX and following administration of P4 (Figure 6H). OVX resulted in a marked rise in serum LH levels over basal in mice with no knockdown (n = 4) and mice with PR knockdown in ARC (n = 4, F (2,12) = 8.10, p = .006; Figure 6I,J, Table S4). However, there was a significant interaction between AAV knockdown and treatment (F (2,12) = 4.59, p = .033), with P4 failing to reduce LH serum levels after OVX in mice with PR knockdown (p = 0.013; Figure 6I,J, Table S4). Together these data indicate that PR knockdown in ARC is sufficient to disrupt P4‐mediated negative feedback of LH.

AAV‐Cre‐mediated PR knockdown in ARC impacts ovarian morphology

Ovarian histology was assessed to determine the downstream impact of reduced P4 negative feedback and resultant impaired cyclicity. Animals with PR knockdown had fewer pre‐ovulatory follicles in comparison to controls (t (15) = 2.76, p = .015, Figure 6H–J); however, there was no difference in the number of corpora lutea (t (15) = 1.55, p > .05; Figure 6K). Analysis of the composition of the largest antral follicle indicated no difference in the percent area occupied by theca cells (t (15) = 0.910, p > .05), or the percent area occupied by granulosa cells (p = .75; Figure 6L). No difference was found in plasma testosterone levels (t (15) = 0.327, p > .05; Figure 6M). This data indicates that PR knock down in ARC decreases folliculogenesis in the ovary, a reproductive trait common in PCOS, and which is linked to increased GnRH/LH pulse frequency and reduced levels of LH:FSH. 47

DISCUSSION

To identify the critical location of P4 negative feedback in the brain, we employed two complementary approaches using transgenic mouse models to assess the effect of PR knockdown on HPG axis function. Developmental knockout of PR exclusively from all GABAergic neurons resulted in delayed puberty onset and some subtle disruption of estrous cycling. However, LH pulse frequency and the ability of P4 to inhibit the post‐OVX rise in LH remained intact in GABAPRKO. In contrast, AAV‐mediated PR knockdown restricted to the ARC neurons of adult female PRfl/fl mice resulted in a significant reproductive phenotype. Knocking down PR expression was sufficient to elevate LH pulse frequency, impair estrous cyclicity, and remove the ability of P4 to inhibit the post‐OVX rise in LH. This had downstream consequences on reproductive features with a decreased number of pre‐ovulatory follicles in the ovary. This work provides direct evidence that PR signalling specifically in the ARC nucleus is required for P4 negative feedback of the HPG axis.

These findings support and extend earlier findings. Administration of the PR antagonist RU486 into the ARC of sheep blocks the ability of P4 to suppress a post‐OVX rise in LH. 24 Similarly, RU486 infusions into the ARC of P4‐treated adult female rats significantly reduce LH pulse interval compared to controls, whereas RU486 administered into the AVPV has no effect. 25 Here, we show that even a moderate loss of PR signalling within the mouse ARC disrupts P4 negative feedback regulation of LH and downstream function.

The retention of P4 negative feedback and modest reproductive phenotype in animals with PR loss from all GABA neurons was surprising given the widespread loss of PR signalling. These data suggest that the neuronal phenotype mediating P4 feedback in the ARC is not GABAergic, but limitations to the approach should be considered. Alternate pathways or proteins may compensate for the loss of PR activity to preserve GnRH function. It is possible that the expected excitatory effects of GABA‐specific PR knockdown on GnRH secretion are counteracted via non‐GABAergic neurons in the GnRH neuronal network. Likewise, allopregnanolone, a metabolite of P4, can allosterically modulate GABAA receptors to inhibit GnRH neurotransmission, 48 potentially counteracting the loss of PR‐mediated effects in GABA neurons.

Of interest, the reproductive phenotype of GABAPRKO females was not entirely unaffected despite intact P4 negative feedback, and the delayed puberty onset found here is similar to the delayed first estrus reported in prenatally androgenized PCOS‐like mice 21 , 49 that show impaired P4 feedback and reduction of PR expression in ARC GABA neurons. 20 While the current study cannot identify the specific GABAergic population responsible for these effects, P4 positive feedback via GABAergic AVPV kisspeptin neurons may be impacted. 50

While the specific neuronal populations in the ARC which are important for P4 negative feedback remain to be determined, the pronounced reproductive phenotype observed following PR knockdown in the ARC, which would have targeted both GABA and glutamatergic neurons, suggests that P4 negative feedback may involve more than a single cell type. A number of neuronal subtypes in the ARC that co‐express PR have been identified, including KNDy neurons (co‐expressing kisspeptin, dynorphin, neurokinin B), 51 , 52 somatostatin, POMC, and a small subset of NPY. 52 Of these, the glutamatergic KNDy neuron population in the ARC has been proposed as the most probable candidate to mediate P4 negative feedback on GnRH/LH pulsatility. 53 , 54 However, similar to our findings here in GABAPRKO mice, conditional deletion of PR from KNDy neurons does not impact GnRH/LH pulse generation. 34 The authors concluded that either some other mechanism is required for P4 negative feedback, or only a small number of PR expressing kisspeptin neurons (11%) are required to maintain P4‐mediated negative feedback on GnRH/LH pulsatility. Developmental compensation may have also played a role in these outcomes, suggesting that different ARC neuron phenotypes can compensate to mediate P4 negative feedback when PR is excised from a single population during development.

While modest PR knockdown in the ARC mimics the hyperactive LH secretion and impaired P4 negative feedback reported in PCOS and the prenatally androgenized (PNA) mouse model, testosterone levels were not affected. Clinical and animal model evidence support hyperactive GnRH/LH secretion promoting elevated androgen production by theca cells in the ovary, 55 so the absence of elevated testosterone was unexpected. This outcome could be due to timing or the magnitude of LH hyperactivity. Theca cell hyperplasia and elevated androgen biosynthesis that follow increased LH pulse frequency activation may require more time to develop than allowed for in the current study. It would be of interest to follow animals with PR knockdown for a longer duration to capture the development of PCOS‐like features over time. An alternative hypothesis is that a measurable increase in androgen production requires a greater elevation in LH pulse frequency than was elicited here.

On considering that folliculogenesis was impaired following PR knockdown in the ARC, with a reduced number of antral follicles in the ovary, this indicates that FSH secretion may be affected. The secondary FSH surge following ovulation is crucial for the recruitment of antral follicles in the following cycle, 56 and previous studies implicate PR actions in controlling the secondary surge. 57 Although not measured here, it would be interesting to determine whether the FSH dynamics of GABAPRKO and PRfl/fl mice also differ.

In the current study, tissue was collected 5 weeks following transfection with AAV‐Cre to knock down PR expression. It would be of interest to determine whether a longer period of reduced P4 feedback and increased LH pulse frequency would result in more dramatic downstream changes. In addition, blood sampling for LH was performed during diestrus when progesterone levels are low, 58 , 59 whereas changes in LH pulsatility may be of greater magnitude during estrus, the stage in which P4 negative feedback is greatest. 60 Due to the low frequency of LH pulses during estrus, 60 this would require the sampling period to be extended beyond 2 h. Despite this, our ability to detect increased LH pulse frequency during diestrus indicates that the lost sensitivity of ARC neurons to P4 has a robust effect on P4 negative feedback.

Together, this work defines the ARC as a critical site for P4 negative feedback regulation of the reproductive axis. Future studies incorporating intersectional transgenics or CRISPR approaches will be required to dissect out the relative contributions of specific afferent phenotypes like KNDy and GABA neurons. This work also shows that a modest decrease in P4 signalling specifically within the ARC can evoke aspects of HPG axis dysfunction seen in PCOS, supporting the role of gonadal hormone insensitivity in the brain in PCOS pathology.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

PRflox/flox mice possessing Flox sites flanking exon 2 of PR were kindly provided by John Leydon at the Baylor College of Medicine, 61 and were crossed onto GnRH‐GFP mice to generate homozygous PRfl/fl × GnRH‐GFP+/+ mice (now referred to as PRfl/fl). Vgat‐Cre × tdTomato mice were crossed with PRfl/fl × GnRH‐Gfp+/+ mice to generate Vgat‐Cre+/− tdTomato+/+ × PRfl/fl GnRH‐Gfp+/+ mice (now referred to as GABAPRKO). Genotypes were determined by PCR analysis, with primers listed in Table S1. Mice were housed in open top cages under a 12:12 h light/dark cycle at 21°C with ad libitum access to water and standard chow. All animal procedures were approved by the University of Otago Animal Ethics Committee.

Stereotaxic surgery for AAV injections in PRfl /fl mice

Stereotaxic surgery was performed on adult (3–4 months) female PRfl/fl mice. Stereotaxic coordinates for targeting the arcuate nucleus were derived from 62 : −1.26 mm A‐P, ±0.3 mm M‐L from bregma, and −6.0 mm D‐V from the top of the brain. Bilateral 200 nL injections of AAV/DJ‐CMV‐mCherry‐T2A‐iCre (AAV‐Cre) or AAV/DJ‐CMV‐mCherry (AAV‐mCherry) were performed using a 32G Hamilton 0.5 μL Neuros Syringe. Validation of AAV‐Cre‐mediated excision of PR in PRfl/fl mice was performed as previously described. 63 Mice with fewer than 10 transfected cells in the ARC and/or transfected cells in regions external to the ARC were included as an additional “AAV‐Cre no knockdown” control group (Figure S3).

Perfusion

Mice were transcardially perfused in the diestrus stage of the estrous cycle with 20 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde. The skull was removed and the brain was collected and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose. Ovaries were removed and post fixed overnight at 4°C, then stored in 70% EtOH until processing for histology. Perfused brain tissue was sectioned on the coronal plane at 30 μm in a 1:3 series through the hypothalamus using a freezing stage microtome. Sectioned tissue was stored at −20°C in cryoprotectant until processed for immunohistochemistry (IHC).

Immunohistochemistry

Fluorescent IHC was performed on free floating brain sections to label PR in GABAPRKO, and PR and AAV‐associated mCherry in PRfl/fl virally transfected animals. Sections were washed overnight in TBS at 4°C, followed by three 10 min washes in TBS at room temperature. Tissue was incubated in 0.3% Triton X‐100‐TBS to permeabilise the cell membranes, followed by a 1 h blocking step in 0.3% Triton X‐100‐TBS, 10% FBS, 0.25% BSA, and a 1 h blocking step in 0.3% Triton X‐100‐TBS, 5% NGS, 25% BSA. Sections were incubated in rabbit anti‐PR (rbPR67771;1:200 63 ) alone or with chicken anti‐mCherry (ab205402;1:10,000) in blocking solution at 4°C for 65 h. Sections were washed in TBS, incubated in goat anti‐rabbit Alexa Fluor647 (ab150079;1:500), and goat anti‐chicken Alexa Fluor568 (ab175477;1:500) for 3 h, then washed in TBS. Brain sections were mounted onto gelatin‐coated slides and cover‐slipped with Fluoromount‐G mounting medium. For IHC analysis, tissue was imaged at 20× using a Nikon A1 inverted confocal microscope. For GABAPRKO, two representative sections from mid‐ARC were assessed for knockout validation. For PRfl/fl, Z‐stacks spanning 16 μm (2 μm apart, pinhole 1.2 AU) were taken in six representative sections, two each from the rostral, mid, and caudal arcuate nucleus (bregma coordinates −1.22, −1.7, −2.54 mm) to assess PR knockdown. Cell counts were performed using ImageJ2 (v2.14.0/1.5f ).

Reproductive phenotyping

Puberty onset and estrous cyclicity

Puberty onset of GABAPRKO mice was measured by daily examination for the age of vaginal opening, after which daily vaginal smears were performed to detect first estrus. To evaluate estrous cyclicity, vaginal cytology of GABAPRKO was monitored from P60–P80. Vaginal cytology of PRfl/fl was monitored for 14 days preceding stereotaxic injection of AAV virus (Pre‐AAV). Three weeks following virus injection, PRfl/fl vaginal cytology was monitored for a further 14 days (post‐AAV). An estrous cycle was defined by the interval of Proestrus (P) to P, with only normally cycling mice (>1 proestrus/14 days) included in analysis.

Ovarian histology and analysis

Ovaries were processed and analysed as previously described. 21 , 22 Briefly, fixed ovaries were paraffin embedded and sectioned at 5 μm, with one section collected every 50 μm for analysis. Tissue was stained with haematoxylin and eosin and imaged with an Olympus BX51 microscope using a 4× objective for pre‐ovulatory follicle and corpora lutea counts, and a 10× objective of the largest antral follicle for analysis of theca cell layer and granulosa cell layer area. Cell layer thickness was measured using ImageJ software.

Progesterone negative feedback

Tail‐tip blood samples were collected in a capillary tube from adult females in diestrous, before animals were bilaterally ovariectomised (OVX) under 2% isofluorane anaesthesia. Three days later, an additional “post‐OVX” blood sample was collected and mice received a subcutaneous P4 capsule (0.12 mg/day, Innovative Research of America) under isofluorane anaesthesia. A further blood sample was collected 2 days after P4 implantation for assessment of P4 negative feedback of LH.

Serial blood sampling

Adult females were habituated by daily handling for 4–5 weeks before serial tail‐tip blood sampling to measure LH pulsatility. Briefly, 4 μL of whole blood was collected into 50 μL PBS + 0.05% Tween‐20 from mice in diestrous, every 6 min for a 2 h period. Blood samples were snap frozen on dry ice and stored at −20°C until required for ELISA.

Hormone measurements

Circulating LH levels were assessed using an established, ultrasensitive LH ELISA, performed as previously. 22 , 60 , 64 High binding 96 well microplates were coated with 50 μL/well of capture antibody (bovine LHb518B7 monoclonal antibody, 1:1000 in PBS, RRID: AB_2756886; Pablo Ross, UC Davis, CA). Mouse LH‐RP reference was provided by Albert F. Parlow (National Hormone & Pituitary Program, Torrance, CA). To detect bound LH, a rabbit polyclonal antibody for LH (1:10,000, AFP240590Rb; RRID: AB_2665533; National Hormone and Pituitary Program, Torrance, CA), and a polyclonal goat anti‐rabbit‐HRP secondary (1:1000; RRID: AB_2617138; DAKO) were used. Assay sensitivity was 0.04 ng/mL, and intra‐ and inter‐assay coefficients of variance were 3.6% and 14.6%, respectively. Reformulated PULSAR software was used to detect LH pulses, using settings for intact female mice (G1 = 3.5, G2 = 2.6, G3 = 1.9, G4 = 1.5, and G5 = 1.2). 65 Basal LH concentration was calculated as the average of the 6 lowest non‐zero values, and amplitude of individual LH pulses was determined within PULSAR software. Only animals with detectable pulses were included in analysis to ensure LH pulse dynamics were assessed in animals with a functioning pulse generator.

To measure the plasma concentration of testosterone, a pre‐AAV blood sample from tail and a terminal post‐AAV blood sample from the vena cava were collected from GABAPRKO and PRfl/fl mice using heparin‐coated capillary tubes. Samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 4G and plasma was recovered and stored at −20°C. Testosterone levels were assessed using a commercially available rat/mouse testosterone ELISA according to the manufacturer's instructions (LDN GmbH&Co.KG, Nordhorn, Germany, #AR E‐8000R) with the inclusion of an additional standard of 0.05 ng/mL. Each sample was run in duplicate. The intra‐assay coefficient of variation was 6.2%.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (Version 10.4.1). Normality of data was assessed using the D'Agostino & Pearson test and Shapiro–Wilk test. Data from two groups were compared using unpaired t test for normally distributed data and Mann Whitney tests for non‐normal data. For AAV injected PRfl/fl mice, colocalisation of PR and mCherry was analysed using the Welch's ANOVA test, and the extent of PR knockdown was analysed using the Brown–Forsythe ANOVA test. Pearson's correlation coefficient was used to determine whether the extent of PR knockdown in ARC corresponded to the degree of AAV‐Cre transfection. Wilcoxon matched‐pairs signed rank test was used for PRfl/fl estrous cyclicity data, correcting for multiple comparisons using the Holm‐Sidak method. Two‐way repeated measures ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparisons test or Sidak's multiple comparisons test were used for GABAPRKO estrous cyclicity and P4 negative feedback data. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, with p ≤ .05 considered statistically significant.
