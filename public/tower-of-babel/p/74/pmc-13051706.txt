Quantifying the Opportunity Cost of Resident Involvement in Total Hip and Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Muzamil Ahmad, J. Adam Driscoll, Young Jee, Nicholas Durand, Betina B. Hinckel, Leonardo Cavinatto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResident participation in total hip arthroplasty and total knee arthroplasty (THA/TKA) is important, but the time-driven financial impact remains unclear. We quantified the “opportunity cost” of resident involvement in THA and TKA.MethodsPrimary THA and TKA cases were identified at a single academic center (2013-2017). Cases were attending-only (AO) or resident-involved (RI). Fellows excluded. RI was stratified between juniors (postgraduate year I-III) and seniors (postgraduate year IV-V). Outcomes included operative efficiency and financial productivity. Relative value units/case were fixed at 19.6 to isolate time-driven effects.ResultsA total of 3,217 AO and 1,148 RI THA cases, and 4,174 AO and 1,235 RI TKA cases were analyzed. RI increased operative time (THA +4.0 minutes, 95% CI [2.3, 5.7]; TKA +7.9 minutes, 95% CI [6.7, 9.1]; both p<0.001) but reduced set-up time (THA -1.7 minutes, 95% CI [-2.2, -1.2]; TKA -1.6 minutes, 95% CI [-2.0, -1.2]; both p<0.001), partially offsetting total operating room (OR) time (THA +2.3 minutes; TKA +6.3 minutes). Juniors concentrated these effects. Seniors approximated AO efficiency and had quicker THA total OR times. Per-case opportunity costs were small: operative-time (THA $36.81; TKA $76.51) and total OR time (THA $15.16; TKA $7.22). Seniors showed a $12.99 per-case savings on a THA total OR time basis.ConclusionsResident participation in primary THA/TKA carries small, experience-dependent costs, partially offset with seniority and quicker set-up times. These findings may reflect anticipatory workflow gains and offer insights into schedule optimization.

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) and total knee arthroplasty (TKA) are among the most frequently performed operations in the United States, with demand projected to continue rising as the population ages [1,2]. Institutions and residency programs must deliver quality surgical care while balancing the education and training of residents; however, resident participation can introduce adjustments in workflow that may interrupt a program’s financial potential, critical to maintaining and expanding residency positions. Thus, it is critical that the economics of resident participation are fully understood in order for residency programs and hospitals to adapt to any financial adjustments to successfully train competent orthopaedic surgeons.

Prior studies evaluating resident involvement in orthopaedic surgery have mainly focused on early postoperative complications or operative times [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. In arthroplasty specifically, studies have found modest increases in operative time with resident involvement, with no significant increases in early postoperative complications [[7], [8], [9]]. The literature is scarce in examining whether any observed efficiency impact is found across resident postgraduate year (PGY) in THA and TKA procedures. Furthermore, the literature is limited on analyzing the economics of resident participation in THA and TKA on the basis of operative time, case set-up time, total operating room (OR) time, and how these differences impact relative value units (RVUs). In order to fully investigate this, an important principle to quantify is the “opportunity cost” of resident involvement. The opportunity cost may be thought of as the amount of cases and revenue that could have been generated had that time not been spent teaching. In other words, the opportunity cost strictly represents the time-driven reductions in attending per-case RVU productivity and is not to be confused with the “financial impact” of training which encompasses institutional financial costs overall, such as, but certainly not limited to: facility revenue, staffing fees, and downstream postoperative revenue. While previous studies have investigated the opportunity cost of resident involvement in sports medicine and shoulder arthroplasty, a noticeable gap exists in the literature in quantifying the opportunity cost of total hip and knee arthroplasty [10,11].

When considering resident experience, limited data exist on any experience-dependent impacts on the opportunity costs of training. Resident contributions in surgery are not a uniform variable as operative efficiency evolves as trainees progress. Literature has shown that early trainees typically require more operative time and supervision, whereas senior trainees perform tasks with greater autonomy and efficiency [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. This experience-efficiency curve of resident surgical training becomes an important principal to account for since opportunity costs are directly influenced by time-based productivity metrics, allowing for the characterization of opportunity costs in early vs late training to avoid masking any underlying heterogeneity between junior and senior residents. In order to account for these differences, a single-center design enhances internal validity by providing consistent and standardized perioperative protocols and staffing models to capture and characterize any minute-level differences and workflow effects.

Therefore, the purpose of this study is to define the opportunity cost of resident participation in total hip and knee arthroplasty by quantifying differences in operative time, total OR time, case set-up time, and RVUs per hour, stratifying these differences between junior (PGY I-III) and senior (PGY IV-V) residents to account for any experience-dependent differences in operative efficiency. We hypothesize that resident involvement will have an increased per-case opportunity cost dependent on resident experience, with juniors demonstrating increased operative times and senior participation approximating attending performance.

Material and methods

Following institutional review board exemption, a registry-based, retrospective analysis was conducted using the Michigan Arthroplasty Registry Collaborative Quality Initiative (MARCQI) database, identifying all primary elective TKAs (Current Procedural Terminology [CPT] 27447) and THAs (CPT 27130) performed at a single level 1 university hospital in Royal Oak, Michigan, between July 2013 and December 2017.

Utilizing MARCQI, TKA and THA cases were identified via database query using their respective CPT codes, 27447 and 27130, respectively. Extracted data included patient demographics (age, sex, race), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, comorbidities, preoperative lab values (creatinine, hemoglobin), operative time (incision to closure), set-up time (room-in to incision), RVUs, and case personnel. Specific factors related to surgical complexity outside of the aforementioned comorbidities were not captured in this cohort. Cases included for analysis were adult primary TKA and THA; excluded cases were those performed for nonosteoarthritis diagnoses, revision arthroplasty, simultaneous bilateral THA, and cases with fellow involvement. Cases missing entry data on attending presence, fellow presence, or resident PGY-level were filtered out and excluded from analysis. Study groups consisted of attending-only (AO) vs resident-involved (RI) cases. Resident involvement was stratified based on PGY, defined a priori as junior (PGY I-III) and senior (PGY IV-V) to reflect early vs late stages of training; however, PGY-based definitions may vary across institutions and specialties.

Primary outcomes were operational efficiency and financial metrics. Operational efficiency metrics included operative time (incision to closure), case set-up time (room-in to incision), and total OR time (operative time + case set-up time). Financial metrics included RVUs/h and RVUs/case. Mean differences in operative time, total OR time, RVUs/case, and RVUs/h between AO and RI cases were examined (overall and by PGY-level).

RVU production rate and incremental RVU loss attributable to resident participation were computed using conversion formulae outlined in Figure 1. Opportunity costs per case (USD) were estimated using the generic 2020 Medicare Conversion Factor ($36.09/RVU) to translate RVUs/h into cost data correlating to the cost of resident education. RVUs per case were fixed at 19.6 (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services work RVUs for primary THA/TKA) to isolate the time-driven component of opportunity costs, avoiding confounding from additional coding modifiers or case-complexity effects that influence real-world billed RVUs. Statistical analysis was performed using Python. All analyses were performed using complete case data, with no missing data present. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using 2-sample tests of means (Welch t-tests) (AO vs any resident; AO vs junior; AO vs senior). Effect sizes are reported as absolute mean differences vs AO (Δ) with 95% CIs. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05. Analyses were intentionally unadjusted to quantify the opportunity cost under a real-worse case mix rather than estimate a risk-adjusted causal effect of resident involvement. As such, standardized mean differences (SMDs) are presented to contextualize the magnitude of any baseline differences.

Results

For THA, AO vs RI cases differed in patient body mass index (29.2 ± 5.7 AO, 29.6 ± 5.9 RI; P = .026, SMD = 0.069). For TKA, AO vs RI cases differed in patient age (67.2 ± 9.5 years AO, 65.8 ± 9.5 years RI; P < .001, SMD = 0.147), hospital vs private clinic (5.60% AO, 9.90% RI; P < .001, SMD = 0.161), ASA classification (ASA 1-2: 55.10% AO vs 48.30% RI; ASA 3-4: 44.90% AO vs 51.70% RI; P < .001, SMD = 0.136), body mass index (31.5 ± 6.1 AO, 32.3 ± 6.4 RI; P < .001, SMD = 0.128), smoking status (never: 56.50% AO vs 52.20% RI; previous: 37.00% AO vs 39.30% RI; current: 6.00% AO vs 8.40% RI; P < .001, all SMD<0.090), and diabetes (16.90% AO, 20.80% RI; P = .002, SMD = 0.100). With respect to THA cases (n = 3217 AO; n = 1148 RI) (Table 3), mean operative time was significantly longer for RI cases compared to AO cases (80.5 min RI vs 76.5 min AO; 95% CI: 2.3-5.7; P < .001). When stratifying between junior (n = 699) and senior (n = 449) residents, this increase in mean operative time was concentrated among junior residents (85.7 min; P < .001), whereas senior residents aligned closer to AO cases (77.1 min; P = .536), as depicted in Figure 2. With case set-up time, RI cases demonstrated a significant reduction over AO cases (30.1 min RI vs 31.8 min AO; 95% CI: -2.2 to −1.2; P < .001). Senior residents demonstrated the greatest reduction in mean case set-up time (29.2 min; P < .001), whereas juniors only had marginal improvements over AO (31.5 min; P = .367), as depicted in Figure 3. In total OR time, AO cases had a mean of 108.3 min, RI cases overall at 110.6 min, junior residents at 117.2 min, and senior residents at 106.3 min. Precision of these differences, depicted in Table 3 as Δ vs AO (95% CI), confirms small absolute effects largely concentrated among junior residents. For operative time, RI cases overall demonstrated a lower productivity rate compared to AO cases (14.61 RI vs 15.37 AO RVUs/h; Δ −0.76 RVUs/h), which corresponded to a 1.02 reduction in RVUs/case at an opportunity cost of $36.81 per case. Similarly, for total OR time, RI cases overall demonstrated a reduction in productivity rate compared to AO cases (10.63 vs 10.86 RVUs/h; Δ −0.23 RVUs/h), corresponding to a 0.42 reduction in RVUs/case and an opportunity cost of $15.16 per case. When stratifying by resident level, junior resident operative times displayed a mean of 13.72 RVUs/h (Δ −1.65 RVUs/h vs AO), resulting in a 2.36 reduction in RVUs/case and an $85.17 opportunity cost per case. For total OR time, junior residents showed a mean of 10.03 RVUs/h (Δ −0.83 RVUs/h vs AO), resulting in a 1.61 reduction in RVUs/case and a $58.10 opportunity cost per case. Senior resident operative times demonstrated a mean of 15.25 RVUs/h (Δ −0.12 RVUs/h vs AO), resulting in a 0.15 reduction in RVUs/case and a $5.41 opportunity cost per case. When stratifying between resident level (n = 795 junior; n = 440 senior), both junior (81.7 min; P < .001) and senior (80.4 min; P < .001) residents demonstrated significant increases in mean operative times compared to AO, as depicted in Figure 2. With case set-up time, RI cases were significantly quicker in this aspect compared to AO (28.5 min RI vs 30.1 AO min; 95% CI: −2.0 to −1.2; P < .001), and both junior (29.2 min; P = .01) and senior (28.1 min; P < .001) residents upheld this advantage over AO, as depicted in Figure 3. In terms of total OR time, AO cases had a mean of 103.1 min, RI cases overall at 109.4 min, junior residents at 110.9 min, and senior residents at 108.5 min. Precision of these differences, depicted in Table 4 as Δ vs AO (95% CI), confirms small absolute effects largely concentrated among junior residents. For operative time, RI cases had a lower productivity rate compared to AO cases (14.54 RI vs 16.11 AO RVUs/h; Δ −1.57 RVUs/h), corresponding to a 2.12 reduction in RVUs/case at an opportunity cost of $76.51 per case. Similarly with total OR time, RI cases had a reduction in productivity rate compared to AO cases (10.75 RI vs 11.41 AO RVUs/h; Δ −0.66 RVUs/h), corresponding to a 0.20 reduction in RVUs/case at an opportunity cost of $7.22 per case. Discussion

In this single-center MARCQI review of primary THA and TKA cases, resident participation had small, quantifiable effects on OR efficiency that were highly dependent on resident experience and seniority. Compared with AO cases, RI cases increased operative time modestly (THA +4.0 min; TKA +7.9 min) while reducing case set-up time (THA -1.7 min; TKA -1.6 min). Because these opposing effects partially offset each other, the net difference in total OR time was less prominent (THA +2.3 min; TKA +6.3 min), suggesting gains in workflow efficiency may offset intraoperative teaching time. In practical terms, the net total OR time differences observed were small. These magnitudes, while statistically detectable, are generally below any meaningful thresholds that would change the number of cases completed in a typical day of arthroplasty. When translated to an RVUs/h framework, these time differences translated into minor per-case opportunity costs on an operative time basis (THA $36.81; TKA $76.51), which was further attenuated when calculated on a total OR time basis (THA $15.16; TKA $7.22). The observed productivity losses with resident involvement were largely concentrated among junior residents. Junior residents accounted for nearly all increases in operative times and opportunity costs, whereas seniors approximated AO efficiency. In THA, senior resident participation approached AO operative time. When total OR time was considered, senior residents yielded modest per-case savings of $12.99, consistent with faster set-up times. In TKA, seniors remained marginally slower than AO on the basis of operative time, but were near-neutral on a total OR time basis, also consistent with the observed gains in efficiency surrounding case set-up time.

These patterns align with trainee learning and workflow familiarity, suggesting that resident experience may improve team efficiency, anticipatory preparation, and case turnover. Furthermore, the observed reductions in set-up times with residents involvement may reflect the benefits of added personnel to aid in preoperative tasks through task delegation and parallel workflow processing. Residents assist with patient positioning, tourniquet placement in TKAs, prepping, draping, instrument readiness, and anticipating behaviors, allowing other staff members to complete standard preoperative procedures to increase overall team efficiency. The intriguing observation for senior resident involvement showing cost-saving in THA, but not in TKA, could possibly reflect the procedural workflow differences and experience-dependent task allocation between these 2 procedures; however, this discrepancy is hypothesis-generating and warrants future investigation.

The observed findings complement prior arthroplasty literature which have variably reported increases in operative times with resident involvement [[7], [8], [9]], while also adding elements often absent in the current literature: separating operative time from total OR time in order to capture upstream and downstream impacts; translating these time differences into RVU-based productivity metrics; and stratifying by resident level. The consistent pattern of reduced case set-up time in RI cases demonstrates that intraoperative education is not synonymous with lower productivity, especially if the participating resident is of higher seniority, offsetting any increases in opportunity costs.

However, this appears to be contrary to reported literature in orthopaedic sports medicine and shoulder arthroplasty, which demonstrate decreased resident efficiency with seniority due to greater operative autonomy [10,11]. The most plausible explanation for this contradiction is that THA and TKA are highly standardized, and as residents progress, they gain confidence and familiarity with operative procedural steps that are learned and repeated over time. Additionally, with increased resident experience, greater intraoperative anticipatory needs may contribute to overall gains in operative efficiency. Prior studies on surgical training corroborate our findings, reporting reductions in operative times with increased resident seniority [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. In total, these factors likely explain the observed reduction in THA and TKA operative times and opportunity costs with greater resident seniority.

Despite increasing true operative time overall, resident involvement appears to play an important role in minimizing total OR time, attributable to quicker set-up times compared to AO cases. While statistically significant, per-case opportunity costs for case operative time were modest (THA $36.81; TKA $76.51), with the complete view of resident efficiency appreciated when evaluating per-case opportunity costs for total OR time (THA $15.16; TKA $7.22). If taken at face value, these amounts aggregated at a high volume may seem financially relevant; however, these per-case cost estimates isolate only the time-linked component and do not consider the many downstream benefits of resident training such as increased workforce capacity, coverage of call and clinics, increased scholarly output, and allow attendings to coordinate care between multiple ORs, greatly increasing productivity [[16], [17], [18]]. The consistent finding of reductions in set-up times suggests that resident training has positive impacts in upstream efficiency, especially among seniors, which demonstrated cost savings in THA cases when compared to AO. Although several groups reached statistical significance, the absolute time and dollar differences were small and further attenuated on a total OR time basis. In terms of typical OR scheduling and case turnover, such deltas are unlikely to alter daily case capacity. The practical takeaway is not to limit resident participation but to optimize efficiency, supervision, and room set-up/closing for junior residents where the observed impacts are greatest, likely a higher-value pathway than reducing trainee participation.

With these findings, residency programs could consider neutral offsets to improve their financial health while preserving education and minimizing downstream delays. From a logistics standpoint, case lists heavy with junior participation may benefit from scheduling shorter add-on cases or cases with less complexity to better absorb the modest increases in operative time, while senior residents may be better suited for time-sensitive THA blocks in order to leverage the greatest reductions in set-up times. As a practical example, schedules with heavy junior participation may be optimized to prevent junior case clustering by adding predictable and shorter cases in-between so as to avoid any additive or downstream minute-level delays. For senior residents, scheduling may favor high-volume case lists where the high productivity gains can be concentrated, rather than spreading senior residents thinly across a schedule and diluting these gains. Such strategies may offset any productivity losses to avoid restricting resident participation and exposure in the OR. Additionally, compensation to attendings involved in intraoperative resident education may be warranted given the reduction in attending productivity.

This study has several limitations important for readers to consider. First, this study was limited by its retrospective nature and the data available in the MARCQI database at a single institution within the United States, with local practice patterns and resident education that limit generalizability. Interpretation may differ across institutions or country where training structures and staffing models may vary, and our findings should be considered within the context of U.S. residency participation and workflows. In regard to registry personnel, fellow participation was excluded using filters and indicated registry fields; however, misclassification of fellow presence (ie, fellow being marked as not present, when in reality a fellow was present) could have inflated any observed associations, and is an inherent limitation to retrospective, registry-based studies to keep in mind. Second, the use of operative time, total OR time, and case set-up time as a gauge of resident efficiency does not fully capture the broader “financial impact” that residents have on an institution. To add, opportunity costs capture time-driven reductions in attending per-case RVU productivity and does not capture revenue generated through factors such as parallel-room utilization, downstream patient management, or call coverage, key elements that may offset any differences in minute-level intraoperative time. Therefore, the opportunity cost of the current paper solely captures the intraoperative effects on RVU productivity with resident involvement in THA/TKA cases and is not to be misconstrued as presenting a broader financial impact. Third, we did not perform covariate-adjusted sensitivity analyses for group baseline differences in order to preserve a real-world case mix to quantify the opportunity cost. Although statistically significant baseline differences did exist, clinically they were nearly the same and the magnitude of SMDs were small overall. Nevertheless, residual confounding from nonrandom resident assignment or unmeasured case complexity could have influenced the observed time differences. For example, the increased presence of comorbidities in a specific group, such as obesity, could have contributed to unmeasured perioperative complexity that increased operative duration independent of resident participation. Fourth, RVUs per case were treated as a constant (19.6 units) so as to isolate time effects, and while appropriate, case complexity or add-on billing could have varied case to case and is not captured with this approach. Fifth, attending surgeon identifiers were not available in the registry for attending-level adjustments, and attending-specific efficiency differences could have influenced operative duration, which is a major determinant of operative duration; differential resident assignment across surgeons could therefore serve as confounding for the observed associations. Sixth, our time metrics do not capture post-closure (closure to room-out) time metrics, missing any post-closure teaching (eg, dressing and wound management) which may prolong total OR time, potentially underestimating opportunity costs. Lastly, as resident case assignment is nonrandom, the observed time differences may be influenced by individual case complexity and preferential assignment. If residents were preferentially assigned to more complex cases, our estimates likely overstate time penalties. On the contrary, if residents were preferentially assigned to simpler cases, they may understate any effect, limiting generalizability across institutions where different resident scheduling and staffing practices may exist. This unmeasured selection bias could influence estimates in either direction and should be considered when interpreting the observed findings. Despite these limitations, this study reports useful quantitative benchmarks of financial and productivity metrics of resident involvement in THA and TKA cases, quantifying the opportunity cost, and filling in a noticeable gap in the literature on this matter; however, these findings must be interpreted in the context of the aforementioned limitations. Future studies should be multi-institutional with risk adjustment to be able to analyze more granular data and to assess the impact of practice patterns in resident education that may vary institutionally.

Conclusions

Resident participation in primary THA and TKA cases carries a small, quantifiable, experience-dependent opportunity cost that is primarily driven by modest increases in operative time (THA +4.0 min; TKA +7.9 min) that are partially offset by quicker room set-up times, producing small net changes in total OR time (THA +2.3 min; TKA +6.3 min). Modeled per-case opportunity costs were minor on an operative time basis (THA $36.81; TKA $76.51) and further attenuated on a total OR time basis (THA $15.16; TKA $7.22), with this effect largely concentrated among junior residents. Seniors approached attending efficiency and were near-neutral or cost-saving. As absolute differences and opportunity costs were operationally small overall, resident participation is unlikely to meaningfully alter daily scheduling capacity. In high-volume settings where per-case costs can accumulate, these findings may inform optimized case scheduling in order to capture specific resident productivity gains to mitigate any incremental costs, rather than limiting educational involvement. Future work is warranted to validate these findings.
