Underutilization of Hand Corticosteroid Injections and Arthroplasty for Minority Demographics
Authors: Christopher M Dussik, Amy Phan, Akhil Dondapati, Jeffrey Coombs, Danielle Wilbur, Ronald Gonzalez, Constantinos Ketonis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe management of hand osteoarthritis is a cornerstone of hand surgery practice. Corticosteroid injections and surgical interventions form the mainstay of treatment for those patients with symptoms recalcitrant to less-invasive measures. Health care disparities have been increasingly recognized, particularly regarding variations in pain management. This study aimed to determine whether such disparities exist across different racial and ethnic groups in the treatment of hand osteoarthritis.MethodsWe used the TriNetX database to evaluate patients diagnosed with hand osteoarthritis between January 1, 2010 and December 31, 2024. Diagnostic and billing codes were used to identify patients, assess overall health care utilization postdiagnosis, and gauge the use of corticosteroid injections and surgical intervention across different demographics. Outcomes were stratified based on racial and ethnic identification. Matching was performed to mitigate risk for confounding. Odds ratios were used to describe differences in the probability of receiving treatment across groups of interest.ResultsA total of 896,636 patients diagnosed with hand osteoarthritis were identified. Non-Hispanic White patients had nearly twice the odds of undergoing corticosteroid injections and demonstrated consistently higher rates of surgical treatment compared with minority populations across unmatched analyses. After matching for demographic and comorbidity variables, these disparities largely persisted despite similar overall health care utilization.ConclusionsMinority demographics have lower odds of receiving corticosteroid injections for the treatment of hand osteoarthritis. Similarly, these groups undergo surgical management at considerably lower rates. These findings highlight the persistent disparities in treatment faced by underrepresented groups and underscore the critical importance of considering social determinants of health in the management of upper extremity conditions.Type of study/level of evidencePrognosis IIC.Key words: Arthritis, Arthroplasty, Carpometacarpal, Interphalangeal joint, Metacarpophalangeal

Osteoarthritis of the hand is the most common form of degenerative joint disease, with its prevalence only expected to increase in the coming years because of the aging global population.1,2 The clinical manifestations of the disease are heterogeneous, with some patients exhibiting relatively benign symptomatology despite radiographically severe disease. However, symptomatic hand osteoarthritis is well-documented to be associated with the loss of strength and function.3,4 Risk factors for the development of hand osteoarthritis include advanced age, female sex, heart disease, obesity, diabetes, smoking, and inflammatory arthropathies.5,6 In most symptomatic cases, hand osteoarthritis may be managed conservatively through activity modification, intermittent immobilization, and topical or oral anti-inflammatory medications.7 However, in cases of disease recalcitrant to these measures, more invasive measures may be necessary, ranging from intra-articular corticosteroid injections to various forms of joint arthroplasty and, potentially, joint arthrodesis.

Racial and ethnic identity has been consistently associated with reduced rates of surgical intervention across a range of surgical specialties.8, 9, 10 Similar disparities have been documented in orthopedic extremity trauma, spine, and joint arthroplasty.11, 12, 13, 14, 15 In particular, there is growing awareness of the underutilization of surgical intervention for the treatment of osteoarthritis across multiple orthopedic subspecialties. Both racial and socioeconomic factors have been well-documented to influence the rate of total knee and total hip arthroplasties, with minority demographics undergoing comparatively fewer surgical treatments compared with non-Hispanic White counterparts.16, 17, 18 Similar trends have been observed in upper extremity arthroplasty, specifically total shoulder and total elbow arthroplasties, across a period spanning more than a decade.19, 20, 21 In contrast, the literature on hand osteoarthritis is comparatively limited in evaluating the underutilization of treatment modalities, particularly hand arthroplasty techniques.22 We posited that similar trends may exist within hand surgery and that, if further characterized, such findings could inform efforts to promote more equitable care. Therefore, we aimed to assess treatment utilization for hand osteoarthritis across demographic groups in the United States and to determine whether underutilization patterns in hand surgery mirror those reported in other orthopedic subspecialties.

Materials and Methods

We performed a retrospective analysis within TriNetX, a multi-institutional research database that is largely centralized in the United States and that deidentifies and aggregates data from a total of 108 distinct health care organizations, with data from over 140 million patients. These data were derived from health care organizations, including 57 academic centers, 50 nonacademic centers, and 1 uncategorized facility. Deidentification is performed according to the standards defined in Section §164.514(a) of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule. The process of deidentifying the data are attested to through a formal determination by a qualified expert, as defined in Section §164.514(b)(1) of the HIPAA Privacy Rule. The TriNetX database was queried on August 19, 2025. This study was deemed exempt by our institutional review board.

Data were collected for patients diagnosed with hand osteoarthritis between January 1, 2010 and December 31, 2024. Patients with hand osteoarthritis were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision codes (Table 1). Rates of both corticosteroid injection and surgical intervention for the treatment of hand osteoarthritis were identified using current procedural terminology codes. Surgical interventions of interest included carpometacarpal (CMC) arthroplasty or arthrodesis, metacarpophalangeal (MCP) arthroplasty or arthrodesis, and interphalangeal (IP) arthroplasty or arthrodesis. Overall exposure to the health care system was assessed using current procedural terminology codes for either inpatient or outpatient evaluations.

Patients were subcategorized into cohorts based on patient-reported race and ethnicity. These were defined as non-Hispanic White and minority cohorts, with the minority cohort comprising patients who identified as Hispanic, Black or African American, or Asian or Pacific Islander. A combination of chi-square and Student t tests was used to compare categorical and continuous variables between cohorts, respectively. The rates of corticosteroid injection and surgical intervention among minority populations were compared with those of non-Hispanic White patients using odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. To account for potential confounding, logistic regression was performed to account for age, sex, and medical comorbidities to facilitate one-to-one greedy propensity score matching to assess procedural differences between racial and ethnic groups. The comorbidities controlled for in the matched analyses were active diagnoses present at or prior to the time of hand osteoarthritis diagnosis and included diabetes mellitus (E08−E13), obesity (E65−E68), malnutrition (E40−E46), vascular disease (I70−I79), kidney disease (N17-N19), nicotine dependence (F17), and inflammatory polyarthropathies (M05−M14). One-to-one matching successfully paired 99.7% to 100% of patients in each minority cohort with a corresponding non-Hispanic White patient. To account for heterogeneous follow-up and minimize potential treatment bias, outcomes in the matched cohorts were assessed within a fixed 3-year period following diagnosis. Statistical significance was set at P < .05 for all analyses.

Results

Demographics and medical comorbidities

In total, 896,636 patients were diagnosed with hand osteoarthritis between January 1, 2010 and December 31, 2024 (Table 2). The majority—677,768 patients (76%)—identified as non-Hispanic and White. In contrast, 218,868 patients (24%) were identified as belonging to a minority racial or ethnic population, which was comprised of patients identifying as Hispanic, Black or African American, and Asian or Pacific Islander. The median follow-up for patients in the non-Hispanic White cohort was 1,460 days (interquartile range: 1,887 days), whereas patients in the minority population cohort were followed for a median of 1,295 days (interquartile range: 1,829 days). When stratified by race, the largest minority group was Black or African American patients (112,566 patients, 13% of the overall cohort), followed by Hispanic patients (67,005 patients, 7% of the overall cohort) and Asian or Pacific Islander patients (37,760 patients, 4% of the overall cohort). On average, patients in the non-Hispanic White cohort were slightly older, with a mean age of 65 years, compared with 60–64 years among minority subpopulations. Women comprised approximately 70% of all patients diagnosed with hand osteoarthritis across all groups, although minority cohorts showed a slightly higher proportion of women relative to the non-Hispanic White cohort. The rates of medical comorbidities were heterogeneous between non-Hispanic White patients and patients from minority populations.

Overall health care exposure and management of hand osteoarthritis

We used health care encounters as a proxy to assess overall exposure to the health care system (Table 3). On average, non-Hispanic White patients had 21.4 health care encounters during the period of interest after diagnosis, which was slightly higher than the average number of encounters experienced by the overall minority population at 21.3 (P < .05). When further subdivided by race, African American patients had a higher average number of encounters at 23.7, whereas Hispanic and Asian/Pacific Islander patients had fewer encounters, averaging 18.4 and 20.0, respectively (P < .05).

Corticosteroid injections were administered at significantly higher rates in non-Hispanic White patients with hand osteoarthritis (11.6%) compared with minority cohorts, where rates ranged from 5.8% to 6.7% (P < .05). From a surgical perspective, White patients were also more likely to undergo multiple forms of surgical management. All types of arthroplasty procedures—including CMC, MCP, and IP arthroplasty—were performed more frequently in non-Hispanic White patients compared with minority cohorts. Conversely, although CMC arthrodesis, MCP arthrodesis, and IP arthrodesis were performed less often among the overall minority group compared with non-Hispanic White patients, this effect was not consistent across all minority subgroups. Comparable rates of CMC arthrodesis were observed in both Black/African American and Hispanic cohorts, MCP arthrodesis in the Black/African American cohort, and IP arthrodesis in the Hispanic cohort (P > .05).

Matched analyses of treatment patterns and odds for hand osteoarthritis according to race and ethnicity

Given the heterogeneity of demographic and comorbidity variables observed across the cohorts in our study, we performed separate matched analyses to minimize the potential for confounding. Overall, minority cohort patients were less than half as likely to undergo surgical intervention compared with their matched non-Hispanic White counterparts (P < .05), with 3,503 minority patients (1.6%) undergoing surgery versus 7,453 non-Hispanic White patients (3.4%; Fig. 1). On average, each surgical patient in the non-Hispanic White cohort underwent slightly more procedures than their minority counterparts, with a mean of 1.14 procedures per surgical patient compared with 1.11 (P < .05). After comorbidity matching, the odds of undergoing corticosteroid injection remained significantly lower across all minority cohorts, with ORs ranging from 0.50 (95% CI, 0.48–0.53) in Asian/Pacific Islander patients to 0.55 (95% CI, 0.53–0.57) in Hispanic patients (Table 4). Regarding surgical intervention, the rates of all surgical treatment modalities were decreased in the overall minority cohort (Fig. 2). When stratified by racial subcategories, the rates of CMC arthroplasty were consistently lower, with ORs ranging from 0.23 (95% CI, 0.20–0.27) to 0.45 (95% CI, 0.41–0.48), and MCP arthroplasty showed similarly diminished odds, with ORs ranging from 0.16 (95% CI, 0.08–0.30) in the Asian/PI cohort to 0.63 (95% CI, 0.46–0.89) in the Black or African American cohort). Similarly, the rates of MCP arthrodesis were diminished across each individual cohort. Although the odds for CMC arthrodesis, IP arthroplasty, and IP arthrodesis were generally lower in minority populations, no statistically significant difference was observed for CMC arthrodesis, IP arthroplasty, or IP arthrodesis rates between Hispanic patients and the control cohort after matching.

Discussion

Hand osteoarthritis is a highly prevalent condition, affecting up to 80% of the geriatric population. Although most cases can be managed conservatively, progressive pain and deformity may result in reduced strength and functional impairment. This decline in function can, in turn, result in downstream consequences such as loss of income from paid employment and increased dependence on family members for unpaid domestic responsibilities.23 In this study, we evaluated a large sample of patients with hand osteoarthritis. After performing matched analyses controlling for demographic factors, medical comorbidities, and socioeconomic variables, we identified lower rates of corticosteroid injection and surgical intervention among several underrepresented groups.

Disparities in the medical management of osteoarthritis have been studied in detail over the last 20 years. In large part, these have been focused on the evaluation of hip and knee osteoarthritis with relatively little attention paid to the hand.24, 25, 26 In 2018, Abbate et al27 conducted a retrospective analysis of over 1,000 patients with knee osteoarthritis across three health care organizations in North Carolina. They found no statistically significant difference in the use of intra-articular cortisone injections between non-Hispanic White patients and minority patients.27 In a retrospective study involving 105,873 patients with hip or knee osteoarthritis, Wu et al28 investigated disparities in diagnostic work-up, imaging, and treatment. After matching for diagnosis, biological sex, race, drug use, ethnicity, and insurance payer, they found that both African American and Asian patients were less likely to receive intra-articular knee cortisone injections, with ORs of 0.94 (95% CI, 0.89–0.98) and 0.83 (95% CI, 0.71–0.97), respectively. No significant difference was observed among Hispanic American patients. In our study, we found consistently lower odds of receiving cortisone injections for hand osteoarthritis across all minority demographics, with ORs ranging from 0.50 (95% CI, 0.48–0.53) in Asian/Pacific Islander patients to 0.55 (95% CI, 0.53–0.57) in Hispanic patients. This occurred despite relatively comparable overall health care utilization, with an average of 21.4 health care encounters post-diagnosis in the non-Hispanic White cohort compared with 21.3 encounters in the minority cohort (P < .05). Although this difference was statistically significant, we posit that it is not clinically meaningful and reflects comparable exposure to health care providers after diagnosis with hand osteoarthritis. Of note, these data do not reflect the interplay between health care exposure and insurance subtype, given the absence of insurance information present in the TriNetX database. These findings highlight a potential barrier to interventional care that may contribute to unnecessary pain, functional decline, and financial hardship for affected patients.

Unequal access to joint arthroplasty has been well-documented across multiple orthopedic subspecialties. However, work investigating disparities within the realm of hand arthroplasty remains sparse. There has been a wealth of literature over the last 20 years highlighting the underutilization of both total hip and total knee arthroplasties in minority demographics, particularly as they affect African Americans and Hispanic Americans.17,29, 30, 31 In addition, with regard to the upper extremity arthroplasty, a 2023 meta-analysis by Zareef et al32 assessed the utilization and outcomes across 28 manuscripts published over a span of nearly 25 years. Among the 18 studies that investigated health care utilization of anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty, reverse total shoulder arthroplasty, and/or shoulder hemiarthroplasty across various racial and ethnic groups, the authors identified consistent underutilization among minority populations, although the gap in utilization has improved over time. To our knowledge, Johnson et al22 have published the only study to date evaluating the influence of race on the management of hand osteoarthritis. In their analysis of 270 patients who presented to a community-based health system between December 2014 and February 2023 for the treatment of CMC osteoarthritis, they found that non-White patients were less than twice as likely to undergo CMC arthroplasties relative to White patients. In contrast, our own analysis demonstrated that all minority demographic groups were less likely to undergo CMC or MCP arthroplasties compared with White patients despite similar exposure to the health care system. Additionally, Asian and African American patients were significantly less likely to receive IP arthroplasties. Although Hispanic patients also showed a trend toward lower rates of IP arthroplasty, this finding did not reach statistical significance (OR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.61–1.13). Our findings contribute to the growing body of evidence indicating that well-documented racial and ethnic disparities in surgical utilization for hip, knee, and shoulder osteoarthritis also extend to the domain of hand surgery.

This study is not without limitations. As with all database studies, our analysis relies on the accuracy of diagnostic and procedural codes, which cannot be verified on an individual case basis. Consequently, miscoding or billing errors may inadvertently affect the quality of the data. Additionally, our ability to assess more nuanced aspects of patient care is limited. These include delays in presentation, care received at institutions not participating in the TriNetX network, and the preoperative radiographic burden of disease. Moreover, surgeon race and ethnicity could not be assessed within the confines of the TriNetX database, precluding evaluation of the potential influence these factors may have on a patient’s progression to surgical intervention. As other studies have found associations between outcomes and patient–surgeon ethnic concordance, this may be an avenue for future research.33 We are also unable to evaluate preoperative function, pain levels, or patient-reported outcomes following the intervention. These limitations are inherent to large, database-driven studies. Most notably, although we controlled for medical comorbidities that may contribute to disparities in care, we were unable to account for key socioeconomic variables such as insurance status or social/area deprivation indices. Although we attempted to mitigate this by evaluating overall health care exposure postdiagnosis, differences in insurance coverage and related access to care may therefore represent unmeasured confounders and could contribute to the disparities observed in this study. Future work that incorporates socioeconomic data will be essential to more fully characterize and address the drivers of inequity in elective hand surgery. Although single-institution retrospective or prospective studies may better capture these parameters, they do so at the cost of the large sample size afforded by TriNetX.

Using a large, multi-institutional database, we identified nearly 900,000 patients with degenerative joint disease of the hand. Our analysis revealed underutilization of corticosteroid injections across all evaluated minority demographics, with minority groups collectively receiving approximately half as many injections as non-Hispanic White patients. We also observed a widespread decrease in the utilization of surgical interventions, including CMC, MCP, and IP joint arthroplasties, and this trend remained consistent across both unmatched and matched analyses. Although the specific etiology of these treatment disparities lies beyond the scope of a database-driven study, the stark differences observed underscore the need for further research into potential barriers to equitable care, including those related to insurance, culture, access, and systemic factors. Future investigations may help identify modifiable drivers of inequity in elective hand procedures and evaluate strategies to improve access and outcomes for minority populations.
