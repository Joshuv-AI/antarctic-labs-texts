Impact of Combined Frailty and Sarcopenia on Postoperative Outcomes and Survival in Elderly Gastric Cancer Patients Undergoing Gastrectomy
Authors: Eunju Lee, Mira Yoo, Dongjae Jeon, So Hyun Kang, Tae-Han Kim, Sang-Hoon Ahn, Yun-Suhk Suh, Do Joong Park, Hyung-Ho Kim, Young Suk Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeFrailty and sarcopenia are associated with adverse postoperative outcomes in elderly patients undergoing gastrectomy for gastric cancer. However, large-scale evidence remains limited. This study aimed to investigate the associations of frailty and sarcopenia with postoperative outcomes and long-term survival in this population.Materials and MethodsThis retrospective study included patients aged ≥70 years who underwent gastrectomy for gastric cancer between December 2011 and May 2021 and had preoperative comprehensive geriatric assessment and abdominal computed tomography. Frailty was defined as a multidimensional frailty score >6, and sarcopenia was defined as the L3 skeletal muscle index in the lowest tertile for each sex. The patients were categorized as non-frail/non-sarcopenic (NF/NS), frail or sarcopenic (F or S), or frail and sarcopenic (F/S).ResultsA total of 508 patients (mean age, 77.6±4.6 years; 64.0% male) were analyzed. Eighty-two patients (16.1%) experienced Clavien-Dindo grade ≥II complications within 30 days. In multivariable logistic regression analysis, the F/S group was an independent predictor of postoperative complications (odds ratio, 2.73; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.07–6.98; P=0.036). In multivariable Cox regression analysis, both the F or S and F/S groups showed significantly worse overall survival (hazard ratio [HR], 2.17 and 3.11, respectively; both P<0.001). The F/S group showed significantly worse cancer-specific survival (HR, 2.46; 95% CI, 1.02–5.93; P=0.046).ConclusionsCombined frailty and sarcopenia were independently associated with worse postoperative outcomes and survival in elderly patients with gastric cancer. Either condition alone was also associated with worse overall survival, suggesting a dose-response relationship and highlighting the importance of comprehensive geriatric assessment.

INTRODUCTION

South Korea has one of the highest incidence rates of gastric cancer worldwide [12] and has entered a super-aged society as of 2025, with individuals aged ≥65 years accounting for over 20% of the population [3]. The current Korean guidelines for resectable gastric cancer primarily recommend upfront surgery [4]. According to a nationwide survey conducted by the Korean Gastric Cancer Association, the proportion of elderly patients undergoing gastrectomy for gastric cancer has increased substantially in recent years, with those aged ≥70 years accounting for >30% of all surgical cases [5]. In this context, optimizing surgical decision-making and accurately predicting outcomes in older patients have become increasingly important in clinical practice.

Frailty and sarcopenia are recognized risk factors for worse postoperative outcomes, including increased complication rates, prolonged hospital stays, and higher mortality [678]. Frailty is defined as an age-related multidimensional syndrome of physiological decline characterized by marked vulnerability to adverse health outcomes, whereas sarcopenia is defined as the loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength that occurs with advancing age [9101112]. Frailty in older patients often presents with symptoms such as weakness, fatigue, and medical complexity, resulting in reduced tolerance to surgical or medical interventions [9].

As more elderly patients undergo surgical treatment for gastric cancer, the importance of preoperative geriatric assessment has become increasingly evident. The prevalence of frailty in geriatric surgical patients is substantially higher than in the general community-dwelling older population [8]. These assessments play a key role in guiding surgical decision-making and in predicting postoperative outcomes. Both frailty and sarcopenia have been individually associated with adverse postoperative outcomes, including complications, prolonged hospital stay, and mortality [713]. However, although the impact of each condition alone has been well documented, limited evidence exists regarding clinical outcomes in patients presenting with only one condition, either frailty without sarcopenia or sarcopenia without frailty.

Despite the growing interest in preoperative geriatric assessments, large-scale studies that comprehensively evaluate the combined role of frailty and sarcopenia in predicting postoperative outcomes in elderly patients undergoing gastrectomy for gastric cancer remain limited. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the impact of frailty and sarcopenia, using multidimensional validated tools, on short-term postoperative outcomes and long-term survival in elderly patients undergoing gastrectomy for gastric cancer.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

A retrospective review was conducted of patients aged ≥70 years who underwent curative-intent gastrectomy for gastric cancer between December 2011 and May 2021 at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital. This study included patients who underwent preoperative comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) and abdominopelvic computed tomography (CT) within 6 weeks before surgery.

Patients who did not undergo distal, total, proximal, or pylorus-preserving gastrectomy were excluded. Patients undergoing combined resection of other organs, those with distant metastasis, double primary cancers, or pathological T4b disease were also excluded.

Frailty and sarcopenia

Frailty was assessed using the multidimensional frailty score (MFS) derived from preoperative CGA. The MFS comprises several variables, including malignancy status, Charlson Comorbidity Index, serum albumin level, activities of daily living, instrumental activities of daily living, dementia, delirium risk, Mini Nutritional Assessment, and mid-arm circumference [8]. Frailty was defined as MFS >6, a disease-specific threshold optimized for elderly patients undergoing gastrectomy. Although the original MFS was validated using a cutoff >5 in the general elective surgical population [8], a previous study demonstrated that this conventional threshold lacked prognostic significance in elderly patients with gastric cancer, whereas MFS >6 was significantly associated with 1-year mortality [14]. Therefore, MFS >6 was adopted as the cutoff for frailty in this study.

Sarcopenia was assessed using the L3 skeletal muscle index (SMI) derived from preoperative abdominopelvic CT scans [15]. A single axial CT slice at the level of the third lumbar vertebra (L3) was used for analysis. Image segmentation and quantification were performed using AsanJ-Morphometry™ software (Asan Image Metrics, Seoul, Korea; http://datasharing.aim-aicro.com/morphometry), which automatically calculated pixel count and cross-sectional area (CSA, cm2) of skeletal muscle, including the psoas muscle. Skeletal muscle was identified within a Hounsfield unit (HU) threshold range of −29 to +150 HU [16]. The criteria for sarcopenia were applied separately to men and women, and the lowest tertile of SMI for each sex was defined as sarcopenia. This approach differs from commonly used international criteria such as European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP)2/Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2019 [1112]; however, conventional cutoffs resulted in a low prevalence of sarcopenia in our cohort, limiting the statistical power. Therefore, we adopted a cohort-specific, tertile-based definition.

Subgroup

Patients were initially classified into 4 groups based on frailty and sarcopenia status: frail/sarcopenic, frail/non-sarcopenic, non-frail/sarcopenic, and non-frail/non-sarcopenic. The frail/non-sarcopenic and non-frail/sarcopenic groups were then combined, resulting in 3 final groups: non-frail/non-sarcopenic (NF/NS), frail or sarcopenic (F or S), and frail and sarcopenic (F/S).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Python version 3.11. Categorical variables were analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test, whereas continuous variables were analyzed using Student’s t-test. Categorical variables are presented as numbers (%), while continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the date of surgery to the date of death from any cause or last follow-up. Cancer-specific survival (CSS) was defined as the time from the date of surgery to the date of death attributable to gastric cancer; patients who died from other causes were censored at the date of death. Mortality data were obtained from the Ministry of Data and Statistics of the Republic of Korea. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to identify the independent prognostic factors for OS and CSS. In regression analyses, the NF/NS group was used as the reference group. Variables with P<0.05 in univariate analysis, as well as clinically relevant variables considered potentially influential, were included in the multivariable model.

To evaluate whether the prognostic effect of frailty-sarcopenia status differed by tumor stage, an interaction test between tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage and frailty-sarcopenia status was performed for OS. If a statistically significant interaction was observed (P<0.05), subgroup analysis was planned; otherwise, a stage-stratified univariate Cox regression analysis was performed within each stage stratum. For stage-stratified survival analyses, pairwise log-rank tests with Bonferroni correction were performed to identify specific group differences, with an adjusted significance level of P<0.017 (0.05/3). A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (IRB number: B-2202-737-104).

Baseline clinicopathological characteristics

Patients were classified into 3 groups: non-frail/non-sarcopenic (NF/NS), frail or sarcopenic (F or S), and frail and sarcopenic (F/S). Values are presented as mean ± standard deviation or number (%).

BMI = body mass index; ASA = American Society of Anesthesiologists; TNM = tumor, node, metastasis.

Mean age differed significantly among the groups: NF/NS group, 76.7±4.2 years; F or S group, 77.8±4.5 years; and F/S group, 81.2±5.0 years (P<0.001). The proportion of male patients also differed significantly among the groups, being highest in the NF/NS group (69.5%), followed by the F/S group (59.3%), and the F or S group (56.6%; P=0.015). Body mass index (BMI) also differed significantly among groups, with the highest value in the NF/NS group (25.3±2.6 kg/m2), followed by the F or S group (22.4±3.1 kg/m2) and the F/S group (20.2±2.2 kg/m2) (P<0.001).

Regarding type of gastrectomy, distal gastrectomy was most common across all groups (75.3% in NF/NS, 71.4% in F or S, and 75.9% in F/S). Billroth II was the most frequently used reconstruction method in all the groups. The proportions of patients who underwent Billroth II reconstruction were 36.6% in the NF/NS group, 40.0% in the F or S group, and 44.4% in the F/S group.

Pathological characteristics

Tumor size was significantly larger in the F/S group (6.0±3.6 cm) than in the F or S group (4.1±2.9 cm) and the NF/NS group (3.5±2.4 cm) (P<0.001; Table 1). There were no significant differences in proximal resection margin (PRM) or distal resection margin (DRM) among the three groups (P=0.115 and P=0.556, respectively).

The number of retrieved lymph nodes was similar across groups (NF/NS, 51.8±19.0; F or S, 54.9±21.7; F/S, 54.9±20.5) (P=0.227). However, the number of positive lymph nodes was significantly higher in the F/S group (5.3±11.0) than in the F or S group (3.2±7.2) and the NF/NS group (1.7±5.2) (P<0.001).

TNM stage was significantly associated with frailty and sarcopenia status (P<0.001). Stage I disease was most common in the NF/NS group (71.0%), decreasing to 61.7% in the F or S group and 46.3% in the F/S group. In contrast, stage III disease was most common in the F/S group (40.7%), decreasing to 22.3% in the F or S group and 12.9% in the NF/NS group.

Operative and perioperative outcomes

There was no significant difference in operation time among the 3 groups (NF/NS, 181.0±65.2 minutes; F or S, 180.9±61.2 minutes; F/S, 178.6±69.4 minutes; P=0.968) (Table 2). However, the total length of hospital stay was significantly longer in the F/S group (13.3±12.9 days), compared with 8.9±7.0 days in the F or S group and 8.5±7.8 days in the NF/NS group (P<0.001). Postoperative hospital stay was also prolonged in the F/S group (9.7±10.9 days), compared with the F or S group (6.7±5.6 days) and the NF/NS group (6.9±7.5 days) (P=0.023).

Postoperative complications within 30 days

A total of 82 patients (16.1%) experienced postoperative complications of Clavien-Dindo grade ≥II within 30 days (Table 2). The overall postoperative complication rate (Clavien-Dindo grade I or higher) was significantly higher in the F/S group (35.2%) than in the NF/NS group (25.8%) and the F or S group (18.3%) (P=0.026). When limited to grade ≥II complications, the rate remained highest in the F/S group (27.8%), followed by the NF/NS group (16.1%) and the F or S group (12.6%) (P=0.029). The distribution of the highest Clavien-Dindo grade also differed significantly among the groups (P=0.01), with grade IIIa or higher complications occurring more frequently in the F/S group (11.1%) than in the NF/NS group (7.6%) and F or S group (2.8%).

Although not statistically significant, systemic complications were observed more frequently in the F/S group (24.1%) than in the NF/NS group (15.8%) and the F or S group (13.7%) (P=0.196). Similarly, local complications occurred in 9.3% of patients in the F/S group, 7.2% in the NF/NS group, and 4.0% in the F or S group (P=0.229).

Independent predictors of early postoperative complications

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, several factors were independently associated with postoperative complications of Clavien-Dindo grade II or higher within 30 days (Table 3). Male sex was associated with a higher risk (odds ratio [OR], 2.82; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.51–5.28; P=0.001), while higher BMI (OR, 1.13; 95% CI, 1.03–1.24; P=0.011) and TNM stage III (OR, 2.47; 95% CI, 1.31–4.68; P=0.005) were associated with increased risk. The F/S group was identified as a significant independent predictor, with an odds ratio of 2.73 (95% CI, 1.07–6.98, P=0.036) compared with the NF/NS group. Other factors including age, type of gastrectomy, and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification were not statistically significant in the multivariable model.

Survival

OS

OS outcomes were analyzed according to combined frailty and sarcopenia status (Fig. 1A). The median survival time (MST) was not reached in the NF/NS group, whereas it was 7.07 years in the F or S group and significantly shorter at 4.32 years in the F/S group. Five-year survival rates in the NF/NS, F or S, and F/S groups were 84.6%, 68.2%, and 44.8%, respectively.

In multivariable Cox regression analysis adjusted for age, sex, BMI, ASA classification, and pathologic TNM stage, the F or S group had a significantly higher risk of death compared with the NF/NS group (hazard ratio [HR], 2.17; 95% CI, 1.40–3.39; P<0.001), while the F/S group had the highest risk (HR, 3.11; 95% CI, 1.71–5.65; P<0.001) (Table 4). These findings suggest a dose-response relationship, with combined frailty and sarcopenia associated with the highest risk and either condition alone associated with an intermediate risk.

In Stage I, both the F or S group (P=0.005) and the F/S group (P=0.002) showed significantly worse OS than the NF/NS group, whereas no significant difference was observed between the F or S group and the F/S group (P=0.344) (Fig. 1B). No significant differences were observed between the F or S group and the NF/NS group (P=0.684), or between the F or S group and the F/S group (P=0.021) based on the Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold (Fig. 1C). In Stage III, both the F or S group (P=0.007) and the F/S group (P=0.001) showed significantly worse OS than the NF/NS group, whereas no significant difference was observed between the F or S group and the F/S group (P=0.425) (Fig. 1D).

CSS

CSS outcomes were also analyzed according to combined frailty and sarcopenia status (Fig. 2A). The Kaplan–Meier curve demonstrated a significant difference in CSS among the three groups (log-rank P<0.001), and pairwise log-rank tests with Bonferroni correction confirmed that all three comparisons showed significant differences (NF/NS vs. F or S: P=0.002; NF/NS vs. F/S: P<0.001; F or S vs. F/S: P=0.002) (Fig. 2A). In multivariable Cox regression analysis, the F/S group showed significantly worse CSS compared with the NF/NS group (HR, 2.46; 95% CI, 1.02–5.93; P=0.046), while the F or S group did not reach statistical significance (HR, 1.77; 95% CI, 0.85–3.67; P=0.127) (Table 5). Stage-stratified CSS analysis was performed only for Stage III owing to an insufficient number of cancer-specific deaths in stages I and II (n=8 each). In Stage III, the F/S group showed significantly worse CSS compared with the NF/NS group (HR, 2.67; 95% CI, 1.23–5.77; P=0.013), while the F or S group did not reach statistical significance (HR, 1.91; 95% CI, 0.93–3.91; P=0.077) (Supplementary Table 2).

Subgroup analyses by frailty and sarcopenia status

When analyzed separately, frailty was strongly associated with poorer OS (Supplementary Fig. 1). Frail patients had a shorter MST (5.16 years) and lower 5-year OS rate (52.2%) compared with non-frail patients (MST not reached; 5-year OS rate 81.5%; HR, 2.88; 95% CI, 2.06–4.01; P<0.001). Similarly, sarcopenia was also associated with worse OS: sarcopenic patients (n=163) had an MST of 7.03 years, whereas MST was not reached in non-sarcopenic patients (n=345), with 5-year OS rates of 64.2% and 79.7%, respectively (HR, 1.90; 95% CI, 1.37–2.64; P<0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

DISCUSSION

Consistent with previous studies, this study observed that elderly gastric cancer patients with coexisting frailty and sarcopenia had significantly worse postoperative morbidity than those with either condition alone or neither condition [13]. Concurrent frailty and sarcopenia were significantly associated with postoperative complications and long-term survival. Importantly, patients with either frailty or sarcopenia alone showed worse OS than those with neither condition but better survival than those with both conditions, suggesting a dose-response relationship. These findings highlight the importance of establishing appropriate treatment plans based on the differential prognosis among patients with both conditions, either condition alone, or neither condition. Compared with the NF/NS group, the F/S group showed a higher hazard ratio for OS (HR, 3.11) than for CSS (HR, 2.46), suggesting that frailty and sarcopenia may be associated with increased non-cancer mortality, potentially reflecting vulnerability to cardiovascular events, infections, or other competing risks common in the elderly.

When frailty and sarcopenia were comprehensively assessed from multiple perspectives, including geriatric variables and CT-based measures of skeletal muscle mass, the prognostic risk was lower in patients with either condition alone than in those with both conditions. This differential risk profile may provide practical value in clinical decision-making, suggesting that patients with combined frailty and sarcopenia should be prioritized for targeted preoperative interventions. Evaluating frailty and sarcopenia from complementary perspectives may allow for more accurate assessment and prediction of clinical outcomes in older adults [1718].

Several methods have been used to define frailty and sarcopenia. In our study, frailty was assessed using the MFS, with a cutoff >6. Sarcopenia was defined based on the L3 SMI measured on abdominopelvic CT, with the lowest sex-specific tertile classified as sarcopenia. We adopted this cohort-specific approach for several reasons. First, validated cutoff values for CT-derived L3 SMI in surgical oncology populations have not been firmly established by the AWGS or the EWGSOP. EWGSOP2 defines sarcopenia as low muscle strength, low muscle quantity or quality, and low physical performance [11]. Similarly, the AWGS 2019 defines sarcopenia as age-related loss of muscle mass plus low muscle strength and/or low physical performance, with muscle mass measured using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) or bioimpedance, not CT [12]. These criteria were developed using DXA-measured appendicular skeletal muscle mass combined with functional assessments such as handgrip strength and gait speed, which differ from the CT-based assessments commonly used in surgical oncology settings. Moreover, universal cutoff values may not be generalizable across different cancer types, and cancer-specific definitions of sarcopenia remain to be established. Second, when applying the conventional EWGSOP2 criteria based on muscle strength, muscle quantity or quality, and physical performance, the proportion of patients classified as sarcopenic was too small for meaningful analysis; therefore, we adopted a tertile-based definition. Third, previous studies reported that this method effectively differentiates prognoses among groups, supporting its usefulness in patients with gastric cancer undergoing gastrectomy [19]. Nevertheless, this cohort-specific definition may limit direct comparability with studies that use standardized criteria. Among the various available assessment methods, further studies are warranted to determine the approach that most accurately predicts outcomes related to frailty and sarcopenia.

Although the ASA physical status score is a widely used indicator of perioperative risk, it was not a significant predictor of early postoperative complications in the multivariable logistic regression analysis. This finding may be explained by the collinearity with frailty measures, as both the ASA score and MFS capture the overlapping domains of comorbidity and functional status. Additionally, the ASA classification may have limited discriminatory power in this elderly cohort, as most patients were classified as ASA II or III. The MFS, which incorporates multiple geriatric assessment domains, including comorbidity, nutritional status, and functional capacity, may more comprehensively capture the physiological reserve relevant to postoperative outcomes in elderly patients.

These findings are particularly relevant in East Asia, where gastric cancer is highly prevalent and the population is rapidly aging. Given differences in treatment strategies between regions, with upfront surgery commonly performed in East Asia and neoadjuvant chemotherapy more frequently used in Western countries, a comprehensive preoperative assessment including frailty and sarcopenia evaluation is particularly important for guiding optimal treatment decisions. Based on this assessment, patients who are not immediately suitable for upfront surgery may benefit from multimodal prehabilitation and nutritional support to improve their condition and enhance surgical feasibility [202122]. In patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy, modifiable risk factors can be addressed during this period, thereby improving the feasibility and safety of subsequent surgery. Identifying such modifiable risk factors through combined frailty and sarcopenia assessment and tailoring the intensity and duration of prehabilitation programs may represent a critical step in optimizing outcomes for elderly gastric cancer patients.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design may have introduced inherent selection and information bias. Second, given the observational nature of this study, our findings suggest an association between combined frailty/sarcopenia and outcomes rather than a causal relationship. Potential confounding factors, such as the completion of adjuvant chemotherapy or prehabilitation interventions, were not fully accounted for and may have influenced survival outcomes. Third, the stage-stratified cancer-specific survival analysis was limited to Stage III owing to the small number of cancer-specific deaths in stages I (n=8) and II (n=8), which precluded reliable estimation in earlier stages. Fourth, this study was conducted at a single high-volume center, which may have limited the generalizability of the findings. Fifth, only a small proportion of frail or sarcopenic patients in this cohort received structured prehabilitation, postoperative rehabilitation, or nutritional support, limiting our ability to evaluate the effect of such interventions. Finally, the definitions of frailty and sarcopenia used in this study, although based on previously used approaches, may differ from those used in other studies, potentially affecting the comparability of the results.

In conclusion, the coexistence of frailty and sarcopenia is associated with worse postoperative outcomes and OS in elderly patients undergoing gastrectomy for gastric cancer. Even when only one of these conditions was present, OS remained worse than in patients with neither condition, suggesting a dose-response relationship. Assessment based on frailty or sarcopenia alone may not fully capture prognostic heterogeneity. Comprehensive assessment incorporating multiple dimensions may provide more refined prognostic information compared with single-domain evaluation and may help refine individualized treatment planning. Early recognition of high-risk patients and implementation of tailored preoperative interventions should be considered important components of surgical oncology practice for geriatric gastric cancer patients. Future large-scale, prospective, multicenter studies are needed to determine the effectiveness of prehabilitation and nutritional interventions in patients identified as high-risk through comprehensive frailty and sarcopenia assessment, and to establish standardized protocols that can be broadly implemented across different healthcare settings.
