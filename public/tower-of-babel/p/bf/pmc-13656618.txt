Diagnostic accuracy of stool Xpert MTB/RIF assay for adult pulmonary tuberculosis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Kaiyong Wang, Yajing Su, Yupei Dai, Guohang Shen, Yang Chen, Qili Feng, Xue Lin, Di Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe etiological diagnosis of adult pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) mainly relies on sputum or other respiratory specimens. However, some suspected patients cannot provide adequate sputum, limiting conventional diagnostic methods. Stool is an easily obtainable, non-invasive specimen and, combined with nucleic acid amplification techniques, may offer a new adjunct approach for rapid PTB diagnosis.ObjectiveTo systematically evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of stool Xpert MTB/RIF for adult PTB.MethodsPubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Library were searched through April 20, 2026. Study quality was assessed using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) tool. A random-effects model was applied to pool diagnostic accuracy estimates, evaluating stool Xpert against microbiological reference standards, including mycobacterial culture or Xpert testing on conventional specimens.ResultsThirteen studies involving 3,033 adult patients were included. Stool processing and testing procedures varied considerably across studies, particularly in reagent types and pre-treatment steps such as homogenization and filtration. Against microbiological reference standards, pooled sensitivity and specificity were 84% (95% CI: 77%–89%) and 96% (95% CI: 90%–99%), respectively. Sensitivity was 84% in both people living with HIV(PLHIV) and uninfected populations, whereas specificity was higher in uninfected individuals (99%, 95% CI: 89%–100%) than in PLHIV patients (91%, 95% CI: 78%–97%). Significant heterogeneity was observed. Meta-regression by region and reference standard showed significant sensitivity differences between African and Asian studies and between studies using sputum culture versus other reference standards, suggesting potential sources of heterogeneity. Against a composite clinical reference standard, pooled sensitivity and specificity were 59% (95% CI: 43%–74%) and 100% (95% CI: 94%–100%), respectively.ConclusionStool Xpert MTB/RIF demonstrates high specificity and good sensitivity for diagnosing adult PTB, with comparable sensitivity in PLHIV and uninfected populations. Its non-invasive and convenient nature supports its potential as a rapid adjunct diagnostic tool. However, sensitivity may be influenced by region, sample processing methods, and choice of reference standard. Further standardized prospective studies are needed to define its clinical utility and clarify its role in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-14003-0.Keywords: Xpert MTB/RIF, Adult pulmonary tuberculosis, Stool, Infectious diseases

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the most significant infectious diseases worldwide and continues to pose a persistent threat to public health [1, 2]. According to the World Health Organization, approximately 10.8 million people developed TB globally in 2024, and about 1.25 million individuals died from the disease, indicating that TB remains one of the leading infectious causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide [3, 4]. Although global diagnostic capacity for TB has improved, approximately 8.3 million newly diagnosed and reported TB cases were documented in 2024; however, a considerable proportion of cases still remain undiagnosed, delayed in diagnosis, or unreported [5]. Pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) is the most common and epidemiologically significant clinical form of TB. Mycobacterium tuberculosis primarily affects the lungs and can be transmitted through airborne droplet nuclei generated by coughing, sneezing, or expectoration from infected individuals [6, 7]. Therefore, the early, rapid, and accurate diagnosis of PTB is of great importance for reducing disease transmission, improving patient prognosis, and advancing global TB control objectives.

Currently, the definitive diagnosis of PTB primarily relies on microbiological examinations of sputum or other respiratory specimens, including acid-fast bacilli smear microscopy, mycobacterial culture, and nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) [8]. Among these methods, Xpert MTB/RIF has been recommended by the World Health Organization as one of the initial diagnostic tests because of its rapid turnaround time and relatively high diagnostic performance [9]. However, these diagnostic approaches are highly dependent on the acquisition of qualified respiratory specimens. In clinical practice, certain patient populations, such as elderly individuals, critically ill patients, and patients unable to expectorate sputum, often have difficulty providing adequate sputum samples, thereby limiting the clinical applicability of these methods [10, 11]. In addition, mycobacterium tuberculosis infection may present with a low bacillary burden in some patients, and when accompanied by Human immunodeficiency virus infection, this may further reduce the sensitivity of conventional diagnostic methods [12, 13].

To overcome the challenges associated with obtaining respiratory specimens, increasing attention has been directed toward non-invasive alternative samples. In recent years, stool specimens have attracted considerable interest because of their ease of collection, non-invasive nature, and high patient compliance [14]. Previous studies have demonstrated that Mycobacterium tuberculosis originating from the respiratory tract can enter the gastrointestinal tract through swallowing and subsequently be detected in stool samples [15]. This mechanism provides a theoretical basis for the molecular diagnosis of TB using stool specimens. In previous studies on pediatric tuberculosis, stool-based Xpert testing demonstrated high specificity and moderate sensitivity, suggesting its potential clinical utility in populations from whom respiratory specimens are difficult to obtain [16].

However, compared with pediatric tuberculosis, studies investigating the application of stool Xpert in adult PTB remain relatively limited. Several studies have reported its diagnostic performance; nevertheless, the findings have been highly variable. Significant inconsistencies exist among studies regarding stool sample collection, preprocessing procedures, and testing strategies, which may substantially influence the diagnostic results. In addition, factors such as HIV infection status, geographic variation, and differences in reference standards may further affect diagnostic accuracy. To date, there remains a lack of comprehensive evidence synthesis specifically focused on adult PTB populations to systematically evaluate the diagnostic value of this method.

Therefore, it is necessary to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis to integrate the available evidence, thereby providing a more accurate assessment of the overall diagnostic performance of stool Xpert MTB/RIF for adult PTB and exploring potential sources of heterogeneity and influencing factors. Accordingly, the present study aimed to systematically evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of stool Xpert MTB/RIF for adult pulmonary tuberculosis, in order to provide evidence-based support for its application in clinical practice.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [17] and was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number: CRD420261399012.

Inclusion criteria

This study defined the inclusion criteria based on the PIRT framework for diagnostic accuracy studies, including participants, index test, reference standard, and target condition [18]. Eligible studies were required to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of Xpert MTB/RIF and Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra using stool samples for the diagnosis of adult Pulmonary tuberculosis, and to provide sufficient data to construct a 2 × 2 contingency table, including true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN), and false negatives (FN), or to report information enabling the calculation of diagnostic performance measures such as sensitivity and specificity.

Considering the potential variability in reference standards across studies, we accepted studies using Mycobacterium tuberculosis culture, respiratory specimen Xpert results, or composite clinical reference standards as the reference standard. The study population included adults suspected of or undergoing diagnostic evaluation for PTB, including both PLHIV and non-infected individuals, all of whom underwent stool Xpert testing.

Studies included in the quantitative synthesis were limited to published original research, including prospective, retrospective, and cross-sectional studies, and were required to report or provide sufficient data to extract or calculate diagnostic accuracy measures. Reviews, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, conference abstracts, case reports, commentaries, animal studies, and other non-original studies were excluded from the quantitative analysis; however, relevant reviews and prior meta-analyses were used for background context, reference tracing, and comparison of findings in the discussion. Studies were excluded if diagnostic accuracy data could not be extracted or calculated, if the full text was unavailable, or if they were duplicate publications. In cases of overlapping study populations, only the study with the largest sample size or the most complete data was included.

Search strategy and study selection

This study was conducted in accordance with a pre-specified protocol and predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. A systematic search was performed in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library for studies evaluating stool-based Xpert MTB/RIF and Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra in the diagnosis of pulmonary tuberculosis in adults. The search period covered database inception to April 20, 2026, with no language restrictions applied. To maximize sensitivity and specificity, tailored search strategies were developed and optimized for each database according to their respective indexing systems and search interfaces, ensuring comprehensive retrieval of relevant evidence.

A combination of controlled vocabulary and free-text terms was used. The search strategy was constructed around key concepts including “tuberculosis,” “stool/feces,” “Xpert assay,” and “adults.” Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” were applied to combine and expand search terms, with appropriate adjustments made for each database. Detailed search strategies are provided in Table S1.

Study screening was performed independently by two reviewers. Both reviewers had received training in systematic review and meta-analysis methodology and had backgrounds in tuberculosis-related clinical or diagnostic research; one was a junior-level researcher, and the other was a mid-level researcher with relevant research experience. Before the study selection process, both reviewers received standardized training on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, screening procedures, data extraction form, and quality assessment tool, and a pilot screening of selected articles was conducted to calibrate the screening criteria.

First, titles and abstracts were screened to exclude duplicates, reviews, case reports, conference abstracts, and clearly irrelevant studies. Subsequently, the full texts of potentially eligible articles were assessed, and final study selection was performed according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any disagreements during screening were resolved through discussion; if consensus could not be reached, a third senior researcher made the final decision. This senior researcher had expertise in tuberculosis diagnostic research and systematic review/meta-analysis methodology. To reduce the risk of missing eligible studies, the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were further screened to identify additional potentially eligible studies, thereby improving the completeness and reliability of the evidence base.

Data extraction

Prior to data extraction, the research team developed a standardized and structured data extraction form to ensure consistency and completeness of data collection. All data from eligible studies were independently extracted by two reviewers and entered into a pre-designed Microsoft Excel database. The extracted data were subsequently cross-checked for accuracy and consistency. Any discrepancies were first resolved through discussion and consensus; if disagreement persisted, a third reviewer was consulted to verify the data and make the final decision. The following information was extracted: (1) study characteristics, including first author, publication year, country or region, study design, and study period; (2) participant characteristics, including sample size, age, sex distribution, HIV status, and PTB diagnostic status; (3) test-related information, including stool Xpert MTB/RIF assay method, sample processing procedures (e.g., homogenization, filtration, and reagent type), reference standard used, and testing platform; and (4) outcome data, including TP, FP, TN, and FN required to construct 2 × 2 contingency tables, as well as diagnostic performance indicators such as sensitivity and specificity.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers using the QUADAS-2 tool [19]. This instrument evaluates the risk of bias across four key domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing, while also assessing applicability concerns in the first three domains. Each domain was judged according to the signaling questions recommended by QUADAS-2 and categorized as “low risk,” “high risk,” or “unclear risk.” Studies were classified as “unclear risk” when insufficient information was available to permit a definitive judgment. Quality assessment was performed independently by two reviewers, and the results were cross-checked for consistency. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. If disagreement persisted, a third reviewer was consulted to make the final decision until full consensus was reached.

Statistical analysis

This study performed diagnostic meta-analysis using the Midas module in Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) [20, 21]. Based on extracted TP, FP, FN, and TN, 2 × 2 contingency tables were constructed for each included study. Pooled estimates of sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratio (PLR), negative likelihood ratio (NLR), and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated.

A bivariate random-effects model was applied to jointly pool sensitivity and specificity. A summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curve was generated, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to comprehensively evaluate the overall diagnostic performance of stool Xpert MTB/RIF for adult pulmonary tuberculosis. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using the I² statistic, with I² > 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. In the presence of significant heterogeneity, meta-regression and subgroup analyses were further conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis was performed using a leave-one-out approach to assess the robustness and stability of the pooled results.

Threshold effects were evaluated using Spearman correlation analysis between the logit of sensitivity and the logit of (1 − specificity). A statistically significant threshold effect was considered present when P < 0.05. In addition, RevMan 5.4 software was used to visually present the risk of bias assessment results [22]. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study selection process

A total of 121 potentially relevant records were initially identified through the database search. After removing 8 records clearly identified as review articles, 113 studies remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 65 studies were excluded due to irrelevance to the research topic. Full-text articles of the remaining 48 studies were retrieved and assessed for eligibility. At this stage, 4 review articles, 9 duplicate publications, and 3 studies with unavailable full texts were excluded. After full-text evaluation, an additional 13 studies were excluded due to insufficient or ineligible diagnostic data, and 6 studies were excluded because stool specimens were not used for testing. Ultimately, 13 studies were included in this diagnostic meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

The prevalence of pulmonary tuberculosis based on microbiological reference standards varied considerably across studies, ranging from 6.0% to 74.9%. In contrast, studies using composite clinical reference standards reported relatively higher prevalence rates, ranging from 23.5% to 80.9%.All included participants were adults, with a median or mean age ranging from 26 to 59.6 years. Some studies reported a broader age distribution, with ranges extending from 18 to 88 years, indicating coverage of a wide adult age spectrum. Overall, the sex distribution was relatively balanced across studies.

In terms of the clinical characteristics of the study participants, among the 13 included studies, one study reported previous TB exposure history [23], two studies reported a history of previous tuberculosis disease [24, 30], and three studies reported both previous TB disease history in some participants and previous TB exposure history in others [25–27]. Four studies provided relatively detailed information on antiretroviral therapy status [23, 24, 30, 32]. In total, seven studies reported HIV-related information; of these, two studies included only adults living with HIV [23, 30], whereas the remaining five studies included mixed populations of HIV-infected and HIV-uninfected participants [24, 28, 32, 33, 35].

Sample processing

There were substantial variations in stool sample preprocessing procedures across the 13 included studies, as summarized in Table S2-S4. Overall, the processing methods mainly included simple one-step protocols, OMNIgene-based processing, PBS homogenization, NALC-NaOH decontamination, modified Petroff method, and molecular extraction-based approaches.

Among the included studies, 4 applied a simplified “simple one-step” procedure [23, 25, 26, 30], in which approximately 0.8–1.0 g or 1 g of stool was directly mixed with Xpert sample reagent, Xpert/Ultra reagent, or related decontamination solutions. After vortexing and short incubation or sedimentation, the supernatant was used for testing, with some protocols emphasizing avoidance of centrifugation to minimize mycobacterial loss. Two studies used OMNIgene-SPUTUM–based processing [24, 32], in which samples underwent homogenization, incubation, centrifugation, washing, and resuspension prior to testing with Xpert reagents. Another 2 studies applied PBS homogenization [29, 34], involving sample homogenization, vortexing, settling, or low-speed centrifugation, followed by testing of the supernatant. In addition, 2 studies used the NALC-NaOH decontamination method [27, 31], in which samples were diluted, decontaminated, supplemented with PBS, and centrifuged, with the resulting pellet used for microscopy, culture, and Xpert testing. One study employed a modified Petroff method involving NaOH/KOH decontamination [35], neutralization, and centrifugation prior to analysis. Another study performed DNA extraction followed by IS6110-targeted molecular detection [33]. One study only stated that sample processing followed the manufacturer’s instructions for the Xpert MTB/RIF assay and a standardized hospital protocol, without reporting detailed preprocessing parameters [28].

Risk of bias and applicability assessment

The overall risk of bias and applicability concerns of the included studies are summarized in Fig. 2, and detailed assessments for each study are presented in Figure S1b. Overall, the quality of the index test domain was relatively high and stable across the 13 included studies, with most studies rated as low risk of bias and low concerns regarding applicability. In general, the stool Xpert MTB/RIF testing procedures were clearly described and were conducted in accordance with manufacturer instructions, WHO recommendations, or predefined laboratory protocols. In addition, several studies repeated testing in cases of invalid or indeterminate results, further supporting the overall robustness of the index test domain.

Summary of the risk of bias assessment according to Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies Version 2 (QUADAS-2)

In the flow and timing domain, no studies were judged to be at high risk of bias; most were rated as low or unclear risk. Although many studies collected stool and respiratory specimens within a similar timeframe, some studies had issues such as missing samples, culture contamination, invalid test results, sample storage prior to testing, or incomplete paired testing, leading to a relatively higher proportion of unclear risk in this domain.

Regarding the reference standard domain, substantial variability was observed across studies. Studies using sputum culture, MGIT culture, or composite microbiological reference standards were generally considered at low risk of bias. However, some studies were rated as unclear or high risk due to limited sensitivity of the reference standard, insufficient independence of clinical classification, or inadequate control of misclassification bias.

Across all QUADAS-2 domains, a total of 9 high-risk judgments were identified, mainly concentrated in the patient selection and reference standard domains. Patient selection accounted for 6 high-risk ratings and represented the primary source of bias. Several studies did not enroll consecutively recruited adults with suspected pulmonary tuberculosis under real-world clinical conditions; instead, they used case–control designs or included patients with confirmed tuberculosis, smear- or Xpert-positive sputum samples, healthy controls, or non-tuberculosis controls, introducing potential selection bias. The reference standard domain accounted for 3 high-risk ratings, mainly related to the choice and applicability of the reference standard. These included using sputum GeneXpert or sputum Xpert Ultra as the reference standard instead of sputum culture or composite microbiological reference standards, or combining clinically probable tuberculosis cases with microbiologically confirmed cases within the reference classification, potentially introducing misclassification bias.

Meta-analysis of diagnostic accuracy

When using microbiological reference standards as the comparator, the sensitivity of stool Xpert across the 13 included studies ranged from 60% to 100%, and specificity ranged from 43% to 100% (Fig. 3a). The pooled sensitivity was 84% (95% CI: 77%–89%), and the pooled specificity was 96% (95% CI: 90%–99%). Substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed, with I² values of 91.76% for sensitivity and 95.56% for specificity. The summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curve analysis showed an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.94 (95% CI: 0.91–0.96) for microbiological reference standards (Fig. 3b).

(a, b) Forest plots and summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curves showing the sensitivity and specificity of stool Xpert for diagnosing TB, using microbiological testing as the reference standard; (c, d) Forest plots and SROC curves showing the sensitivity and specificity of stool Xpert for diagnosing TB, using a composite clinical reference standard; CI: confidence interval

When using composite clinical diagnostic criteria as the reference standard (Table S5), 4 studies were included [25, 26, 33, 35]. The sensitivity of stool Xpert ranged from 45% to 86%, while specificity ranged from 98% to 100% (Fig. 3c). The pooled sensitivity was 59% (95% CI: 43%–74%), and the pooled specificity was 100% (95% CI: 94%–100%). In this subgroup, substantial heterogeneity remained for sensitivity (I² = 93.75%), whereas heterogeneity for specificity was low (I² = 27.74%). SROC analysis under the composite clinical reference standard yielded an AUC of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.93–0.97) (Fig. 3d).

Overall, stool Xpert demonstrated consistently high specificity under both reference standards, whereas sensitivity varied considerably across studies and reference standards.

Diagnostic accuracy stratified by HIV status

A subgroup analysis was performed according to HIV infection status. Among the 13 included studies, 7 provided detailed diagnostic accuracy data for stool Xpert in HIV-positive adults [23, 24, 28, 30, 32, 33, 35], while 6 studies reported data for HIV-negative adults [25–27, 31, 33, 34]. All analyses in this subgroup were conducted using microbiological reference standards.

In PLHIV patients, the sensitivity of stool Xpert ranged from 63% to 100%, and specificity ranged from 43% to 99% (Fig. 4a). The pooled sensitivity was 84% (95% CI: 72%–91%), and the pooled specificity was 91% (95% CI: 78%–97%). Substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed, with I² values of 91.63% for sensitivity and 95.61% for specificity. The SROC analysis showed an AUC of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.91–0.95) in HIV-positive patients (Fig. 4b).

(a, b) Forest plots and summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curves showing the sensitivity and specificity of stool Xpert for diagnosing TB in people living with HIV; (c, d) Forest plots and SROC curves showing the sensitivity and specificity of stool Xpert for diagnosing TB in people not living with HIV; CI: confidence interval

In HIV-negative patients, sensitivity ranged from 60% to 91%, and specificity ranged from 77% to 100% (Fig. 4c). The pooled sensitivity was 84% (95% CI: 74%–91%), and the pooled specificity was 99% (95% CI: 89%–100%). In this subgroup, both sensitivity and specificity showed substantial heterogeneity, with I² values of 94.90% and 94.53%, respectively. The SROC analysis indicated an AUC of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.93–0.97) in HIV-negative patients (Fig. 4d).

Overall, the pooled sensitivity of stool Xpert was comparable between PLHIV and HIV-uninfected patients, whereas specificity was higher in HIV-negative individuals.

Subgroup analysis

Using the microbiological reference standard as the comparator, we first performed subgroup analyses of the 13 included studies according to the stool-based molecular testing method. In the six studies that used Xpert MTB/RIF to test stool samples [25, 27, 28, 31, 34, 35], the pooled sensitivity and specificity were 86% (95% CI: 74%–93%) and 99% (95% CI: 82%–100%), respectively, with corresponding I² values of 96.77% and 97.90%; the area under the SROC curve was 0.95 (95% CI: 0.93–0.97) (Figure S2a). In the other seven studies that used Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra to test stool samples [23, 24, 26, 29, 30, 32, 33], the pooled sensitivity and specificity were 82% (95% CI: 75%–88%) and 93% (95% CI: 87%–96%), respectively, with corresponding I² values of 76.29% and 85.73%; the area under the SROC curve was 0.93 (95% CI: 0.91–0.95) (Figure S2b). Overall, both testing methods showed good diagnostic accuracy, particularly high diagnostic specificity, although varying degrees of between-study heterogeneity remained within each subgroup.

In addition, among the 13 included studies, four studies used the same stool processing method, namely the simple one-step method [23, 25, 26, 30]. Therefore, we further performed a subgroup analysis of sensitivity and specificity for this processing method. The sensitivity of the four studies ranged from 60% to 87%, and the specificity ranged from 77% to 100%. The pooled sensitivity was 74% (95% CI: 62%–83%), and the pooled specificity was 95% (95% CI: 81%–99%). Heterogeneity analysis showed that the I² values for sensitivity and specificity in this subgroup were 86.66% and 91.49%, respectively, indicating substantial between-study heterogeneity. SROC analysis showed an area under the curve of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.84–0.90) for this subgroup (Figure S2c). These findings suggest that stool Xpert/Ultra retained high specificity when stool samples were processed using the simple one-step method, although the pooled sensitivity was relatively lower. Meanwhile, the persistent heterogeneity within this subgroup suggests that stool processing methods may influence diagnostic performance, but they do not fully explain the between-study differences.

Meta-regression analysis

Given the substantial heterogeneity observed in the overall analysis, univariable meta-regression and subgroup analyses were further conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. First, study region was used as a covariate. Among the 13 included studies, 7 were conducted in Africa [23, 24, 28, 30, 32, 33, 35] and 6 in Asia [25–27, 29, 31, 34]. Meta-regression results indicated a statistically significant association between study region and the sensitivity of stool Xpert (P < 0.05), suggesting differences in sensitivity estimates between African and Asian studies. However, no statistically significant association was observed between region and specificity (Fig. 5a).

(a) Meta-regression analysis stratified by region (Asia vs. Africa); (b) Meta-regression analysis stratified by whether sputum culture was used as the reference standard; (c) Meta-regression analysis based on differences in the use of Xpert MTB/RIF and Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assays

In addition, considering differences in reference standards across studies, a further meta-regression was performed based on whether sputum culture was used as the reference standard. After excluding one study that used a mixed reference standard (including both culture-based and non-culture-based components) [33], 12 studies were included in this analysis, of which 6 used sputum culture as the reference standard [24, 27, 29, 31, 32, 34] and 6 used non-culture reference standards [23, 25, 26, 28, 30, 35]. The results showed a significant association between reference standard type and sensitivity (P < 0.001), indicating that the choice of reference standard may influence pooled sensitivity estimates. No statistically significant association was observed between reference standard type and specificity (Fig. 5b).

In addition, we used the testing method as a covariate to compare the effect of Xpert MTB/RIF and Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra on the diagnostic performance of stool samples. Among the 13 included studies, six studies used Xpert MTB/RIF, whereas seven studies used Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra. Meta-regression showed that the testing method was also statistically associated with sensitivity (*P < 0.05), suggesting that the sensitivity of stool-based testing may differ between the two methods, whereas no statistically significant effect on specificity was observed (Fig. 5c).

Furthermore, when restricting the analysis to studies using sputum culture as the uniform reference standard (n = 6) and pooling data based on microbiological reference standards, the pooled sensitivity and specificity were 89% (95% CI: 86%–92%) and 97% (95% CI: 88%–99%), respectively. Heterogeneity was reduced for sensitivity (I² = 0%) but remained moderate for specificity (I² = 54.92%) (Figure S3-1). Compared with the overall pooled analysis, this subgroup analysis showed increased sensitivity and markedly reduced heterogeneity, further supporting the findings of the meta-regression. Overall, regional differences, reference standard type, and testing method may be potential sources of heterogeneity in this study.

Sensitivity analysis

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the influence of individual studies on the pooled diagnostic performance estimates. As shown in Figure S4, after sequential omission of each study, the pooled sensitivity and specificity remained largely unchanged in the overall analysis as well as in the HIV-positive and HIV-negative subgroups, indicating that the main diagnostic accuracy estimates were robust and stable. Compared with the overall pooled results including all studies, heterogeneity decreased to varying degrees after exclusion of individual studies. The reduction in heterogeneity was more pronounced in the HIV-negative subgroup for sensitivity, whereas only limited changes were observed in the overall analysis and the HIV-positive subgroup. These findings suggest that although certain individual studies may have contributed to between-study heterogeneity, none had a substantial impact on the overall pooled estimates of diagnostic accuracy.

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed using Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test. As shown in Fig. 6, the P values of Deeks’ tests for all analyses were greater than 0.05, indicating no significant evidence of publication bias in the included studies.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the diagnostic value of stool Xpert for adult pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB). Overall, stool Xpert showed high specificity and good overall diagnostic performance, with more stable results when microbiological methods were used as the reference standard. By contrast, when a composite clinical diagnostic standard was used as the reference, the pooled sensitivity decreased, whereas specificity remained high. This may be related to differences in case definitions across reference standards: microbiological reference standards are mainly based on culture or molecular testing results and usually identify cases with clearer microbiological evidence and relatively higher bacillary loads [36]; however, composite clinical diagnostic standards may include some clinically diagnosed cases with low bacillary loads or without definite microbiological evidence, thereby reducing the estimated sensitivity of stool Xpert. Therefore, a positive stool Xpert result has considerable value in supporting the diagnosis of PTB, but a negative result alone is insufficient to exclude the disease and should be interpreted together with clinical manifestations, imaging findings, and microbiological results from pulmonary specimens.

From a clinical application perspective, stool Xpert currently appears to be more suitable as an adjunctive diagnostic tool within the diagnostic pathway for adult PTB, rather than a replacement for standard microbiological methods such as sputum-based testing or culture [37]. Although respiratory specimens remain the primary basis for diagnosing adult PTB [38], in clinical practice, some patients have no sputum, scant sputum, difficulty expectorating, or poor-quality specimens [39], making it difficult to obtain qualified sputum specimens promptly at the initial diagnostic visit. In such situations, stool specimens offer practical advantages, including ease of collection, non-invasiveness, and improved patient acceptability [27, 40]. These features make stool-based testing a feasible alternative diagnostic approach for patients with suspected PTB who cannot provide adequate respiratory samples [25]. Therefore, the clinical value of stool Xpert lies primarily in its complementary role within existing diagnostic algorithms, particularly for patient subgroups in whom high-quality sputum specimens are difficult or impossible to obtain.

This study demonstrated that stool Xpert exhibits high specificity, suggesting that positive results provide strong diagnostic support for pulmonary tuberculosis. In patients with suspected PTB presenting with typical clinical symptoms or radiological findings, a positive stool Xpert result may further strengthen diagnostic confidence [25, 41]. Compared with specificity, the sensitivity of stool Xpert should be interpreted with greater caution. Stool testing mainly relies on Mycobacterium tuberculosis or its DNA that has been swallowed into the gastrointestinal tract; therefore, the results may be influenced by the patient’s bacillary load, gastrointestinal transit, and the complexity of the stool matrix.

This study found that sensitivity varied across different reference standards. When a composite clinical reference standard was used, sensitivity was relatively lower, which may be attributable to the inclusion of microbiologically negative but clinically highly suspected PTB cases. Such patients typically have low mycobacterial burden or intermittent shedding, which reduces the likelihood of detection by molecular assays [42]. In addition, composite clinical definitions may introduce a proportion of non-bacteriologically confirmed cases, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true sensitivity of the test. In contrast, when sputum culture or other microbiological methods were used as the reference standard, included cases were more likely to have definitive bacteriological evidence and higher bacillary loads, resulting in relatively higher sensitivity estimates. In this study, after restricting the analysis to studies using sputum culture as the reference standard, the pooled sensitivity and specificity reached 89% and 97%, respectively, with substantially reduced heterogeneity. This further suggests that the choice of reference standard is a key factor influencing estimates of diagnostic accuracy. However, these findings should still be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of studies in this subgroup analysis.

Therefore, when evaluating the diagnostic performance of stool Xpert, it is important to distinguish between its ability to identify microbiologically confirmed PTB and its ability to detect clinically diagnosed PTB in the overall patient population. The former more directly reflects the intrinsic performance of the assay, whereas the latter better represents real-world clinical practice but is also more susceptible to variations in case composition, disease severity, and diagnostic uncertainty. Future studies should clearly define the composition of reference standards and report diagnostic accuracy separately under different standards to enhance comparability and interpretability.

In the stratified analysis, this study further explored the impact of HIV infection status on diagnostic performance. The results showed that the pooled sensitivity was generally similar between PLHIV and non-infected populations, whereas specificity differed to some extent. Given that patients with HIV infection often present with atypical clinical manifestations, heterogeneous bacillary distribution, and difficulty in obtaining sputum samples [39, 43], stool Xpert may still hold particular clinical value in this population. However, the available evidence remains limited, and the included studies varied in the proportion of PLHIV participants, immune status, antiretroviral therapy status, and reference standards. In addition, no PLHIV participants were reported in the Asian study populations included in this review. Therefore, the optimal application scenarios for stool Xpert among adults living with HIV across different regions remain insufficiently defined.

This study observed substantial between-study heterogeneity, particularly in sensitivity. Meta-regression analysis suggested that regional differences, type of reference standard, and testing methods may be potential sources of heterogeneity. Regional variation may reflect differences across studies in population characteristics, disease burden, bacterial load, stage of presentation, and laboratory conditions. The type of reference standard may influence sensitivity estimates by affecting case definitions and the degree of microbiological confirmation. Meanwhile, differences between Xpert MTB/RIF and Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra in detection limits and result interpretation may also impact the detection rate of MTB in stool samples. Given the limited number of included studies and the potential overlap among study characteristics, these findings should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory analyses.

In addition to region, reference standard, and testing platform, stool sample processing procedures may also have an important impact on test results. The included studies differed substantially in terms of stool amount, homogenization, decontamination, centrifugation, filtration, sedimentation, and the final fraction used for testing. Because the stool matrix is complex and may contain PCR inhibitors and unevenly distributed target DNA [44, 45], different processing procedures may affect bacillary recovery, DNA release, and the efficiency of inhibitor removal, thereby leading to differences in diagnostic performance. In the present study, studies using the simple one-step method showed high specificity, but the pooled sensitivity was relatively low, and substantial heterogeneity remained within this subgroup. This suggests that sample processing methods may influence diagnostic performance, but they do not fully explain the between-study differences. Although some pediatric stool testing studies have supported the value of the simple one-step method in resource-limited and primary healthcare settings [46, 47], evidence regarding stool sample processing procedures in adults remains limited, and is currently insufficient to determine whether any single processing method has a clear advantage. Future studies should compare diagnostic performance while also reporting invalid or error results, processing time, resource requirements, and feasibility for implementation in primary healthcare settings.

The sensitivity analysis demonstrated that sequential exclusion of individual studies did not materially alter the pooled estimates, indicating that the overall findings are relatively robust. Although certain studies contributed to between-study heterogeneity, they did not change the overall direction of the effect. In addition, Deeks’ funnel plot did not suggest significant publication bias. Nevertheless, given that diagnostic meta-analyses are inherently influenced by variations in study design and reference standards, the results should still be interpreted with caution in specific clinical contexts.

This study has several strengths. First, it specifically focuses on the adult PTB population, thereby complementing existing evidence that has largely concentrated on pediatric or selected populations. Second, by distinguishing between different reference standards and incorporating multidimensional subgroup analyses and meta-regression, this study provides a more in-depth methodological exploration of factors influencing diagnostic accuracy. Third, this study systematically summarizes stool sample processing procedures, which helps to explain inter-study variability from a laboratory and technical perspective.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, the number of included studies was relatively limited, particularly those using a composite clinical diagnostic standard, which may affect the stability of the results. Second, substantial differences existed across studies in patient selection and stool sample processing procedures, resulting in considerable overall heterogeneity and potentially affecting the precision and generalizability of the findings. Third, some studies did not adequately report details of sample processing or the implementation of blinding, limiting the assessment of potential sources of bias. Fourth, the reference standards used across the included studies were not entirely consistent, which may have influenced the pooled estimates of diagnostic performance and limited the direct comparability of results between studies. Finally, this study was based on aggregate data, which precluded adjustment at the individual patient level and therefore limited our ability to fully evaluate the effects of factors such as age, immune status, and bacillary load on diagnostic performance.

Conclusion

Stool Xpert demonstrates high specificity and favorable overall diagnostic performance for pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) in adults, particularly serving as a rapid, non-invasive adjunctive diagnostic tool for patients who are unable to provide adequate respiratory specimens. A positive result provides strong supportive evidence for PTB diagnosis. However, sensitivity is influenced by factors such as reference standard, regional differences, and sample processing procedures, and remains limited to some extent; therefore, a negative result cannot reliably exclude the disease. Future well-designed prospective studies with larger sample sizes, standardized reference standards, and harmonized stool processing protocols are warranted to further clarify the clinical utility of stool Xpert across different settings and patient populations.
