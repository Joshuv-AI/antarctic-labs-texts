A non-linear pattern of exercise capacity in heart failure across ejection fraction categories
Authors: Małgorzata Kurpaska, Paweł Krzesiński, Martyna Świerkowska, Katarzyna Piotrowicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to compare exercise capacity (EC) and comorbidity profiles across left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) defined heart failure (HF) categories. From a retrospective, single-centre registry, we analysed 196 individuals with established HF who underwent cardiopulmonary exercise testing and a 6 min walk test (6MWT). EC differed significantly across LVEF categories but not in a linear fashion. The percent of predicted peak oxygen uptake (VO₂) was significantly lower in HF with reduced LVEF (HFrEF, n = 89) than in HF with preserved LVEF (HFpEF, n = 36) and HF with mildly reduced LVEF (HFmrEF, n = 71) (65.9% vs. 76.6% and 76.8%, p < 0.001). Ventilatory inefficiency (VE/VCO₂ slope) was more pronounced in HFrEF than in HFpEF (35.3 vs. 31.7; p = 0.002), while the proportion with VE/VCO₂ slope > 36 did not differ across groups. The achieved workload and 6MWT distance were comparable across groups. Comorbidity profiles diverged meaningfully: HFmrEF had the lowest prevalence of chronic kidney disease (p = 0.009) and type 2 diabetes (p = 0.025). Notably, HFpEF exhibited the highest prevalence of anaemia (p = 0.0013). HFmrEF displays an EC profile closer to HFpEF than to HFrEF, while anaemia emerges as a particularly important comorbidity in HFpEF.

Introduction

Stratifying heart failure (HF) by left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) provides a practical clinical framework, yet it does not fully capture the heterogeneity in exercise capacity (EC), which arises from tightly coupled interactions among the cardiovascular, respiratory, skeletal muscle and endocrine systems1,2. Although the mechanisms underlying reduced EC have been extensively investigated in HF with reduced LVEF (HFrEF) and preserved LVEF (HFpEF)3–6, evidence in HF with mildly reduced LVEF (HFmrEF) remains inconsistent, particularly when interpreted in the context of comorbidity burden, which may substantially modify exercise limitations3,7–9.

Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) offers an objective, integrative assessment of EC in HF10 independent of resting LVEF. Reduced EC is typically anticipated in HFrEF. However, the extent to which CPET-derived functional profiles differ across the full LVEF spectrum and how comorbidities shape these differences remain insufficiently defined.

This study aimed to compare CPET-assessed EC parameters and comorbidity rates in HF patients with preserved, mildly reduced and reduced LVEF.

Methods

The study population was selected from 517 patients evaluated at our centre between February 2022 and March 2024 who were considered for cardiac rehabilitation due to HF exacerbation, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or acute coronary syndrome (ACS) occurring within the previous 56 days. The inclusion criterion was a diagnosis of HF with known LVEF (assessed within the previous 56 days).

The study exclusion criteria were cardiothoracic surgery within the previous 56 days (n = 51) and no CPET performed (n = 42).

We also evaluated the patients’ histories of comorbidities, blood haemoglobin (HGB) levels and Cockroft–Gault formula estimated glomerular filtration rates (eGFRs). Anaemia was defined as HGB levels < 12 g/dl for women and < 13 g/dl for men11. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was defined as an eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73 m2 sustained for at least 3 months.

Echocardiographic records provided data on LVEF and left ventricular diastolic dysfunction or valvular disease. Valvular disease was defined as moderate-to-severe aortic, mitral or tricuspid insufficiency or stenosis.

CPET was conducted on an Ergoselect stationary bicycle (Geratherm Respiratory GmbH; Germany) with a CORTEX gas analyser system (Biophysik GmbH; Germany) and was continued until the patient’s maximum tolerated level. The ramp protocol was individualised to obtain the maximum predicted workload (Wasserman equation1 at minute 10 of the exercise. We evaluated peak-exercise oxygen uptake (peak VO2), percentage of predicted peak VO2 (peak VO2%pred.), exercise workload, minute ventilation-to-carbon dioxide output (VE/VCO2) slope and oxygen pulse (peak VO2-to-heart rate ratio, peak VO2/HR). A moderate-to-severe decrease in EC was defined as a peak VO2 < 70% pred.12; significantly impaired ventilatory efficiency was defined as a VE/VCO2 slope > 36.

We also assessed a 6 min walk test (6MWT) distance and the percentage of the predicted 6MWT distance, with the test repeated within 3 days13.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Military Institute of Medicine - National Research Institute (Resolution 32/24 from June 19, 2024). Informed patients consent statement has been waived by Institutional Review Board of the Military Institute of Medicine - National Research Institute due to retrospective analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Statistica software (version 12, StatSoft, USA). The normality of distribution was verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as means with standard deviations (SDs), while those not normally distributed were presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). For comparisons among the three groups (HFpEF: LVEF ≥ 50%; HFmrEF: LVEF 41%–49%; HFrEF: LVEF ≤ 40%), one-way ANOVA was applied for normally distributed variables, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test, with post-hoc multiple comparisons of mean ranks for all groups. Categorical variables were analysed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. When the overall test was significant, pairwise post-hoc comparisons were additionally performed with the Bonferroni correction. A p-value < 0.05 (two tailed) was considered statistically significant.

Results

The 196 patients with HF included in this study were stratified into three subgroups: HFpEF (n = 36), HFmrEF (n = 71) and HFrEF (n = 89). There were no statistically significant differences in sex or age distribution among these groups.

Patients with HFrEF exhibited lower peak VO2 values and a higher rate of peak VO2 < 70% than those with HFmrEF, as well as the lowest peak VO2%pred. among all EF categories (Table 1; Fig. 1). The VE/VCO2 slope was the steepest in HFrEF (Table 1; Fig. 1). However, there were no intergroup differences in the rate of VE/VCO2 slope > 36, nor in achieved workload and 6MWT distance. In addition, no differences regarding CPET parameters were noted between HFmrEF and HFpEF.

Discussion

Our observations confirm that HF subgroups defined by LVEF differ significantly in EC. Importantly, however, EC did not decline in a strictly proportional manner with decreasing LVEF. Although the groups achieved a comparable exercise workload and did not differ with respect to age or sex, EC was lowest in HFrEF. This was reflected by a peak VO₂ percent predicted approximately 10% points lower in HFrEF than in HFpEF and HFmrEF. Overall, the CPET profiles indicated broadly comparable EC in HFpEF and HFmrEF. In addition, HFpEF exhibited the highest prevalence of anaemia, whereas HFmrEF was associated with a significantly lower prevalence of T2DM and CKD compared with HFrEF.

Different HF phenotypes have also been associated with distinct CPET profiles in prior studies3,4,6,14. In one report, EC was highest in HFpEF, intermediate in HFmrEF and lowest in HFrEF3. By contrast, our data suggest that exercise performance in HFmrEF more closely resembles that observed in HFpEF than in HFrEF.

Reduced EC in HFrEF is biologically plausible given impaired myocardial contractility, lower stroke volume and diminished cardiac output, all of which limit oxygen delivery during exertion2,15,16. In addition, CKD and T2DM, which are both prevalent in HF populations, may further constrain peak VO2 through peripheral mechanisms, including impaired skeletal muscle oxygen extraction and utilisation17,18.

One could expect the highest EC in patients with HFpEF. However, the symptoms in this group may be equally or even more severe than those in other HF phenotypes, as supported by objective findings. The key determinants of reduced peak VO₂ in HFpEF are believed to include chronotropic incompetence and impaired peripheral oxygen diffusion resulting from structural and metabolic abnormalities in skeletal muscle15,16,19. Moreover, HFpEF has been associated with exercise-induced functional mitral regurgitation4 and abnormal left atrial reservoir strain20, which further compromise cardiovascular haemodynamics. By contrast, exercise intolerance in HFmrEF appears to be primarily related to peripheral factors16 and left atrial dysfunction20.

Despite significant differences in peak VO₂, no differences in achieved workload were observed between the subgroups, a finding consistent with previous reports3. This finding may suggest that varying contributions from central factors (cardiac output), ventilatory inefficiency and peripheral mechanisms (oxygen extraction and muscular metabolism) result in similar functional outcomes across HF categories.

A hallmark of HF is ventilatory inefficiency, reflected by an increased VE/VCO₂ slope relative to healthy individuals21. Ventilatory inefficiency is a recognised marker of advanced HF, regardless of EF and the progress of HF treatment9,22. In the context of CPET, patients classified as ventilatory class I are expected to have a relatively favourable prognosis compared with those in higher ventilatory classes (more than 20% of patients in each HF category showed VE/VCO2 slopes > 36). Elevated VE/VCO2 slopes across the LVEF spectrum result from reduced cardiac output, impaired LV relaxation, elevated filling pressures and peripheral muscle dysfunction23. Although HFpEF had the lowest median VE/VCO2 slope among the three groups, all subgroups fell within ventilation class 1 and had similar frequencies of VE/VCO₂ > 36, indicating clinical stability without significant pulmonary congestion.

An increased VE/VCO2 slope arises from augmented dead-space ventilation and ventilation–perfusion (V/Q) mismatch during exercise23. Pulmonary hypoperfusion due to myocardial dysfunction can trigger a pulmonary vagal reflex through excess stimulation of J mechanoreceptors by vascular distension during congestion, leading to hyperventilation and reduced PaCO221. As previously noted, the VE/VCO₂ slope adds valuable prognostic and pathophysiologic insights beyond LVEF. Elevations in VE/VCO₂ slope have been linked to comparable N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide concentrations and echocardiographic markers of increased LV filling pressure (E/e′)23,24. The slope also reflects secondary pulmonary hypertension: Both invasive (mPAP)22 and echocardiographic (PASP)24 pulmonary pressures, as well as pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR)21,22, correlate with VE/VCO₂. In HFrEF, VE/VCO₂ also correlates with PCWP25, whereas in HFpEF, PCWP rises markedly during exercise26 but does not correlate with VE/VCO₂22. Pecchia et al.5 questioned whether the rapid PCWP increase in HFpEF during exertion represents a primary pathophysiologic mechanism or a consequence of diastolic dysfunction and reduced atrial compliance. Interestingly, nitroglycerin administration during exercise lowered PCWP but worsened dyspnea, likely due to interstitial lung fluid shifts and aggravated V/Q mismatch27. Exercise-induced mitral regurgitation has also been proposed as a mechanism of reduced ventilatory efficiency in HF, most frequently in HFrEF (60%), less so in HFmrEF (41%) and least in HFpEF (35%)4.

Comparable COPD rates among subgroups suggest that differences in VE/VCO₂ are not primarily driven by chronic lung disease. Nevertheless, further analyses, including assessment of the VE/VCO₂ Y-intercept, which reflects dynamic changes in dead-space ventilation, are warranted28.

Consistent with previous findings3,7–9, we observed the highest rates of T2DM and CKD in HFrEF patients. Conversely, HFmrEF patients exhibited a significantly lower prevalence of these comorbidities, which likely contributed to their better EC. This underscores the strong impact of comorbidities on CPET indices across HF phenotypes, including both HFpEF and HFmrEF. Our earlier analysis confirmed that T2DM and CKD are independent predictors of reduced VO₂ and an increased VE/VCO₂ slope29. T2DM may impair EC through both myocardial contractile dysfunction and reduced skeletal-muscle oxygen extraction17,18, while CKD contributes via elevated ventilatory cost, V/Q mismatch, mitochondrial dysfunction and autonomic imbalance30.

Interestingly, in our cohort, anaemia was more frequent in HFpEF than in other HF subtypes. The reported prevalence of anaemia in HFpEF ranges from 19% to 68%31, and similar findings of the lowest HGB in HFpEF have been described7,8. Anaemia in HF correlates with poorer quality of life7, higher the New York Heart Association class, elevated NT-proBNP, greater comorbidity burden (CKD, T2DM)8 and increased mortality and hospitalisation risk8,31. Its multifactorial pathogenesis includes chronic blood loss, iron deficiency, inflammation, malnutrition32, cardiorenal mechanisms and reduced erythropoietin synthesis31. Although guidelines recommend intravenous iron therapy primarily for HFrEF2, iron deficiency occurs with a similar frequency across all EF categories33. Importantly, iron deficiency shows a stronger association with low peak VO₂ than anaemia itself, given the role of iron in myoglobin and mitochondrial enzyme function33,34. Data on iron deficiency in HF were not available in our registry; however, such information would have been valuable for interpreting the prevalence of anaemia, particularly in patients with HFpEF.

Anaemia adversely affects cardiac structure and function regardless of EF; it is associated with a 25% increase in LV mass, more pronounced remodelling, plasma volume expansion32 and impaired global longitudinal strain31. It also correlates with preload-dependent markers of diastolic dysfunction (E/A, E/e′) and elevated right-sided pressures (RAP, PASP)35. Additional mechanisms include activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone and sympathetic systems, promoting remodelling and fluid retention32. Diminished iron availability impairs myocardial energy efficiency, which is further exacerbated by hypoxia due to reduced oxygen delivery31. Consequently, lower haemoglobin levels reduce EC—specifically peak VO2, exercise duration and intensity—by limiting arterial oxygen delivery, peripheral extraction and ventilatory efficiency (higher VE/VCO2 slope), even without changes in cardiac output32. The higher VE/VCO₂ slope and lower minute ventilation in anaemic HFpEF patients suggest possible respiratory muscle weakness32. The anaemia-related reduction in arteriovenous O2 difference32 may be especially deleterious in HFpEF, in which compensatory mechanisms for impaired oxygen delivery are blunted4. Therefore, the higher anaemia prevalence in our HFpEF subgroup may partly explain their lack of superiority in EC over HFmrEF.

The 6MWT remains a simple tool for assessing EC in HF, especially in advanced disease, HFrEF and multimorbidity13. Accordingly, we routinely complement CPET with 6MWT in our patients. In this study, 6MWT distance did not differ among HF subgroups, whether expressed in absolute terms, as percent predicted or as the proportion of subjects achieving < 100% predicted distance. A trend towards a higher proportion of patients achieving < 100% predicted distance in HFpEF was noted. Meta-analysis findings align with this pattern, showing shorter 6MWD in HFpEF versus HFrEF6. However, some reports indicate a poor correlation between 6MWT and CPET parameters in elderly HFpEF patients36. The discrepancy likely reflects the multifactorial nature of 6MWT performance, which depends on muscle strength, coordination, nutritional status and overall physical conditioning37 rather than solely on cardiopulmonary function.

Limitations

Certain methodological limitations, including unequal intergroup distribution, stem from the retrospective design of this study. In addition, LVEF classification was based on echocardiography performed during an ACS episode and shortly after PCI, prior to cardiac rehabilitation. Consequently, we cannot exclude that in some patients, left ventricular systolic function improved by the time CPET was conducted, potentially leading to misclassification across LVEF strata and attenuating observed between-group differences. However, we used the last available echocardiography and the median time between calculation of LVEF and CPET was 23 (13–43) days.

Moreover, the study cohort included patients assessed after ACS/PCI alongside individuals with HF of different aetiologies. Such heterogeneity (ischaemic vs. non-ischaemic HF; myocardial infarction vs. no infarction) may translate into distinct pathophysiological mechanisms of exercise limitation across LVEF-defined phenotypes and could therefore confound between-group comparisons of EC.

In addition, the time interval between the index ACS/PCI event and CPET, as well as the timing and intensity of cardiac rehabilitation, was not uniform. This variability may have influenced peak VO2 and VE/VCO2 slope through spontaneous recovery after the acute event and/or training-related improvements, thereby contributing to within-group variability and potentially blunting true interphenotype differences.

Although the overall tests indicated statistically significant differences among the groups for selected variables (peak VO2/HR and coronary artery disease), post-hoc analyses did not demonstrate significant pairwise contrasts. This pattern is consistent with a modest, distributed effect across the three-category comparison rather than strong separation between any two groups; it may also reflect limited statistical power due to sample size and within-group variability.

Our study is based on a retrospective analysis of clinically acquired CPET and echocardiographic data and does not involve machine learning models or automated decision systems. Therefore, it is not directly exposed to adversarial or data-poisoning risks38. Nevertheless, we recognize that data integrity, secure storage, and protection against unauthorized manipulation are essential for any digital health dataset, and these principles are particularly relevant if such physiological data are to be integrated into future AI-driven risk stratification tools.

Conclusions

In summary, CPET distinguished EC across LVEF-defined HF categories, confirming the lowest EC in HFrEF. Importantly, HFmrEF demonstrated a functional profile that was closer to HFpEF than HFrEF, with comparable CPET-derived indices despite differences in resting LVEF. This finding supports the concept that HFmrEF shares key pathophysiological determinants of exercise intolerance with HFpEF, rather than representing a simple intermediate stage along the LVEF continuum.

Among comorbidities, anaemia emerged as a particularly relevant modifier of EC, being most prevalent in HFpEF and potentially explaining why HFpEF did not demonstrate functional superiority over HFmrEF.

Overall, our results suggest that CPET provides clinically meaningful information beyond resting LVEF, enabling a more nuanced characterisation of functional HF phenotypes and highlighting comorbidity-driven mechanisms of exercise limitation.
