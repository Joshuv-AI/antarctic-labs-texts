A new era in surgical oncology: preliminary insights into the hinotori™ surgical robot system’s role in rectal surgery using the double bipolar method
Authors: Hidetoshi Katsuno, Koji Morohara, Tomoyoshi Endo, Yuko Chikaishi, Kenji Kikuchi, Kenichi Nakamura, Kazuhiro Matsuo, Takahiko Higashiguchi, Tetsuya Koide, Tsunekazu Hanai, Zenichi Morise
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe da Vinci™ Surgical System, recognized as the leading surgical robotic platform globally, now faces competition from a growing number of new robotic surgical systems. With the expiration of key patents, innovative entrants have emerged, each offering unique features to address limitations and challenges in minimally invasive surgery. The hinotori™ Surgical Robot System (hinotori), developed in Japan and approved for clinical use in November 2022, represents one such entrant. This study demonstrates initial insights into the application of the hinotori in robot-assisted surgeries for patients with rectal neoplasms.MethodsThe present study, conducted at a single institution, retrospectively reviewed 28 patients with rectal neoplasms treated with the hinotori from November 2022 to March 2024. The surgical technique involved placing five ports, including one for an assistant, and performing either total or tumor-specific mesorectal excision using the double bipolar method (DBM). The DBM uses two bipolar instruments depending on the situation, typically Maryland bipolar forceps on the right and Fenestrated bipolar forceps on the left, to allow precise dissection, hemostasis, and lymph node dissection.ResultsThe study group comprised 28 patients, half of whom were male. The median age was 62 years and the body mass index stood at 22.1 kg/m2. Distribution of clinical stages included eight at stage I, five at stage II, twelve at stage III, and three at stage IV. The majority, 26 patients (92.9%), underwent anterior resection using a double stapling technique. There were no intraoperative complications or conversions to other surgical approaches. The median operative time and cockpit time were 257 and 148 min, respectively. Blood loss was 15 mL. Postoperative complications were infrequent, with only one patient experiencing transient ileus. A median of 18 lymph nodes was retrieved, and no positive surgical margins were identified.ConclusionsThe introduction of the hinotori for rectal neoplasms appears to be safe and feasible, particularly when performed by experienced robotic surgeons. The double bipolar method enabled precise dissection and hemostasis, contributing to minimal blood loss and effective lymph node dissection.Keywords: Robot-assisted surgery, Rectal neoplasm, Minimally invasive surgery, Hinotori™ surgical robot system

Introduction

The development of robot-assisted surgery for rectal cancer represents significant progress in the realm of surgical oncology. Over the past decade, the adoption of robotic technologies, particularly the da Vinci™ Surgical System, has markedly changed minimally invasive surgeries. These advancements provide enhanced precision, dexterity, and better visualization, which may contribute to improved clinical outcomes and the effectiveness of surgeries in the confined space of the pelvis [1–3]. However, the emergence of alternative robotic systems, such as the hinotori™ Surgical Robot System (hinotori), prompts continued efforts to improve surgical instruments and methodologies for rectal cancer treatment.

The Medicaroid Corporation (Kobe, Japan), a joint venture between Sysmex Corporation and Kawasaki Heavy Industries, Ltd., has produced this novel Japanese surgical robotic platform. The hinotori initially received regulatory approval from the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency in 2020 for clinical use in urology. Subsequently, in November 2022, it was approved for gastrointestinal surgery and gynecology, leading to its introduction into various surgical fields. Following these approvals, several case reports surfaced [4–6], demonstrating the platform's wide applicability. However, the current version of the hinotori does not yet include a vessel sealing device or a linear stapler among its available instruments, posing a challenge when attempting to achieve results comparable to da Vinci-assisted surgeries.

To overcome this limitation, we adopted the double bipolar method (DBM) in robot-assisted rectal surgery (RRS) [7]. Surgeons typically rely on laparoscopic coagulation shears in conventional laparoscopic surgery. Therefore, using only monopolar scissors in RRS could be impractical, especially when dealing with obese patients, performing lymph node dissection around the root of the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA), and encircling the rectal wall during tumor-specific mesorectal excision (TSME). In contrast, the DBM, as previously reported, is more effective in RRS because it allows for precise tissue handling and vessel sealing without excessive damage. The bipolar current is confined to the tissue between the forceps' jaws, minimizing unintended deep tissue injury, unlike monopolar currents that can penetrate deeper. Additionally, using Maryland bipolar forceps (MBF) in DBM permits swift and efficient dissection, tissue coagulation, and control of smaller blood vessels.

In view of current situation, this study evaluates the role of the hinotori in RRS, employing the DBM to assess its safety, feasibility, and potential benefits. This retrospective study aims to provide insights that may guide future practices and technological advancements in surgical oncology [8].

Materials and methods

Study design

A single-institution retrospective observational study was conducted to evaluate the safety and feasibility of RRS using the hinotori. This study covered the period from November 2022 to March 2024, involving 28 consecutive patients diagnosed with rectal neoplasms. A prospective colorectal database, containing clinical information on patient characteristics, preoperative assessments, surgical outcomes including postoperative complications and pathological outcomes maintained at our institution, was used for the analysis. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval under number HM24-029 from the Institutional Review Board of Fujita Health University.

Patient demographics

The criteria for using the hinotori in RRS were clinical stage I to IV rectal neoplasms, mainly adenocarcinoma. Patients with tumors that massively invaded adjacent organs were excluded from the study because it was the initial period of introducing the novel surgical robotic platform. Patient characteristics, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), and clinical stage of the disease, were documented for the analysis. Preoperative staging was assessed through colonoscopy, barium enema, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging, with the TNM stage classified according to the 9th edition of the Japanese Classification of Colorectal, Appendiceal, and Anal Carcinoma [9]. According to this classification, the rectum is defined as extending from the promontory to the upper edge of the anal canal, necessitating the use of an enema study to accurately assess the extent of the tumor. As a rule, all patients underwent a barium enema as part of their preoperative assessment. However, in cases where stenosis was observed, Gastrografin was used instead of barium. Two lead surgeons (HK and KM), each with over 100 robot-assisted surgeries using the da Vinci and accredited by the Japanese Society of Endoscopic Surgery through its endoscopic surgical skill qualification system, participated as primary cockpit surgeons in this study.

hinotori™ surgical robot system

The hinotori, like the da Vinci, features an “Operation Unit” with four robotic arms, a “Surgeon Cockpit” and a “Monitor Cart” (Fig. 1a-c). In contrast to the da Vinci, it boasts several advancements: Firstly, its robotic arms have eight axes (Fig. 1a), offering enhanced flexibility and reduced interference with each other and the patient's body, alongside a monitoring system that alerts when potential interference is detected. Secondly, it introduces a docking-free system that adjusts the pivot position of the instrument without the need to connect the trocar to the robotic arm, thereby providing adequate space around the trocars and minimizing tissue damage due to excessive traction. Thirdly, the surgeon cockpit is equipped with a 3D viewer that can be flexibly positioned (Fig. 1b), aiming to alleviate neck and shoulder fatigue. Lastly, the hinotori system employs a 16:9 wide monitor for an expanded field of view, in contrast to the da Vinci's 4:3 monitor [10].

Operative technique

The operating theater configuration is shown in Fig. 2a. Our technique using the DBM for da Vinci-assisted surgery has been previously reported [7]. Following this approach, a single-docking, totally robotic technique was employed without moving the Operation Unit, utilizing the five ports (Fig. 2b). The setup and use of different instruments on each robotic arm during surgery are illustrated in Fig. 3. Arm 1, equipped with Fenestrated Bipolar Forceps, and Arm 2, with the endoscope, remain fixed throughout the entire procedure. Arm 3, however, has three instruments that are interchanged depending on the surgical requirements. For Arm 4, the choice of instrument is influenced by the surgeon's preference. A docking-free system can provide a clear and unobstructed area around the trocar (Fig. 4a). During the medial-to-lateral approach in RRS, Monopolar Curved Scissors were used for mobilization with a long surgical stride. MBF were utilized around vessels and nerves for D3 lymph node dissection at the root of IMA (Fig. 4b). When TSME was needed, they were also used to expose the rectal serosa circumferentially for transection by linear stapler (Fig. 4c). Depending on the tumor location, the assistant uses either Arm 3 or Arm 4 to deploy a linear stapler to transect the rectum. Once the dissection of the rectum was completed, the robotic instruments are disengaged, and the Operation Unit is moved backward from the patient. The remaining procedures, including anastomosis, are performed with a conventional laparoscopic approach.

Statistical analysis

All statistical data were analyzed using the JMP Pro 15 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Continuous variables are presented as medians (range), and discrete variables are presented as proportions and percentages.

Results

The clinical characteristics of the consecutive 28 patients are presented in Table 1. Of these, 14 (50%) were male, with a median age of 62 years (range, 53–87) and body mass index of 22.1 kg/m2 (range, 18.4–28.4). Clinical staging revealed 8 (28.6%) patients with stage I, 5 (17.8%) with stage II, 12 (42.9%) with stage III, and 3 (10.7%) with stage IV. The distance from the lower edge of the tumor to the anal verge, as measured preoperatively by digital rectal examination or colonoscopy, was 8.5 cm (range: 0–15 cm).

The surgical outcomes are shown in Table 2. All patients successfully underwent RRS without any intraoperative adverse events or conversion to alternative surgical approaches. Of the 28 patients, 26 underwent stapled anastomosis with a double-stapling technique (DST), and among the 27 cases including one for ISR, a diverting stoma was created in 9 (33.3%). Lateral lymph node dissection was performed in 2 cases (7.1%). The median operative time was 257 min (range, 167–584), and the cockpit time was 148 min (range, 79–472), respectively. Blood loss was relatively low at 15 mL (range, 3–67), and none of the patients required blood transfusions. There were no reoperations or mortalities, and every patient underwent R0 resection. Complications of Clavien-Dindo Grade 3 or higher were absent. The only minor complication was a transient ileus in one patient (4.8%), which resolved with conservative management. The median postoperative hospital stay was 12 days (range, 8–21 days), and no readmissions occurred within 30 days after discharge.

Discussion

Robot-assisted surgery has emerged as an important advancement in minimally invasive treatment for rectal cancer. The REAL trial revealed that the incidence of positive circumferential resection margins (CRM) and postoperative complications of CD Grade 2 or higher were significantly lower in robot-assisted surgery compared to conventional laparoscopic procedures [11], highlighting the potential benefits of robot-assisted surgery in rectal cancer treatment. This finding contrasts with the results of the first international joint RCT, the ROLARR trial [12], and the Korean-based COLRAR trial [13], where no significant superiority of robot-assisted surgery was observed. Despite these mixed outcomes, the proliferation of large-scale clinical trials over the past decade has contributed to the rapid adoption of robotic-assisted surgery in rectal cancer treatment [2, 14, 15]. Most of these surgeries have been performed using the da Vinci, specifically up to the Xi model. However, with the expiration of da Vinci's patents, numerous novel surgical robots have emerged from various companies, signaling an era of fierce competition and innovation.

In Japan, the regulatory approval of multiple systems, including the da Vinci SP (Intuitive Surgical Inc., Sunnyvale, CA, USA), the hinotori [16, 17], the Hugo™ RAS system (Medtronic plc., Dublin, Ireland), and the Saroa surgical robot (Riverfield Inc., Tokyo, Japan) [18], reflects this trend. The clinical outcomes of the present study using novel Japanese robot system at a single university hospital demonstrate its safety and feasibility in the management of rectal neoplasms, with no intraoperative complications or conversions. These findings align with recent research [19], which has shown that robotic surgery offers distinct advantages over laparoscopic procedures, especially in more challenging cases. A recent meta-analysis [3] comparing robotic surgery to laparoscopic surgery after neoadjuvant therapy showed that robotic surgery resulted in improved short-term outcomes, such as lower conversion rates and reduced blood loss. Additionally, a meta-analysis [8] demonstrated that RRS was associated with reduced postoperative complications and similar oncologic outcomes compared to laparoscopic surgery. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first retrospective study to reveal the favorable short-term outcomes of the hinotori when conducted by experienced surgeons.

While the hinotori shares a similar basic structure with the da Vinci in terms of the number of robotic arms and their manipulation as of April 2024, the hinotori lacks certain advanced energy devices, including vessel sealing systems, ultrasonic coagulating shears, and a linear stapler for transection of the rectum. which are common in more established platforms such as the da Vinci. Since the hinotori was only recently launched in Japan, it is not yet fully equipped with these advanced instruments [10]. Consequently, the assistant surgeon in this series of surgeries had to assist in the procedures by using a laparoscopic linear stapler to transect the rectum, requiring closer coordination between the surgeon and the assistant compared to the almost solo surgery with the da Vinci. In the present study, the use of the DBM helped to overcome the absence of advanced energy devices by enabling efficient dissection and hemostasis, leading to minimal blood loss and effective lymph node dissection around the IMA. While the da Vinci is equipped with advanced energy devices, we performed the DBM without utilizing these devices, as reported in our previous study [7]. Although direct comparison is challenging, our findings indicate that, despite the operative time being approximately 30 min longer with the hinotori, the number of lymph nodes retrieved was comparable, and the rate of postoperative complications was low.

The study has several limitations. Firstly, it is a retrospective study with a small sample size at a single institution, and no direct comparison with conventional laparoscopic rectal cancer surgery was made. Secondly, the follow-up period was relatively short, as the study period ended in March 2024. This limits our ability to assess long-term oncological outcomes. Our primary focus was on perioperative outcomes, such as safety, feasibility, and short-term postoperative results, rather than long-term survival or recurrence rates. Thirdly, there is likely selection bias favoring patients with lower BMI and less advanced disease stages, as the surgeries using the hinotori are in their initial stages. Furthermore, the study was conducted exclusively on Japanese patients, and the observed safety and feasibility may primarily apply to the Asian population. Additionally, the current study was conducted by surgeons with extensive experience in robot-assisted surgery, specifically in RRS, which may influence the generalizability of the results. Lastly, the study was conducted based on the Japanese definition of the rectum, which extends from the promontory to the upper edge of the anal canal. This definition may differ from those used in Western countries, potentially influencing tumor classification. All patients were assessed using barium enema to ensure accurate classification, with Gastrografin used in cases of stenosis. This classification method was consistently applied to ensure the accuracy of tumor identification and assessment.

These preliminary results are promising, but future prospective studies with larger patient cohorts and longer follow-up periods are necessary to validate the novel robotic system's role in rectal cancer surgery and to assess long-term oncological outcomes. Furthermore, long-term observation of prognostic factors such as relapse-free survival and overall survival should be evaluated to fully understand the significance of RRS using the hinotori. Despite these limitations, the findings warrant further investigation in larger, prospective studies to confirm the hinotori's role in RRS and potentially other surgical modalities.

We believe that the rapid upgrade of the surgical robot in response to surgeons' feedback, just over a year after receiving regulatory approval in the field of gastrointestinal surgery, is partly due to Medicaroid Corporation's status as a domestic Japanese company. For instance, the clutch pedal and the scope pedal were located close together and had similar shapes in the previous version of the surgeon cockpit (Fig. 5a), leading to frequent incorrect operation during manipulation. Moreover, it was challenging to distinguish between the foot pedals for the incision mode and the coagulation mode on the electric cautery. The current model has been improved to allow for more intuitive pedal operation, as shown in Fig. 5b. Similarly, in the previous model, the clutch could only be operated using the foot pedals (Fig. 5c), giving it a feel similar to the da Vinci's second-generation S model. However, the current model is equipped with a hand clutch (Fig. 5d), allowing for additional clutch control directly from each hand separately. In September 2023, the hinotori received approval for sale from Singapore's Health Sciences Authority, indicating that the company must now focus on meeting international needs, not just those within Japan. This will require ongoing efforts to address the expanding global market.

Moving forward, future research should prioritize prospective, multicenter studies with larger cohorts to validate our findings. Comparative studies involving other emerging robotic systems like the Hugo™ RAS system and the Saroa Surgical Robot will help clarify the relative advantages of the hinotori. Additionally, long-term outcomes on disease-free and overall survival are crucial to establish the hinotori's oncological efficacy.

Conclusion

This retrospective observational study demonstrates that RRS using the hinotori appears to be both safe and technically feasible for the Asian population. Despite some limitations due to the current lack of advanced energy devices, the use of the DBM allowed for effective dissection and hemostasis. The safety and feasibility were particularly observed when performed by surgeons with extensive experience in robotic procedures. Further investigations with a larger sample size and longer follow-up are required to validate the novel robotic system's role in rectal cancer surgery and to assess oncological outcomes.
