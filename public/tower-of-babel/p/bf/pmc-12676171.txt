Case Summary

Case 1

A 60-year-old female with diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension (HTN), and coronary artery disease (CAD) (prior primary percutaneous coronary intervention to right coronary artery) developed recurrent P. aeruginosa bacteremia after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). This was complicated by severe mitral regurgitation due to valve perforation and mobile vegetations confirmed on transesophageal echocardiography. She completed 6 weeks of IV antibiotics via a peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) line, but vegetations persisted. The cardiothoracic surgery team evaluated her and recommended mitral valve replacement, with detailed family counseling regarding risks and perioperative/postoperative antibiotic needs. However, her condition worsened with fever, watery stools, and sepsis on re-admission. Despite multidisciplinary management (ID, CTS, gastroenterology, neurology), including antibiotics (meropenem) and inotropes, she developed atrial fibrillation, pericardial effusion, shock liver, acute kidney injury, seizures, and cerebellar infarcts. Owing to multiorgan dysfunction, surgery was not feasible, and after the family opted for do not resuscitate and ventilator withdrawal, she died.

Case 2

A 64-year-old male with non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) underwent elective PCI with drug-eluting stents to the left main (LM)–left anterior descending (LAD), LAD, and diagonal, and plain old balloon angioplasty to the left circumflex (LCX). He was initially discharged stable. On readmission, he developed fever with blood cultures positive for P. aeruginosa, consistent with endovascular infection. He required prolonged IV antibiotics (piperacillin/tazobactam → cefepime → ceftazidime, ciprofloxacin, amikacin) via PICC. Complications included atrial fibrillation (treated with IV amiodarone) and a large pericardial effusion (22 mm) without tamponade. He was discharged stable after antimicrobial therapy.

Case 3

A 63-year-old male with DM, HTN, prior PCI, and preserved EF (55%) presented with chest pain and was diagnosed with NSTEMI. Left heart catheterization (LHC) revealed 2-vessel CAD with severe LAD and diagonal disease. PCI to LM–LAD with drug-eluting stent (Orsiro 2.5 × 26 mm, 3.0 × 35 mm) was performed successfully. He was discharged stable. On his second admission, he presented with fever and chest pain. Labs showed CRP 49 and procalcitonin 0.76. Blood culture grew P. aeruginosa. He was treated with antibiotics and supportive care and was discharged stable. At follow-up, he remained alive and well.

Case 4

A 50-year-old male with HTN, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and prior LAD PCI presented with anterior wall myocardial infarction and severe 3-vessel CAD. Deemed high risk for CABG, he underwent successful PCI to obtuse marginal branch 1 (OM1) and LAD. He was discharged stable. On readmission, he presented with NSTEMI (EF 45%). LHC revealed patent LAD and left circumflex stents with aneurysms in both vessels, with stable right coronary arter disease (Figure 1). He developed fever; empiric IV piperacillin/tazobactam was started. Positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) was advised to differentiate infective vs traumatic aneurysm, but blood cultures were negative. He was discharged stable. At follow-up, chest pain was attributed to musculoskeletal causes. He remained alive and stable.Figure 1Preinfection Angiogram Showing Normal Poststenting Appearance Without Complications

Case 5

A 63-year-old male with DM, HTN, obesity, and smoking history presented with NSTEMI. Echo showed EF 40%. Angiography revealed severe proximal LAD stenosis, and PCI to LAD was performed successfully. On readmission, he presented with chest pain and melena. LHC showed aneurysmal outpouchings in the stented LAD (mycotic aneurysm) (Figure 2). Blood cultures were negative. He was treated with meropenem and vancomycin (later de-escalated). Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) revealed pangastritis. A mild pericardial effusion resolved on repeat echo. On follow-up, he had pleuritic chest pain and was found to have massive aneurysmal dilatation of the proximal and distal LAD. Project Rationale

The emergence of bloodstream infections in a controlled procedural setting raised significant alarm, particularly given the institution's strong track record. With THI's active participation in NCDR registries and recognition as a center of excellence in a resource-limited environment, maintaining clinical integrity was paramount. There was also an external imperative: protecting institutional reputation, maintaining performance benchmarks, and ensuring eligibility for national awards and certifications. Prompt investigation was essential to safeguard current and future patients undergoing life-saving cardiac procedures.

Project Description

A multidisciplinary task force was immediately convened, including cardiology, infectious diseases, microbiology, infection control, pharmacy, nursing, and hospital administration.

Initial steps included:•Re-education of all CCL staff on hand hygiene and the aseptic technique.•Comprehensive environmental sampling of the CCL (trolleys, imaging equipment, injector systems, contrast warmers, procedural surfaces).•Review and reinforcement of standard operating procedures (SOPs).•Temporary scale-down of procedural volume to limit spread and facilitate investigation.

When environmental cultures were negative and no procedural breaches were identified, the scope of investigation expanded. Pharmacy-sourced intravenous medications (heparin, midazolam, nalbuphine, saline) were cultured, with no growth detected. Attention turned to contrast media. Two bottles from the same lot as previously used in affected cases were cultured and yielded P. aeruginosa with the same antimicrobial susceptibility profile as patient isolates, confirming the outbreak source.

Project Deliverables

Based on the findings of the root cause analysis, several immediate and long-term corrective actions were implemented to contain the outbreak and strengthen infection-control practices. The key project deliverables included the following:•Immediate withdrawal of the implicated contrast media brand.•Replacement with an alternate, verified contrast product.•Notification to all clinical areas and submission of findings to the supplier for formal root cause analysis.•Reporting to regional public health authorities.•Revision of SOPs to mandate sterility checks for contrast and other high-risk consumables.•Proposal to establish vendor audit systems and enhanced infection-control surveillance.

Project Outcome, Impact, and Future Directions

After withdrawal of the contaminated contrast media, no new cases of P. aeruginosa bloodstream infection occurred. Ongoing surveillance, including sterility testing of contrast media from new lots and patient monitoring, confirmed outbreak resolution.

Discussion

This case highlights the complexity of infection control in procedural environments dependent on external supplies. Despite adherence to protocols, a sealed but contaminated product introduced P. aeruginosa directly into the intravascular space. P aeruginosa is well known for its ability to survive in moist environments, including fluids and gels that appear sterile.1,3 Even strong infection prevention and control (IPC) programs are vulnerable without robust supply chain oversight.4

Nosocomial outbreaks linked to contaminated intravenous fluids, gels, and medical products are well documented, with Pseudomonas frequently implicated because of its resilience in aqueous solutions.2,3,5 Contrast media, however, is rarely suspected until other sources are excluded, underscoring the need to evaluate all patient-contacting products regardless of packaging or sterility claims.5,6 Across contexts such as contrast media, injectors, and endoscopes, unifying factors include Pseudomonas' persistence in fluids and the need for strict single-use or discard policies. Routine surveillance cultures of parenteral solutions are uncommon outside outbreak settings, creating blind spots for contamination.7

Future risk mitigation requires single-use prefilled syringes,8 discarding opened contrast bottles within 4 hours9,10 and considering periodic sterility testing of stored contrast.11 Staff education and supply chain audits, including cold-chain integrity, are also critical.10,11

Broadly, P aeruginosa is a major cause of healthcare-associated bloodstream infection, often associated with high mortality when unrecognized or inadequately treated.3 Rapid cluster detection, thorough environmental and product evaluation, and decisive corrective action are essential. As emphasized in international IPC guidelines, multidisciplinary collaboration, supplier engagement, and national reporting strengthen institutional safety and public health preparedness.1,3

Conclusions

Even in advanced CCLs, infection outbreaks can arise from overlooked sources. This investigation demonstrates the importance of thorough root cause analysis when clustered postprocedural infections occur. Identification of sealed contrast media as the outbreak source not only resolved the cluster but also drove lasting institutional changes. Proactive sterility checks, vendor accountability, and cross-departmental vigilance are essential to infection prevention in high-risk procedural environments.

For other institutions, particularly in resource-limited settings, this case emphasizes the need for transparent investigations, rigorous surveillance, and supply chain scrutiny to safeguard patient safety.
