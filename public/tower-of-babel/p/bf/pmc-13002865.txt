Thermodynamic and exergoeconomic analysis of a solar-assisted LiBr/H₂O ejector–absorption refrigeration system with triple-layer thermal storage
Authors: Abdeljelil Chammam, Rosull Saadoon Abbood, Shaikh Hasibul Majid, I. B. Sapaev, D T Arunkumar, Akanksha Mishra, Ripendeep Singh, Abinash Mahapatro, Reza Alayi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapid increase in global cooling demand, particularly in regions with high solar potential, has emphasized the urgent need for sustainable and electricity‑independent refrigeration technologies. In response to this challenge, this work proposes a novel solar‑assisted single‑effect Lithium Bromide–Water (LiBr/H₂O) absorption refrigeration system incorporating a supersonic ejector and a triple‑layer solar thermal storage unit. The design aims to maximize energy recovery and reduce operating cost through combined thermodynamic and thermoeconomic optimization. Governing mass and energy conservation equations are established and solved using the Engineering Equation Solver (EES). Energy, exergy, and cost assessments are performed for both ejector‑assisted and conventional configurations to quantify improvements in the Coefficient of Performance (COP), exergy efficiency, and component cost rate under various solar irradiation and generator temperatures. Results reveal that ejector integration enhances COP by 12.7% and exergy efficiency by 11.3%, while reducing total investment cost by 9% compared to the baseline cycle. The optimized configuration achieves coefficient of performance of 0.74 and solar coefficient of performance of 0.58 under solar irradiation of 973 W/m², confirming marked enhancements in thermodynamic efficiency, cost effectiveness, and overall system sustainability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41158-2.

Introduction

Climate change and the global effort to minimize fossil-fuel dependency have intensified the transition toward renewable energy solutions. Among various clean energy sources—including wind, biomass, and geothermal systems—solar energy has emerged as one of the most accessible and powerful options for sustainable cooling applications. In regions with high solar radiation, such as central Iran, solar panels and thermal collectors provide significant potential to support refrigeration systems and reduce the growing pressure on electrical grids. This approach is aligned with international initiatives promoting green technologies and lowering greenhouse gas emissions. Numerous studies have explored solar-assisted cooling as part of this global movement1,2. For this reason, extensive efforts are underway in scientific and academic centers around the world to introduce refrigeration systems based on renewable energy. For example, Kardgar3 presented an integrated system with 92% energy efficiency, including simultaneous production of electricity, cooling and heating using solar energy, geothermal energy, steam turbine and heat pump. Raouf panah and Seyed4 analyzed a solar compression refrigeration system in the Isfahan climate using the TRNSYS software. They reported the minimum area of photovoltaic panels required to power the compressor. Gunawan et al.5 conducted a study on the technical and economic performance of a 19 kW monobloc absorption chiller in Isfahan. The results of their research showed that by using a 30 m2 solar collector, annual savings equivalent to 895 m3 of natural gas can be achieved. Solar absorption chillers have attracted the attention of the scientific community as an active and growing research area. Evidence of this dynamism can be seen in the publication of numerous scientific articles in this field in recent years; for example, references6–8 are examples of these studies. A persistent challenge in thermal engineering is the effort to significantly enhance the energetic and exergy efficiencies of refrigeration technologies. Strategies employed range from the introduction of novel refrigerants9,10 and to the integration of heat recovery mechanisms11 and the optimization of heat exchangers12, or the use of multi-component mixtures13,14. Among these approaches, the ejector has emerged as a particularly compelling solution, primarily due to its inherent advantages: the total absence of moving mechanical parts, a simplified structural design, low fabrication costs, and minimal maintenance demands15.

Recent investigations have also emphasized the role of hybrid and geo-solar configurations integrating economic and environmental optimization perspectives. For instance, Bahrami et al. optimized a solar–geothermal system using real solar data from ParsaAbad‑e‑Moghan through cost, energy, and environmental indices16. Similar advanced soft‑computing techniques have been employed for the optimization of biogas‑fueled cogeneration systems and hybrid geothermal–solar plants using coupled artificial neural networks and genetic algorithms. Moreover, several studies have conducted exergy/cost‑based optimization of complex hybrid plants integrating Compressed Air Energy Storage (CAES), heliostat solar fields, and biomass‑fired gas turbines. The thermodynamic and exergoeconomic evaluation of high‑performance geothermal‑based multigeneration systems capable of simultaneous power, cooling, and hydrogen production has further demonstrated the rising significance of combined renewable and exergoeconomic approaches in energy system design17,18.

In refrigeration cycles, the strategic incorporation of an ejector—either replacing the expansion valve entirely or diverting a portion of the flow before the valve—provides an opportunity to recover energy that would otherwise be lost. This method not only mitigates system energy dissipation but also facilitates the optimization of compressor work, leading to a marked improvement in overall cycle performance19,20. For instance, Parvez et al.21 integrated an ejector into a novel solar-biomass hybrid system. By linking a Rankine cycle, an ejector, and an absorption refrigeration cycle, they successfully utilized low-grade energy sources, such as waste heat and steam turbine output, to achieve simultaneous power generation and cooling. The analysis confirmed an overall energetic efficiency of 42.29% and an exergy efficiency of 39.72%. Similarly, Yin et al.22 presented a solar ejector subcooling system to upgrade the basic trans critical carbon dioxide cycle. Their evaluation indicated a 22.96% increase in the modified COP. While the system cost escalated by 7.98% to 17.9% (at 5 °C subcooling), the substantial 18.34% to 26.22% COP increase fully justified this capital investment. In essence, the ejector was central to enabling solar subcooling and maximizing the inherent efficiency of the base cycle.

Absorption cycles represent an alternative, compressor-free refrigeration method, driven by thermal energy (e.g., solar or waste heat) and utilizing chemical working pairs (e.g., lithium bromide/water)23. Their main benefits lie in reduced electrical consumption and the ability to leverage affordable or waste heat streams. The literature documents various configurations that incorporate ejectors into these absorption systems24,25. Sirwan et al.26, for example, studied an ejector absorption system with a flash tank, reporting a modest 3% increase in COP. Conversely, Farshi and Khalili27 demonstrated that an ejector heat pump configuration significantly enhanced the system’s performance from a thermo-economic perspective. The current body of research extensively covers the utilization of ejectors to improve absorption cycle performance, as reflected in numerous contemporary works28-32.

Despite significant progress in solar-assisted refrigeration systems, several critical gaps remain unaddressed in the literature. The present study aims to fill these gaps through the following key contributions:

Materials and methods

Problem definition and governing equations

The system under investigation consists of a single‑effect absorption refrigeration cycle driven by a solar heat‑absorption subsystem. A schematic representation of the overall system configuration, including the absorption cycle, ejector loop, and stratified thermal storage unit, is provided in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Schematic of the solar-assisted single-effect LiBr/H₂O absorption refrigeration system integrated with an ejector and a triple-layer solar heat-storage unit.

Schematic of the solar-assisted single-effect LiBr/H₂O absorption refrigeration system integrated with an ejector and a triple-layer solar heat-storage unit.

To minimize environmental impacts within the absorption section, a lithium bromide–water (LiBr/H₂O) solution is employed as the working pair. The ejector is modeled based on a constant‑area, one‑dimensional steady‑flow assumption to ensure consistency in mass and energy conservation. The internal ejector flow is characterized by a fixed entrainment ratio (ω), adopted from validated literature correlations, in accordance with the methodology recommended by Zhou et al. (2023)15. The mixed flow at the ejector outlet is assumed to leave as saturated vapor. This assumption guarantees thermodynamic consistency between the ejector discharge and the condenser inlet, as both the primary and secondary streams are fully vaporized and the operating pressure remains above the saturation limit of the LiBr/H₂O working pair.

Solar resource and climatic characterization of Kabul, Afghanistan

The solar‑assisted system is modeled using real climatic data corresponding to Kabul, Afghanistan (latitude 34.5° N, longitude 69.2° E, elevation 1790 m). This location represents a semi‑arid continental climate with high solar irradiance and pronounced diurnal temperature variations during the summer season. The detailed solar and meteorological characterization of the study site is provided in Fig. 2.Fig. 2Solar resource and climatic characterization of Kabul, Afghanistan: (a) Diurnal variation of solar irradiance and ambient temperature (mid‑July); (b) Annual variation of average daily solar irradiance and ambient temperature.

Solar resource and climatic characterization of Kabul, Afghanistan: (a) Diurnal variation of solar irradiance and ambient temperature (mid‑July); (b) Annual variation of average daily solar irradiance and ambient temperature.

Meteorological inputs, including Global Horizontal Irradiance (GHI), diffuse solar radiation, and ambient temperature, were obtained from the Meteonorm 8.1 database, which provides hourly synthetic but statistically validated climatic data derived from long‑term satellite observations (1991–2020). To ensure model consistency and represent peak cooling conditions, a clear‑sky midsummer day in mid‑July was selected. On this representative day, the daily mean solar irradiance reaches approximately 973 W m⁻², while the ambient temperature varies between 23 °C in the early morning and 36 °C around solar noon. These location‑specific climatic inputs are employed in the solar collector and stratified thermal storage sub‑models (Eqs. 1–8), ensuring physically consistent simulations and enabling realistic assessment of diurnal solar fluctuations and thermal stratification behavior.

Multi‑objective optimization framework

To systematically enhance the thermodynamic and exergoeconomic performance of the proposed solar‑assisted LiBr/H₂O ejector–absorption refrigeration system, a comprehensive multi‑objective optimization framework was integrated into the modeling structure. The optimization aims to identify the optimal compromise between energetic performance and economic feasibility, building on the validated thermodynamic and exergoeconomic formulations presented earlier.

The selection of decision variables in the multi‑objective optimization was based on three primary criteria: (i) strong thermodynamic and exergoeconomic influence on system performance, (ii) direct controllability under practical operating conditions, and (iii) frequent adoption in the optimization of solar‑assisted absorption refrigeration systems reported in the literature. Accordingly, the generator temperature, evaporator temperature, and absorber temperature were selected as key thermal decision variables, as they significantly govern the driving thermal potential, cooling capacity, solution circulation ratio, and system irreversibility levels. These factors have a direct impact on the thermodynamic performance and annualized cost rate of absorption systems. In addition, the ejector entrainment ratio was selected as a hydraulic decision variable due to its influence on pressure recovery, mass flow redistribution, expansion irreversibility mitigation, and generator heat demand. From an economic perspective, ω strongly affects the heat‑transfer areas of the generator, absorber, and associated heat exchangers, thereby influencing the total annualized cost rate.

Based on these considerations, four dominant operating parameters were finally selected as decision variables—namely the generator temperature, evaporator temperature, absorber temperature, and ejector entrainment ratio. Their allowable ranges (Table 5) were established based on thermodynamic feasibility, crystallization avoidance, solar‑collector temperature limits, and literature‑reported operating conditions. Two conflicting objective functions were defined: minimizing the total annualized cost rate and maximizing the overall exergy efficiency. Selection of the optimal compromise solution

To obtain a single representative operating condition from the resulting Pareto‑optimal solution set, a post‑processing decision‑making procedure was applied. The optimal solution was identified based on the minimum normalized Euclidean distance to the ideal point. The ideal point was defined as the simultaneous minimum of the total annualized cost rate and maximum of the overall exergy efficiency among the non‑dominated solutions. Each objective function was normalized to eliminate scale effects, and the Euclidean distance of each Pareto‑optimal solution to the ideal point was calculated. The solution exhibiting the shortest distance was selected as the optimal compromise operating condition. This approach ensures a balanced trade‑off between economic and thermodynamic objectives, avoids subjective weighting factors, and guarantees that the selected operating point represents a physically meaningful and practically implementable system state. The resulting compromise solution is highlighted and discussed in the Pareto front presented in the Results section.

Sensitivity analysis of decision variables

To assess the robustness and stability of the proposed multi‑objective optimization framework, a sensitivity analysis was conducted with respect to the selected decision variables, namely the generator temperature, evaporator temperature, absorber temperature, and ejector entrainment ratio. The main objective of this analysis is to quantify the relative influence of each decision variable on the objective functions and to verify that the optimization results are not overly sensitive to small perturbations in operating conditions.

A one‑at‑a‑time (OAT) sensitivity approach was adopted, in which each decision variable was independently varied within its prescribed operating range (Table 5), while all other variables were maintained at their baseline values. This method enables clear identification of cause‑and‑effect relationships between individual decision variables and the system performance indicators, and it has been widely employed in sensitivity assessments of thermal and exergoeconomic systems.

The sensitivity of the total annualized cost rate is primarily associated with variations in the generator temperature and ejector entrainment ratio. Changes in generator temperature directly affect the input heat requirement, solution circulation ratio, and logarithmic mean temperature difference in the generator, which in turn influence the required heat‑transfer area and corresponding investment cost. Similarly, the entrainment ratio governs the redistributed mass flow rates through the ejector and generator, affecting both thermal load allocation and component sizing, thereby exerting a pronounced impact on the overall cost rate. From a thermodynamic perspective, the overall exergy efficiency exhibits strong sensitivity to the evaporator and absorber temperatures. Variations in evaporator temperature significantly alter the refrigeration effect and the exergy content of the cooling product, while changes in absorber temperature influence solution equilibrium conditions and irreversibility generation in the absorber. Although generator temperature also affects the exergy balance, its dominant influence is observed through economic rather than purely thermodynamic pathways. Overall, the consistent dominance of the entrainment ratio and generator temperature in both economic and exergetic sensitivity analyses confirms the physical relevance of the selected decision variables and verifies the robustness of the NSGA‑II optimization framework. Multi-objective exergoeconomic optimization

Following the single-objective exergoeconomic assessment, a multi-objective optimization is performed to identify the optimal trade-off between the total annualized cost rate and the overall exergy efficiency. The resulting Pareto front, which represents the set of non-dominated optimal solutions, is illustrated in Fig. 13. The curve demonstrates a typical conflict between economic and efficiency objectives. Initially, a moderate increase in total cost leads to significant gains in exergy efficiency. This corresponds to design modifications that are highly effective, such as minor enlargements of heat transfer areas or slight adjustments to the Ejector performance ratio.

A crucial feature of the Pareto front is the Knee Point, which is identified on the curve. This point represents the best compromise solution: an exergy efficiency of 60% is achieved at a relatively favorable total cost rate. Beyond this Knee Point, the curve’s slope increases dramatically. This indicates that further marginal improvements in exergy efficiency​ (e.g., from 60% to 62%) require a disproportionately sharp increase in the capital investment, resulting in a significantly higher total cost rate. The Knee Point, therefore, offers the most balanced, economically and energetically sensible operating condition for the proposed solar-assisted cooling system.

To identify a single representative operating condition from the set of non‑dominated solutions, a Pareto‑based decision‑making criterion was applied. The optimal point was selected as the compromise solution corresponding to the minimum normalized Euclidean distance to the ideal point, defined by the minimum total annualized cost rate and maximum overall exergy efficiency. This method avoids subjective weighting of objective functions and provides a balanced trade‑off between thermodynamic performance and economic feasibility. Consequently, the highlighted point in Fig. 13 represents the most desirable operating condition for practical implementation of the proposed system.

Extended economic assessment and sensitivity analysis

The economic assessment of the proposed solar-assisted LiBr/H₂O ejector–absorption refrigeration system was performed to evaluate its cost-effectiveness under realistic operating conditions. The total investment cost includes the collector, storage tank, generator, absorber, evaporator, and condenser while neglecting the minor contributions of the ejector and valves due to their comparatively low prices.

The annualized total cost rate was computed to be 2.13 $/h, and the CRF was determined using an interest rate of 8% and a lifetime of 20 years (CRF of 0.09818). The resulting payback period (PBP) equals 4.2 years, indicating an economically viable design with short cost recovery and durable payback performance compared to conventional LiBr/H₂O chillers.

Figure 14 presents the economic sensitivity analysis, illustrating how the total annual cost varies with collector area and generator temperature. As observed, the cost decreases with increasing generator temperature up to 150 °C because of improved system efficiency, but rises slightly beyond 160 °C due to additional heating demand. Conversely, cost increases quadratically with the collector area, emphasizing capital expenditure’s dominant role in overall economics.

The sensitivity trends show that reducing the collector area by 40% from the baseline design reduces the total cost by 9%, while maintaining system performance within 5% of its optimal COP of 0.74. The collector and generator collectively account for 55.4% of total system cost, followed by the evaporator (22.8%), absorber (12.6%), and other components below 10%. This distribution highlights the solar subsystem’s strong influence on economic feasibility and points out where future optimization should focus.

Conclusion

This study successfully conducted a comprehensive energy, exergy, and exergoeconomic analysis on a novel solar-assisted single-effect LiBr/H2​O absorption refrigeration system. The integration of a supersonic ejector and an advanced triple-layer solar storage tank was the focal point of the research. Evaluating the performance metrics of this integrated configuration against a conventional baseline absorption cycle demonstrated substantial and simultaneous improvements across thermodynamic and economic domains.

Key Findings are Summarized Below:

Thermodynamic Enhancement: The synergetic effect of the ejector and stratified storage significantly boosted system performance. Specifically, the SCOP showed an improvement of 12.7%, while the overall exergy efficiency increased by 11.3%.

Optimal Operation: Optimization results clearly identified an optimal generator temperature generator temperature of 150 °C, which maximized both performance indices, yielding a peak COP of 0.74 and exergy efficiency of 0.58.

Ejector and Storage Efficacy: The entrainment ratio (ω) analysis confirmed the ejector’s efficiency, leading to a 42% reduction in the required solar collector area at optimal operating conditions. Furthermore, the thermal stratification model validated the effectiveness of the triple-layer tank in maintaining the necessary high-temperature energy supply for the generator.

Economic Viability: From an exergoeconomic standpoint, the proposed system is highly competitive, achieving an approximate 9% reduction in the total cooling production cost compared to the reference system.

Future Optimization Targets: The exergoeconomic breakdown indicated that the evaporator component carries the highest unit exergy cost and component cost rate. This designation identifies the evaporator as the prime candidate for targeted cost-reduction optimization in subsequent design iterations.

Recommendations for Future Work:

Future studies should focus on a multi-objective optimization that incorporates seasonal variations, alongside a dynamic analysis of the ejector’s performance under fluctuating solar radiation conditions.
