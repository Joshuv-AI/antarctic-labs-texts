Development of Balangu seed mucilage nanoparticles fabricated through ultrasonication-antisolvent method for encapsulation of gallic acid
Authors: Mohammad Mahdi Rostamabadi, Hajar Shekarchizadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGallic acid (GA) is a naturally occurring polyphenol offering a vast diversity of biological attributes. The objective of this study was to design, characterize, and assess GA-loaded Balangu seed mucilage (BSM) nanoparticles (NPs) via a newly developed ultrasonication-antisolvent method of NP preparation. GA-loaded BSM NPs displayed a round-shaped and homogenous morphology and possessed particle sizes of 20–320 nm with a GA loading efficiency of ~ 52.8% and zeta potential of − 29.5 mV. The thermal behavior of NPs and the bioactive-carrier interaction were studied by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, respectively. The FTIR results revealed that GA successfully entrapped into the mucilage NPs. However, there was a diverse thermal stability of the developed nano-system prior and following the GA encapsulation. Overall, the simultaneous application of ultrasonication and antisolvent approaches was found to be an effectual strategy to produce nanoscale structures and encapsulate biologically active ingredients like GA that may find uses in food/pharmaceutical industries.Keywords: Gallic acid, Balangu seed mucilage, Ultrasonication, NanoencapsulationSubject terms: Nanoscale materials, Nanoscale materials, Biomaterials

Introduction

Gallic acid (GA) or 3,4,5-trihydroxy benzoic acid is a naturally occurring polyphenol that exhibits various biological activities, such as antioxidant, anticancer, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, anti-tyrosinase, and antimicrobial properties1. However, GA has low physicochemical stability (especially against light, heat, and oxygen), which restricts its food/pharmaceutical applicability due to oxidation, photodegradation, and thermal degradation. When exposed to light, GA can undergo photochemical reactions that break down its antioxidant properties, while heat can accelerate these reactions, leading to a loss of bioactivity. Additionally, exposure to oxygen can promote oxidative degradation, further diminishing its therapeutic and preservative effects2. Moreover, GA exhibits undesirable sensory characteristics, such as astringency and a bitter taste, which can limit its palatability in food products3. Encapsulation can protect GA from environmental factors by providing a barrier that minimizes exposure to light, heat, and oxygen. Encapsulation has been shown to stabilize bioactive compounds like GA, extending their shelf life and improving their bioavailability4,5. For example, a study by Karaman6 demonstrated that encapsulating GA in yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) microcapsules significantly reduced its bitterness, enhancing consumer acceptance without compromising its bioactive properties of GA. Similarly, Nájera-Martínez et al.7 reported that GA encapsulated in a polymeric and pH-sensitive matrix of pectin/alginate resulted in a smoother taste profile in food products, making it more suitable for inclusion in functional foods. Recent rapid progress in nanotechnological platforms presents a valuable opportunity to overcome these limitations. Currently, potential nano-driven encapsulation approaches have been successful in food systems for the protection of various biologically active ingredients. Nanotechnology offers unique biofunctional attributes to bioactive materials, principally depending on the chosen nanoencapsulation strategy and nanovehicle utilized, improving their efficiency8,9.

Various encapsulation methods have been employed to produce nanostructures of high physicochemical potential. As a physical method, ultrasonication has represented potent ability in the fabrication of diverse nanodimensional structures by applying mechanical sound waves, which possess frequencies typically higher than 20 kHz10. Ultrasonication is an environmentally friendly method, offers numerous advantages such as reducing the processing time and increasing the chemical reaction efficacy. Sinusoidal movement of sonication waves would result in creating cavitation bubbles over an ultrasonication treatment of a liquid medium. Further expansion of bubbles intensely increases the shear, pressure, and temperature of the surrounding medium locally11,12. This energy can break down larger particles into smaller ones, leading to the formation of nanostructures.

Antisolvent precipitation is a powerful technique for producing nanostructured delivery systems. The antisolvent precipitation method involves dissolving a polymer or bioactive compound in a solvent, followed by the introduction of an antisolvent, which leads to a decrease in the solubility of the polymer13. This reduction in solubility causes the polymer to precipitate from the solution. As the solvent is mixed with the antisolvent, the solution reaches a supersaturation point, at which the polymer becomes less soluble and begins to nucleate14. The rate of addition of the antisolvent, the relative volumes of solvent and antisolvent, and the temperature of the system all influence the degree of supersaturation, which in turn affects the size and distribution of the nanoparticles. Rapid addition of the antisolvent can lead to faster nucleation and smaller particles, while slower addition may result in larger particles due to a more gradual precipitation process. Once nucleation occurs, the polymer chains aggregate to form nanoparticles, which can be stabilized by surfactants or other stabilizing agents to prevent aggregation15,16. The antisolvent precipitation technique also offers benefits in terms of cost-effectiveness, ease of scaling up, and process simplicity17. According to the literature, the ultrasound-assisted antisolvent approach has been successfully utilized to develop nanoparticles (NPs) as a continuous and scalable route18,19.

Balangu seed mucilage (BSM) is a water-soluble, high molecular weight, and biodegradable/biocompatible polysaccharide containing a wide variety of bioactive agents (such as phenolic acids and flavonoids). BSM could be a valuable source of a hydrocolloid because of its potent thickening, stabilizing, and emulsifying features, which, therefore, possess great potential to be utilized in a wide range of pharmaceutical/food applications20. The emulsifying and stabilizing properties of BSM are beneficial for the successful encapsulation of bioactive compounds like GA, as they help prevent undesirable aggregation and improve the retention of GA within food matrices21. The emulsifying properties of BSM allows it to form stable emulsions by reducing the interfacial tension between GA and the surrounding medium. This leads to the formation of uniform droplets or nanoparticles, which not only protect GA from environmental factors but also prevent its agglomeration or precipitation during processing or storage. Additionally, the stabilizing properties of BSM contribute to improving the retention of GA in food matrices by enhancing the distribution and dispersibility of GA in food formulations, preventing localized concentrations that could lead to undesirable sensory attributes like bitterness or astringency22,23.

A wide variety of synthetic and natural polymer matrices have been successfully implemented for GA encapsulation, such as chitosan NPs24, lentil flour and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose nanofibers25,26, protein-polysaccharide complexes based on whey protein concentrate and pectin27, and poly(lactide-co-glycolide) (PLGA) NPs28.

Thereby, the current study suggests developing BSM NPs through the ultrasound-assisted antisolvent method and its application for GA encapsulation as a model hydrophilic bioactive compound.

Material and methods

Materials

Balangu seeds were purchased from a local market in Isfahan, Iran. Gallic acid (GA) and ethanol were obtained from Merk (Darmstadt, Germany).

Balangu seed mucilage (BSM) extraction

BSM was extracted through a hydration process according to the method described by Sadeghi-Varkani et al.23. Briefly, the seeds were mixed with distilled water (40 °C) in the ratio of 1:35 (w/v) upon mild stirring for ~ 24 h. Swelled seeds were filtered through 60–175 mesh filters to isolate pure gum from the seed-water mixture. The filtrate was then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was finally freeze-dried and stored at 4 °C for further application.

Preparation of BSM nanoparticles

Sonication of BSM solution

Freeze-dried BSM powder was dissolved in water and gently blended on a stirrer at room temperature to prepare a homogenous 1% (w/v) BSM solution. The prepared solution was sonicated by a 20 kHz ultrasound apparatus (Top Sonic Co., Tehran, Iran) at 300 W for one hour (2s on/ 2s off) at 25 ± 1 °C by applying a 13 mm sonicate horn. GA was added to the primary BSM solution at 20% concentration (based on the dry weight of the mucilage) to prepare the GA-loaded BSM solutions.

Antisolvent precipitation of sonicated solutions

An antisolvent procedure was used to precipitate the BSM nanostructures (BSM NPs) from the sonicated solutions. The BSM or BSM containing GA solutions (50 ml of 2 mg/ml) were thoroughly added to 500 ml ethanol and mixed by a stirrer for 5 min. The mixture was centrifuged at 4500 g for 10 min at 15 °C. The pellet was kept at room temperature (24 h) for ethanol evaporation and then freeze-dried.

Characterization of BSM nanoparticles

GA loading efficiency

The loading efficiency of GA in BSM NPs was evaluated according to the method of Neo et al.29 with slight modifications. Firstly, the λmax of GA was determined by recording the absorption spectrum of GA solution (dissolved in 80% ethanol) at wavelengths of 150–800 nm using a spectrophotometer (UV 2450, Shimadzu, Columbia, USA). The maximum absorbance was obtained at 280 nm. The surface (unencapsulated) GA was separated by dissolving 10 mg of the NPs in 25 ml ethanol (80%) and then filtration. The filtrate absorption was measured at 280 nm to measure the unencapsulated amounts of GA. The GA amount was calculated against a predetermined GA standard calibration curve. Finally, the GA loading efficiency was obtained according to Eq. 1.

Zeta potential

The zeta potential (ξ) of the pure BSM NPs and GA-loaded BSM NPs solutions were measured by a Zeta sizer Nano-ZS (Malvern 103 Instruments, UK) equipped with an MPT-2 pH auto titrator.

Morphology

The morphology and diameter of the BSM NPs and GA-loaded NPs were assessed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Philips XL30, 125 Eindhoven, Netherlands). The samples were gold-coated via a sputter coater (Bal-tec, Capovani Brothers Inc., New York City, NY, USA) before microscopy investigation. The experiment was conducted at a voltage of 20 kV and a working distance of 8–12 mm, with a magnification of × 32,000. The diameter of particles was measured using the Image J software (Maryland, USA) via scanning at least 50 particles.

Chemical analysis

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) assessment was performed to obtain information about the chemical interactions between the BSM matrix and loaded GA. Specimens were mixed with KBr and tableted. The FTIR spectra were recorded in the wavenumber range of 500–4000 cm−1 with FTIR spectrophotometer apparatus (Bruker tensor 27).

Thermal analysis

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of free GA, pure BSM, BSM NPs, and GA-loaded BSM NPs was performed using a TGA instrument (Metler-Toledo, Switzerland). Samples were dried at 50 °C for 1.5 h before measurements. Ten mg of the sample was precisely weighed in a TGA pan and heated from 20 to 500 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min for TGA analysis. Nitrogen was used as the purge gas at a flow rate of 25 ml/min.

Apparent viscosity

The apparent viscosity of pure BSM, BSM NPs, and GA-loaded BSM NPs solutions (5% w/v) was evaluated via a controlled-strain rheometer (Paar Physica Rheometer MCR 301, Anton Paar GmbH, Austria) with a cone-and-plate geometry at room temperature. The viscosity measurements were performed using a 25-mm cone/plate viscometer, and the strain applied during the measurement was set to 1% to ensure accurate readings in the low shear-rate region.

Color analysis

Color parameters (L*, a*, and b*) were determined using a colorimeter (Nippon Denshoku, Japan) to investigate the effect of nanosization on the color of the prepared samples.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed by a SPSS® computer program (version 21.0, IBM, USA). The data were compared via analysis of variance (ANOVA) using Duncan’s test with a 95% confidence.

Results and discussion

GA loading efficiency

Unique properties of BSM such as biodegradability, ease of applicability, flexibility, biocompatibility, accessibility, and nontoxicity have provided its wide range of usage for bioactive delivery applications30. The encapsulation efficiency of GA within BSM NPs was 52.8%, indicating relatively proper entrapment of the bioactive payload in seed gum-based structures. In this context, similarly, Rezaeinia et al.31 produced electrospun nanofibers incorporated with bergamot essential oil using BSM as wall material to enhance the flavor of food matrices. Falahati & Ghoreishi32 introduced BSM as a promising plant mucilage, which is cost-effective and has medicinal properties for designing monolith aerogel-based delivery vehicles. In another study by Choi and Chang27, gallic acid was loaded into a complex of pectic polysaccharide and whey protein concentrate, which served as the coating material, resulting in a significantly higher encapsulation efficiency.

Zeta potential

Zeta potential is an indication of electric potential that exists around particles within a solution. A system of zeta potential value of higher than + 30 mV and lower than − 30 mV typically is potent in hindering the system aggregation and provides a considerable degree of stability33. The zeta potential value of non-treated BSM solution was − 18.8 ± 0.01 mV, which revealed not very good stability of BSM solution against precipitation. According to Pathak et al.34, this negative charge could be related to the COOH domains and amino acid side chains of the mucilage structure. Martin et al.35 reported a zeta potential value of ~ − 25 mV for mucilage solution of the Pereskia aculeate leaves.

For the treated BSM sample that contained nanosized particles, the zeta potential value remarkably reduced to − 29.5 ± 1.0 mV, indicating the higher stability of the produced nanosized system. As stated in the study of O’Brien36, the decrease in the zeta potential of the sonicated mucilage solution could be relevant to the improved stability of polysaccharide chains upon the cavitation process. In this regard, Lago et al.37 reported a reduction in zeta potential value of nano-sized Pereskia aculeata Miller mucilage compared to ordinary emulsions. In another investigation by Sahoo et al.38, the zeta potential of − 45 mV for poly (d,l-lactide-co-glycolide) NPs was reported. The significant negative charge was attributed to the uncapped carboxyl end groups of the polymer located on the particle surface.

Morphology

To study the morphological aspects of specimens, SEM assessments were performed on GA-free and GA-loaded BSM NPs, as depicted in Fig. 1. The applied nanosization process resulted in the creation of semi-aggregated heterogeneous NPs with an average diameter of 190 to 320 nm (Fig. 1a and c). As can be seen in Fig. 1b and d, the addition of 20% GA into the BSM system was accompanied by the production of homogeneous NPs with no aggregation, a pseudo-spherical morphology, a smooth surface, and a smaller size (with an average diameter of 90 nm). These trends could probably result from the presence of GA in the polymeric BSM solution, which led to the significant decrease in the viscosity of the continuous phase, improved the movement of dispersed droplets, higher stability of the GA-BSM system, and therefore generation of smaller and uniform nanocapsules39–41. Similarly, according to a study by Khoshakhlagh et al.42, adding 10% d-limonene to the Alyssum homolocarpum seed gum solution, resulted in pseudo-spherical electrosprayed nanocapsules, featuring reduced particle aggregation and greater uniformity in nanocapsule sizes.

Chemical analysis

The FTIR spectra of pure BSM, unloaded BSM nanostructures, free GA, and GA-loaded nanostructures are represented in Fig. 2. At the pure BSM spectrum, a broad peak was observed around 3210–3654 cm−1, indicating the stretching of free hydroxyl groups of BSM molecules, the hydrogen binding within the mucilage structures, as well as the water molecules connected to the BSM side chains. The absorption peak at 2929 cm−1 was ascribed to C-H groups of the polymer matrix. The characteristic peaks at 1447 and 1645 cm−1 were attributed to COO- symmetric and asymmetric stretching, respectively, corroborating the presence of uronic acid groups in the gum structure. The absorption peak at 1076 cm−1 was relevant to C-O and C–O–C bond stretching43–45.

FTIR spectra of GA, pure BSM, BSM NPs, and GA-loaded BSM NPs.

The spectrum of GA demonstrated characteristic bands around 3000 − 3500 cm−1, attributing to the O–H stretching and hydrogen linkages. The band observed at 1637 cm−1 were corresponding to the carboxylic acid groups, while the intensive band at 1030 cm−1 was relevant to the benzene ring vibrations. The bands at 1461 cm−1 and 1549 cm−1 were assigned to C–O stretching vibrations. The pure GA also revealed the characteristics band at 1352 cm−1, which were due to the bending vibrations of C-H in the ring and hydroxyl group of the phenol alcohol29,46,47.

The FTIR spectrum of BSM NPs revealed that no remarkable change in the chemical structure of samples had occurred during the preparation of NPs. These findings were in accordance with the research conducted by Pandey et al.48. The FTIR spectrum of bioactive-containing NPs was similar to that of the free carrier26. We could not identify peaks of GA in the GA-loaded BSM NPs, as the main absorption peaks of GA were masked by the main bands of the BSM matrix. There was no new bond in GA-loaded BSM NPs spectrum indicating the absence of chemical interaction between the GA and the carrier matrix. So, GA was entrapped physically inside the BSM NPs.

Thermal analysis

As can be observed from the TGA results (Fig. 3), the thermal degradation of all four specimens occurred in three discrete steps. The first degradation step mainly corresponded to moisture evaporation. The last two steps could mainly result from the thermal degradation of side chains and core chain of materials49. The TGA results showed that the free GA specimen demonstrated the least thermal stability. However, its incorporation within BSM nanostructures dramatically enhanced its thermal resistance. When compared to nanostructures of BSM, the primary BSM polymer showed higher thermal decomposition temperatures. This could be ascribed to the increasing surface area and surface –OH groups of BSM NPs after ultrasonication, facilitating the volatilization and the composition of polymer upon heating50. These results were in contrast with the study by Fuensanta et al.51, which revealed that the nanostructures synthetized by the free-radical copolymerization method possessed higher thermostability after nanosization. This demonstrates the effect of various encapsulation/nanosization techniques on the thermostability of the resulting NPs.

TGA thermograms of GA, pure BSM, BSM NPs, and GA-loaded BSM NPs.

Apparent viscosity evaluation

The results of viscosity measurements are represented in Fig. 4. In the case of the pure BSM solution, it was observed that increasing the shear rate reduced the viscosity of the solution in two distinct stages; a sharp primary reduction, which was followed by a secondary gentle diminution in the viscosity. This behavior is typical of shear-thinning solutions that were similarly reported for other gum solutions such as tragacanth52 and quince seed mucilage53. The reduction of viscosity at elevated shear rates could result from the aligning of the polysaccharide chains in the direction of the applied shear. Similar shear thinning behavior was also observed for the BSM NPs and GA-loaded BSM NPs solutions, which indicated that the BSM mucilage could potentially preserve its nature through the nanosization process. However, a remarkable decrease in the viscosity of both BSM NPs and GA-loaded BSM NPs solutions was observed due to the nanosization process. It can be explained by the fact that reducing the size of BSM particles through the fabrication of mucilage nanostructures enhanced the opportunity of active agents of polysaccharide molecules to interact with the surrounding water and elevated its solubility. On the other hand, as the polysaccharide chain length of BSM decreased, the possibility of water entrapment and creating viscosity reduced. Chang et al.54 utilized the sonication-antisolvent method to produce starch nanoparticles and reported a similar decrease in the solution viscosity after the ultrasound treatment and fabrication of nanoparticles. A similar decrease in sonicated gum tragacanth solutions was also reported by Farzi et al.55.

The results revealed a remarkable increase in L* value after the nanosization as compared to the primary BSM. This could be resulted from the reduction in size of polymeric structures following the ultrasonication process. Indeed, the acoustic cavitation led to the reduction in size of the system’s particles, which led to an enhanced transition of light in this sample57. Besides, the surface charge of the produced nanoparticles was another important factor in increasing the brightness of nanoscale particles. The more negatively charged particles, the more L* value58.

Conclusion

Nanoencapsulation of the bioactive molecule GA within BSM NPs was successfully achieved using the developed ultrasonication-antisolvent method. The produced NPs were negatively charged and within the nanometer range. Applying the ultrasonication-antisolvent process to a pure BSM solution resulted in the formation of nonhomogeneous and aggregated NPs, while the incorporation of GA led to a remarkable decrease in viscosity and zeta potential of the system, thereby inducing the fabrication of smaller, round-shaped NPs. No chemical interactions between GA and the BSM matrix were observed following nanoencapsulation. TGA analysis confirmed a significant improvement in the thermal stability of the loaded GA. This study, therefore, presents an efficient strategy for developing nanostructured systems for the encapsulation of aqueous-based bioactive ingredients like GA. However, further in vitro studies, such as cytotoxicity and antioxidant activity assays, and in vivo studies evaluating the bioavailability and therapeutic effects of GA-loaded BSM NPs, are necessary to comprehensively assess their practical application in food and pharmaceutical systems. These future studies will help validate the potential of this nanoencapsulation approach for enhancing the stability, bioactivity, and delivery of bioactive compounds.
