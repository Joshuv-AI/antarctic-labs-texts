Utility of antifilarial antibodies for post-validation surveillance of lymphatic filariasis in Tonga
Authors: Harriet L S Lawford, ‘Ofa Tukia, Joseph Takai, Sarah Sheridan, Selina Ward, Holly Jian, Beatris Mario Martin, Reynold ‘Ofanoa, Stefanie Vaccher, Leanne Robinson, Fiona Angrisano, Colleen L Lau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPost-validation surveillance (PVS) is essential to detect resurgence of lymphatic filariasis (LF) following elimination as a public health problem. Testing for antifilarial antibodies (Ab) alongside antigen (Ag) may increase sensitivity for identifying persistent transmission. We evaluated the utility of Ab-based serosurveillance for PVS in Tonga, which eliminated LF in 2017. A multi-pronged, risk-stratified cross-sectional survey incorporating community, school, an outpatient clinic, and volunteer recruitment was conducted in May-July 2024. Blood samples were tested for Ag using Alere™ Filariasis Test Strips and Ab against Bm14 Ag and Wb123 Ag using multiplex bead assays (MBA). Demographic associations with Ab seropositivity were assessed and pairwise comparisons were used to estimate mean differences in Ab median fluorescence intensity (MFI-bg) between Ag-positives and Ag-negatives. Ag and Ab results were available for 1,727 of 1,787 participants (97%; mean age 29 years). Prevalence was 2% for Ag, 3% for Bm14 Ab, and 5% for Wb123 Ab. Ab positivity was associated with male sex, age ≥ 19 years, and residence in outer islands. Ag-positives had significantly higher mean MFI-bg values for Bm14 Ab (p < 0.001) and Wb123 Ab (p < 0.001) than Ag-negatives. Wb123 Ab demonstrated the highest concordance with Ag status: 71.8% of Ag-positive participants were also Wb123 Ab-positive, while 96.4% of Ag-negative participants were Wb123 Ab-negative. Abs show promise as a complementary PVS tool and, when integrated into MBA platforms, have the potential to offer a cost- and resource-efficient approach to strengthening LF surveillance.Keywords: Lymphatic filariasis, Post-validation surveillance, Tonga, Antibody serosurveillance, Antigen, Multiplex bead assaysSubject terms: Diseases, Immunology, Medical research

Introduction

Lymphatic filariasis (LF) is a mosquito-borne neglected tropical disease (NTD) caused by parasitic nematodes, of which three species are known to infect humans: Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia timori, and B. malayi. W. bancrofti causes approximately 90% of cases globally and is the only species known to cause LF in the Pacific Region1. Stage three larvae are deposited on the human host’s skin where they penetrate the wound left by the mosquito, enter the bloodstream, and migrate to the lymphatic system where they mature into adult worms2. The resultant inflammation causes lymphatic vessel damage and lymphatic dysfunction that predisposes individuals to recurrent bacterial infections. Clinical manifestations can be grouped in three categories: acute attacks (usually associated with local pain, swelling, fever, and chills), lymphoedema/elephantiasis particularly in the lower limbs, and hydrocoele1.

LF is considered one of the leading global causes of permanent or long-term disabilities3. In 1997, the 50th World Health Assembly resolved to eliminate LF as a public health problem by 2020, leading to the inception of the Global Programme to Eliminate LF (GPELF) in 20004. The main strategies of GPELF are two-fold. The first is to interrupt LF transmission by implementing mass drug administration (MDA) with anthelmintics to the entire at-risk population in the 72 endemic countries. The second strategy is to alleviate suffering in the affected population through morbidity management and disability prevention (MMDP).

Global LF elimination goals were not achieved by 2020, and in 2021 the World Health Organization (WHO) Roadmap for Neglected Tropical Diseases 2021–2030 (The Roadmap) was released, providing recommendations to strengthen programmatic responses to NTDs through shared goals and disease-specific targets5. The Roadmap continues to advocate for GPELF’s efforts and aims for 58 of the 72 LF-endemic countries to have eliminated LF as a public health problem by 2030. To date, over 10 billion cumulative anthelmintic treatments have been delivered through MDA programmes, resulting in a 74% decrease in LF infections globally and 21 endemic countries meeting criteria for elimination as a public health problem6.

WHO has a formal process for validating countries as having achieved elimination of LF as a public health problem; countries are required to submit a dossier including (i) survey results demonstrating sustained reduction of LF infection below target thresholds (< 1% LF antigen (Ag)-prevalence in areas where Aedes are the main vector); (ii) plans for MMDP; and (iii) a commitment for post-validation surveillance (PVS). As per the WHO 2nd Edition of the Lymphatic Filariasis Monitoring & Evaluation manual; the primary and minimum aim of PVS is to “ensure that recrudescence has not occurred and infection is still below target thresholds”, and the secondary aim to “verify elimination of transmission”7. Prior to the release of these guidelines, there was limited awareness around the need for PVS following elimination as a public health problem7 and of the 21 countries that have eliminated LF as a public health problem to date, limited PVS activities have been reported. Worryingly, in several countries that have conducted PVS activities, persistent LF transmission has been identified8–12.

It is critical that PVS is conducted to protect the substantial gains that have been made globally towards eliminating LF. Prevalence of filarial Ag remains the main indicator used in programmatic surveys to assess target thresholds for elimination of LF, and Ag positivity is an important indicator of current or recent infection and may indicate ongoing transmission. However, LF surveillance is challenging due to the long period of subclinical infection, which can make infected individuals difficult to identify. This is particularly relevant in post-validation settings where Ag prevalence is low. Therefore, sensitive surveillance tools are needed for PVS to effectively identify persistent LF transmission.

Additional surveillance approaches, including antifilarial antibody (Ab) testing, may provide complementary information on infection status and potential areas of residual transmission that require further investigation13,14. Compared with Ag, antifilarial Ab are often present at higher prevalence15 and may develop before patent infection or antigenemia16. Ab may therefore provide earlier indications of infection than Ag testing alone and offer complementary information to support PVS. Recent work has demonstrated the potential utility of Ab surveillance to complement Ag testing, particularly in low-prevalence settings in the Pacific region17.

However, important evidence gaps remain regarding the operational interpretation of Ab positivity, as Ab responses may reflect historical or recently cleared infection, persistent Ab responses, or potential early infection rather than definitive evidence of active transmission. Furthermore, few studies have evaluated the utility of antifilarial Ab surveillance in post-validation settings, where infection prevalence is low, transmission is often highly focal, and surveillance objectives shift from measuring programme impact to detecting residual transmission and preventing resurgence. Operational evidence is therefore needed to better understand how Ab surveillance may complement Ag testing, inform WHO recommendations, and support targeted, risk-based PVS approaches in elimination settings.

In resource-limited post-validation settings, approaches that allow integrated surveillance approaches may be particularly valuable. Integrated serosurveillance (ISS) using multiplex bead assays (MBA) can detect Ab against multiple diseases simultaneously, including LF, using as little as 10µL of blood from dried blood spot (DBS) samples. Serosurveillance is widely applied to assess population immunity to vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs) and is considered the gold standard for measuring population immunity18. ISS using MBA has been used to measure markers of pathogen infection and population immunity alongside VPDs, and offers a potentially cost-effective means of incorporating multi-pathogen Ab surveillance to complement LF Ag testing15,17,19.

The Kingdom of Tonga is an archipelago of over 170 islands located in the South Pacific, stretching more than 800 km from North to South. Tonga was validated by WHO as having eliminated LF as a public health problem in 2017; the last national MDA was in 2005 (85% coverage) and in 2006, MDA was conducted in the remote northern island of Niuatoputapu (in the Ongo Niuas island group) only (92% coverage).21 In accordance with WHO guidelines, the national MDA programme in Tonga utilised combination therapy with diethylcarbamazine (DEC) and albendazole (ALB)20. Tonga’s last LF surveillance activity was a school-based transmission assessment survey (TAS) conducted in 2015, in which one child tested positive in Niuatoputapu20. No further community surveillance of LF was conducted until a recent PVS survey (Tonga Operational Research for Post-validation Surveillance for Elimination of Lymphatic Filariasis 2024 [TOPELF 2024]) was conducted in 2024, in which 39 Ag-positive individuals were identified21.

This study presents the results of Ab testing that was conducted using MBA on DBS collected during TOPELF 2024. The aim of this study was to evaluate the utility of LF Abs to support PVS. The specific objectives were to (i) describe prevalence of Wb123 and Bm14 Abs, (ii) explore demographic and geographic associations for Ab positivity, (iii) evaluate geographic clustering of Ab-positive participants, and (iv) assess concordance between Ab and Ag status. This study provides one of the first evaluations of antifilarial Ab surveillance within a WHO-validated elimination setting in the Pacific. By assessing Ab prevalence, geographic clustering, and concordance with Ag status, we aimed to generate operational evidence on how Ab testing may complement existing PVS approaches and support more targeted surveillance activities.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the National Health Ethics and Research Committee of the Tonga Ministry of Health (MOH) (Ref#20240129) on 22 February 2024 and ratified by The University of Queensland’s Human Research Ethics Committee (Project#2024/HE000493) on 12 March 2024. Processes for obtaining informed consent have been described19. Briefly, written informed consent was obtained from adult participants prior to enrolment, and written parent/guardian consent was obtained for participants aged < 18 years. Participants were advised that they could withdraw consent at any time without prejudice. Written information, consent forms, and questionnaires were provided and administered by local field teams in Tongan or English using culturally appropriate procedures. All methods were performed in accordance with the approved ethical guidelines and regulations.

Study design and target sample size

The study design has been described previously21. Briefly, a four-pronged study was designed including surveys of (i) communities, (ii) primary schools, (iii) high schools (including boarding schools), and (iv) a diabetes outpatient clinic at Vaiola Hospital, the main tertiary referral hospital in Tongatapu. Individuals who approached the study team requesting to be tested were included as ‘volunteers’. Participants ≥ 5 years old were recruited from three island groups: Tongatapu, Ha’apai, and Ongo Niuas. Six primary schools that recorded Ag-positive children in 2011 TAS-2 were considered ‘high-risk’, and the communities where these schools were located considered ‘high-risk’ communities. An additional ‘high-risk’ community and its primary school were selected due to high Ag prevalence recorded in the 2004 mid-term (B) survey. Four primary schools that recorded no Ag-positive children in 2011 TAS-2, 2014 TAS-3, and communities that recorded no Ag-positives in the pre-stop MDA (C) survey in 2006, were selected as ‘low-risk’ sites and were considered reference populations against which LF Ag and Ab prevalence from ‘high-risk’ sites could be compared.

The published protocol details participant selection and target sample size19. Following consultation with the Tonga MOH, Vaipoa community (n = 75 participants) was included as, given the difficulty in accessing the Ongo Niuas island group, Tonga MOH felt it pertinent to test all communities on the island. This brought the total target sample size to 1,955. Methods for household selection and data collection have been described previously21.

Sample collection

Electronic questionnaires were administered using EpiCollect5 software (https://five.epicollect.net/; V76.1.0) on smartphones. Questionnaires collected data on demographics (age, sex, country/place of birth, village of residence, occupation) and travel history in the past 12 months (international and within Tonga). GPS coordinates of households and schools were recorded using smartphones where possible. Enrolled participants had 300–400 µL of blood collected by finger prick into heparin-coated BD Microtainer® Blood Collection Tubes for Ag testing using Alere™ Filariasis Test Strips (FTS) (Abbott, Scarborough, ME), to make DBS using Cellabs Tropbio Filter Paper Blood Collection Disks™ (all participants), and to make thick blood films on slides for parasitological identification of microfilariae (Mf) (Ag-positive participants only).

Multiplex bead assays

DBS were eluted into 96-well plates and then diluted to a final concentration of 1:400 using standard MBA protocols, as previously described22,23. All samples were tested for antifilarial Ab to Bm14 and Wb123 using MBA. Sample plates were read on a Bio-Plex 200 instrument (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA)24. For internal quality control purposes, four controls were used in each assay: a buffer blank containing the assay buffer only (to subtract any background noise), two pools of reference sera from known Ab-positives, and finally a negative control serum with known negative LF status. Seropositivity thresholds for each antigen were defined using the mean plus three standard deviations of the median fluorescence intensity-background (MFI-bg) values from a panel of 81 individuals from non-endemic regions, consistent with standard United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (US CDC) methodologies23. MFI-bg values were used, with pre-defined cut-offs applied to classify seropositivity.

Definition of subgroups used in the analysis

Participants were considered (i) Ag-positive if they had a positive FTS result, (ii) Ab-positive if they were seropositive to at least one Ab, and (iii) dual-positive if they were seropositive to both Bm14 Ab and Wb123 Ab.

Statistical analysis

Data was analysed using Stata statistical software (StataCorp, Version 18.0, College Station, TX). Summary statistics with 95% logit-transformed confidence intervals (95% CI) for Ag and Ab prevalence were calculated by setting (community, primary school, high school, clinic, volunteers) and island group (Tongatapu, Ha’apai, Ongo Niuas) using the “proportion” command.

Differences in demographic characteristics by settings were tested using univariate logistic regression using the “logistic” command. Crude associations between demographic data and Ab positivity were assessed using univariate logistic regression. All multivariable models were adjusted for sex (males vs. females), age group (< 19 years vs. ≥ 19 years), and island group (Tongatapu, Ha’apai, and Ongo Niuas). These variables were selected a priori as known or suspected risk factors for LF transmission and because they were complete across the dataset. Village-level clustering was not included in the multivariable models due to small sample sizes within some villages, which limited model stability.

Pairwise comparisons were conducted to calculate mean difference in Bm14 Ab and Wb123 Ab MFI-bg in Ag-positive vs. Ag-negative individuals. Concordance between Ab and Ag status was assessed by calculating the proportion of Ab-positive individuals among Ag-positive participants and the proportion of Ab-negative individuals among Ag-negative participants, using Ag status as an operational comparator rather than a definitive reference standard.

Geographic clustering at the island-group and village levels were assessed using intra-cluster correlation coefficient (ICC) estimated using a multi-level logistic regression model (Stata commands “melogit” and “estat icc”) adjusting for age and sex. Intra-cluster correlation assumes that observations in a cluster are more similar (homogenous) than observations outside a cluster, therefore observations within a cluster are correlated and those outside a cluster are independent25. ICC values range from 0 to 1 and measure how similar observations are; a higher ICC indicates that the outcome measure is more homogeneous (there is higher degree of clustering at that level). Geographic information systems software ArcGIS Pro (v10.7.1, Environmental Systems Research Institute, Redlands, CA) was used to manage spatial data and produce maps.

Results

Study population

Fieldwork for TOPELF 2024 was conducted in two stages. The first stage took place from 9 May to 6 June 2024 (in Tongatapu and Ha’apai) and the second stage from 11 to 19 July 2024 (in Ha’apai and Ongo Niuas). Across both stages of fieldwork, a total of 1,787 (92.3% of the target sample size of 1,955) participants were enrolled from Tongatapu, Ha’apai, and Ongo Niuas, encompassing 12 communities (n = 844), 11 primary schools (n = 416), five high schools (n = 247), a diabetes outpatient clinic (n = 220), and volunteers (n = 60). The mean participant age was 29.5 years (age range: 5–89 years) and 46% were male (Table 3). Valid Ab results were available for 1,768 (98.9%) participants.

Antifilarial antibody seroprevalence estimates

Overall, 1,727 (97%) had both FTS and Ab results (mean age 29 years; range 5–89); more participants were positive for at least one Ab (100/1727) than were Ag-positive (39/1727) (χ²=343.90, p < 0.001). The highest Ab prevalence was seen in the community survey; 68 (8.1%; 95% CI: 6.4–10.1) participants were Wb123 Ab-positive; 46 (5.5%; 95% CI: 4.1–7.2) were Bm14 Ab-positive; and 37 (4.4%; 95% CI: 3.2-6.0) were dual-positive. Most Ab-positives were found in community members from ‘O’ua (Wb123 Ab = 46.4%; Bm14 Ab = 28.6%; dual-positive = 26.8%). Among primary school students, 10 (2.4%; 95% CI: 1.3–4.5) were Wb123 Ab-positive, five (1.2%; 95% CI: 0.5–2.9) were Bm14 Ab-positive, and four (1.0%; 95% CI: 0.4–2.6) were dual-positive. The highest number of Ab-positives among primary school students were among those from ‘O’ua (Wb123 Ab = 71.4%; Bm14 Ab = 57.1%; dual-positive = 42.9%). Five (2.1%; 95% CI: 0.9–4.9) high school students were Wb123 Ab-positive and two (0.8%; 95% CI:0.2–3.2) were Bm14 Ab-positive, with no high school students testing dual-positive. Three (1.4%; 95% CI: 0.5–4.3) participants from the diabetes clinic and two (3.4%; 95% CI: 0.9–12.6) volunteers were Wb123 Ab-positive (Table 1 and Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study provides new evidence supporting the potential role of Ab testing to complement Ag testing for LF surveillance in a post-validation setting in Tonga. While previous studies have explored antifilarial Ab responses in endemic and post-MDA settings, evidence from countries that have already achieved elimination of LF as a public health problem remains limited. Our earlier work identified persistent LF transmission in Tonga based on Ag testing alone;22 the present findings extend this evidence by demonstrating the operational utility of Ab surveillance in a validated elimination setting. Specifically, Ab testing identified a larger number of individuals with LF seromarkers, highlighted geographic clustering of infection signals, and identified populations that may warrant enhanced follow-up surveillance and investigation.

As outlined in the WHO 2nd Edition of the Lymphatic Filariasis Monitoring & Evaluation manual, individuals testing Ab-positive can be assumed to have been exposed, infected, or previously infected; however, Ab positivity may not indicate ongoing transmission26. Nonetheless, Ab signals in young children may represent “recent exposure and suspected recent transmission”7. In this study, 22 of the 100 Ab-positive participants were born post-MDA cessation (aged < 19 years), including 13 children aged <12 years who were born well after MDA had ended. Six of the 13 Ab-positives aged <12 years were from ‘O’ua, a community where high Ag prevalence was also identified[21]. Although antifilarial Ab can persist for years following exposure or treatment, and some cross-reactivity with other helminths has been reported 27, currently available antifilarial Ab assays are not perfectly specific so a small number of Ab-positive children may be expected in the absence of recent transmission[28]. However, interpretation of Ab positivity in this study is strengthened by the observed geographic clustering of Ab-positive individuals and concordance with our previously reported Ag and Mf findings. Furthermore, antifilarial Ab responses may be detectable prior to the development of antigenemia, providing complementary information on potential recent exposure that may not be identified through Ag testing alone [28]. Collectively, these findings suggest that Ab positivity in younger children may represent a useful surveillance signal for identifying communities where residual transmission warrants intensified surveillance and follow-up. More broadly, they highlight the importance of interpreting Ab results alongside Ag findings, geographic clustering, and other epidemiological indicators in post-validation settings, given that the operational utility of Ab surveillance as a standalone tool remains insufficiently defined.

21 Ab-positive participants were identified across all three island groups, suggesting that LF infection and/or transmission could be geographically widespread throughout Tonga, even if at low levels. As our survey only targeted populations from three of the five island groups, our results further emphasise the need for continued and expanded LF surveillance to better understand the geographic extent of LF infection and/or transmission in Tonga. All Ab-positive individuals identified were from high-risk communities or schools, demonstrating the value of our study design using historical data to inform targeted surveillance approaches.

Older males and residents of outer islands were also identified as groups at higher risk of LF infection, indicating that targeted surveillance among these populations may improve the efficiency and effectiveness of PVS. Similar patterns were found for Ag positivity22, suggesting that streamlined targeted PVS approaches incorporating both Ag and Ab surveillance may be useful in Tonga and potentially applicable to other Pacific settings. We observed high ICC at the village level in this study, likely reflecting clustering of LF infection, as has been reported in other countries and territories in the Pacific region29,30.

The substantial village-level clustering observed in this study suggests that antifilarial Ab surveillance may support more targeted and risk-based PVS approaches. Under the WHO LF Monitoring and Evaluation 2nd Edition framework, “further investigation is warranted in communities in which a signal is detected to determine whether the signal represents ongoing transmission”;7 thus, Ab-positive signals may help prioritise areas for enhanced surveillance, repeat investigations, or integration with additional surveillance activities. While current WHO recommendations focus on treating households of Ag-positive individuals, our findings suggest that extending this approach to the village level could help prevent resurgence in post-elimination settings. Mayfield et al. demonstrated the successful use of reactive case detection, a form of snowball sampling, to identify and target locations of LF infection in Samoa31. A similar approach may therefore have utility in PVS settings.

Although definitive operational Ab thresholds for triggering programmatic action remain to be established, persistent or geographically clustered Ab positivity, particularly among younger age groups born following MDA cessation, may represent useful signals for further investigation in post-validation settings. For example, areas with persistently low Ab positivity may potentially be prioritised for reduced surveillance intensity, whereas areas with elevated or geographically clustered Ab positivity may warrant ongoing monitoring and further investigation. Future studies incorporating formal spatial hotspot analyses and longitudinal follow-up may help refine operational thresholds and decision rules for Ab-based PVS.

Antifilarial Ab demonstrated high concordance with Ag-negative participants in previous studies from Samoa17 and American Samoa32. We similarly observed high concordance with Ag-negative participants, although concordance with Ag-positive participants was lower in our study. Ag-positive participants had significantly higher mean MFI-bg values for Bm14 Ab and Wb123 Ab compared with Ag-negative participants, suggesting that MFI-bg may help distinguish Ag-positive individuals. In this context, Ab responses alone, particularly high MFI-bg values, may have potential to identify Ag-positive individuals; however, this requires further validation and a better understanding of Ab dynamics. Wb123 emerged as the most informative Ab marker, demonstrating the highest concordance with Ag positivity and the strongest overall agreement relative to Bm14 Ab or dual-positivity. While all Ab markers demonstrated high concordance with Ag-negative participants (> 96%), the higher concordance observed for Bm14 Ab and dual-positivity was accompanied by lower agreement with Ag-positive individuals.

Given that FTS is an imperfect reference standard in low-prevalence settings, Ab-positive/Ag-negative individuals may not necessarily represent false positives, but could instead reflect residual circulating Abs, early pre-patent or pre-antigenaemic infection, or infections not detected by Ag testing. These findings suggest that Wb123 Ab may have utility as a sensitive surveillance marker for PVS, whereas dual-positivity approaches may be useful in settings where greater specificity or stronger evidence of exposure is desired. Overall, these findings further support the potential programmatic utility of Ab testing for identifying areas that may warrant enhanced or reduced surveillance intensity.

Beyond the specific findings from Tonga, this study advances the evidence base for LF PVS by moving the discussion of Ab surveillance from theoretical utility to operational application. Our results demonstrate how Ab testing can be incorporated into risk-based surveillance frameworks to identify populations and geographic areas that may warrant enhanced follow-up surveillance and investigation in post-validation settings. Furthermore, integrated multiplex serosurveillance platforms offer important operational advantages for PVS by providing a cost-efficient and scalable complement to Ag testing, particularly where integrated multi-pathogen surveillance systems are already established. Previous work has highlighted several limitations of rapid Ag tests for LF surveillance, including high unit cost, short shelf life, and logistical challenges in reaching remote island communities7. Given current global funding constraints for NTD programmes and the need for sustainable PVS approaches, the ability to integrate LF testing into serosurveys for other diseases (such as VPDs) provides a compelling opportunity. DBS collection is minimally invasive, low-cost, and durable under appropriate storage conditions. Furthermore, MBA can measure Ab responses to nearly 100 pathogen-specific Ag simultaneously from a single sample, enabling LF surveillance to be integrated alongside VPDs and other priority diseases while maximising resources and strengthening national surveillance systems.

Overall, our findings have several important implications for LF PVS programmes. First, Ab surveillance may provide complementary information to Ag testing by identifying geographic clustering and exposure patterns in low-prevalence settings where Ag-positive individuals are uncommon. Second, integration of Ab testing into MBA platforms may support more operationally efficient surveillance through simultaneous assessment of multiple pathogens from a single blood sample. However, interpretation of Ab positivity remains challenging due to imperfect assay specificity, persistent Ab responses, and uncertainty regarding operational thresholds for programmatic action. As such, Ab surveillance should currently be interpreted as a complementary, risk-based surveillance approach intended to identify areas warranting further investigation, rather than as a standalone indicator of ongoing transmission. Further longitudinal and operational research is needed to establish validated decision-making frameworks capable of distinguishing meaningful transmission signals in low-prevalence and post-validation settings.

This study has several limitations. Interpretation of Ab positivity remains challenging, because currently available surveillance tools cannot definitively determine whether seropositive individuals have active, historical, or recently resolved LF infection. Furthermore, interpretation of Ab signals is complicated by persistent Ab responses, potential assay cross-reactivity, and uncertainty regarding operational thresholds for programmatic action. This study utilised a multi-pronged, risk-stratified design incorporating community surveys, schools, outpatient clinic attendees, and self-presenting volunteers to maximise the potential to detect LF infections; however, the study was not designed as a population-representative survey, and prevalence estimates should not be interpreted as such. In addition, the cross-sectional design restricts temporal interpretation of transmission dynamics. As such, Ab testing should not be interpreted as a standalone confirmatory diagnostic approach for active LF transmission, but rather as a complementary surveillance tool intended to identify geographic clustering, elevated-risk populations, or areas warranting further surveillance and investigation. Interpretation of Ab signals is therefore likely to require integration with epidemiological context, spatial clustering patterns, and additional parasitological or entomological indicators. Further work is needed to establish validated operational thresholds and decision-making frameworks capable of distinguishing meaningful transmission signals from Ab positivity related to historical infection, resolved infection, or assay cross-reactivity. Standardisation of seropositivity cut-off values remains a key priority to ensure comparability across studies and laboratories.

Conclusion

Antifilarial Ab surveillance shows promise as a complementary tool for PVS, offering a scalable approach to support LF elimination efforts, particularly in the face of funding shortages for NTD monitoring and evaluation. We have shown that MBA platforms can enable concurrent assessment of LF Ab prevalence and geographic clustering, facilitating multi-pathogen serosurveillance and identification of populations or regions that may warrant enhanced follow-up. However, findings should be interpreted within the context of the multi-pronged study design, which included participants from communities, schools, an outpatient clinic, and volunteers, rather than as fully population-representative prevalence estimates.

While important challenges remain regarding interpretation of Ab positivity and the establishment of operational thresholds for programmatic action, Ab testing may provide valuable additional information to support risk-based surveillance approaches in low-prevalence and post-validation settings. Integration of Ab testing into MBA platforms offers a practical opportunity to strengthen and sustain surveillance through multi-pathogen serosurveillance activities. Further longitudinal and operational research is needed to establish validated decision-making frameworks and determine how Ab signals can best be incorporated into routine PVS activities. Collectively, our findings contribute to the growing evidence base supporting the use of Ab surveillance to sustain LF elimination gains and strengthen LF PVS in the Pacific.
