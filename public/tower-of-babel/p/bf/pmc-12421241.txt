Measuring alignment between the ADRC UDS data elements, FDA, and EHR data standards
Authors: Zhan Wang, Kayla Torres, Helen Foster, Gary Walker, Maryam Y Garza, Amy Palmer, Elizabeth C LeRoy, Rhonda Facile, Melissa Kirwin, Meredith N Zozus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWe compared and measured alignment between the Health Level Seven (HL7) Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) standard used by electronic health records (EHRs), the Clinical Data Interchange Standards Consortium (CDISC) standards used by industry, and the Uniform Data Set (UDS) used by the Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers (ADRCs).METHODSThe ADRC UDS, consisting of 5959 data elements across eleven packets, was mapped to FHIR and CDISC standards by two independent mappers, with discrepancies adjudicated by experts.RESULTSForty‐five percent of the 5959 UDS data elements mapped to the FHIR standard, indicating possible electronic obtainment from EHRs. Ninety‐four percent mapped to the CDISC standards, demonstrating high compatibility with industry standards.DISCUSSIONThe study highlights the feasibility of harmonizing ADRC data with industry and clinical standards. CDISC demonstrated superior alignment with ADRC UDS data, whereas FHIR showed potential for improvement through resource maturation and enhanced standardization.Highlights Forty‐five percent of Alzheimer's Disease Research Center Uniform Data Set (ADRC UDS) data elements could be mapped to Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR), indicating potential electronic health records (EHRs) extraction.Ninety‐four percent of ADRC UDS data elements could be mapped to Clinical Data Interchange Standards Consortium (CDISC) Study Data Tabulation Model (SDTM), showing high industry compatibility.Identified areas for improving data standards harmonization in Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) research.Systematic mapping method aligns ADRC UDS with Health Level Seven (HL7) FHIR and CDISC SDTM standards.Results support feasibility of data sharing across ADRC research, EHRs, and industry. Keywords: Alzheimer's disease and related dementias research, Clinical Data Interchange Standards Consortium, data reuse, data standard, FHIR

BACKGROUND

The last two decades have seen increased emphasis on data sharing and reuse, the most recent of which were the National Institutes of Health (NIH) data management and sharing policy that went into effect in January of 2023 1 and the interest in reuse of real‐world data (RWD) in decision‐making of regulated therapeutics. 2 , 3 , 4 Standards facilitate reuse of shared data through conveying data definition and decreasing the effort to pool data from different studies, settings, or organizations. Realizing these benefits when different standards exist requires assessing the equivalence of the information content of the different standards. Benefits of doing so include facilitating (1) reuse of data from electronic health records (EHRs) in research, (2) combining relevant data across studies, and (3) use of registry and EHR data to better monitor the safety of marketed therapeutics. Experience shows that RWD sources such as EHRs and longitudinal observational data collection may better foreshadow effectiveness compared to reliance on data collected in the confines of tightly controlled clinical trials. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9

Research on Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) is undertaken in multiple settings, each with specific data standards. The Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (ADRC) 10 program has collected longitudinal data annually on patients with Alzheimer's disease (AD) for over 30 years. Today, these data include clinical data, cognitive assessments, imaging, genetic information, and biological samples. The clinical data adhere to the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) Uniform Data Set (UDS). 11 , 12 By providing a standardized, comprehensive dataset across multiple centers, the UDS enables researchers to identify subtle phenotypic differences among participants, potentially leading to more targeted therapeutic approaches. This standardization also facilitates the correlation of clinical data with genetic and biomarker information, paving the way for the development of personalized treatment strategies. Aducanumab and lecanemab are the two new therapeutics to slow the progression of AD. 13 , 14 , 15 Both therapeutics have post‐marketing safety commitments from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA). 16 , 17 Research is needed to monitor the safety of marketed therapeutics and develop new and improved therapeutics. As such, the UDS becomes valuable for needs such as assessing post marketing safety, disparities, and heterogeneity of treatment effect. Furthermore, the ability to track longitudinal changes in cognitive function and disease progression allows for more precise patient stratification and tailored interventions. At the same time, efficiency and accuracy of longitudinal data acquisition may be increased by extracting data directly from EHRs, for which standards are also required.

The Health Level Seven (HL7) Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) standard is designed primarily for interoperability in health care settings, facilitating the exchange of clinical data between different systems and organizations. It represents the structure and content of data as they exist in EHRs. 18 The Clinical Data Interchange Standards Consortium's (CDISC) Study Data Tabulation Model (SDTM) is designed specifically to represent clinical research data in a standardized format for regulatory submissions and data analysis, with pharmaceutical industry data adhering to these submission standards as required by the FDA. For Alzheimer's research, CDISC provides an Alzheimer's Therapeutic Area User Guide (TAUG) to further support the use of SDTM in this specific domain. 19 HL7 FHIR and CDISC SDTM serve distinct but complementary purposes in the health care and research ecosystems. The harmonization of these standards is crucial for potentially reducing the cost and time required for clinical trials while increasing their relevance to real‐world clinical practice.

Through previous studies, we now understand the feasibility and generalizability of an FHIR‐based eSource solution for the direct extraction of EHR data in clinical research. 20 , 21 The differences in these standards and their potential impact on reuse in ADRC research have not been assessed previously. Mapping and comparing NACC UDS to the FHIR and CDISC standards is essential for achieving interoperability, integration, and standardization in ADRC research. It ensures that valuable research data can be effectively shared and utilized, improving both research outcomes and patient care. Understanding the differences and purposes of each standard helps in creating accurate mappings and maintaining data integrity across various platforms and use cases. Doing so was the objective of this work. Identifying data elements in common across these standards will inform reuse of data across traditional boundaries, such as using EHR data for research, and using existing data to assess post‐market safety and possibly efficacy toward slowing cognitive decline.

METHODS

The UDS specifies the variables of interest for ADRD research, serving as a comprehensive set of data elements crucial for ADRD studies. By mapping from UDS to the other standards, we can assess how well existing EHR data and industry clinical trial data can fulfill the needs of ADRD research.

Eleven packets in the ADRC UDS (Table 1) were mapped to the FHIR Release 4 (R4) and CDISC SDTM standards v3.2. Data elements were not included in the total count if they were not typically part of EHR data collection; thus, the UDS Form Z1X: Form Checklist and any headers, footers, and form completion status fields were excluded from the mapping. Two mappers independently performed the FHIR and CDISC mappings, followed by an adjudication process.

Mappers identified the appropriate FHIR resource(s) and CDISC domain(s) and variable(s) within the domain(s) for each UDS data element. First, UDS data elements are assigned into two groups: “Yes” (mappable) or “No” (not mappable) to an FHIR resource and a CDISC domain, respectively. If a UDS data element was assigned to a “Yes” (FHIR and/or CDISC), each UDS data element was mapped to the appropriate FHIR resource field or CDISC domain variable. Discrepancies could happen at either of the two mapping levels (domain vs field levels). Inter‐rater reliability was calculated by dividing the total number of data elements (both “Yes” and “No”) by the total number of the data elements mapped in the same way by the two mappers.

The results of the FHIR and CDISC mappings, and any discrepancies, were reviewed and adjudicated by experts in the FHIR and CDISC standards, respectively (one expert for each standard who had at least 5 years of experience working with each standard and had completed certified training programs). One discrepancy adjudication example from UDS Form D2: Clinician‐assessed Medical Conditions is shown in Table 2, where there was disagreement in how to map the data element “Arthritis Region affected” to CDISC. The mapping rate of a packet was calculated by dividing the number of “Yes” for FHIR or CDISC by the total number of the data elements in the packet. Mapping distribution percentage was calculated by dividing the number of “Yes” for each FHIR resource or CDISC domain by the total number of “Yes” for FHIR or CDISC.

RESULTS

A UDS packet is a structured collection of forms that together define the set of data elements to be collected during a specific type of participant visit within the ADRC program. Each packet is tailored to a particular visit context, such as an initial visit packet (IVP), follow‐up visit packet (FVP), telephone initial packet (TIP), or telephone follow‐up packet (TFP), and specifies which forms are required or optional for that visit type. The UDS version 3 packets share substantial overlap, particularly in core domains such as demographics, family history, medications, functional status, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and clinician diagnosis (e.g., A1, A2, A3, A4, B4, B5, B6, B7, B9, D1, D2). The IVP packet is the most comprehensive, including full physical and neurological exams and the complete in‐person neuropsychological battery, whereas FVP is designed for follow‐up and omits subject health history, and TIP and TFP are streamlined for telephone administration by omitting physical exams (B1 and B1a), with TFP also omitting neurological examination findings (B8). The overall overlap among these packets is high, with ≈90% of data elements being shared across all four, and the remaining differences largely attributable to the method of administration and visit type requirements. Given this high sharing rate, the comprehensive nature of the IVP, and the fact that UDS4 is being positioned as a unified collection system that consolidates previous packet‐specific variations, we provide inter‐rater reliability and mapping results for each UDS package, as well as a form‐by‐form breakdown of the mappings to FHIR and CDISC using UDS3 IVP and UDS4 as representative paradigms. ,

DISCUSSION

We employed a systematic mapping method to align the ADRC UDS with the HL7 FHIR and CDISC standards. Subsequently, we evaluated the mapping results to analyze the alignment between ADRC UDS and HL7 FHIR and between ADRC UDS and CDISC. Fewer of the ADRC UDS form fields corresponded to data elements in FHIR compared to CDISC (45% vs 94%). The practical implication is that 45% or less of the UDS data could be obtained from health care facility EHRs. Today, FHIR would not be capable of serving as a comprehensive data exchange standard to ferry the ADRC dataset for secondary use. In comparison, the CDISC standard was able to represent up to 94% of the UDS dataset with aggregation or reduced granularity. Several factors contributed to these differences across different sections of the UDS packets.

First, mapping questionnaires used to characterize participants' feelings or symptoms (e.g., “Are you basically satisfied with your life?”) presents challenges for FHIR. These questionnaires are often study specific and not typically part of EHR systems. Unless they are standardized and integrated within the EHR, capturing these data is not feasible. However, if the questionnaires are built into the EHR, the Questionnaire and QuestionnaireResponse resources can appropriately map this information. The Questionnaire resource contains metadata (the actual question), whereas the QuestionnaireResponse resource includes participant responses.

Second, the uncertainty regarding the availability of FHIR resources due to low maturity level led to disagreements between the two mappers. Ultimately, the FHIR mapping results rely only on the FHIR resources with a maturity level of 3 or greater. The lack of a mature Consent, ResearchSubject and ResearchStudy resource means that many UDS elements related to study participation, visit tracking, and subject identifiers cannot be mapped to FHIR.

In addition, sites vary in whether research participants are actual patients at the facility, which affects the data available in the EHR. Furthermore, the care relationship with a participant impacts the type and extent of data retrievable from the EHR. This issue is particularly pronounced if sites do not document research visits within the EHR, thereby limiting the available data for mapping. Finally, the quality of EHR data itself has long been a concern. Poor data quality in the mapped EHR fields can significantly hinder accurate data capture. It is essential for studies to measure data quality at each site to ensure that mapped data are usable data.

CDISC was designed to hold data from clinical research case report forms (CRFs) regardless of the therapeutic area or type of study, although admittedly, CDISC was initially designed for clinical trials. The design, which emphasized exact representation of data from study CRFs, resulted in a higher mapping rate of 94%. CDISC includes standard questionnaires that can be accessed from the CDISC controlled terminology, for example, the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) section in UDS Form B4. 22 For the extended questions or the appendix questions that are not a part of the standard questionnaire, custom questionnaire codes need to be created and appended to the standard questionnaire, such as in the NACC FTLD BEHAVIOR & LANGUAGE DOMAINS section.

A key consideration in mapping UDS data elements to FHIR and CDISC standards is the use of standardized terminologies and controlled vocabularies, which underpins semantic interoperability. FHIR relies on external, well‐established code systems such as ICD (International Classification of Diseases) and SNOMED CT (Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine—Clinical Terms) for representing clinical concepts, particularly in fields like Condition.code. In contrast, CDISC employs its own controlled terminology, developed and maintained in collaboration with the National Cancer Institute's Enterprise Vocabulary Services (NCI‐EVS), to ensure consistency and regulatory compliance across clinical research datasets. This distinction has important implications for harmonization, as mapping between UDS, FHIR, and CDISC often involves not only aligning data models but also reconciling differences in the underlying terminologies used to encode clinical information.

The scope and breadth of FHIR and CDISC also influence their mapping performance and research utility. CDISC's higher mapping rates reflect both its maturity as a research‐focused standard and its explicit design for comprehensive clinical research and regulatory submissions, which closely mirrors the structure and data types of UDS. In contrast, FHIR is designed primarily for real‐time health care data exchange and EHR interoperability, resulting in more limited coverage of specialized research forms, neuropsychological instruments, and nuanced clinical assessments. The greater number of data elements and domains in CDISC provides more opportunities for direct matches, whereas FHIR's coverage is strongest for standardized clinical data such as demographics, medications, and diagnoses. Both standards are evolving to address emerging needs: for example, CDISC has expanded to include domains and supplemental qualifiers for monoclonal antibody therapies and blood‐ or tissue‐based biomarkers relevant to AD, whereas FHIR supports laboratory and biomarker data through resources such as Observation, although coverage of novel biomarkers may lag behind research requirements. Of note, the commonality in unmapped elements across both standards can be attributed to the specialized nature of registry‐specific identifiers and administrative data, which often fall outside the primary design purposes of FHIR and CDISC.

Although the mappings were considered “matched,” a subset involved differences in granularity or required data derivation. For example, UDS elements such as “Has subject smoked more than 100 cigarettes in his/her life?” lack a direct CDISC variable but were mapped using derived fields (e.g., Substanse Use domain variables like SUDOSFRQ and SUENDAT). Similarly, CDISC's use of Supplemental Qualifiers (SuppQual) allowed representation of nuanced UDS responses (e.g., “Recent/Active” vs “Remote/Inactive” medical conditions) through combinations of variables like MHOCCUR and MHENRTPT. These mappings preserved semantic equivalence but occasionally aggregated or inferred data, meaning the mapping rate includes both one‐to‐one matches and cases where indirect mapping was necessary. For FHIR, granularity mismatches were more pronounced: EHR‐derived data (e.g., “loss of consciousness > 30s”) often lacked the specificity required by UDS (e.g., “30 s to 5 min” vs “ >5 min”), resulting in mappings that retained conceptual alignment but not precise response granularity.

The other case in UDS Form A5: Subject Health History: “If any of the conditions still require active management and/or medications, please select “Recent/Active.”” The available responses for this item are: Absent, Recent/Active, Remote/Inactive, and Unknown. For FHIR mapping, if the relevant clinical condition code (International Classification of Diseases (ICD) or Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine—Clinical Terms (SNOMED CT)) was not found in the participant's EHR data code, the response was “Absent.” If the condition code was present, we then compared the temporal attributes Condition.onset[x] and Condition.abatement[x] to the date of the visit. If Condition.onset[x] indicated the condition began before or during the visit and Condition.abatement[x] was not present or indicated the condition was ongoing at the time of the visit, the response was “Recent/Active.” Conversely, if Condition.abatement[x] indicated that the condition had resolved prior to the visit, the response was “Remote/Inactive.” A similar strategy was used for CDISC mapping. If MHOCCUR (Medical History Event Occurrence: used when the occurrence of specific medical history conditions is solicited, to indicate whether or not [“Y”/“N”] a medical condition) is “N,” then the response was absent. If MHOCCUR is “Y”, if MHENRTPT (Medical History End Relative to Reference Time Point: identifies the end of the event as being before or after the reference time point defined by variable MHENTPT) is “ONGOING,” then the response was active. If MHENRTPT is “BEFORE,” then the response was inactive. Although this is not direct mapping, both FHIR and CDISC can cover the data element using computer algorithms with determined statements and multiple related fields. Thus, although mapping rate reflects structural compatibility, we should note that some mappings involve logical derivation and granularity mismatches rather than exact equivalence.

The longitudinal nature of the UDS is a key strength, enabling nuanced tracking of disease progression and treatment response over time. However, aligning external data standards such as FHIR and CDISC with the temporal structure of UDS introduces notable challenges. For example, although UDS visits are typically conducted annually, CDISC datasets may use 18‐month intervals, and EHR‐derived FHIR® data can be recorded at irregular or more frequent intervals. This temporal misalignment complicates direct mapping and may require additional data processing, such as biomarker results, which may be collected or reported asynchronously relative to clinical visits and present additional complexity when integrating longitudinal datasets. Although a comprehensive analysis of temporal harmonization is beyond the scope of this manuscript, we acknowledge that temporal resolution is a critical consideration for real‐world data integration and may impact both data quality and analytic workflows.

Another limitation of this study is the inherent subjectivity and complexity involved in the mapping process. Although the use of two independent reviewers and an adjudication process adds rigor, mapping between UDS, FHIR, and CDISC standards was shaped by the reviewers’ interpretations, specific research objectives, and the context of each data element. The qualifications of the reviewers, although grounded in experience with their respective standards, may not encompass the full breadth of clinical, technical, and research expertise required for all possible ADRD use cases. Mapping outcomes can vary depending on expertise, local implementation, and evolving standards, and should be continually revisited as standards mature and research needs evolve.
