Wedge-less V-Shaped Distal Femoral Osteotomy Through Lateral Approach to Correct Valgus Malalignment
Authors: Sheetal Gupta, Parvind Singh, Rahul Verma, Abhishek Kulkarni, Gaurav Jindal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoronal-plane deformities of the knee are prevalent skeletal abnormalities in the adolescent population. Although valgus alignment is observed less frequently than varus alignment, it predisposes patients to several pathologic sequelae, including cartilage lesions, as well as degenerative changes within the lateral compartment leading to osteoarthritis, lateral patellofemoral joint instability, and attenuation of the medial collateral ligament. The current literature describes multiple modalities for distal femoral osteotomy such as lateral opening-wedge, medial closing-wedge, dome, wedge-less spike, and wedge-less V osteotomy techniques.

Coronal-plane deformities of the knee are prevalent skeletal abnormalities in the adolescent population. Although valgus alignment is observed less frequently than varus alignment, it predisposes patients to several pathologic sequelae, including cartilage lesions, as well as degenerative changes within the lateral compartment leading to osteoarthritis, lateral patellofemoral joint instability, and attenuation of the medial collateral ligament. The current literature describes multiple modalities for distal femoral osteotomy such as lateral opening-wedge, medial closing-wedge, dome, wedge-less spike, and wedge-less V osteotomy techniques. The wedge-less V osteotomy of the distal femur, initially described by Aglietti et al.,1 was primarily indicated for the correction of genu valgum deformity due to lateral-compartment osteoarthritic changes.

Surgical Technique

The operation is performed under anesthesia (general/spinal) with the patient supine on a radiolucent operating table under tourniquet control (Video 1). The knee is flexed to 60° during the operation to avoid pressure in the popliteal area by keeping a large bolster under it (Figs 1 and 2).

Limb positioning: left lower limb in supine position with bolster under knee. A tourniquet is applied to the thigh proximally. Surface marking is performed for a skin incision in the midline of the lateral aspect of the distal femur.

With the left knee in supine position with a bolster under the knee, a 10- to 15-cm incision is made along the midline of the lateral aspect of the distal femur, centered over the lateral epicondyle. The subcutaneous tissue and fat are dissected, and the iliotibial (IT) band is identified.

A skin incision approximately 8 to 10 cm long is made along the lateral aspect of the distal femur extending proximally from the level of the lateral joint line. The iliotibial band is identified and incised in line with the incision (Fig 3). The vastus lateralis is identified and elevated anteriorly (Figs 4 and 5). The periosteum is incised and elevated anteriorly and posteriorly to expose the femoral metaphysis. The intermuscular septum is identified and released. A radiolucent retractor is placed posteriorly to protect the neurovascular structures (tibial nerve and popliteal artery).

After dissection of subcutaneous tissue and fat, the iliotibial band (IT) is identified and incised in line with the skin incision (Left knee).

After incision of the iliotibial band, the vastus lateralis muscle is identified and elevated from the intermuscular septum, exposing the distal medial aspect of the femoral shaft. The arterial branches of the profunda femoris are coagulated (Left knee).

After adequate exposure, a distal femoral locking plate is provisionally placed over the lateral femur to plan the osteotomy site, and the position is confirmed under fluoroscopy (Left knee).

The V-shaped osteotomy is marked in the frontal plane; the posterior arm of the osteotomy is longer than the anterior arm because this helps resist flexion of the distal fragment (Fig 6). The marked osteotomy site is bicortically drilled with a K-wire facilitating precise bone cutting, followed by use of an oscillating saw with a thick, coarse blade under continuous saline solution irrigation (Fig 7). The osteotomy is then completed with a thin osteotome (Figs 8 and 9). This technique decreases the risk of heat necrosis and helps in thinning the medial cortex without undue periosteal disruption. Aggressive division of the periosteum and soft tissues on the medial side can make the osteotomy more unstable.

A V-shaped osteotomy is marked over the lateral aspect of the distal femur (Left knee), with the apex of the V directed toward the metaphysis of the distal femur. The posterior limb of the V is made slightly longer than the anterior limb to prevent flexion of the distal fragment. The plate position is then reconfirmed to ensure that an adequate number of screws can be placed both proximally and distally.

Multiple bicortical drill holes are created along the marked V-shaped osteotomyline to facilitate precise bone cutting (left knee).

All drill holes are joined using a thin osteotome to facilitate easy passage of the saw blade (left knee).

The V-shaped osteotomy is completed with a saw gun under continuous saline solution irrigation to prevent heat necrosis of bone. Care must be taken when moving medially to avoid aggressive division of the medial soft tissue and periosteum because this may render the osteotomy unstable (Left knee).

A gentle valgus thrust is applied to break the medial cortex (Fig 10). After the osteotomy is completed, the knee is extended and the deformity is corrected with a gentle manual varus force. A small part of the bone from the proximal anterior and posterior segments of the lateral cortex is removed using a bone nibbler or saw for easy plate application. The medial cortex usually does not open up because of intact soft-tissue cover; therefore, the correction is obtained primarily by the lateral penetration and impaction of the cancellous bone. No wedges are taken, and medial-lateral translation is not desirable or necessary because the osteotomy is close to the center of rotation of angulation. The alignment of the leg is repeatedly evaluated in extension, and the amount of lateral cortical penetration is closely monitored to obtain the planned correction.

A gentle valgus force is applied at the osteotomy site to break the medial hinge, followed by a gentle varus force to achieve the planned correction, which is then confirmed with fluoroscopy (left knee). (INTRA-OP, intraoperative.)

The osteotomy is provisionally fixed with K-wires and then finally stabilized by internal fixation with a distal femoral locking plate and screws (Fig 11, Fig 12, Fig 13, Fig 14, Fig 15). The stability of the osteotomy in flexion and extension is evaluated on the table after correction of the deformity. The tourniquet is released, and hemostasis is achieved. A suction drain is inserted, and the wound is closed in layers. All the instruments and implants used in the aforementioned procedure are obtained from Biotek (BioFix-DF).

The osteotomy site is provisionally fixed with 2 K-wires in the anteroposterior direction so that it does not interfere with plate application (left knee).

A small elevated part of the proximal lateral cortex is removed for easy application of the plate (Left knee).

The osteotomy is finally stabilized by internal fixation with a distal femoral locking plate and screws (Left knee).

The patient is kept in a long-knee immobilizer for 3 weeks, followed by a hinged knee brace. Range of movement is initially kept at 0° to 90° for 1 week and then increased to 120° for the next week. Full movements are allowed by the end of 4 weeks. The patient is kept non–weight-bearing for 6 weeks; full weight-bearing is then permitted. Strength training is allowed at 3 months; jogging, at 6 months; and return to sports, after 9 months. Discussion

We perform distal femoral wedge-less V osteotomy through a lateral approach. The supracondylar V osteotomy through a lateral approach is a very useful technique because surgical time and blood loss are minimal and, in our opinion, it has a short learning curve. The V shape of the osteotomy allows large correction while preserving stability. Correction is obtained by lateral penetration and crushing of the cancellous bone. Mediolateral translation is not necessary because no wedges are taken.1

However, in its original description, internal fixation was not performed; instead, cast immobilization was considered for a prolonged period.1, 2, 3 We have performed internal fixation with a distal femoral locking plate. Internal fixation with a plate has the advantage of early mobility and rehabilitation, which may reduce patient morbidity and save time compared with correction without a plate.4,5

Valgus deformity of the knee is usually observed in the distal femur, and the most common corrective procedure is probably varus closing-wedge distal femoral osteotomy.6 Closing- or opening-wedge osteotomy carries the disadvantage of causing limb length discrepancies.6 Aglietti et al.1 were the first authors to describe the supracondylar V osteotomy; the technique was later modified by various surgeons, including Agarwal7 and Gupta et al.,4,5 mainly in the manner of fixation. Whereas Aglietti et al. used no fixation at all, Agarwal used staples and, later, K-wires (Agarwal and Shaharyar); Gupta et al. used an L plate to stabilize the osteotomy.4,5,7,8,9 We do not use or advise the use of a contralateral proximal humeral plate for fixation because, in our opinion, it is a low-profile plate and not ideal for weight-bearing joints such as the knee.

Dietz and Weinstein10 described a similar procedure called “spike osteotomy.” The most common diagnosis for which they performed this osteotomy was Blount disease (8 cases). They concluded that it was a safe and effective surgical procedure but reported loss of correction greater than 5° in 4 cases.

In addition, a systematic review has shown that V osteotomy is a reliable method of distal femoral osteotomy to attain good correction with excellent clinical outcomes even with variations of fixation.11 Wedge-less distal femoral osteotomy with K-wire fixation is a viable option for correction of genu valgus deformity with potential advantages of minimal blood loss, no leg length discrepancy, nonrigid fixation, and early union as compared with other treatment options.12
