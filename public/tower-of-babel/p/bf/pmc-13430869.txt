Healthcare utilization and morbidity preceding a dementia classification: the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study
Authors: Meng Pan, Virginia Pate, David Knopman, Michele Jonsson-Funk, Priya Palta, Anna M. Kucharska-Newton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDementia is frequently underdiagnosed in its early stages, but changes in healthcare utilization may precede diagnosis by several years. We examined healthcare patterns and chronic conditions before cognitive impairment among the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study participants to better understand this pre-diagnostic period.MethodsWe included ARIC visit 6 (2016–2017) participants with continuous 5-year prior Medicare coverage (2011–2018) and used negative binomial regression to compare longitudinal trends in hospitalization in participants with Part A coverage, and emergency department (ED) and ambulatory care use in participants with Part A&B coverage, in the 5 years prior to and 1 year following an incident dementia according to ARIC syndromic classification. Inverse probability weights were used to balance participants with and without incident dementia on baseline age, race, sex, education, and prevalent comorbidities. Principal diagnosis discharge codes were used to assess chronic conditions associated with hospitalization and ED visits, whereas all available diagnosis codes were used for ambulatory care visits.ResultsAmong 3,923 eligible participants (mean age 79.6 ± 4.8 years; 59% women; 24% Black), 325 (8.3%) were classified as incident dementia at ARIC visit 6 or 7. During 5 years prior to visit 6, participants with incident dementia compared with non-dementia participants, had on average higher use of inpatient (0.12 vs. 0.08 hospitalizations per 6-month) and ED services (0.28 vs. 0.18 visits per 6-month) but lower ambulatory care use (3.53 vs. 3.76 visits per 6-month). Participants with incident dementia experienced more frequent hospitalizations for acute myocardial infarction (3.8% vs. 1.7%), chronic kidney disease (4.1% vs. 2.6%), congestive heart failure (4.7% vs. 1.6%), and stroke/TIA (8.5% vs. 2.6%), and more frequent ambulatory care visits for atrial fibrillation (23% vs. 15%), than non-dementia participants within 5 years prior to incident dementia.ConclusionsCompared with non-dementia participants, those with incident dementia had higher average hospitalization and ED utilization but lower ambulatory care visits, with differences observable 3 years before incident dementia. Cardiovascular disease and concomitant cerebrovascular disease are important contributors to dementia. Close monitoring of healthcare-seeking patterns may help identify individuals at high risk of dementia earlier in the disease course.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14815-5.

Introduction

Dementia represents a compromise in a person’s cognitive abilities and is diagnosed with evidence of substantial impairment to memory and other cognitive domains that are severe enough to interfere with activities of daily life. Approximately 13.4% of Medicare beneficiaries aged 65 years and older in the United States, approximately 8 million, may receive a diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease or related dementia (ADRD) [1]. However, dementia status is significantly under-ascertained in clinical practice [2–4]. The under-detection of dementia, especially in the early stages of the condition, could be due to the difficulty in distinguishing it from normal cognitive aging or short-term cognitive function loss following other diseases (e.g., depression), as well as the slow disease progression itself [5]. Moreover, persons with poor healthcare access (e.g., minority populations) may disproportionately experience shortcomings in the detection of dementia and communication of its risk [6]. As a result, approximately 40% of dementia cases remain undiagnosed, and most patients receive a diagnosis of dementia late in the course of the disease when the effectiveness of potential interventions is significantly diminished [6, 7].

Despite the challenges of early detection, distinct patterns of healthcare utilization could emerge as early as 5–10 years before dementia diagnosis [8]. Understanding the specific changes in health-seeking behavior may help healthcare professionals identify early periods in dementia development. Recognizing these patterns can support timely diagnoses and early management of risk factors, and ultimately prevent or delay the onset of dementia [9]. Most of the interest in the clinical management of dementia has focused on the use of healthcare services incurred after a dementia diagnosis [10–14], and relatively little is known about care preceding a dementia diagnosis [9, 15–17]. Studies examining healthcare services before and after a diagnosis of dementia have largely focused on changes in the costs or proportions of healthcare visits, but reasons for visits remain unknown [17–19]. Therefore, in this study, we examined patterns of healthcare use and related chronic comorbidities preceding the classification of dementia among participants of the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study.

Methods

Data source

The ARIC Study is a community-based, prospective cohort of 15,792 men and women aged 45–64 years at baseline, recruited from four U.S. communities in Forsyth County, North Carolina; Jackson, Mississippi; Minneapolis suburbs, Minnesota; and Washington County, Maryland. Study participants were followed from the baseline visit (1987–1989) until the present through subsequent clinic visits, annual (semiannual since 2012) telephone interviews and active surveillance of hospitalizations and mortality [20]. Detailed information about the content of the ARIC clinical examinations and telephone follow-up interviews can be obtained from the ARIC Study website [21, 22]. Data from ARIC cohort participants were linked with the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) claims data for the years 1991–2020 via a finder file that included participants’ social security numbers, gender and date of birth. (Fig. 1) Among the total number of study participants with available social security numbers (n = 15,744), 238 died before 1991, and 607 died after 1991 but before reaching the CMS Medicare eligibility age of 65 years, leaving 14,899 eligible ARIC study participants. The crosswalk file between the ARIC study finder file and the CMS Medicare Beneficiary Summary file yielded 14,702 patients in the ARIC-CMS linkage dataset (98.7% match) [23].

Analytical cohorts

We assembled a retrospective cohort of all ARIC participants aged 70 years or older who had no missing dementia classifications at Visit 6 (2016–2017) and were not classified as having dementia during Visit 5 (2011–2013). Additionally, we followed those participants who were free of dementia at Visit 6 until Visit 7 (2018–2019) if they had no missing dementia classification at Visit 7 (Fig. 1). We required participants to have continuous Medicare Part A enrollment for at least 5 years preceding the date of dementia classification at Visit 6 or Visit 7 (Cohort 1 used in the examination of hospitalized events). We then required these participants to have continuous Medicare Part A and B enrollment for at least 5 years preceding the date of dementia classification at Visit 6 or Visit 7 in addition to Medicare Part A enrollment (Cohort 2 used in the examination of emergency department (ED) and ambulatory care visits).

Exposure

Dementia status was ascertained at Visits 5, 6 and 7 using standard protocols [24]. Briefly, trained interviewers administered a standardized battery of neurocognitive tests, which included the Word Fluency Test [25], Digit Symbol Substitution [26], Delayed Word Recall [27], Digit Span Backward [26], Boston Naming [28], Animal Naming [25], Trail Making Tests A and B [29], Incidental Learning [30], and Logical Memory tests [26]. Participants meeting criteria for possible cognitive impairment based on neurocognitive test performance underwent a comprehensive neurological examination. Additional information about participants’ cognitive and functional status was sought from informants when necessary. Study participants were classified as cognitively normal, having mild cognitive impairment, or having dementia based on expert review of participants’ responses to this comprehensive battery of neurocognitive tests, the Mini-Mental State Exam (MMSE), the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) scale, the Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ), and calculations of annualized cognitive decline [31]. A syndromic diagnosis of mild cognitive impairment was based on diagnostic guidelines established by the National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA) workgroups [32]. Criteria for syndromic dementia classification were based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition (DSM-5) and the 1984 National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke and the Alzheimer’s Disease and Related Disorders Association (NINCDS-ADRDA) dementia criteria [32–35].

Throughout this manuscript, “incident dementia” refers to the incident dementia according to ARIC syndromic classification among participants free of a syndromic dementia classification at the preceding visit (e.g., visit 6 participants free at visit 5; visit 7 participants free at visits 5 or 6). Participants with mild cognitive impairment or normal cognition were combined into the “non-dementia” comparison group.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was healthcare utilization, defined as the average number of hospitalizations, Emergency Department (ED) visits, and ambulatory care visits, divided by the number of eligible ARIC Medicare beneficiaries. The frequencies of hospitalizations, ED visits, and ambulatory care encounters were examined separately using 6-month time windows. The date of Visit 6 or Visit 7 was considered as the index date. For example, if participants were classified as having dementia at Visit 6, their index date was the date of Visit 6; if participants were classified as non-dementia at Visit 6 but classified as having dementia at Visit 7, they were classified as having dementia and their index date was the date of Visit 7; if they were classified as non-dementia at both Visit 6 and 7, their index date was the date of Visit 6. We employed a 5-year lookback period from and a 1-year follow-up period after the index date to examine change in healthcare utilization among participants classified with incident dementia and non-dementia at either Visit 6 or Visit 7.

In secondary analysis, we examined the prevalence of chronic conditions associated with healthcare encounters preceding incident dementia classified according to the ARIC syndromic definition. Chronic conditions were identified from the principal diagnosis discharge codes related to each hospitalization and ED visit and all the diagnosis codes related to ambulatory care visits, according to the 27 chronic care management (CCM) Chronic Conditions Data Warehouse (CCW) algorithms [36].

Covariates

Baseline demographic covariates were measured at Visit 5, including age in years, race (Black vs. non-Black), sex (female vs. male), education level (less than high school vs. high school or higher) and smoking status (current, former, and never smoker). Prevalent comorbidities were ascertained from information collected at Visit 5 (e.g., hypertension, diabetes) or events follow-up until Visit 5 date (e.g., heart failure, chronic heart disease, and stroke). Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg or self-report of the use of antihypertensive medications. Diabetes was defined as fasting plasma glucose level ≥ 126 mg/dL non-fasting plasma glucose level ≥ 200 mg/dL, or self-report of the use of anti-diabetic medications. Heart failure, coronary heart disease, and stroke were identified from surveillance of hospitalized events.

Statistical analysis

We used negative binomial regression [37] to estimate the longitudinal trends of 6-month average healthcare utilization in the 5 years prior to and 1 year following incident dementia among confounder-balanced incident dementia and non-dementia comparison groups. The two groups were balanced on age, sex, race, education level, and prevalent comorbidities (chronic heart disease, heart failure, diabetes, and stroke) at Visit 5 via inverse probability weighting (IPW) from a propensity score model (with the non-dementia group as a reference). We also report the prevalence of CCW chronic conditions within 5 years preceding incident dementia in the total population and stratified by dementia status. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Among 3923 participants (mean age 79.6 ± 4.8 years, 59% women, 24% Black), nearly three-quarters of the participants (72%) had hypertension, 31% had diabetes, and 13% had heart failure or chronic heart disease at Visit 5 (Table 1). A total of 325 participants (8.3%) were classified with incident dementia at Visit 6 (n = 245) or at Visit 7 (n = 80). Among the 3879 participants with continuous prior 5-year Medicare Part A coverage (Cohort 1), 1635 (42.5%) participants had prior 5-year continuous Medicare Part A &B coverage (Cohort 2) (Figure S1, Table S3). Among Cohort 1, 318 (8.2%) were classified as having incident dementia; while among Cohort 2, 142 (8.7%) were classified as having incident dementia (Table S1-S2). Compared with those non-dementia at Visit 6 or Visit 7, individuals with incident dementia classification at either of those visits were more likely to be Black, older, more likely to have education less than high school and had greater prevalence of comorbidities. These baseline covariates were balanced after propensity score weighting (Table S1-S2, Figure S2). Participants in Cohort 2 were slightly older and had more hypertension than those in Cohort 1 (Table S3).

During the 5 years prior to the ARIC dementia classification, participants classified as having incident dementia had, on average, greater utilization of inpatient services (0.12 [95% CI: 0.08, 0.18] vs. 0.08 [95% CI: 0.07, 0.09] per 6 months) and ED services (0.28 [95% CI: 0.19, 0.43] vs. 0.18 [95% CI: 0.16, 0.21] per 6 months) but lower ambulatory care use (3.53 [95% CI: 2.96, 4.20] vs. 3.76 [95% CI: 3.58, 3.95] per 6 months) than did non-dementia participants (Fig. 2a and b). Individuals with incident dementia tended to gradually increase their hospitalization frequency within the 5 years preceding dementia classification, whereas non-dementia participants maintained a stable hospitalization rate over a comparable period of observation, with the difference in hospitalization rates peaking at 1 year to 6 months preceding dementia classification (Fig. 2a). The trend in hospitalizations remained the same when the study population was restricted to participants with both Part A and B coverage (Figure S3). We observed a consistently greater use of ED services in the 5 years preceding an ARIC dementia classification among participants with incident dementia than those who were non-dementia (Fig. 2b). The use of ambulatory care services was lower overall among ARIC participants with incident dementia than among non-dementia participants; however, the pattern was not consistent throughout the entire 5 years preceding dementia classification (Fig. 2b). The differences in healthcare utilization trends between individuals with incident dementia and non-dementia participants were observable at 3 years preceding the dementia classification (Figure 2a and b). During the 1 year following incident dementia classification, participants with incident dementia had fewer hospitalizations and ambulatory care visits but more ED visits than non-dementia participants. The differences in the half-year average hospitalization and ambulatory care visits between incident dementia and non-dementia participants tended to decrease after dementia classification, whereas the difference in the half-year average ED visit rate remained (Figure 2a and b).

The most frequent principal diagnosis for hospitalizations occurring within 5 years prior to incident dementia classification was stroke/TIA (8.5%) among participants with incident dementia versus rheumatoid arthritis/osteoarthritis (RA/OA) (8.7%) among non-dementia individuals. Participants with incident dementia experienced more frequent hospitalizations for acute myocardial infarction (3.8% vs. 1.7%), chronic kidney disease (4.1% vs. 2.6%), congestive heart failure (4.7% vs. 1.6%), and stroke/TIA (8.5% vs. 2.6%) than non-dementia participants within 5 years prior to incident dementia (Table 2). The most frequent diagnosis for ambulatory care visits occurring within 5 years prior to dementia status classification was hypertension (63% and 67% among participants with incident dementia and non-dementia, respectively). Compared with non-dementia participants, participants with incident dementia had more ambulatory care visit diagnoses for atrial fibrillation (23% vs. 15%), congestive heart failure (10% vs. 5.3%), and stroke/TIA (10% vs. 2.7%) (Table 2).

Discussion

In this retrospective analysis of healthcare utilization among ARIC study cohort participants with linked CMS claims, we observed a higher frequency in the use of inpatient and ED services and lower use of ambulatory care services among participants with incident dementia as compared to non-dementia participants. Stroke/TIA and congestive heart failure were the most frequent diagnoses observed within 5 years preceding incident dementia.

Interaction with health-care services triggers dementia assessment and ultimately diagnosis [38]. The observed high rates of hospitalization and ED use, paired with low outpatient engagement prior to an incident dementia classification, suggest missed opportunities for pre-clinical dementia detection within primary care facilities. The findings highlight the need for a systematic approach to integrating low-cost, time-efficient cognitive screening into routine primary care visits for high-risk populations [39]. The observed high prevalence of stroke/TIA and congestive heart failure diagnoses preceding incident dementia underscores the contribution of cardiovascular disease and cerebrovascular disease to dementia risk and reflects the challenges of managing chronic conditions as cognitive function deteriorates [40, 41]. These patterns indicate a need for comprehensive care models, including routine monitoring and medication management to reduce acute care utilization and delay transition to cognitive impairment [42].

Our findings support observations from prior studies. Kumar et al. described health utilization trends preceding dementia identification in a Health and Retirement Study (HRS)-Medicare linked cohort and reported that people living with dementia had a greater prevalence of outpatient, ED, inpatient hospital, skilled nursing, and home health use in the 6 years preceding dementia identification than did those without dementia across a comparable timespan [17]. Abbass et al. used ICD-9/10-CM codes to identify older adults with newly diagnosed dementia in Medicare Advantage claims from third-party proprietary commercial data and reported an increase in all healthcare utilization among people with newly diagnosed dementia within one year preceding dementia diagnosis and a high rate of congestive heart failure visits in this population [9]. Using the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS) data linked with Medicare fee-for-service claims, Nothelle et al. observed that older adults with diabetes and incident dementia had higher rates of hospitalization, ED visits, and post-acute SNF but fewer outpatient visits (primarily specialty visits) over time compared to those without dementia [16]. Jacobson et al. compared healthcare utilization before and after a dementia diagnosis among Medicare fee-for services and Medicare Advantage beneficiaries and reported that both groups had more inpatient and outpatient visits one month before dementia diagnosis [18]. Dementia classification used in the above studies were limited by low sensitivity and specificity because dementia identification was either through self-report, probabilistic definitions based on telephone interviews for cognitive status (TICS) assessments, or clinical diagnostic codes, leading to substantial dementia misclassification [43]. The present study is based in the ARIC dementia classification derived from a comprehensive expert review of multiple clinical and cognitive criteria offering greater confidence in accurate case identification.

We used the linkage of ARIC study cohort data with CMS Medicare claims, which enabled us to leverage comprehensive neurocognitive assessment in the examination of healthcare encounters and associated chronic conditions [23, 24]. We observed that participants classified with incident dementia in the ARIC cohort were, on average, 7 years older and had lower educational attainment than those free of dementia. Assessment of healthcare utilization in these two groups would be highly confounded without appropriate adjustment for age and educational attainment [44, 45]. Therefore, we used inverse propensity score weighting to minimize the confounding impact of baseline imbalances in age and other covariates on the assessment of the frequency of healthcare visits, although the crude results (without baseline covariate balancing) showed the same trend (Figure S4).

Our study has several limitations. We relied on dementia classification at the ARIC clinical visit to assemble an analytical cohort of participants with and without dementia. The participants had to be alive and attend the clinical visit to participate in the cognitive assessment; therefore, we could not capture participants who were deceased or lost to follow-up before assessment. This is likely to lead to an underestimation of dementia incidence. Although propensity score weighting was applied to balance measured baseline covariates, some selection bias and residual confounding may remain and should be considered when interpreting the findings. We used the date of Visit 6 or 7 to identify the index date of incident dementia; however, incident dementia could have been diagnosed clinically during the years between Visit 5 and Visits 6 or 7. In that case, our observed trend in health care utilization may have been subject to misclassification of the timing of syndromic dementia classification. Moreover, participants with a syndromic diagnosis of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or normal cognition were collectively referred as non-dementia, creating a clinically heterogeneous comparison group. MCI may represent an intermediate or prodromal stage of dementia and could attenuate observed differences and limit interpretability of comparisons with the dementia group. Due to the limited sample size, we did not separate ambulatory care visits into primary care and specialty visits. Thus, we could not provide more detailed information that may have explained the lower frequency of ambulatory care visits among those with, as compared to without incident dementia. ED and ambulatory findings may not be generalized to all Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services Medicare beneficiaries, particularly those enrolled in Medicare Advantage plans.

In conclusion, we found higher average inpatient and ED utilizations but a lower frequency of ambulatory care visits among participants with incident dementia than non-dementia participants, with differences observable at 3 years preceding the incident dementia classification. Findings from this study shed light on patterns of care that foreshadow a diagnosis of dementia and support the belief that cardiovascular disease and concomitant cerebrovascular disease are important contributors to dementia.
