Background

Mucoepidermoid carcinoma (MEC) represents a malignancy primarily of the salivary glands, however there have been rare instances in which primary thymic MEC can occur, accounting for as little as 1–2% of thymic carcinoma cases.1,2 In such instances where it arises in the anterior mediastinum, the tumor often presents unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment. This case report presents a patient with an anterior mediastinal mass that was later immunohistologically diagnosed as mucoepidermoid carcinoma following surgical resection. This report aims to review the histologic, immunologic, and radiologic findings of MEC while discussing them within the contexts of this uncommon presentation in order to better guide the clinical evaluation and management of this rare pathology.

Case presentation

A 27-year-old female with past medical history notable for obesity and polycystic ovary syndrome and with a family history of oculopharyngeal muscular dystrophy presented with initial complaints of subjective clicking sensation and pharyngitis over the preceding two months with associated dysphagia to liquids and solids. Her review of systems was otherwise unremarkable.

A thorough examination of the head and neck was noncontributory and despite her family history of oculopharyngeal muscular dystrophy no ptosis or fascial muscle weakness was appreciated. A homogenous thyroid gland of normal size was found on exam and there were no masses or lymphadenopathy.

Dysphagia studies included a negative swallowing function study which showed no sustained laryngeal penetration or tracheal aspiration of liquid, pureed, and solid foods. The patient’s symptoms persisted prompting a CT of the neck, revealing enlargement of the palatine and lingual tonsils without associated inflammation or abscess and a prominent anterior mediastinal mass in the visualized chest with a heterogeneous density and associated mildly enlarged lymph nodes along the internal jugular chain. Peripheral blood flow cytometry failed to reveal any abnormal lymphoid or progenitor cell populations. A complementary CT scan of the chest was obtained, demonstrating a round 2.4 cm mass in the upper mediastinum arising from or adjacent to the thymic gland (Fig. 1). No hilar or mediastinal lymphadenopathy was visualized.

Radiography of anterior mediastinum. Axial (A) and sagittal (B) computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest. A round 2.4 cm mass was visualized in the upper anterior mediastinum, lower in density than the adjacent thymic gland which in turn was displaced to the right and cephalad. No mediastinal or hilar adenopathy present

Differential diagnoses included thymoma, teratoma, or combined with mildly enlarged cervical lymph nodes and tonsillar enlargement possible lymphoma. A CT-guided biopsy of the mediastinal mass was attempted for tissue diagnosis to guide future management, however this was aborted due to an inability to adequately sedate the patient during the procedure. This was further complicated by hemorrhagic shock and pericardial tamponade necessitating emergent pericardiocentesis and successful rescue of the patient. The patient was subsequently transferred to the intensive care unit where her pericardial drain was removed the following day after tolerating a five hour clamp trial and repeat echocardiogram showed trace pericardial effusion unchanged from echocardiogram obtained prior to her pericardiocentesis.

Pathology from the minimal tissue obtained at biopsy was inconclusive, showing scant blood vessels, adipose tissue, and fragments of mesothelium. Pericardiocentesis cytology was similarly noncontributory, with no malignancy identified on cytology. In light of her symptomatology and with radiographic evidence concerning for possible malignancy, it was determined that complete surgical resection of the anterior mediastinal mass was warranted.

With use of the DaVinci robotic platform, a four port right robotic thymectomy was performed and the mass resected without complication. Measuring 5.5 × 4.1 × 2.5 cm the 17.3 g specimen demonstrated a 3.3 cm lesion ensheathed by a fibrous capsule with prominent cystic changes. Included were a small number of tumor cells among the cyst wall comprised of bland-appearing mucus and epidermoid cells. No mitotic activity was appreciated in the tumor, with mild cytologic atypia evidenced. The surrounding thymic tissue was normal in character without lymphovascular invasion although perineural invasion was observed. Further review by colleagues at the Mayo Clinic in Rochester, MN confirmed the initial findings and on subsequent immunohistochemistry staining demonstrated diffuse positivity in the tumor cells for tumor protein 63 (p63), a transcription factor of the p63 gene family expressed across several tumor subtypes [3]. These findings were deemed consistent with low-grade mucoepidermoid carcinoma with cystic changes (Fig. 2).

Histopathological study of subject anterior mediastinal mass. Tissue specimen demonstrated a well-circumscribed lesion surrounded by fibrous capsule with prominent cystic changes. There was a relatively small amount of tumor cells in the cyst wall comprised of bland-appearing mucus cells and epidermoid cells consistent with low-grade mucoid epidermoid carcinoma. P63 staining demonstrated diffuse positivity in tumor cells. No mitotic activity was appreciated in the tumor. Cytologic atypia was mild. (Hematoxylin eosin, original magnifications ×100 [A], [B])

Due to the perineural invasion seen on pathology, the patient was discussed at multidisciplinary tumor board and was deemed at high risk for long-term cardiac effects of radiation because of her young age and a plan for continued surveillance was pursued. The patient has been followed for approximately fourteen months at time of writing, initially notable for persistent dysphagia and progressive fatigue with speech further treated with tonsillectomy. She remained with persistent lingual tonsil hypertrophy presumed secondary to laryngopharyngeal reflux that with antireflux therapy improved significantly. Despite this she will be pursuing elective tonsillectomy in the following months. She is without manifestation of any concerning signs or symptoms of mass recurrence.

Discussion and conclusions

The first characterization of MEC as a salivary gland malignancy was in 1945 by the work of Foote and Becker where they described a then unique collection of salivary gland tumors as “muco-epidermoid” [4]. Although they approximated incidence at 5% of all major and minor tumors of the salivary gland, by modern estimates MEC represents the most common primary salivary gland malignancy in both adults and children at nearly 30% identified as MEC [5, 6]. Distribution favors the parotid gland in as many as 70% of reported cases [7, 8]. These tumors comprise a heterogenous family of malignancies with mucus-secreting, epidermoid, and intermediate cell types whose clinical behavior ranges from indolent to aggressive depending on their histological grade [9]. As a result, consensus lacks on grading systems with debate as to whether binary grading is sufficient or if there is a place for intermediate staging [10–16]. Irrespective, mitotic rate, perineural or lymphatic invasion, cystic architecture, and cytologic atypia correlate with severity [17].

Although primarily a malignancy of the salivary glands, there have been rare reports of MEC originating in locations such as the tonsils, esophagus, lung, breast, and pancreas [18–23]. Incidence of thymic MEC is similarly rare, with a cursory search of the literature revealing fewer than sixty case reports across PubMed dating to 1980 [24–26]. The 5th edition of the World Health Organization’s (WHO) Classification of Endocrine and Neuroendocrine Tumors has recently identified MEC as among salivary gland-type carcinomas of the thyroid, highlighting an increasing awareness of this pathology [27, 28].

Whether the incidence of this pathology is truly on the rise or this represents the growing body of medical knowledge is unclear, however what remains apparent is that despite the fleetingly small incidence MEC poses a potential challenge in diagnosis and management for thoracic surgery. When present symptoms are vague or nonspecific and can be easily confounded with other etiologies such as in this case where concurrent tonsillar pathology may contribute to the patient’s subjective complaints of dysphagia. Still, a majority of cases are asymptomatic and are found incidentally on imaging.

This case had perineural invasion, which by some reports can be seen in 60% of cases and has been associated with poor prognosis [7, 29]. One report identified perineural invasion as an independent prognosticator of disease specific survival in salivary malignancies with increased rates of soft tissue invasion, positive surgical margins, and 12–24 month mortality suggesting that in extra-salivary malignancies this may confer similarly poor outcomes [30]. Similar questions have arisen in other oral cancers, such as in one report of a hyalinizing clear cell carcinoma with perineural invasion that the authors attribute to high risk features possibly warranting radiation or chemotherapy as part of postoperative management [31].

The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines recommend considering postoperative radiotherapy in instances of salivary MEC when high risk or concerning features are present, defined as tumor spillage, perineural invasion, or intermediate/high-grade histology [32]. Thymic carcinomas of all types with R0 resections have a five year survival of only 60% by some estimates [33]. However, when separated by the Masaoka staging system, low staged carcinomas may in fact not require adjuvant chemotherapy [34]. The current NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines In Oncology for Thymomas and Thymic Carcinomas, Version 2.2025 emphasizes that for R0 resections without capsular invasion as in this case radiation therapy is not necessary, although surveillance consisting of chest CT with contrast every 6–12 months for two year followed by annually until 5 years postoperatively is appropriate as was performed in this case [35]. Were this to have invaded the capsule, then algorithmically this would favor consideration for postoperative radiotherapy which although discussed was deferred in this instance.

Unlike thymomas, thymic carcinomas rarely overlap with paraneoplastic syndromes such as myasthenia gravis [36]. In accordance with the NCCN guidelines of suspected thymic tumors, a multidisciplinary tumor board was consulted during the management of this patient and it was determined that the mass represented a surgically resectable tumor [35]. As such, during her preoperative evaluation myasthenia was not considered in this patient.

Molecular studies represent an area of increasing interest in the diagnosis and management of thymic carcinomas, although there is little data available with specific relation to MEC. TP53 data suggests overexpression in some forms of thyroid carcinoma, although putative genes of interest such as FOXN1, KIT, and EGFR remain active areas of study [37]. Recent genetic studies have isolated aberrant protein products from fusions of cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB) and mastermind-like gene (MAML) across numerous MECs across the body, the results of which have been suggested to convey worsening prognosis through disruption of the cAMP/CREB and NOTCH1 pathways [38, 39]. These may prove fruitful in future diagnostic and treatment modalities.

The rarity of thymic MEC and its radiological overlap with more common anterior mediastinal masses such as thymomas or germ cell tumors underscores the importance of histological evaluation for accurate diagnosis. This report represents an instance whereby robotic-assisted thymectomy was successfully performed, enabling precise and minimally invasive resection. The patient recovered uneventfully and was discharged expeditiously, illustrating the efficacy and safety of surgical management.

This case contributes to the limited literature on thymic MEC and underscores the critical role of early recognition, thorough evaluation, and multidisciplinary collaboration in diagnosing and treating rare mediastinal tumors. While long-term follow-up is essential to monitor for recurrence, the mild atypia, absence of mitotic activity, and normal thymic tissue surrounding the tumor suggest a favorable prognosis, however this is in turn measured against perineural invasion and will require surveillance as outlined by the NCCN. Despite the rarity of this pathology, the role of the surgeon remains central not only diagnosis, but in determining outcomes and clinical prognosis as the completeness of resection drives postoperative care for early stage thymic tumors. Excellent surgical technique remains essential in the multidisciplinary armamentarium for treating these complex pathologies.
