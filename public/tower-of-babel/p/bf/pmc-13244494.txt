Development of novel biomechanical outcomes using motion capture to characterize hand function in multiple sclerosis
Authors: Samhitha M Rai, Mengke Du, Joshua Johnston, Farren B.S. Briggs, Jay Alberts, Marisa McGinley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Nine-Hole Peg Test (9HPT) is limited to a single outcome and can be insensitive to impairment in people with MS (PwMS).Objectives:To develop novel biomechanical outcomes that better characterize upper extremity function in PwMS.Methods:16 PwMS and 16 healthy controls (HC) completed the manual dexterity test (MDT, an iPad adaptation of 9HPT). A Kinect® device was used to capture bilateral 3-dimensional, multi-joint hand motion and an advanced mathematical model was constructed to yield novel outcomes. Cross-sectional associations between the MDT outcomes and clinical characteristics (arm spasticity, dysmetria, upper extremity weakness) were evaluated and effect size calculated.Results:16 PwMS (mean age 55.91 years, disease duration 13.90 years) and 16 HC (mean age 40.45 years) completed the study. The novel measure of Time to Grab had a large effect size for dominant hand (η2p 0.23) and non-dominant hand (η2p 0.17). Several other novel measures (Actual Distance-xy (Insertion to Removal), Time to Grab, Time to Lift, Movement Delay (PiH), Movement Time (PiH), Movement Time (EH), and Abnormal Records of Local Maxima (Insertion to Removal)) and the traditional outcome of MDT total time had a large effect size for the non-dominant hand (η2p 0.25, 0.17, 0.17, 0.32, 0.23, 0.17, 0.20, 0.15, respectively).Conclusions:MDT Total Time showed an ability to discriminate PwMs from HCs supporting the continued use as a conventional measure. Several novel measures were also identified that demonstrated potential to discriminate PwMS from HCs and correlated with various clinical measures that the traditional measure did not.

Introduction

Multiple Sclerosis (MS) is a chronic immune-mediated disease characterized by demyelination and neurodegeneration of the central nervous system. MS can significantly impact upper extremity function, leading to impairments in daily activities and subsequently increased dependency and reduced quality of life (Newsome et al., 2019; Martin et al., 2006; Lamers and Feys, 2014). Several studies have shown that a relatively high percentage of people diagnosed with MS report upper limb dysfunction, which has been noted even in the early stages of the disease (Lamers and Feys, 2014; Holper et al., 2010; Johansson et al., 2007). Thus, there is a strong need to be able to accurately assess upper extremity function of people with MS.

However, the current validated and most widely utilized clinical assessment for upper extremity function used for people with MS, the 9-Hole Peg Test (9HPT), only assesses a single outcome measure of total time. Furthermore, the 9HPT only evaluates the ability to perform fine, precise unilateral manual movements and does not evaluate other critical aspects of upper limb function such as proximal movements of the upper limb (e.g. reaching, lifting), gross manual dexterity (e.g. grasping and handling of large objects) or complex coordinated bimanual tasks (e.g. eating with knife and fork) which are important for performing many activities of daily living (Lamers and Feys, 2014). Furthermore, the single outcome measure from the 9HPT does not differentiate the underlying reason for impairment (e.g. weakness, incoordination) and is often insensitive to subtle changes to upper extremity function that can occur at extreme stages of the disease (Newsome et al., 2019; M Alt Murphy et al., 2018).

One proposed method to better characterize upper extremity function is the analysis of motion capture data, which has already been used to construct full-body models in the context of joint movement and gait analysis for healthy adults (M Alt Murphy et al., 2018; Jeyasingh-Jacob et al., 2024). Motion capture analysis is used routinely for the evaluation of lower extremity function but very few studies have used it for upper extremity function, with most studies focusing on patients with cerebral palsy or as a means to evaluate upper-extremity impairment after stroke (Rao et al., 2020). Motion capture data analysis provides the ability to quantify a range of motion tasks including the movements that are utilized to complete the 9HPT (M Alt Murphy et al., 2018; Jeyasingh-Jacob et al., 2024). In this study we utilized motion capture data to develop advanced mathematical models to yield novel outcomes to further characterize upper extremity function in people with MS. The primary focus of this study is to better understand how a digital tool such as the MDT, which has an array of richer data compared to the 9HPT can be leveraged to develop additional outcome measures beyond total time. Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional single-site study was conducted at the Cleveland Clinic Mellen Center for MS Treatment and Research. Research visits were completed from 11/2021 through 12/2022. Approval from an ethical standards committee (Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board) to conduct this study was received (#19–719). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants

Fifteen patients with a definitive diagnosis of MS and fifteen healthy adults were recruited from the Mellen Center for this study. The inclusion criteria for both groups included age 18–75 years, ability to understand the purpose of the study and provide informed consent, literacy of written English language as displayed on software tool and/or ability to understand and comprehend software tool from the audio instructions provided in English. An additional inclusion criterion for MS participants was a clinician confirmed diagnosis of MS. An additional inclusion criterion for healthy controls was no neurological or cognitive impairment as defined by a score of ≥ 26 on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MOCA). Exclusion criteria included a serious eye and vision problem (e. g., Uncorrected low vision as visual acuity of <20/60 of the better eye; Cataracts; Glaucoma) that would affect their ability to use technology.

Procedure

All participants enrolled in the study completed a single study visit. All participants completed the Manual Dexterity Test, which is a validated iPad® adaptation of the 9HPT (Rao et al., 2020). During this test, study participants were instructed to rotate the pegboard from the kickstand position to the front of the iPad and place the pegboard directly on the iPad screen. The holes in the pegboard are designed to serve as receptacles for aluminum pegs. When inserted in a hole, the pegs are mechanically and electrically connected to the iPad screen, enabling precise measurement of peg insertion and removal times. The participant received auditory and visual instructions for the MDT module. The participant then removed all 9 pegs from the storage area and placed them in the row at the bottom of the pegboard. The participant was instructed to choose their dominant hand, then using that hand, place the pegs one at a time in the pegboard grid, in any order. Then without pausing, the participant removed the pegs one at a time and returned them to the bottom row. For each trial, the participant was asked how many pegs were dropped during the trial.

The participant completed one test with their dominant hand and one test with their non-dominant hand. There was a 60 s time limit for each trial of the MDT. The

MDT Test collects and stores the timed data for every peg insertion and removal. An Azure Kinect® device was used to capture bilateral 3dimensional, multi-joint hand motion while performing the MDT (Fig. 1).

Motion capture and mathematical modeling

For each test the total time required to complete the MDT was captured. In addition, we aimed to measure, quantify, and model each of the constitutive actions and subactions that add up to the total time. The iPad only captures the time and location of each peg’s insertion or removal, therefore an Azure Kinect device, mounted overhead and looking down at the table during the test allowed determination of the location of the hand at all timepoints. This device was used to capture color, depth, and infrared frames as well as accelerometer data that was used to correct the images for any skewness. The color frames were fed into a hand-tracking software to track 21 distinct joints (4 for each finger plus the 1 for the wrist) for each frame. Combined with the depth data from each frame, 3-D coordinates were computed for each joint at every timepoint.

The insertion/removal times of each peg, along with the row/grid status of each peg allowed for computing the nominal xy-distance of the peg-mapping strategy and the motion capture allowed for the determination of the total xy-distance traveled. Positions and time values were used to compute bulk hand movement speeds. An ideal speed profile was modeled by utilizing parabolic speed assumptions in which the hand starts at 0 speed, reaches a maximum speed during the bulk hand movement, and then decelerates to 0 speed which was coupled with assuming that the peak speed is a function of distance traveled. Any deviance from this speed profile for an action would then be reflected as error(s) during that action.

The output metrics for each test were constructed utilizing modeling techniques as well as incorporating the motion capture data. For example, Actual Distance-xy was created using the motion capture data, while the combination of Actual Distance-xy and the ideal speed profile were utilized to determine the number of errors during an action. The combination of motion capture data and speed profiles were also used to construct the removal and insertion time metrics as well as the movement delay time values

Outcome measures

Demographic characteristics of all study participants were collected which included age at visit, sex, race, ethnicity, and years of education. Additional clinical characteristics in the MS cohort collected included disease course (relapsing vs progressive), Patient Determined Disease Steps (PDDS), current use of neuropathic pain and/or spasticity medication, and clinician reported upper extremity weakness, spasticity and dysmetria. Clinician reported clinical characteristics were obtained from the most recent standard of care visit, which was within 12 months from the research visit. All clinical measurements were treated as binary variables.

The total time it took for each participant to complete the MDT as well as novel biomechanical outcomes were determined for the two cohorts. The novel biomechanical outcomes were developed as described above. The novel biomechanical outcomes captured functions such as distance travelled for each peg, speed of peg movement, time to grab each peg, time to lift each peg, time the peg was moving in the participants hand, movement delays, and time to insert the peg (Fig. 2). The individual peg movement data was summarized for each function and each hand and reported in seconds. (Supplemental Tables 1–4).

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and counts with percentages for categorical variables. Distribution of the MDT outcome measures (traditional and novel) was assessed visually and tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with age as a covariate was used to compare the MDT outcome measures performance between MS and HC groups. Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta squared (η2p) and interpreted as small (0.01 – 0.059), medium (0.060 – 0.13), and large (≥ 0.14) (primer, 1992). For PwMS, MDT outcomes were presented as median (IQR) values, stratified by clinical characteristics and separately for each hand. Due to the small sample size (16 PwMS) and further stratification, group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Cross-sectional associations between MDT outcomes and clinical characteristics were evaluated using Point-Biserial correlation coefficients for binary characteristics and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients for continuous characteristics. Correlation magnitudes of 0.4 and 0.7 were interpreted as moderate and strong, respectively (Akoglu, 2018). Comparisons were considered statistically significant if p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1 (R Core Team 2023).

Results

A total of 16 PwMS (mean age 55.91 years, disease duration 13.90 years) and 16 HC (mean age 40.45 years) completed the study (Table 1). For the PwMS, 4 were not on a disease-modifying therapy (DMT), 4 were on an oral DMT (dimethyl fumarate and fingolimod) and 8 were on an infusion (CD20 monoclonal antibody and natalizumab).

In addition to the traditional outcome of MDT total time, 9 novel outcomes were also developed that included measurements of time (Time to Grab, Time to Lift, Time to Insert, Movement Time), distance (Nominal Distance-xy, Actual Distance-xy), speed (Action Peak-xy Speed), movement (Movement Delay), and errors (Abnormal Records of Local Maxima), when a peg was in hand (PiH), and when the hand was empty without a peg (EH). Nominal Distance-xy captures the perfect path the peg should take whereas Actual Distance-xy captures the actual path that the peg took. Abnormal Records of Local Maxima included errors such as dropping a peg.

Correlations between traditional MDT total time outcome and novel measures (Table 2)

Only time to lift in the non-dominant hand had a strong correlation with the traditional MDT total time outcome (0.71, o < 0.05). No other novel measures had any significant correlations with MDT total time in the non-dominant hand (p > 0.05 for all). In the dominant hand, time to lift (0.57), movement time (PiH) (0.59), and movement time (EH) (0.57) had significant moderate correlations with MDT total time (p < 0.05). Action Peak xy speed (insertion to removal) (−0.44), moement delay (PiH) (−0.43), abnormal records of local maxima (removal to insertion) (0.45), and abnormal records of local maxima (insertion to removal) (0.50) had moderate correlations, but they were non-significant (p > 0.05).

Effect Size (Table 3 and 4)

Dominant hand movements

For dominant hand, Time to Grab had a large effect size when comparing PwMS to healthy controls (η2p 0.23). MDT Total Time, Actual Distance-xy (Insertion to Removal), Movement Time (PiH), Movement Time (EH), Abnormal Records of Local Maxima (Removal to Insertion) and Abnormal Records of Local Maxima (Insertion to Removal) had a medium effect size (0.11, 0.09, 0.06, 0.09, 0.13, 0.09, respectively).

Non-dominant hand movements

For non-dominant hand, MDT Total Time, Actual Distance-xy (Insertion to Removal), Time to Grab, Time to Lift, Movement Delay (PiH), Movement Time (PiH), Movement Time (EH), and Abnormal Records of Local Maxima (Insertion to Removal) had a large effect size (0.25, 0.17, 0.17, 0.32, 0.23, 0.17, 0.20, 0.15, respectively). Abnormal Records of Local Maxima (Removal to Insertion) had a medium effect size (0.056).

Hand comparisons

Time to Grab (0.23, 0.17) had a consistent large effect size for both dominant and non-dominant hand. Several novel measures and the traditional outcome of MDT total time had a large effect size for the nondominant hand, but the effect size was attenuated in the dominant hand as either medium (movement time [PiH], movement time [EH], abnormal records of local maxima [insertion to removal], total time), small (actual distance xy removal to insertion, time to lift), or negligible (movement delay [PiH],).

Clinical measure correlations (Fig. 3 and 4)

Disability, disease duration, and MS course

Movement Delay (PiH) was moderately correlated with disease duration for dominant and non-dominant hand (−0.55, −0.52), while MDT Total Time was only moderately correlated for the dominant hand (0.52), but not the non-dominant (0.23). Several other measures had a moderate or strong correlation with disease duration for dominant hand but not for non-dominant hand (Movement Delay (EH), Time to Lift, Action Peak-xy Speed (insertion to removal). PDDS had no measures with consistent correlations across both hands, but several with moderate or strong correlation in a single hand (Dominant Hand & Time to Lift and Non-Dominant Hand & MDT Total Time).

Clinical signs and symptoms

Movement Time (EH) (0.52, 0.64) and MDT Total Time (0.46, 0.49) were moderately correlated with arm spasticity for dominant and non-dominant hand, respectively, but only Movement Time (PiH) for the dominant hand had a significant correlation (p < 0.05).

Several measures had moderate correlations with dysmetria in a single hand (Movement Delay (PiH) and Time to Insert), but only Time to Insert was significant (p < 0.05). Similar findings were seen for upper extremity weakness, use of spasticity medication, and use of neuropathic pain medication where moderate correlations were only observed for a single hand and only significant in the non-dominant hand as outlined in Fig. 4.

Discussion

Upper extremity dysfunction is a common symptomology of MS and deficits in upper limb functions have been shown to negatively impact quality of life and the ability to perform activities of daily living for individuals with MS (Krishnan and Jaric, 2008; Seferoğlu et al., 2024; Schwid et al., 1999; Valè et al., 2020). However, despite the knowledge of how disabling and impactful upper extremity dysfunction can be, the current evaluation of upper limb function (9HPT) provides a limited assessment and disability scaling (EDSS) typically place a greater emphasis on walking/mobility compared to hand function. These limitations in the current assessments result in an incomplete assessment of upper limb deficits (Newsome et al., 2019; Marrie et al., 2022). In this study we utilized motion capture to develop novel outcomes measures from a digital adaptation of the 9HPT to begin to refine characterization upper limb function.

Motion capture technology offers the ability to more accurately capture changes in motor functions which has implications for improving accuracy of both diagnosis and monitoring of disease progression (Cabaraux et al., 2022). Currently, motion capture technology has been used predominantly in Parkinson’s disease and dementia patient populations and has demonstrated promising outcomes. These studies have demonstrated the ability to provide rich granular information for specific movements related to gait and tremor which were not possible with traditional models of evaluation. However, despite the amount of data that can be obtained from such studies, there are still limitations with anchoring that data with clinically meaningful information (Jeyasingh-Jacob et al., 2024; Movement disorders moment, 2024; Chan et al., 2019; Zhan et al., 2018; Fernandes et al., 2024; Andreopoulou et al., 2019; Eltoukhy et al., 2017; M Alt Murphy et al., 2018).

This study was a first attempt to develop novel biomechanical outcomes of upper extremity function and identify potential clinical signals that may be meaningful. The focus of the study was to model and assess an effective way to digest the rich data provided by complex digital technologies. Most current digital devices in practice have still been distilled to single outcome measure similar to the paper and pencil version, which does not take advantage of the data obtained. For example, the Multiple Sclerosis Performance Test (MSPT), an iPad adaptation of the Multiple Sclerosis Functional Composite (MSFC), demonstrated a new technology-focused approach to quantifying MS related disability but provided no additional outcome data beyond the traditional MSFC measure of total time (Rudick et al., 2014).

In this study, we sought to bridge the current gaps in both upper extremity function characterization in people with MS and utilization of technology-enabled motion capture to record upper extremity biomechanical outcomes. This study aimed to develop novel upper extremity outcomes that improved upon the traditional 9HPT outcome of total time to characterize upper extremity function in PwMS. This study utilized a novel method of motion capture to develop biomechanical outcome measures that leveraged a digital version of the 9HPT. Several novel measures were identified that demonstrated an excellent ability to discriminate PwMS from healthy controls and correlated with disease duration. Those measures are Movement Delay (PiH), which demonstrated a consistent moderate correlation for dominant and nondominant hand, while MDT Total Time was only moderately correlated for dominant hand, but not non-dominant hand. Some additional novel measures which had moderate correlations with clinical symptoms include Movement Time (EH) for arm spasticity and Movement Delay (PiH) and Time to Insert with dysmetria. This may demonstrate the potential of novel measures to quantify unique clinical characteristics that are complementary and distinct to traditional total time, but a more longitudinal study is needed to assess that.

The traditional outcome of Total Time has a defined clinically meaningful change of 20 % which helps clinicians identify when a patient has a meaningful worsening. Although this is valuable, it does not characterize the reason for upper limb dysfunction. For example, a PwMS may have a slowing in Total Time secondary to a decrease in dexterity from a sensory deficit or an increase in spasticity which would require different treatment approaches. In this study, we aimed to develop novel outcome measures that not only have the potential to identify subtle dysfunction but also may have unique signals for specific clinical deficits. Although this study is limited to a single time point, it was observed that several of the novel measures moderately correlated with clinical measures including arm spasticity and upper extremity weakness. Future studies are needed to determine if these relationships persist longitudinally and offer unique markers for specific clinical deficits that could guide treatment recommendations.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted with a small cohort of individuals which limits the generalizability of the findings and clinical implications of the study findings. Second, this study only collected data from a single time point which does not allow for characterization of sensitivity to changes over time. Third, we did not collect the average or median time from clinical assessment to the time the performance measures were collected and instead just used a visit that was within 12 months. Finally, this study utilized two pieces of specialized equipment: the Kinect® device used for the motion capture and the MDT which has a specialized iPad case for administration. Utilizing the motion capture allowed for the development of the advanced mathematical models to compute the novel outcomes, but now that the models have been created the use of the Kinetic device will not be needed. The iPad device used for the 9HPT captures all peg movements with timestamps for each removal and insertion. The model created in this study only requires the timestamp information to generate the novel outcomes therefore the motion capture is not needed moving forward. Generalizability is still limited due to the use of the iPad based 9HPT.

This study provides novel insight into upper extremity function of PwMS. Although digital technologies have great promise to facilitate more accurate and granular assessments of function there have been challenges in the development of meaningful outcomes. Our study attempted to leverage the rich data obtained from a digital adaption of the 9HPT to develop novel biomechanical outcomes and begin to understand the potential clinical relationships of these measures. Although we identified several novel measures that were excellent at discriminating MS from HC there was significant variability for the correlation of these measures with clinical outcomes. These findings support the ongoing potential of biomechanical outcomes but also highlight the need for longitudinal studies to identify the outcomes with the greatest potential to consistently detect meaningful change over time. Additionally, as the findings compared dominant and non-dominant hand performance, future studies could be expanded by incorporating a patient-reported measure such as the ABILHAND questionnaire to better capture functional hand use and assess whether hand dominance changes secondary to MS.
