The Predictive Value of the Combined FVC/DLCO Ratio and 1-Minute Heart Rate Recovery After Exercise in Stable Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease with Pulmonary Hypertension
Authors: Ning Wang, Jia Xing, Yuqiang Zheng, Jing Wang, Linlin Liu, Ying Tian, Zhaobo Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the predictive value of combining the pulmonary function index forced vital capacity to diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide ratio (FVC/DLCO) with 1‑minute heart rate recovery (HRR1) in stable chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) complicated by pulmonary hypertension (PH), and to develop a machine learning‑based prediction model.MethodsA total of 159 stable COPD patients (COPD group) and 52 stable COPD patients with PH (COPD+PH group) were enrolled. Baseline data were collected. Predictive performance of FVC/DLCO and HRR1 alone and in combination (logistic regression) was assessed. Four machine learning models (Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, SVM) were trained using all features.ResultsThe COPD+PH group showed significantly higher FVC/DLCO and lower HRR1. The combined FVC/DLCO+HRR1 model achieved an AUC of 0.781 (95% CI: 0.709–0.853), outperforming either marker alone. The Random Forest model performed best (AUC = 0.923, 95% CI: 0.881–0.961; accuracy 0.892; sensitivity 0.871; specificity 0.912), with good calibration. FVC/DLCO and HRR1 were the top two predictors.ConclusionCombining FVC/DLCO with HRR1 enhances predictive accuracy for PH in stable COPD. The Random Forest model shows excellent performance, but external validation is needed before clinical implementation.Keywords: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary function, heart rate recovery, prediction model, machine learning, random forest

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common, preventable respiratory condition characterised by persistent respiratory symptoms and airflow limitation and remains a leading cause of death worldwide.1–3 Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a frequent and severe complication of COPD, closely associated with chronic hypoxaemia, disruption of the pulmonary vascular bed, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammatory responses.4–6 The coexistence of COPD and PH significantly increases cardiopulmonary burden, leading to reduced exercise tolerance, poorer quality of life, increased risk of acute exacerbations, and higher mortality.7 Nevertheless, early identification and accurate diagnosis of PH in patients with COPD remain challenging in routine clinical practice. Right heart catheterisation (RHC) is the gold standard for PH diagnosis; however, as an invasive procedure with considerable cost and associated risks, it is impractical for widespread or repeated use in COPD patients.4,8 Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is the most commonly employed non‑invasive screening tool, enabling indirect estimation of pulmonary artery systolic pressure through parameters such as tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TRV). However, the clinical limitations of non‑RHC‑based PH diagnosis are substantial in COPD. Hyperinflation and emphysema often lead to poor acoustic windows and unreliable TRV measurement, reducing the accuracy of echocardiography for mild‑to‑moderate PH.9,10 Clinical assessment alone lacks specificity and detects PH only at advanced stages. These limitations underscore the need for simple, reproducible functional indicators to aid early identification. Therefore, identifying simple, non‑invasive, and reliable biomarkers or functional indicators to improve early diagnosis of COPD‑related PH is of great clinical importance.

Pulmonary function testing is fundamental to the diagnosis and assessment of COPD. The diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (DLCO) reflects the integrity of gas exchange across the alveolar–capillary membrane. In PH, DLCO is often markedly reduced due to a diminished pulmonary vascular bed, vascular remodelling, and interstitial changes.11 The ratio of forced vital capacity to DLCO (FVC/DLCO) has emerged as a potentially more specific indicator of pulmonary vascular involvement, and its clinical value in COPD‑associated PH is increasingly recognised.12,13 Heart rate recovery (HRR), defined as the rate at which heart rate declines following exercise cessation, primarily reflects parasympathetic reactivation and serves as a key measure of autonomic balance.14 Autonomic dysfunction plays a significant role in the pathophysiology of both COPD and PH. Patients with COPD and PH, owing to chronic hypoxia, oxidative stress, and systemic inflammation, are more susceptible to reduced parasympathetic tone, which may manifest as slowed HRR.15 The 1‑minute heart rate recovery value after exercise (HRR1) has been shown to correlate closely with prognosis in various cardiopulmonary diseases. Accordingly, the present study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic utility of FVC/DLCO and HRR1, both individually and in combination, for detecting PH in patients with stable COPD.

In recent years, machine learning techniques have been increasingly applied in medical research. Their capacity to integrate high‑dimensional, multimodal clinical data and uncover complex nonlinear relationships enables the construction of more accurate predictive models.16,17 In the context of COPD and PH, machine learning models have demonstrated potential for phenotype stratification and prognostic prediction. Building upon this, the present study further developed and compared multiple machine learning models—specifically Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, and Support Vector Machine—by incorporating multimodal clinical features, with the aim of identifying optimal strategies to assist in the early diagnosis of PH in COPD patients.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

This study enrolled patients with stable COPD who were followed up as outpatients or inpatients in the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine, Hengshui People’s Hospital, between May 2023 and December 2024. The diagnosis of COPD was established according to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) guidelines,18 requiring the presence of respiratory symptoms (dyspnoea, chronic cough, or sputum production), a history of risk factor exposure, and a post‑bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio <0.70. The diagnosis of PH was made in accordance with the latest European Society of Cardiology/European Respiratory Society (ESC/ERS) guidelines19 confirmed either by right heart catheterisation (resting mean pulmonary artery pressure [mPAP] ≥ 25 mmHg) or by multidisciplinary team assessment based on comprehensive clinical evaluation (including typical symptoms, signs, echocardiographic findings suggestive of high PH probability, and evidence of right heart failure). Clinical stability was defined as the absence of acute exacerbation within the preceding 4 weeks (ie, no worsening of respiratory symptoms requiring increased medication or hospitalisation).

Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥40 years; (2) confirmed stable COPD. Exclusion criteria were: (1) concurrent pulmonary diseases potentially affecting lung function or pulmonary artery pressure; (2) concurrent severe cardiac disease; (3) known alternative causes of PH; (4) use of medications significantly affecting heart rate within the past month; (5) musculoskeletal, neurological, or other conditions precluding pulmonary function testing or the 6‑minute walk test; (6) pregnancy or lactation; (7) refusal to participate.

Based on these criteria, 159 patients with stable COPD alone (COPD group) and 52 patients with stable COPD complicated by PH (COPD+PH group) were enrolled. Of the 52 COPD+PH patients, 21 (40.4%) were RHC-confirmed and 31 (59.6%) by MDT. All participants provided written informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hengshui People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2023124).

Data Collection and Processing

All data were collected within one week of enrolment by uniformly trained investigators.

Baseline Data

Demographic information (age, sex, smoking history, height, weight, BMI) was obtained via questionnaires and physical examinations. Detailed medical histories and medication use were recorded.

Laboratory Tests

Morning fasting venous blood samples were analyzed using an automated haematology analyser for white blood cell count (WBC), mean platelet volume (MPV), platelet count (PLT), and red cell distribution width standard deviation (RDW‑SD). High‑sensitivity C‑reactive protein (hs‑CRP) was measured by immuno-turbidimetry, and N‑terminal pro‑B‑type natriuretic peptide (NT‑proBNP) by electro-chemiluminescence.

Cardiac Ultrasound

Standard colour Doppler ultrasound was performed with the patient in the left lateral decubitus position. Measured parameters included pulmonary artery diameter (PAD), tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TRV), estimation of pulmonary artery systolic pressure (simplified Bernoulli equation, ΔP = 4V2) when TRV was measurable, and left ventricular ejection fraction (EF).

Pulmonary Function Testing

All subjects underwent spirometry (Vmax22D, USA) according to ATS/ERS standards. Forced vital capacity (FVC) and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) were measured, and the FVC/DLCO ratio was calculated.

Six‑Minute Walk Test (6MWT)

Conducted on a flat 30‑m corridor following ATS guidelines. Total distance walked (6MWD) was recorded. Perceived exertion was assessed using the modified Borg scale before and immediately after exercise. Post-exercise 1-minute heart rate recovery (HRR1) was defined as the heart rate recorded at the immediate conclusion of the 6MWT minus the heart rate recorded at 1-minute post-exercise. All tests were performed on the same day where possible, under stable medication and without supplemental oxygen unless clinically required.

Model Construction and Evaluation

Traditional Prediction Models

Univariate logistic regression models were built using either FVC/DLCO or HRR1 as the sole independent variable, with PH presence (yes = 1, no = 0) as the dependent variable. A multivariate logistic regression model (Combined model) incorporated both FVC/DLCO and HRR1 as independent variables. A separate multivariable logistic regression model was then constructed, incorporating all variables that were statistically significant in univariate analysis, to assess the independent associations with PH.

Machine‑Learning Models

Four machine learning models (Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, SVM) were built using all baseline features. Missing continuous data were imputed with the median. For tree‑based models, raw values were used; for SVM with RBF kernel, features were standardized to mean 0 and SD 1. To ensure robust performance, bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations was applied. In each iteration, hyperparameters were tuned via 10‑fold cross‑validation on the bootstrap sample, optimizing for AUC; the final model was evaluated on out‑of‑bag samples. Performance metrics (AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1) were averaged over 1000 iterations, with 95% CIs from the bootstrap percentiles. Optimized hyperparameters were: Random Forest (ntree = 500, mtry = 4, nodesize = 2); XGBoost (learning_rate = 0.1, max_depth = 5, subsample = 0.8, colsample_bytree = 0.8); LightGBM (num_leaves = 31, learning_rate = 0.05, feature_fraction = 0.8); SVM (C = 10, gamma = 0.1).

Performance Evaluation

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted for each indicator, the combined model, and the machine‑learning models. The area under the curve (AUC) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated. The DeLong test was used to compare AUC differences (α = 0.05, two‑tailed). For traditional models, the optimal diagnostic threshold was determined by maximizing the Youden’s index (J = sensitivity + specificity − 1); corresponding sensitivity and specificity were reported. For machine‑learning models, accuracy, AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and F1‑score were computed. Feature importance ranking was extracted for the best‑performing model. Model calibration was evaluated using a calibration plot.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline data were analyzed with SPSS 25.0. Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using independent‑samples t‑tests; non‑normally distributed continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range) and compared with the Mann‑Whitney U-test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequency (percentage) and compared by chi‑square test. A two‑tailed P < 0.05 was considered significant. For logistic regression and machine learning models, bootstrap resampling (1000 replicates) was used to estimate AUC, sensitivity, and specificity, assessing model stability. ROC curve analysis, DeLong tests, and machine‑learning modelling were performed with R software version 4.0.2.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 159 patients with stable COPD alone (COPD group) and 52 patients with stable COPD complicated by pulmonary hypertension (COPD+PH group) were enrolled in this study. There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of age, sex distribution, body mass index (BMI), or smoking history (all P > 0.05). Regarding laboratory findings, the COPD+PH group exhibited significantly higher levels of inflammatory markers (white blood cell count [WBC], high‑sensitivity C‑reactive protein [hs‑CRP]), N‑terminal pro‑B‑type natriuretic peptide (NT‑proBNP), mean platelet volume (MPV), and red cell distribution width–standard deviation (RDW‑SD), along with a significantly lower platelet count (PLT) compared to the COPD group (all P < 0.05). Echocardiographic assessment revealed a significantly wider pulmonary artery diameter (PAD), higher tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TRV), and lower left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) in the COPD+PH group (all P < 0.05). The forced vital capacity to diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide ratio (FVC/DLCO) was significantly elevated in the COPD+PH group (P < 0.05). In the 6‑minute walk test (6MWT), patients with COPD+PH demonstrated a significantly shorter walking distance (6MWD), reduced 1‑minute heart rate recovery (HRR1), and a higher post‑exercise Borg score (all P < 0.05). Predictive Performance of FVC/DLCO and HRR1

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated that the univariate model using FVC/DLCO yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.704 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.626–0.782). At the optimal cut‑off value of 0.441, determined by Youden’s index, the sensitivity and specificity were 75.0% and 59.1%, respectively. The univariate model based on HRR1 achieved an AUC of 0.709 (95% CI: 0.631–0.788), with an optimal cut‑off of 16.283 beats (sensitivity: 80.8%, specificity: 52.2%). The distribution of these markers, illustrated in Figure 1, shows that higher FVC/DLCO values and lower HRR1 values were associated with the COPD+PH group. The combined logistic regression model, incorporating both FVC/DLCO and HRR1, achieved a significantly improved AUC of 0.781 (95% CI: 0.709–0.853), outperforming either single marker alone (vs FVC/DLCO: Z = 3.21, P = 0.001; vs HRR1: Z = 2.89, P = 0.004) (Figure 2). Multivariable logistic regression was performed including all variables that were significant in univariate analysis. As shown in Supplementary Figure S1, the FVC/DLCO ratio and HRR1 remained independently associated with PH. These findings confirm that both markers provide independent predictive value beyond conventional parameters.

Performance of Machine Learning Models

The predictive performance of all four machine learning models is summarised in Table 2. The Random Forest model demonstrated the highest overall predictive capability, achieving an accuracy of 0.892, an AUC of 0.923 (95% CI: 0.881‑0.961), a sensitivity of 0.871, a specificity of 0.912, and an F1‑score of 0.881. The calibration curve (Figure 3) showed good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, with the bias‑corrected curve close to the ideal line. The XGBoost model also performed well, with an accuracy of 0.870 and an AUC of 0.900 (95% CI: 0.850‑0.940). The LightGBM model achieved an accuracy of 0.840 and an AUC of 0.860 (95% CI: 0.800‑0.920), while the Support Vector Machine (SVM) model yielded an accuracy of 0.810 and an AUC of 0.880 (95% CI: 0.820‑0.940).

Feature Importance in the Random Forest Model

Feature importance analysis was performed on the optimal Random Forest model to identify the variables most critical for distinguishing patients with COPD+PH from those with COPD alone. As illustrated in Figure 4, which ranks variables by their Mean Decrease in Gini (with importance increasing from top to bottom), FVC/DLCO and HRR1 emerged as the two most significant predictors. Other important contributors included NT-proBNP, TRV, 6MWD, and PAD. These findings, derived from a machine learning approach, robustly confirm the central role of FVC/DLCO and HRR1 in predicting the presence of PH in patients with stable COPD.

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the predictive value of combining a pulmonary function indicator (FVC/DLCO) and an autonomic function indicator (HRR1) with machine learning algorithms for identifying PH in patients with stable COPD. The main findings can be summarised as follows: (1) Patients with COPD and PH exhibited more pronounced systemic inflammation, increased cardiac workload, elevated pulmonary vascular pressures, impaired gas exchange, reduced exercise tolerance, and autonomic dysfunction manifested as slowed HRR1. (2) The combined use of FVC/DLCO and HRR1 yielded significantly better predictive performance (AUC = 0.781) than either marker alone, underscoring the complementary value of multidimensional assessment in this clinical setting. (3) Among four machine learning models, the Random Forest model incorporating multimodal features achieved the highest predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.923), with feature importance analysis reaffirming FVC/DLCO and HRR1 as key predictors.

Regarding the diagnosis of PH, we acknowledge that RHC remains the gold standard per ESC/ERS guidelines. However, its routine use in stable COPD is limited by invasiveness, cost, and availability. The MDT assessment approach—combining high‑probability TTE findings, clinical evidence of right heart failure, and elevated NT‑proBNP—serves as a pragmatic, real‑world alternative for early identification. Importantly, our analysis confirmed no significant differences in key parameters between RHC‑confirmed and MDT‑diagnosed subgroups, mitigating concerns about misclassification bias.

The elevated FVC/DLCO ratio observed in the COPD+PH group likely reflects an imbalance between pulmonary vascular dysfunction and parenchymal destruction. Specifically, DLCO reduction—attributable to vascular remodelling, capillary bed loss, and impaired alveolar–capillary membrane integrity—may exceed the reduction in FVC resulting from airflow limitation and hyperinflation.11,20 Consequently, the FVC/DLCO ratio may serve as a more specific indicator of pulmonary vascular involvement during COPD progression. Slowed HRR1, on the other hand, clearly indicates impaired autonomic function, particularly diminished parasympathetic reactivation following exercise, which has been associated with disease severity and poorer prognosis in cardiopulmonary conditions.14,21 By combining FVC/DLCO (reflecting parenchymal and vascular function) with HRR1 (reflecting autonomic regulation), the prediction model captures information from distinct pathophysiological pathways, thereby producing a synergistic effect that enhances discriminative capacity. The modest specificity of the combined FVC/DLCO+HRR1 model (52.2%) is a limitation. A low‑specificity screening tool would generate false positives, potentially leading to unnecessary downstream testing. Therefore, this model may be best used as a rule‑out test (high sensitivity to exclude PH when negative) or in combination with other parameters such as NT‑proBNP or TRV.

The application of machine learning models addresses limitations of traditional logistic regression, particularly its restricted ability to capture nonlinear interactions.22 The superior performance of Random Forest (AUC = 0.923) demonstrates that integrating multimodal data can uncover complex discriminative patterns. However, the high AUC should be interpreted cautiously due to the relatively small sample size and lack of external validation.

From a clinical perspective, the invasive nature of right heart catheterisation and the operator‑dependent limitations of echocardiography underscore the need for accessible, cost‑effective screening tools. Pulmonary function tests and the 6‑minute walk test are routinely performed in COPD follow‑up, are non‑invasive, and carry minimal patient burden. Prediction models based on these widely available metrics therefore offer practical feasibility for widespread implementation. The proposed FVC/DLCO + HRR1 combination, together with the high‑performance Random Forest model, could serve as practical screening instruments for COPD‑related PH. This approach may be particularly valuable for patients with inconclusive echocardiographic findings or those unsuitable for invasive haemodynamic assessment, enabling timely identification of high‑risk individuals who warrant further evaluation and targeted intervention.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as a single centre investigation, it included a relatively modest sample size, particularly within the COPD‑PH subgroup. Future multi‑centre, large‑scale prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings across diverse populations and clinical settings. Second, although the diagnosis of PH adhered to established guidelines, not all cases were confirmed by right heart catheterisation, which may introduce some degree of misclassification bias. Third, while the machine learning models demonstrated robust internal performance, their inherent “black‑box” nature poses challenges for complete transparency and direct clinical interpretability. Efforts to enhance model explainability, such as through feature importance ranking and the development of nomograms or simplified scoring systems, represent important directions for future research.

Conclusions

The FVC/DLCO ratio combined with HRR1 may help identify PH in stable COPD, and the Random Forest model showed promising performance in this derivation cohort. However, external validation is required before these findings can be considered for clinical practice.
