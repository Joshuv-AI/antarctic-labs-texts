Utility of omnipolar mapping–guided cavotricuspid isthmus block
Authors: Hideyuki Hasebe, Yoshitaka Furuyashiki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOmnipolar mapping is an emerging technology with the potential to identify the critical conduction site in the cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI).ObjectiveThis study aimed to elucidate the efficacy of omnipolar mapping–guided targeted ablation to create a CTI block.MethodsPatients who underwent a CTI block using radiofrequency applications (RFAs) were included. Omnipolar mapping was performed during CTI-dependent atrial flutter or pacing from the coronary sinus with a drive train (S1) and a single extra stimulus (S2), and 2 omnipolar maps were created: one with annotation of local potentials after S1 pacing (S1 map) and the other after S2 pacing (S2 map). RFAs were preferentially attempted at the atrial activation focusing sites (AAFSs) where atrial electrical excitation conducts centripetally toward those sites and centrifugally away from them in the omnipolar map.Results50 patients were included. AAFSs were identified in 33 of 38 patients in whom an omnipolar map was created during sustained atrial flutter and in the S2 map in 11 of 12 patients in whom an omnipolar map was created during programmed pacing from the coronary sinus. No AAFS was identified in the S1 map. In the 44 patients in whom AAFSs were identified, a block line in the CTI was completed only by RFAs at the AAFSs, and a continuous linear ablation in the CTI was not necessary.ConclusionAn omnipolar mapping system can identify critical sites for a CTI block by visualizing AAFSs. The AAFSs might be preferable ablation targets in a targeted CTI block.

Key Findings ▪Omnipolar technology can visualize the activation vector. A critical conduction site, that is, atrial activation focusing site (AAFS), in the cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI) could be identified in most patients using omnipolar technology.▪A block line in the CTI was completed by radiofrequency applications at the AAFSs, and a continuous linear lesion was not necessary among patients in whom AAFSs were identified.▪Omnipolar mapping during programmed pacing was useful to identify AAFSs in patients in whom sustained atrial flutter could not be induced.

Introduction

The creation of a complete bidirectional block line across the cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI) has been the established ablation strategy for CTI-dependent atrial flutter (AFL). However, in some patients, it is difficult to create a durable bidirectional block in the CTI owing to several anatomic variants, such as prominent pectinate muscles, a prominent Eustachian ridge, and a sub-Eustachian pouch.1, 2, 3 Usually, an anatomic approach in which an ablation line is created across the CTI from the ventricular side to the inferior vena cava (IVC) around the 6-o’clock position is adopted to create a CTI block.4,5 However, several previous studies have reported that targeted ablation in the CTI is also useful, implying that a continuous linear ablation is not always required.6,7

Omnipolar mapping is an emerging technology that uses a clique, or collection of electrodes, to generate multiple bipolar and unipolar electrograms.8,9 Omnipolar technology calculates electrograms at each clique in 360° so that the angle at which the wavefront is reaching the mapping catheter does not matter. Therefore, omnipolar mapping can visualize the activation vector and has the potential to identify the critical conduction site in the CTI. In this study, we aimed to elucidate the efficacy of omnipolar mapping–guided targeted ablation to create a CTI block.

Methods

Study subjects

We prospectively enrolled patients undergoing a de novo CTI block for clinically documented CTI-dependent AFL or CTI-dependent AFL induced during the ablation procedure for atrial fibrillation in Shizuoka Saiseikai General Hospital between July 2021 and April 2024. All patients underwent an ablation protocol as described later. The study protocol was approved by the local institutional review board (approval number: 20240601), and all patients provided a written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Catheter ablation

General principles

All antiarrhythmic drugs were discontinued for at least 5 half-lives before the procedure. Electrophysiological study and catheter ablation were performed under conscious sedation with midazolam. Surface electrocardiograms and intracardiac electrograms were continuously monitored and stored on a LabSystem PRO (Bard Electrophysiology) recording system. A duodecapolar electrode deflectable catheter (Inquiry; Abbott) was placed in contact with the tricuspid annulus (TA), and a decapolar electrode deflectable catheter (Inquiry; Abbott) was inserted into the coronary sinus (CS). An omnipolar map was created in the CTI using an Advisor HD Grid mapping catheter (Abbott) and the EnSite Velocity 3-dimensional mapping system (Abbott). The details of omnipolar mapping were described previously.8,9 In brief, omnipolar technology derives electrograms from the E-field of a uniform traveling wave that is sensed by at least 3 closely spaced unipolar electrodes lying along the plane of the myocardium. In addition, omnipolar electrograms provide localized and time-consistent measures, such as maximal voltage and activation direction. Radiofrequency energy was delivered using a TactiFlex contact-force irrigated-tip ablation catheter (Abbott) at a power of 35 W. Completion of the CTI block was confirmed by mapping during pacing from the CS. The block in the opposite direction was confirmed by the conventional differential pacing method.

Mapping and ablation

If the patient presented in sustained AFL or sustained AFL was induced by burst atrial pacing from the CS, an omnipolar map was created during AFL. If the patient presented in sinus rhythm and sustained AFL was not induced by burst atrial pacing from the CS, an omnipolar map was created during pacing from the CS. We hypothesized that critical conduction sites would be highlighted by extra stimulation.10,11 Therefore, we applied atrial pacing from the CS with a drive train (S1) at 600 ms and a single extra stimulus (S2, coupled at 20 ms above the atrial effective refractory period), and 2 omnipolar maps were created: one with annotation of the local potentials after S1 pacing (S1 map) and the other after S2 pacing (S2 map).

In the omnipolar maps, particular attention was paid to the conduction vector. The atrial activation focusing site (AAFS) was defined as a site where atrial electrical excitation conducts centripetally toward the site and centrifugally away from the site. Radiofrequency applications (RFAs) were preferentially attempted at AAFSs with a duration of 30 seconds. If no AAFSs were identified, an anatomic ablation line was created by RFAs with a duration of 40 seconds across the CTI from the ventricular side to the IVC. If the block line in the CTI could not be completed by RFA at the AAFSs, a new omnipolar map in the CTI was created during AFL or constant pacing from the CS. Additional RFAs were delivered at the newly identified AAFSs. These procedures were repeated until the block line in the CTI was completed. Follow-up

The patients were scheduled for an outpatient clinic visit approximately 4 weeks after the procedure and every 2–6 months thereafter for at least 2 years after the ablation session. A 24-hour Holter electrocardiogram was performed 6 months after the ablation.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables are reported as number and percentage. Statistical differences were tested using the Wilcoxon test. Statistical significance was considered when the P value was ˂.05. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Prism Software Inc).

AAFSs identified in omnipolar maps

An omnipolar map was created during sustained AFL in 3 of the 15 patients with paroxysmal AFL, in all 15 patients with persistent AFL, and in all 20 patients in whom sustained AFL was induced during catheter ablation for atrial fibrillation. In the 38 patients in whom an omnipolar map was created during sustained AFL, AAFS was identified in 33 of the patients (87%) but not identified in the remaining 5 patients (13%). In the 12 patients in whom an omnipolar map was created during pacing from the CS, no AAFS was identified in the S1 map. However, AAFS was identified in the S2 map in 11 of these patients (92%). In the 11 patients, the interval from the pacing spike to local electrograms at the AAFSs during S2 pacing was significantly longer than that during S1 pacing (40 [36–42] vs 72 [68–80] ms; P < .001). In the remaining patient (8%), no AAFS could be identified even in the S2 map.

In summary, AAFSs were identified in 44 of 50 enrolled patients (88%), and the distribution of the 44 AAFSs identified is shown in Figure 4B. Fragment potentials were observed on 36 initially identified AAFSs (82%), double potentials on 6 sites (14%), and either of the 2 on 37 sites (84%).

Ablation results

Among the 44 patients (88%) in whom AAFSs were identified, a block line in the CTI was completed by 2 RFAs (1–2) at the AAFSs in 24 patients (55%). In the remaining 20 patients, a block line could not be completed by 3.5 RFAs (3–4) at the AAFSs. A new omnipolar map was created during AFL in 9 of these patients and during constant pacing from the CS in the remaining 11 patients. A new AAFS was identified in all of these patients, and a block line in the CTI was completed by additional RFA at the newly identified AAFS. The number of RFAs was significantly larger at the initially identified AAFSs than that at the newly identified AAFSs (2 [2–3] vs 1 [1–2]; P = .03).

An anatomic CTI block was performed in the 6 patients in whom no AAFS was identified. A block line was successfully created with 10.5 RFAs (8–13) in all 6 patients. The total number and duration of RFA were significantly smaller in patients treated with AAFS-guided approach than those treated with an anatomic approach (total number of RFAs 4 [3–5] vs 10.5 [8–13]; P < .001; total duration of RFA 120 [90–150] vs 420 [320–520] seconds; P < .001).

Preexisting conduction block

The activation maps created after the completion of the block line showed the preexisting conduction blocks in all patients in whom AAFSs were identified. Double potentials were observed on the preexisting conduction block region in 33 patients (75%), fragment potentials in 37 patients (84%), and either of the 2 in 41 patients (93%) in the initial map. In the voltage map of the initial map, the bipolar voltage amplitude on the preexisting conduction block region was significantly lower than that on the AAFS (0.9 [0.5–1.5] vs 1.8 [0.9–3.4] mV; P < .001). A scar was observed on the preexisting conduction block region in 4 patients (9%).

Ablation outcome

The mean follow-up period was 25 ± 9 months. None of the enrolled patients experienced recurrent AFL. Five patients (10%) had AF after the ablation procedures, and 4 of these 5 patients (80%) underwent catheter ablation of AF. All these 4 patients had been treated with an AAFS-guided approach. No reconnection of the block line in the CTI was observed in any of the 4 patients.

Discussion

Main findings

In most patients, AAFSs were identified in the omnipolar maps created during CTI-dependent AFL or programmed pacing from the CS. In these patients, the block line in the CTI was completed only by RFA at the AAFSs, and a continuous linear ablation between the TA and IVC was not necessary. The total number and duration of RFA were significantly smaller in patients treated with an AAFS-guided approach than those treated with an anatomic approach, suggesting the efficacy of the AAFS-guided approach. In the patients in whom omnipolar maps were created during programmed pacing from the CS, although AAFSs were obscure in the S1 map, AAFSs could be identified in the S2 map. The activation maps created after the completion of the block line showed the preexisting conduction blocks.

AAFS and preexisting conduction block

By comparing the map before and after the CTI block, the AAFSs corresponded to the conduction gaps situated either between preexisting conduction blocks or between a preexisting conduction block and an anatomic barrier such as the TA or IVC. The presence of a preexisting conduction block would cause electrical excitation to converge in the conduction gap. In our study, preexisting conduction block was observed, and a continuous linear ablation was not necessary in 88% of the patients.

Waki et al12 investigated the muscular architecture of the CTI in 50 hearts obtained at autopsy. The CTI was made up of longitudinal or obliquely arranged bundles of atrial myocardium separated by connective tissue. In this study, nonuniform muscular architectures in the CTI, such as various muscular defects, small and large, and fibrous Eustachian valve were observed in 74% of the hearts obtained at autopsy. In contrast, in the clinical study by Baccillieri13 using angiography, a complex CTI morphology, such as a pouch, was observed only in 11% of the patients studied. In our study, a scar was observed on the preexisting conduction block region in only 9% of the patients, which is consistent with the study by Baccillieri.13 In contrast, preexisting conduction blocks were identified in 88% of our patients. Considering these proportions, it is likely that only a small proportion of the preexisting conduction blocks are associated with clinically detectable abnormal structures, such as a pouch, whereas the majority are associated with nonuniform muscular architectures that can only be identified through anatomic examination. The low-voltage amplitude of the preexisting conduction block region might be associated with the nonuniform muscular architectures. Further studies are needed to clarify this issue.

Conventional bipolar local activation time mapping comprises a collection of local activation times. Therefore, it could be influenced by several factors such as variation of tachycardia cycle length and unstable reference catheter electrocardiogram. In contrast, omnipolar mapping, which is based on a traveling wave relationship between unipolar and directional bipolar waveforms, does not require a separate reference time.8,9,14 Therefore, it can be robust to cycle length or detection variations and can directly visualize the wavefront.

Although the AAFS was obscure in the S1 map, it could be identified in the S2 map in most patients. Lammers et al15 showed that premature beats increased inhomogeneity in conduction and decreased conduction velocity in the rabbit atrium. The inhomogeneous conduction and conduction slowing induced by extra pacing might have contributed to the high lightning of AAFS in the S2 map.

After ablation of initially identified AAFSs, additional AAFSs were noted on remapping. Among the 11 patients in whom an omnipolar map was created during pacing from the CS and AAFSs were identified in the initial maps, the initially identified AAFSs were not detected on the S1 maps, whereas the newly identified AAFSs were detected on the S1 maps. The number of RFAs was significantly larger at the initially identified AAFSs than that at the newly identified AAFSs. This finding suggests that the width of the newly identified AAFSs was shorter than that of the initially identified AAFSs, which might be associated with the manifestation of the newly identified AAFSs in the S1 maps.

In our study, the omnipolar map was created only during either sustained AFL or pacing from the CS. Therefore, it is unclear whether there is a concordance in AAFS between the 2 rhythms. Even during AFL or during pacing from the CS, electrical excitation is thought to pass between the preexisting conduction blocks, resulting in the same site being identified as the AAFS. However, further evaluations are needed to accurately clarify this issue.

Previous studies

Several previous studies have reported the efficacy of the targeted CTI block.6,7,16, 17, 18 Sato et al16 revealed that maximum voltage-guided ablation was effective, implying that a continuous ablation between the TA and IVC is not essential. Mechulan et al17 tested the efficacy of a “two line ablation technique” in which 2 different, nonoverlapping, and incomplete lines over the CTI were created. Bidirectional block was achieved by “this two lines ablation technique” in most patients, suggesting that muscular bundles in the CTI are heterogeneous, as opposed to a continuous sheet. Vallès et al18 reported on the utility of a targeted CTI block using omnipolar technology in a different manner from our approach. In their study, a bidirectional CTI block was achieved by ablation at the area of slow conduction identified by omnipolar technology. Conduction gaps are often accompanied by conduction slowing. In our study, fragment potentials were observed on 82% of the initially identified AAFSs, double potentials on 14% of the sites, and either of the 2 on 84% of the sites. This finding suggests a conduction disturbance on the AAFS. Further studies are needed to accurately evaluate the association between slow conduction and AAFS.

Clinical implications

The greatest advantage of an omnipolar mapping–guided CTI block is its ability to enable efficient ablation while avoiding unnecessary radiofrequency energy applications at the preexisting conduction blocks. The success rate of the conventional anatomic approach is sufficient. However, apart from success rate, unnecessary RFAs in the CTI are associated with several issues including the production of pain, risk of cardiac perforation, and the possibility of damaging elements of the normal atrioventricular nodal conduction pathway.19,20

This personalized ablation can prevent potential difficulties and complications in a CTI block. In particular, RFA should be avoided in pouch-like recesses and areas with sparse myocardium, given that it poses a high risk of cardiac perforation. In 2 large angiographic studies, the presence of a long isthmus, concave morphology, and pouch-like recesses was associated with a high number of RFAs and long x-ray exposure.5,21 Our method may increase the efficacy and safety of a CTI block especially in patients with such anatomic variants.

Although AAFS could be identified during sustained AFL in most patients, it is often difficult to induce sustained AFL. However, an omnipolar mapping–guided CTI block can be applied in such cases because omnipolar mapping using programmed pacing (S2 map) allowed us to identify AAFSs.

Limitations

First, the sample size in the present study is small. Second, the determination of the AAFS is subjective and requires some degree of familiarity. However, for those who regularly use omnipolar mapping, it may not be particularly challenging. Third, although the association between AAFSs and anatomic structures is an important issue, no detailed anatomic assessment of the CTI was performed in the present study. Further studies are needed to clarify this issue.

Conclusion

An omnipolar mapping system can identify critical conduction sites for a CTI block by visualizing AAFSs. The AAFSs might be preferable ablation targets in a targeted CTI block.
