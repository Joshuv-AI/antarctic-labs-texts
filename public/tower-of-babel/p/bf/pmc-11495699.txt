Comparison of a full tibial tunnel or an all-inside technique for anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction: An observational study
Authors: Shengwen Xiang, Zhijiang Ao, Zihao Ren, Guangqing Cai, Zhen Hu, Weiguo Hu, Yangbo Liu, Xing Li, Licheng Wei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study aims to compare the effectiveness of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction performed with either a full tibial tunnel technique or an all-inside technique. Data from patients with ACL rupture who underwent ACL reconstruction using autogenous hamstring tendon grafts between January 2019 and July 2021 were retrospectively reviewed. Patients were followed up for a minimum of 24 months. Preoperative and postoperative International Knee Documentation Committee scores, Lysholm knee scores, visual analog scale pain scores, Tegner activity scale scores, C-reactive protein levels, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, white blood cell (WBC) count, D-dimer levels, and neutrophils were recorded. At the final follow-up, side-to-side differences were assessed with the pivot shift test, Lachman test, anterior drawer test, and a knee laxity-testing device. This study included 135 patients with ACL rupture, with 62 patients (43 males and 19 females, mean age: 34.42 ± 12.79 years) undergoing the full tibial tunnel technique, and 73 patients (58 males and 15 females, mean age: 32.41 ± 11.01 years) undergoing the all-inside techniques (AITs). There were no significant differences in postoperative International Knee Documentation Committee scores, Lysholm knee scores, or Tegner activity scale scores, except at postoperative 6 months. visual analog scale pain scores showed significant improvement in patients that underwent the AIT compared to the full tibial tunnel technique at postoperative 1 week, 2 weeks, and 1 month; however, there were no significant differences at postoperative 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months. C-reactive protein levels, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, WBC count, D-dimer levels, and neutrophils were significantly lower in patients that underwent the AIT compared to the full tibial tunnel technique at postoperative 1 month, but there were no other significant differences in these outcome measures, except for WBC count at postoperative 3 months. There was no significant difference in knee stability at the last follow-up. In ACL reconstruction, functional outcomes following the AIT are satisfactory and similar to the total tibial tunnel technique, but patients experience less pain and a reduced early postoperative inflammatory response. These results highlight the promising potential of AITs in the clinical management of ACL rupture and advocate for their widespread adoption.

1. Introduction

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries are common in sports. ACL rupture affects the stability of the knee joint and can interfere with an individual’s ability to participate in sports and activities of daily living. If not appropriately treated, ACL injuries can lead to pain, joint damage, and disability.[1]

The most widespread ACL reconstruction technologies include femoral fixation using a cortical suspension device and tibial fixation with an interference screw in a full tibial tunnel. Traditionally, independent femoral tunneling through an anteromedial portal is used to perform ACL reconstruction. However, this technique is associated with several potential pitfalls such as socket blowout or difficulty visualizing the position of the guide when the knee is brought into hyperflexion.[2,3]

Unlike this conventional approach, all-inside techniques (AITs) use suspension fixation and a tibial socket instead of a complete tunnel. The femoral tunnel is created using an outside-in technique and inverted drilling is used to create the tibial tunnel. Advantages of AITs include small incisions, cortical bone preservation, shorter graft length, improved graft integration, reduced postoperative pain, and improved aesthetics.[3–8] Previous studies have demonstrated that total internal reconstruction can achieve the same clinical effect as traditional reconstruction[6,9]; consequently, AITs are considered a new option for ACL reconstruction.

Studies investigating the comparative effects of AITs and the full tibial tunnel technique are scarce. Thus, this retrospective analysis aimed to compare the effectiveness of ACL reconstruction based on a full tibial tunnel or an AIT, with a focus on indices of function, inflammation, thrombosis, and biomechanics.

2. Methods

2.1. Study population

The was a retrospective review of medical records of a convenience sample of patients who attended the Department of Orthopedics, Changsha Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital (also known as Changsha Eighth Hospital), between January 2019 and July 2021. Inclusion criteria were: (1) ACL rupture and (2) subsequent ACL reconstruction using autogenous hamstring tendon grafts. Exclusion criteria were: (1) knee joint fractures; (2) grade II or grade III tears of the knee medial collateral ligament, posterior cruciate ligament, lateral collateral ligament, posteromedial corner, or posterolateral corner; (3) meniscus sutures; or (4) previous ACL reconstructive surgery.

This study was approved by the ethics committee of our hospital (Approval No. 2023072017) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Signed informed consent was obtained from all participants.

2.2. Surgical techniques

All surgeries were performed by the same experienced chief physician who has been engaged in orthopedic surgery for 20 years, mainly specializing in surgeries of knee and shoulder joints.

All patients underwent ACL autograft reconstruction with general anesthesia, no tourniquet, and nerve block.

AIT: Harvested tendons (diameter ≥ 8 mm, achieved using the semitendinosus tendon alone in all cases) were woven and sutured into 4 strands, and the ends were suspended on an adjustable loop cortical suspensory device (Tightrope system, Arthrex). Femoral and tibial sockets were constructed using guide pins and retrograde drilling with 4.0 mm drill bits. Prepared tendons were pulled into the sockets using previously placed traction sutures. A titanium adjustable loop (Tightrope system, Arthrex) was used for femoral fixation, and a polyetheretherketone bone anchor system (Arthrex) was used for tibial fixation.

Conventional ACL reconstruction based on a full tibial tunnel: Semitendinosus and gracilis tendons were harvested, folded in half, and twisted and braided using X-519 sutures (diameter of prepared tendon > 8 mm). A femoral socket was constructed using an anteromedial portal drilling approach, the femoral cortex was drilled with a cannulated drill, and the femoral tunnel was expanded with a femoral drill. A guide pin was used to locate the tibial insertion point of the ACL, and an appropriately sized, fully threaded reamer was used to construct a full-length tibial tunnel in an antegrade manner. A Polydioxanone band was used to pull the graft through the anteromedial portal into the femur. An adjustable loop cortical suspensory device (Tightrope system, Arthrex) was used for femoral fixation, and an interface screw (Tightrope system, Arthrex) (measuring 2 mm in diameter larger than the tibial tunnel diameter and 35 mm in length) and self-made portal nails were used for tibial fixation.

2.3. Rehabilitation

Postoperatively, all patients had their affected limbs immobilized with braces. Following recovery from anesthesia, patients engaged in quadriceps isometric training and ankle pump exercises. Passive flexion and extension exercises of the knee joint at the bedside commenced on postoperative day 2, aiming to achieve immediate full knee extension equal to the normal, contralateral knee. Full weight-bearing was allowed. Patients were instructed to return to running at 3 months and sports that involved sudden pivoting or cutting at 6 months after surgery. The follow-up period for all patients spanned 2 years.

2.4. Outcome measures

Data following extensive clinical, subjective, and objective assessments were recorded including operative time, preoperative and postoperative International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) scores, Lysholm knee scores, visual analog scale (VAS) pain scores, Tegner activity scale scores, C-reactive protein (CRP) levels (immunoturbidimetry; Roche COBAS 8000 molecular analyzer), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) (Beijing PUC-2068B), white blood cell (WBC) count (flow cytometry + DNA/RNA fluorescence staining; Sysmex XN-2000), D-dimer levels (immunoturbidimetry; Stago STA-R analyzer), and neutrophils (flow cytometry + DNA/RNA fluorescence staining; Sysmex XN-2000). At the final follow-up, side-to-side differences were assessed with the pivot shift test, Lachman test, anterior drawer test, and a knee laxity-testing device (KT-1000 arthrometer).

2.5. Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 23.0. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the Student t test and repeated measures analysis of variance. Non-normally distributed data were compared using nonparametric tests. Count data were assessed using the chi-square test. A significance level of P < .05 was deemed statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Patient characteristics

This study included data from 135 patients with ACL rupture. Of these, 62 patients (43 males and 19 females; mean age, 34.42 ± 12.79 years [range, 15–60 years]) underwent the full tibial tunnel technique, and 73 patients (58 males and 15 females, mean age, 32.41 ± 11.01 years [range, 17–60 years]) underwent an AIT. Preoperative patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. There were no significant differences in gender, age, body mass index, injured side, and time from injury to surgery between the full tibial tunnel technique and the AIT. There was no significant difference in median (interquartile range) operative time between the two surgical techniques (full tibial tunnel, 91 [89, 93] minutes; AIT, 90 [90, 93] minutes; z/t value −0.076; P = .939).

3.2. IKDC scores

For both surgical techniques, postoperative IKDC scores at 18 months were significantly improved compared to preoperative IKDC scores. However, postoperative IKDC scores at 24 months were not significantly different compared to postoperative IKDC scores at 18 months. There were no significant differences in IKDC scores between the surgical techniques at any point in time.

Repeated measures analysis of variance showed that the main effect of surgical technique on IKDC score was not significant (F = 1.557, P = .214), the main effect of time on IKDC score was significant (F = 8184.086, P < .05), and the interaction effect (surgical technique*time) was significant (F = 18.967, P < .05) (Table 2, Fig. 1).

3.4. VAS pain scores

For the AIT, the postoperative VAS pain score at 1 week was significantly improved compared to the preoperative VAS pain score. For the full tibial tunnel technique, the postoperative VAS pain score at 1 week was not significantly different from the preoperative VAS pain score. VAS pain scores at and after postoperative 2 weeks were significantly improved for both surgical techniques compared to preoperative VAS pain scores.

For the AIT, postoperative VAS pain scores at 1 week, 2 weeks, and 1 month were significantly improved compared to the full tibial tunnel technique. There were no significant differences in VAS pain scores between the surgical techniques at postoperative 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months.

Repeated measures analysis of variance showed that the main effect of surgical technique on VAS pain score was significant (F = 17.294, P < .0001), the main effect of time on VAS pain score was significant (F = 356.169, P < .0001), and the interaction effect (surgical technique*time) was significant (F = 18.480, P < .0001) (Table 4, Fig. 3).

3.5. Tegner activity scale

For both surgical techniques, postoperative Tegner activity scale scores exhibited a significant improvement when compared to preoperative Tegner activity scale scores. Notably, for the AIT, the Tegner activity scale score at 6 months post-surgery demonstrated a significant improvement compared to the full tibial tunnel technique. However, no significant differences in Tegner activity scale scores between the two surgical techniques were observed at other postoperative time points.

Repeated measures analysis of variance showed that the main effect of surgical technique on the Tegner activity scale score was not significant (F = 1.212, P = .273), the main effect of time on the Tegner activity scale score was significant (F = 440.789, P < .0001), and the interaction effect (surgical technique*time) was significant (F = 2.785, P = .014) (Table 5, Fig. 4).

3.6. Other outcomes

The effects of surgical techniques on markers of inflammation (ESR, CRP, WBC, and neutrophils), thrombosis (D-dimer), and stability of the knee are described in the Supplemental Content (Figure 1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N743 and Tables 1–7, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N744).

3.7. Adverse events

For both surgical techniques, no complications such as wound infection, joint stiffness, ligament re-rupture, or nerve damage were reported during follow-up.

4. Discussion

This study aimed to compare the outcomes of patients who underwent ACL reconstruction using the full tibial tunnel or the AIT. The findings revealed no significant differences in patient baseline characteristics including body mass index, time from injury to surgery, operative time, or postoperative IKDC scores, Lysholm knee scores, and Tegner activity scale scores (except at postoperative 6 months) between the two surgical techniques. Both surgical techniques led to a significant improvement in postoperative VAS pain scores when compared to their respective preoperative scores; however, VAS pain scores showed significant improvement in patients that underwent the AIT compared to the full tibial tunnel technique at postoperative 1 week, 2 weeks, and 1 month. CRP levels, ESR, WBC count, D-dimer levels, and neutrophils were significantly lower in patients that underwent the AIT compared to the full tibial tunnel technique at postoperative 1 month, but there were no other significant differences in these outcome measures, except for WBC count, at postoperative 3 months. There were no significant differences in knee stability between the two surgical techniques. There were no complications such as wound infections, joint stiffness, ligament re-ruptures, or nerve damage during the follow-up for any patient.

This study should improve knowledge around ACL reconstruction.[10] Overall, the findings align with previous reports, suggesting that AITs exhibit similar clinical efficacy and lower postoperative pain levels at an early stage when compared to traditional full-tunnel ACL reconstruction techniques.[4,5,9,11–14] Previous prospective studies directly comparing the two techniques showed that AITs tend to be associated with fewer complications than conventional methods[3,4]; although, most patients undergoing ACL reconstruction will develop mild osteoarthritis in the long term.[15]

The results of this study imply AITs offer several advantages compared to traditional full-tunnel ACL reconstruction techniques. These include significantly improved VAS pain scores in the early postoperative period. This advantage of the AIT approach may be attributed to factors such as the smaller skin incision, single bundle reconstruction, and reduced periosteal irritation.[11–13,16,17] However, there were no significant differences in VAS pain scores between the two surgical techniques at postoperative 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months.

Markers of inflammation were significantly lower in patients that underwent the AIT compared to the full tibial tunnel technique during the first month after surgery, but there was no significant difference at 3 months, except for WBC count. This may explain why the early pain response was milder in patients that underwent the AIT. Consistent with this, previous studies have suggested that the AIT is associated with less trauma, better bone preservation, requires fewer tendon grafts, and has a milder early pain response than the full tibial tunnel technique.[18–21]

D-dimer level was significantly lower in patients that underwent the AIT compared to the full tibial tunnel technique during the first month after surgery, but there was no significant difference at 3 months. As postoperative D-dimer level may indicate risk for postoperative thrombosis, theoretically, patients that underwent the full tibial tunnel technique were at a higher risk of postoperative thrombosis than the AIT, but in this study, no patients experienced postoperative thrombosis, indicating that both techniques were safe and effective.

The comparison of biomechanical parameters between patients that underwent the AIT or the full tibial tunnel technique indicated both techniques provided adequate stability. The AIT employs suspension fixation rather than the conventional interfacial screw extrusion. Some researchers have associated this with a “bungee effect” (resulting in longitudinal graft motion) or a “windshield-wiper effect” (resulting in transverse graft motion), which may contribute to bone socket enlargement.[21,22] Conversely, others suggest that the use of a closed socket in AITs, as opposed to full tunnels in conventional ACL techniques, provides less graft length for the bungee cord and windshield-wiper phenomena.[14,16] Biomechanical testing has demonstrated that suspension fixation has a higher load limit compared to screw fixation, with no significant increase in graft slippage.[22] Radiographic assessments, including X-rays and CT scans, have shown that sockets created for AITs exhibit less expansion and better preservation of bone stock compared to full bone tunnels.[23–27]

Although our results support the use of AITs in the clinical management of ACL rupture, it is important to acknowledge the potential drawbacks of AITs, including the potential for long operative times, as mastering newer and more complex surgical techniques like AIT can be time-consuming and come with a steep learning curve.[11,20] Notably, in this study, there was no significant difference in operative time between the full tibial tunnel technique and AIT.

It is essential to acknowledge the limitations of this study, including its use of a convenience sample of patients with no formal sample size calculation, small sample size, and relatively short follow-up period. The small sample size may be because few patients were eligible for this study due to the rare occurrence of isolated lesions of the ACL.[28] To provide a more comprehensive assessment of the safety and efficacy of ACL reconstruction based on the full tibial tunnel or the AIT, well-designed case-control studies and randomized controlled trials with larger cohorts and longer-term follow-up are warranted.

5. Conclusions

In ACL reconstruction, functional outcomes following the AIT are satisfactory and similar to the total tibial tunnel technique, but patients experience less pain and a reduced early postoperative inflammatory response. These findings support the potential of AITs as promising techniques that deserve broader utilization in the clinical management of ACL rupture.
