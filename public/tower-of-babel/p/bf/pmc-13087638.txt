Measuring Environmental Noise for Babies in Special Care Nurseries and Neonatal Intensive Care Units on a Large Scale: Research Methods
Authors: Laura Potter, Loretta Gasparini, Can Ertan, Valerie Sung, Jing Wang, Melissa Wake
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsNoise in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) and special care nurseries (SCN) may have adverse physiological effects on the unwell neonates they care for, including permanent hearing impairment. However, existing studies have typically involved a small number of NICUs, noise has not been measured in special care nurseries (SCNs), and randomization to determine causal associations between noise and outcomes is likely impossible. This paper aims to describe a feasible and accurate method for the measurement of noise across multiple NICUs and SCNs to estimate the levels of noise to which individual babies are exposed.MethodsData will be collected in three steps: (1) An Environmental Characterisation Survey that will provide information regarding NICU/SCN layout and help to plan noise intensity data collection; (2) Noise intensity data collection using sound level meters and literature‐based estimates to quantify environmental and device‐associated noise levels; (3) Extraction of clinical data from babies' medical records to determine the duration of exposure to different noise sources during admission. No noise exposure patterns across domains (e.g., ambient, device‐related) and time periods of each neonate will be described.ConclusionThis method should produce valid and rich data whilst also being low in required resources and research burden. If enough NICUs and SCNs take part and between‐unit noise variation is substantial, this would enable future research to model presence and size of causal relationships of noise exposure with neonatal and later linked outcomes, such as hearing loss.

Introduction

Noise levels in Neonatal Intensive Care Units (NICUs) may have adverse effects on neonates in both the short and long term. The United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), along with the American Academy of Paediatrics (AAP), recommends hospital noise level limits of 45 decibels (dB) during the day and 35 dB at night [1]. Studies measuring noise in these environments consistently show levels exceeding these recommendations by between 8 and 60 dB [2]. In the short term, exposure to loud noise causes agitation and triggers a stress response in neonates [1, 3]. This can elevate blood pressure, heart rate and respiratory rate, as well as disrupt sleep, reduce oxygen saturation, and stimulate apnea [1, 3, 4, 5]. One study of 61 newborns in a Brazilian NICU found significant heart rate elevation and oxygen desaturation upon loud noise exposure [6]. In the long term, excessive noise may cause neonatal hearing loss secondary to cochlear damage [7]. Hearing loss can result in developmental difficulties, including in the domain of speech and language [8, 9].

Given these physiologic impacts and the significant Australian newborn NICU/SCN admission rate, calculated to be 18.1% in 2020, accurate noise measurement in this setting is crucial [10]. Recent studies [11, 12] examining noise alongside clinical parameters reflect a growing international interest in understanding how environmental noise influences infant health and care practices. These trends highlight the need for stronger, standardized data to guide clinicians and researchers. On review of the literature, we found studies from around the globe that have measured noise in NICUs using various methodologies [2]. Importantly, all 48 studies found noise levels to be in excess of EPA and AAP recommendations. Of these studies, most measured ambient room noise levels within NICUs, and very few studies measured noise levels generated by devices (e.g., incubators, ventilators, Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) etc.) in simulated NICU settings or the noise levels of specific sources (e.g., people, phones, doors, etc.). Only six of the 48 studies measured noise across more than one site, with three performed across three different NICUs [2].

Significant gaps remain in this research area. The largest studies to date have involved no more than three NICU sites [1, 13, 14], with no studies to our knowledge in SCNs. The extent to which noise levels among NICUs, among SCNs and between NICUs and SCNs differ is unknown but likely substantial due to variations in care, equipment and building characteristics. The impact of noise on babies' immediate and later physiologic and neurodevelopmental outcomes is unknown, as is the relationship between noise impact and the severity of newborn illness or gestational age. Despite the recommendations above, the “safe” noise threshold for NICUs and SCNs remains to be empirically established. If noise data at the individual infant level could be collected across large populations of sick newborns in multiple NICUs/SCNs, these questions could be answered using a natural experimental causal approach.

This paper provides an opportunity to address these gaps in the literature. It aims to develop a method (including equipment, processes, and workflow) for estimating noise across multiple NICUs and SCNs at scale, including neonatal exposure to noise levels from both the room (ambient) and specific medical devices. The proposed methodology will focus on feasible large‐scale estimation of noise exposure whilst still producing accurate and detailed measures that can be applied to wider population‐based cohort studies.

Methods

Measuring Noise

The process of deciding how to measure environmental noise was multifaceted. The research team consulted a literature review [2] and protocol development that had been conducted by medical students supervised by our research team in 2022 on the topic of noise measurement in NICUs/SCNs in the Australian state of Victoria. Next, an updated literature review was performed, including studies published after the 2022 review, and consulted with a neonatal paediatrician and senior research fellow working in Victoria.

Levels of Noise Measurement

Two levels of noise measurement were identified: individual baby level and environment level. At the level of the individual baby, a measurement device could be placed close to each baby's ear to estimate their noise exposure. This could occur for all babies, or only some, extrapolating to estimate the noise exposure of all other babies. Alternatively, at the level of the environment, one could estimate the noise levels of each NICU or SCN room and devices and extrapolate that each baby in those rooms experiences roughly the same noise level during their admission. The pros and cons of both levels of measurement were evaluated, outlined in Table 1. Sound measurement at the individual level was determined to be unfeasible at large scale: it would be time consuming, the monitors could intrude on care, and they could introduce risks including of infection. Consequently, the decision was made to measure noise only at the level of the environment.

Sources of Environmental Noise

Environmental noise was further subdivided into two categories of noise sources: (1) specific medical devices, and (2) ambient noise in each room of the NICU/SCN. The proposed sources of environmental noise measurement, rationale for measurement, and method of data collection (discussed later in this paper) are outlined in Table 2. The medical devices for which noise should be measured are incubators, ventilators, CPAP, cardiorespiratory monitors, and infusion pumps. These were chosen because previous studies showed they contributed most substantially to noise in NICU settings, and they are used across all levels of care in Victorian NICUs/SCNs [5, 15, 16]. Ambient environmental noise should be measured at least across a 24‐h period in each room of a NICU/SCN and encompasses noise from different sources including, for example, people, phones, doors, cupboards, and handwashing stations [2].

Noise Parameters

Noise exposure should be measured as noise intensity. This is to be measured in dB with A‐weighted sound pressure levels to approximate sound frequencies perceived by the human ear [17]. Measurements will also be reported in unweighted dBSPL scale to account for potential differences in auditory sensitivity between neonates and adults. Response time is the rate at which a measurement device, such as a sound level meter, responds to noise. Slow (S) response time is a response to noise in 1 s and is used to measure ambient noise. Impulse (I) response time is a more rapid response to noise and is used to measure short, loud noises, such as a door closing. Both S and I response times should be used to detect general (continuous or Gaussian) and impulsive (short high‐amplitude bursts usually of high intensity) noises [17].

The following four general noise outcomes should be measured: Leq_day and Leq_night. Leq is the equivalent continuous sound level, in dB, with the same total sound energy as the fluctuating levels measured. It is used to determine an average sound level.L10_day and L10_night. Data Collection

The first step is the distribution of an Environmental Characterisation Survey, seen in Figure 1, to each site for staff to complete. This survey will help plan noise intensity data collection. It will provide an understanding of the layout of each NICU/SCN, including the number of rooms, beds, and specific medical devices located in each room. The resultant information, in conjunction with NICU/SCN site visits, will be used to determine the number of devices required and the most suitable location for each measurement device at each site.

NICU/SCN environmental characterisation survey.

In step two, noise intensity data should be collected according to medical devices and ambient noise, as outlined in Table 2. These data will be used to estimate how much noise a baby is exposed to during their stay in a NICU/SCN. Regarding medical devices, noise intensity data from existing literature have been extracted. Table 3 lists for each type of device the function being measured, the measurement in dB and the literature source. Regarding ambient noise, noise intensity data should be collected at least across a 24‐h period in each room using sound level meters (SLMs) [2]. International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) 61672 [6] is an international standard for SLMs, categorized into class 1 and class 2, with class 1 being more precise. This standard sets out the requirements for how these meters should respond to different frequencies and time variations in sound, ensuring they can accurately capture the intensity and variation of sound levels. IEC 61672 SLMs capture key data, including (1) sound pressure level indicating the loudness of the sound, (2) frequency weighting adjusting the meter's response at different frequencies, and (3) time weighting reflecting how quickly the meter responds to changes in sound levels (fast, slow, impulse).

This period is long enough to accurately estimate noise exposure, whilst remaining feasible to repeat across multiple NICU/SCN sites using limited equipment. Staff or research team at each site should be appropriately trained in using SLM devices. In terms of device placement, on review of the literature, SLMs are most often placed in the center of the room, but the details for how this is done are vague [2]. The proposed method involves suspending SLMs in the air to avoid sound obstruction and surface‐borne noise (e.g., hanging from an overhead equipment boom). However, variation in within‐room location between sites due to inherent structural variation is likely to be needed and will be recorded. A standardised placement protocol will be developed and documented across all sites.

In step three, each individual baby's time exposed to noise should be estimated using data extracted from NICU/SCN babies' clinical records. For our current study—Generation Victoria (GenV) [18], the ethical approval is in place (Royal Children's Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC)‐2019/11), including consent to access clinical data.

The details of clinical data extraction and ethics approvals have been published previously [19]. Extracted clinical data should specify both the duration each baby stayed in a NICU/SCN and the dates and duration of specific noise‐generating medical devices under usual operating conditions. For ventilators, the existing literature only reported noise measurements at different flow rates (L/min) [10]. It is recommended to collect NICU/SCN babies' clinical data for ventilator flow rate, which can be used to determine an average flow rate used for babies across the sites for which this method is used. This average value can then be used in data analysis as described later in this paper. Extraordinary acoustic exposures such as transport, imaging, or surgical procedures should also be recorded in clinical notes/logs. This information should be collected where possible to account for their contribution to cumulative exposure.

Data Management and Analysis Plan

Noise intensity data will be stored in each SLM in the form of a digital file that can be retrieved once measurements are completed at a site.

The measured general noise outcomes will allow analysis of environmental noise. The analysis plan of impulsive noise outcomes is beyond the scope of the proposed methods described in this paper, but the raw data should be made available for research use (e.g., to be planned in a future protocol).

To estimate noise exposure for each baby admitted to an SCN/NICU, environmental (room‐level) and clinical (device‐associated) data will be collected. Room‐level noise exposure will be captured using sound level meter (SLM) data summarized as Leq and L10 values during daytime and nighttime periods. Clinical data on the duration of use for medical devices (e.g., ventilators, CPAP, etc.) will be linked to average noise levels reported in the literature or obtained from empirical measurements.

Exposure patterns across noise domains (e.g., ambient, device‐related) and time periods will be described. These patterns will be explored using multivariable modeling techniques to examine associations between different types, durations, and intensities of exposure and infant outcomes. Input from acoustic experts and medical statisticians will guide the final analytical approach.

Statistical analysis methods will depend on specific research questions and outcomes (which is outside the scope of this paper). An example is examining whether noise exposure in a NICU/SCN is the cause of hearing outcomes at 5 years. In this case, NICU/SCN noise exposure estimates could be linked to a statewide hearing outcomes database (e.g., the Victoria Infant Hearing Screening Program (VIHSP)). NICU/SCN noise exposure indicators could be added to a regression model along with confounding variables, to estimate the magnitude of the causal effect. If NICU or SCN noise exposures are found to vary more or less randomly at given levels of care (i.e., approximating natural experiments), clustered hearing outcomes could also be compared at the unit level to triangulate estimates of causality.

Strengths and Limitations

There are three main strengths of this method. The first is that multiple sources of noise exposure will be measured using both general and peak outcome measures. Whilst producing rich data, this remains a low burden and non‐intrusive method as there is no direct contact with babies, and some data are sourced from the literature. Second, this study provides a methodology to measure noise exposure for much larger sample sizes than previously achieved. This allows for greater precision than do the smaller sample sizes to date, and also allows for natural experiment approaches that capitalize on unit‐by‐unit variations in noise exposure. Noise measurement at the level of all individual babies is not feasible at scale due to the time and resources it requires and the risks it poses, whereas measuring at room level and then extrapolating to all babies passing through those rooms is much more feasible and poses no risks to babies. Third, this study provides an opportunity for future causal analyses linking certain amounts of noise exposure with specific neonatal physiologic and neurodevelopmental outcomes. This could, for example, generate new knowledge about safe thresholds of noise for infants at various gestational ages and severities of illness, expecting that very immature ears or brains could be more susceptible to permanent damage than those of term babies.

There are three main limitations of this method. A key limitation is that noise exposure will be estimated rather than directly measured at the individual baby level. Room‐level SLM data may not fully capture individual variability in exposure, and some device‐generated noise may be recorded both in environmental and clinical data sources, creating potential for overlap. Ideally, cumulative, longitudinal noise exposure would be assessed using accurate, serial, individual‐level monitoring. However, this is rarely feasible at scale due to resource, ethical, and logistical constraints. For example, Generation Victoria (GenV) [19]—a statewide birth cohort in Victoria, Australia—has recruited over 9000 SCN/NICU babies, making it impractical to measure noise exposure for every individual baby. Second, noise levels across day and night will be assumed to be constant, when in fact babies will be exposed to variations in noise throughout the day and night that this method will not capture. Third, observer bias may result in the underestimation of noise levels, for example, staff closing doors or speaking more quietly. This is unavoidable, as staff will be informed that noise levels are being recorded. However, it will be minimized by educating staff regarding the study, asking them to go about their work as usual, and measuring noise over a 24‐h period. Fourth, incubators may amplify noise by acting as resonance chambers, potentially increasing infants' exposure [20]. While this effect is recognized, variations between incubator models remain poorly understood and warrant further investigation. Last, this method is a concept and has not undergone real‐world testing. Therefore, our assumptions of feasibility and accuracy at scale are not yet confirmed. The results of this pilot study will be crucial in determining the method's effectiveness and identifying areas for refinement before broader implementation.

Conclusion

It is well established that NICUs are noisy places and that this excessive noise has adverse physiological effects on neonates. This method allows measurement of noise on the largest scale to date. This method will produce valid and rich data whilst remaining low burden. Various sources of noise will be considered, and noise levels will be operationalized in multiple ways. There is no direct contact with babies and some data are sourced from existing studies in the literature, making it low burden and of negligible risk. Finally, the proposed method provides an opportunity to establish causal relationships between noise exposure and neonatal outcomes in future research.
