How can cities accelerate, support and evaluate actions for active movement for health: protocol for CITY-MOVE, a multicase implementation research study in six cities in three continents
Authors: Josefien van Olmen, Lukar Thornton, Rawlance Ndejjo, Ruth Lowry, Edwin Wouters, Juan Carlos Correa Senior, Taícia Helena Negrin Marques, Jim Walker, Lieke Oldenhof, Hilde Bastiaens, Zalika Klemenc Ketiš, Catherine De Witte, Job van Exel, on behalf of CITY-MOVE Consortium
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionNon-communicable diseases (NCDs) are a leading cause of global mortality, disproportionately affecting low and middle-income countries (LMICs). Physical inactivity, a key contributor to NCDs, is prevalent worldwide despite evidence supporting the health benefits of physical activity (PA). Cities, while often associated with barriers to PA, also present unique opportunities to enhance PA through systemic, context-sensitive interventions or so-called actions. However, evidence on effective city-level PA strategies, particularly in LMICs, remains limited. The CITY based interventions to stimulate active MOVEment for health (CITY-MOVE) project aims to accelerate, support and evaluate the implementation of PA actions at the city level by adapting the WHO Global Action Plan on Physical Activity into locally relevant strategies across six cities worldwide, accompanied by a cross-contextual evaluation framework to ensure transferability and scalability.Methods and analysisThis multicase study examines 13 PA actions in six cities (Bogotá, Lima, Kampala, Antwerp, Rotterdam and Ljubljana) across three continents, addressing both early (design and implementation) and late (evaluation) action stages. Early-stage actions employ action research in Living Labs to codesign and implement PA initiatives with local stakeholders, while late-stage interventions focus on retrospective evaluations of implementation outcomes. The framework integrates the Medical Research Council guidance on complex interventions with the Context and Implementation of Complex Interventions. Mixed methods are employed, including document review, interviews, participatory workshops and quantitative analysis of PA and NCD indicators. A cross-contextual Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA) framework will synthesise findings to inform scalability and transferability of actions.Ethics and disseminationEthics approvals were obtained from local review boards in the participating cities.Dissemination will occur at three levels: local, regional and global. Locally, findings will be shared with city authorities, non-governmental organisations (NGOs) and healthcare providers through Living Labs and policy dialogues. At the regional level, knowledge will be spread across cities in Europe, Latin America and East Africa through Communities of Practice and the use of tools like the MCDA framework. Globally, the project will contribute to the scientific community and international organisations such as the WHO and UN-Habitat, by sharing results through open access publications, conferences and global networks to ensure widespread dissemination and sustainability of the project’s impacts.Registration detailsThis study and its outcomes are publicly accessible on OSF (https://osf.io/mn8zd/) and ZENODO (https://zenodo.org/communities/citymove/).Keywords: Implementation Science, Exercise, PUBLIC HEALTH, Health policy

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are the leading cause of death globally.1 One of the main drivers of the NCD epidemic is physical inactivity (PIA)—globally, about 7.2% of all-cause mortality are attributed to PIA.2 Conversely, physical activity (PA) is one of the key health behaviours that can reduce NCDs. PA reduces premature mortality, it is protective against a range of NCDs including cardiovascular and chronic respiratory diseases, type 2 diabetes, several cancers, osteoarthritis and dementia, it improves mental health and promotes healthy ageing.3 Despite the abundance of evidence of the benefits of PA, globally, 81% of adolescents and a quarter (27.5%) of adults are physically inactive.4 From a policy perspective, there is thus an urgent need for knowledge on the contextual determinants of PA.

PA is influenced by determinants in the individual, social, built and natural environments.5 The global transition to urban living has changed these environments for many people. Estimates suggest that 4.2 billion people (55% of world population) lived in cities in 2018 and by the year 2050, 68% of the world’s population will live in urbanised areas.6 The role of cities in PA and PIA is double. On the one hand, increasing urbanisation is associated with decreased total PA across the globe.7 Conversely, cities also have the potential to address many of the above-cited determinants of PA through interventions in the built or social environment, enhancing PA in all population groups. There are large inequalities in the association between urban context and PA.7 Certain groups are particularly vulnerable to being excluded from PA opportunities in their locality. People in groups of low socioeconomic status are doubly affected by the unequal distribution of fewer facilitators and more barriers.8 They often live in deprived areas with higher environmental risks and have less access to options for recreational PA, both in their neighbourhood and in other parts of the city. Barriers to PA include lack of transport and of gender-inclusive and convenient spaces as well as perceptions of high crime and safety issues. Financial constraints significantly influence PA participation, especially in urban areas where informal accessible options such as play streets and natural public spaces are scarce and where fee-based programmes, dominated by market actors, have become a prevailing model.9 Since the COVID-19 pandemic, awareness has increased of the need to develop and implement solutions in these disadvantaged neighbourhoods and to address the barriers experienced by the most vulnerable groups. Various case studies of participatory planning and community engagement in this field have been documented.10 However, formal evaluations of such case studies are less prevalent. Notable exceptions are studies adopting a natural experimental design,10 quasi-experimental design,11 a prospective quality improvement preintervention–postintervention study design with matched control parks,12 survey studies13 and randomised controlled trial approaches.14 15 These studies generally show benefits of participatory planning and community engagement approaches to increase PA, while some studies report no differences.10 Noticeably, the majority of existing studies primarily focus on parks as specific intervention sites within cities, thereby overlooking a broad variety of intervention sites across cities and countries. Our study fills this gap by adopting a Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA) approach to map effects across intervention sites, cities and countries, while ensuring attention for contextual differences.

City environments are thus a key determinant of NCDs through several intersecting social, cultural and environmental factors. There is a need for contextualised and segmented approaches to monitor the variation and disparities in determinants and effects at the city level. Given the untapped potential of cities to promote PA, especially leisure and transport-related PA,16 there is an urgent need to support cities in an evidence-based system approach to promote PA taking into account social determinants and involving the least active.

General (thus non-city-specific) evidence-based solutions for increasing PA exist and have been synthesised in the WHO Global Action Plan on Physical Activity (GAPPA).5 GAPPA aims to achieve a 15% relative reduction in PIA in adults and adolescents by 2030 and includes 20 evidence-based actions across four domains: Active People, Active Societies, Active Environments and Active Systems. The term actions is broader then dominant public health term of intervention and emphasises the practical steps needed for implementation and the systemic approach, often involving different sectors. A combination of actions at individual, social and built environment levels in one package has been demonstrated to be most effective for increasing PA.2 Consistent with the GAPPA framework, we use the term actions rather than isolated interventions. The GAPPA framework should, therefore, be operationalised using a whole-of-system approach, involving all relevant government departments and stakeholders across multiple sectors, at different levels while taking into account other developments in the context.

When applied to the city context, however, implementing this system approach is a challenge for most cities in terms of coordination, timeframes for interventions and addressing competing interests and policy priorities. There is limited guidance to support cities in adapting actions to the local context, specifically regarding how they can combine and sequence the multiple approaches to achieve efficient and effective change. Furthermore, many city authorities lack robust data systems to monitor and evaluate ongoing actions, particularly in LMICs. Consequently, many cities do not have the required longitudinal data to provide evidence about the scale and the (often long) duration necessary for actions to have an impact.

Study aims and objectives

The project’s overall aim is to accelerate, support and evaluate the implementation of PA actions at the city level. This will be achieved through implementation research focused on adapting and applying the GAPPA actions, accompanied by a cross-contextual evaluation framework to ensure transferability and scalability. The study objectives are: (1) developing a comprehensive and comparative CITY based interventions to stimulate active MOVEment for health (CITY-MOVE) GAPPA Theory of Change (ToC); (2) adapting and refining city-GAPPA to six cities across three continents, through engagement with local stakeholders and addressing key uncertainties and resources in each context; (3) support cities in the successful implementation of early-stage city-GAPPA actions through action research in Living Labs; (4) assessing acceptability, reach, adoption, feasibility, fidelity, adaptation and sustainability of selected city-GAPPA actions in each city; (5) improving the development and utilisation of routinely collected data as a tool to support successful implementation and (6) generating cross-contextual evidence on implementation, evaluation and scalability through MCDA for 13 actions in six cities.

Methods

Study design

The study uses a multicase study design, whereby a case represents a GAPPA action implemented in a specific urban context. Thirteen actions from six cities will be examined, covering different stages of the implementation cycle. This approach enables the study to address objectives relevant to both early and later stages of implementation.

Six cases will focus on actions in the early stages (design, policy decision and planning), while seven will focus on later stages (implementation or postimplementation). These cases are evenly distributed across the cities, allowing each city to explore both early and late-stage intervention questions.

The six early-phase cases will adopt an action research approach in Living Labs, in which CITY-MOVE researchers together with local stakeholders will be involved in setting up and codeveloping PA actions over time. Research questions include: How can actions be tailored to the local urban context taking into account feasibility? How can diverse stakeholders be effectively engaged and is conflict managed? How do stakeholders perform in effective planning and comprehensive and inclusive implementation? The seven late-stage actions will have a retrospective evaluation design focusing on implementation outcomes and effect evaluation.

Study settings and GAPPA actions

Six cities have been selected as study settings, through a combination of purposive and convenience sampling. Purposive selection ensured variation in geographic spread (Latin America, East Africa and Europe), income level (low-income, middle-income and high-income countries), ratio of economic growth to population growth (struggling, emerging, stabilising or thriving cities17) and potential to learn from different target populations. In parallel, convenience factors such as existing partnerships and ongoing or planned actions aligned with the GAPPA framework enabled deeper engagement and feasibility for implementation research. These cities are Bogota (Colombia), Lima (Peru), Kampala (Uganda), Rotterdam (The Netherlands), Antwerp (Belgium) and Ljubljana (Slovenia). Overall theoretical framework

The overall theoretical framework guiding the CITY-MOVE project is based on the Medical Research Council framework for developing and evaluating complex interventions in four steps (ie, identification, feasibility, implementation and evaluation) with central core elements,18 adapted to allow for a multicase study analysis. The CITY-MOVE overall framework (figure 1) has four elements that will be part of the 13 individual case study analyses, and a fifth element for the multicase study analysis. The four steps for implementation comprise of (1) addressing core elements (ie, context, local stakeholders, local targets and research questions, key uncertainties and resources and equity) (13 actions); (2) early-stage implementation through Living Labs and action research (six actions); (3) late-stage implementation evaluation (seven actions) using implementation evaluation frameworks and (4) building capacity for monitoring and effect evaluation (13 actions). Phase 5, the multicase study analysis focuses on the development of a joint ToC and an MCDA framework for performance measurement of the 13 actions. The ToC will serve as the basis for identifying key decision-making criteria across cases and guiding the collective evaluation of the projects. The MCDA method enables differentiated weighting of these criteria in each city, helping to balance generalisability with sensitivity to local context. This protocol describes the methods for data collection and analysis in each implementation phase, starting from phase-specific research questions. Specific research questions demonstrate the focus of each implementation phase, without implying boundaries between the phases. For instance, data on key elements (phase 1) will also be addressed in phases 2–5, and certain phase 3 implementation questions can also be addressed in the early-stage actions, if relevant.

To structure the complexity of the implementation in each action, and to assess context and implementation in an integrated way, we use an additional framework to classify research questions also in another way: the Context and Implementation of Complex Interventions (CICI) framework19 positions an action, its implementation and its stakeholders within a context and allows for a time perspective. The research questions have, therefore, also been classified into six domains of implementation: the action; the stakeholders; the implementation process; outcomes; the multilevel city context and time, being the embedding and adaptation over time. The focus on implementation domains shifts over the implementation phases: the core elements focusing on action, local stakeholders, context and adaptation over time; the early-stage action on cocreation and implementation through action research in Living Labs; the late-stage implementation evaluation on implementation and outcomes and the monitoring and effect evaluation on building capacity in the action context. The multicase study phase will identify the key criteria of decision-making, analyse the collected data about these criteria and assess the performance of the 13 actions along these multiple criteria using MCDA.

Data collection and analysis along implementation phases

Specific research questions guide the focus of data collection in each of the phases of the 13 actions and the separate phase of the multicase study analysis. The phases are conceptually distinct but feed into one another, and, therefore, run largely in parallel rather than strictly sequential. This allows for iterative learning cycles, where insights generated in one phase can inform adjustments in others. For example, the core component phase comes first, as the elements defined there are essential to understanding and refining the activities in all other phases. Similarly, the late-stage implementation evaluation of seven actions can inform the design of the six early-stage actions. The results of phases 3 and 4 can enrich one another through mutual identification and interpretation of data. Phase 5 runs throughout the project, starting from the core elements for the ToC and leading towards the identification of relevant data for the MCDA. Phase 1, core components (January 2025–March 2026)

This addresses the core elements for each action and includes four CICI domains: stakeholders, the action, the multilevel city context and time. Data in the stakeholder domain include stakeholder analysis, organisational analysis of culture, management support and capacity; in the action domain, it includes gender, inclusivity and diversity; in the context domain, contextual key needs, risks, uncertainties and resources, governance, infrastructure and resources to support the implementation; and in the time domain, the context dynamics. Data collection methods comprise of document review, iterative open interviews and semistructured interviews with key informants, participatory observation and participatory stakeholder workshops. Online supplemental table 3 contains an overview of research questions, key variables and information, measurement instruments and data collection methods for the studies in each phase. A full table with details on processes of data collection, study populations, measurement instruments and references per research question is provided in online supplemental web annex 4.

The stakeholder analysis starts from a stakeholder mapping20 to be followed by an analysis of organisational roles and governance of each organisation, and an analysis of the relationships and interorganisational governance.21 Interorganisational governance will be visualised in an organigram that shows the multilevel organisational system related to the action, the relationships between and the decision-making power of relevant organisations. The context analysis will explore physical, social capital and economic resources available for each action through community asset mapping22 and assess the key needs, risks and uncertainties. Each element will be listed into predefined categories and scored for its relevance for the action. With the Gender Inclusivity Diversity Checklist,23 we will document how the actions help to address equality, inclusivity and diversity among women, older adults, people living with disability and those from disadvantaged communities. For the late-stage actions, this includes an assessment of changes over time, through a narrative analysis.24 Where possible, we will use UCINET software to quantify the network dimensions such as size, connections, distribution and segmentation of each action.25 The results of the stakeholder and context analysis will be validated in a participatory workshop with purposively invited stakeholders who will make Strengths Weaknesses Opportunities Threats analysis of the action.

Phase 2, early-stage implementation (July 2025–December 2026)

This will be examined through six case studies, in which the objective is to support cities in the successful implementation of early-stage city-GAPPA actions. The research questions focus on the action (How can this be tailored to the local context?), and implementation process (How can we facilitate inclusive and comprehensive implementation? How can we address needs and barriers in implementation?). These will be addressed through coproduction by means of action research in Living Labs. Living Labs can be defined as sites or platforms where multiple stakeholders from different domains (science, society, government, business) can work on complex urban challenges through coproduction, experimentation and learning, which can also involve sharing and addressing conflicting interests and needs.26 The main tool will be the Urban Lab Kit developed and tested in Rotterdam.27 Action research involves developing reciprocal relationships with stakeholders to develop practical knowledge used to adapt actions to specific community needs,28 through iterative Plan-Do-Check-Act action cycles. Data collection methods include participatory observations (recorded in fieldnotes), interviews and document analysis.

The findings of phase 1 will be the starting point for the six case studies of the early-stage actions. Key stakeholders and community members from local communities, public service providers, businesses and the government will be selected as participants in the Living Labs. Researchers from the city project teams will facilitate the process of coproduction, learning and implementation through action research. The data collected by researchers will be inductively analysed.

Phase 3, late-stage implementation evaluation (October 2024–September 2026)

In seven case studies of late-stage actions, we assess implementation process and outcomes. The former include action delivery,29 adoption,30 implementation strategies, implementation fidelity, maintenance and sustainability31 and costs. Data collection methods include document review, key informant interviews and validated checklists. Detailed information is provided in online supplemental web annex 2-SOP2. Research questions on the implementation outcomes are: How many and which people have engaged in the action32? What was the effect of the action on PA in the target groups? What was the effect of the action on NCD outcomes? What was the effect of the action on other outcomes (eg, social, environmental, economic)? Have equality and inclusivity changed? To answer these, we will focus data analysis of five priority outcomes: action reach,33 PA in the action target population,34 prevalence of NCDs and NCD-related deaths in target population,5 levels of air pollutants in the target area35 and destination inclusivity.36

The implementation process and outcome measures will be operationalised for each late-stage action together with the context expert in a cross-city workshop. Quantitative measures will be retrospectively analysed for their change over time in the action area, to the extent that time series are available. Control groups are not part of the design. Instead, we prioritised investing project resources in building the capacity of each city to use routine data for ongoing monitoring and evaluation. A structured format will be developed to support best practice approaches in assessing effects, including the potential use of control groups and longitudinal follow-up. However, data collection, extraction and analysis methods are tailored to the availability of data and local implementation realities. Qualitative information will be analysed through thematic analysis. Action delivery will be analysed through the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDIER) checklist29 and implementation strategies categorised according to Powell.37 Adoption of the action will be measured by % of scalable units (eg, organisations, counties or municipalities) approached that initiate the late-stage action or its components,30 implementation fidelity by the action components that were implemented according to plan and the % of participants having received the full dose of the action.30 Maintenance and sustainability will be scored on a 1–7 scale for the eight domains of the programme sustainability assessment tool.31 Reach will be measured by the proportion of the target group that has engaged in the action.38 PA in the action target population34 will be measured by change among target and/or participant group in average of minutes of moderate and/or vigorous PA per week. NCD deaths will be measured by NCD-related (un/age-)adjusted death rates in the target population,5 levels of air pollutants in the target area by Data Integration Model for Air Quality35 or local air quality indicators, and destination inclusivity by the differences between access measures across neighbourhoods.36 The evaluation of the late-stage actions will identify gaps in action implementation and make recommendations for the early-stage actions.

Phase 4, capacity for monitoring and effect evaluation (January 2024–June 2027)

The objective in this phase is to assess and strengthen the urban capacity (part of the context domain in CICI framework) in their supporting action development, implementation and evaluation of GAPPA actions. Research questions include the monitoring capacity and tools of cities to monitor relevant trends and outcomes and the utilisation of data for decision-making, design and evaluation. A set of minimally required data will be established to map data availability. It is constructed from the GAPPA framework and related references,34 and it will guide the mapping of the existing routine data collected by local authorities and the identification of gaps and inconsistencies in these data. Potential indicators are extracted from existing routine data will be analysed for suitability by a learning classifier developed for this purpose.39 Additionally, the assessment will include the evaluation of Geographic Information Systems (GIS) data on environmental features and population characteristics.40 For data utilisation, we will explore the timeframe of data, possibilities to combine spatial data on populations with GIS data on built environment and individuals’ health behaviour data and the users of data. Data collection methods are document review and key informant interviews, which will be thematically analysed.

Phase 5, multicase study analysis (January 2024–December 2027)

The final phase is meant to generate evidence from different contexts on implementation, evaluation and scalability. The first research question (Can we structure the action selection process in cities taking into account locally relevant criteria and evidence?) will be addressed through developing an MCDA framework41 357 that can be applied across the 13 cases. An initial set of criteria for successful implementation of actions will be developed based on the literature and workshops and interviews with stakeholders in phases 1, 2 and 3. These criteria will be operationalised in measures of performance together with the local stakeholders. These measures can relate to process, costs and outcomes, may be quantitative or qualitative indicators of performance, and will consist of a core set of measures applicable to all 13 actions, and these types of actions in general, and sub-sets of city- or action-context specific sets. This MCDA framework will then feed the next research question (How did the actions perform according to the MCDA framework?), and be applied to measure the performance of each of the 13 actions. Data for scoring the actions on the criteria will be compiled from the case study reports (phases 2–3), available local routine data (phase 4) and, if needed, alternative data sources from other actions in similar contexts, or similar actions in other contexts. In each of these steps, we aim for good quality data (objective 5) that will allow for appropriate statistical analysis and satisfactory comparability between actions. Data for weighing the criteria will be collected through a survey among action stakeholders (in phases 2 and 3), generating an overall set of relative weights for the core set of criteria and additional, local sets of relative weights for the subsets of criteria. Local validation of the MCDA framework will occur in 13 workshops convening key stakeholders for each action to discuss the MCDA framework, the scoring and weighing of the criteria and reflections on the performance results and their implications for policy recommendations. To be inclusive and practical, we will strive for a comprehensible approach for measuring preferences over criteria. An MCDA tool will be developed based on the core set of criteria (and their relative weights) developed in this project, but adaptable to local needs and preferences, and disseminated so that it can be used by local authorities and stakeholders in participatory design, implementation and evaluation of PA actions at the city level in other contexts.

The dissemination strategy for the CITY-MOVE project is structured across three levels: local, regional and global. At the local level, the project aims to engage a wide range of stakeholders, including city authorities, NGOs, public health institutes and healthcare providers. This will be achieved through Living Labs, policy dialogues and the distribution of policy briefs to ensure that research findings are translated into local practice. The results will directly inform policymakers, health professionals and local communities, influencing policy and public health practices.

At the regional level, the focus is on spreading knowledge across cities in Europe, Latin America and East Africa. This will be done through Communities of Practice, online repositories and conference presentations, allowing other cities to adopt and adapt the tools and methods developed by the project. A key tool for regional dissemination is the MCDA framework, which will help cities incorporate the project’s findings into local policies and actions.

At the global level, the project will contribute to the wider scientific community and global organisations such as the WHO and UN-Habitat. Findings will be shared via open access journals, conference presentations and participation in global networks, further strengthening the global push for effective urban health actions. Additionally, communication activities will include blogs, podcasts and videos aimed at specific target groups within the cities, raising awareness of the benefits of the actions. The use of social media and targeted media will also help amplify the reach and impact of the project’s results.

This dissemination plan ensures that the results of the CITY-MOVE project are shared widely, not only within the cities involved but also across regions and internationally, supporting the replication and sustainability of the project’s impacts far beyond its lifespan.
