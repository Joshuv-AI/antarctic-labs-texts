Using Resonance-Enhanced Multiphoton Ionization Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry to Quantitatively Evaluate the Transfer Behavior of A Compound in An Oil-in-Water-in-Oil Emulsion during Preparation
Authors: Minori Minami, Tomohiro Uchimura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In the present study, an oil-in-water-in-oil (O1/W/O2) emulsion during preparation was measured in real time by resonance-enhanced multiphoton ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (REMPI-TOFMS), and the behavior of a compound in the O1 phase could be evaluated as it transferred to the O2 phase due to differences in the stirring speeds and preparation temperatures. By applying a two-step emulsification method to prepare an O1/W/O2 emulsion, the time constants for the transfer of a compound at a preparation temperature of 23 °C were calculated to be 3.6 ± 0.3 and 2.0 ± 0.1 min at stirring speeds of 300 and 500 rpm, respectively. The corresponding time constants at a preparation temperature of 7 °C became longer7.8 ± 0.7 and 4.2 ± 0.2 min, respectively. Based on these values, the calculated rate constants were found to be directly proportional to the practical stirring speed used for the preparation of the O1/W/O2 emulsion. The transfer of a compound from the O1 to the O2 phase in an O1/W/O2 emulsion after preparation normally occurs because of the concentration gradient. Regarding the actual transfer of components during the preparation process, the transfer is assumed to not be driven solely by the concentration gradient from the O1 to the O2 phase. Rather, processes associated with preparation, such as the decrease in the size of W droplets due to subdivision, which facilitates transfer, as well as to the direct transfer (dissolution) of O1 components to the O2 phase during the subdivision process, are thought to play a significant role. REMPI-TOFMS enables the direct analysis of emulsions, and allows a quantitative evaluation of the component-transfer behavior that occurs during the preparation of an O1/W/O2 emulsion.

Introduction

An emulsion is a system in which water is dispersed in oil or oil is dispersed in water as small droplets. Two types of emulsions exist as simple structures: a water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion, in which water droplets are dispersed in an oil phase, and an oil-in-water (O/W) emulsion, in which oil droplets are dispersed in a water phase. In addition, there are multiple emulsions known as an emulsion of an emulsion. A typical example is a double emulsion consisting of three phases: inner, intermediate, and outer. Multiple emulsions include an oil-in-water-in-oil (O1/W/O2) emulsion, in which water droplets (a W phase) encompassing oil droplets (an O1 phase) are dispersed in an outer oil phase (an O2 phase), and a water-in-oil-in-water (W1/O/W2) emulsion, where oil droplets (an O phase) encompassing water droplets (a W1 phase) are dispersed in an outer water phase (a W2 phase). Double emulsions are prepared using several methods such as mechanical agitation, phase inversion, and two-step emulsification. − When an O1/W/O2 emulsion is prepared with two-step emulsification, the first step is to prepare an O1/W emulsion (an O1/W primary emulsion), and the obtained emulsion is mixed and stirred with a liquid O2 phase in the second step. In a double emulsion, it is quite important to understand the release behavior in which the target component contained in an inner phase is gradually released to an outer phase over time. Many academic studies have focused on the correlation between the encapsulation, or the release rate, of the ingredients and the preparation conditions. , The transport of compounds between microfluidic droplets has been verified using electrospray ionization quadrupole mass spectrometry (ESI-QMS). Many studies have focused on the practical applications of ingredients in food, medicines, and other products. ,

In a double emulsion, the target ingredient should be retained in the inner phase at the desired initial concentration and released at the desired release rate. The release behavior of a component in a double emulsion from the inner to the outer phase is generally evaluated by measuring the component in the outer phase following a pretreatment such as centrifugation, and by utilizing analytical methods such as absorbance measurement and high-performance liquid chromatography. However, centrifugation could break both the inner and middle phases, which often results in excessive leakage of the encapsulated target component in the inner phase. In other words, it is difficult to maintain the original conditions of a system when using conventional analytical methods to evaluate emulsions. To obtain a desired double emulsion, it is essential not only to rigorously consider the preparation conditions but also to directly evaluate the emulsion before, during, and after preparation without a pretreatment such as centrifugation.

Resonance-enhanced multiphoton ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (REMPI-TOFMS) is a mass spectrometric method with high selectivity in which the atoms or molecules of interest are irradiated with laser pulses, and the ions induced by the absorption of photons are separated and detected by the mass-to-charge (m/z) ratio. , This technique is applied to spectroscopic, environmental, and food analyses, and generally can be utilized for the real-time measurement of a gas sample. On the other hand, we have applied this method to the direct analysis of a liquid sample of an emulsion without pretreatment. − REMPI utilizes an ultraviolet pulsed laser that enables soft and selective ionization of only the components to be measured. Therefore, even samples in which many components are present in high concentrations could be analyzed without pretreatment. In other words, direct analysis of an emulsion could be achieved while maintaining the original dispersion conditions and properties. Moreover, TOFMS is a nonscanning type of MS; therefore, all the induced ions produced per pulse could be detected. This is a valuable characteristic for the real-time measurement of an emulsion because, for example, even if two or more detectable compounds are contained in a single droplet, all components could be detected as corresponding ions without omitting any of them.

Figure S1 features a schematic of the time profiles obtained using REMPI-TOFMS to measure several types of emulsions. A series of mass spectra was obtained when an emulsion was measured online (Figure S1a). Extracting the peak areas for ions of interest from a series of mass spectra allows the construction of a time profile for a corresponding component. When an O/W emulsion containing a detectable hydrophobic analyte species was analyzed, both a weak base signal and positive spikes appeared in the time profile (Figure S1b); the former was derived using a small amount of detectable component solubilized to the W phase (or O droplets so small that they could not be detected as spikes), and the latter suggests that a hydrophobic analyte component had localized in the inner O droplets. , When the W/O emulsion was measured, however, both a base signal with a constant value and negative spikes were obtained (Figure S1c); the former was derived from a detectable component present in the outer O phase, and the latter indicated that the detectable component was almost absent from the inner W droplets. , Furthermore, the measurement of an O1/W/O2 emulsion revealed a base signal with a constant value and both positive and negative spikes (Figure S1d). The base signal was derived mainly from a detectable component present in the O2 phase. The positive spikes were detected from a component in the O1 droplets, and the negative spikes arose, in short, from W droplets that contained either few or no O1 droplets. Analyzing these signals for gradual increases in the intensity of the base signal would allow us to evaluate the behavior of a detectable component in the O1 phase as it transferred to the O2 phase over time in an O1/W/O2 emulsion. However, when measuring an O1/W/O2 emulsion prepared using a two-step emulsification method in a previous study, a base signal with significant intensity was apparent from the beginning. This suggested that a significant amount of detectable analyte was already present in the O2 phase immediately after the preparation was completed.

This was the point at which it occurred to us that REMPI-TOFMS would be applicable to a direct real-time measurement of an O1/W/O2 emulsion, both after and during the preparation process. As discussed below, it was apparent that two processes were involved in the transfer of a component from the O1 to the O2 phase during the preparation process. The first of these processes involves a transfer based on the concentration gradient described above. Many researchers have studied the release behavior of double emulsions by focusing on their release behavior after preparation. However, during the preparation of an O1/W/O2 emulsion, W droplets are gradually subdivided by agitation, which decreases the diameter of the W droplets over time. Therefore, a transfer rate based on the concentration gradient would be expected to vary with the degree of the subdivision of W droplets. In addition, the second of these processes is the direct transfer of an O1 component to an O2 phase when an O1 droplet comes into contact with the O2 phase during the shearing of W droplets. The transfer of a component from an O1 droplet to an O2 phase during the preparation of an O1/W/O2 emulsion via the two-step emulsification method is considered to be the result of the sequential or simultaneous occurrence of the above processes. In the present study, a real-time measurement of an O1/W/O2 emulsion during preparation was attempted using REMPI-TOFMS, and the kinetics of the transfer behavior of a component in the O1 to the O2 phase during subdivision was investigated. The effects of stirring speed and preparation temperature in the second step of the two-step emulsification process were evaluated with respect to the transfer of a component of the O1 phase derived from the subdivision of W droplets.

Experimental Section

Reagent

Styrene, an analyte species that can be ionized by the ultraviolet laser pulses used in the present study, was used for the O1 phase. Distilled water was used for the W phase, and a mixture of cyclohexane and n-hexane was used for the O2 phase. Sorbitan monooleate (Span 80, hydrophile–lipophile balance (HLB) 4.3) and polyoxyethylene sorbitan monolaurate (Tween 20, HLB 16.7) served as the surfactants. All reagents were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Osaka, Japan), and the distilled water was prepared in our laboratory.

Sample Preparation

A two-step emulsification method was used to prepare the O1/W/O2 emulsion. Figure shows a schematic of the preparation procedure. In the first step of the preparation of the O1/W primary emulsion (1. First step in Figure ), a Tween 20 aqueous solution was prepared as the W phase. In addition, styrene (2.473 mL) was added to a 50 mL vial container, and the homogenizer was fixed in a position above the liquid for the O1 phase without direct contact. Thereafter, the homogenizer was run at 7000 rpm and a liquid W phase (20 mL) was added dropwise. The liquid was stirred for 5 min to obtain the O1/W primary emulsion. The ratio of the O1 and W phases was adjusted to 1:8 (v/v). The concentration of the Tween 20 was adjusted to 1 g/L of an O1/W primary emulsion (to be finally 0.03 g/L of the O1/W/O2 emulsion), and the styrene concentration was adjusted to a final level of 3 g/L of the O1/W/O2 emulsion.

Schematic of the preparation procedure using a two-step emulsification method. (1) First step of the preparation of the O1/W primary emulsion. (2) Second step of the preparation of the O1/W/O2 emulsion. (2′) (Control experiment) Second step of the preparation of the O1/W/W emulsion (an O1/W emulsion) instead of an O1/W/O2 emulsion.

In the second step of the preparation of the O1/W/O2 emulsion (2. Second step in Figure ), a mixture of cyclohexane and n-hexane (90:10 (v:v)) was prepared for the O2 phase, and Span 80 was then dissolved in this mixture. This solution containing no analyte species was stirred using a magnetic stirrer (50, 300, and 500 rpm), and a capillary column was inserted for sample introduction, as described below. Five minutes later, the O1/W primary emulsion prepared in the first step was added dropwise to the O2 phase, which allowed measurement of the O1/W/O2 emulsion during preparation. The ratio of the O1/W primary emulsion to the O2 phase was adjusted to 3:97 (v/v). The final height of the O1/W/O2 emulsion was 3.5 cm. The concentration of the Span 80 was adjusted to a final level of 10 g/L of the O1/W/O2 emulsion. The baffle plates were attached to the vial container during the preparation and measurement of this emulsion (Figure S2).

As a control, an O1/W/W emulsion (i.e., an O1/W emulsion) also was measured during preparation. This step was a simple dilution of the primary O1/W emulsion (2′. Second step in Figure ). The only difference between the second steps was whether the liquid inside the vial in advance was a solution for either the O2 or the W phase (Tween 20 aqueous solution). The stirring speed used in the second step was 300 rpm. All preparation procedures described above (and the following measurements) were performed at room temperature (23 °C).

In addition, to confirm the temperature dependence of the transfer of the analyte compound during preparation, an O1/W/O2 emulsion prepared at 7 °C was analyzed. Other than temperature, the preparation conditions were the same as those for preparation at 23 °C, as described above. Both the analyte styrene (O1 phase) and the Tween 20 aqueous solution (W phase) were precooled in a refrigerator. In the first step of the preparation for an O1/W primary emulsion, the vial containing styrene was immersed in cold water, and a Tween 20 aqueous solution was added dropwise while the homogenizer was running (7000 rpm). In the second step of the preparation of the O1/W/O2 emulsion, the preparation (and subsequent measurement) was performed during cooling with a cool plate (CP-085, Scinics, Tokyo, Japan) to bring the sample to 7 °C.

REMPI-TOFMS

A schematic of the experimental setup utilizing REMPI-TOFMS appears in Figure . A pair of concentric capillary columns was constructed for the sample introduction. The inner and outer diameters and length of the inner capillary column were 50/150 μm and 115 cm, respectively, and those of the outer capillary column were 320/450 μm and 25 cm, respectively (GL Sciences, Tokyo, Japan). An inner capillary column was inserted into one of the two holes in the lid of the vial and the height of the tip was adjusted to 1 cm above the bottom of the vial. The sample flowed through the inner capillary column through the evacuation force of the TOFMS vacuum. To promote the smooth vaporization of the sample, ambient air was introduced through the outer capillary column at 2 mL/min, which was adjusted using a flow meter (RK-1250, Kofloc, Kyoto, Japan). The capillary tips were indirectly heated to 40 °C by a heater mounted on the flange of the TOFMS to eliminate the risk of clogging at the tips caused by the heat of vaporization during the vaporization of the emulsion.

Schematic of the experimental setup using REMPI-TOFMS. The O1/W primary emulsion prepared in the first step was added dropwise to the O2 phase, which allowed measurement of the O1/W/O2 emulsion during preparation. The sample flowed through the inner capillary column through the evacuation force of the TOFMS vacuum. To promote the smooth vaporization of the sample, ambient air was introduced through the outer capillary column, which was adjusted using a flow meter. The capillary tips were indirectly heated using a heater mounted on the flange of the TOFMS. A cool plate was employed when the prepared temperature of the O1/W/O2 emulsion was 7 °C.

The details of the REMPI-TOFMS system have been previously described , and are briefly summarized here. The fourth harmonic generation of an Nd:YAG laser (GAIA II, 266 nm, 4 ns, 10 Hz, Rayture Systems, Tokyo, Japan) was utilized as an ionization laser. The laser pulse energy was adjusted to 10 μJ, and the pulses were focused using a plano-convex lens (f = 200 mm). The distance between the laser focal point and the tip of the outer capillary was adjusted to 2 mm. The pressure in the vacuum chamber for the application of TOFMS was approximately 2 × 10–2 Pa when a mixture of styrene and cyclohexane was introduced. The time profile for styrene was constructed from a series of mass spectra obtained by extracting the areas of the molecular ion peak (m/z 104) and the fragment ion peak (m/z 103). Fitting of the time profile was performed using scientific data analysis software (Origin, Lightstone, Tokyo, Japan).

Optical Microscope

An inverted microscope (ECLIPSE, TE2000-U, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) with an objective lens (Plan Fluor, 20×, numerical aperture 0.45, Nikon) was used to observe the morphologies of the prepared O1/W/O2 emulsion. A small quantity of the O1/W/O2 emulsion was removed during the second step of the preparation, and the results were observed as soon as possible.

Results and Discussion

Real-Time Measurement of an O1/W/W (O1/W) Emulsion during Preparation

First, an O1/W/W (O1/W) emulsion prepared by the same procedure as that for the two-step emulsification for an O1/W/O2 emulsion was measured in real time to verify whether positive spikes derived from styrene that localized as O1 droplets could be confirmed in the time profile. In this experiment, the liquid for the W phase was first measured while being stirred at 300 rpm, and then the O1/W primary emulsion prepared in the first step was added dropwise after 5 min. The O1/W primary emulsion was prepared in advance via a homogenizer operated at a stirring speed of 7000 rpm. Therefore, O1 droplets were assumed to be sufficiently refined and not further subdivided when they were dropped into the W phase with stirring at 300 rpm in the second step of the preparation process. Incidentally, of course, the W phase that was added was not subdivided, but merged with the W phase that was originally in the vial.

As shown in Figure , a signal was confirmed ca. 8 min after the start of the measurement, which amounted to ca. 3 min after the addition of the O1/W primary emulsion. An interval of 3 min was required for the sample to pass through the capillary column, which was introduced to TOFMS. The linear velocity was calculated to be 1150 mm/180 s = 6.4 mm/s. A large number of positive spikes appeared in this time profile, which is thought to have derived from styrene dispersed as small oil droplets in the sample. An averaged time profile for every 2 min is also shown in Figure . While some fluctuations were observed, a nearly constant peak area was observed immediately after the O1/W primary emulsion was added.

The vertical axis represents the peak area (mV·ns), which was derived from the signal intensity (mV) and the widths of the flight time (ns) of the molecular ion peak and that of the fragment ion peak for styrene in a series of mass spectra.

Real-Time Measurement of O1/W/O2 Emulsions during Preparation

From the results mentioned above, if an O1/W/O2 emulsion was measured during preparation and styrene was present in high concentrations in the O1 phase, positive spikes would also be obtained, and the release of styrene into the O2 phase would be confirmed as a gradual increase in the intensities of the base signal. O1/W/O2 emulsions were prepared via two-step emulsification by dropping an O1/W primary emulsion into an O2 phase while stirring at speeds of 50, 300, and 500 rpm.

The appearance of the sample in a vial stirred at 50 rpm in Figure S3 shows how this was an insufficient force, and the O1/W droplets did not disperse in the O2 phase but instead sank to the bottom of the vial (Figure S3a). In addition, these droplets were subdivided under the condition of being ground by a magnetic stir bar, but the resultant droplets were sufficiently large to be visible even 25 min after the addition of the O1/W primary emulsion (Figure S3b). In any case, under stirring at 50 rpm, the O1/W droplets did not disperse, indicating that this is not a typical condition for preparing O1/W/O2 emulsions.

On the other hand, the O1/W droplets in the O1/W/O2 emulsions prepared at 300 and 500 rpm were dispersed. Visual observation of an O1/W/O2 emulsion during stirring revealed that droplets could be observed until ca. 9 min after the addition of an O1/W primary emulsion at 300 rpm and until ca. 4 min at 500 rpm; each of the droplets in both vials then became so small that they were no longer visible to the naked eye. Thus, the W droplets were subdivided over time during the second step in the preparation. Microscopic images of the O1/W/O2 emulsion prepared at 300 rpm are shown in Figure . At both 5 and 20 min after the addition, W droplets containing O1 droplets were confirmed. Again, the O1/W primary emulsion was stirred in advance using a homogenizer at a stirring speed of 7000 rpm, and the O1 droplets were regarded as sufficiently fine.

Microscopic images of an O1/W/O2 emulsion obtained by the addition of an O1/W primary emulsion to an O2 phase. Stirring speed in the second step of the two-step emulsification method: 300 rpm. Time after the addition: (a) 5 min and (b) 20 min.

The time profiles for styrene obtained when the stirring speeds in the second step were 50, 300, and 500 rpm and the preparation temperature was 23 °C are shown in Figure . In this measurement, the liquid for the O2 phase was measured beforehand, and the O1/W primary emulsion was added dropwise 5 min after starting the measurement. As a result, all base signals gradually increased when prepared at any stirring speed. These results are considered to reflect the fact that styrene was transferred from the O1 to the O2 phase. Here, the rise in the base signal around a measurement time of 8 min was sharp for the time profiles obtained at 300 and 500 rpm, whereas it was somewhat slower at 50 rpm. As mentioned previously, in the experiment stirred at 50 rpm, the O1/W droplets sank to the bottom of the vial. On the other hand, the tip of the capillary column for sample introduction was positioned at a height of 1 cm from the bottom of the vial. Therefore, styrene, which transferred from the O1 to the O2 phase at the bottom of the vial, is considered to require a certain amount of time to reach the sample inlet under soft stirring at 50 rpm.

Several negative spikes appear in the time profiles obtained at 300 and 500 rpm. Negative spikes were thought to arise from W droplets during subdivision that contained either few or no O1 droplets. However, the reproducibility was poor, and negative spikes seldom appeared in some measurements.

Negative spikes were detected to some extent in the time profiles at 300 and 500 rpm; however, positive spikes were only slightly detectedat least not with the same strong intensity as in the time profile of the O1/W/W (O1/W) emulsion, as Figure illustrates. As a reference, when an O1/W primary emulsion was added dropwise to the O2 phase without stirring it was aspirated by the tip of the capillary column for sample introduction as it was sinking to the bottom, which produced intense positive signals that indicated the presence of a high concentration of styrene in the O1 phase (Figure S4). Therefore, intense positive spikes could not be obtained under the sample preparation conditions, as shown in Figure , where the O1/W droplets were dispersed while being subdivided. The reasons for this are discussed below by considering the process of subdivision during preparation (Figure S5). During the preparation of an O1/W/O2 emulsion, where the subdivision of W droplets occurs, the transfer of a component from the O1 to the O2 phase is considered to involve not only the release of the component owing to a general concentration gradient (Figure S5a), but also to the direct transfer of the component during the simulaneous subdivision of a W droplet (Figure S5b). In other words, it is thought that after an O1/W primary emulsion was added, O1 droplets frequently came into contact with the O2 phase during the subdivision of W droplets, and some, or almost all, of the components in the O1 phase transferred (dissolved) to an O2 phase. As a result of these processes, the amount of styrene in the O1 phase was considered to have decreased to an undetectable amount as a positive spike.

The results of the fitting are shown in Figure together with the time profiles, using the equation of the decay expressed by A=A∞(1−exp(−t/τ)) where A ∞ is the peak area at t → ∞ and τ is the time constant. In the fitting calculation, the data from the first few minutes were masked because the analyte species, styrene, did not pass through the inner capillary column to be quantified by TOFMS.

While the fitting result obtained at 50 rpm is reasonable, it was somewhat inaccurate during the initial stage of the preparationin the rising portion of the signal. As mentioned above, this was because the transfer of styrene from the bottom of the vial to the capillary inlet required an extended amount of time owing to the insufficient stirring. On the other hand, the time profiles obtained at 300 and 500 rpm could be traced well, and the time constants were calculated to be 3.6 ± 0.3 and 2.0 ± 0.1 min, respectively.

As mentioned above, visible observation of an O1/W/O2 emulsion during stirring showed that the subdivision was in progress for ca. 9 and 4 min at 300 and 500 rpm, respectively, following the addition of an O1/W primary emulsion. These times correspond to the times when the signal intensities of the corresponding fit results achieved more than 90% of the total. This means that the final time when the miniaturization of droplets could be visually confirmed and the completion time of the increase in the base signals for styrene were almost the same; the completion time also refers to the component transfer from the O1 to the O2 phase.

The time profiles of styrene obtained at a preparation temperature of 7 °C are also shown in Figure . Compared with the time profiles at 23 °C, the rise of the base signal was slower in the experiment stirred at 50 rpm. A plausible reason for this is the decrease in the diffusion rate of styrene with decreasing temperature, which likely slowed the diffusion of styrene from the bottom of the vial into the tip of the capillary column for sample introduction. As a result, the fitted curve was not a good match with the time profile. On the other hand, the curves fit the time profiles obtained at 300 and 500 rpm. The corresponding time constants were calculated to be 7.8 ± 0.7 and 4.2 ± 0.2 min, respectively.

The relationships between the stirring speed in the second step for O1/W/O2 emulsion preparation and the time constant and rate constant, which is the reciprocal of the time constant, obtained at both temperatures are shown in Figure . In any case, compared with the time constants obtained at 23 °C, those obtained at 7 °C increased; in other words, the rate constant decreased. A potential reason is that, in addition to the temperature dependence of the diffusion rate of styrene from the O1 to O2 phases, the increase in viscosity with cooling makes it more difficult for the W droplets to subdivide. As a reference, it was confirmed that Tween 20 cooled in a refrigerator was less prone to flow when dropped onto a glass slide and tilted than that at room temperature, which suggests an increase in viscosity. Li et al. reported that decreases in viscosity and cohesive force with increasing temperature are beneficial for emulsification. Hwang et al. also reported that the increased viscosity of an O2 phase in an O1/W/O2 emulsion inhibits the release of the component (retiol) in O1 droplets, which results in a lower release rate. Previous studies have evaluated double emulsions after preparation. On the other hand, the present study shows that the influence of preparation conditions such as stirring speed and sample temperature on the transfer behavior of the O1 component during preparation could be discussed from a kinetic perspective.

As mentioned previously, at a stirring speed of 50 rpm, the O1/W droplets settled and were therefore not dispersed. In other words, regarding Figure b, for the practical stirring speeds used during preparation (i.e., speeds at which the droplets are dispersed), the rate constant can be considered proportional to the stirring speed. As the stirring speed increases, the subdivision progresses more easily. Therefore, in addition to the general concentration gradient-driven transfer from the O1 to the O2 phase, a direct transfer (dissolution) of the O1 component into the O2 phase due to subdivision is also considered to occur.

Under these experimental conditions, the rate constant observed at 23 °C was approximately twice that observed at 7 °C. Such quantitative results provide valuable information for exploring the optimal conditions for encapsulating the components. It would be interesting to determine if the proportional relationship persists at higher stirring speeds. Unfortunately, a preliminary experiment showed that an O1/W/O2 emulsion prepared at higher stirring speeds could not be measured when cycrohexane was stirred at a speed of 600 rpm in an experimental system using baffle plates because of the formation of small bubbles in the liquid sample.

Conclusions

In the present study, an O1/W/O2 emulsion was measured during preparation in real time using REMPI-TOFMS, and the behavior of a compound, styrene, as it transferred from the O1 to the O2 phase could be evaluated under different conditions such as stirring speed and preparation temperature. As a result of attempting to detect O1/W droplets during subdivision in the second step of the two-step emulsification method, the negative spikes for styrene indicated the existence of W droplets. However, positive spikes that would be detected if highly concentrated styrene exists as O1 droplets in an O1/W droplet were hardly detected. Moreover, the final time for subdivision observed visually and the completion time of the increase in the base signals for styrene were almost in agreement. These findings strongly suggest that the transfer of the O1 phase component to the O2 phase occurred during the preparation process. Furthermore, this study clarified the relationship between the preparation conditions for an O1/W/O2 emulsion and the time and rate constants of component transfer. REMPI-TOFMS has a unique feature that is not present in conventional methods, and this allows a quantitative evaluation of the transfer behavior of a compound during the preparation of a double emulsion. This method could also be applied to the evaluation of an O1/W/O2 emulsion containing multiple ingredients from the preparation stage. Therefore, REMPI-TOFMS would be useful for increasing productivity and functionality, as well as for improving quality control for double emulsions.
