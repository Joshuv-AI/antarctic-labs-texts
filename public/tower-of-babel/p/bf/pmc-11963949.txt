Portrayal of Acute Myocardial Infarction in Popular Film: A Review of Gender, Race, and Ethnicity
Authors: Kirsten E. Shaw, Kevin M. Harris, Amy Diedrich, Amy K. Holbrook, Larissa Stanberry, Michelle D. Carlson, Retu Saxena
Published: 2024

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death in women in the United States and disproportionately affects patients from racial and ethnic minority groups. 1 , 2 Efforts to raise awareness about CVD among women and underrepresented patients from ethnic and racial minority groups have been largely unsuccessful, with recent national surveys showing a significant decline in public awareness that CVD is the leading cause of death in women. 2 Given the increasing influence of popular media narratives on public health, we sought to investigate how acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is depicted in popular film to understand the representation of women, diverse populations, and symptoms. 3 , 4

Using the filtered search tool, the term “heart attack” was used to identify films depicting AMI in the Internet Movie Database. Movies were sorted from most to least popular per Internet Movie Database rating. To identify 100 AMI scenes in movies, 172 top‐ranked movies were reviewed consecutively. Movies were excluded if they failed to show the actor experiencing AMI, or if they depicted a nonischemic cardiac arrest. Cardiac arrest secondary to nonischemic causes was determined by cardiologist review of movie scenes and plots. Gender, race, and ethnicity data were acquired via movie plot descriptions or actor self‐identification if the former was unavailable. This project used publicly available nonpatient data and, therefore, did not require institutional review board approval. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Of the 100 scenes analyzed, 83 were US films and 17 were international films. Film years ranged from 1932 to 2022, with the distribution of gender, race, and ethnicity shown in the Figure. Of 100 scenes, 10 (10%) depicted women and 90 (90%) depicted men. The representation of people of color experiencing AMI was 6 (6%) in contrast to the 94 (94%) of scenes featuring non‐Hispanic White people. Of 10 women portrayed, 1 (10%) was non‐White (Asian, Black or African American, and Hispanic or Latino/a individuals) and 5 (5.6%) of 90 men were non‐White. There were no Black women represented. The 2 most prevalent presenting signs or symptoms of AMI were falls and loss of consciousness. Seventy‐nine men (88%) fell at the time of their MI, whereas all 10 women (100%) fell. Eight women (88%) and 61 men (68%) experienced loss of consciousness. Chest pain was portrayed by 5 women (50%) and 60 men (67%). Four women (40%) screamed or yelled at the time of their MI, compared with 26 men (29%). Five women (50%) and 57 men (63%) clutched their chest at the time of their MI.

Popular film depictions of AMI are underrepresentative of women and patients from racial and ethnic minority groups. Despite attempts of national movements (eg, Go Red for Women) to raise awareness that CVD is the leading cause of death, public awareness among women, and particularly among women from populations in ethnic and racial minority groups, has declined in the past decade. 2 In congruence with this, we found that since 2009, only 1 film portrayed a woman with AMI, compared with 28 films portraying a man.

We also found that films predominantly portray more severe symptoms of AMI, such as falls and loss of consciousness, as well as a higher rate of more unusual presentations, such as screaming. This may perpetuate false beliefs on typical symptoms of AMI, which can be more subtle, particularly in women.

Gonsalves et al, among others, have demonstrated the significant influence that popular media narratives can have on the public's understanding of health, including CVD. 3 , 4 In fact, data from the 2003 American Heart Association survey demonstrated that women obtain most of their information about heart disease from mass media, with up to 45% of women receiving heart disease information from magazines. 4 This issue is compounded by the tendency of popular media to frame CVD as a “man's disease” and underrepresent people of color, as supported by our study. There is a critical need for ongoing public education, particularly via efforts that leverage popular media and social media platforms, to correct misconceptions about CVD. Such initiatives should emphasize the following: (1) CVD is the leading cause of death in both men and women in the United States and disproportionately affects patients from racial and ethnic minority groups; and (2) AMI often presents with more subtle symptoms, particularly in women, contrary to the more severe or atypical presentations portrayed in film. As champions for health equity, the American Heart Association has committed to advancing cardiovascular health for all by 2024, as outlined in their 2022 Call to Action. 5 To achieve this goal and reduce cardiovascular health disparities based on gender, race, and ethnicity, it is imperative to increase awareness about the impact of CVD among the public, with a special focus on women and patients from racial and ethnic minority groups. Therefore, targeted initiatives to enhance awareness among these groups are crucial for improving public understanding of CVD and fostering more equitable cardiovascular outcomes.
