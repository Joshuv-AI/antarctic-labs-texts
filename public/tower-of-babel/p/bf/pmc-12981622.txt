Intramuscular administration of mesenchymal stromal cells in a cat with asthma: case report
Authors: Pamella Anderson de Oliveira, Clara Passos Maeda, Priscila Barbalho Bianchi, Jonathan Edwin Baracho Trindade Hill, Amanda Baracho Trindade Hill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAsthma is one of the most common respiratory diseases in cats and humans. Cats are the only domestic species that develop a syndrome similar to asthma in humans, characterized by type I hypersensitivity and consequent narrowing of the airways. Because of the chronic nature of the condition and the adverse effects of long-term corticosteroid use, exploring new therapeutic strategies is crucial for improving the health and well-being of affected cats. We present a novel therapeutic approach using IM mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), a delivery route not previously reported for asthma in any species. A 7-year-old cat with asthma had severe clinical signs, including coughing, apathy, vomiting, and dependence on corticosteroids and bronchodilators. The cat received 3 IM injections of 1 × 106 MSCs per kg body weight at 30-day intervals. The cat experienced lasting clinical improvement. No adverse effects were observed, and corticosteroids and bronchodilators were successfully discontinued without subsequent asthma attacks during 2-years of follow-up. Anti-emalso etics were withdrawn without recurrence of frequent vomiting, and the animal’s appetite improved sufficiently to achieve a 0.6 kg weight gain. In addition, a quality-of-life questionnaire indicated substantial improvement in vitality and comfort (P < .05). This case highlights the potential of IM MSCs as an alternative treatment for asthma in cats, offering a safe and potentially effective option for minimizing corticosteroid dependence. Additional studies are warranted to confirm the therapeutic benefits and broader applicability of MSCs.Keywords: translational medicine, feline medicine, bronchitis, lung diseases, Pneumology, cell therapy

Introduction

Asthma in cats is a chronic inflammatory disease of the lower airways that has increased in prevalence in recent decades.1 Asthma affects approximately 1%-5% of cats, regardless of age, breed, or sex.2 Asthma in cats resembles the disease in humans. Spontaneously occurring conditions in animals provide valuable opportunities to evaluate the feasibility, safety, and biological activity of novel stem cell-based treatments through clinical trials.3–5 Species such as horses, cats, and dogs are not only important patients in veterinary medicine but also share environments with humans. Their biological and physiological similarities, along with comparable pathologic and therapeutic responses, make them ideal models for studying human diseases.6 These models also account for complex interactions between genetic factors and environmental influences.7 Leveraging natural disease models aligns with One Health principles by decreasing reliance on experimental animal models, advancing human health research, and improving prevention and treatment strategies.6–8 Furthermore, conducting trials in animals with naturally occurring diseases offers more relevant data for regulatory approvals and translational medicine. Notably, cats are the only domestic species to develop a syndrome resembling asthma in humans, characterized by type I hypersensitivity, inflammation, and airway narrowing.9

Management of asthma in cats typically involves the use of PO or inhaled corticosteroids and bronchodilators that decrease inflammation, consequently improving airway function. However, these treatments can have adverse effects and often are prescribed for the cat’s entire life. In addition, they may not always produce the desired therapeutic effect or prevent the disease from progressing.10 These considerations emphasize the importance of exploring new treatments for asthma in cats, because existing treatments may be inadequate and lead to adverse effects. In this context, cell therapy has emerged as a promising alternative, drawing attention for its potential to address underlying physiologic imbalances and improve outcomes.

Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) possess the ability to secrete a variety of anti-inflammatory and regenerative molecules.11 These include cytokines, growth factors, and extracellular vesicles, which can modulate immune responses, decrease inflammation, and promote tissue repair. By releasing these bioactive substances, stromal cells can help create a favorable microenvironment for healing and regeneration,11 making them a promising option for treating various inflammatory and degenerative conditions. This regenerative potential is a key reason for the growing interest in cell therapies in both veterinary and human medicine. Currently, no consensus exists regarding the optimal approach for using MSCs in asthma and the effectiveness of MSC for asthma can vary based on factors such as administration route, dosage, and timing.12 We present a case of naturally occurring asthma in a cat treated with MSCs via IM injection.

Case report

This case was managed in compliance with the ethical guidelines established by the Brazilian National Council for the Control of Animal Experimentation and with the approval from the Ethics Committee of the Centro Universitário Max-Planck, reference number PAX002-2020.

A 7-year-old male cat, presented to the Centro Universitário de Jaguariúna Veterinary Teaching Hospital in Jaguariúna, Brazil, was diagnosed with asthma 2 years earlier. The diagnosis was based on clinical signs, including chronic cough and increased expiratory effort, and thoracic radiographs showed a bronchial pattern consistent with a diagnosis of asthma (Figure 1A). At the time, routine blood tests and abdominal ultrasonography identified no abnormalities. Differential diagnoses such as infectious diseases, pulmonary parasitism, and heartworm infection were excluded by laboratory and imaging studies. Since the initial diagnosis, the respiratory condition had been managed pharmacologically with corticosteroids and bronchodilators. Prednisone (Prediderm 5 mg PO) initially was prescribed in 2 cycles: from days 1 to 7, 5 mg q24 h; and from days 8 to 14, 2.5 mg PO q24h. Because of insufficient clinical improvement, fluticasone and salmeterol (Seretide) spray was used during acute asthmatic episodes at a dosage of one puff q12h for 14 days. In addition, the cat experienced frequent vomiting, which was managed with ondansetron (Vonau) 4 mg PO q12h for 14 days.

Radiographic imaging of lung condition over time and intramuscular cell therapy application. (A) Radiograph of the lung taken at initial diagnosis, showing radiopacity in the caudal bronchi. (B) Radiograph of the lung taken just before cell therapy, indicating a progression of the respiratory condition since the initial diagnosis. (C) Radiograph of the lung 30 days after completing the treatment course, showing improvement, with the lung returning to a level of radiopacity similar to the initial diagnostic image. This suggests a regression of disease severity following treatment.

The cat received 3 IM injections of allogeneic MSCs, isolated from healthy young feline donors, exhibiting a fibroblast-like morphology, and provided by CellTech (Tecnologias com Células-Tronco Research Center, Brazil). The treatment consisted of 3 injections of 1 × 106 MSCs per kg body weight at 30-day intervals. During the clinical examination conducted immediately before the first cell treatment, the cat exhibited persistent coughing.

On thoracic radiographs taken at diagnosis, opacity can be observed in the caudal lung fields, accompanied by slight calcification of the bronchial walls, consistent with chronic bronchial disease (Figure 1A). In the second radiograph, taken a year after diagnosis (Figure 1B), this opacification had intensified, indicating disease progression. Thirty days after the third treatment with MSCs, another radiographic examination was performed (Figure 1C), showing slight improvement but lung field opacification at the interstitial and bronchial levels was still visible, as well as calcification of the bronchial walls and sparse peribronchial infiltrates with a heterogeneous appearance and distribution, secondary to bronchopathy.

The cat showed no local or systemic adverse effects during treatment or in the 2-year follow-up period, providing evidence for the safety of the treatment. After the first MSC treatment, the use of the inhaler was discontinued. Thirty days after the final application, the owner reported increased activity, more engagement with other pets, and that the animal was displaying hunting behavior during play with other cats and participating in interactions with the owners, behaviors not observed before treatment. Because of remission of clinical signs, ondansetron and corticosteroids were discontinued. The cat’s weight increased from 4.8 kg before treatment to 5.4 kg afterward. The weight gain coincided with complete and persistent remission of acute asthmatic episodes, as well as improved appetite, suggesting a clinically meaningful response to treatment.

An evaluative questionnaire on the animal’s quality of life also was conducted before and after the treatment. The scale consisted of 20 items related to health and quality of life, with response options ranging from 0 (not at all) to 6 (couldn’t be better) in 3 domains: vitality, comfort, and emotional well-being as previously described.13

As a single-case report, statistical analysis followed the “AB design” for single-patient studies,14 providing an appropriate framework for repeated measures and supporting the evaluation of changes over time. Statistical analysis was carried out using Friedman’s test with n, because a Shapiro–Wilk test rejected the null hypothesis of normality of the distribution. Results were considered significant if P value < .05.

As observed in Figure 2, significant improvements in all 3 domains, as well as in overall score, were observed after treatment. The cat’s vitality score increased from 1.27 ± 1.61 before treatment to 5.10 ± 0.94 after treatment. The comfort score rose from 2.00 ± 2.20 pretreatment to 5.37 ± 1.19 posttreatment. Similarly, the emotional well-being score improved from 0.57 ± 0.79 before treatment to 5.29 ± 0.95 after treatment.

Discussion

In this case, the IM administration of MSCs stabilized the cat’s clinical condition, demonstrated by the observed improvement in quality of life and normalization of respiratory status such that the previously prescribed daily corticosteroids and bronchodilators could be discontinued without local or systemic adverse effects. The cat’s antiemetics also were suspended without a return of vomiting, and the cat’s appetite increased enough for it to gain 0.6 kg over the course of the study.

The observed improvement in the cat’s quality of life may be attributed to anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory bioactive factors secreted by the administered MSCs.11 The pathogenesis of asthma involves the activation of T helper 2 (Th2) lymphocytes, which release cytokines, notably interleukins 4 and 5 (IL-4 and IL-5). These cytokines contribute to the production of immunoglobulin E (IgE) by B lymphocytes, the proliferation of mast cells, and the growth and activation of eosinophils, as well as the expansion of their progenitors in the bone marrow.1,4,15 As previously reviewed,16 MSCs modulate the Th2 to Th1 ratio and allergic responses by secreting transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), interferon gamma (IFN-γ), IL-10, and various interleukins (ILs; IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13), enhancing regulatory T (Treg) cells, decreasing IgE, mast cells, goblet cells, nitrosative stress, and eosinophils, ultimately decreasing phagocytic activity in alveolar macrophages to control asthmatic injury.

A clear difference was observed between the pretreatment questionnaire, and after treatment, when the cat had become much more active. A previous study using the same scale stated that an increase of 0.98 in vitality, 0.8 in comfort, and 0.58 in emotional well-being was sufficient to recategorize animals from the “sick” group to the “healthy” group.13 This case report supports the effectiveness of MSC treatment with an increase of 3.83 points in vitality, 3.37 points in comfort, and 4.72 points in emotional well-being.

Although posttreatment radiographs did not show complete remission of the observed bronchial pattern 30 days after treatment, the cat exhibited substantial clinical improvement and prompted the question of whether additional doses of MSCs would be required to fully resolve the observed lung abnormalities, despite the therapeutic effects seen. A previous study that also investigated cell treatment in cats with asthma showed that MSCs may have a delayed effect in decreasing airway inflammation and remodeling in cats with experimentally induced asthma, because these changes were only observed 9 months after cell injection.17 Another study showed that, although MSCs did not decrease airway inflammation or hyperresponsiveness in cats with chronic asthma, they significantly improved lung attenuation and bronchial wall thickening on computed tomography scans by month 8.18 Notably, a major limitation in the diagnosis is the reliance on thoracic radiographs, which are neither sensitive nor specific for the condition.1 This problem emphasizes the challenges of relying solely on imaging to assess disease progression or resolution in asthmatic cats.

From a clinical standpoint, remission was clearly evident after the 3-dose treatment regimen, suggesting that radiographic changes may not always reflect clinically meaningful outcomes. This discrepancy emphasizes the complexity of assessing recovery, because clinical improvements are influenced by a range of systemic factors, and MSCs respond dynamically at a systemic level. We hypothesize that structural changes in the lungs do not fully capture the essential aspects of patient recovery, which could influence the design of clinical trial assessments in the future. To better understand the relationship between structural changes and functional recovery, additional longitudinal studies are needed.

Although IV administration is the most commonly used route of delivery in preclinical lung disease studies, alternative approaches, such as intratracheal administration, also have been investigated and were shown to be equally or potentially even more effective.19 IV injection of cells typically results in short-lived post-infusion persistence,20 which may limit therapeutic efficacy. This limited persistence can be attributed to a combination of factors, including an immediate blood-mediated inflammatory reaction,21 and the administered cells becoming physically trapped in lung capillaries.22 In fact, a previous study23 suggested that an inverse relationship exists between the number of cells retained by the lung and the targeting efficiency of cell therapy. The relative contribution of delivery route and cell clearance to clinical outcomes is presently an open question, but clearance is an order of magnitude faster (2-14 days) in IV administration than in IM administration (up to 150 days), providing evidence that IM administration improves cell persistence.24,25 In other conditions, such as colitis, IM administration has been shown to be preferable in a direct comparison to IV and SC administration,26 raising the question of whether a similar situation may be true for a wider set of conditions. Taking these data into consideration from a wider perspective, it follows logically that if, as is now generally accepted, that the primary therapeutic action of MCS is their secretory activity, then a more persistent cell will exert a more persistent therapeutic effect, potentially resulting in improved clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

In summary, our case report demonstrates that IM administration of MSC substantially improved the quality of life of a 7-year-old cat with chronic asthma, allowing for the discontinuation of conventional medications and resulting in a remission of clinical signs. The animal exhibited improved vitality, comfort, and emotional well-being posttreatment, emphasizing the potential of MSC treatment in veterinary medicine. Veterinary clinical trials offer critical insights into the safety and effectiveness of treatments for potential applications in humans while also enhancing the standard of care for animal patients. Our results suggest that optimizing delivery methods, such as using IM administration of MSC, may yield better therapeutic outcomes for chronic respiratory conditions, and should be confirmed by randomized longitudinal studies.
