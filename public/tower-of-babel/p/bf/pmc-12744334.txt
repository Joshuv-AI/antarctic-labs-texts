The Effect of Live Environmental Music Therapy and Prerecorded Music on State Anxiety, Stress, Pain, and Well-Being Levels of Patients and Caregivers in the Emergency Department Waiting Room: A Multicenter Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Mark Ettenberger, Angélica Hernández, Moshé Amarillo, Raúl Suarez, Ana María Díaz, Ornella Fiorillo Moreno, Ana María Moreno Quintero, Bryan Alonso Ríos Suarez, Lina Marcela Gómez González, Guiselle Alexandra Cristancho Olaya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo determine the effect of live environmental music therapy (EMT), prerecorded music (same music as EMT), and a control condition on state anxiety, stress, pain, and well-being levels in patients and caregivers in the emergency department (ED) waiting areas of 2 general hospitals in Colombia, South America.MethodsThis was a pragmatic, multicenter randomized clinical trial with 3 arms: standard care + EMT, standard care + prerecorded music, and standard care only. Participants included 256 adult patients and 174 caregivers. Primary outcome was the six-item State-Trait Anxiety Inventory. Secondary outcomes were stress and pain levels, assessed with Visual Analog Scales (Visual Analog Stress Scale and Visual Analog Pain Scale), and well-being, assessed with the Well-Being Numerical Rating Scales.ResultsResults indicated that both music therapy interventions were associated with statistically significant reductions in anxiety, stress, and pain levels in patients and caregivers. Improvements in psychologic well-being were observed primarily in the group receiving live music therapy. Overall, live EMT showed better improvements across the emotional and physical outcomes.ConclusionThe results of this study indicate that live EMT and prerecorded music implemented by trained music therapists can be effective strategies for improving mental health and reducing pain in patients and caregivers in the ED waiting area.Keywords: music therapy, recorded music, anxiety, stress, well-being, emergency department, waiting room

The Bottom Line

Many patients and caregivers experience mental health difficulties in the emergency department waiting areas. In this multicenter study, live environmental music therapy and prerecorded music delivered by certified music therapists were found to positively impact patients’ and caregivers’ state anxiety, stress, and well-being levels, in addition to reducing patients’ pain levels. Music therapy may thus be a safe, effective, and humanizing strategy to improve patient and caregiver mental health during wait times in the emergency department.

Introduction

Background

Emergency departments (EDs) provide care to patients who seek immediate medical attention. Many EDs rely on a robust and effective triage system that prioritizes patients depending on the urgency of their medical conditions.1 As the ED is one of the primary hospital entry points for patients across all ages and needs, its environment presents unique challenges for both patients and healthcare professionals.2, 3, 4 When visiting the ED, patients and caregivers frequently experience elevated levels of anxiety and stress.5,6 Patient distress results not only from their injuries and pain but also from the uncertainty surrounding diagnosis and treatment.7,8 Environmental factors like seating, lighting, and ambient noise, as well as unmet needs such as hunger and thirst, can further exacerbate discomfort.9,10 Additionally, issues such as long wait times, overcrowding, privacy concerns, or witnessing medical emergency procedures have been shown to affect patient and caregiver experience in the ED.5,11 High anxiety levels are particularly relevant in this regard, as they may remain undetected by nursing staff and can negatively impact patient recovery and perception of care quality.10,12, 13, 14 Several studies have shown that satisfaction with care quality in the ED is dependent on a variety of factors, including effective pain management in patients and anxiety levels in caregivers.15, 16, 17

Importance

This underlines the importance of a holistic and patient-centered approach to care experience in the ED, considering the physical, environmental, and psychologic needs of both patients and accompanying caregivers or family members.18 Music therapy and other music-based interventions are increasingly being integrated into EDs to address these challenges. Music’s potential to positively affect the body’s nervous and neurochemical system provides a unique opportunity for its application in health settings.19,20 Furthermore, music can aid in emotional release and regulation, foster a sense of safety, and facilitate social connection.21,22

Several studies have demonstrated the potential of prerecorded music to alleviate anxiety and pain in adult ED patients.23, 24, 25 For example, Mandel et al26 conducted a 3-year study involving 180 patients, revealing that individual live music therapy sessions significantly decreased stress and pain levels. However, a recent meta-analysis on music in the ED did not find statistically significant differences in anxiety, pain, distress, or vital signs of patients, pointing to the large heterogeneity of studies.27 Similarly, a previous literature review on music in general medical waiting areas not only highlights its potential to improve patients’ well-being but also stresses limitations in existing research, such as methodological inconsistencies, small sample sizes, and a lack of detailed descriptions of musical interventions.28 Furthermore, most studies in waiting rooms have focused so far on prerecorded music, with limited exploration of live music approaches. Exceptions hereof are the studies by Van Dokkum et al,29 who investigated the impact on live music performances by visiting artists in hospital waiting rooms, and Rossetti et al,30 who applied live environmental music therapy (EMT) in a radiation therapy waiting room, demonstrating significant reductions in anxiety and perceived waiting time for both patients and caregivers. As no previous studies were found that focused specifically on adult patients and their caregivers in the ED waiting room, this study tries to fill some of the existing knowledge gaps by investigating the effect of both live EMT and prerecorded music on mental health and pain in patients and caregivers in the ED waiting area.

Goals of This Investigation

The main objective of this study was to determine the effect of live EMT versus prerecorded music versus standard care alone on state anxiety levels of adult patients and caregivers in the emergency waiting rooms of 2 general hospitals in Colombia, South America.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study was a pragmatic, multicenter, randomized clinical trial (RCT) with 3 arms:

•Intervention group 1 (IG1): standard care + live EMT

Intervention group 1 (IG1): standard care + live EMT

•Intervention group 2 (IG2): standard care + prerecorded music

Intervention group 2 (IG2): standard care + prerecorded music

•Control group (CG): standard care alone

Control group (CG): standard care alone

Ethics approval was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of the Fundación Universitaria Sanitas (CEIFUS 1779-24, approval date: June 18, 2024). The study was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06510153, registration date: July 19, 2024), and the study protocol has been published in Hernández et al.31 All participants signed a written informed consent. This study follows the CONSORT guidelines for reporting randomized trials,32 and the CONSORT extensions for social and psychologic interventions33 and nonpharmacologic treatments.34 Data collection lasted from September 3 to December 10, 2024.

The study took place at the ED waiting areas of 2 general hospitals in Colombia. Both EDs belong to the same health care provider, which guarantees similarities in terms of care teams, infrastructure, and care philosophy: the ED waiting area of hospital site A has 40 seats, the one in hospital site B has 38 seats, and both EDs admit patients from subsidized, public, or private health care insurance. Most common reasons for ED visits at both hospitals are pain (abdominal and pelvic), fever, and intestinal infections. Annual ED visits are approximately 40,000-50,000 patients per hospital site, from which about 85% are classified as triage levels 3, 4, or 5 based on the Emergency Severity Index (ESI). Patients who fall under these triage categories do not have immediate life-threatening conditions and are not high-risk or likely to deteriorate in their medical condition.35

Selection of Participants

Participant selection was done by convenience sampling of patients and caregivers present at the ED waiting areas on predefined intervention days (Tuesdays, Wednesdays, and Thursdays) and intervention times (11 am and 3 pm). The inclusion criteria were adult patients and caregivers present in the ED waiting rooms during the intervention and control conditions, patients with triage levels 3, 4, or 5 according to the ESI, and patients and caregivers who self-reported having the necessary literacy and cognitive abilities to understand and fill out the questionnaires. The exclusion criteria were patients and caregivers who did not consent to participate in the study and who self-reported having hearing problems. Block size randomization of multiples of 3 generated by a computer software (Excel, version 2408) was used to randomly assign any of the 3 intervention conditions (EMT, prerecorded, control) to any given intervention day (Tuesday, Wednesday, and Thursday) and intervention time (11 am and 3 pm). This guaranteed a random and equal distribution of each intervention condition to each intervention day and time. No participant has repeated twice in an intervention condition. Sample Size Calculation

Sample size calculation was performed using G∗Power 3.1.9.736 and based on the primary outcome, considering a 3 × 2 factorial design (3 groups with 2 repeated measures each). The treatment effect between the groups was determined using a two-way analysis of variance and repeated measures, with a statistical power of 0.80, a significance level of 0.05, and an effect size of η2 = 0.01. The effect size was determined based on previous research30 and the expected magnitude of differences between the groups. The sample size calculation resulted in 246 participating patients. For caregivers, a separate sample size calculation was performed, for which the effect size was also determined in a previous study.30 Considering an effect size of η2 = 0.017, the sample size resulted in 147 participating caregivers.

Interventions

The description follows the updated EQUATOR Network guidelines for reporting music-based interventions.37

Environmental music therapy: IG1

EMT consists of improvised live music that aims to modulate and improve the hospital sound environment, using improvised musical elements and structures to enhance a sense of safety and comfort. The intervention followed the 4 steps of EMT described by Rossetti et al30: assessment of the environment, initiation of the intervention, development of the intervention, and closure. First, the musical instruments were cleaned according to a published cleaning and disinfection protocol.38 When arriving at the ED waiting room, the music therapists (all with master’s level training and clinical experience in hospital contexts) observed the space, got a sense of how many patients and caregivers were present, looked for an optimal spot to provide the intervention, and assessed the current auditory environment. Before starting, the music therapists presented themselves and explained their role and the aim of the music. It was mentioned that participants could participate in a way that was most comfortable for them, for example, by closing their eyes, focusing on their breathing, imagining a favorite landscape or a safe place, or just continuing their current activities. The musical instruments used were a classical guitar (Yamaha C-40), an ocean drum (a round double-skinned frame drum that imitates the sound of ocean waves), and tubular bells (aluminum tubes of different sizes that hang on cords and create long-lasting tones when struck with a soft mallet). The music was improvised, started slowly and gradually, was without lyrics or words, consisted of simple chord progressions in major tonalities, was slow to moderate in tempo, and included free-flowing vocal or instrumental melodies. The music was continuously adapted according to the music therapists’ assessment of the environment including moments of tension and release. A more detailed description of the intervention can be found in the published study protocol.31

The length of the intervention was based on the study by Rossetti et al30 and was set to 20 minutes. The same music therapists delivered the interventions at both hospital sites throughout the study process. Although no standardized script was used for presenting the interventions and no formal treatment fidelity assessment was carried out, music therapists were previously trained in the intervention, explained the procedures in similar terms, and were provided with regular feedback and supervision to ensure coherence of intervention delivery across sites.

Prerecorded music: IG2

A recording of the same musical structures and elements using the same musical instruments as the live music was created for IG2. The recording was made by the same music therapists who provided the live EMT. A Bluetooth speaker (JBL Go 3) was positioned centrally in the waiting area to play back the music. The recording lasted the same time as the EMT intervention (20 minutes), and the music therapists were present at the ED waiting areas during playback, but did not interfere otherwise.

Standard care only: CG

The CG received standard care only according to each hospital’s care protocols. During standard care, the music therapists were present at the waiting areas, but did not engage in any activities. The control condition lasted for 20 minutes.

Measurements

At the predefined intervention days and times, a member of the research team explained the study to patients and caregivers present at the ED waiting areas at each hospital site. Then, the informed consent and the first set of questionnaires were filled out by those who agreed to participate and fulfilled the inclusion and exclusion criteria. To avoid differential effects of music therapists being present during EMT and prerecorded music, but not during the control condition, music therapists were present during all intervention conditions but did not interfere with participant selection, recruitment, or data collection procedures. Due to the nature of the interventions and the context, music therapists, patients, caregivers, and data collectors were not blinded for any of the outcome measures or group allocation. All data were subsequently stored in an electronic database, and the accuracy of data transcription from the paper to the electronic format was regularly checked. Data analysis was performed by a blinded member of the research team.

Outcomes

Primary outcome measure

The primary outcome was the change in state anxiety levels from pre- to postintervention timepoints measured with the Spanish version of the six-item State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-6), a short version of the original state anxiety scale of the STAI.39 The STAI-6 consists of 6 items evaluated on a 4-item Likert scale, with higher scores indicating higher anxiety levels. The STAI-6 was developed by Chlan et al,40 and its Spanish version was validated by Perpiñá-Galvañ et al.41,42 STAI-6 scores can be converted to full STAI scores by multiplying them by 20/6.30,43,44

Secondary outcome measures

•Stress: Stress levels were measured by the Visual Analog Stress Scale (VAS-S).45 The VAS-S consists of a 10-cm line divided into 10 points, each separated by 1 cm, with 0 indicating the absence of stress and 10 the maximum possible stress.

•Pain (for patients only): Perceived pain levels of participating patients were assessed with the Visual Analog Pain Scale (VAS-P), which equals the VAS-S in its form. VAS scales are widely used in pain research, and minimum clinically important differences pre- and postinterventions range from 9 mm46 to 14 mm.47

•Well-being: Patient and caregiver well-being was measured by the Well-being Numerical Rating Scales (WB-NRSs) developed by Bonacchi et al.48 The WB-NRSs consist of a line divided into numbers from 1 to 10: 1 indicates a state of absolute distress, and 10 a state of absolute well-being. Sociodemographic and basic medical data

Additionally, basic sociodemographic and medical data were extracted from patients’ medical history, such as age, primary diagnosis at the time of admission, triage classification, and the presence of a caregiver or not, among others.

Data Analysis

For data analysis, JASP was used Ref. 49. Delta scores (postintervention minus preintervention) were calculated for each outcome variable to examine the effect of the intervention conditions (live EMT, prerecorded music, and control). Comparisons with the CG were conducted using Welch t tests. Statistical significance was set at α < 0.05, and 95% CIs were reported to estimate the precision of the effect sizes. For the secondary analysis, participants were stratified by anxiety levels (below versus above the clinically significant cutoff, <40 or ≥40).50 Comparisons between intervention conditions within each stratum were also performed using Welch t tests, applying the same statistical significance criterion and reporting CIs. Additionally, we examined patient acuity, waiting time, and caregiver presence as potential covariates; however, as these variables did not affect the primary outcomes, they are not reported in detail. Missing data were minimal and, given their negligible impact, were not addressed with specific statistical procedures.

Results

Participant Characteristics

In this study, 258 patients and 176 caregivers participated. Most patients were female 69.3% (179/258), with a mean age of 43.1 years. The self-reported waiting time before the intervention began was on average 1 hour and 40 minutes. Most patients (97% [252/258]) were classified as triage levels 3 or 4, and all had a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15/15 upon admission to the ED. Among caregivers, about 67.2% (119/176) were female, with a mean age of 43.1 years. There were no statistically significant differences in any of the baseline variables between the 3 groups. Main Results

Music interventions significantly reduced patients’ STAI scores compared with the CG (mean difference = −8.3; 95% CI, [–10.9, −5.6]; P < .001), with a large effect size (Cohen d [d] = −0.8). Live EMT produced the greatest reduction in anxiety scores (mean difference = −8.7; 95% CI, [–12.6, −4.8]; P < .001; d = −0.9), whereas prerecorded music yielded a slightly smaller but still substantial reduction (mean difference = −7.8; 95% CI, [–11.4, −4.2]; P < .001; d = −0.8). When pooling scores across group interventions and comparing the hospital sites, no statistically significant difference in STAI scores was observed (mean difference = 1.6, 95% CI, [−0.9, 4.2], P = .21; d = 0.1).

Caregivers showed outcomes similar to patients. Live EMT produced a significant reduction in STAI scores compared with the CG (mean difference = −7.9; 95% CI, [−12.2, −3.7]; P < .001; d = −0.82), and prerecorded EMT also led to a significant decrease (mean difference = −6.6; 95% CI, [−10.8, −2.4]; P < .001; d = −0.68). No statistically significant differences were found between the 2 EDs assessed (mean difference = −2.1; 95% CI, [−4.9, 0.9]; P = .158; d = −0.2).

Secondary analysis of main results

Longer waiting times in the ED were associated with increased anxiety, particularly among control patients whose baseline anxiety levels were below the clinical cutoff (<40) (mean difference = 6.5; 95% CI, [0.5, 12.5]; P < .03; d = 0.56; Fig 2). This time-dependent increase in anxiety was attenuated in patients receiving the prerecorded EMT intervention (mean difference = −1.1; 95% CI, [−4.1, 6.1]; P = .679; d = −0.1) and reversed in those receiving live music therapy, who showed a decrease in anxiety levels over time (mean difference = −4; 95% CI, [−7.1, 0.8]; P = .01; d = −0.5). In contrast, control patients with baseline anxiety levels above the cutoff ≥ 40 did not exhibited a time-dependent increase in anxiety (mean difference = −0.5; 95% CI, [−2.5, 1.4]; P = .615; d = −0.05) and experienced consistent reductions regardless of intervention type (prerecorded music: mean difference = −8.3; 95% CI, [−11.2, −5.4]; P < 0.001; d = −0.74; live EMT: mean difference = −8.3; 95% CI, [−11.1, −5.5]; P < 0.001; d = −0.82).

Secondary Results

Stress

Both music therapy interventions reduced self-reported stress levels in patients compared with the CG. Live music therapy led to a significant but small reduction (mean difference = −23 mm; 95% CI, [−31 mm, −15 mm]; P < .001; d = −1.04), and the prerecorded music therapy intervention also showed a significant effect (mean difference = −19 mm; 95% CI, [−27 mm, −11 mm]; P < .001; d = −0.85). Caregivers demonstrated a comparable pattern of improvement, with both interventions yielding significant reductions in stress as compared with controls (mean difference = −73 mm; 95% CI, [−103 mm, −43 mm]; P < .001; d = −0.75).

Pain

Music interventions reduced patients’ self-reported pain levels compared with the CG. Live music therapy produced a significant reduction (mean difference = −9.9 mm; 95% CI, [−17 mm, 2 mm]; P = .005; d = −0.47), whereas prerecorded music showed a smaller yet statistically significant decrease (mean difference = −9.6 mm; 95% CI, [−17 mm, −2 mm]; P = .008; d = −0.45).

Well-being

When pooled, both music therapy interventions improved patients’ psychologic well-being compared with the CG (mean difference = 2.2; 95% CI, [0.1, 4.4]; P = .03; d = 0.28). Live music therapy significantly improved psychologic well-being (mean difference = 3.0; 95% CI, [−0.3, 5.7]; P = .02; d = 0.33), whereas the prerecorded music condition did not differ significantly from the CG (mean difference = 2.6; 95% CI, [−0.4, 5.7]; P = .1; d = 0.24). No statistically significant effects were found for other dimensions of the WB-NRSs in either patients or caregivers (Fig 3). Limitations

There are several limitations that need to be considered. First, as this is the first multisite RCT on music therapy in the ED waiting area in Colombia, generalizations of the results to other contexts and cultural or geographic regions need to be made cautiously. Second, no information is available about what percentage of participants had a previous mental health diagnosis, as this is not consistently reported in the hospital systems. This may be relevant, as participants with a psychiatric diagnosis might respond differently to the interventions and therefore could have affected the results. Furthermore, we have not distinguished between patients who might have had additional stressors, such as pregnant women for example. This should be considered in future studies. Third, as the study took place with lower acuity patients, on weekdays and during daytimes, and was based on specific inclusion and exclusion criteria, selection bias might have played a role. Therefore, our study population might not be representative of patients and caregivers visiting the ED on weekends or during nighttime hours for example. In addition, some inclusion and exclusion criteria were self-reported by participants, such as having necessary cognitive and literacy skills or impaired hearing. However, participants might have just refused to take part in the study, without mentioning a specific reason. Thus, we do not know what percentage of participants declined due to these issues. Different studies assessing the impact of music therapy at various times and days and with specific subpopulations are needed to further assess the generalizability of our results. Fourth, blinding of patients, caregivers, music therapists, and data collectors was not viable in this study. As patients and caregivers were constantly on the move in the ED (some entered and some were called for medical check-up or exams), the data collector had to be aware of those participants who were continuously present during the interventions and who filled out both the pre- and postintervention measurements. This might have led to a Hawthorne effect, as patients and caregivers were aware of the presence of data collectors. Although we have tried to make sure that the study conditions remained the same during all intervention conditions (ie, music therapists were also present at the waiting areas during the control condition), this should be considered in future studies. Fifth, as participants were naturally aware of which intervention they had been randomly assigned, treatment expectancy might have played a role. This concern has previously been mentioned in music therapy studies.31,51,52 However, additional questionnaires might also increase discomfort and stress in patients and the time needed to fill them out. In this study, about 25% of participants did not manage to fill out the postintervention questionnaires, as they had been called to medical exams or check-ups during the intervention conditions. Thus, adding more questionnaires might lower the number of participants who are able to be present throughout all study phases. Fifth, in this study, treatment fidelity was not formally assessed. Although regular team meetings and supervision of the music therapists guaranteed a close follow-up and detection of any protocol deviations, individual styles of music therapists might have had an impact on the results. Lastly, although center effects were not observed in this study, there might be additional cultural, social, and other confounding factors that could have influenced the outcomes. Therefore, the overall results of this study should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

In this study, both live EMT and prerecorded music resulted in a statistically significant decrease in anxiety and stress levels in both patients and caregivers, a statistically significant decrease in pain levels in patients, and a statistically significant increase in psychologic well-being for the live EMT group. For participants with anxiety levels above the clinical cutoff (≥40), both live EMT and prerecorded music reduced anxiety levels. For those with anxiety levels below the cutoff (<40), prerecorded music helped to keep anxiety levels stable, whereas EMT decreased anxiety levels. These findings are highly relevant, as studies have shown that high anxiety levels can negatively impact patient recovery in the ED and that they are associated with an overall increased healthcare utilization across care settings.53,54 Besides patients’ mental health, that of caregivers is relevant, as high anxiety levels in this population are associated with lower care satisfaction.17 Furthermore, a recent scoping review highlights the lack of studies reporting interventions to lower caregivers’ distress in the ED.11

The reduction of pain levels of patients in both music therapy groups warrants attention, as pain is one of the main reasons for ED visits.55,56 Additionally, effective pain management has been found to be associated with greater care satisfaction in ED patients.15,16 Although there is a scarcity of studies that measure stress in ED patients, a recent study highlights that 98.8% of patient companions in the ED presented some sort of stress, including several risk factors for high stress levels and subsequent impending violence.17 In a future study, additional measures of patient and caregiver satisfaction could be used to detect potential spillover effects of the anxiety, stress, and pain-reducing effects of music therapy in the ED waiting area. Although well-being was generally high even at baseline for all intervention groups, only the EMT group showed a significant increase in psychologic well-being. This might hint at the particularity of live music therapy as a resource-oriented approach to mental health in the ED and align music therapy with a humanized and person-centered care model.57 However, EMT showed no effect on the other dimensions measured with the WB-NRSs (physical, social, spiritual, and general). This is somewhat surprising as it could be hypothesized that a decrease in anxiety, stress, and pain would lead to an increase in well-being generally. A reason might be that well-being is not a construct that is easily being measured for a specific moment in time (eg, such as state anxiety) and that participants might have scored according to their well-being beyond the current moment. Another reason might be that well-being, anxiety, and stress are not directly interrelated. Such issues could be further explored in future studies.

The results of this study present several overlaps with previous research on music-based interventions and music therapy in the ED, for example, with respect to the reduction of anxiety and pain levels of patients.18, 19, 20,58 However, most studies in the ED were conducted in the observation area or with patients individually. With respect to waiting areas in medical contexts, positive effects of music-based interventions and therapies have been reported in radiation oncology, hospital lobbies, or general health care waiting spaces,29,30,59 but studies in the ED waiting areas are scarce. Only 1 similar study was found, but it used prerecorded classical music in the pediatric ED.60 Thus, comparability of our results remains limited, and more studies in this specific context and setting are needed before considering any definite conclusions.

It should be highlighted that in this study, not only live EMT but also the prerecorded EMT session was found to be effective for both patients and caregivers. This is relevant as not all EDs might have access to or be able to hire a music therapist. Using recordings that are created or supervised by music therapists might thus be a relatively easy and cost-effective way to improve patient and caregiver mental health and well-being during wait times. This is in line with a recent multilevel meta-analysis on music therapy and anxiety, which found that music listening interventions designed or offered by music therapists showed the strongest effects.61

In summary, the results of this study indicate that both live EMT and prerecorded music implemented by trained music therapists can be effective strategies for improving mental health in patients and caregivers in the ED waiting area. More international multisite studies are needed to further confirm these results.
