Pharmacokinetic profiles and egg residue patterns of levamisole in laying hens at two dosing rates and two routes of administration
Authors: Jeong-Won Kim, Dae-Hwan Kim, Ji-Soo Jeong, Jin-Hwa Kim, Chang-Yeop Kim, Je-Won Ko, Tae-Won Kim
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe levamisole maximum residue limit for edible fat, kidney, and muscle of chickens is 0.01 mg/kg. However, no maximum residue limit has been established for eggs. In the present study, the pharmacokinetic profile and levamisole residue in the eggs from laying hens were investigated using ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. A single dose of levamisole (30 mg/kg) was administered via the intramuscular or oral route, and an additional egg residue study was performed with 300 or 600 mg/kg commercial LEV drug (30 or 60 mg/kg as levamisole) orally. The limit of quantification was 0.0056 μg/mL and 0.0015 mg/kg for plasma and eggs, respectively. The plasma concentration was below the limit of quantification 10 and 12 h after intramuscular and oral administration, respectively. The half-life of the absorption phase was comparable between the intramuscular and oral routes, which was approximately 1 h, and the mean maximum concentration value was significantly higher in intramuscular (2.29 ± 0.30 μg/mL) than in oral (1.45 ± 0.38 μg/mL) route. The relative oral bioavailability after intramuscular administration was 92.3%. In the egg residue study, dose-dependent area under concentration and maximum concentration were observed after single oral administration of 30 and 60 mg/kg egg residue, and the calculated withdrawal period for both 30 and 60 mg/kg groups based on the positive list system standard (0.01 mg/kg) was 7 d after the treatment.Key words: egg, laying hen, levamisole, pharmacokinetics, residue

INTRODUCTION

Laying hens play an important role in the global poultry economy (Ma et al., 2021) and are a critical source of food and income for people (Soudkolaei et al., 2021). However, the constant reemergence of helminths has increased the demand for chicken products. Parasites cause prevalent infections in chickens worldwide, and diseases that interfere with food production and health (Nnadi and George, 2010). Therefore, poultry health and production require pragmatic parasite control approaches.

Levamisole (LEV) is an anthelminthic drug that has been successfully used to treat parasitic, viral, and bacterial infections (Scheinfeld et al., 2004). The drug l-2,3,5,6-tetrahydro-6-phenylimidazo(2,1-b) thiazole is a well-known synthetic imidazothiazole derivative that has been widely used in veterinary and human medicine (Fernández et al., 1998; Solomon and Hayes, 2017). LEV is the L-isomer of tetramisole found in racemic mixtures. The parent compound tetramisole was initially introduced as an anthelmintic for veterinary use but it was soon demonstrated that the anthelmintic activity is entirely due to the L-isomer of LEV (Nielsen and Rasmussen, 1983).

LEV is highly effective in controlling gastrointestinal and pulmonary nematodes (Sahagún et al., 2000). In poultry, it is used to treat ascariasis, one of the most prevalent parasitic diseases and about 30 mg/kg reported to be effective in chicken (Kolanović et al., 2018; Feyera et al., 2021). However, it has not been approved by the FDA for use in poultry and the limited data on pharmacokinetics and withdrawal time available (El-Kholy et al., 2006). Moreover, the use of LEV in egg-laying hens for human consumption is prohibited (Kolanović et al., 2018).

Eggs containing veterinary drug residues pose risks to human health, including increased bacterial resistance to antibiotics (Goetting et al., 2011). El-Kholy and Kemppainen (2005) reported that egg laying significantly increased the volume of distribution, total body clearance, and bioavailability of LEV. Furthermore, compared with other anthelmintics, the withdrawal period is relatively longer (2 wk) (El-Kholy and Kemppainen, 2005; Goetting et al., 2011). In the United States, the maximum residual limit (MRL) of LEV for edible tissue of cattle, sheep, and pigs is 0.01 mg/kg (Food and Drug Administration, 2023). The MRL for edible fat, kidney, and muscle of chickens in Korea is 0.01 mg/kg (Ministry of Food and Drug Safety, 2020). However, no MRL has been set for eggs.

In 2024, the Korean government will implement a positive list system (PLS) for livestock products (U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2023). Under this system, the MRL for drugs without an established MRL is set below 0.01 mg/kg to ensure the safety and quality of livestock products. A withdrawal period is required for egg-laying hens and residual data are required to properly evaluate the withdrawal period of poultry-derived foods (Moreno et al., 2015). The aims of this study were 1) to compare pharmacokinetic (PK) profiles after single intramuscular (IM) and oral gavage (PO) administration and 2) to confirm egg residue and absorption profiles following the clinical dose-level treatment of the commercial drug in chickens.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Chemicals and Reagents

LEV analytical standard (L380000, purity >95%) was obtained from the European pharmacopeia reference standard. One kilogram of commercial LEV drug (Daesung Microbiology Lab, Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea) contained 100 g of LEV HCl and 900 g of the excipient, including glucose. Other chemicals, including ammonium formate, formic acid, methanol, acetic acid, acetonitrile, and hexane were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO).

Animal Experiments

The animal studies used 2 different designs. For IM and PO comparison study, 10 healthy laying hens (Hy-line Brown, 2.3 ± 0.2 kg, Sungwonfarm, Sejong) were acclimatized for 7 d in cages and samples were collected. Water was provided ad libitum and food was provided twice daily. After acclimatization, the hens were randomly segregated into IM and PO groups (n = 5 per group), and the dose for both groups was 30 mg/kg. In all PO group, LEV was administrated orally using an oral gavage. LEV was administered to the breast muscles in the IM group. All drugs were dissolved in tap water, and it was confirmed that they were completely dissolved. In all groups, 1 mL of blood was collected from the wing veins at 0, 0.25, 0.75, 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, 12, 24, 36, 48, and 72 h after LEV administration.

To test commercial drugs with 2 dose levels, 24 healthy laying hens (Hy-line Brown, 2.3 ± 0.2 kg, Sungwonfarm, Sejong) were randomly allocated into 2 groups (n = 12/group) after 1-wk acclimatization and the commercial LEV drug was administered (300 or 600 mg/kg as 30 or 60 mg/kg LEV, respectively) through single PO. The 2 dose groups were established for the commercial LEV drug in chickens: one group followed the recommended clinical dosage (Feyera et al., 2021), whereas the other administered a double dosage for a conservative evaluation of egg residue under overdosing conditions.

Blood samples (1 mL) were collected from all groups in heparinized tubes at 0, 0.25, 0.75, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, and 30 h after drug treatment.

The eggs were collected for up to 7 and 14 d from LEV administration, respectively, for residue studies. After each egg collection, the eggs were mixed well and a 2 mL aliquot was withdrawn. Plasma and egg samples were stored at −70°C until further analysis. All experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chungnam National University (Daejeon, Korea; 202112A-CNU-193) and performed in compliance with the Guidelines for Animal Experiments of Chungnam National University.

Plasma and Egg Sample Extraction

Egg homogenate (2 g) was transferred to a 50 mL polypropylene tube and mixed with 20 μL methanol and 10 mL 10 % acetic acid in acetonitrile. After vortexing for 15 min, the tubes were centrifuged at 2,500 × g for 10 min and the upper layer (10 mL) was transferred to another polypropylene tube. Hexane saturated with acetonitrile (10 mL) was added, and the mixture was vortexed for 10 min. The lower layer was evaporated with nitrogen at 40°C and reconstituted with 2 mL methanol. After shaking, the tubes were centrifuged at 2,500 × g for 10 min. An aliquot of 50 μL of this supernatant was injected into UPLC for the analysis. Plasma samples were prepared following the protocol described above but for 1/10th of the volume.

UPLC Analysis and Method Validation

The UPLC system (UPLC 1290 TQ 6470, Agilent) with a Hypersil GOLD C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 3 μm; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) delivering by a flow rate of 0.25 mL/min, was used for chromatography analysis. The mobile phase consisted of 20 mM ammonium formate (in distilled water) (A) and formic acid-methanol (0.2:99.8; v/v) (B). A gradient solvent program was run with the following conditions: 0 to 2.1 min (90% A), 2.2 to 4 min (90%–5% A), 4 to 8 min (5% A), 8 to 10 min (5%–90% A), and 10 to 11 min (90% A). The methods used to analyze plasma and eggs were validated. All samples were fortified with mebendazole as the internal standard. Detection of LEV was conducted in product ion scan mode. The collision energy was determined at 20eV. For the quantitative analysis of sample, data acquisition was conducted in the multiple-reaction-monitoring (MRM) mode, monitoring transition m/z 205.1→145.3 and 178.1 (for LEV), m/z 296.1→264.2 (for internal standard). The limits of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ) were defined according to the International Conference on Harmonization (ICH) guidelines with a signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio of 10. Quality control (QC) samples of LEV were used to perform an evaluation precision and recovery. Intraday precision was evaluated by analyzing 3 replicates of QC samples on the same day. The interday precision was evaluated by analyzing the QC samples 3 times per day over 3 d. The recovery of LEV from plasma and eggs was calculated by comparing the content of LEV spiked in blank samples and the theoretical concentrations.

Pharmacokinetic and Statistical Analysis

One-compartmental PK analysis was performed using ThothPro (ThothPro LLC, Gdańsk, Poland). The maximum concentration (Cmax) of LEV and the time required to reach Cmax (Tmax) were determined. Compartmental model analysis was used to determine the following PK parameters of LEV: absorption rate constant (K01), elimination rate constant (K10), the half-life of the absorption and elimination phases (K01_HL and K10_HL, respectively), and total area under the curve (AUC), clearance/bioavailability (CL/F), and volume of distribution/bioavailability (Vd/F). Log-linear regression with a 95% confidence interval and a 99% upper tolerance limit was applied to egg concentration to set the withdrawal period. The concentrations of LEV were calculated using WT 1.4 software, adopted by the Committee for Veterinary Medicinal Products of EMA (European Medicines Agency). To evaluate statistical significance between groups, an unpaired t test was carried and the significance were marked as P < 0.05.

Plasma Protein Binding Assay

Plasma protein binding assay was performed according to Petkova et al. (2022) with slight modification. A plasma sample (450 µL) from each of the 3 laying hens was added to 500 ppb LEV (50 µL), then incubated at 37°C for 1h. The mixture was filtered using polytetrafluoroethylene membrane (Acrodisc CR 13 mm, 0.45 µm, Pall Life Sciences, Mumbai, India). RESULTS

Pharmacokinetic Study

The UPLC method was validated and found to be specific and linear (with a range of 0–5 μg/mL) for LEV in plasma (R2 > 0.94). The LOD and LOQ for plasma were determined as signal-to-noise ratios of 10, which were 0.0017 and 0.0056 μg/mL for plasma, respectively. The recovery for plasma was 98.3 ± 1.1%. All the intra- and interday relative standard deviations for plasma were lower than 8%.

No adverse effects were observed in any chickens during the experiment. Egg Residue Analysis

The UPLC method was validated and found to be specific and linear (with a range of 0–5 mg/kg) for LEV in egg (R2 > 0.98). The LOD and LOQ for egg were 0.0004 and 0.0015 mg/kg, respectively. The recovery for eggs was 97.4 ± 5.6%. All the intra- and interday relative standard deviations for plasma and eggs were lower than 8%.

The concentration of LEV in eggs was determined after IM and PO administration of LEV analytical standard at 30 mg/kg in Figure 3. The LEV peak concentrations in the egg after PO and IM administration was 1.23 ± 0.67 mg/kg and 0.52 ± 0.17 mg/kg, respectively (Table. 3). Although the concentration of LEV in eggs gradually decreased, LEV was present 6 d after IM or PO injection. The LEV content of the eggs was reduced in a time-dependent manner; LEV was lower than LOD in the eggs collected 7 d after IM or PO administration in Figure 3.

DISCUSSION

LEV is recognized as a biopharmaceutical classification system (BCS) class 1 drug that is readily absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract and excreted in urine after being metabolized by the liver (Kouassi et al., 1986; Kolanović et al., 2018; del Moral-Sanchez et al., 2019).

Considering mass medication to livestock, the convenience of administration is a crucial factor to consider, alongside PK properties (Kim and De Jesus, 2023). In this study, PK profiles of LEV were compared between the PO and IM route as well as in egg residues. LEV PO administration showed a significantly delayed Tmax with reduced Cmax compared to the IM administration. The average AUC value in the PO group was comparable to the IM group and the relative oral bioavailability was about 92.3%.

Although the absorption rate constant was evidently lower in PO than in IM, the overall exposure of LEV to the body after PO administration was not hindered. This observation might be attributed to the fact that LEV belongs to BCS class 1, which is characterized by high solubility and high permeability (Papich and Martinez, 2015).

Moreover, LEV is known to undergo extensive hepatic metabolism in various species; 3% to 10% of LEV dose was excreted unchanged in the urine, and the possibility of an extensive first-pass effect has been suggested in humans (Villanueva et al., 2003; Kambayashi et al., 2023). Although the metabolism of LEV in laying hens has not been previously reported, the present study suggests that the first-pass effect after PO administration in laying hens is similar to that after intramuscular administration. This inference is supported by the comparable AUC values between the PO and IM groups. Nevertheless, such predictions may be premature due to the absence of intravenous administration data, highlighting the need for further research.

PLS is a management standard that sets the MRL at 0.01 mg/kg for drugs for which the MRL has not been established, with the aim of managing the risks associated with drug exposure (U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2023). The egg residue profiles were similar between the PO and IM groups, in both routes, the levels of LEV were found to be below the PLS standard at 7 d after drug administration. LEV residues were likely incorporated after administration and stored in preovulatory yolks, which take 4 to 6 d to accumulate and reside in eggs after egg formation (Kaspers, 2016). However, the observed level was below the PLS standard 7 d after treatment, suggesting that the initial distribution to the liver may not have been significant.

After confirming the difference in PK and egg residue after PO and IM administration with chemical LEV standard, we proceeded to verify the PK changes associated with different doses and the impact on residual levels in eggs using a commercial LEV drug.

In the present study, the low- and high-dose groups received 300 and 600 mg/kg of commercial LEV drug, and 30 and 60 mg/kg of LEV, respectively. The commercial LEV drug contained 10% of LEV as well as 90% of excipients including, glucose. These excipients may have an impact on the bioavailability of the drug by altering its absorption and permeability (Yau et al., 1987; Panakanti and Narang, 2012). The low-dose group in this study received the same dose as that used in the previously conducted PO PK experiment with a chemical standard. The PK parameters from both experiments were identical, without significant differences, suggesting no absorption alteration of LEV due to the excipients in the commercial LEV drug.

As expected, the present commercial LEV drug PO administration showed dose-proportional changes in dose-dependent PK factors, AUC and Cmax (Eisenblaetter et al., 2020). However, K10_HL and Vd/F, dose-independent PK indexes, also showed dose-proportional increment with statistical differences. The major primary PK factors are the drug absorption rate constant, CL/F, and Vd/F, which are not dose-proportional but are influenced by factors such as the formulation and physiological changes (Terry, 2019). The relationship between plasma binding affinity and saturation is not straightforward and proportional; however, in some cases, it has been reported that high plasma protein binding can lead to nonlinear pharmacokinetics due to saturation in plasma protein binding (Ding et al., 2015). In the present study, the plasma binding affinity of LEV was approximately 82%, evidently higher than those reported previously, which was ranging from 15% to 32%, including chickens, rabbits, sheep, cows, horses, and pigs (Sahagún et al., 1997). Apart from the plasma protein binding saturation, the increase in Vd/F with delayed drug elimination after a dose increase may be attributed to the dose-dependent increase in drug tissue binding (Keller et al, 1984; Kerns and Di, 2008). Further studies are needed to elucidate the exact reason for dose-dependent increase in Vd/F of LEV in laying hens.

Kolanović et al. suggest 17.2 d of withdrawal time for LEV in eggs after 25 mg/kg of PO LEV administration for 2 consecutive days under below the limit (0.025 mg/kg), which is 2.5 times higher than the PLS (0.01 mg/kg), and El-Kholy and Kemppainen report a 9-d withdrawal period for eggs after 40 mg/kg of single PO LEV under 0.1 mg/kg limit, which is 10 times higher than the PLS standard (El-Kholy and Kemppainen, 2005; Kolanović et al., 2018). Several factors may affect withdrawal period determination, including the type of analytical device, treatment dose, and MRL level designated. In this study, although the drug exposure dose proportionally increased after a single PO administration of 30 and 60 mg/kg, the withdrawal periods were unchanged by the present dose range under the PLS standard.

The oral route may be a robust clinical route of administration for LEV, as confirmed by its high relative oral bioavailability in laying hens. Furthermore, the excipients from the present commercial LEV drug did not affect LEV absorption, and the dose range from 30 to 60 mg/kg of LEV had a 7-d withdrawal period, comparable to that under the PLS standard. Further studies are warranted to analyze egg residue and assess the dose-proportionality of PK for different dosage regimens of LEV to ensure food safety against LEV egg residue-related issues.
