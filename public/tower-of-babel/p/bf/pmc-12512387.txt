Impact of the OSCE on anxiety levels and perceptions of dental students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Aurora Saiz Rodríguez, Ana Antoranz, Gema Cidoncha, Maria Jesús Pardo Monedero, Marta Muñoz-Corcuera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile objective structured clinical examination (OSCE) is a widely used method for assessing clinical competencies in dentistry, it can be a significant source of anxiety for students. Neither the extent to which dental students experience anxiety related to this exam nor the students’ perceptions of the test’s clinical value have been formally measured.ObjectiveThis study assessed post-OSCE anxiety in 5th-year dental students, explored gender differences, and evaluated perceptions of realism and educational value compared with prior clinical practice.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study of 5th -year dental students with a bachelor’s degree in dentistry at the European University of Madrid (2023–2024). After completion of the OSCE, each student anonymously completed the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), a validated questionnaire designed to measure their state of anxiety, and they reported their perception of the clinical relevance of the OSCE. The overall STAI scores and a comparison of male and female scores were recorded.ResultsA total of 229 dental students completed the OSCE, 200 of whom participated in our study. 82% of the students reported “medium-high” or “very high” anxiety levels. Males presented significantly higher median anxiety scores than females did. No relationship was found between age and anxiety level, regardless of sex. A total of 83.5% of the students felt that the stations were representative of real life and that their previous clinical experience with patients during their two years of clinical internships had prepared them well for the OSCE, enabling them to demonstrate their professional skills. 92% of the students passed the exam.ConclusionsOSCE induces moderate to high levels of anxiety in dental students, particularly among males. Despite this, students generally view the OSCE positively, considering it a useful, realistic, and formative tool.Keywords: OSCE, Dental education, Anxiety, Perceptions, Competence assessment

Background

Anxiety is an emotion characterized by feelings of worry, nervousness, and fear about the future or uncertain situations. It is a natural response to stress, but when excessive or persistent, it can affect an individual’s mental health and well-being [1]. According to the Yerkes–Dodson law, moderate levels of anxiety may enhance performance, whereas excessive anxiety can impair cognitive functioning and outcomes [2]. Similarly, Eysenck and Calvo’s Processing Efficiency Theory suggests that anxiety consumes cognitive resources, particularly attention, which can interfere with complex tasks such as those encountered in the Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) [3]. In conflict-affected countries such as Palestine, mental health challenges among university students have been widely documented. A cross-sectional study conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic reported high prevalence rates of anxiety and depression, influenced by various sociodemographic and academic factors [4]. Moreover, evidence indicates that targeted interventions, including cognitive behavioral therapy, can substantially reduce psychological distress among medical students during periods of heightened academic and environmental stress [3]. Given that the OSCE is a high-stakes examination that can intensify anxiety levels, these findings highlight the importance of recognizing and addressing both academic and contextual stressors to promote students’ psychological well-being.

The OSCE, introduced by Dr. Ronald M. Harden in 1970, is widely used in health sciences as a reliable and standardized method to assess clinical, communicative, and diagnostic competencies [5, 6]. Various instruments have been employed to measure anxiety during such evaluations, including physiological markers (e.g., salivary cortisol) and validated questionnaires [7–9]. Among these, the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) distinguishes between temporary (state) and enduring (trait) anxiety [10–12]. This distinction is crucial for determining whether anxiety is a situational response to the OSCE or a general predisposition. The reliability and several measures of validity of the STAI has previously been assessed. For reliability, the STAI has been found to have high internal consistency with Cronbach alpha coefficients ranging from 0.80 to 0.95, and acceptable test-retest reliability, ranging from 0.60 for up to two-month intervals to 0.90 for one-week intervals. Assessments of the content, construct, concurrent and discrimination validity of the STAI provide evidence of strong validity [13, 14].

The European University of Madrid (EUM) provides health sciences students with a simulated hospital for clinical training, accredited by the Society for Simulation in Healthcare (SSH). Many dental schools, including EUM, have incorporated the OSCE to assess clinical, communicative, and therapeutic judgment skills in simulated environments [15, 16]. Since 2017, to pass the “Supervised Practices” course in the 5th year, students must demonstrate proficiency in both clinical practice, as assessed by supervising clinical faculty members and the OSCE. Previous research indicates that OSCEs often generate more stress than traditional written exams, due to time constraints and continuous observation of student performance [15, 17].

By combining validated measures of anxiety with students’ perceptions, this study provides a comprehensive understanding of the impact of the OSCE on dental students, addressing both its psychological effects and its perceived relevance to clinical training.

A key distinction is made between performance effectiveness (the quality of the outcome) and processing efficiency (the cognitive cost of achieving that outcome), with anxiety more likely to impair the latter [18]. Although anxiety in OSCEs has been studied in medical and nursing education, evidence in dental education is still limited, particularly regarding student´s perceptions of realism and educational value. Few studies have explored the interaction between prior real-patient clinical training and student´s experiences with simulated OSCE stations.

In this context, our study provides empirical evidence on dental students’ experiences of anxiety during the OSCE and their perceptions of this assessment tool. Although this is a descriptive study, the findings may serve as a foundation for future research exploring the relationship between anxiety and academic performance through a more robust theoretical lens.

Methods

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the European University of Madrid on March 19, 2024 (internal code CIPI 2024–619). The research was conducted via a questionnaire administered to 5th-year students with a bachelor’s degree in dentistry at the EUM during the 2023–2024 academic year after they had completed the OSCE. Participation was voluntary and anonymous.

Prior to the administration of the OSCE, all 5th-year dental students were invited to participate in the study during a scheduled briefing session held in a classroom within the simulated hospital facility. During this session, students received detailed instructions regarding the structure and execution of the OSCE, including the number of stations, the order in which they would be completed, and the time allocated for each station.

Students who agreed to participate in the study were asked to sign an informed consent form. This consent was obtained in the same classroom, immediately after the OSCE had been completed and prior to the administration of the questionnaire.

A convenience sampling method was employed. The final sample consisted of those who chose to participate and met the inclusion criteria.

The OSCE consisted of five stations, four of which involved simulated patients. Each station had an allotted time of 8 min, resulting in a total exam time of 40 min. The students had completed two years of clinical practice with real patients before taking the OSCE. It is important to note that all participating students had previously completed other OSCE during their 4th year of dental education, providing them with prior experience with this assessment format.

Each station was designed to simulate a distinct clinical scenario. These included: [1] a patient presenting with acute necrotizing gingivitis (NG); [2] a case involving a molar with non-restorable root caries; [3] an intraoral lesion consistent with epulis fissuratum caused by a prosthesis; [4] a patient missing the lower left second premolar; and [5] the interpretation of a panoramic radiograph.

Students were required to interact with simulated patients, where they were expected to establish a diagnosis and propose a treatment plan for each case. In the radiographic station, students were asked to identify the type of dentition (primary, mixed, or permanent), estimate the patient’s age, diagnose radiolucent lesions and dental pathologies, recognize previous treatments, assess dental positioning anomalies, and suggest appropriate treatment plans.

Each student completed the OSCE individually, without collaboration. Evaluation was conducted by faculty members located in adjacent rooms separated by one-way mirrors. This setup allowed evaluators to observe and listen to students without being seen or heard, thereby minimizing the anxiety associated with direct in-room observation.

After completing the OSCE, the students were given a questionnaire consisting of two sections. The first part collected demographic information, such as gender and age, along with four multiple-choice questions and an open-ended question to gather students’ perceptions of the OSCE (Table 1). These open-ended responses were descriptively reviewed to identify recurring themes or notable remarks that could enrich the quantitative findings. Due to the exploratory nature of the study and the limited qualitative input, no formal analysis method was employed.

The second part of the questionnaire included the State Anxiety section of STAI, which assesses anxiety using a 4-point Likert scale: 0 = not at all, 1 = somewhat, 2 = moderately so, and 3 = very much. Positively worded items were scored directly, while negatively worded items were reverse-scored. The positively worded items included questions 3, 4, 6, 7, 9, 12, 13, 14, 17, and 18; all remaining items were negatively worded and scored accordingly.

The STAI was administered in Spanish using the validated translation developed by Charles D. Spielberger and colleagues, in collaboration with psychologists from ten Latin American countries. This Spanish version has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, including high internal consistency and equivalence with the original English version, and is widely used in both clinical and academic settings. Results

Among the 229 students who took the OSCE, 200 (87.3%) participated. The group included 114 females (57%) and 86 males (43%), with a median age of 24 years (IQR = 22–51).

92% of the students reported that their previous clinical practice had helped them perform the OSCE without difficulty. The majority felt that the simulated patient stations were representative of real-life scenarios and that the OSCE allowed them to better reflect their professional skills. The anxiety-state subscale did not follow a normal distribution. The first quartile was 23, the third quartile was 33, and the median was 28, indicating a medium-high level of anxiety. The distribution of anxiety levels was as follows: very low (2.94%), medium-low (5.29%), medium (10%), medium-high (45.88%), and very high (35.88%). In total, 82% of the students experienced medium–high or very high anxiety.

With respect to gender, 51.55% of the females reported medium to high anxiety, whereas 20.62% reported very high anxiety. In contrast, more than half of the male students (56.16%) reported very high anxiety, with 38.36% reporting medium–high anxiety. The median anxiety score for males was 31, whereas for females, it was 27, indicating statistically significant differences between the two groups (p = 0.0103).

Age was not correlated with anxiety levels, although students aged 26–30 years presented the highest percentages of very high and medium‒high anxiety levels. The mean age was slightly greater for students reporting medium or very low anxiety levels (Table 5).

Discussion

The introduction of the OSCE in the “Supervised Practices” course at the EUM aims to provide a more comprehensive assessment of students’ clinical competencies. This aligns with previous research highlighting the positive impact of the OSCE on assessment and student learning [20, 21]. By integrating theoretical and practical knowledge in a time-constrained environment, the OSCE fosters critical thinking and decision-making [20, 22].

At EUM, the OSCE was designed to include simulated patient stations to assess communication skills and patient management. Students rated this format as the most realistic and useful, preferring it over other assessment methods [23–27]. However, some students noted the limitations of using simulated patients, such as the inability to perform physical examinations, which affected the credibility of certain scenarios [22]. These perceptions are echoed in other studies. A recent mixed-methods study revealed that while dental students appreciated the OSCE as a tool for developing clinical competencies, many expressed concerns about its clinical relevance and the stress it induced. Only 36.7% of students reported receiving adequate feedback, and 50% questioned the realism of OSCE scenarios [28].

This study revealed that a substantial majority of 5th-year dental students experienced moderate to high levels of anxiety after taking the OSCE, with 82% reporting “medium–high” or “very high” anxiety levels. Males presented significantly higher anxiety scores than females did. This is consistent with studies from other institutions [15, 29–31], although findings regarding gender differences are inconsistent [32–36]. The elevated anxiety levels observed among dental students in this study are consistent with previous findings from other Palestinian university populations. For example, during the COVID-19 pandemic, high rates of anxiety and depression were documented among students, driven by both academic pressures and socio-environmental challenges [4]. More recently, during the war that began on October 7, 2023, university students in Palestine reported significant psychological distress, with resilience identified as a protective factor [37]. The alignment of our results with studies conducted in different stress contexts underscores the cumulative impact of academic demands and broader external stressors on student mental health, particularly in conflict-affected regions.

Although age was not significantly correlated with anxiety levels, younger students (22–25 years) presented higher levels of anxiety than older students did. This may be due to the narrow age range of participants, most of whom were in their early to mid-twenties. Older students may have developed better coping mechanisms or clinical confidence, which could mitigate anxiety. Additionally, male students experienced more anxiety as they aged, whereas female students experienced lower levels of anxiety in older age groups. These findings are consistent with previous research. In a Taiwanese study examining dental students during their first OSCE, researchers found no significant association between age and anxiety levels or between anxiety and noncognitive performance [30].

More than 80% of the students believed that the assessment stations were representative of real-life clinical situations, and indicated that they found the OSCE to be a useful and realistic tool for assessing their clinical competencies. Overall, 92% of the participating students successfully passed the exam. These findings reflect the structure of dental education in Spain, where students undergo extensive clinical training in their final years. The presence of simulated hospital facilities, may enhance the perceived realism of OSCE stations. Comparative studies across institutions could help explore regional differences in anxiety and perception.

The study has several strengths, including a large sample size of 200 participants, which enhances the validity of the findings. It utilized the validated STAI to measure anxiety levels accurately and examines gender differences in anxiety responses, providing insightful demographic comparisons. The combination of quantitative data and qualitative feedback allowed for a comprehensive understanding of students’ experiences. Moreover, the study assessed a real-world examination, ensuring that the findings are relevant to dental education. Notably, despite the high anxiety levels reported, the majority of the students perceived the OSCE positively, and saw it as a beneficial component of the curriculum.

Limitations

One weakness of the study is that it does not provide students’ performance scores on the OSCE, leaving uncertainty about whether the reported anxiety levels were beneficial or detrimental to their performance. We are currently working on future studies to correlate students’ OSCE performance scores and pass/fail rates with their levels of anxiety, aiming to gain a better understanding of the relationship between anxiety and academic outcomes.

Another limitation is the absence of a baseline STAI pre-test administered to the same group of students prior to the OSCE. Without this measure, it is not possible to determine whether the moderate to high levels of anxiety observed are uniquely attributable to the OSCE experience itself, or whether they reflect a more generalized level of anxiety that dental students carry throughout their training. To address this limitation, we are conducting follow-up studies that will include longitudinal pre- and post-OSCE assessments, in order to better isolate the specific impact of the OSCE on student anxiety.

Finally, the age range of participants was relatively narrow, which may have affected the ability to detect associations between age and anxiety.

Conclusions

The OSCE is considered a valid, realistic, and useful assessment tool by dental students. Although it induces moderate to high levels of anxiety—especially among males—students generally perceive it as a positive and formative experience.

Based on these findings, we recommend that institutions provide preparatory sessions and stress management resources prior to the OSCE to help students cope with anxiety. Additionally, incorporating feedback mechanisms and enhanced simulated patient training may improve the perceived realism and educational value of the OSCE.

While no significant relationship was found between age and anxiety, future studies should explore this further using broader age distributions and longitudinal designs.
