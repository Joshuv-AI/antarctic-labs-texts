Patient- and implant-level factors associated with peri-implant diseases: a retrospective clinical and radiographic study of 968 implants
Authors: Ulfat Mashadiyev, İzzet Melih Gürkan, Hasan Gündoğar, Hakan Özdemir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeri-implant diseases are among the most clinically significant biological complications of implant therapy, yet the relative contribution of patient- and implant-related factors to their development remains incompletely understood. This retrospective study examined the associations between demographic, clinical, and implant-related variables and peri-implant disease status in a large clinical cohort.MethodsA total of 199 patients with 968 dental implants placed between January 2015 and January 2023 were evaluated. Variables recorded included demographic characteristics, systemic conditions, smoking status, oral hygiene habits, implant-related parameters, clinical indices (plaque index, gingival index, probing depth, attached gingiva width, and gingival thickness), and radiographic marginal bone loss. Peri-implant disease status — classified as peri-implant health, peri-implant mucositis, or peri-implantitis according to the 2017 World Workshop criteria — was the primary outcome. Multivariable generalized estimating equations (GEE) models were applied to account for the clustering of multiple implants per patient.ResultsOf the 968 implants, 713 (73.6%) were classified as peri-implant health, 173 (17.9%) as peri-implant mucositis, and 82 (8.5%) as peri-implantitis. In multivariable GEE analysis, smoking ≥ 10 cigarettes/day (OR = 2.55, 95% CI: 1.07–6.10 for mucositis; OR = 4.40, 95% CI: 1.60–12.08 for peri-implantitis), inadequate tooth brushing (OR = 4.65, 95% CI: 1.99–10.86; OR = 5.90, 95% CI: 2.21–15.77), and thin gingival phenotype (OR = 4.22, 95% CI: 2.09–8.51; OR = 15.64, 95% CI: 6.08–40.23) were independently associated with both conditions. Longer implant functional duration was additionally associated with peri-implantitis (OR = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.30–1.90; p < 0.001).ConclusionWithin the limitations of this retrospective study, smoking, inadequate oral hygiene, thin gingival phenotype, and longer implant functional duration were independently associated with peri-implant diseases. These findings suggest that peri-implant soft tissue assessment and patient-level risk assessment should be integral components of long-term implant maintenance protocols.Keywords: Dental implants, Peri-implant diseases, Peri-implantitis, Risk factors, Gingival phenotype, Smoking, Oral hygiene

Background

Dental implants have become one of the most widely used approaches for the restoration of missing teeth, supported by decades of clinical evidence demonstrating high long-term survival rates in both partially and fully edentulous patients [1, 2]. The phenomenon of osseointegration — the direct, load-bearing union between living bone and the implant surface — provided the biological rationale for implant-based rehabilitation and remains central to its clinical success [3]. Nevertheless, despite ongoing refinements in implant design, surface technology, surgical techniques, and maintenance protocols, biological complications involving the peri-implant tissues continue to present a significant clinical challenge and may compromise the long-term outcome of implant therapy [4, 5].

Among the most clinically relevant of these complications are peri-implant diseases — inflammatory conditions that develop in the soft and hard tissues surrounding osseointegrated implants [5, 6]. The 2017 World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases and Conditions established a widely adopted framework distinguishing two entities: peri-implant mucositis, defined by reversible soft tissue inflammation in the absence of radiographic bone loss, and peri-implantitis, characterised by concurrent soft tissue inflammation and progressive destruction of the supporting alveolar bone [6]. Reported prevalence figures for these conditions differ considerably across the literature, a variability attributable to heterogeneity in diagnostic case definitions, patient populations, implant systems evaluated, duration of follow-up, and the frequency of supportive maintenance provided [7, 8].

The development of peri-implant diseases is recognised as a multifactorial process, arising from interactions among microbial challenge, host immune responses, local tissue characteristics, and patient-level modifying factors [5, 6]. Several risk indicators have been identified in the literature, including insufficient plaque removal, smoking, a prior history of periodontitis, systemic conditions such as diabetes mellitus, and infrequent or absent supportive maintenance therapy [6, 9, 10]. However, the precise contribution of individual factors and the extent to which they interact with one another remain areas of active investigation, particularly given the wide variation in study designs and diagnostic criteria used across published studies.

Beyond systemic and behavioural risk factors, the morphological characteristics of the peri-implant soft tissues have attracted growing research interest in recent years. Sufficient keratinized mucosa and adequate gingival dimensions are thought to facilitate effective oral hygiene, improve patient comfort during maintenance procedures, and potentially contribute to the long-term stability of peri-implant tissues [9, 10]. Previous studies have examined the influence of keratinized mucosa width, attached gingiva dimensions, and gingival phenotype — including mucosal thickness — on peri-implant tissue health [11–13]. Findings, however, remain inconsistent, and whether these soft tissue parameters independently predict peri-implant disease susceptibility or primarily reflect confounding patient-level factors is not yet established.

Although a substantial body of literature has examined individual risk indicators for peri-implant diseases, fewer studies have simultaneously assessed both patient-level and implant-level variables within sufficiently large clinical cohorts. A further methodological consideration is that implant-based analyses frequently overlook the nested structure of the data: when multiple implants are placed in the same patient, measurements from those implants cannot be treated as statistically independent observations. Failure to account for this within-patient clustering can lead to underestimated standard errors and inflated significance, potentially producing misleading associations. Statistical frameworks designed to handle correlated data — such as generalized estimating equations (GEE) — are therefore preferable for this type of analysis [14].

Accordingly, the novelty of the present study lies not in identifying previously unknown risk factors, but in comprehensively evaluating established patient- and implant-level variables within a single multivariable analytical framework while appropriately accounting for the clustering of multiple implants within the same patient. This approach provides more robust estimates of the independent associations between these variables and peri-implant disease status in a large clinical cohort. Against this background, the present retrospective clinical and radiographic study aimed to evaluate the associations between patient- and implant-level characteristics and peri-implant disease status in a cohort of 968 dental implants from 199 patients. Factors of interest included demographic variables, systemic health status, smoking behaviour, oral hygiene practices, implant-related parameters, and peri-implant soft tissue characteristics — with GEE regression applied to account for the clustering of implants within individual patients.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Eskişehir Osmangazi University, Eskişehir, Turkey (Approval No: E-25403353-050.99-2300166164). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion in the study.

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective analysis included 199 patients with a combined total of 968 dental implants placed at a single university-based periodontology department between January 2015 and January 2023. Patients were eligible if they were aged 18 years or older, had implants in active function, and had complete clinical and radiographic records available for review. Cases were excluded when clinical documentation was insufficient, when available radiographs did not permit reliable peri-implant bone level assessment, or when the data required to determine peri-implant disease status were missing.

All relevant patient and implant data — including demographic information, systemic health history, smoking behaviour, oral hygiene habits, clinical examination findings, soft tissue measurements, and radiographic outcomes — were systematically extracted from patient records and entered into a dedicated database. All clinical and radiographic assessments were carried out by a single calibrated examiner (U.M.), and intra-examiner reproducibility was confirmed prior to data collection.

Clinical assessment

Patient-level variables included age, sex, systemic health conditions, smoking status, and self-reported oral hygiene practices. At the implant level, the following clinical parameters were recorded: plaque accumulation using the plaque index (PLI) [11], soft tissue inflammation using the gingival index (GI) [12], probing depth (PD), attached gingiva width (AGW), and gingival thickness.

All clinical and radiographic assessments were performed by a single calibrated examiner (U.M.). Prior to the main data collection, intra-examiner reproducibility was evaluated using a randomly selected subset of implants. Clinical measurements were repeated after a two-week interval under identical examination conditions. Agreement between repeated measurements was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) for continuous variables and Cohen’s kappa statistic for the categorical variable. The ICC values for keratinized mucosa width and attached gingiva width were 0.988 and 0.774, respectively, while Cohen’s kappa value for gingival phenotype classification was 0.703, indicating good to excellent intra-examiner reproducibility.

Probing was performed at six sites per implant (mesiobuccal, midbuccal, distobuccal, mesiolingual, midlingual, and distolingual) using a Williams periodontal probe. AGW was derived by subtracting the PD from the total keratinized mucosa width measured at the same sites. Based on published thresholds, AGW was dichotomised as adequate (≥ 2 mm) or inadequate (< 2 mm) [13, 15]. Gingival phenotype was classified as thick or thin based on visual assessment and probe transparency through the marginal tissue, in keeping with previously described criteria [7, 16]. Smoking exposure was categorised as lighter (< 10 cigarettes/day) or heavier (≥ 10 cigarettes/day), with the latter group defined by sustained use for at least five years.

Implant-level data retrieved from patient records included jaw location, anterior or posterior position, opposing dentition type, and duration of implant function.

Radiographic evaluation

Panoramic radiographs were obtained under standardised conditions using a Planmeca Promax 2D unit (Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland; 68 kVp, 16 mA, 16 s exposure). Bone level measurements were made digitally using the linear measurement tool within the PACS software system (Synapse Mobility, Fujifilm Medical Systems, USA).

For each implant, the peri-implant marginal bone level was recorded as the linear distance between the implant shoulder and the most coronal point of bone-to-implant contact, measured on the mesial and distal aspects. Marginal bone loss was evaluated as the radiographic difference between baseline and the most recent follow-up examination. Because the study was retrospective and cross-sectional, these measurements were analyzed as cross-sectional outcomes rather than time-to-event variables [17]. Marginal bone loss was not annualized, and differences in follow-up duration were instead addressed by including implant functional duration as an independent variable in the multivariable GEE model.

Definition of peri-implant disease groups

Each implant was categorised into one of three groups according to the 2017 World Workshop diagnostic framework [3]: peri-implant health, peri-implant mucositis, or peri-implantitis.

Peri-implant health was defined as the absence of clinical signs of inflammation (bleeding and/or suppuration on probing) and the absence of radiographic bone loss beyond initial physiologic bone remodeling [3]. Peri-implant mucositis was defined as bleeding on probing (BoP), with or without increased probing depth, in the absence of radiographic bone loss beyond initial physiologic bone remodeling [3]. Gingival Index (GI) scores were recorded separately as clinical variables and were used only for descriptive and analytical purposes, not for disease classification. Peri-implantitis was diagnosed according to the 2017 World Workshop classification. In the absence of previous examination records, peri-implantitis was defined by bleeding and/or suppuration on probing, probing depth ≥ 6 mm, and radiographic marginal bone loss ≥ 3 mm relative to the post-surgical baseline, consistent with the epidemiological case definition recommended by the 2017 World Workshop [3].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data distribution was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Normally distributed continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation; non-normally distributed variables as median (minimum–maximum). Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages.

Between-group differences across the three peri-implant disease categories were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, with post hoc pairwise testing by Dunn’s method. Categorical data were compared using the Pearson chi-square test or the Fisher–Freeman–Halton test, as appropriate for cell counts.

Because patients contributed varying numbers of implants, measurements from implants belonging to the same patient are not statistically independent. To address this clustering, multivariable generalized estimating equations (GEE) regression was used as the primary inferential approach. Candidate variables were selected based on potential associations observed in univariate screening and on clinical relevance. Results are expressed as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05. Because the exact timing of peri-implant disease onset was not available in this retrospective cross-sectional study, survival analyses such as Cox regression were not applicable. Therefore, GEE was considered the most appropriate analytical approach for evaluating cross-sectional associations.

Results

Patient and implant characteristics

The study cohort comprised 199 patients (111 male, 55.8%; 88 female, 44.2%) with a total of 968 dental implants. The mean patient age was 56.06 ± 9.45 years.

Among the patient-level characteristics, 16.1% reported smoking ≥ 10 cigarettes/day. The majority (81.9%) brushed at least once daily, while 31.7% reported regular interdental brush use. A systemic condition was recorded in 35.2% of patients; the most frequent were hypertension, diabetes mellitus, thyroid disorders, hypercholesterolemia, and osteoporosis.

At the implant level, 79.1% of implants were in the posterior region and 20.9% in the anterior region. In terms of peri-implant soft tissue characteristics, a thin gingival phenotype was observed in 40.3% of implants and a thick phenotype in the remaining 59.7% (Table 1).

Disease status distribution differed significantly by smoking category and oral hygiene habits (p < 0.05). Following adjustment, smoking intensity, tooth brushing frequency, and thin gingival phenotype were independently associated with both peri-implant mucositis and peri-implantitis. Implant functional duration showed a borderline association with peri-implant mucositis but was independently associated with peri-implantitis (Table 4).

For peri-implant mucositis, heavier smoking (≥ 10 cigarettes/day) was associated with more than twice the odds of disease (OR = 2.55, 95% CI: 1.07–6.10; p = 0.035). Inadequate tooth brushing frequency (OR = 4.65, 95% CI: 1.99–10.86; p < 0.001) and thin gingival phenotype (OR = 4.22, 95% CI: 2.09–8.51; p < 0.001) were also independently associated with peri-implant mucositis after full adjustment.

For peri-implantitis, effect sizes were consistently greater. Heavier smoking (OR = 4.40, 95% CI: 1.60–12.08; p = 0.004), inadequate brushing (OR = 5.90, 95% CI: 2.21–15.77; p < 0.001), and thin gingival phenotype (OR = 15.64, 95% CI: 6.08–40.23; p < 0.001) were each independently associated with peri-implantitis. Longer implant functional duration was additionally and independently associated with peri-implantitis (OR = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.30–1.90; p < 0.001), while age and systemic conditions did not reach significance in the adjusted model.

Discussion

This retrospective study examined a range of patient- and implant-level characteristics in relation to peri-implant tissue health in a cohort of 968 implants. Four variables — smoking intensity, oral hygiene adequacy, gingival phenotype, and implant functional duration — were independently associated with peri-implant disease status after adjustment for within-patient clustering using GEE regression. Taken together, the findings are consistent with a multifactorial disease model in which behavioural, biological, and local tissue factors interact to determine peri-implant outcomes [3, 5]. Rather than identifying previously unknown risk factors, the principal contribution of the present study lies in the simultaneous evaluation of established patient- and implant-level variables within a single multivariable GEE framework that appropriately accounts for clustering of multiple implants within individual patients.

The rates of peri-implant mucositis (17.9%) and peri-implantitis (8.5%) observed in the present cohort are broadly consistent with figures reported in large-scale epidemiological analyses of peri-implant diseases [4, 17]. Variability in published prevalence estimates is well recognised and largely reflects differences in case definitions, patient selection, follow-up duration, and the frequency with which maintenance is provided [7, 8]. The use of the 2017 World Workshop diagnostic framework in the current study supports comparability with more recent literature and allows interpretation of the findings within a standardised clinical context [3].

Smoking was independently associated with both peri-implant mucositis and peri-implantitis in the present study, with heavier smokers (≥ 10 cigarettes/day) showing markedly elevated odds of disease in the adjusted GEE model. This is in keeping with a substantial body of evidence linking tobacco use to adverse peri-implant outcomes [5, 7]. Several biological mechanisms have been proposed to explain this relationship: nicotine and its metabolites impair local vascular perfusion, suppress neutrophil and macrophage activity, and blunt the early inflammatory response in ways that may allow bacterial biofilm to establish and progress with less tissue resistance [5, 7]. At the same time, smoking is also associated with poorer oral hygiene practices in some populations, making it difficult to isolate its independent biological effect entirely. Nevertheless, the persistence of the association after adjusting for oral hygiene in the present model suggests a contribution that extends beyond behaviour alone.

The strong association between inadequate tooth brushing frequency and both disease outcomes — with odds ratios of 4.65 for mucositis and 5.90 for peri-implantitis — underscores the central role of biofilm control in peri-implant tissue health. This is consistent with longitudinal data showing that patients who receive structured supportive maintenance programmes have substantially lower rates of peri-implant disease progression compared with those who do not [8]. In practical terms, the present data reinforce the importance of reinforcing oral hygiene instructions at every maintenance visit and tailoring recall intervals to individual risk profiles rather than applying a one-size-fits-all approach.

One notable finding of this study was the large effect size associated with a thin gingival phenotype in the peri-implantitis model (OR = 15.64). While the direction of this association is supported by several previous investigations linking reduced mucosal thickness to peri-implant tissue vulnerability [9, 10, 18], the magnitude observed here is larger than typically reported, which may partly reflect characteristics of the specific cohort or the classification thresholds used. A thin mucosal cuff may offer less physical resistance to apical migration of the inflammatory infiltrate and may be associated with greater difficulty in maintaining effective plaque control, particularly in patients with limited manual dexterity or suboptimal technique [9, 13]. Whether correcting gingival thickness through augmentation procedures would reduce disease risk in susceptible patients remains an open question that prospective intervention studies would need to address [10]. Although the association between thin gingival phenotype and peri-implantitis was strong, the relatively wide confidence interval indicates limited precision of the estimate, likely reflecting the relatively small number of peri-implantitis cases. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted with appropriate caution. The present cross-sectional findings identify associations rather than causal relationships and therefore should not be interpreted as evidence that gingival augmentation procedures or intensified maintenance protocols reduce peri-implant disease risk. Confirmation of the effectiveness of such interventions requires well-designed prospective longitudinal and interventional studies.

Longer implant functional duration was independently associated with peri-implantitis but not with mucositis in the adjusted model. This finding is consistent with long-term follow-up studies in which the prevalence of biological complications tends to accumulate over time [4, 19]. It is important to note, however, that duration of function is not itself a modifiable risk factor; rather, it reflects the cumulative exposure to all other risk-modifying variables across the lifespan of the implant. Patients with longer-functioning implants who also carry other risk indicators — smoking, inadequate hygiene, thin mucosa — may represent a particularly vulnerable group in whom intensified monitoring would be warranted [8]. Because the exact timing of peri-implant disease onset could not be determined retrospectively, implant functional duration was interpreted as cumulative exposure time rather than as a time-to-event variable. Consequently, the observed association should be interpreted within the limitations of the study design.

Systemic conditions, including diabetes and hypertension, were not independently associated with peri-implant disease status in the multivariable model. This finding warrants cautious interpretation. The heterogeneity of systemic diagnoses within the study population, combined with the absence of data on disease severity, glycaemic control, and concurrent medication, limits the conclusions that can be drawn. It is plausible that any systemic effect was attenuated by these uncontrolled variables, or that the study was insufficiently powered to detect modest associations at the disease-group level. The existing literature on systemic conditions and peri-implant disease remains mixed, and the present results do not permit firm conclusions either for or against a systemic contribution [5, 6].

Although the present study identified several independent factors associated with peri-implant diseases, additional clinically relevant variables that could not be evaluated should also be considered when interpreting these findings. Previous studies have shown that a history of periodontitis, individual susceptibility to periodontal disease, residual periodontal pockets greater than 5 mm, and the ratio of bone loss in relation to age are important determinants of peri-implant disease development [20]. In addition, the distance between the restoration margin and the crestal bone, prosthetic design characteristics, and adherence to supportive peri-implant maintenance therapy have also been reported to substantially influence the development and progression of peri-implant diseases [21, 22]. Because these variables were not consistently available in our retrospective database, they could not be incorporated into the present analyses. Nevertheless, these variables should be regarded as potential unmeasured confounders and considered when interpreting the present findings as well as when designing future prospective studies.

Another limitation relates to the assessment of implant functional duration. Although all implants were placed in our institution and the duration in function was available, peri-implant diseases were assessed only at a single cross-sectional examination, and the exact time of disease onset was unknown. Consequently, the data structure did not permit a valid time-to-event analysis using methods such as Kaplan–Meier or Cox regression. Prospective longitudinal studies with repeated follow-up assessments are needed to better evaluate the temporal relationship between implant function time and peri-implant disease development.

The principal methodological strength of this study is the application of GEE regression, which appropriately accounts for the non-independence of multiple implants within the same patient — a consideration that is often overlooked in implant-based research and that, when ignored, can lead to underestimated standard errors and spuriously significant findings [3, 4]. The relatively large sample of 968 implants from 199 patients also provided reasonable statistical power to detect associations across multiple predictor variables simultaneously. Accordingly, the present findings contribute to the existing literature by providing more robust adjusted estimates of well-established patient- and implant-level risk indicators while minimizing bias associated with correlated implant observations, thereby strengthening the available evidence regarding factors associated with peri-implant diseases. That said, the findings should be interpreted within the constraints described in the Limitations section.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be taken into account when interpreting the results. The retrospective design precludes causal inference; while the associations identified are plausible and consistent with the existing literature, directionality cannot be established from cross-sectional observations drawn from historical records. Unmeasured confounders — including periodontal history, implant surface characteristics, prosthetic design, loading protocols, and medication use — could not be fully accounted for and may have influenced the observed associations.

Radiographic bone level measurements were performed on panoramic radiographs rather than periapical films or cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT). Although standardised acquisition settings were used and measurements were calibrated against known implant dimensions, panoramic imaging carries inherent limitations in spatial resolution and geometric accuracy that may introduce measurement error, particularly for smaller bone level changes. The reliance on a single examiner, while ensuring consistency, also means that inter-examiner reliability cannot be formally assessed.

The classification of gingival phenotype was based on visual inspection and probe transparency, which, while clinically practical, is an inherently subjective approach. More objective methods — such as transgingival probing or ultrasonographic measurement — would provide greater precision in future studies. Finally, the single-centre setting and the characteristics of the study population may limit generalisability to broader clinical contexts with different implant systems, patient demographics, or maintenance protocols.

Furthermore, because the exact timing of peri-implant disease onset was unavailable, time-to-event analyses such as Cox regression could not be performed. Consequently, implant functional duration was interpreted as cumulative exposure time rather than as a temporal predictor of disease development. Future prospective longitudinal studies with standardized follow-up intervals and accurately recorded dates of peri-implant disease onset are warranted to evaluate peri-implant disease progression using survival analyses, including Kaplan–Meier or cumulative-incidence approaches where appropriate.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this retrospective study, smoking, inadequate oral hygiene, thin gingival phenotype, and longer implant functional duration were each independently associated with peri-implant disease. The magnitude of the associations — particularly for gingival phenotype and oral hygiene — suggests that soft tissue evaluation and behavioural risk factor assessment should be considered routine components of pre-implant planning and long-term maintenance. These findings should be interpreted as exploratory associations rather than established causal pathways; prospective multicentre studies with standardised protocols, longer follow-up, and detailed characterisation of soft tissue and maintenance variables are needed to confirm and extend the present observations.
