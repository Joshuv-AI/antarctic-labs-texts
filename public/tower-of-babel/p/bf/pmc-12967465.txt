Diabetic Striatopathy in an Elderly Female With Poorly Controlled Type 2 Diabetes: A Case Report
Authors: Ujjwal Kumar Shah, Prabesh Panta, Alok Kumar Jha, Shekhar Jaiswal, Suman Aryal, Prabind Gupta, Shreya Kakshapaty, Chandrashekhar Deuba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiabetic striatopathy (DS) is a rare complication of uncontrolled diabetes mellitus (DM), primarily affecting elderly patients and characterized by choreiform movements with basal ganglia imaging abnormalities. It has a good prognosis and completely resolves with optimal glucose control meeting individualized glycemic goals with a basal‐bolus insulin regimen and sometimes adjunctive anti‐chorea medications.

Introduction

Diabetic striatopathy (DS) is a rare neurological complication of diabetes mellitus (DM), frequently described in the literature using terms such as “hyperglycemic non‐ketotic hemichorea/hemiballism,” “chorea/hemichorea associated with non‐ketotic hyperglycemia,” “diabetic hemiballism‐hemichorea,” and “chorea, hyperglycemia, basal ganglia syndrome.” Its estimated prevalence is approximately 1 in 100,000, with most reported cases from Asia and predominantly affecting elderly females with type 2 DM [1]. DS typically presents during a hyperglycemic state with (a) unilateral or bilateral chorea/ballism, and/or (b) neuroimaging findings of striatal hyperdensity on non‐contrast CT (NCCT) or T1‐weighted hyperintensity on MRI. Optimal glycemic control usually reverses both clinical and radiological findings, though some patients may also require anti‐chorea drugs [1].

We present the case of an elderly woman with poorly controlled type 2 DM presenting with unilateral choreiform movements, who improved with adequate blood glucose control and tetrabenazine therapy.

Case History/Examination

An 83‐year‐old woman presented with a three‐day history of involuntary movements, initially involving the left side of the face and later extending to the left upper and lower limbs. Movements were irregular, purposeless, and more prominent distally. It worsened with voluntary action and disappeared during sleep. There was no history of hemiparesis, slurring of speech, abnormal body movements, loss of consciousness, facial deviation, abnormal posturing, behavioral changes, headache, fever, or vomiting.

Medical history included 10 years of type 2 DM, hypertension, chronic kidney disease (CKD stage G4, eGFR 21.7 mL/min/1.73 m2), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) with cor pulmonale, hypothyroidism, and anemia. She had no family history of movement disorders or seizures. There was no history of intake of drugs causing chorea.

On examination, she was hemodynamically stable and alert (GCS 14/15, E4V4M6). The milkmaid grip sign was positive. Power on the right side was normal, while correct assessment on the left was difficult due to excessive choreiform activity. Higher mental function, cranial nerve, and sensory examinations were normal. Other systemic examinations were unremarkable.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Laboratory investigations revealed a random blood glucose level of 327 mg/dL, HbA1c of 14.8%, glycosuria and albuminuria, but no ketonuria or blood ketones. ABG and serum electrolytes were normal. Serum creatinine was 2.2 mg/dL. Peripheral blood smear, serum electrolytes, liver and thyroid function tests, and vitamin B12 levels were within normal limits.

NCCT of the brain showed hyper‐attenuation in the right basal ganglia involving the caudate and lentiform nuclei. MRI revealed corresponding T1‐weighted hyperintensity and T2/FLAIR hypo‐intensity in the same regions, without surrounding edema or hemorrhage (Figure 1). Autoimmune markers such as ANA were not tested, limiting the exclusion of lupus‐related chorea. Other differentials were ruled out (Table 1).

A diagnosis of DS was made. She was managed with a basal‐bolus insulin regimen consisting of long‐acting insulin glargine 10 units once in the morning and rapid‐acting insulin aspart 4 units pre‐meal, titrated upward by 2–4 units based on daily fasting and postprandial glucose monitoring. Tetrabenazine 25 mg daily was also added for symptomatic control of chorea, consistent with recommended starting doses in the literature [2, 3].

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient showed remarkable improvement following strict glycemic control between 90 and 150 mg/dL before meals which was set based on her individualized glycemic goal. Her involuntary movements completely resolved within 24 h of initiating insulin therapy and tetrabenazine. She was monitored in the ICU for an additional 24 h to ensure neurological stability and subsequently transferred to the medicine ward. After a total hospital stay of 4 days, she was discharged on a basal‐bolus insulin regimen with appropriately adjusted oral hypoglycemic agents and continued tetrabenazine therapy with target fasting blood glucose set at 90–150 mg/dL. Comprehensive counseling was provided regarding strict glycemic control, adherence to medications, and recognition of early warning signs of hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia.

She was advised follow‐up every 2 weeks with capillary glucose testing before lunch and dinner. At her first follow‐up, she remained asymptomatic with no recurrence of choreiform movements. Her one‐week average blood glucose levels were 98–110 mg/dL before lunch and 108–126 mg/dL before dinner. Basal‐bolus insulin was continued (10 units of insulin glargine once in the morning and 4 units of insulin aspart before each meal). Her individualized glycemic goals of fasting blood glucose 90–150 mg/dL and HbA1C less than 8.0% were determined considering her age and comorbidities, in line with ADA Standards of Care 2025 [4].

Discussion

This report describes a rare case of hemichorea in an elderly patient with type 2 DM. Chorea refers to brief, irregular, involuntary movements resulting from continuous, random muscle contractions typically associated with lesions in the basal ganglia, which can be either unilateral or bilateral; the former is referred to as hemichorea. It may result from genetic, infectious, autoimmune, structural basal ganglia lesions such as stroke, neoplasms, drug‐induced, or metabolic causes [5, 6]. In a review of 176 patients with DS, 96.6% had type 2 DM, while only 3.4% had type 1 DM. The average blood glucose and HbA1c levels were 414 mg/dL and 13.1%, respectively [1]. DS rarely occurs in new‐onset DM or in young people with type 1 DM [1, 6, 7, 8]. Our patient, being an elderly female with uncontrolled T2DM and an HbA1c of 14.8%, fits the typical demographic and metabolic profile seen in DS.

Clinically, DS often presents with unilateral chorea or hemichorea, most frequently involving arm‐leg or arm‐leg‐face combinations. Left‐sided involvement is slightly more prevalent, with a left‐to‐right ratio of 1.2:1 [1], aligning with our patient's presentation. Bilateral involvement is rare (< 10%) [1]. Chorea involving bulbar muscles (e.g., dysarthria, dysphagia) has also been documented [9].

The pathogenesis remains unclear. One theory suggests that non‐ketotic hyperglycemia shifts cerebral metabolism toward anaerobic pathways, leading to regional GABA depletion in the basal ganglia. The depletion of GABA results in disinhibition of the thalamocortical motor pathway, manifesting as choreiform movements. In contrast, ketotic states may allow GABA resynthesis via acetoacetate, explaining the lower DS incidence in diabetic ketoacidosis [1, 10, 11]. This metabolic theory is supported by the fact that 81.7% of patients with documented ketone status in the review were non‐ketotic [1].

Imaging typically shows hyper‐attenuation of the striatum on CT and T1‐weighted hyperintensity with T2/FLAIR hypo‐intensity on MRI. The isolated putamen is most commonly involved, followed by combined caudate‐putamen nucleus involvement. Concomitant involvement of the caudate, putamen, and globus pallidus occurs in over one‐quarter of patients. While various conditions such as hypertensive hemorrhage, Wilson's disease, metabolic encephalopathies, and toxic insults may also cause striatal hyperintensities, DS is typically unilateral and lacks associated mass effect or surrounding edema. These features help distinguish it radiologically. Four main hypotheses have been proposed for the pathophysiology of the striatal imaging abnormalities in DS: petechial hemorrhage, mineral deposition (calcium or magnesium), myelin destruction, and infarction with astrocytosis. Of these, petechial hemorrhage and metabolic injury with astrocytic proliferation are most consistent with the reversible nature of symptoms and imaging changes in many cases [1, 10].

Treatment is centered on glycemic control with insulin therapy. The ADA Standards of Care 2025 recommends individualized glycemic control with HbA1C targets < 7.0%–7.5% for healthy older adults, < 8.0% for complex/intermediate older adults, and no HbA1C target for very complex/poor health older adults as they have minimal benefit from stringent glycemic goals [4]. Our patient falls into the complex/intermediate older adults' category since she has multiple comorbidities like HTN and CKD and her individualized glycemic goal was set to maintain HbA1C < 8.0%. In the review, while 25.6% of patients recovered with insulin therapy alone, the majority (73.3%) required adjunctive anti‐chorea medications. Haloperidol was the most commonly used (39.7%), followed by tetrabenazine (4%), and others [1]. Tetrabenazine primarily inhibits the vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2), reducing vesicular storage and synaptic release of dopamine, serotonin, and norepinephrine, which are instead degraded in the cytoplasm. It also has mild dopamine receptor‐blocking effects. By lowering striatal dopamine activity, TBZ helps control hyperkinetic movement disorders, including hemiballismus [2]. TBZ is usually started at 12.5–25 mg daily and titrated up to 100 mg as tolerated. While therapeutic daily doses ranging from 6.25 to 300 mg have been reported in the literature, the manufacturer recommends an initial dose of 12.5 mg once daily, with weekly titration by 12.5 mg. For doses > 37.5 mg, administration should be divided into three daily doses, and the maximum recommended dose is 100 mg3. Our patient responded well to 25 mg once daily, consistent with the lower end of effective therapeutic dosing.

Recurrence was observed in 18.2% of cases, highlighting the importance of long‐term glycemic control. It was observed that radiological abnormalities usually resolve within 3 months on CT and 8 months on MRI [1]. DS has a favorable prognosis when identified and treated early [1, 10, 11].
