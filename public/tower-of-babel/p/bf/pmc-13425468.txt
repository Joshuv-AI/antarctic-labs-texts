Health Risk Assessment of Dietary Arsenic Exposure and Selenium Deficiency in Tibet Based on In Vitro Gastrointestinal Simulation
Authors: Juyi Sun, Huimin Yu, Lei Chai, Xiaoping Wang, Ruixuan Zhang, Yunqiao Zhou, Huike Dong, Ping Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Tibetan Plateau has a distinctive high-As and low-Se geochemical background. This geochemical characteristic can be transferred to the human body via food intake, thereby posing potential health risks. Conventional assessments based only on total elemental concentrations may not reflect the fraction released during gastrointestinal digestion. In this study, the Unified Bioaccessibility Method (UBM) was combined with Monte Carlo simulation to evaluate dietary As exposure and Se intake from typical Tibetan foods. The results showed a low dietary Se/As ratio of 0.80, far below the national average of 4.35. Bioaccessibility-adjusted modeling reduced estimated As exposure, but carcinogenic risk remained above 10–4 in all age groups, with the highest mean risk in children. Estimated Se intake was also below the recommended level. After simulated gastrointestinal digestion, the As–Se correlation increased from r = 0.40 to 0.58, indicating a stronger postdigestion association. These findings highlight the need to incorporate bioaccessibility into dietary exposure assessment and suggest that Tibetan populations may face both elevated As-related risk and insufficient Se intake.

Introduction

Arsenic (As) is a globally prevalent toxic element and Group 1 carcinogen, with dietary intake accounting for 60%–90% of human exposure, making it a major threat to food safety and public health. − In contrast, selenium (Se) is an essential human nutrient critical for antioxidant defense, immune function, and thyroid regulation, and must be obtained through diet. Se deficiency is linked to endemic diseases including Keshan and Kashin–Beck diseases. −

The health risk of dietary As exposure is not determined by As concentration alone, but may also be influenced by Se nutritional status. Se strongly antagonizes As toxicity by facilitating As excretion through complexation, enhancing antioxidant activity, and improving As methylation. , Conversely, Se deficiency exacerbates As accumulation and cellular damage. Therefore, insufficient dietary Se intake may aggravate the potential health risks associated with As exposure.

The Tibetan Plateau represents a typical high-As and low-Se region because of its distinctive tectonic activity, long-term weathering processes, and unique geochemical background. Soil As levels in the Tibetan Plateau are relatively high, with an average concentration of 19.7 mg·kg–1, exceeding the national average of 11.2 mg·kg–1. In contrast, soil Se levels are generally low compared with the national background level. Local residents traditionally rely heavily on locally produced highland barley and livestock products, which may transfer the regional high-As–low-Se geochemical signature into the human diet through the soil–crop–livestock system. − However, the combined dietary challenge of As exposure and Se insufficiency in this high-altitude food system remains insufficiently studied.

Currently, most dietary risk assessments rely on total elemental concentrations, which may not accurately represent the fraction released from food matrices during gastrointestinal digestion. For example, As bioaccessibility in staple foods has been reported to range from 11.6% to 105%, depending on food matrix, processing method, and digestion conditions. , However, few studies have integrated in vitro bioaccessibility data with probabilistic exposure modeling to jointly evaluate As-related toxicological risk and Se nutritional inadequacy under realistic gastrointestinal digestion scenarios, particularly in high-altitude dietary systems such as the Tibetan Plateau. ,

Therefore, this study combines an in vitro digestion model with a Monte Carlo simulation to investigate the release of As and Se during digestion and to evaluate the role of bioaccessibility in exposure assessment. The objectives are to (1) determine the concentrations of As and Se in major food items; (2) quantify their bioaccessibility using an in vitro digestion model; (3) examine the relationship between As and Se during digestion; and (4) assess exposure risks using Monte Carlo simulation. The findings of this study will provide scientific support for mitigating As-related health risks, alleviating Se deficiency, and formulating targeted nutritional intervention strategies in high-altitude areas.

Materials and Methods

Study Area

The Tibetan Plateau is located in central Asia and southwestern China (78°25′–99°06′ E; 26°50′–36°53′ N), covering an area of approximately 1.2 million km2, with a resident population of about 3.65 million. Due to the constraints of the extreme high-altitude climate and geographic conditions, food diversity in this region is limited, leading to a distinctive dietary pattern. Highland barley, yak and mutton, butter (butter tea), dairy products, and sweet tea constitute the core components of the traditional local diet. Agriculture is mainly concentrated in river valleys with relatively favorable conditions, including the Yarlung Zangbo River basin and the “Three-River” regions (Qamdo, Nyingchi, Lhasa, Xigaze, and Shannan), as well as parts of Ngari. In contrast, the northern plateau and alpine meadow regions are dominated by pastoral systems due to harsher environmental conditions. Sample Collection and Preparation

Based on regional dietary patterns, 42 representative food samples were collected across six major food categories: cereals, meat and meat products, dairy products, fruits and vegetables, tea, and seasonings. These categories were selected because they represent commonly consumed foods in the traditional Tibetan diet, including highland barley products, yak and mutton, dairy products, butter tea or tea products, and locally used condiments. Samples were obtained from local farmsteads and markets in representative agricultural and pastoral areas of Tibet. Detailed information on sampling region, food category, sample type and source form is provided in Table S3. All samples were sealed in clean polyethylene bags, transported to the laboratory under cold conditions, and stored at −4 °C until analysis. Before analysis, samples were ground using a clean grinder, homogenized, and passed through a suitable sieve. All containers and tools were precleaned with dilute nitric acid and rinsed thoroughly with ultrapure water to minimize contamination.

Hot-Plate Acid Digestion

Aliquots (0.5 g) were digested using 6 mL HNO3 + 2 mL 30%H2O2 in PTFE vessels. A stepped heating program on an electric hot plate was implemented (100–160 °C). The resulting clear digestate was diluted to 20 mL with ultrapure water and filtered (0.22 μm) for ICP-MS analysis (PerkinElmer NexION 1000G).

In Vitro Gastrointestinal Simulation

The Unified Bioaccessibility Method (UBM) was applied to determine the in vitro bioaccessibility of As and Se in food samples. Prior to digestion, all samples were prepared in forms that closely reflected their commonly consumed edible states. Foods normally consumed after cooking, such as meat, rice, noodles, and vegetables, were cooked before the digestion experiment. Meat samples were boiled until fully cooked and subsequently homogenized. Ready-to-eat or dry foods, including tsampa, dried meat products, tea leaves, chili powder, and milk residue, were directly homogenized or ground before analysis. For tea-related samples, dry tea leaves were used for total elemental concentration analysis, whereas brewed tea infusions were used for dietary exposure assessment when tea consumption was represented by tea liquid intake.

In brief, 2.0 g of each prepared sample was subjected to a three-stage in vitro digestion procedure comprising oral, gastric, and intestinal phases. The oral phase was simulated with artificial saliva containing α-amylase for 3 min. The gastric phase was conducted using pepsin at pH 1.20 ± 0.05 for 2 h, and the intestinal phase was then performed with pancreatin and bile at pH 6.30 ± 0.10 for 2 h (Table S2). Following digestion, the final chyme was centrifuged at 9000 rpm for 20 min, and the supernatant was filtered and collected for subsequent elemental quantification.

It should be noted that the UBM assay estimates in vitro bioaccessibility rather than in vivo bioavailability. In this study, bioaccessibility was defined as the fraction of As or Se released from the food matrix into the soluble digestive fluid after simulated gastrointestinal digestion. This fraction represents the portion that is potentially available for intestinal absorption. In contrast, bioavailability refers to the fraction of an ingested element that is actually absorbed across the intestinal epithelium, enters systemic circulation, and becomes available for biological utilization or toxicological effects. Therefore, the bioaccessibility values obtained from the UBM model were used to calculate bioaccessibility-corrected intake, but they should not be interpreted as actual absorbed doses in humans.

Quality Assurance and Control (QA/QC)

Analytical accuracy was evaluated using certified reference materials (CRMs), including wheat (GBW10011a) and chicken (GBW10018), representing plant-based and animal-derived food matrices, respectively. The recoveries of As and Se in the CRMs ranged from 85.8% to 115.4%. The limits of detection (LODs) were 4 μg/kg for As and 3 μg/kg for Se. Procedural blanks were included in each analytical batch to monitor potential contamination during sample digestion and instrumental analysis. Matrix spike recovery tests were conducted for representative samples from different food categories, and the results fell within the acceptable recovery range. All samples were analyzed in triplicate, and the coefficient of variation (CV) was generally below 5%.

Health Risk Characterization

Bioaccessibility (BA, %) was calculated as the ratio of the soluble concentration released during in vitro digestion to the total concentration in the food sample 1 BA(%)=CdigestCtotal×100 where C digest represents the concentration of the soluble metal fraction in the gastrointestinal extract, and C total is the total concentration of the metal in the food sample.

The Estimated Daily Intake (EDI) was determined to characterize dietary exposure using eq 2 EDI=Ctotal×IR×BABW where C total is the total concentration of the metal in food, IR is the daily ingestion rate, BA is the bioaccessibility (%), and BW is the average body weight.

The Total Cancer Risk (CRt) represents the incremental probability of an individual developing cancer over a lifetime. 3 CRt=∑EDI×Pi×CSF where EDI is the bioaccessibility-corrected daily intake of total As, Pi is the proportion of inorganic As (iAs) to total As in each food category, and CSF is the cancer slope factor for inorganic As. In this study, the oral cancer slope factor for inorganic As was set at 1.5 (mg/kg/day)−1 according to the USEPA.

Because the toxicity of As is highly dependent on its chemical species, the cancer risk assessment was based on the estimated inorganic As intake rather than total As intake. The category-specific Pi values used in this study were as follows: fruits and vegetables, 77%; grains and grain products, 84%; meat and meat products, 100%; milk and dairy products, 26%; tea, 90%; and seasonings, 80%. − These values were derived from published data on the relative proportions of inorganic As in different food categories.

According to USEPA risk interpretation criteria, a CR value exceeding 10–4 is generally considered potentially unacceptable; values between 10–6 and 10–4 are considered acceptable or tolerable, while values below 10–6 indicate negligible risk. Sensitive populations, particularly children and pregnant women, may face greater health concerns from dietary iAs exposure. Children are especially vulnerable because they generally have higher food intake per unit body weight and are undergoing rapid growth and development.

Statistical and Probabilistic Modeling

A Monte Carlo simulation with 10,000 iterations was applied to construct a probabilistic dietary exposure and risk assessment model. This approach is consistent with recent applications of Monte Carlo simulation in health risk assessment of potentially toxic element exposure, where MCS has been used to incorporate exposure variability and uncertainty and to identify upper-bound risk scenarios and sensitive subgroups.

A daily exposure scenario was adopted in the simulation. In each iteration, one bioaccessible concentration value was randomly sampled from the measured data set for each of the six food groups and used as the effective exposure concentration for that food group. The daily intake rate of each food group was assigned according to age-specific dietary intake parameters. Adult intake values were based on Tibet-specific food consumption data, whereas intake values for other age groups were estimated using a ratio-based correction method. − Specifically, national age-specific dietary intake values were adjusted using correction factors derived from the ratio between Tibet-specific adult intake and national adult intake for the corresponding food groups. The revised dietary intake parameters used in the Monte Carlo simulation are summarized in Table S3.

To reflect population variability, the daily intake rate was assumed to follow a log-normal distribution. The total daily bioaccessible intake of As and Se was calculated by summing the contributions from all six food groups. The resulting intake estimates were further used to evaluate dietary inorganic As-related carcinogenic risk and daily Se intake.

Data visualization was performed using Origin 2021, Monte Carlo simulations were conducted using Python (version 3.12.3), and statistical analyses were carried out using Microsoft Excel 2003 and SPSS (version 13.0).

Results and Discussion

Concentration Profiles of As and Se in the Tibetan Diet

Characteristics of Total As and Se Concentrations across Diverse Food Categories

As concentrations varied markedly among different food groups. Meat samples showed the highest mean As concentration (156.34 μg/kg), followed by cereals (133.08 μg/kg). The overall ranking of As concentrations was: meat and meat products > cereals > dairy products > seasonings > tea > fruits and vegetables (Figure a).

Characteristics of total As and total Se concentrations in the Tibetan diet. (a) Distribution of As and Se across different food categories; (b) Comparison of As and Se concentrations among different sampling regions. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

The elevated As levels in cereals may be partly associated with the high-As geochemical background of the Tibetan Plateau. The widespread distribution of As-rich parent materials, such as shale and schist, contributes to high soil As levels. In addition, strong tectonic activity and geothermal processes further increase the background concentration of As in surface soils, reaching 19.7 mg·kg–1, which is significantly higher than the national average in China (11.2 mg·kg–1). In major agricultural regions such as Lhasa and Xigaze, both natural weathering and human activities promote As accumulation in farmland soils. , Under this high-As background, As may be transferred from soils to crops through the soil–crop system. Staple crops such as highland barley can absorb and accumulate As during growth, leading to elevated levels in grains. The relatively high As levels in meat and meat products may be related to livestock exposure through forage, drinking water, and incidental soil ingestion during grazing. These pathways may contribute to As accumulation in animal-derived foods.

Se concentrations also showed clear differences among food groups. As shown in Figure a, the overall ranking was: dairy products > meat and meat products > seasoning > tea > cereals > fruits and vegetables. The Tibetan Plateau is widely recognized as a Se-deficient region. Previous studies have reported low soil Se levels in agricultural areas, for example, 0.11 ± 0.08 mg·kg–1 in Lhasa and 0.184 mg·kg–1 in Xigaze, with up to 43% of soils in major highland barley-producing areas classified as Se-deficient. ,, This widespread soil Se deficiency limits Se bioavailability for plant uptake, leading to consistently low Se accumulation in cereals, fruits, and vegetables grown in the region.

Despite this, dairy products (284.27 μg/kg) and meat (189.71 μg/kg) showed relatively high Se concentrations, suggesting that animal-derived foods may serve as important dietary Se sources in this region. This may be explained by two main factors. First, grazing animals continuously ingest local forage, and this long-term dietary intake may contribute to Se accumulation in animal-derived foods. Second, studies on sheep have shown that Se can be efficiently converted into organic forms in the body and transferred to offspring via the placenta or milk, or stored at high levels in muscle tissues. Organic Se has a higher retention efficiency in muscle compared to inorganic forms. As a result, meat and dairy products may act as important Se reservoirs and provide a more direct dietary source of Se than plant-based foods in this region.

Regional Variations of Total As and Se Concentrations in the Tibetan Diet

Marked spatial variations in As and Se concentrations were observed across different regions of Tibet (Figure b). Ngari showed the highest Se concentration (282.66 ± 226.77 μg/kg), accompanied by relatively high As levels (131.28 ± 66.24 μg/kg), suggesting a pattern of relatively high Se supply accompanied by elevated As exposure. Qamdo and Xigaze exhibited comparatively elevated As concentrations (136.22 ± 187.85 μg/kg and 157.71 ± 95.23 μg/kg, respectively), which may be associated with regional geochemical backgrounds and food accumulation processes. In contrast, Lhasa showed the lowest levels of both As and Se (79.61 ± 57.52 μg/kg and 77.29 ± 104.17 μg/kg), representing a low-exposure scenario.

Bioaccessibility (%) of As and Se in the Tibetan dietary system. (a) Variations in As and Se bioaccessibility across different food categories; (b) Spatial distribution of As and Se bioaccessibility among different sampling regions. Values are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

These spatial patterns are broadly consistent with regional differences in soil geochemical backgrounds. Previous studies have reported that surface soil As concentrations in Ngari, Qamdo, and Xigaze reach 20.08, 24.39, and 20.55 mg·kg–1, respectively, while farmland soils in Lhasa range from 18.68 ± 11.00 mg·kg–1, with localized point-source contamination observed in certain areas. , These regional differences may influence As transfer from soils to crops, forage, and animal-derived foods. In agro-pastoral regions such as Qamdo and Xigaze, livestock may be exposed to As through local forage, drinking water, and incidental soil ingestion. In pastoral areas such as Ngari, long-term grazing on local forage may partly explain the observed “high As–high Se” pattern in livestock products.

Compared with other regions in China (Table ), the Tibetan diet showed significantly higher As concentrations (138.88 μg/kg vs 27.63 μg/kg) and slightly lower Se levels (111.73 μg/kg vs 120.11 μg/kg). The Se/As ratio (0.80) was markedly lower than the national average (4.35), indicating a typical “high-As–low-Se” pattern. In cereals, As concentrations (133.08 μg/kg) were approximately 4–5 times higher than the national average, while Se levels (49.62 μg/kg) were comparable to moderate levels nationwide. Meat and dairy products exhibited particularly elevated concentrations of both As and Se (As: 156.34 and 132.69 μg/kg; Se: 185.46 and 284.27 μg/kg), exceeding average levels reported in most regions of China. Overall, the Tibetan dietary pattern can be characterized as “high As with moderate Se”. The interpretation of this dietary pattern should be further linked to exposure assessment, because total element concentrations alone cannot fully represent health risks. Recent studies on PTEs in foods and beverages have shown that EDI, THQ, HI, and CR are useful indices for evaluating ingestion-related health risks, and that Monte Carlo simulation can better characterize variability in contaminant concentrations, intake rates, body weight, and high-percentile risks. These findings support the need to evaluate the Tibetan diet not only by total As and Se concentrations, but also by dietary intake, bioaccessibility, and probabilistic risk estimates. ,

In a broader geographical context, the Tibetan dietary As–Se pattern shares some similarities with other high-altitude or geochemically enriched regions, where local food systems can reflect the elemental background of soils, water, and forage. For example, studies from the Mongolian Plateau have shown that livestock products can serve as important Se sources despite low soil Se conditions, while rice, poultry, or crop-based foods are important As exposure sources in parts of South and Southeast Asia. − In Andean regions, geothermal activity and As-rich geological settings have also been linked to elevated As levels in local crops. , These comparisons suggest that the Tibetan diet represents a distinctive highland food system in which animal-derived foods may contribute both to trace-element exposure and nutrient supply.

Overall, the Tibetan dietary exposure pattern reflects a unique combination of high As and moderate Se under high-altitude and high-background geochemical conditions, distinguishing it from other global highland regions.

Dietary Bioaccessibility

Previous sections have highlighted the characteristics of high total As and Se concentrations within the Tibetan dietary system; however, recent toxicological evidence emphasizes that the total elemental concentration in food does not equate to the effective dose absorbed by the human body. Given that traditional risk assessments based solely on total concentrations tend to significantly overestimate actual human exposure levels, , the incorporation of bioaccessibility (BA) has emerged as a critical approach for re-evaluating the authentic dietary exposure risks, thereby providing more realistic and reliable data for the risk characterization in this study.

Bioaccessibility of As and Se in Different Diet Components

Clear differences in the bioaccessibility of Se and As were observed among different food groups (Figure a). Cereals showed the highest Se bioaccessibility (approximately 54%), indicating that Se in cereals is readily released during digestion. In contrast, meat samples exhibited the lowest Se bioaccessibility (approximately 20%). Dairy products, vegetables, condiments, and tea showed intermediate levels. The relatively low Se bioaccessibility in meat is mainly related to its chemical form and matrix characteristics. Se in animal tissues is predominantly present as selenocysteine, which is tightly bound to muscle proteins and is not easily released during gastrointestinal digestion. In addition, protein denaturation during cooking may further strengthen this binding, thereby limiting Se release. In contrast, although cereals contain lower total Se concentrations (49.62 μg/kg), their bioaccessibility is significantly higher. The breakdown of structural barriers during processing and digestion facilitates the release of Se from the starch matrix, leading to a higher proportion available for absorption. Consistent with previous studies, Se bioaccessibility is strongly dependent on its chemical form. Plant-derived organic Se typically shows high bioaccessibility (80–94%), whereas animal-derived Se (18–25%) and Se-enriched foods (1–24.32%) tend to exhibit lower values. −

As bioaccessibility also varied significantly among food groups, with mean values ranging from 8.50% to 33.49% (Figure a). Condiments and vegetables showed the highest bioaccessibility (33.49% and 32.37%, respectively), followed by dairy products (22.13%). Cereals and meats showed similar levels (approximately 14%–15%), while tea exhibited the lowest value (8.50%). The low As bioaccessibility in cereals is mainly attributed to the plant matrix, which is rich in dietary fiber. This structure can limit As release by binding or physically trapping metal-associated compounds during digestion. − In contrast, the relatively higher As bioaccessibility observed in dairy products and meat may be related to the behavior of organic As species during digestion. Unlike many metals that tend to precipitate under intestinal conditions, organic As compounds, which are more common in animal-derived foods, may undergo transformation reactions in the presence of bile salts and digestive enzymes. These processes can increase their solubility or convert them into more bioaccessible forms, resulting in enhanced As release in the intestinal phase. − Consistent with previous studies, As bioaccessibility varies widely across different food types. For example, berries from Canada show relatively low As bioaccessibility (12–45%), whereas fungi and wild rabbit exhibit higher values (22–76%). In staple foods, rice from China has been reported to exhibit a broad range of As bioaccessibility (33.1–89.4%), reflecting substantial variability associated with food composition and structure. ,

Regional Variations in As and Se Bioaccessibility

Significant spatial heterogeneity in the bioaccessibility of As and Se was observed across the Tibetan Plateau (Figure b). Lhasa exhibited the highest Se bioaccessibility (40.83%), whereas Ngari showed relatively low bioaccessibility for both Se (17.24%) and As (19.87%). Qamdo presented the highest As bioaccessibility (23.05%), indicating a potential high-release region. In contrast, Xigaze showed comparatively low bioaccessibility for both As (15.19%) and Se (28.12%), representing a low-release pattern.

The bioaccessibility of As and Se is governed by multiple factors, including geographic background, and processing conditions. , For example, rice, a major dietary source of As, exhibits wide regional differences in bioaccessibility, ranging from 7–31% in Spain to as high as 90% in Brazil, with intermediate values reported in Mexico (71%), the United States (∼61%), and China (55–57%). , These variations can be attributed to region-specific geochemical conditions that regulate elemental speciation and binding within food matrices. Differences in soil pH, redox potential, and mineral composition can alter the partitioning of As between inorganic and organic forms, as well as its association with mineral phases or organic matter. , In addition, coexisting elements in crops may compete for or share binding sites within proteins, phytates, or polysaccharides, thereby modifying metal–matrix interactions and influencing their release behavior during gastrointestinal digestion.

Overall, regional geochemical background can influence bioaccessibility by altering the chemical forms and stability of As and Se during digestion. These findings indicate that spatial variability in bioaccessibility is not solely determined by total concentration, and that incorporating bioaccessibility into exposure assessment is essential for more accurate health risk evaluation.

Correlation Shifts between As, Se, and Other Elements Pre- and Postdigestion

Changes in elemental correlations before and after in vitro digestion Figure provide information on the release behavior of As, Se, and other elements under simulated gastrointestinal conditions. Prior to digestion, correlations among elements were generally weak. After digestion, several correlations increased, suggesting that enzymatic degradation and changes in pH may promote the simultaneous release of elements from food matrices. The correlation between As and Se increased from r = 0.40 before digestion to r = 0.58 after digestion, indicating a stronger postdigestion association. This change may be associated with the disruption of food matrices during digestion.

Heatmap of correlation matrices between heavy metals and nutritional elements in food samples pre- and postgastrointestinal extraction. (a) Pearson correlation coefficients based on total elemental concentrations; (b) Pearson correlation coefficients based on the bioaccessible fraction in the intestinal phase. The color scale represents the strength of the correlation (r), ranging from −1 (blue) to 1 (red).

In addition, changes in the correlations between As/Se and matrix-related elements may reflect coordinated release behavior during simulated digestion. For example, the correlation coefficient between Se and Mg increased from 0.32 to 0.57, while that between As and Mg increased from 0.40 to 0.48, and the Se–Zn correlation remained relatively high (r ≈ 0.52). These patterns may be associated with the degradation of food matrix components, during enzymatic digestion. Mg and Zn are commonly associated with structural components or metalloproteins in food matrices and may be released together with As and Se as these matrices are decomposed. , In addition, the relatively strong correlations between As–Fe and Se–Fe before and after digestion suggest that Fe-containing phases may be involved in regulating the release and mobility of As and Se. Iron colloids or Fe-bearing particles can adsorb As and Se through surface complexation or coprecipitation, which may influence their corelease behavior and bioaccessibility. Dietary components such as polyphenols may also affect redox conditions or form metal complexes, thereby potentially modifying element release during digestion. ,

Overall, the observed correlation shifts indicate correlated release behavior under simulated gastrointestinal conditions. Further studies involving As and Se speciation, intestinal uptake models, animal experiments, or human biomonitoring are required to clarify potential biological interactions between As and Se.

Risk Assessment Based on Monte Carlo Simulation and Dietary Recommendations

Risk Assessment

Monte Carlo simulation was used to characterize the variability and uncertainty of dietary exposure and health risks associated with As and Se intake. In the present study, 10,000 iterations were performed to estimate the probabilistic distributions of cancer risk (CR) for dietary iAs exposure and daily Se intake. Compared with deterministic assessment, this approach provides risk distributions rather than single-point estimates and is useful for evaluating high-percentile risks and sensitive subgroups.

Bioaccessibility-adjusted Monte Carlo simulation indicated that dietary iAs exposure was associated with considerable carcinogenic risk across all age groups in Tibet (Figure a). In the simulation, bioaccessible total As concentrations were converted to iAs using food-category-specific iAs/total As ratios. Children aged 2–7 years showed the highest mean CR, with a value of 6.47 × 10–4, followed by students aged 8–12 years (4.94 × 10–4), adolescents aged 13–19 years (3.68 × 10–4), adults aged 20–50 years (3.51 × 10–4), middle-aged adults aged 51–65 years (3.39 × 10–4), and elderly individuals aged >65 years (2.93 × 10–4). The mean CR for children was approximately 1.85 times higher than that for adults, reflecting their higher dietary intake per unit body weight and greater sensitivity during early development. According to commonly used USEPA risk interpretation criteria, these results suggest that dietary iAs exposure may represent a non-negligible carcinogenic risk for the Tibetan population, with children being the most sensitive age group. Pregnant women should also be considered a sensitive population for iAs exposure because inorganic As can cross the placental barrier and may affect fetal development. However, pregnant women were not modeled separately in this study due to the lack of pregnancy-specific dietary intake and body-weight data for Tibet. This subgroup should therefore be prioritized in future dietary exposure assessments and public health interventions.

Monte Carlo-based probabilistic assessment of dietary risk and Se intake (n = 10,000). (a) Probability density distributions of As-related CR across age groups. (b) Probability density distributions of daily Se intake.

The Monte Carlo-based estimation of daily Se intake showed that Se intake remained below the recommended level across all age groups, although intake varied by age (Figure b). The mean daily Se intake ranged from 16.28 to 24.93 μg/day. Adults aged 20–50 years had the highest estimated intake, with a mean value of 24.93 ± 5.05 μg/day, followed by adolescents aged 13–19 years (20.64 ± 4.25 μg/day), middle-aged adults aged 51–65 years (18.95 ± 3.85 μg/day), students aged 8–12 years (18.07 ± 3.86 μg/day), children aged 2–7 years (16.91 ± 4.27 μg/day), and elderly individuals aged >65 years (16.28 ± 3.23 μg/day). Compared with the adult RNI for Se of 50 μg/day, adult intake reached only approximately 50% of the recommended level, while elderly intake reached only about 33%. These results indicate that the Tibetan diet may provide insufficient Se intake, especially for elderly individuals and other nutritionally vulnerable groups.

Overall, the Monte Carlo results consistently showed that dietary iAs-related carcinogenic risk exceeded 10–4 in all age groups, while estimated Se intake remained below the recommended level. These findings indicate that Tibetan residents may simultaneously face elevated dietary iAs exposure and insufficient Se intake. Therefore, continued monitoring of As and Se in Tibetan foods and region-specific strategies to reduce iAs exposure and improve Se nutrition are needed.

Dietary Recommendations

The As–Se distributions based on total concentrations and bioaccessible concentrations are shown in Figure . The total concentration plot (Figure a) showed clear differences among food categories, indicating that Tibetan foods differ substantially in their overall As and Se burdens. Dairy products and some meat products generally contained relatively high Se levels, whereas several meat products, cereals, and tea-related samples showed comparatively higher As levels.

Dashed lines indicate Se/As ratios of 4 and 0.8, and shaded areas represent relatively Se-rich, intermediate, and high-As–low-Se zones. After in vitro digestion, the distribution pattern changed markedly (Figure b), suggesting that food matrices influenced the release of As and Se into simulated gastrointestinal fluids. Compared with total concentrations, bioaccessible fractions are more relevant to dietary exposure assessment because they represent the soluble portion potentially available for intestinal absorption. Using Se/As ratios of 4 and 0.8 as reference lines, dairy products and fresh meat were mainly located in the relatively Se-rich zone, whereas air-dried meat, butter tea, tea leaves, and some vegetables were located in the high-As–low-Se zone. These results indicate matrix-dependent differences in As and Se release during digestion. However, this distribution should be interpreted as a food-matrix-related release pattern rather than direct evidence of As–Se biological interaction.

To reduce potential dietary risks, interventions should focus on monitoring and managing environmental sources and food production areas rather than restricting the consumption of traditional foods. In regions or food categories with relatively high bioaccessible As, regular monitoring of As in soil, irrigation water, forage, livestock products, and processed foods is recommended. At the same time, strategies to improve Se intake should be considered, particularly for populations with low estimated Se intake. Se biofortification of staple crops, such as highland barley, and appropriate dietary diversification with Se-rich foods may help improve Se nutritional status. − However, such strategies should be implemented cautiously and supported by local field trials, because the effects of Se supplementation on As uptake, As bioaccessibility, and human health outcomes may vary with soil properties, crop type, food matrix, and dietary habits. Overall, region-specific monitoring, dietary diversification, and targeted nutritional intervention may provide a more culturally acceptable approach to reducing iAs exposure while improving Se intake in Tibetan populations.

Limitations and Uncertainties

Several limitations and uncertainties should be acknowledged. First, the available certified reference materials did not fully cover all food matrices analyzed in this study, such as dairy products, tea, fruits and vegetables, and condiments. Nevertheless, CRM recoveries, procedural blanks, duplicate analyses, triplicate measurements, and matrix spike tests supported the reliability of the analytical results. Second, direct As speciation analysis was not conducted. Therefore, carcinogenic risk was estimated using published food-category-specific inorganic As proportions, and the results should be interpreted with caution. Third, complete age-stratified dietary intake data for Tibet were unavailable. Adult intake parameters were updated using Tibet-specific consumption data, whereas intake values for other age groups were estimated using a ratio-based correction method. In addition, pregnant women were not separately modeled due to the lack of pregnancy-specific dietary intake and body-weight data for Tibet, although they should be considered a priority subgroup in future dietary exposure assessments and risk management strategies. Although these approaches improved the assessment compared with total concentration-based and national intake-based estimates, future studies should include larger-scale local dietary surveys, pregnancy-specific exposure parameters, direct As and Se speciation analysis, and more matrix-matched CRMs to further refine exposure and risk estimates.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the concentrations, in vitro bioaccessibility, and dietary health risks of As and Se in major food categories consumed in Tibet. The results showed a dietary pattern characterized by relatively high As exposure and insufficient Se intake. Bioaccessibility varied among food categories, indicating that total concentrations alone may not fully represent potential dietary exposure. After bioaccessibility adjustment, Monte Carlo simulation showed that dietary inorganic As exposure may pose considerable carcinogenic risks across all age groups, with the highest risk observed in children aged 2–7 years. Meanwhile, estimated Se intake remained below the recommended level for all age groups, particularly among elderly individuals. These findings suggest that Tibetan residents may face the combined challenge of elevated iAs-related health risk and inadequate Se nutrition. Future studies should incorporate local dietary surveys, As and Se speciation analysis, in vivo validation, and epidemiological evidence. From a public health perspective, continued monitoring of As and Se in Tibetan foods and region-specific strategies to reduce iAs exposure and improve Se intake are recommended.
