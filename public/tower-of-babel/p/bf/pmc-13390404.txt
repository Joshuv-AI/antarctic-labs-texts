Shared genetic architecture between depression and migraine: a large-scale genome-wide cross-trait analysis
Authors: Min Zhang, Qiyin Li, Yuan Zhao, Yadong Peng, Jiangyou Yu, Cheng Gan, Liwei Tang, Keyi Liu, Ying Liu, Zheng Zhang, Xinglian Wang, Mingshuang Tang, Chenggang Jiang, Dongqing Gu
Published: 2026

AbstractBackgroundDepression and migraine are highly prevalent neuropsychiatric disorders that frequently co-occur. However, the extent to which they share genetic determinants, particularly for postpartum depression (PPD), an underexplored subtype, remains insufficiently understood.MethodsWe performed a large-scale genome-wide cross-trait analysis using summary statistics for depression phenotypes (overall depression, major depressive disorder [MDD], PPD) and migraine subtypes (migraine, migraine with aura [MA], migraine without aura [MO]). Background

Migraine and depression are two leading causes of neuropsychiatric disability worldwide and disproportionately affect women. Beyond their individual burden, these disorders frequently co-occur, suggesting biological interdependence rather than coincidental comorbidity [1–6]. Epidemiological evidence indicates that individuals with migraine have a two- to three-fold increased risk of developing depressive symptoms, whereas depression is associated with greater migraine frequency, greater severity, and poorer treatment responses [7–9]. This bidirectional association carries substantial clinical implications, including delayed diagnosis, prolonged treatment durations, decreased quality of life, and increased socioeconomic costs [6, 10, 11]. Despite extensive research, the mechanisms underlying this comorbidity remain incompletely understood.

Several lines of evidence support shared neurobiological foundations across migraine and depressive disorders [12]. Both conditions exhibit dysregulation of monoaminergic signaling, particularly serotonergic, dopaminergic, and noradrenergic pathways. Aberrant hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis responses, neuroimmune activation, and altered neural network connectivity have also been implicated [13, 14]. Genetic studies further highlight their heritability (major depressive disorder [MDD]: 30–40%; migraine: 40–50%) [15, 16]. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified disorder-specific susceptibility loci related to synaptic plasticity (BDNF), neurotransmitter transport (SLC6A4), and inflammatory pathways (TNF) [17, 18]. However, mechanistic overlap remains insufficiently characterized, in part due to limited cross-phenotype genomic investigations.

Few studies have examined shared genetic architecture between depression and migraine. Linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) revealed moderate genome-wide genetic correlations (rg≈0.23–0.32) [19], and cross-trait meta-analyses have identified 14 pleiotropic loci [20]. Mendelian randomization (MR) studies provide early evidence of a stronger depression-to-migraine direction, although weaker reverse-direction effects remain possible and require further clarification [21]. Nevertheless, these investigations have major limitations that restrict mechanistic interpretation and translational potential. First, prior studies have focused almost exclusively on overall depression or MDD, while clinically distinct subtypes, particularly postpartum depression (PPD), remain largely overlooked. PPD is a female-specific phenotype involving abrupt endocrine shifts, inflammatory activation, and neuroplastic changes postpartum, yet its genetic relationship with migraine has never been evaluated at the genomic level. Second, prior cross-trait analyses have rarely achieved variant-level resolution: fine-mapping and colocalization are seldom implemented, leaving it unclear whether shared signals reflect true causal variants or linkage disequilibrium. Third, MR-based causal inference has relied primarily on standard bidirectional MR, which is vulnerable to horizontal pleiotropy. Multivariable MR (MVMR) may help separate direct from indirect effects and clarify whether comorbidity reflects genuine directionality or shared liability, but its application remains limited, particularly across migraine subtypes (MA vs. MO), which show distinct neurobiological features.

Therefore, a critical unresolved question is whether depression, particularly its postpartum subtype, shares genetic architecture with migraine at the variant level, and whether this relationship reflects true directional effects or shared liability. To address this, we conducted a large-scale cross-trait genomic analysis across multiple depression phenotypes, including the relatively underexplored subtype PPD, and migraine. We combined complementary LD-based statistical frameworks (LDSC, genetic covariance analyzer), cross-trait meta-analysis, fine-mapping, and colocalization to identify pleiotropically associated loci and prioritize those with evidence for shared causal signals. Furthermore, we leveraged bidirectional MR and MVMR designs to determine potential directional causal relationships while accounting for confounding pathways. By integrating complementary analytical approaches, this study provides new insights into the genetic relationship between depression and migraine and introduces PPD into cross-trait genomic analyses, thereby providing a genomic basis for future mechanistic and translational investigation of depression-migraine comorbidity.

Methods

Study design and data sources

The overall study design is illustrated in Fig. 1, comprising four main components: genetic correlation analyses, identification of pleiotropically associated loci, functional analysis, and evaluation of potential causal relationships between depression and migraine.

Summary statistics were obtained from publicly available GWAS repositories or through consortium-approved access. Specifically, summary statistics for depression (N = 807,553) and MDD (N = 173,005) were retrieved from published GWAS of the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC); [22] summary statistics for postpartum depression (N = 236,178) were sourced from FinnGen release 8 (https://r8.finngen.fi/pheno/O15_POSTPART_DEPR). Additionally, GWAS summary data for migraine (N = 873,341) and its subtypes (MA: N = 718,476; MO: N = 697,356) were provided by the International Headache Genetics Consortium (IHGC), which represents one of the largest genetic studies of migraine to date. Data access was approved through formal application to the consortium [18]. Detailed information for each dataset can be found in Supplementary Table S1. Given that these GWAS datasets were derived from large consortia, potential sample overlap between exposure and outcome datasets cannot be completely excluded, particularly through shared population-based cohorts such as the UK Biobank. As only summary-level data were available, the exact degree of overlap could not be quantified.

Genetic correlation analyses

Genome-wide genetic correlation analyses

To evaluate the genetic correlation between depression and migraine, we employed conventional LDSC and GNOVA [23, 24]. For LDSC, precomputed linkage disequilibrium (LD)-scores were used, which were derived from approximately 1.2 million common and well-imputed single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in European populations represented by the Hapmap3 reference panel. Sample overlap may lead to inflation of genetic correlation estimates, thus we examined the LD score regression intercept, which accounts for confounding biases including population stratification and potential sample overlap. GNOVA, potentially more powerful than LDSC, was used to estimate the genetic correlation across about 5 million well-imputed SNPs from the 1000 Genomes Project. The estimates were partitioned among 11 functional categories of SNPs [25], quartiles of minor allele frequency (MAF), and regions involved in transcription for seven broadly-defined tissue types [24].

Local genetic correlation analyses

Local genetic correlation analyses enabled the identification of region-specific genetic correlations. We used SUPERGNOVA, which estimates local genetic covariance and local genetic correlation from GWAS summary statistics within approximately independent linkage disequilibrium (LD) blocks [26]. Specifically, SUPERGNOVA partitions the genome into approximately 2,353 LD-independent blocks, decorrelates GWAS z-scores using eigenvectors of the local LD matrix, and estimates local genetic covariance between two traits using weighted least-squares regression. This covariance quantifies the extent to which SNP effect sizes are shared within a given genomic region, with positive values indicating concordant genetic effects and negative values indicating discordant effects. Local genetic correlation is subsequently obtained by standardizing the local covariance by the SNP-based heritability of each trait.

Cross-trait meta-analysis

Cross-trait meta-analysis was conducted using the cross-phenotype association (CPASSOC) method [27]. Pairwise SHet was used to combine the summary statistics of depression and migraine. The clumping function parameters of PLINK [28] -- clump-p1 5e-8 --clump-p2 1e-5 --clump-r2 0.2 --clump-kb 500 were applied to obtain independent SNPs. Significant pleiotropically associated SNPs were defined as variants with PCPASSOC<5 × 10− 8 in the cross-trait meta-analysis and Psingle−trait<5 × 10− 3 in each individual trait. The additional single-trait threshold was applied to ensure at least nominal evidence of association for both traits and to reduce the likelihood of cross-trait signals driven predominantly by one phenotype.

Fine-mapping credible set analysis

We further identified a 99% credible set of causal variants using an efficient Bayesian fine-mapping method, FM-summary (https://github.com/hailianghuang/FM-summary) [29]. Briefly, for each of the pleiotropically associated loci identified as significant by the cross-trait meta-analysis, variants within 500 kb around the significant index SNP were extracted and used as input for FM-summary. This method estimated the posterior inclusion probability (PIP) of each variant, indicating the probability of a true association with the trait or disease. To establish the 99% credible set, SNPs were sorted in descending order of PIP, and the probabilities were summed until the total reached at least 99% [30, 31].

Colocalization analysis

To further assess whether the association signals for the two traits originate from a common causal variant rather than nearby but distinct variants, we conducted colocalization analysis using the Coloc framework. Coloc evaluates five alternative hypotheses about the presence and sharing of causal variants and returns posterior probabilities for each. For every pleiotropically associated index SNP, we extracted summary statistics for variants within ± 500 kb and calculated the posterior probability for H4 (PPH4), which reflects the probability that both traits are influenced by the same causal variant. Loci with PPH4 > 0.8 were regarded as strong evidence of colocalization, and 0.5 < PPH4 ≤ 0.8 as suggestive evidence.

Functional annotation

Variant annotation

The Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) (https://grch37.ensembl.org/info/docs/tools/vep/index.html) [32] and Haploreg4.2 (https://pubs.broadinstitute.org/mammals/haploreg/haploreg.php) [33] were used for detailed functional annotation of the variants identified by CPASSOC. In addition, 3DSNP (https://omic.tech/3dsnpv2/) [34] was utilized to identify genes interacting with the pleiotropically associated SNPs for depression and migraine through 3D chromatin loops in different cell types. Significant SNP-gene pairs (false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.05) associated with the pleiotropically associated SNPs for depression and migraine were further evaluated in 48 human tissues obtained from the GTEx Portal.

GTEx tissue enrichment analysis

To investigate the tissue-specific patterns underlying the pleiotropically associated loci identified in CPASSOC, SNP-based tissue enrichment analysis was performed using the Functional Mapping and Annotation (FUMA) platform [29]. This approach systematically evaluates the relationship between GWAS-associated loci and tissue-specific gene expression patterns across 48 general tissues from GTEx (version 8). Specifically, gene property analysis was implemented in FUMA to assess whether genes near GWAS-significant SNPs showed preferential expression in particular tissues. The analysis used 1000 permutations to establish empirical significance thresholds, generating permutation-derived P-values for each tissue. To account for multiple comparisons, the FDR Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (FDR < 0.05) was applied.

Gene-based association analyses

The multi-marker analysis of genomic annotation (MAGMA) approach was applied to identify gene-level associations for each trait and to examine overlapping gene-level signals between depression and migraine [35]. Specifically, MAGMA was performed separately for each trait by incorporating LD between markers and testing aggregate multi-marker associations at the gene level. SNPs from the GWAS summary statistics were initially annotated to 19,427 protein-coding genes as defined in NCBI build 37.3. Subsequently, gene-level association tests were performed using the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 reference panel to identify independent associations. Finally, significant gene-based findings from each single trait were intersected to identify genes showing overlapping gene-level associations with both traits.

Two-sample Mendelian randomization

Instrument strength and power assessment

To explore potential causal relationships between depression and migraine, bidirectional MR analyses were conducted (Supplementary Methods). For the genetic instruments used in MR analyses, R2 was calculated to represent the proportion of variance in an exposure factor explained by the instrumental variables (IVs). F-statistic was calculated to represent the strength of the association between the instruments and risk of exposure of interest, and an F-statistic > 10 was considered indicative of strong instrument strength. MR power was calculated based on online web tools (https://sb452.shinyapps.io/power/).

Univariable MR analyses

The fixed-effect inverse-variance weighted (IVW) model was used as the primary analysis method to estimate the potential causal effect of exposure on outcome [36]. This approach combines SNP-specific Wald estimates under the assumption that all genetic instruments are valid and free of horizontal pleiotropy.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of the IVW estimates, we conducted leave-one-out tests and evaluated horizontal pleiotropy using the MR-Egger intercept [37]. Additional sensitivity analyses were performed using the MR-PRESSO [38], weighted median and MR-Egger methods to further examine the robustness of the results [37, 39].

Multivariable MR analyses

Multivariable Mendelian randomization (MVMR) analyses were performed to evaluate whether the observed associations remained consistent after adjustment for selected correlated traits. The analyses separately adjusted for antidepressant use, body mass index (BMI), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), smoking status, alcohol consumption, and sleep duration, which were selected based on prior evidence linking them to both depression and migraine and their availability in publicly accessible GWAS datasets [40–45].

All analyses were performed based on R software (version 4.1.2). The “TwoSampleMR” [46], “MendelianRandomization” [47], and “MRPRESSO” R packages [38] were used in the MR analyses.

Multiple testing correction

Multiple testing correction was applied across all analytical frameworks. For global genetic correlation analyses and MR analyses, Bonferroni correction was applied across nine phenotype-pair comparisons (P < 5.56 × 10− 3). For local genetic correlation analyses, Bonferroni correction was applied across 2,353 LD blocks (P < 2.12 × 10− 5). Genome-wide significance thresholds were applied for CPASSOC analyses (PCPASSOC <5 × 10− 8 with Psingle−trait <5 × 10− 3). Gene-based analyses used Bonferroni correction across all tested genes (P < 2.57 × 10− 6), while tissue enrichment analyses were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method (FDR < 0.05). Detailed thresholds are summarized in Table S2.

Results

Global and local genetic correlations between depression and migraine

Using LDSC, we found robust positive global genetic correlations between depression and all migraine subtypes, including overall migraine (rg=0.289, P = 3.36 × 10− 33), MA (rg=0.314, P = 1.12 × 10− 7), and MO (rg=0.245, P = 7.37 × 10− 9), all of which remained significant after multiple testing correction (Table 1). These patterns were consistent across MDD and PPD, with each showing significant correlations with migraine and its subtypes, with comparable effect sizes (Table 1). The LDSC intercepts ranged from 0.973 to 1.055 (Table S3). GNOVA produced concordant results, reinforcing the presence of substantial genome-wide genetic overlap between depression and migraine (Table 1).

Local correlation analysis using SUPERGNOVA further revealed several region-specific signals. A shared genetic region on chromosome 5 (90504842–93602613) showed significant correlations between depression and migraine/MO, as well as between MDD and migraine (Table S4, Fig. S1). Another shared region on chromosome 6 (94920611–97093037) showed significant correlations between MDD and both migraine and MO. Additional significant regions were detected on chromosome 7 (73997700–76550299) between depression and MA, on chromosome 2 (123244233–124730999) between PPD and MA, and on chromosome 16 (9659584–9997178) between PPD and MO (Table S4; Fig. S1).

Cross-trait meta-analysis of depression and migraine

As shown in Table S5, pairwise CPASSOC analyses identified 39 unique genome-wide significant pleiotropic loci across depression and migraine traits (PCPASSOC <5 × 10− 8; Psingle−trait <5 × 10− 3). Among these, 28 genome-wide significant loci showed pleiotropic associations between overall depression and migraine (Fig. 2; Table S5). The strongest locus was observed at rs30266 (PCPASSOC=5.86 × 10− 17), which is located within a chromosome 5 intergenic region. The second strongest signal, rs17303101 (PCPASSOC=5.27 × 10− 16), lies within ASTN2 locus on chromosome 9, a region previously implicated in neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders. The third most significant locus, rs12588874 (PCPASSOC=2.40 × 10− 12), is located on chromosome 14 and maps to a cluster of genes including AREL1, DLST, FCF1, PROX2, RPS6KL1, and YLPM1.

Subtype analyses further revealed additional trait-specific patterns of pleiotropy. One locus (rs67981811, PCPASSOC=4.72 × 10− 20) was shared between depression and MO (Fig. 2; Table S5). In MDD, four independent loci (rs76025409, rs727241, rs17303101, and rs28540738) showed pleiotropic associations with migraine, including rs17303101, which also represented a leading signal in the overall analysis. For PPD-migraine, a single locus (rs1885234 maps to ASTN2, PCPASSOC=2.56 × 10− 10) demonstrated a pleiotropic association with migraine (Fig. 2; Table S5).

Fine-mapping and colocalization of candidate shared loci

For each of the 39 pleiotropically associated loci identified through CPASSOC, we established 99% credible sets of causal variants using fine-mapping. As detailed in Table S6, credible sets varied across trait pairs, including 19 SNPs for overall depression-migraine, 60 SNPs for depression-MO, 11 SNPs for MDD-migraine, seven SNPs for MDD-MA, and 247 SNPs for MDD-MO.

Colocalization analysis (Table 2; Table S7) identified nine loci with evidence of colocalization (PPH4 > 0.50), among which two (rs11877758 and rs138709872) showed strong evidence for a shared causal variant (PPH4 > 0.8). Notably, the ASTN2 locus (indexed by rs17303101), showed suggestive evidence of colocalization (PPH4 = 0.63) and a 99% credible set comprising 12 variants.

Functional annotation and tissue enrichment analysis

To elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying the 39 pleiotropically associated loci identified by CPASSOC, we performed functional annotation (Tables S8-S11). VEP and HaploReg indicated that the majority of candidate shared variants were intronic or intergenic, with many overlapping regulatory chromatin marks, DNase I hypersensitive sites, or transcription factor motifs (Table S8; Table S9). Using 3DSNP, we identified 138 genes interacting with these pleiotropically associated SNPs through chromatin interactions across diverse cell types (Table S10). GTEx Portal analysis revealed 245 significant SNP-gene regulatory pairs (FDR < 0.05) across multiple tissues (Table S11). Tissue enrichment analysis further highlighted that the genes mapped from cross-trait associated loci between depression and migraine were preferentially expressed in several brain regions, particularly cortex-related tissues, underscoring neural pathways in their genetic overlap (Fig. S2).

Gene-based association analyses

To complement the SNP-level annotations, we next conducted gene-level association analyses using MAGMA. We identified 53 genes significantly associated with migraine (Table S12) and 93 genes associated with depression (Table S13). Intersection of single-trait MAGMA results highlighted two genes, ASTN2 and DLST, that showed significant gene-level associations with both depression and migraine (Table S14).

Mendelian randomization results of potential causal relationships

We conducted a bidirectional two-sample MR analysis to investigate the potential causal relationships between depression and migraine. As shown in Table S15, the R2 ranged from 0.82% to 1.15% for IVs of depressive-related exposures, whereas the R2 was smaller for IVs of migraine, particularly for MA and MO. Nevertheless, the genetic instruments showed adequate strength across all analyses, with F-statistics ranging from 19.13 to 93.82, all exceeding the conventional threshold of 10. Statistical power varied across analyses. Forward MR analyses for depression- and MDD-related exposures were generally well powered (power ≥ 90%), whereas the PPD-related analyses showed moderate power, ranging from approximately 55% to 61%. In reverse MR, analyses using overall migraine as the exposure were well powered (> 99%), however, analyses involving MO and MA were underpowered.

In the forward direction (Table 3; Table S16), genetically predicted depression liability was significantly associated with increased migraine risk (OR = 1.439, P = 6.09 × 10− 20), with consistent associations observed for MO (OR = 1.502, P = 4.73 × 10− 5) after multiple testing correction. Genetically predicted MDD liability was also significantly and positively associated with migraine (OR = 1.207, P = 6.69 × 10− 6) (Table 3). Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the primary findings (Table S16). Although depression showed nominal association with increased risk of MA (OR = 1.333, P = 0.010) and PPD exhibited a weak positive effect on migraine (OR = 1.007, P = 0.046), neither association survived Bonferroni correction or was supported by sensitivity analyses.

In the reverse direction analysis, genetically predicted migraine liability showed small but significant effects on depression (OR = 1.037, P = 2.56 × 10− 4) and MDD (OR = 1.066, P = 6.15 × 10− 4), with both associations remaining significant after multiple testing correction and corroborated by sensitivity analyses (Table 3; Table S16). Genetically predicted MA liability was also significantly associated with MDD risk (OR = 1.204, P = 3.05 × 10− 4), whereas the association between MO and MDD did not withstand Bonferroni correction (OR = 1.056, P = 0.007). No robust evidence supporting a potential effect on PPD risk was identified.

In MVMR analyses (Fig. 3; Fig. S3), the potential causal effect of depression on migraine remained consistent after adjustment for T2DM, BMI, current smoking, alcohol consumption per day, sleep duration, or antidepressant use, with effect estimates broadly similar to those from the univariable MR (OR range: 1.439–1.585) (Fig. 3A; Fig. S3A). Comparable patterns were observed for the effects of depression on MO and for the effect of MDD on migraine, with the estimates remaining broadly similar after adjustment for the selected correlated traits.

In contrast, the association between genetically predicted migraine and depression remained significant after adjusting for BMI (P = 0.001), but was attenuated to the null when accounting for T2DM, current smoking, alcohol consumption, sleep duration, or antidepressant use (all P > 0.05) (Fig. 3B; Fig. S3B). Similar attenuation patterns were observed for the migraine-to-MDD and MO-to-MDD associations after adjusting for the same covariates (Fig. 3B; Fig. S3B).

Discussion

This large-scale genome-wide cross-trait study demonstrates substantial genetic overlap and an asymmetric pattern of potential causal relationships between depression and migraine. By integrating genetic correlation analyses, cross-trait meta-analysis and bidirectional MR, we identified 39 pleiotropically associated loci enriched in neural pathways, two of which showed strong evidence of colocalization, and observed stronger MR evidence from depression to migraine than in the reverse direction. These results advance the understanding of this clinically important comorbidity and provide a basis for future mechanistic and translational investigation.

At the genome-wide level, our findings of significant genetic correlations between depression and migraine (rg=0.289) align with prior reports (rg~0.2–0.3) [19, 48]. Notably, our subtype-specific analyses show a similar pattern, with stronger correlations observed for MA (rg=0.314) compared to MO (rg=0.245), consistent with the pattern reported in the Brainstorm Consortium, where MA (rg=0.28) also showed stronger correlations than MO (rg=0.23) [19]. This observation is directionally consistent with clinical reports that MA patients display heightened psychiatric burden [7], potentially reflecting diverging neurobiological underpinnings between migraine subtypes. These results underscore the value of phenotypic specification in cross-trait genomic studies, rather than treating depression and migraine as unitary constructs.

At the locus level, Bahrami et al. previously identified 14 jointly associated loci using MiXeR and conjunctional FDR approaches [20]. Using a different analytical framework (CPASSOC), our cross-trait meta-analyses identified 39 genome-wide significant pleiotropically associated loci across depression-related phenotypes and migraine-related phenotypes. Among these loci, several map to regions previously reported, including ASTN2, PAX5, and MAD1L1, indicating partial overlap with earlier findings. In contrast, the majority of loci were not reported in Bahrami et al., suggesting that our analysis captures additional candidate shared genetic signals beyond those identified in prior studies. Importantly, rs11877758 which maps to the CELF4 gene, showed the strongest evidence of colocalization (PPH4 = 0.869). CELF4 encodes an RNA-binding protein belonging to the CELF/BRUNOL family, which plays a key role in post-transcriptional regulation [49]. Experimental studies have implicated CELF4 in neuronal excitability, synaptic function, and sensory neuron sensitivity, processes relevant to both migraine biology and neuropsychiatric vulnerability [50, 51]. In addition, recent GWAS have also implicated CELF4 in migraine-related traits [52]. Thus, CELF4 may represent a data-driven candidate locus for shared genetic mechanisms between depression and migraine, although further functional validation is still required.

In addition, ASTN2 should be interpreted more cautiously. Although two loci (rs17303101 and rs1885234) mapped to ASTN2 showed support across cross-trait meta-analysis, fine-mapping, and overlapping gene-level analyses, the colocalization evidence for rs17303101 was suggestive rather than strong (PPH4 = 0.630). Therefore, this locus may reflect either a shared causal signal or LD-driven overlap. ASTN2 encodes astrotactin-2, a neuronal protein involved in neurodevelopmental processes, particularly neuronal migration, and synaptic function [17]. Previous GWAS have implicated ASTN2 (rs6478241) in migraine susceptibility, suggesting possible involvement in pain-related neural pathways [48]. In addition, genetic studies have linked ASTN2 variation to the reduced hippocampal volume, a structure integral to emotional regulation and nociceptive modulation [53]. These observations suggest that ASTN2 may represent a biologically plausible candidate. However, its specific role in the shared genetic architecture between depression and migraine remains hypothesis-generating and requires experimental validation.

In terms of MR-based causal inference, bidirectional MR analysis suggested an asymmetric relationship between depression and migraine. The estimated effect from depression to migraine was stronger and more consistent across MR methods, whereas reverse-direction estimates were smaller and less robust. This pattern is partly consistent with previous evidence. Epidemiological studies have consistently reported strong comorbidity and have often suggested a bidirectional association [7, 9]. In contrast, a recent bidirectional MR study reported a significant effect from major depression to migraine, but found little evidence supporting potential reverse causality without covariate adjustment [21]. One possible explanation for the difference may relate to statistical power and instrument strength. These MR findings should be interpreted cautiously. Although the forward analyses were generally well powered, several subtype-specific and reverse-direction analyses had limited power, which may reduce precision and limit the detection of modest effects. In addition, the attenuation of reverse associations in MVMR suggested that part of the observed bidirectional signal may be influenced by selected correlated traits or shared risk pathways, although alternative explanations such as adjustment for intermediate variables cannot be excluded. Moreover, residual horizontal pleiotropy and violation of the exclusion restriction assumption cannot be completely excluded despite sensitivity analyses. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as providing evidence consistent with potential causal relationships, rather than definitive proof of causality.

Subtype-specific analyses suggested potential heterogeneity between migraine subtypes. MR analyses indicated a relatively stronger and more consistent effect for MO, whereas genetic correlation analyses showed differences in magnitude across subtypes. Biologically, this may reflect differences in underlying mechanisms, with MA being more closely linked to vascular dysregulation [2], whereas MO may be more tightly linked to central neural sensitization. However, these interpretations remain speculative and require further validation.

Compared with previous cross-trait genomic studies of depression and migraine, the present study extends the field by integrating PPD, a clinically distinct and relatively underexplored subtype, into a comprehensive cross-trait genomic framework. Although PPD showed significant global and local genetic correlations with migraine, particularly for MA (rg=0.376), the MR analyses did not provide strong evidence supporting a causal relationship after multiple testing correction. These findings suggest that PPD may share aspects of genetic susceptibility with migraine while potentially involving distinct biological mechanisms. PPD is characterized by abrupt endocrine withdrawal, shifts in neuroimmune activation, and transient monoaminergic instability, all of which interact with migraine-relevant pathways. Therefore, PPD may provide a useful clinical context for investigating how hormonal transitions influence neuropsychiatric and pain-related vulnerability. In addition, our study further extends prior work by integrating fine-mapping and colocalization analyses to distinguish statistical cross-trait associations from loci with stronger evidence for potential shared causal signals, and by applying both bidirectional and multivariable MR analyses to further evaluate the robustness and potential directionality of the observed cross-trait associations.

These findings may inform future mechanistic and translational investigations. The MR results support further investigation of depression-related liability as a possible contributor to migraine susceptibility, particularly in genetically informed and longitudinal study designs. Candidate loci such as CELF4 may help prioritize biological hypotheses for future functional validation in patients with depressive symptoms, including postpartum women.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, our analyses were primarily based on cohorts of European ancestry, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Second, potential trait heterogeneity in depression measures may attenuate subtype-specific signals, especially for PPD, which currently lacks large, well-powered GWAS. Third, while MR minimizes confounding, residual horizontal pleiotropy remains possible despite sensitivity analyses. Fourth, potential sample overlap between exposure and outcome GWAS datasets cannot be completely excluded, particularly through shared cohorts such as the UK Biobank. Sample overlap may inflate association estimates toward observational correlations and could potentially influence multiple analytical components of the present study, including LDSC, cross-trait meta-analysis, colocalization, and MR analyses. Although LDSC intercepts were examined to assess potential inflation in genetic correlation analyses, and strong genetic instruments were used to reduce weak-instrument bias in MR analyses, these approaches do not fully eliminate bias related to participant overlap. Therefore, residual overlap-related bias may still be present and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Fifth, statistical power was limited in several subtype-specific MR analyses, particularly those involving MA and MO, due to small numbers of instruments, low variance explained, and highly imbalanced case-control ratios. Therefore, null findings from these analyses should be interpreted as inconclusive and require validation in larger GWAS datasets. Sixth, the MVMR analyses should be interpreted cautiously because the selected covariates may not fully represent the complex network of correlated phenotypes underlying depression and migraine, and some included variables may partly represent downstream mediators rather than true confounders. Finally, although our functional annotations highlight plausible biological mechanisms, such as synaptic plasticity and neuroinflammation, experimental validation is necessary to confirm the roles of prioritized genes (e.g., CELF4) in comorbid pathophysiology. Further studies integrating longitudinal phenotyping, hormone measurements, and multi-omics data are needed to validate these pathways.

Conclusion

In summary, this study advances current knowledge of the genetic relationships between depression and migraine by identifying pleiotropically associated loci, providing MR-based evidence consistent with an asymmetric pattern of potential causal relationships, and incorporating postpartum depression as a clinically relevant subtype. Overall, the findings support stronger evidence for a potential depression-to-migraine effect, while weaker reverse-direction associations should not be disregarded. These findings improve our understanding of depression-migraine comorbidity and provide a genomic basis for future mechanistic and translational investigations. Future research should focus on validating these associations in larger and more diverse datasets and functionally characterizing prioritized loci before clinical translation can be considered.
