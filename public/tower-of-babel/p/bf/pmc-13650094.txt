LAMP2A-dependent chaperone mediated autophagy regulates Notch signaling and controls neural stem cell differentiation
Authors: Daphne Antoniou, Maria Xilouri, Dimitrios Gkikas, Dimitrios Vlachakis, Matina Tsampoula, Nikos Malissovas, Konstantina Mastori, Valeria Kaltezioti, Elpinickie Ninou, Christina Kyrousi, Stavros Taraviras, Leonidas Stefanis, Panagiotis K. Politis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Protein degradation systems have profound effects on signaling pathways and molecular networks that regulate organogenesis. Chaperone Mediated Autophagy (CMA) has been linked to neurodegenerative and neurodevelopmental diseases, however, its role in mammalian brain development remains poorly understood. Here, we identify a regulatory role of CMA pathway in neural stem cell (NSC) differentiation. We show that CMA is active in NSCs, while expression of LAMP2A, the principal limiting component of CMA, is associated with the induction of neurogenesis. Most importantly, overexpression and loss-of-function experiments of LAMP2A in NSCs suggest an inductive role of CMA in neuronal differentiation. Moreover, we demonstrate that LAMP2A is involved in NSC fate decisions by interfering with NOTCH1 signaling pathway. Our data support a model in which LAMP2A-dependent CMA contributes to neuronal fate acquisition through suppression of NOTCH1 signaling, and provide a mechanistic framework that may help explain the reported genetic involvement of LAMP2 gene and CMA in neurodevelopmental disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06330-x.

Introduction

In the last few decades, many signaling pathways and molecular networks have been associated with the ability of neural stem cells (NSCs) to generate the enormous cellular complexity of the mammalian brain [1]. Notably, NSC fate decisions depend not only on signal identity, but also on the strength and duration of pathway activation, which is largely controlled by the intracellular protein degradation pathways. Despite this progress, the implication of intracellular protein degradation pathways in the molecular mechanisms that regulate NSC properties for self-maintenance, proliferation and differentiation remains less well characterized.

Autophagy is a lysosomal-based, physiological and highly conserved intracellular degradation pathway, essential for the maintenance of cellular homeostasis [2], and is implicated in a broad range of brain-related diseases, including neurodevelopmental disorders. The main autophagic systems are macroautophagy, microautophagy and Chaperone Mediated Autophagy (CMA). While macroautophagy is a general degradation system by which damaged proteins or organelles are transported through vacuoles into the lysosome [3], CMA is a highly selective, non-vesicular process. In particular, it targets specific proteins with the KFERQ-like recognition motif, or a biochemically-related one, in their amino acid sequence [4] and directly directs them to lysosomal integration. This motif is recognized by HSPA8/heat shock protein family member 8 (also known as HSC70) that mediates the localization of the targeted proteins to the lysosomal membrane. This complex binds to the monomeric form of the lysosome-associated membrane protein type 2 A (LAMP2A), which then multimerizes and acts as a receptor of the pathway. LAMP2A constitutes the principal limiting component of the CMA pathway [3, 5]. Most importantly, while macroautophagy has been shown to directly affect differentiation of NSCs and mammalian brain development [6, 7], the role of CMA has not been studied in this context.

Interestingly, human mutations in the LAMP2 gene have been linked to Danon disease, a condition that combines cardiomyopathy and intellectual dysfunction [8, 9]. It has also been reported that LAMP2 gene mutations are associated with X-linked intellectual disability, a developmental brain disorder [10]. Moreover, reduced activity of the pathway is observed in neurodegenerative conditions such as Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease and certain tauopathies, whereas increased levels of LAMP2A and thus increased CMA activity are detected in various cancer cells [11–13]. In general, although CMA dysregulation is correlated to many brain-related diseases, including neurodevelopmental disorders [14], nothing is known about its physiological role in brain development.

These data raised the intriguing hypothesis for a possible implication of the CMA pathway in central nervous system organogenesis. Consistent with this hypothesis, we show here that LAMP2A is expressed in the mammalian brain during development, associated with neurogenesis, and that the CMA pathway is active in NSCs. Gain- and loss-of-function studies in NSCs further suggest that LAMP2A promotes neuronal differentiation. Mechanistically, our data support a model in which LAMP2A-dependent CMA degradation pathway is a negative regulator of NOTCH1 signaling activity in NSCs. Because LAMP2A is the principal limiting component of CMA, we use its manipulation as a functional entry point into the pathway, and interpret these findings together with biochemical experiments that distinguish CMA from macroautophagy.

Results

LAMP2A expression is correlated to neuronal lineage in the developing cortex

To examine the involvement of CMA in neural development, we initially investigated the expression pattern of LAMP2A in the embryonic cortex of mouse and rat brain (Fig. 1). For this purpose, we used an anti-LAMP2A antibody against a unique epitope of LAMP2A isoform that specifically recognizes this protein [9]. Interestingly, higher expression levels of LAMP2A were observed in the mouse outer cortical layers (marginal zone, MZ) (i.e. arrowhead in Fig. 1B), as compared to inner layers (ventricular zone, VZ) (i.e. arrow in Fig. 1B), while immunostainings with only secondary antibodies gave no signal (Figure S1A). In particular, LAMP2A was mainly detected in TUBB3+ (βΙΙΙ-Tubulin+) neurons of the MZ (i.e. brackets in Fig. 1C) and to a lesser extent in neural stem/progenitor populations of the VZ, as indicated by co-staining with NESTIN as well as the cortical progenitor markers PAX6 and SOX2 (Figure S2A-B and Figure S3). These data indicate that, although LAMP2A is also present in VZ neural stem/progenitor cells at E12.5, its expression is enriched in the neuronal lineage and is associated with the induction of neuronal differentiation. Consistently, the expression domain of LAMP2A is gradually expanded as embryonic development proceeds from E12.5 to E17.5 and newly produced TUBB3 + neurons are generated and migrated towards the cortical plate (Fig. 1A-G). Similar expression pattern was observed in the rat cortex during embryonic development (Fig. 1H-L and Figure S2B). Next, we asked whether the CMA pathway is active in NSCs during embryonic development. To this end, we employed an ex vivo culture system using NSCs derived from rat developing cortex, which have the ability to self-renew and proliferate, as well as to differentiate into neurons and astrocytes upon withdrawal of growth factors (GFs) [15–17]. We first studied the expression of the two basic components of CMA, LAMP2A and HSPA8. Both components were highly expressed at the mRNA level as compared to the Nestin gene, a neural stem cell marker (Fig. 2A, B). We were also able to detect expression of the related proteins (Fig. 2C and Figure S1B), although at a lower expression level as compared to the adult cortex, a brain region previously reported to exhibit enhanced CMA activity [18–21]. These expression data indicate that the core molecular components of the CMA pathway are present in NSCs. To determine whether CMA is functionally active, we next measured lysosomal-dependent proteolysis using a pulse-chase assay. This analysis showed that CMA activity is readily detectable in NSCs, at levels comparable to macroautophagy under our experimental conditions (Fig. 2D). Interestingly, although it is well-established that macroautophagy critically regulates neural differentiation [6, 7, 22], the involvement of CMA is unknown. The observation that CMA is active in NSCs and the in vivo immunostaining data (Fig. 1), implied a regulatory action in neuronal differentiation. To initially address this, we examined the expression pattern of LAMP2A in neural cell types, derived from ex vivo cultured NSCs. In agreement with the in vivo results, these experiments displayed higher expression of LAMP2A in TUBB3 + neurons, compared to GFAP+ astrocytes (Fig. 2G, H, I), and lower expression in NESTIN + or SOX2 + proliferating NSCs (Fig. 2E, F, I). Notably, the restricted expression of LAMP2A to only a subset of SOX2 + or NESTIN+ cells suggests heterogeneity within the proliferating NSC population. This observation, together with the limited LAMP2A+ population in the VZ in vivo and the stronger enrichment of LAMP2A in TUBB3 + cells (Figs. 1, S2, and S3), is consistent with the idea that LAMP2A becomes induced in progenitors that have initiated the neuronal differentiation process. Moreover, co-stainings with the LAMP1 lysosomal marker showed extended co-localization with the signal from the anti-LAMP2A antibody in ex vivo cultured NSCs (Figure S1B-C), further confirming our antibody specificity. LAMP2A is necessary and sufficient to induce differentiation of NSCs towards TUBB3 + neurons

To test this hypothesis, we performed lentiviral-mediated shRNA knockdown of LAMP2A with two different sequences targeting the rat Lamp2a mRNA (Fig. 3A-C). Remarkably, under differentiation conditions of ex vivo-cultured NSCs [3 days in vitro (DIV) in a conditional medium without growth factors], shRNA-mediated knockdown of LAMP2A (LV1) caused a significant reduction in the number of TUBB3 + neurons, which indicates that LAMP2A deficiency impairs neuronal differentiation (Fig. 3D, E). The same effect of LAMP2A knockdown on neuronal differentiation was observed with the second shRNA construct (LV2) (% of GFP+ cells that are TUBB3+: shSCR = 10.18 ± 1.2 versus shLV2 = 6.86 ± 2.2, P < 0.001, n = 4). Conversely, no significant differences were observed in the proportions of GFAP+ astrocytes (Fig. 3F, G), RIP+ oligodendrocytes (Fig. 3H, I), or NESTIN+ NSCs (Fig. 3J, K) under these conditions. In addition, to test whether the reduction in TUBB3 + cells was due to shRNA-induced apoptosis, we investigated its effect on the activated CASPASE 3 marker for cell death as well as on Ethidium Homodimer (EthD-1) fluorescence marker for dead cells. No significant changes were noticed in these cell death indices (Fig. 3L-O). We further examined the correlation between the reduction of TUBB3 + neurons and apoptosis each day during the differentiation protocol after lentiviral infection. Neuronal cells were significantly decreased only 3 days after lentiviral infection, whereas activated CASPASE 3 was slightly increased during the second day of the time-course experiment (Fig. 3P, Q). In this time interval, double TUBB3/activated CASPASE 3 immunostainings indicated that this increase was not due to the death of TUBB3 + neurons (Fig. 3R). Because reduced neurogenesis after LAMP2A knockdown could also reflect altered NSC proliferation, we examined mitotic activity by phospho-histone H3 (pH3) immunostaining. The pH3 index did not differ significantly between shLAMP2A and shSCR GFP+ NSCs under proliferation conditions (Figure S4). We next addressed whether LAMP2A overexpression is sufficient to affect differentiation towards the neuronal phenotype. AMAXA electroporation in ex vivo-cultured NSCs using a LAMP2A expression plasmid, revealed a significant induction in the number of TUBB3 + neurons, whereas the proportion of GFAP+ astrocytes was not significantly changed (Fig. 4A-C), in agreement with the shRNA data. Similarly, adenoviral-mediated overexpression of LAMP2A, previously shown to induce CMA activity [11, 23–25] was adequate to enhance neuronal differentiation of NSCs (Fig. 4D-I). Overall, these data show that LAMP2A is sufficient and necessary to promote neuronal differentiation of NSCs. Given that LAMP2A is the principal limiting component of CMA, these findings are consistent with a role for LAMP2A-dependent CMA in this process. LAMP2A suppresses NOTCH1 signaling pathway to induce neuronal differentiation

To explore the possible mechanism of LAMP2A-dependent CMA action on the induction of neuronal lineage progression, we examined the effect of LAMP2A overexpression on various molecular markers, characteristic for the transition of NSCs from progenitor cell stage to post-mitotic neurons. Interestingly, adenoviral-mediated overexpression of LAMP2A induced NeuroD1 and Rbfox3 (NeuN) neuronal markers, and down-regulated Foxg1, Pax6, Tbr2 and Ngn2 intermediate neuronal progenitor markers (Fig. 4J). A subset of these genes was also affected by the shRNA-mediated knockdown of the Lamp2a gene (Figure S5). Most importantly, a major reduction under overexpression conditions was observed on the mRNA levels of Sox2 and Hes5 genes, which are markers for self-renewing NSCs. These observations indicate the involvement of LAMP2A and by extension of CMA in the suppression of NSC identity and consequently induction of their neuronal differentiation. Since down-regulation of Sox2 and Hes5 genes is one of the earliest events marking the initiation of neurogenesis [26, 27], we hypothesized that LAMP2A may directly affect the expression of these genes to induce neuronal differentiation.

To explore this scenario, we first tested whether CMA degradation pathway directly affects the stability of SOX2 protein. It has been previously shown that SOX2 transcription factor is able to bind and activate the transcription of its own gene by a positive feedback loop [28, 29]. Therefore, we wanted to examine whether CMA directly targets SOX2 protein and consequently disrupts its feedback loop, which could also explain the observed reduction of Sox2 mRNA levels. Such a hypothesis would predict that down-regulation of protein expression will precede the reduction of mRNA levels. However, we were not able to observe such discrimination during the time-course of adeno-viral mediated LAMP2A infection experiment (Fig. 5A, B). Both mRNA and protein levels were similarly reduced as early as 24 h after initiation of the experiment (Fig. 5A, B). Furthermore, LAMP2A overexpression was not sufficient to target exogenously expressed SOX2 protein by a CMV promoter-driven construct (Fig. 5C). Next, we investigated whether NOTCH1 signaling pathway is regulated by LAMP2A modulation, since Sox2 is directly regulated at the transcriptional level by NOTCH1, and Hes5 is a downstream effector of this pathway [30–32]. Consistent with this scenario, LAMP2A overexpression was sufficient to impair the well-established ability of constitutively active NOTCH1 isoform [Notch1-intracellular domain (NICD)] to transactivate the Hes5 promoter (Fig. 5D, E). Moreover, adenoviral-mediated LAMP2A overexpression significantly reduced the endogenous NICD protein levels in NSCs (Fig. 5F, G), suggesting that CMA pathway targets NICD at the protein level. In agreement, Notch1 mRNA levels were not decreased but were significantly increased, possibly due to a feedback cellular response to the reduced NICD protein levels (Fig. 5H).

To further investigate this effect of LAMP2A on NICD protein, we tested whether the CMA pathway significantly contributes to NICD protein degradation. To this end, we applied selective inhibitors of lysosomal and proteasomal pathways in ex vivo cultured NSCs. First, we showed that inhibition of total lysosomal activity by NH4Cl caused dramatic accumulation of endogenous NICD protein as compared to control conditions (ctr), whereas inhibition of proteasomal activity by epoxomicin did not have such an effect (Fig. 5I, J). These data suggest that, under our ex vivo NSC culturing conditions, NICD turnover is more strongly associated with lysosomal degradation than with proteasomal degradation machinery. However, taking into consideration that macroautophagy and CMA are the two main lysosomal degradation systems, we next applied on NSCs the selective inhibitor for macroautophagy 3 methyl-adenine (3MA) to discriminate between these two pathways. Conversely to the outcome of the NH4Cl inhibitor, 3MA did not increase NICD protein levels under our ex vivo cortical NSC conditions, arguing against a major contribution of canonical macroautophagy to NICD turnover in this experimental setting and favoring a role for LAMP2A-dependent CMA (Fig. 5I, J). The ability of 3MA and NH4Cl to inhibit macroautophagy and total lysosomal activity, respectively, was verified with LC3-II flux measurement (Fig. 5I, K). Consistently, by applying the autophagic inhibitors in exogenously supplied NICD, we further confirmed the lysosomal-mediated degradation of NICD via the CMA pathway (Fig. 5L, M). In particular, we showed that accumulation of exogenously supplied NICD is induced by inhibiting total lysosomal activity with NH4Cl but not by inhibiting macroautophagy with the specific inhibitor 3MA (Fig. 5L, M), in a manner similar to the endogenous NICD (Fig. 5I, J). Furthermore, under the same experimental conditions, there were no CMA-dependent differences on Sox2 protein levels (Figure S6), confirming our previous findings on the indirect effect of LAMP2A on Sox2 gene expression.

Ιn agreement with these observations, siRNA-mediated knockdown of Atg5 did not alter NICD protein levels under these conditions (Fig. 6A-C), while analysis of the LC3-II flux confirmed inhibition of macroautophagy (Fig. 6A, D), further indicating that canonical macroautophagy is not the predominant route of NICD degradation in our ex vivo NSC system. Given that CMA substrates are recognized and bound by the HSPA8, the LAMP2A interacting partner in CMA protein machinery, we wanted to examine whether endogenous NICD is co-localized with HSPA8 and/or LAMP1 lysosomal marker. Accordingly, immunostainings in NSCs indicate that NICD with HSPA8 or LAMP1 proteins are distributed in the same sub-cellular compartments with a high degree of co-localization (Pearson correlation coefficiency value for HSPA8 and LAMP1 are 0.685 ± 0.033 and 0.545 ± 0.07, respectively) (Fig. 6E). To confirm that NICD is a CMA substrate, we proceeded with the gold standard method used for in vitro reconstitution of CMA activity [33, 34]. To that end, cell produced human flag-NICD (overexpression in human HEK293T cells) was first purified and then incubated, in the presence of recombinant HSPA8, with lysosomes isolated from adult rat brain, in the presence (+) or absence (-) of protease inhibitors. Immunoblot analysis indicates that flag-NICD was efficiently bound to and uptaken into the lysosomes through the CMA degradation pathway (Fig. 6F, G). In the presence of protease inhibitors reduced ability of degradation of NICD was observed, further suggesting that NICD is a CMA substrate. These data support a model in which NICD can be translocated and degraded into lysosomes via a LAMP2A/CMA-dependent pathway [35]. In addition, to evaluate whether the observed LAMP2A effect on inducing neuronal differentiation is mediated by its regulatory action on NICD degradation, we performed overexpression experiments with both factors in NSCs. The aim of these experiments was to test whether overexpression of NICD is able to rescue the effect of LAMP2A on neuronal differentiation. We initially confirmed that under our experimental conditions (ratio of NICD vs. LAMP2A vectors = 2/1) the majority of LAMP2A-IRES-GFP positive cells were also positive for exogenously supplied NICD-myc (arrows in Figure S7A). More importantly, overexpression of NICD together with LAMP2A significantly counteracted the LAMP2A-induced increase in neuronal differentiation (TUBB3 index in Figure S7B, C). We also showed that overexpression of LAMP2A can reverse the effect of NICD on neuronal differentiation (Figure S7D, E) by using a higher amount of LAMP2A expression vector as compared to NICD.

Next, we asked whether NOTCH1 contains a CMA-recognition motif in its amino acid sequence, since CMA substrates are typically recognized by HSPA8 through KFERQ-like motifs and delivered to lysosomes for degradation [36–39]. A conventional sequence search did not identify a canonical KFERQ-like motif in full-length human or mouse NOTCH1. We therefore applied a multidimensional bioinformatic analysis based on physicochemical similarity to known KFERQ-like motifs and identified a single atypical candidate motif, KRRRQ, located 6 amino acid residues after the beginning of the NICD region (Figure S8A, B). This motif is highly conserved among vertebrate species, supporting its possible functional importance. To assess its relevance, we generated a mutant NICD construct in which the five residues of the KRRRQ motif were replaced by alanines. In contrast to wild-type NICD, the mutant NICD protein did not display the same pattern of accumulation following inhibition of lysosomal activity and macroautophagy (compare Figure S8C, D with Fig. 5L, M). These findings support the view that the conserved KRRRQ motif contributes to the lysosomal/CMA-dependent turnover of NICD.

Discussion

During cortical development a complex network of interactions between proteins, complexes and pathways regulates the acquisition of neural cell fates. For many of these factors post-translational availability is controlled in a spatiotemporal manner. Autophagic pathways play significant roles in these regulatory cascades through the degradation of unwanted proteins in order to maintain cellular homeostasis and differentiation potential in NSCs [42]. In agreement, it has been previously shown that macroautophagy is critically involved in cortical neurogenesis. Knockdown experiments of Atg5, a gene encoding an essential protein for the formation of autophagosomes, led to an increase in neural progenitor cells and inhibition of differentiation during cortical development. Additionally, suppression of Atg5 caused dendritic and axonal morphological impairment [6, 22]. Consistently, Fimia G. M. et al. showed that genomic ablation of macroautophagy-related genes resulted in aberrant formation of nervous system and unbalanced cell proliferation during development of mouse embryo [7, 42–45]. In addition, macroautophagy is strictly connected to apoptosis during the early stages of brain morphogenesis [46, 47]. In contrast to the wealth of information on the role of macroautophagy in neural development, the involvement of CMA remains unclear. Notably, regulation of NOTCH1 signaling by macroautophagy has been reported previously, including evidence that this pathway regulates NOTCH1 degradation and modulates stem cell development and neurogenesis [6]. Although this may appear to differ from our 3MA and Atg5-loss-of-function data, the two studies likely interrogate distinct autophagic routes and/or cellular contexts. Wu et al. examined canonical macroautophagy-associated trafficking of Notch through ATG16L1-positive autophagosome precursor vesicles, whereas our data in differentiating cortical NSCs indicate that, under the specific conditions tested here, NICD turnover is more strongly linked to LAMP2A-dependent CMA than to canonical macroautophagy. We therefore do not exclude that macroautophagy also contributes to NOTCH1 regulation in other developmental stages, subcellular compartments, or stem/progenitor contexts, but our data support the view that CMA represents an additional and functionally relevant mechanism of NICD control in cortical NSCs. Here, we also extend these findings by providing functional evidence that the selective LAMP2A-dependent CMA protein degradation pathway contributes to NSC fate determination. In particular, we show that the CMA pathway is active in NSCs. Moreover, LAMP2A, which constitutes the principal limiting component of the CMA pathway [3, 5], is expressed in mouse and rat cortex during embryonic development and is highly associated with the neuronal lineage. Interestingly, LAMP2A was detected only in a minority of SOX2 + and NESTIN+ proliferating NSCs and in a subset of VZ progenitors in vivo, suggesting that LAMP2A activity may not be a uniform feature of all neural progenitors but may instead characterize a transitional subpopulation poised to exit the stem cell state and initiate neuronal differentiation. Most importantly, it is involved in the acquisition of early neuronal identity, indicating a significant role for this degradation system in cortical development. Specifically, we demonstrate that LAMP2A protein is required for NSCs differentiation towards TUBB3 + neurons in ex vivo differentiation assays. Although we observed non-significant trends in the GFAP+ astrocytic population after LAMP2A manipulation, these data do not support a firm conclusion that LAMP2A directly regulates astrogliogenesis or induces a broad cell fate switch between neuronal and astrocytic lineages.

Interestingly, previous studies suggest that the LAMP2 gene is correlated with neuronal deficiencies and intellectual dysfunction in the human brain. It has been reported that mutations on the LAMP2 gene, mainly affecting mRNA splicing, can cause Danon disease (OMIM #300257), which is characterized by mental retardation and other symptoms [8, 48]. Danon disease is an X-linked dominant multisystem disorder caused by loss-of-function mutations in LAMP2 (located at Xq24), clinically defined by hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, skeletal myopathy, and intellectual disability, the latter affecting approximately 70% of male patients. In addition, LAMP2 gene has been identified in a large scale human exome sequencing as one of the genes associated with X-linked intellectual disabilities [10]. These reports are consistent with our findings in rodent embryonic cortex, concerning the necessity of LAMP2A for neural differentiation. To further elucidate the neuropathology of neurodevelopmental disorders such as Danon disease, previous studies used as a model LAMP2-deficient mice. Histological and behavioral analyses of the adult mice revealed wide spread astrogliogenesis in the CNS as well as behavioral anomalies [9]. Taken together, these data are in good agreement with our observations and further support our hypothesis that LAMP2A receptor and consequently CMA is critically implicated in the early stages of neurodevelopment.

In this study, we identified LAMP2A as a critical regulator of Sox2 expression and NOTCH1 signaling, two major suppressors for the initiation of neurogenesis in NSCs [13, 49–53]. NOTCH1 activity is strongly associated with the control of proliferation, differentiation and apoptosis thus affecting morphogenesis of different organs, including nervous system. Concerning neural development, NOTCH1 is a master regulator of the balance between stemness and neuronal differentiation. Due to its vital role in neural cell fate acquisition, NOTCH1 activation is a highly regulated process at multiple levels and consists an extremely conserved molecular mechanism. Here, we propose that LAMP2A-dependent CMA modulates the equilibrium of NICD protein levels in NSCs to relieve the negative action of NOTCH1 signaling on the initiation of neuronal differentiation. In particular, we show that induction of the activity of the CMA pathway by LAMP2A overexpression is sufficient to strongly reduce Sox2 expression and NOTCH1 signaling activity in ex vivo cultured NSCs as well as in vivo, in the mouse developing cortex. Moreover, in the embryonic cortex, LAMP2A overexpression decreased the proportion of electroporated GFP+ cells remaining in the VZ/SVZ and increased their distribution within the adjacent TUBB3 + neuronal zone, consistent with enhanced progression toward neuronal differentiation in vivo. These observations are consistent with an effect of LAMP2A on neuronal differentiation of cortical progenitor cells within the embryonic cortex, but the current IUE experimental design does not allow us to define with precision the final neuronal subtype or cortical layer identity of the affected cells. It will therefore be important in future studies to determine whether LAMP2A also affects later aspects of cortical development, including neuronal maturation and subtype specification. In addition, by a series of biochemical experiments and transcriptional assays, we first excluded a direct effect of CMA on SOX2 protein and consequently demonstrated that manipulation of CMA activity alters the levels of activated form of NOTCH1, e.g. NICD. Consistently, we showed that NICD is co-localized at the same cellular compartments with HSPA8, the mediator for the CMA target binding and can be degraded by an in vitro system of isolated lysosomes, in a CMA-dependent manner. Although previous studies have implicated the ubiquitin–proteasome system in NICD degradation [54–56], our epoxomicin data suggest that, under the specific murine NSC cultured conditions used here, the lysosomal-CMA route represents an additional and significant contributor to NICD turnover, complementing the proteasomal mechanisms described in other cellular contexts. In addition, our bioinformatic motif analysis found a conserved atypical KFERQ-like sequence in the NICD amino acid sequence, and mutation of this motif altered the lysosomal/CMA-associated accumulation pattern of exogenous NICD, further supporting an interaction of NICD with the CMA machinery. Although these data suggest a functional contribution of the KRRRQ motif to NICD CMA-linked turnover, we cannot exclude that additional sequence features or structural determinants also participate in NICD recognition by the CMA machinery. We therefore suggest that our findings define a more specific LAMP2A-dependent CMA mechanism contributing to the regulation of NOTCH1 signaling during neuronal differentiation. In support of this proposed mechanism, overexpression of constitutively active NOTCH1 was sufficient to rescue the action of LAMP2A on neuronal differentiation. Together with previous evidence that canonical macroautophagy regulates NOTCH1 degradation and modulates stem cell development and neurogenesis [6], our findings support the view that distinct autophagic pathways can converge on NOTCH1 signaling in a context-dependent manner during brain development. Moreover, an important issue here is the degree of specificity that can be exclusively assigned to CMA. Much of our functional observations are based on manipulation of LAMP2A, the principal limiting component of the CMA pathway. We therefore consider that LAMP2A gain- and loss-of-function cannot totally exclude broader effects on lysosomal homeostasis. However, a number of independent observations performed in this study support the interpretation that the NOTCH1-related effects are linked specifically to CMA rather than broader lysosomal effects: CMA is active in NSCs in pulse-chase assays, NICD accumulation is induced by inhibition of total lysosomal function but not by 3-MA-mediated inhibition of macroautophagy, Atg5 knockdown does not increase NICD levels, endogenous NICD colocalizes with HSPA8 and LAMP1, and purified NICD is taken up by isolated lysosomes in an in vitro CMA reconstitution assay. Thus, our data strongly support a LAMP2A-dependent CMA mechanism, while broader lysosomal contributions cannot be completely ruled out.

To conclude, our results indicate that the LAMP2A-dependent CMA pathway is an important regulator of NSC differentiation during neurodevelopment. Our data suggest that LAMP2A-dependent CMA is implicated in the degradation of NOTCH1 protein, thus affecting the differentiation of NSCs and consequently brain physiology. Although our experimental evidence strongly favors a CMA-dependent mechanism, we cannot fully exclude that some effects of LAMP2A modulation reflect broader lysosomal regulation. Future studies using additional pathway-specific tools will help further refine the contribution of CMA to developmental NOTCH1 control. Finally, we would like to propose that part of the important roles of LAMP2A-dependent CMA in the physiology and/or homeostasis of other tissues and cancer may be mediated via its ability to inhibit NOTCH1 signaling.

Methods

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee (Athens Prefecture Veterinarian Service; K3237/11-05−2012) and took place in the animal facilities of the Center for Experimental Surgery of the Biomedical Research Foundation of the Academy of Athens. All animals were handled in strict accordance with good animal practice as defined by the relevant European and Greek animal welfare bodies.

RNA extraction and real-time RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated by TRI reagent solution (AM9738, Ambion/RNA, Life Technologies) according to manufacturer’s instructions followed by treatment with RQ1 DNase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Detailed procedures and information of the primers are provided in Supporting Information.

Immunofluorescence

Rodent embryonic brains were washed in 4% PFA for 4 h and left o/n in 30% sucrose in PBS. The tissue then was embedded in OCT, sectioned transversely at 12 μm and collected on super-frost slides. Detailed procedures and information of the antibodies are provided in Supporting Information.

Quantification of co-localization of NICD with HSPA8 and NICD with LAMP1

Detailed procedures and information are provided in Supporting Information.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was isolated from cultured NSCs, HEK293A, N2A or embryonic tissues with lysis buffer RIPA plus protease inhibitor cocktail. The homogenates were centrifuged at 17.000 g for 15 min at 40 C. Detailed procedures and information of the antibodies are provided in Supporting Information.

Culture of NSCs, knockdown and overexpression studies

Neurosphere cultures from E16.5 rat cortical tissue were prepared as described previously [15–17]. Detailed procedures and information are provided in Supporting Information.

Luciferase assays

Luciferase-reporter assays were performed with luciferase/β galactosidase kits (Promega) [15–17, 57]. Detailed procedures and information are provided in Supporting Information.

Intracellular protein degradation

Detailed procedures and information are provided in Supporting Information.

In Situ Hybridization on cryosections

Non-radioactive in situ hybridization on cryosections were carried out as previously described [15, 16]. The plasmid containing the sequence of mouse Hes5 mRNA for the preparation of the probe was kindly donated by Dr. A. Louvi. In utero electroporation (IUE)

IUE was performed in mouse embryos E14.5 as previously described [22, 58]. Detailed procedures and information are provided in Supporting Information.

Isolation of rodent lysosomes and in vitro reconstitution of CMA

This protocol involves the purification of Flag-tagged NICD protein, the isolation of the lysosomes and the reaction of in vitro reconstitution of CMA. Detailed procedures and information are provided in Supporting Information.
