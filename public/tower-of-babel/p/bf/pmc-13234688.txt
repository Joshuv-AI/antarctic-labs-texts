Out-of-Pocket Costs and Health-Related Social Needs Among Households With Children
Authors: Michael Caniglia, Laura J. Chavez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat is the association between high out-of-pocket medical spending and health-related social needs (ie, the ability to access healthy foods, pay essential bills, and live in quality housing) among US households with children?FindingsIn this cohort study of 6940 households with children, out-of-pocket medical financial burden exceeding either 5% or 10% of household income was associated with food insecurity in the following year.MeaningThese findings suggest that high out-of-pocket medical costs may make it more difficult for households to afford health-related social needs, potentially contributing to adverse health outcomes.

Introduction

High out-of-pocket medical bills strain US household budgets.1,2,3 Families in the US often rely on private health insurance plans accessed through their employer or government-facilitated health insurance marketplaces. In addition to monthly insurance premiums, these plans require that families pay a share of the costs associated with accessing medical treatment or purchasing prescription drugs.4 Burdensome medical costs make it harder for households with children to visit physicians, adhere to treatment plans, and meet basic needs.5,6,7

Out-of-pocket medical financial burdens occur when a household spends a substantial share of its income on copayments, deductibles, and prescription drugs.3,8,9 Approximately 4% of US households with children spend 10% or more of their income on out-of-pocket costs.3 Out-of-pocket medical spending even consumes substantial income shares among households enrolled in federally subsidized marketplace, Medicaid, and Medicare health insurance programs.9,10,11 Rising health care costs could mean that more households with children experience medical financial burden in 2026.12

Health-related social needs (HRSNs) are defined as a household’s ability to meet needs for medical care, purchase healthy food, pay bills, and live in safe housing.13,14,15,16 Scholarship has indicated that individuals who cannot address their HRSNs may have higher long-term health care costs.17,18 However, limited research has considered whether out-of-pocket medical spending itself is associated with households’ capacities to meet HRSNs. Expensive medical care may make it harder for households with children to afford necessities.19,20 Although scholarship has highlighted medical debt as a correlate of not meeting HRSNs,21 households may still have trouble affording HRSNs, even without incurring medical debt.

Households can enter medical financial burden via 2 mechanisms: rising medical costs and declining incomes. Illness-related medical spending can consume large portions of household income.22,23 At the same time, adults may take time away from work when either they or a child becomes ill, reducing their earnings.24 The combination of rising costs and declining earnings can strain finances, making it more difficult for households to meet basic needs and stressing parents.7,25

Households that experience out-of-pocket medical financial burden may differ from those that do not across a range of health and socioeconomic characteristics that influence both their risks of high spending and HRSNs. However, most studies have not accounted for selection processes when estimating the association between medical spending and HRSNs.18,21,26 In addition, much of the literature has used cross-sectional data to examine the association between high out-of-pocket costs and HRSNs among households with children.14,18,19,26 The relative absence of longitudinal research makes it difficult to identify whether out-of-pocket costs are independently associated with difficulty accessing HRSNs.

The aim of this study was to improve scholars’ understanding of the association between out-of-pocket medical burden and HRSNs (households’ capacity to purchase healthy meals and pay bills) by accounting for potential confounders and establishing temporality. The study is also the first to our knowledge to also consider the association between medical costs and poor housing quality.

Methods

This cohort study used data from the US Census Bureau’s Survey of Income and Program Participation (SIPP). The SIPP’s deidentified and publicly available data did not require institutional review board approval or informed consent in accordance with the Common Rule. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cohort studies.

The SIPP asks respondents detailed questions about income, medical expenditures, and HRSNs during the previous year.27 All topics are covered in the same interview. The SIPP interviews participating households once per year during a 4-year period (panel). The SIPP initiated new panels in 2014 and 2018. Additional overlapping panels started in 2020, 2021, and 2022. A panel that started in 2019 was terminated during a government shutdown.21 This study used the first 3 years of each panel to investigate the longitudinal association between out-of-pocket medical financial burden and later HRSNs. It included households present across each of the first 3 years.27 Although additional years are available, the sample size would decline by more than 20% if requiring participation across all 4 years. Because each SIPP interview covers information about the previous year, data in this study were from 2013 to 2023. Questions remained the same throughout the observation period.27 The Census Bureau imputes missing data before releasing the SIPP results, so there were no missing values in this analysis.28

Medical financial burden and HRSNs were measured at the household level, so only the householder, defined as either the head of the household or the person responsible for paying the rent or mortgage, is included in the study.27,29,30 We limited the study to households with children present in each of the first 3 years of their panels. The final analytic sample largely reflected the composition of all households with children that started the study (comparisons shown in eTable 1 in Supplement 1). One substantive difference was that householders remaining in the study were slightly more educated and had higher incomes than those who left before year 3. Given that limited education and low incomes are leading correlates of HRSNs,31 the results may underestimate the overall prevalence of HRSNs.

Outcome Variables

This study featured 3 types of HRSNs previously studied using the SIPP.29,31 These binary indicators simplified responses to a battery of survey questions about the previous year. First, food insecurity (yes = 1) was determined if a householder answered affirmatively at least 2 of the 6 items of the validated US Department of Agriculture food insecurity scale.32 Second, difficulty paying bills (yes = 1) was determined if a household reported not paying all its rent, mortgage, or utility bills. Third, poor housing quality (yes = 1) was determined if any of the following conditions were substantial and occurred for much of the past year: cracks in ceilings or walls, holes in floor, pests, or plumbing problems. eTable 2 in Supplement 1 presents all HRSN questions.

Focal Variables

Measures of out-of-pocket medical financial burdens in the second year of each panel were the focal variables. The SIPP asks respondents to report how much each household member paid for out-of-pocket expenses, such as copays for physician and dental visits, diagnostic tests, prescription medicine, eyeglasses and contact lenses, and medical supplies in the previous year.28 The out-of-pocket spending measure does not include over-the-counter drug purchases or insurance premiums.2,3 Households were characterized as experiencing 5% out-of-pocket medical financial burden if their total out-of-pocket spending exceeded 5% of their adjusted gross income.33,34 They experienced 10% out-of-pocket medical financial burden if they spent 10% of their adjusted gross household income on out-of-pocket costs.3,8 Both thresholds are commonly used in the literature.3,8,33,35 Adjusted income accounted for economies of scale within households by dividing gross income by the square root of the number of household members.34,36 Income and out-of-pocket costs were inflation adjusted to 2024 US dollars using the Consumer Price Index.

Main Model Covariates

Models analyzing study outcomes used year 1 characteristics to account for demographic confounders associated with HRSNs.29,31 For instance, the main models measured householder age (18-44, 45-64, and ≥65 years), nativity (US born or not US born), and sex (male or female). The SIPP asks respondents to self-report their racial and ethnic identities. This study used 5 mutually exclusive racial and ethnic groups: Hispanic, non-Hispanic Asian, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, and other (American Indian or Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, or multiracial).21 Race and ethnicity were used as variables to account for the structural barriers members of some groups face when attempting to access care. Models also accounted for the householder’s employment status (employed, unemployed, or not in the labor force) and marital status (never married; married; or divorced, separated, or widowed). The householder’s health insurance status was noted as none, private, Medicare, Medicaid, military, or other (Indian Health Service, relative’s coverage, or unspecified public assistance).27 Household-level variables measured the highest education attainment among adults (high school degree, some college, bachelor’s degree, or more than a bachelor’s degree), housing tenure (own, rent, or reside in unit without payment), number of adults (1, 2, or ≥3), number of children (1, 2, or ≥3), presence of a person with a disability, and the presence of an adult aged 65 years or older. A final household measure, income-to-poverty ratio (<1.00, 1.00-1.99, 2.00-2.99, 3.00-3.99, ≥4.00), categorized the ratio of total household income relative to the federal poverty level for each household size.21 Geographic census regions (Northeast, Midwest, South, or West) and year at study entrance were also included in the model.

Statistical Analysis

The data analysis was performed between October 6, 2025, and February 27, 2026. We first used baseline characteristics at each panel’s year 1 to generate separate inverse probability of treatment weights (IPTWs) that considered selection into each type of out-of-pocket burden. Then, we examined descriptive statistics using SIPP’s longitudinal sample weights. The SIPP’s longitudinal weights addressed nonrandom attrition and ensured national representativeness.27 Survey weighted percentages are presented alongside the unweighted number of households meeting each descriptive characteristic. Finally, multivariable logistic regression models using IPTWs were used to estimate the association between out-of-pocket burden in year 2 and HRSNs in year 3. Standard errors were clustered by US state to account for correlation of outcomes associated with state policies.37 Results are presented using odds ratios (ORs) and estimated probabilities.

The IPTW is a form of propensity score modeling used to account for an array of household and householder characteristics associated with health and the probability of entering out-of-pocket burdens. This approach helped to isolate the association between out-of-pocket burden at year 2 and HRSNs at year 3 from baseline factors that may predispose households to out-of-pocket burden. A logistic regression was used to estimate the association between year 1 characteristics and a household’s propensity to experience a given out-of-pocket burden in year 2. Propensity score models frequently include measures not included in the main models to improve similarities across the treatment and control groups.38,39 This study’s propensity score models included variables covering households’ HRSN indicators, neighborhood conditions, public assistance receipt, and health care use (eg, total number of visits to physician offices, total number of days sick, whether a household member was ever hospitalized) at year 1.3,14,40,41,42 eTables 3 and 4 in Supplement 1 provide the propensity score estimations.

Propensity scores identified the probability that a household experienced treatment (ie, out-of-pocket burden). Propensity score weights were then multiplied by the SIPP’s longitudinal survey weights to generate IPTWs.43 Separate IPTWs were created for the risks of a household experiencing the 5% and 10% out-of-pocket burdens. The eAppendix in Supplement 1 provides more information on calculating IPTWs. No differences between either set of treated and untreated groups across year 1 characteristics were found using χ2 tests (balance statistics provided in eTables 5 and 6 in Supplement 1). Because IPTWs do not create a perfect counterfactual, the most salient demographic and health variables within the literature were used as covariates in the outcome models.38,39

Analyses were performed using the svy command and its subpop option in Stata, version 18 (StataCorp LLC). Stata’s margins command was used to generate estimated probabilities. Statistical significance was measured using 2-sided tests where P < .05. The P values were Bonferroni corrected for multiple hypothesis testing.44

Sensitivity analyses examined whether findings were robust to alternative specifications. Models examined whether results were sensitive to the use of the highest and lowest IPTWs. Additional models estimated the associations between out-of-pocket burdens and HRSNs in models without IPTWs. Other sensitivity analyses investigated whether results remained the same during the COVID-19 pandemic and whether the income-to-poverty ratio moderated the association between out-of-pocket burdens and HRSNs.

Results

Sample Composition and Descriptive Results

A total of 80 817 households were present at the beginning of the 2014, 2018, 2020, 2021, and 2022 panels (Figure 1). After limiting the study to households with children present in each of the first 3 years of their panels, 6940 remained (number of householders [weighted percentage]: 3222 aged 18-44 years [49.0%], 3455 aged 45-64 years [48.7%], and 263 aged ≥65 years [2.3%]; 3891 female [54.5%] and 3049 male [45.5%]; 1435 identifying as Hispanic [20.7%], 417 as non-Hispanic Asian [7.4%], 860 as non-Hispanic Black [13.8%], 4042 as non-Hispanic White [55.6%], and 186 as other [2.6%] race and ethnicity) (Table 1). As noted in Table 2, the weighted percentages showed that food insecurity (10.8%), difficulty paying bills (11.6%), and poor housing quality (17.2%) were not uncommon among the sample’s households in year 3.

Regression Results

In multivariable logistic regression models using IPTWs (Table 3), 5% out-of-pocket burden at year 2 estimated an elevated odds of households with children experiencing food insecurity in year 3 (OR, 1.80; 95% CI, 1.31-2.48). As shown in Figure 2, individuals with 5% out-of-pocket burden in year 2 faced higher estimated probabilities of food insecurity than those with no burdens (15.4% vs 10.5%). Individuals with 10% out-of-pocket burden in year 2 also faced a greater odds of food insecurity than those who did not (OR, 1.90; 95% CI, 1.32-2.75) (eTables 7 and 8 in Supplement 1). Households with 10% out-of-pocket burdens faced meaningfully higher probabilities of later experiencing food insecurity (16.6%) compared with their counterparts (10.9%).

Out-of-pocket spending burdens in year 2 were not associated with a higher probability of other HRSNs measured at year 3. For instance, there was no association between 5% out-of-pocket spending burden and trouble paying bills (OR, 1.30; 95% CI, 0.96-1.77) or poor housing quality (OR, 1.22; 95% CI, 0.95-1.57) in year 3 (Table 3). Similarly, no associations between 10% out-of-pocket burdens at year 2 and trouble paying bills (OR, 1.12; 95% CI, 0.79-1.59) and poor housing quality (OR, 1.25; 95% CI, 0.95-1.65) in year 3 were found.

Sensitivity Analyses

Results were robust across multiple specifications. They remained the same when the bottom 5% and top 5% of IPTWs were replaced with values at the 5th and 95th percentiles, respectively (eTables 9 and 10 in Supplement 1). In analyses using survey weights instead of IPTWs, out-of-pocket burden remained associated with food insecurity (eTables 11 and 12 in Supplement 1). Results were directionally consistent across outcomes when limited to individuals who participated in the SIPP during the COVID-19 pandemic (eTables 13 and 14 in Supplement 1). Furthermore, no evidence suggested that the household income-to-poverty ratio moderated the association between medical financial burden and HRSNs (eTables 15 and 16 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

This cohort study, using a nationally representative longitudinal survey and IPTWs, found that household exposures to out-of-pocket medical financial burdens were associated with an elevated prevalence of food insecurity a year later. Results highlight how health care costs may imperil the well-being of households with children. Outcomes remained consistent when using 5% and 10% thresholds of out-of-pocket burden. This study advances the literature by pointing to the longitudinal associations between multiple thresholds of out-of-pocket burdens and household food insecurity 1 year later. Its use of IPTWs in regression models reduced the risks of household health- and socioeconomic-related selection biases from confounding the association between out-of-pocket burdens and HRSNs.

Persistent food insecurity can harm child health and estimate higher health care expenses.26,45 Children experiencing food insecurity are more likely than their peers to exhibit chronic conditions, report poor mental health, and visit the emergency department.45,46 Food insecurity is also associated with worse child development and academic outcomes.47,48 The inability to access HRSNs may exacerbate poor health and reinforce the challenges households face paying future medical bills.21,26 Multiple factors may explain why out-of-pocket burdens were significantly associated with food insecurity alone. Food insecurity is more closely tied to short-term economic insecurity than housing quality.49 Maintenance deferred during a period of high out-of-pocket spending may contribute to a lagged association between out-of-pocket costs and poor housing quality that does not appear within a year.49 Because of housing’s centrality to daily life, household members may make sacrifices to ensure that rent, mortgage, and utilities are paid.50 For example, adults in financially insecure households sometimes forgo their own basic needs, such as food, to ensure that their children can eat and have a stable home.51 These factors may explain why out-of-pocket burden at year 2 was only associated with household food insecurity at year 3 and no other HRSNs.

Our findings show that the risks of HRSNs were similar across the 5% and 10% out-of-pocket burden thresholds. Both measures were associated with significantly greater risks of food insecurity a year later than households without burdens. Studies using a 10% out-of-pocket burden measure alone may have missed households struggling to meet basic needs in the wake of subthreshold out-of-pocket burdens.52 This research builds on existing work that critiqued the 10% threshold as a narrow tool for identifying households facing high out-of-pocket spending.52,53

Our findings carry implications for clinical practice. The results show that food insecurity risks among households exposed to 5% and 10% out-of-pocket burdens were elevated at least 1 year later. The study highlights the importance of screening for HRSNs following a household’s experience with out-of-pocket burdens. Household financial pressures associated with out-of-pocket burdens may persist well after a patient receives care.54

This study underscores the role of out-of-pocket medical costs as a leading risk factor for food insecurity among households with children. Our results add to an existing evidence base suggesting an association between affordable health care and reduced HRSNs.7,55 Expanding Medicaid eligibility, reinstating subsidies to state health insurance exchanges, and negotiating lower drug prices may improve access to affordable health care.56,57,58

Limitations

Despite using a longitudinal and nationally representative survey dataset, this study had some limitations. First, as an observational study, it could not identify causality. Second, although longitudinal weights account for nonrandom attrition, the SIPP faces ongoing challenges with response rates.59 In this study, responding households in the analytic sample had higher incomes and more education than those that exited SIPP panels. Higher income and education have been associated with a reduced risk of HRSNs.31 Thus, the results may underestimate the association between out-of-pocket medical financial burden and HRSNs. Finally, the study faced challenges inherent to self-reported income and data on HRSNs. Responding households may not have recalled HRSNs in the previous year or chosen socially desirable answers.60,61

Conclusions

This cohort study of US households with children found that out-of-pocket medical financial burdens were associated with an elevated odds of food insecurity a year later. No association was observed between out-of-pocket burdens and difficulty paying bills or poor housing quality. This analysis highlights that out-of-pocket medical spending may be a determinant of household food security. Future research should consider which factors mitigate the association between out-of-pocket medical costs and HRSNs.
