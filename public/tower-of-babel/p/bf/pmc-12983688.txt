Evaluation of mixed response in tumor size and survival in patients with rare cancers treated with dual checkpoint inhibitor therapy (DART SWOG S1609)
Authors: Young Kwang Chae, Megan Othus, Jiyon Lyu, Seyoung Lee, Seungwon Paik, Eliana Dietrich, Chan Mi Jung, Liam Il-Young Chung, Sandip Patel, Razelle Kurzrock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMixed response, where different lesions within the same patient show discordant responses to treatment, remains poorly understood. To better understand the complex effects of mixed response on patient survival, we devised three different definitions of mixed response. This retrospective analysis provides the first evaluation of the association between mixed response and survival outcomes in patients with rare cancers treated with dual checkpoint blockade using ipilimumab plus nivolumab, based on data from 52 baskets in the DART SWOG S1609 trial.MethodsWe included 438 patients with Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) V.1.1-measurable disease and at least two target lesions, after exclusions for ineligibility, early death, or missing data. Overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were compared using log-rank tests and Cox regression, stratified by basket and using a day 65 landmark. A mixed response was evaluated using three definitions: Method 1—RECIST discordance across lesions; Method 2—presence of ≥1 lesion with >5 mm increase or ≥1 with >5 mm decrease; and Method 3—same as Method 2 but with a 1 mm cut-off.ResultsMixed response was significantly associated with worse OS and PFS using both Method 1 (OS: HR 1.80; PFS: HR 1.58) and Method 2 (OS: HR 1.55; PFS: HR 1.57) compared with “all SD/stable per lesion”. Among patients classified as “SD by RECIST”, those who exhibited a mixed per lesion response according to Method 1 had significantly worse OS (median 9.9 months (8.8–12.4)) than those with a non-mixed per lesion response (median 22.7 months (19.2–32.0)). Further stratification showed that any lesion increasing >5 mm was linked to worse outcomes (OS: HR >2.37, p<0.05).ConclusionsMixed response was significantly associated with worse survival outcomes in patients treated with dual immune checkpoint inhibitors, even among those with RECIST-defined stable disease. Our findings suggest that the “worst”-responding lesion drives prognosis, underscoring the limitations of RECIST in capturing clinically relevant heterogeneity. This study highlights the need to incorporate lesion-level assessment into immunotherapy decision-making and provides a foundation for guiding earlier transition to the next line therapies or other therapeutic options. Future studies integrating molecular biomarkers are warranted to refine response evaluation criteria and optimize immune checkpoint inhibitor-based strategies.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors

Introduction

The Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) has long been a cornerstone in assessing tumor burden changes in cancer research. By measuring changes in the sum of diameters of “target” lesions, RECIST categorizes patient responses into complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD). Although RECIST is widely used, its ability to fully capture the diversity of tumor responses, especially to novel therapies, is limited, particularly in its capacity to capture the heterogeneity of tumor responses to emerging therapies. For instance, a patient may exhibit substantial growth in one lesion while others remain stable yet still be classified as having SD under RECIST. While it is intuitive that such a patient might have a different prognosis than one with uniformly stable lesions, large-scale studies investigating this hypothesis have been lacking.

The introduction of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) as a treatment for various cancers has further complicated the evaluation of tumor responses. Among these complexities is the phenomenon of “mixed responses”—where multiple lesions within the same patient may react differently to treatment. Although not formally defined by RECIST, the term “mixed response” has been informally used in clinical practice as a description for evaluating treatment effects in patients with multiple lesions. While mixed responses might intuitively reflect a partial therapeutic effect, their prognostic significance remains unclear. To date, only a limited number of studies have explored mixed responses, with varying definitions of mixed response and focusing primarily on single tumor types.1,5 Previous studies reported 6.8%–22% occurrence of mixed response, which is not uncommon.1 2 6 7 Investigations into the mechanisms behind mixed responses, especially in terms of intratumor heterogeneity, have begun to shed light on this phenomenon.4 8

Previous research has been limited by its focus on single tumor types and a lack of emphasis on the relationship between mixed responses and clinical outcomes.1,5 While there are studies that examine clinical outcomes, these too are restricted to specific tumor types.2 3 This underscores a significant knowledge gap regarding the impact of mixed responses on patient prognosis, highlighting the need for advanced analytical methods capable of unraveling the complex implications of mixed responses on patient outcomes.

In this study, we aimed to define mixed responses and investigate their association with survival outcomes in patients receiving dual immune checkpoint blockade. Two broad approaches were used to define mixed response: one based on a modified application of RECIST using percentage or absolute change in individual lesions (ie, +20% or 5 mm for PD and −30% for PR/CR), and another based on absolute criteria for lesion-level change. For the absolute approach, we applied an arbitrary cut-off of 5 mm, alongside a more stringent 1 mm threshold for contrast. Unified absolute criteria may offer greater utility in practice, as they are often easier to apply than percentage-based measurements.

This analysis uses data from the Dual Anti-CTLA-4 and Anti-PD-1 Blockade in Rare Tumors (DART) SWOG S1609 trial and represents the first study to evaluate the prognostic implications of mixed response across a histologically diverse population of rare tumors treated with dual checkpoint inhibitors. By examining mixed response through three different definitions, this study seeks to unravel the complex dynamics of immunotherapy response, challenge traditional methods of response evaluation, and contribute to the refinement of cancer treatment approaches.

Materials and methods

The analysis population for this work consisted of participants enrolled and treated on SWOG Cancer Research Network trial S1609 (NCT02834013, DART), which was funded by the National Cancer Institute (NCI) and was open at over 1000 clinical trial sites. S1609 used a basket design, and participants were enrolled across 50 histologic baskets and three additional baskets: programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) amplified tumors, cancer of unknown primary, and “not otherwise categorized.” In 52 of the baskets, participants received ipilimumab (1 mg/kg every 6 weeks) plus nivolumab (240 mg every 2 weeks); participants in the basket for PD-L1 amplified tumors received single-agent nivolumab (240 mg every 2 weeks). Eligibility and study schedule have been previously reported. The trial opened to enrollment in 2017 and closed to enrollment in March 2023.

All patients were required to have RECIST V.1.1-measurable disease at study registration. Pre-study (within 28 days prior to study registration) CT or MRI scans of diagnostic quality were required to include the chest, abdomen, and pelvis; patients with head/neck cancer needed chest, abdomen, pelvis, and neck. Scans were required every 8 weeks until week 24, then every 12 weeks for up to 3 years after registration to the study or until progression. Tumor measurements were based on investigator assessment. One basket, patients with gestational trophoblastic disease, used a biochemical response endpoint instead of RECIST; patients from that basket were excluded from the analyses.

Overall survival (OS) was measured from date of study registration to date of death from any cause with patients last known to be alive censored at the date of last contact. Progression-free survival (PFS) was measured from the date of study registration to the first of RECIST progression or death from any cause, with patients last known to be alive without progression censored at the date of last contact. OS and PFS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Log-rank tests and Cox regression models were used to compare OS and PFS between groups. To account for varying failure rates across disease baskets, log-rank tests and Cox regression models were stratified by basket. To account for immortal time bias associated with patients who died or experienced clinical progression before their first on-study scan, we performed response category analysis using the landmark method at day 65. Patients were excluded from the analyses if they progressed, died, were censored before day 65, or had their first scan after day 65.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: death before day 65 (the landmark for analysis based on the first scan) (n=86), failure to receive any protocol therapy (n=71), and having only one lesion (n=203). A total of 438 patients with more than two measurable lesions were included in the mixed response analyses. A mixed response was defined using three methods: Method 1 (“RECIST on individual lesions”) defined mixed response as discordance in per-lesion responses according to RECIST criteria. Method 2 (“Absolute cutoff: 5 mm”) defined it as the presence of at least one lesion with an increase in size or at least one lesion with a decrease in size, using a 5 mm cut-off. Method 3 (“Absolute cutoff: 1 mm”) used the same definition as Method 2 but applied a 1 mm cut-off (figure 1).

Results

Among lesions classified as showing a mixed response, the proportion of those with SD by RECIST increased progressively across the three methods: Method 1 (n=95, 41.9%), Method 2 (n=126, 51.4%), and Method 3 (n=156, 72.2%) (trend p<0.001) (figure 2). A subset of lesions initially categorized as “all SD per lesion” under Method 1 was reclassified as “mixed response” under Method 2, and further as “all increase” under Method 3 (figure 3).

Mixed response per lesion was significantly associated with worse survival outcomes in both Method 1 and Method 2. For OS, the presence of a mixed response was associated with a higher HR in Method 1 (HR 1.80; 95% CI 1.35 to 2.39; p<0.001) and Method 2 (HR 1.55; 95% CI 1.12 to 2.14; p=0.008) (table 1). Similarly, for PFS, mixed response was significantly associated with inferior outcomes in both Method 1 (HR 1.58; 95% CI 1.21 to 2.07; p=0.001) and Method 2 (HR 1.57; 95% CI 1.16 to 2.11; p=0.003) (table 2).

Discussion

Our study explored the relationship between mixed responses and survival outcomes among patients treated with dual checkpoint inhibitors. Notably, many of the mixed responders were categorized as SD by RECIST, underscoring limitations of RECIST in capturing heterogeneous responses (figure 2). The kinetics and heterogeneity of immune checkpoint inhibitor responses are insufficiently captured by RECIST V.1.1, which is cumbersome to use in real-world clinical management outside of clinical trials.8

A subset of lesions initially classified as “all SD per lesion” under Method 1 was reclassified as “mixed response” in Method 2 and, in some cases, further as “all increase” under Method 3 (figure 3). This shift likely resulted from Method 2’s use of absolute size thresholds, which may have disproportionately impacted lesions with larger baseline diameters. As a result, patients with larger lesions were more likely to fall into the “mixed response” under Method 2—potentially contributing to differences in clinical outcomes. Interestingly, however, among the “mixed response”, patients with both increasing and decreasing lesions (one+lesion ↑ > 5 mm & One+lesion ↓ > 5 mm) exhibited worse OS (6.0 months (3.6–11.4)) than those with increasing and stable lesions (One+lesion ↑ > 5 mm & One+lesion ↔), showing a counterintuitive aspect of mixed responses that warrants further investigation (figure 5).

Among patients classified as “SD by RECIST”, those who exhibited a “mixed per lesion” response according to Method 1 had significantly worse survival outcomes compared with those with a “non-mixed per lesion” response (online supplemental Figure S1). Specifically, the HR for OS was 1.90 (95% CI 1.34 to 2.68; p<0.001), and for PFS, it was 1.52 (95% CI 1.10 to 2.12; p=0.012) (table 3). This indicates that even when a patient is labeled as “SD by RECIST”, the presence of heterogeneity across target lesions—that is, mixed per lesion response—may be a predictive marker of poor prognosis.

Method 2 allowed further stratification of these patients by introducing absolute cut-off values for lesion-level changes, thereby making it possible to deeply dive into the survival analysis within the mixed response group (table 4, figure 5). Importantly, our results suggest that survival is largely determined by the “worst” responding lesion rather than the “best”; even a single lesion showing significant increase in size was associated with worse outcomes, regardless of whether other lesions showed stability or shrinkage. This highlights a clinically relevant insight—patient with discordant lesion-level responses may carry a worse prognosis, and close monitoring of the most progressive lesion may be critical in guiding further treatment decisions.

Tumor heterogeneity as an underlying mechanism of mixed response

Despite the development of detection indexes, such as circulating tumor DNA and interleukin-8, and new criteria for immunotherapy such as immune-related response criteria, immune response evaluation criteria in solid tumors (iRECIST) and immune-modified RECIST, our understanding of heterogeneous responses to ICIs remains limited.9

The concept of dissociated or mixed response has been explored in several recent studies as a clinical manifestation of underlying tumor heterogeneity. For example, Guan et al characterized dissociated response as a response where responding and non-responding lesions or new lesions coexist. Their study showed those who received local treatment to the progressing lesions had improved OS (median OS: 32.3 vs 21.9 months for patients with or without local treatment, respectively, p=0.074), suggesting that site-specific interventions for progressing lesions could be considered as the next management plan.7 Also, Lv et al reported that in patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer exhibiting progressive disease with mixed response, most cases occurred in stage IV disease and commonly involved prior local treatment such as radiotherapy. This implies that mixed response may not only reflect tumor biology but also prior treatment history, further complicating response interpretation.10

Large-scale sequencing analyses of solid cancers have identified significant genetic heterogeneity within individual tumors.11,13 Gerlinger et al demonstrated that intratumor mutational heterogeneity exists in primary renal carcinomas and their corresponding metastases, with multiple tumor suppressor genes—SETD2, PTEN, and KDM5C—harboring distinct, spatially separated inactivating mutations within the same tumor. This phenomenon suggests convergent phenotypic evolution, where independent subclones evolve similar functional traits despite genetic divergence.11 Such clonal diversity within a single tumor has been associated with decreased immunosurveillance and diminished responsiveness to ICIs.14 These heterogeneous tumors can adapt to selective pressures imposed by therapy, facilitating immune escape, treatment failure, and drug resistance. Consequently, this intratumoral diversity may serve as a biological basis for mixed responses observed in ICI-treated patients, where some lesions regress while others persist or progress despite therapy.

Limitations

Despite the strengths of our study, including its large and histologically diverse cohort, this study is not without limitations. We lacked molecular data such as tumor mutational burden or immune biomarkers, which limited our ability to correlate clinical response patterns with underlying biological heterogeneity. In addition, detailed histologic subtypes and organ system-based tumor site information were not available in the dataset, which precluded further stratified analyses.

It is noteworthy that mixed response can be difficult to distinguish from pseudoprogression, particularly during the early phases of treatment with ICIs. This phenomenon is addressed in iRECIST, which designates the initial observation of progression as immune unconfirmed PD, requiring subsequent imaging to classify it as immune confirmed PD.9 Therefore, the fact that our study was based on RECIST V.1.1 at the first follow-up should be considered as a limitation. Previous studies have shown that pseudoprogression accounts for some mixed response cases. Haaker et al demonstrated that among 94 patients with metastatic renal cell carcinoma, mixed response occurred in 11 (11.7%) patients; of these, 8 (73%) evolved to confirmed PD, and 3 (27%) evolved towards PR and were thus considered pseudoprogression. Patients with pseudoprogression had favorable outcomes, while mixed response most often developed to progression.15

In our study, the incidence of mixed response was 51.8% (n=227) with Method 1, 55.9% (n=245) with Method 2, and 49.3% (n=216) with Method 3 (figure 2), which is notably higher than the previously reported rates of up to 22% in prior studies.1 2 6 7 This discrepancy suggests that our findings may have limited generalizability to other settings, possibly due to differences in cancer type or patient populations, as our study included only rare cancers and the majority of participants were white (79%) and non-Hispanic (90%) (online supplemental Table S1).

However, it is worth noting that our study represents the largest cohort to date specifically investigating lesion-level mixed responses to immunotherapy. This scale not only enhances the statistical power of our analysis but also allows for a more nuanced exploration of response heterogeneity, providing valuable insights into the prognostic implications of intrapatient variability in tumor response.

Conclusion

This study provides the first comprehensive evaluation of the relationship between various definitions of mixed response and survival outcomes in patients with rare solid tumors treated with dual immune checkpoint blockade. Our findings demonstrate that survival outcomes are primarily driven by the “worst” performing lesion, rather than the “best” performing one; in other words, having an increase in any lesion is associated with worse outcomes, regardless of whether other lesions remained stable or progressed. Furthermore, even among patients classified as “SD by RECIST”, those who exhibited a mixed per lesion response according to Method 1 had a worse OS than those with a non-mixed per lesion response. These results suggest that lesion-level heterogeneity—often overlooked in standard RECIST assessment—has significant prognostic implications and should be carefully considered in clinical decision-making.

Future studies should incorporate blood-level and tissue-level biomarkers to elucidate the mechanisms underlying mixed responses to ICIs and discover biological predictors of mixed response. A deeper understanding will be essential to guide the next management, including decisions regarding continuation of immunotherapy, integration of local therapies, and stratification of patients in future clinical trials involving ICIs.
