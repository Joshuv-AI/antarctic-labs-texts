Identification of potential oncogenic miRNA clusters with a special focus on miR-106b/25 cluster-regulated networks and their clinical utility in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Shreyas Hulusemane Karunakara, Manju Moorthy, Gopalakrishna Ramaswamy, Vinutha Shanubhoganahalli Puttamallapa, Maheshkumari Prakash, Rohit Mehtani, Shama Prasada Kabekkodu, Prashant M. Vishwanath, Prasanna Kumar Santhekadur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The lack of reliable early markers specific to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) limits its effective management. While the genetic factors defining this disease are largely understood, the epigenetic mechanisms that cause HCC remain elusive. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) operate as polycistronic clusters rather than individual genes and are critical to the development and progression of multiple cancers, including HCC. In this study, we identified several miRNA clusters, including the miR-106b/25 cluster (miR-106b-5p, miR-25-3p, and miR-93-5p), that are overexpressed in HCC cells, suggesting their oncogenic function. We then performed whole-transcriptomic sequencing and identified gene targets of this cluster, namely, CAV1, DNAJB4, PTPRD, MFSD2A, TCF4, KLF6, MCC, CYB5A, ESR2, NR4A3, PRKCB, RASSF2, TXNIP, and SOD2, to be downregulated in the HCC spheroids and clinical datasets. Further, gene enrichment analysis revealed associations with critical pathways, such as Wnt signaling, TGF-beta signaling, VEGFA-VEGFR2 signaling, and EGF/R signaling pathways. Furthermore, survival analysis revealed that miR-93-5p (HR = 0.72, p = 0.0246), HCC stage (HR = 2.43, p = 0.0000113), TCF4 (HR = 0.66, p = 0.0106), DNAJB4 (HR = 1.29, p = 0.0214), MCC (HR = 1.35, p = 0.0268), and CYB5A (HR = 0.77, p = 0.0423) affect overall survival (OS). Finally, a combined prognostic model for the miRNA cluster and its target genes via the random forest approach revealed that the miR-106b/25 cluster and its interactome are significantly associated with OS (p < 0.0001), thereby providing a comprehensive understanding of the cluster and its targets in the development and progression of HCC and its use as a potential marker for HCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03834-z.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), a major form of primary liver cancer (PLC), accounts for 758,725 deaths globally [1]. Although mortality due to viral hepatitis-associated HCC have decreased since the early 2000 s, there has been a clear increase in other causes of HCC, such as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) [2]. Annually, 900,000 new cases are reported worldwide, with a vast majority (nearly 72.5%) reported from Asia, with an incidence of 11.6 per 100,000 individuals [3]. The global average for age at diagnosis is 57 years which suggest that poor diagnosis and overall disease management are the driving factors behind increased HCC incidence and mortality [4]. Hence, it is critical to study different molecular players that may be of interest as potential disease markers for HCC diagnosis and prognosis.

Six-monthly ultrasound abdomen with/without measurement of serum alpha-fetoprotein levels (AFP) is routinely used for HCC screening [5]. However, some studies have reported that established biomarkers fall short in their applicability as reliable markers. For example, there is compelling evidence of AFP-negative HCC (ANHC), where measuring serum AFP levels is considered to be insignificant [6]. While the spectrum of HCC biomarkers is constantly expanding, studies have reported the use of proteins (Glypican-3 (GPC-3), Des-γ-carboxy-prothrombin (DCP), and osteopontin) from biopsies; metabolites (creatinine, inosine, indole-3-acetate, hydantoin-5-propionic acid, N-acetylated amino acid (NAA), and galactose) from the urine; and cell-free biomarkers (TERT, CTNNB1, p16, RASSF1A, and p15) from serum as possible markers for HCC [6], which, however, lack sufficient evidence to be considered as efficient HCC markers. Therefore, molecular analyses will help identify specific biomarkers with greater sensitivity and overcome the limitations of existing biomarkers for HCC management.

Genome-wide studies have highlighted the importance of epi-/genomic alterations in the pathogenesis of HCC and their suitability as markers for HCC diagnosis and prognosis [7–11]. miRNAs operate as polycistronic units or miRNA clusters (miRCs), wherein two or more miRNAs are transcribed in the same genomic orientation and exhibit co-expression in their expression patterns [11]. Notably, we and others have previously shown that miRNA clusters are differentially regulated in HCC and are vital to HCC progression [12, 13]. With approximately 159 reported miRNA clusters according to miRBase, reports suggest that they can operate either as oncogenic or tumor suppressor clusters [14]. Given that these miRNAs can target multiple downstream genes and regulate several of the critical biological processes involved in HCC development and progression, understanding how the aberrant expression of miRNAs regulate their target interactome during the tumorigenesis of HCC is important and needs further investigation.

In this study, we report several oncogenic miRNA clusters (oncomiRCs) and tumor suppressor miRNA clusters (TSmiRCs) in HCC cells. We performed an in-depth analysis of one of the oncomiRC-miR-106b/25 clusters, for which we found that all the component miRNAs were upregulated in HCC. We then identified the target gene interactome and validated its expression in HCC spheroids via whole RNA-Seq. Further, critical biological pathways and molecular processes associated with the target genes were identified through enrichment studies. Furthermore, we anticipated the clinical utility of the cluster and its target genes to understand its possible function in terms of overall survival and constructed a combined prognostic model for the miR-106b/25 cluster and target interactome to assess its importance in predicting the survival outcomes of HCC patients. Finally, with the purpose of identifying known and experimental drugs that target the interactome of the cluster, we performed a pancancer drug-gene interaction analysis. Overall, our study is one of the first reports to provide a comprehensive understanding of different oncomiRCs, with a special focus on evaluating the complete miR-106b/25 cluster and its interactome, with equal emphasis given to underpinnings associated with its clinical utility in HCC.

Methodology

Cell culture

Monolayer cell culture

All the cell lines were procured from National Centre for Cell Science (NCCS), Pune, India. The cells were cultured and maintained in minimal essential medium (MEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and a 1% penicillin + streptomycin (PenStrep) cocktail in 5% CO2 at 37 °C until the cells were confluent. Huh7 HCC cells were maintained using DMEM: F12 Ham’s complete media with FBS and antibiotic cocktail as described for HepG2 cells.

Tumor spheroid culture

3D-HCC spheroids were obtained from HepG2 cells. Cells were initially seeded in hanging drops. After day 3, the spheroids were transferred to individual wells of an ultra-low attachment plate and cultured for up to 9 days. The optimal seeding density (2.5k, 5k, 10k, and 20k cells) and the time required (0, 3, 5, 7, and 9 days) for spheroid growth were standardized using morphometric measurements and growth kinetics of the spheroids using the ImageJ software. The spheroids were harvested from each well and washed twice with 1X phosphate buffer saline for further experiments.

RNA extraction

Total RNA was isolated via the TRI reagent (Sigma‒Aldrich, USA). The quality and quantity of the isolated RNA were checked via nanodrop spectrophotometric measurements. miRNA quantification was subsequently performed via a Quibit RNA HS Page Assay (Invitrogen, USA) on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Isolated RNAs from control human liver (#636531-Lot nos. 2305184 & 1210374 A) (TakaraBio, Japan) were used as controls. All the experiments were conducted in biological replicates.

nCounter miRNA assay

The detection and quantification of miRNAs were performed via the nCounter Human v3 miRNA expression assay (NS_H_miR_v3B) on an nCounter analysis system (NanoString Technologies) according to the protocol provided by the manufacturer. This assay works on the principle of unique barcodes designed specifically for housekeeping genes, spike-in controls across species, and 799 endogenous human miRNA targets. The QC of the raw miRNA counts in Reporter Code Count (RCC) format was performed via nSolver software (NanoString Technologies) following the manufacturer’s recommendations.

miRNA data analysis

The normalization of raw reads was performed via geometric means of positive controls and the top 100 highly expressed miRNAs. Normal vs. HepG2 and Normal vs. Huh7 were the comparison groups for the differential miRNA expression (DEmiRNA) analysis. The build ratio utility of the solver was used to calculate the differential miRNA expression (fold change) between the groups. Student’s unpaired t-test was used to calculate the statistical significance of the change in expression between the groups, with specific cut-offs for geometric mean expression being ≥ average counts of assay negative controls and fold change cut-offs being threshold fold changes: ≤ −1.5 (downregulated) or ≥ + 1.5 (upregulated) with p ≤ 0.05 were used to identify differentially upregulated or downregulated miRNAs.

miRNA cluster identification

miRBase offers comprehensive details about annotated human miRNA clusters, along with the annotations of miRNAs [14]. Differentially expressed miRNAs from the nCounter assay were identified and assigned manually by comparing the differentially expressed miRNAs with annotated miRNA clusters from miRBase.

Whole-transcriptomic sequencing

We prepared the libraries via the NEB Ultra II directional RNA-Seq Library Prep Kit (Cat. No. E7760L). The Qubit RNA BR Assay (Invitrogen, Cat. No. Q10211) or the Qubit RNA Assay HS (Invitrogen, Cat. No. Q32852) was used to quantify the RNA samples (normal liver and HCC spheroids). The RNA purity was assessed on a QIAxpert system. The integrity of the RNA in the samples was evaluated at TapeStation via RNA Screen Tape (Agilent, Cat. No. 5067–5576 or Agilent, Cat. No. 5067–5579), after which mRNA library preparation and sequencing were carried out. Briefly, during reverse transcription, 100 ng of RNA was subjected to ribosomal RNA depletion via the QIIME Fast Select rRNAHMR (Cat. No. 334387). First-strand cDNA was synthesized via reverse transcriptase. Next, second-strand cDNA synthesis was performed with DNA polymerase I and RNase H enzymes, followed by adapter ligation and sequencing of the PCR products on an Illumina NovaSeq V1.5 instrument. Before sequencing, the fragment size distribution was evaluated on the fragment analyser via the HS NGS Fragment Kit (1–6000 bp) (Agilent, Cat. No. DNF474–1000) or on the TapeStation via D1000 DNA ScreenTape (Agilent, Cat. No. 5067–5582). The alignment of paired-end reads was mapped to the reference genome (GRCh38) via STAR (v.2.7.11b). The estimation of raw read counts was obtained with FeatureCounts (2.0.1) and subsequently normalized via DESeq2. The fold change was calculated as the ratio of normalized read counts for spheroids to normal samples [downregulated genes with a log2(fold change) ≤ −2 and upregulated genes with a log2(fold change) ≥ + 2, p ≤ 0.05].

Identification, differential expression analysis of lncRNAs and lncRNA‒mRNA interactions

The aligned reads were quantified via the GENCODE lncRNA GTF annotation file via FeatureCounts (2.0.1) for the identification of known lncRNAs. The raw read counts of the lncRNAs were then normalized via DESeq2, and the same threshold as that used for the mRNAs was used to find the significantly downregulated or significantly upregulated known lncRNAs. The lncRNA‒mRNA pairs were identified for each comparison via multiple validated databases, such as NPInter, RNAInter, Starbase, and EVlncRNA. Additionally, the classification of mRNA targets into ‘cis’ interacting targets has been identified via the FEELnc standalone tool.

miR-106b/25 cluster target identification

The target genes showing an inverse correlation with the miR-106b/25 cluster were considered for further downstream analyses. The target genes of oncomiRC were obtained by computing the overlaps of differentially expressed mRNA targets obtained from sequencing, the miRNA target genes predicted from TargetScan (v8.0) [15], miRTarBase (v8.0) [16] and miRDIP (v5.2) [17] tools, the genes identified as tumor suppressor genes from the TSGene database [18], and the genes downregulated in the TCGA-LIHC cohort. We also performed gene signature analysis (GSA) for these gene targets in independent RNA-Seq and microarray public datasets via TNMplot [19]. GSA was performed by calculating the means of the expression of target genes across each patient data obtained from TCGA-LIHC, one by one, via the RNA expression data.

miRNA‒mRNA interaction and pathway enrichment analysis

ShinyGO (v0.80) was used to predict the prominent gene ontologies (GOs) and pathways associated with the gene targets of the miR-106b/25 cluster [20]. The protein-protein interactions of the target genes were determined via STRING (v12.0) [21], with default parameters.

Assessment of the clinical utility of miR-106b/25 oncomiRC and target genes

The clinical utility in terms of overall survival for the differentially expressed oncomiRC was assessed via Cox regression analysis via the DoSurvive tool [22]. Additionally, the significance of oncomiRC target genes in terms of overall survival was also assessed using the same tool. We used the COX regression analyses under the following variable considerations: the patient’s age was treated as a continuous variable. The HCC stage was further classified as early (stage I and stage II) and late (stage III and stage IV) stages and treated as a binary variable. HCC stage classification was either based on the presence or absence of the patient’s pathological stage or the histological stage in the absence of a pathological stage. Further, the bias caused by extreme outliers related to gene expression levels, was normalized by the rank-based inverse transformation method.

Construction of prognostic models

The TACCO online tool was used to evaluate the prognostic significance of the OncomiRC and its identified targets [23]. The genes of the miR-106b/25 cluster and its targets were uploaded to TACCO, and a prognostic model was constructed via the random forest approach. The patients from the TCGA-LIHC cohort were classified into high-risk and low-risk groups on the basis of the median survival in days (statistically significant difference, Wilcoxon rank p < 0.05).

Drug‒Gene interaction identification

The candidate drugs targeting all the differentially expressed target genes (DETGs) were predicted via the inbuilt pipeline of PanDrugs v2.0 based on reported genome-wide OMICS data from over 23 cancer types and over 74,087 drug-gene interactions resulting from 4642 genes and 14,659 chemical compounds that act as potential drugs and are under different stages of therapeutic development across cancers [24]. We specifically queried for drug-gene interactions for DETGs of miR-106b/25 oncomiRC identified in our study. The complete methodology followed in this study is summarized in Fig. 1.

Results

Several oncomiRCs are dysregulated in HCC

We initially compared the miRNA expression between RNAs from control and HepG2 or Huh7 HCC cells to identify the differentially expressed miRNAs in HCC. The nCounter miRNA assay revealed 81 DEmiRNAs (upregulated-27 and downregulated-54) in HepG2 cells. Further, among the upregulated miRNAs, 12 clustered miRNAs (miR-17-5p, miR-18a-5p-miR-17/92-a cluster, miR-106b-5p, miR-93-5p, miR-25-3p-miR-106b/25 cluster, miR-660-5p-miR-188/660 cluster, miR-103a-3p-miR-103a/103b cluster, miR-421-miR-374b/421 cluster, miR-7-5p-miR-7-2/3529 cluster, miR-182-5p-miR-96/183 cluster, miR-106a-5p-miR-18b/363 cluster, and miR-221-3p-miR-221/222 cluster) were upregulated in HCC (Fig. 2a). Furthermore, miR-106b-5p, miR-25-3p belonging to miR-106b/25 cluster was also upregulated in Huh7 cells (Supplementary Table 1). Additionally, small RNA sequencing data revealed that these miRNAs were upregulated in HCC samples compared with adjacent normal liver samples in the TCGA-LIHC cohort (Fig. 2b). While we identified several downregulated clustered miRNAs, this study aimed only to study oncomiRCs in detail. The complete list of all the DEmiRNAs and oncomiRCs identified in this study are provided in Tables 1 and 2.

As all the constituent miRNAs of miR-106b/25 oncomiRC were differentially upregulated, we decided to characterize the gene targets and evaluate their clinical significance in detail.

miR-106b/25 oncomiRC targets multiple genes that act as tumor suppressors

We cultured HCC spheroids using HepG2 and Huh7 cells by combining the hanging drop and forced suspension methods (Fig. 3a). We observed that HepG2 cells, which are a relatively less aggressive subtype of HCC, conveniently formed self-assembled spheroids. However, such tumoroids could not be derived using more aggressive Huh7 cells, which instead formed loose aggregates (data not shown). This prompted us to characterize the cluster targets using HepG2 spheroids. We observed that spheroids derived using HepG2 cells reached maximum growth on day 5 at an optimum seeding density of 5k cells (Fig. 3b–d).

Whole-transcriptome sequencing of pools of 96 spheroids in biological duplicates revealed a total of 2902 upregulated and 3529 downregulated genes in spheroids compared with those in normal liver. Furthermore, known markers of HCC, such as elevated AFP and reduced CRP, have also been reported in HCC spheroids (Fig. 3e). Next, only the DEGs whose expression was negatively correlated with ocomiRC expression were considered for target gene identification. The target genes of oncomiRCs were identified by computing the overlaps between the 3529-underexpressed DEGs identified in spheroids, 3942 downregulated genes from the TCGA-LIHC cohort, 1148-predicted targets of the miR-106b/25 cluster, and 1217-potential coding tumor suppressor genes, which ultimately yielded a total of 14 differentially downregulated target genes of the miR-106b/25 oncomiRC which included-Caveolin 1 (CAV1), DNAJ Heat shock protein family B4 (DNAJB4), Protein Tyrosine Phosphatase Receptor type Delta (PTPRD), Major Facilitator Superfamily Domain-containing protein 2 A (MFSD2A), Transcription Factor 4 (TCF4), Kruppel-like Factor 6 (KLF6), Mutated in Colorectal Cancer or Colorectal Mutant Cancer protein (MCC), Cytochrome B5A (CYB5A), Estrogen Receptor 2 (ESR2), Nuclear Receptor subfamily 4 group A member 3 (NR4A3), Protein Kinase C Beta (PRKCB), Ras Association Domain Family Member 2 (RASSF2), Thioredoxin-interacting Protein (TXNIP), and Superoxide Dismutase 2 (SOD2) (Fig. 4a and b). Clustering analysis of these genes via unsupervised k-means clustering identified at least six different 6 clusters based on their expression patterns. We observed Cluster 1-CAV1, DNAJB4, and PTPRD; Cluster 2- MFSD2A, TCF4, and KLF6; Cluster 3-MCC, CYB5A; Cluster 4-ESR2, NR4A3, PRKCB, and RASSF2; Cluster 5-TXNIP; and Cluster 6-SOD2 as the different clusters on the basis of their differential expression (Fig. 4c).

Furthermore, our transcriptomic analysis revealed that the lncRNA SOD2-overlapping transcript 1 (SOD2-OT1), like the miR-106b/25 cluster, was overexpressed in HepG2 tumuroids (Supplementary Fig. 1a–1c). Furthermore, we predicted a cis-interaction and a negative correlation between SOD2-OT1 lncRNA and SOD2 mRNA, suggesting that both the SOD2-OT1 and the miR-106b/25 cluster can target SOD2 mRNA. miR-106b/25 oncomiRC target genes regulate critical gene ontologies and cell cycle pathways associated with HCC

We then queried the miR-106b/25 oncomiRC targets for gene signature (GSA) and enrichment (GEA). Compared with those in normal tissues, the expression of these genes was consistently downregulated in primary HCC and metastatic tissues (Fig. 5a). Furthermore, GEA revealed that the genes were involved in critical biological processes, such as the regulation of peptidyl serine phosphorylation, the regulation of fatty acid metabolism, the regulation of cellular ketone metabolism, carbohydrate transport, enzyme-linked receptor protein signaling, the hormonal response, the positive regulation of cellular metabolism and biosynthesis, and the negative regulation of cellular signal transduction (Fig. 5b). The key molecular functions included nuclear estrogen receptor activity, superoxide dismutase activity, oxidoreductase activity, TFIIB-class transcription factor binding, nuclear steroid receptor binding and activity, ion channel regulator activity, and Cis-regulatory region-specific DNA binding by RNA pol II (Fig. 5c).

Furthermore, the key pathways regulated by the cluster targets included several cancer-related pathways, such as Wnt signaling, TGF-beta signaling, VEGFA-VEGFR2 signaling, and EGF/R signaling pathways. Other pathways included fetal androgen synthesis, arsenic metabolism and ROS generation, serotonin and anxiety-related events, development of pulmonary dendritic cells and macrophage subpopulations, development of mammary glands during pregnancy and lactation, congenital lipodystrophy, the CRH signaling pathway, male infertility, and focal adhesion (Fig. 5d).

The miR-106b/25 cluster and its interactome are associated with the overall survival of HCC patients

We performed multivariate Cox regression analysis for the miRNAs of miR-106b/25 oncomiRC and its 14 gene targets. Notably, miR-93-5p was significantly associated with OS (p = 0.0246) (Fig. 6a). Moreover, HCC patients with higher expression of miR-93-5p had poorer OS than HCC patients with lower expression of this miRNA [HR = 0.72, 95% CI (0.54–0.96)]. Similarly, TCF4 [HR = 0.66, 95% CI (0.49–0.91), p = 0.0106], DNAJB4 [HR = 1.29, 95% CI (1.04–1.61), p = 0.0214], MCC [HR = 1.35, 95% CI (1.03–1.75) p = 0.0268], and CYB5A [HR = 0.77, 95% CI 0.59–0.99), p = 0.0423] were found to affect the OS of HCC patients (Fig. 6b).

Furthermore, we constructed a combined prognostic model for the miR-106b/25 cluster and its 14 gene targets to assess its actual clinical utility. This panel of 17 genes (3 oncogenic miRNAs and 14 tumor suppressor targets) could classify patients into high-risk and low-risk groups and predict their OS (p < 0.0001), with a high overall sensitivity of 90% and specificity of 94%, respectively (Fig. 7a and b). Discussion

HCC is a multifaceted disease defined by various genetic and epigenetic factors. Genome-wide studies have elucidated the importance of epigenomic alterations associated with miRNA clusters in the development of HCC and further suggested their use as markers for diagnosis and prognosis [8–10]. The present study investigated the expression of various oncogenic miRNA clusters (OncomiRCs) in HCC.

We identified through an nCounter miRNA assay that member miRNAs belonging to the miR-17/92-a, miR-188/660, miR-103a/103b, miR-374b/421, miR-7-2/3529, miR-96/183, miR-18b/363, miR-221/222, and miR-106b/25 clusters are upregulated in both HCC cells and the tumors of TCGA-LIHC cohort, suggesting that these clusters act as oncogenes in HCC. We identified that all the component miRNAs of the miR-106b/25 cluster, namely, miR-106b-5p, miR-93-5p, and miR-25-3p, were upregulated in HCC cells, prompting us to characterize the entire cluster, validate the expression of its target gene network, and predict critical gene ontologies and pathways regulated by these genes in the context of HCC.

We then looked for potential gene targets of these miRNAs by performing whole-transcriptome sequencing of spheroids derived from HepG2 HCC cells. Around fourteen gene targets of the miR-106b/25 cluster were significantly downregulated in HCC. These included the gene targets CAV1, DNAJB4, PTPRD, MFSD2A, TCF4, KLF6, MCC, CYB5A, ESR2, NR4A3, PRKCB, RASSF2, TXNIP, and SOD2, which were mapped to at least six different gene clusters based on their differential expression patterns. We also identified a potential lncRNA-mRNA interaction between SOD2-OT1-SOD2. Additionally, we report that SOD2-OT1 along with miR-106b-5p and miR-93-5p of miR-106b/25 cluster can compete and target SOD2 mRNA by calculating the mean binding energies of such RNA-RNA hybrid interactions.

Various studies have shown the association of HCC progression with these tumor suppressor genes targeted by the miR-106b/25 cluster. CAV1 expression in HCC is context-specific. For example, CAV1 expression is lower in well-differentiated HCC than in advanced and poorly differentiated HCC, with higher CAV1 expression associated with lower survival rates [25]. Similar observations were made in this study where we report CAV1 to be significantly downregulated in a differentiated 3D HCC spheroid derived from HepG2 cells. It has also been shown that CAV1 expression increases upon ethanol treatment in HepG2 cells, suggesting that hepatotoxins such as alcohol can alter CAV1 expression [26]. CYB5A is a well-known electron carrier involved in the transfer of electrons from iron porphyrin elements to oxygenases, resulting in the subsequent reduction of ferric to ferrous states of hemoglobin [27]. Elevated CYB5A can mitigate HCC metastasis by inhibiting STAT3 signaling via STOML2 inhibition [28]. Another cluster target, RASSF2, is reported to be significantly downregulated in HBV-associated HCC, and significant promoter hypermethylation has been reported to be the underlying cause of decreased RASSF2 expression [29, 30]. SOD2, another target gene of the oncomiRC critical for maintaining redox balance, is reported to be downregulated in HCC [31, 32]. Similarly, the TXNIP gene acts as a prominent suppressor gene in HCC. Two independent studies reported that TXNIP-deficient mice develop HCC [33] and that TXNIP overexpression induces ROS-mediated apoptosis in HCC cells via MAPK activation [34]. Furthermore, MFSD2A reportedly inhibits TGFβ/SMAD signaling [35], and studies have shown that improved MFSD2A is a positive indicator of survival in HCC patients [36]. Interestingly, TGFβ signaling was identified as one of the pathways targeted by the miR-106b/25 cluster in this study, suggesting a broader involvement and regulation of TGFβ signaling by this cluster in HCC. Additionally, studies have shown that loss of expression of cluster targets such as NR4A3 [37, 38], KLF6 [39–43], and PTPRD [44–46] promotes HCC development and metastasis. Interestingly, our target gene enrichment analysis also revealed that multiple cancer-associated pathways, such as the TGF-beta signaling, VEGFA-VEGFR signaling, EGF/EGFR signaling, and Wnt/B-catenin signaling pathways, were enriched, and some of these pathways, such as the TGF-beta signaling pathway, were found to be directly regulated by genes such as MFSD2A, suggesting a direct functional link with cancer hallmarks such as angiogenesis and metastasis. Therefore, it can be inferred that the overexpression of the miR-106b/25 cluster results in the downregulation of multiple tumor suppressor targets, thereby promoting HCC development and progression.

The availability of early markers for diagnosis and prognosis is critical for HCC management. We used survival data from HCC patients in the LIHC cohort to determine whether miR-106b/25 ocomiRC and its target tumor suppressor genes affect overall survival. We determined that miR-93-5p, TCF4, DNAJB4, MCC, CYB5A, and HCC stage affect overall survival. Moreover, we constructed a combined prognostic model for the cluster as well as its target genes that can be employed to predict disease prognosis with greater specificity and sensitivity and predict the major genes that affect the prognosis model at large. Although the prognostic model presented in the study predicates prognostic value of the miR-106b/25 cluster and its targets in HCC using fairly large clinical cohorts such as the TCGA-LIHC, further validation of the prognostic outcomes in independent clinical cohorts comprising of diverse subtypes of HCC, especially citing the heterogeneity associated with HCC can help validate the suitability of the proposed panel consisting of 17 genes (3 miRNAs cluster and its 14 mRNA targets) in making better prognostic judgements for effective management of HCC.

Finally, through pancancer analysis, prominent drug-gene interactions for the fourteen gene targets of the miR-106b/25 cluster were identified. We observed approximately 25 drugs to be at different stages of clinical trials and 44 drugs to be approved for different cancers, suggesting a greater scope for repurposing these approved drugs for HCC that are currently administered for other cancer types with similar genetic aberrations associated with the miR-106b/25 cluster. Although this approach provides the information related to all drugs that can potentially target the downstream target genes of the miR-106b/25 cluster across cancer types, development of further functional studies on measuring the effects of the predicted drugs on the miR-106b/25 cluster target gene signatures might provide deeper mechanistic insights into areas such as drug uptake, off-target effect, and specificity of these drugs in HCC.

Limitations

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. Although we report multiple oncomiRCs through experimentation using HCC cell lines, the study falls short in terms of clinical validation using an independent clinical cohort. We have attempted to address this by validating the expressions in larger datasets, such as the TCGA-LIHC dataset. However, we feel that an independent validation using HCC and adjacent normal tissues would have been an added strength to the study. Moreover, HCC is a complex and heterogeneous cancer. Our study relied on the HepG2 spheroid model. Validating these observations in other HCC cell lines and 3D-cellular models can be of relevance. Additionally, we used an in silico pipeline to predict gene targets and subsequently validated them in HCC spheroids via whole-transcriptomic sequencing. Further functional studies aimed at characterizing cluster targets either by siRNA-mediated cluster knockdown or complete knockout of miR-106b/25 oncomiRC via gene editing techniques in vitro and in vivo might provide mechanistic insights into cluster regulation in the context of HCC. We further plan to work in this direction, validate the findings of this study in independent patient cohorts, and develop mechanistic studies to decipher the functions of these oncomiRCs in in vitro and in vivo models of HCC in the future.
