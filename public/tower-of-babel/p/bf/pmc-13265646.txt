Herpes B virus and the neglected risk of zoonotic spillover
Authors: Yujin Kim, Ji Min Lee, Jung Joo Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the zoonotic risk of Simplexvirus macacine alpha 1 (B virus), a macaque-associated alphaherpesvirus that can cause severe and frequently fatal encephalitis in humans, and to assess the potential for cross-border risk through international macaque trade.MethodsA comprehensive literature review was conducted to identify documented human B virus infections between 1932 and 2024. International trade records for Macaca species from the CITES Trade Database (2019–2022) were analyzed for trade volume, source, and purpose. Quarantine regulations across countries were compared, using South Korea as a case study.ResultsAmong 33 documented infections, most were occupational exposures in laboratory settings. Recent cases highlight non-occupational risks, including viral reactivation decades after exposure. From 2019 to 2022, exporter-reported CITES data documented over 170,000 live macaques in international trade, with an increasing proportional share of wild-caught animals. Regulatory frameworks varied by country, with South Korea relying on recommendation-based biosafety oversight, whereas the U.S. applies legally binding import-control measures.ConclusionsAlthough rare, B virus poses a severe and underappreciated zoonotic threat. The increasing wild-caught macaque trade and gaps in quarantine protocols necessitate stronger international coordination and the implementation of standardized biosafety regulations within a One Health framework.Keywords: Simplexvirus macacine alpha 1 (B virus), Zoonotic pathogen, Macaque, Encephalitis, Human infection, Occupational exposure

Introduction

Biosafety in work with nonhuman primates (NHPs) matters for worker safety and public health. Zoonotic transmission occurs via bites, scratches, splashes to mucosa, and contact with contaminated surfaces, with risk concentrated in laboratories, animal facilities, and human-macaque interfaces [1].

Macaques (Macaca spp.) are indispensable models but natural hosts of Macacine alphaherpesvirus 1 (B virus), an alphaherpesvirus in the genus Simplexvirus. Without prompt antivirals, fatality is up to approximately 70% [2]. Human infection is rare; peer-reviewed syntheses indicate several dozen documented cases since 1932 [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. Only one human-to-human transmission has been documented [8]. Among the species involved in international primate trade, Macaca fascicularis and M. mulatta have been identified as having the highest zoonotic disease risks [9]. A seroprevalence study conducted in Thailand found that 80% of M. fascicularis residing in human–macaque interface areas were seropositive for B virus-specific IgG antibodies, underscoring the potential risk of transmission at ecological boundaries [10]. Despite the rarity of human infection and the limited evidence for human-to-human transmission, the ability of the virus to establish latency raises concern that exposure to high-risk animals may occasionally result in unrecognized infections prior to clinical diagnosis.

The recent surge in the international trade of NHPs has raised concerns regarding the potential for B virus introduction and transmission. Macaques, particularly M. fascicularis and M. mulatta, are frequently exported from Southeast Asia, India, and China to countries such as South Korea and the United States for research purposes [23], [24]. However, limited traceability and insufficient pre-import health screening of these animals may increase the risk of undetected zoonotic spillover [11]. Environmental and logistical stressors associated with animal transport may further increase the likelihood of viral reactivation and shedding, increasing opportunities for cross-species transmission, which is central to the One-Health paradigm [12].

To date, B virus is not officially listed as a priority pathogen by the World Health Organization (WHO), although it is recognized as a high-risk zoonotic agent. [13]. Nevertheless, because of its neurotropic properties and high lethality, several national biosafety agencies recommend enhanced biosafety containment measures for facilities handling potentially infected primates. In some jurisdictions, propagation or culture of B virus requires BSL-4 containment because of its high lethality in humans [14], [15], [16]. To mitigate risk of this virus, the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH) has issued guidance on NHP quarantine and zoonotic disease surveillance [17], [18]. In the United States, regulatory instruments such as the Code of Federal Regulations (42 CFR) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) biosafety guidelines provide enforceable protocols for B virus management [19], [20]. Nevertheless, regulatory frameworks and enforcement capacity vary across countries. In countries such as South Korea, where macaques are not native and all research animals are imported, the biosecurity risks associated with zoonotic pathogens vary primarily due to international trade and quarantine practices. However, because quarantine procedures largely rely on nonbinding recommendations rather than mandatory regulatory requirements, this regulatory structure may create gaps in biosafety oversight. Differences in national biosafety frameworks may therefore create asymmetries in the management of transboundary zoonotic risks.

Despite increasing recognition of B virus as a fatal zoonotic pathogen, previous studies have largely examined human infection cases, macaque trade dynamics, or biosafety regulations independently. The interaction between these factors—particularly in the context of expanding international primate trade—remains insufficiently explored. From a One Health perspective, understanding how global animal trade and biosafety governance interact is essential for assessing transboundary zoonotic risks. The review evaluates (1) the epidemiology and risk factors for human B virus infection, (2) the zoonotic implications of global Macaca trade, and (3) the need for One Health-oriented biosafety harmonization, using South Korea as an illustrative case of an import-dependent system.

Materials and methods

Data source and scope

We used the full CITES Trade Database download (unaggregated records) compiled by UNEP-WCMC for the CITES Secretariat [21]. We recorded the dataset version (2024.1), download date, and reporting cut-off as displayed on the portal [22]. Analysis was limited to genus Macaca and term = “live”. Records spanned all available years up to the stated cut-off.

Data preparation

Source CSV files were merged into a single table. Because the CITES database contains records reported independently by importing and exporting countries, duplicate reporting of the same shipment may occur. To minimize potential double counting, analyses were based on quantities reported by exporting countries, while importer-reported quantities were aggregated separately and presented in the Supplementary Data. We retained fields needed to summarize annual trade volumes, purpose codes, and source codes. Code definitions followed Resolution Conf. 12.3 (Rev. CoP19) and the CITES annual-report guidelines [23], [24]. Missing or unspecified values were retained as reported in the original CITES records and summarized separately where relevant.

Normative documents and laws

We consulted CITES Article VIII materials on Parties' annual-report obligations and the Guidelines for annual reports [24]. National legal texts were retrieved from official portals: Republic of Korea (Ministry of Government Legislation, English Statutes, Act on the Prevention of Contagious Animal Diseases), United States Government Code of Federal Regulations, (Title 42, §71.53 Requirements for importers of nonhuman primates), and WOAH standards (Terrestrial Code, Ch. 6.12) [17], [25], [26].

Human case identification

We systematically searched PubMed, the Web of Science Core Collection (excluding the Preprint Citation Index), and Google Scholar from database inception to April 21, 2025. The core search strategy incorporated the following synonyms: Macacine alphaherpesvirus 1, Cercopithecine herpesvirus 1, Herpesvirus simiae, herpes B virus, monkey B virus, and herpesvirus B. Titles and abstracts were screened first, followed by full-text review. Studies were eligible for inclusion if they described human infections with laboratory confirmation (isolation, PCR, or seroconversion) and clinical detail, such as possible exposure history, fever, and neurologic signs. We excluded reports of exposure without illness, veterinary exposures with negative follow-up, editorials, preprints, and duplicate records. Reference lists of included articles were hand-searched to identify additional relevant publications. Screening and data extraction were performed independently by two reviewers. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Using this strategy, 33 published human infections were retained for analysis; this set represents a strict subset of historical totals under our criteria.

Reproducibility

All code and queries are available upon request; version, download date, and filter logic are reported to enable replication. CITES data are self-reported by Parties; estimates should be interpreted with this limitation.

Results

Human B virus cases

Confirmed cases were defined based on the diagnostic criteria reported in the original publications, including virus isolation, PCR detection, or serological evidence of infection. Between 1932 and 2024, 33 confirmed human cases of B virus infection were reported, with 24 cases occurring in the United States. Additional cases were reported in the United Kingdom, Canada, Japan, China, Belgium/the Netherlands (single case spanning both countries) (Table 1). Most infections occurred in individuals who were occupationally exposed to NHPs, including facility workers (n = 12), animal attendants or caretakers (n = 9), researchers or laboratory workers (n = 6), and veterinarians or veterinary technicians (n = 3).

Human Cases of B Virus Infection (1932–2024): Epidemiological and Clinical Summary.

Non-occupational exposure was rare. Only three confirmed cases were classified as non-occupational. One involved a household member of an occupationally exposed individual, suggesting indirect occupational exposure [43]. The remaining two non-occupational cases were a late reactivation 54 years after a bite from a privately kept pet macaque and a community exposure associated with contact with a wild macaque [5], [6] (Supplementary Table 1).

Direct trauma from macaques was the most common transmission route (n = 19, 57.6%). Among these cases, bite-only injuries accounted for nine cases, scratch-only injuries for two cases, and combined bite-and-scratch injuries for six cases; two additional cases involved lacerations. Other reported exposures included needlestick injuries from contaminated instruments (n = 2), contact with bodily fluids (n = 3), and unknown exposure (n = 9).

The incubation period typically ranged from 2 to 30 days. However, rare cases of viral reactivation have been reported decades after the presumed initial infection. A recent Belgium/the Netherlands case report documented viral reactivation 54 years after the presumed initial exposure, illustrating the long-term latency potential of B virus [5].

Clinical outcomes differed markedly before and after the introduction of antiviral therapy. Among the 19 cases reported before antiviral treatment became available in 1987, outcomes were generally poor. Fourteen patients died within approximately one month of symptom onset (14/19, 73.7%). One patient survived for 3 years and 4 months after disease onset. Treatment during this period was largely supportive and included iodine disinfection, corticosteroids, or antibiotics. Among the surviving patients, one developed severe neurological sequela, including inability to walk independently and profound communication impairment.

Following the introduction of antiviral agents such as acyclovir and ganciclovir, survival outcomes were improved. Among the 14 cases reported after antiviral therapy became available, clinical outcomes were unavailable for three patients due to privacy restrictions. Of the remaining 11 cases with documented outcomes, six patients recovered without major complications.

Among the cases reviewed in this study, PCR-based diagnostic testing was reported only in five cases occurred since 2014, corresponding to cases 29–33 in Table 1. In two cases (cases 29 and 30), PCR analysis was performed retrospectively using stored neural tissue samples to confirm the diagnosis [7]. Among the remaining cases in which PCR testing was performed during active disease, one case (case 33) was excluded because the clinical outcome could not be verified [6].

Of the two remaining cases, one patient (case 32) was diagnosed by PCR within three days of hospital admission and received immediate antiviral therapy [5]. In contrast, another patient (case 31) required approximately one month after symptom onset before PCR confirmation was obtained and ultimately died despite supportive treatment in intensive care unit [4].

Data for cases 1–28 are adapted from Knauf and Jones-Engel [3].

International trade of Macaca spp. and implications for B virus transmission

According to data from the CITES, 179,759 live macaques (Macaca spp.), all potential hosts of B virus, were recorded in international trade between 2019 and 2022 (Table 2). Annual volumes declined from 54,448 in 2019 to 17,742 in 2022, peaking at 55,135 in 2021. Of these, 99.46% (n = 179,759) were M. fascicularis. Smaller numbers of M. mulatta (n = 851; 0.47%) and M. nemestrina (n = 58; 0.32%) were also reported. Only minimal numbers of M. nigra and M. radiata have been reported to date. Importer-reported trade totals (Supplementary Table 2) remained with the same overall order of magnitude across 2019–2022. Demand may increase more if biomedical research becomes active. M. fascicularis accounted for more than 98% of the traded macaques, indicating a heavy reliance on a single species.

Trade purposes of Macaca species and B virus exposure environment

CITES trade data from 2019 to 2022 indicate diverse trade purposes for Macaca spp., many of which pose a potential risk of human exposure to the B virus (Table 4). Commercial use was the predominant purpose, accounting for 123,129 individuals (68.5%) with an annual decline from 45,078 (82.79%) in 2019 to 2458 (13.85%) in 2022. Medical use, including biomedical research, was the second most common purpose, with 55,123 participants (30.66%). The proportion increased from 15.62% (n = 8505) in 2019 to 85.85% (n = 15,231) in 2022, representing the highest share of trade in 2022. The scientific research trade declined sharply from 784 individuals in 2019 to 23 in 2022. Minimal numbers were traded for circus/traveling exhibitions, zoo displays, personal ownership, educational use, and reintroduction into the wild. In some cases, the purpose of the trade is unclear or unspecified. These trends illustrate that most macaques are traded for commercial or biomedical research purposes, which inherently involve close contact with humans and increased occupational exposure risks.

Differences in national approaches to the risk of B virus introduction

Policies to mitigate the risk of B virus transmission vary by country and reflect disparities in biosafety priorities and capacities (Table 5). In Chapter 6.12, WOAH recommends that personnel working with macaques be informed of B virus risks, including the lifelong infection status of Asian macaques, and that appropriate prevention, treatment, and response protocols for potential exposures such as bites and scratches be developed and implemented [17].

In the United States, legal mandates under 42 CFR § 71.53 (Requirements for importers of nonhuman primates) require importers to implement personal protective equipment (PPE) programs and maintain standard operating procedures (SOPs). These measures, which are overseen by the CDC, mandate post-exposure protocols and secure transportation systems for diagnostic specimens [26].

South Korea uses a recommendation-based system under the Act on the Prevention of Contagious Animal Diseases and Related Guidelines for Managing Foreign Livestock and Quarantine Facilities [47], [48]. This approach incorporates B virus provisions within export country quarantine expectations while encouraging domestic importers to voluntarily implement pathogen surveillance, biosafety training, and vaccination programs.

These differences indicate heterogeneity in national biosafety governance relevant to B virus risk management. To mitigate transboundary transmission, it is crucial to move beyond voluntary compliance and establish enforceable biosafety regulations. Empowering importing countries with regulatory authorities to verify exporters' compliance and restrict trade when necessary is vital for effective B virus control.

Discussion

Our analysis of 33 documented and reported human infection cases between 1932 and 2024 confirmed that occupational exposure through close contact with Macaca spp.was the primary risk factor for B virus infection. Most exposures occurred in research facilities handling laboratory macaques, and transmission routes included not only traumatic injuries, such as bites and scratches, but also non-traumatic routes, such as contact with small volumes of bodily fluids or mucosal exposure. Notably, a recent Belgian case report documented viral reactivation 54 years after the presumed initial infection, reaffirming the long-term latency potential of B virus [5].

Based on exporter-reported trade-purpose data, 68% of internationally traded Macaca were reported for commercial purposes and 30.7% for medical purposes, including biomedical research (Table 4). These patterns indicate substantial exposure potential for workers involved in transport, quarantine, laboratory animal care, and research [1], [2], [49]. In this context, although EU policy encourages the use of research animals from self-sustaining colonies, reliance on internationally sourced macaques remains high, highlighting a persistent gap between policy and practice. However, these patterns should be interpreted with caution, as international trade data rely on self-reported records that may be subject to reporting inconsistencies. The risk is further compounded when the animals originate from regions with limited surveillance capacity, as breeding constraints in Macaca and uncertainties in Asian supply chains make universal compliance unlikely for all primates [50]. Notably, the rising proportion of wild-caught macaques used in trade is of particular concern, as wild-caught animals may have less certain infection histories and may be less likely to have undergone documented pre-export health screening. Between 2019 and 2022, exporter-reported records documented 179,759 internationally traded live macaques, 99.5% of which were M. fascicularis. B virus can persist latently in macaques, including asymptomatic individuals. Additionally, the presence of confiscated, seized, or unknown-origin animals in trade pipelines introduces nonlinear and unpredictable infection risks, further increasing the likelihood of occupational exposure.

Taken together, the human infection cases identified in this study and the global macaque trade data suggest a structural interface through which B virus exposure can occur. The analysis of 33 documented human infections confirmed that most cases were associated with occupational settings involving close contact with macaques, particularly in laboratory or animal-handling environments. At the same time, international trade data demonstrate the large-scale movement of macaques across national borders. Because macaques are the natural reservoir of B virus, international trade may facilitate the cross-border movement of reservoir hosts and associated spillover risk. This concern is greater for wild-caught animals, whose infection histories are often less certain and whose pre-export health screening may be less consistently documented. Capture, transport, quarantine, and laboratory handling all involve repeated human–animal contact, thereby creating multiple opportunities for exposure. These patterns support the need for stronger traceability, biosafety oversight, and quarantine practices in the international primate trade.

The global transport of macaques represents a potential pathway for the transboundary movement of infected animals. The increasing proportion of wild-caught individuals further amplifies this concern, as these animals are more likely to have been naturally exposed to the virus prior to capture. When considered together, international macaque trade can be understood as potentially creating a global interface between reservoir hosts and high-risk occupational groups. Workers involved in animal transport, quarantine, laboratory research, and animal care may encounter infected macaques originating from diverse geographic regions. This situation highlights the importance of coordinated biosafety management across countries and sectors. Individual institutions cannot manage these safety risks alone, given the occupational exposure patterns identified in this study and the scale of international macaque trade.

However, national responses to B virus risk vary significantly. Some countries adhere to WOAH recommendations at the level of information provision and personal protective equipment guidance, whereas others enforce legally binding requirements, including mandatory PPE use, the establishment of standard operating procedures, and strict post-exposure measures [17], [26], [47], [48]. Competent authorities may grant scientifically justified exemptions, potentially leading to regulatory asymmetry [51]. Such differences in regulatory enforcement and oversight can result in structural disparities in infectious disease risk across countries, highlighting the need for international coordination and harmonization.

The WOAH has proposed guidelines for addressing disease risks in the wildlife trade based on the One Health framework [52]. This approach promotes multisectoral collaboration across human, animal, and environmental health while considering biodiversity conservation, animal welfare, and the regulation of endangered species. In this context, several actions should be prioritized: (1) implementation of a risk assessment framework for potential B virus carriage in traded macaques; (2) establishment of internationally standardized biosafety and post-exposure protocols; (3) enhancement of proactive protection measures for high-risk personnel, including health screening, biosafety training, and vaccination status evaluation; and (4) stricter import controls and quarantine requirements for wild-caught individuals. Despite national frameworks for high-risk pathogen management, intersectoral coordination is often inadequate due to fragmented responsibilities and inconsistent animal classifications. Consequently, pathogens at the human–animal interface may remain poorly regulated, underscoring the need for integrated control mechanisms. Enhanced interagency collaboration and data sharing are essential components of proactive and holistic infectious disease prevention strategies. While these recommendations are informed by available evidence, data limitations across surveillance and reporting systems should be considered in their implementation.

In addition, our analysis identified a 2024 case in Hong Kong demonstrating that exposure can occur outside occupational settings, including through contact with wild animals [6]. In regions such as Thailand, Nepal, Indonesia, Santiago, and Puerto Rico, where humans frequently encounter free-ranging macaques in temples or urban environments, seroprevalence of B virus among macaques has been reported to range from approximately 64% to 100% [10], [53], [54], [55], [56], [57]. Although active viral shedding was identified in only 1.04% of sampled macaques, indicating that shedding is much less common than seropositivity [9], these ecological conditions still create repeated opportunities for human exposure.

With strengthened control measures for occupational exposure and increasing restrictions on keeping wild animals as pets, documented occupational and domestic infections remain rare, but the possibility of under-recognized exposures cannot be excluded [58], [59]. However, interactions between humans and free-ranging wildlife—particularly macaques inhabiting urban or peri-urban environments—may represent an alternative and under-recognized route of exposure [60], [61]. Given the high seroprevalence of B virus among macaques and the presence of active viral shedding, even if infrequent, encounters with these animals could pose a meaningful risk to the general population.

Taken together, these findings suggest that, beyond occupational exposure, additional public health strategies may be required to address non-occupational transmission pathways associated with human–wildlife interactions. Beyond occupational biosafety, public-facing prevention strategies are also needed, including education to avoid direct contact with free-ranging macaques, clear post-exposure guidance, and long-term follow-up systems for exposed individuals. Clear guidelines for all types of exposure, long-term health monitoring, and systems to track potential viral reactivation in exposed individuals are essential.

This review has several limitations. Trade analyses relied on self-reported CITES data and may therefore be affected by reporting delays, missing values, and discrepancies between exporter- and importer-reported records. Human case analysis was restricted to published, laboratory-confirmed infections and may underestimate the true burden of B virus disease. In addition, trade source codes reflect reporting categories rather than individual-level infection status. Accordingly, the present findings are most informative for identifying relative risk patterns rather than precise incidence estimates.

In conclusion, although rare, B virus is a highly lethal zoonotic pathogen with reactivation potential. A One Health approach emphasizing international regulatory harmonization and cross-sectoral collaboration is imperative. Given the growing scale of international laboratory animal trade, integrated lifecycle-based responses and interministerial coordination are urgently required to mitigate the risk of transboundary zoonotic disease transmission.
