Transcriptomic analysis of colorectal carcinoma tissues from a Korean population
Authors: Hoang Bao Khanh Chu, Ji Hye Choi, Jongbum Jeon, Soobok Joe, Hyeran Shim, Jisun Kang, Sheehyun Cho, Hong Seok Lee, Yeong Hak Bang, Jinyeong Lim, Eun Hye Joo, Kyunghee Park, Ji Hwan Moon, Kyung Yeon Han, Woo Yong Lee, Hee Cheol Kim, Seong Hyeon Yun, Yong Beom Cho, Yoon Ah Park, Jung Wook Huh, Jung Kyong Shin, Dae Hee Pyo, Hyekyung Hong, Yourae Hong, Hae-Ock Lee, Jong-Hwan Kim, Jinseon Yoo, Jaeeun Jung, Jin Ok Yang, Woong-Yang Park, Young-Joon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs a disease with a high mortality rate, colorectal cancer (CRC) highlights the importance of comprehending its molecular mechanisms to develop effective therapies. In this study, we conducted RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis on 476 samples consisting of fresh-frozen CRC tissues and adjacent normal tissues obtained from Samsung Medical Center (SMC) in South Korea. By analyzing gene expression differences, we identified that upregulated genes in tumor samples were significantly associated with pathways related to the cell cycle, extracellular matrix (ECM)-receptor interaction, and DNA damage response (DDR). Conversely, genes exhibiting high expression in normal samples were primarily related to metabolic pathways. Tumor samples were found to be enriched in various DDR pathways, prompting an investigation into the enrichment of related genes. Additionally, the analysis focused on the Homologous recombination (HR) pathway, for which the Homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) score was calculated. Furthermore, we categorized colorectal tumor samples into four subgroups using in silico methods to identify the biological and clinical characteristics of each subgroup and compared our results with those of the widely used existing subgrouping methods. Majority of our fibroblast and immune enriched/fibroblast samples aligned with CMS4, characterized by stromal invasion and mesenchymal differentiation, whereas the immune enriched subtype aligned with CMS1, known for its immune-centric profile. To investigate the clinical implications, the composition of the tumor microenvironment (TME) by cell type was examined across four subgroups, along with an immunological analysis. In particular, the calculated HRD score and the expression levels of immune-related genes associated with CRC were analyzed to provide evidence for personalized therapeutic strategies for each subgroup. Our findings provide valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying CRC in Korean patients, particularly in the SMC patient group, and offer opportunities for personalized therapeutic strategies.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Consensus molecular subtypes, CRC intrinsic subtypes, Homologous recombination deficiency, RNA-seq

INTRODUCTION

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second most diagnosed cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide (1). Traditionally, the TNM (Tumor, Node, Metastasis) staging system has been a pivotal tool for predicting the prognostic outcomes of CRC (2). And, growing evidence suggests that tumor progression and recurrence are not only influenced by genetic alterations within cancer cells but also by tumor microenvironmental factors (3). The heterogeneous immune subsets present within the CRC microenvironment intricately interact with tumor cells and stromal components, playing crucial roles in immune evasion and tumor progression (4). Notably, the efficacy of cetuximab, an epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitor, is significantly reduced in right-sided CRC cases, even in RAS wild-type tumors (5). Although high microsatellite instability (MSI-H) has traditionally been associated with poor prognosis, recent studies have shown that deficient mismatch repair (dMMR)/MSI-H CRC exhibits high sensitivity to immunotherapy, leading to improved clinical outcomes. Nevertheless, resistance and tolerance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have been observed in some cases, prompting ongoing research to understand the underlying mechanisms and to develop strategies to overcome these challenges (6). Given the substantial variations in clinical prognoses of CRC based on diverse characteristics, a comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying its development and progression is imperative. However, limited knowledge exists regarding how transcriptomic disparities, including microenvironmental and molecular characteristics, contribute to CRC prognostication. In this study, we performed a comprehensive analysis of RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from normal colon and tumor samples, categorized by various baseline characteristics. This analysis provides a collection of RNA-seq datasets enriched with clinical and molecular information. We examined the molecular characteristics and, notably, conducted subtype-based analyses using in-silico approaches. Specifically, for Korean CRC patients from Samsung Medical Center (SMC), we applied bioinformatics approaches to explore patient-specific therapeutic strategies. These data are expected to have broad applications, including depicting molecular characteristics of subtypes, exploring novel biomarkers, identifying prognostic signatures, and analyzing treatment-related variations.

RESULTS

Clinical characteristics of our study cohort

The characteristics of our study cohort are presented in Table 1. A total of 232 tumor patients diagnosed with CRC from SMC were included in this study. The median age of the patients was 63 years (range: 23-90 years), with 138 patients (59.5%) aged above 60 years and 94 patients (40.5%) aged below 60 years. Regarding sex distribution, the majority of the patients were male (N = 136, 58.6%), while female patients accounted for 41.4% (N = 96). Tumor location varied, with an equal number of cases (N = 90, 38.8%) observed in the right-sided and left-sided colon. Additionally, 52 patients (22.4%) had rectal cancer. The MSI status was also analyzed, revealing that most patients (N = 186, 80.2%) had microsatellite stable (MSS) tumors, while 38 patients (16.4%) had tumors exhibiting MSI-H. A small proportion (N = 4, 1.7%) had low microsatellite instability (MSI-L), and for 4 patients (1.7%), MSI status was not available. Based on the AJCC 8th edition staging system, the majority of patients (N = 140, 60.3%) were diagnosed at stage III, followed by stage II (N = 70, 30.2%). Stage I and IV were less common, with 10 (4.3%) and 12 (5.2%) patients, respectively. The TNM staging system is widely used to classify the severity and extent of cancer by evaluating three key components: the size and extent of the primary tumor (T), the involvement of regional lymph nodes (N), and the presence of distant metastasis (M). Further classification based on tumor stage (T stage) showed that 17 patients (7.3%) were in stage II, while the majority (N = 156, 67.2%) were in stage III. A total of 59 patients (25.4%) had IV tumors. In terms of node stage (N stage), 80 patients (34.5%) had no lymph node metastasis (N0), 107 patients (46.1%) had N1 involvement, and 45 patients (19.4%) had N2 involvement. Metastasis stage (M stage) were present in 13 patients (5.6%), while 219 patients (94.4%) did not exhibit distant metastases. Tumor differentiation was primarily moderate, with 152 patients (65.5%) classified as having moderately differentiated tumors. Well-differentiated tumors were observed in 48 patients (20.7%), while 11 patients (4.7%) had poorly differentiated tumors. Additionally, 16 patients (6.9%) had mucinous tumors, and information on tumor differentiation was unavailable for 5 patients (2.2%). Pathologically, the vast majority of tumors were adenocarcinomas (N = 210, 90.5%), while mucinous carcinomas were diagnosed in 19 patients (8.2%). Pathology data were not available for 3 patients (1.3%). Overall, this cohort provides a comprehensive representation of tumor patients, highlighting key clinical and pathological features relevant to CRC at SMC.

Sample quality check, batch effect removal, and sample filtering of SMC cohort

The RNA-seq data of our cohort comprised 476 normal and tumor samples obtained from SMC in South Korea. A higher proportion of samples in the cohort were matched between tumor and normal compared with unmatched samples (Fig. 1A). All samples exhibited Phred scores above 20 Supplementary Fig. 1A, Supplementary Table 1). The average mapped length distribution of reads across all samples was similar, and the number of mapped reads exceeded 40 million, indicating sufficient saturation for analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1B-D, Supplementary Table 1). The fastq files were aligned using STAR, while RSEM was employed to measure read counts (Fig. 1B). The pre-processing step was conducted without trimming by default, as trimming based on quality scores can significantly impact the estimation of gene and isoform expression levels (7). Consequently, a dataset encompassing RNA-seq data from 476 samples was generated. Upon visualizing the data in dimension plots, the labeled archival (PA-AU) and prospective (PA-PU) samples appeared as separate clusters. To address this batch effect, ComBat (8), a method for batch effect normalization, was utilized to apply batch correction between the two sample groups. Additionally, 13 samples that displayed mixed characteristics between normal and tumor clusters were removed (Supplementary Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table 2). t-SNE analysis of the unfiltered and batch-effect-corrected samples revealed a homogenized pattern between archival and prospective samples (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Notably, the final tumor samples were 232, and the normal samples were 231. Distinct clustering confirmed the separation between the groups (Fig. 1C).

Transcriptomic characterization of tumor samples and adjacent normal samples

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the final normal and tumor samples obtained after t-SNE filtering were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as normality testing indicated that the data did not follow a normal distribution (Supplementary Fig. 3). Keeping the Wilcoxon rank-sum test threshold for selective genes with log2 fold change (FC) between tumor and normal samples higher than 1.5-fold and adjusted P-value < 0.05, we detected 59 genes upregulated in tumor samples and 236 genes upregulated in normal samples (Fig. 2A, B and Supplementary Table 3). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of genes differentially expressed between normal and tumor samples was performed using gene signatures in the KEGG database. Genes highly expressed in tumor samples were related to cell cycle signaling, extracellular matrix (ECM)-receptor interaction, and DNA damage response (DDR) pathway, while upregulated genes in normal samples were associated with metabolism-related signatures (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table 3). In tumors, various pathways related to DDR are enriched, among which the presence of homologous recombination (HR) suggests a close association with homologous recombination defects (HRD). Recent studies have shown that defects in the HR pathway are present in up to 15% of CRC cases, offering promising prospects for developing innovative therapeutic strategies for CRC patients (9). Thus, analyzing SMC tumor samples utilizing HRD information can contribute to the development of tailored treatment strategies for these patients. To this end, we applied the expHRD tool, originally developed as a method for calculating HRD scores based on transcriptomic data, to compute HRD scores. Our results demonstrate that the HRD scores predicted by expHRD are reliable, and the expHRD results indicate that tumors exhibit higher HRD scores compared to normal samples (Supplementary Fig. 4). Furthermore, genes highly expressed in tumor samples included those related to the tumor microenvironment (TME), immunotherapy, and chemotherapy, such as COL1A1, COL1A2, CXCL1, and CCL20 (10-12). In particular, the high expression of MYC, which is associated with HR in DDR, in tumor samples from SMC suggests a strong association between HR and these tumor samples (Fig. 2D) (13). When considered alongside the high expression of CXCL1 and CCL20 genes and the results of GSEA, it highlights the importance of HR-based personalized therapeutic strategies for patients.

Dissecting TME dynamics and subtype interplay in CRC

As CRC diagnoses increasingly affect a younger population and more advanced stages of the disease become prevalent, the urgency to broaden available CRC therapies is growing. Immunotherapy has shown great promise as an alternative treatment when conventional methods fall short, offering improved response durability, reduced recurrence, fewer side effects, enhanced overall survival, and better post-treatment quality of life. Although currently used mainly as an adjunct to first-line treatments like surgery and limited to a subset of patients, such as those with dMMR/MSI-H or mCRC, the role and application of immunotherapy in CRC continue to expand. Ongoing research aims to extend the benefits of CRC immunotherapy to a wider patient base by identifying novel biomarkers and TME that influence the antitumor immune response. Additionally, advancements in combination therapy strategies highlight the importance of comprehensively understanding the immune landscape of CRC to optimize immunotherapeutic approaches (14). In addition, by leveraging cutting-edge TME subtyping methodologies, researchers have examined TME complexity in relation to CRC prognosis, highlighting the importance of accurately characterizing the TME to better understand its role in disease progression and to optimize immunotherapeutic strategies. This has provided a more nuanced understanding of TME heterogeneities in prognosis implications, offering a proof of concept for the predictive value of these microenvironment factors in CRC survival outcomes (15, 16). In this study, SMC data were classified into four distinct subgroups based on 29 TME-related signatures (as depicted in Fig. 3A) (15): immune enriched/fibroblast (IE/F), immune enriched (IE), fibroblast (F), and depleted (D) subtypes. The IE/F subtype was characterized by a high enrichment score for immune infiltration and cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) activation, coupled with a pronounced epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) signature. Notably, this subtype exhibited a reduced proliferation rate gene signature. The IE subtype, distinct for its elevated immune infiltrate, paralleled the IE/F group in terms of an immune-active microenvironment. Conversely, the F and D subtypes demonstrated lower immune activation. The F subtype was marked by minimal lymphocyte/leukocyte infiltration, enhanced angiogenesis, and increased CAF, along with elevated TGF-β pathway activation, a known immune suppressor (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table 4) (17). The D subtype, aligning with immune-deserted phenotypes, exhibited the highest tumor proliferation levels.

Prognostic implications of TME classification in relation to other CRC molecular subtypes

In CRC, the consensus molecular subtypes (CMS) are intrinsically linked to the TME (18). Studies have found that CMS subtypes, particularly CMS4, are associated with a TME characterized by strong stromal activation and mesenchymal features, often leading to poorer prognosis (19). Consistent with previous findings, our analysis revealed that subtypes with high stromal activity, compared with immune-rich subtypes, correlated with poorer prognoses (F vs. D, P-value = 0.05; F vs. IE, P-value = 0.05, Fig. 3C). To address the complex interplay between the TME and molecular subtypes of CRC, we classified renowned CRC-related molecular subtypes. The majority of F and IE/F samples were categorized as CMS4, known for its mesenchymal differentiation and stromal invasion features, and were associated with adverse outcomes. In contrast, the IE subtype showed substantial overlap with CMS1, characterized by robust immune activation. The D subtype predominantly comprised CMS2 and CMS3, subtypes intrinsically linked to cancer cells and notable for metabolic dysregulation (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Table 5). Additionally, we identified the characteristics of the four subtypes classified by 29 functional gene expression signatures (Fges) using the ESTIMATE tool and gene-level expression analysis, confirming the consistency of the previously mentioned features (Supplementary Fig. 5). Finally, classification based on the existing CMS subgroups confirmed similarities with previously known CRC characteristics. By analyzing HRD scores in relation to MSI status and CMS, we found that the MSI group is more closely linked to dMMR than to HR itself. This is supported by the order of HRD scores—MSI-L, MSS, and MSI-H. These findings highlight the interplay of mutation status, alternative DNA repair pathways, and epigenetic modifications (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Analysis of TME composition and HRD-based clinical significance in four TME subgroups, with validation through The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) analysis

Next, we analyzed the composition of the TME using CIBERSORTx. CIBERSORTx results showed that B cell memory accounted for a high proportion overall, while other cell types exhibited differences depending on the four TME subgroups (Fig. 4A). When examining the HRD scores by four TME subgroups, the IE/F and IE groups showed relatively low scores. Furthermore, the expression levels of two CRC-related immune genes, HAVCR2 and GZMA, were both high in the IE/F group, whereas only GZMA expression was high in the IE group (Fig. 4B, C) (20). These results suggest that a personalized treatment approach is necessary for patient management at SMC, with TIM-3 inhibitors being particularly applicable for the IE/F group due to TIM-3 interacting with ligands such as Galectin-9, Phosphatidylserine, HMGB1, and CEACAM1 to deliver immunosuppressive signals (21). A strategy to maintain or enhance T/NK cell activity for the IE group should also consider that the high expression of GZMA indicates an already elevated immune activation state (22). Additionally, the potential application of PARP inhibitors for the D and F groups, which exhibit relatively high HRD scores, could be explored. Finally, we conducted an analysis based on TCGA data and confirmed results similar to the pathway and subgroup-based analysis of SMC CRC. This indicates the robustness of the SMC CRC analysis results (Supplementary Fig. 7).

DISCUSSION

Our dataset showed that genes upregulated in tumor samples were associated with cell cycle signaling, ECM-receptor interaction, and DDR pathways, while those upregulated in normal samples were linked to metabolism-related signatures. In tumors, the enrichment of various DDR-related pathways, including HR, indicates a strong association with HRD. Additionally, we found that genes related to the TME, cancer therapies, and HR were highly expressed in tumor samples of CRC patients. Our study also explored the relationship between TME heterogeneity and CRC patient survival and revealed a significant overlap between TME subtypes, the established CMS, and their functionally relevant biological pathways. The majority of F and IE/F samples aligned with the CMS4 subtype, which is characterized by stromal invasion and mesenchymal differentiation, further correlating these features with adverse outcomes. Conversely, the IE subtype’s alignment with CMS1, known for its immune-centric profile, highlights the potential for leveraging immune activation in therapeutic strategies. These correlations are not only consistent with previous reports but also expand our understanding of how molecular diversity within CRC affects patient survival. Furthermore, the IE/F group exhibited both immune evasion through suppression and simultaneous immune activation in various in silico analyses, indicating that the TME is complex and highly dynamic. Additionally, HRD score-based subgroup analysis and the examination of immune-related gene expression in CRC confirmed findings that support personalized treatment strategies for SMC CRC patients. The IE/F group demonstrated potential applicability for TIM-3 inhibitors, while the IE group suggested a strategy to maintain and enhance T/NK cell activity. Meanwhile, the D and F groups indicated the potential use of PARP inhibitors. These findings were consistent with results obtained from TCGA-CRC analysis. Overall, this study highlighted the clinical characteristics of CRC patients and provided insights into the transcriptomic profiles of tumor samples based on CRC subtypes. Our results offer a representative dataset for validating the relationship between clinical and transcriptomic features. As we continue efforts to build more comprehensive transcriptomic profiles, future studies based on this dataset could further enhance our understanding of CRC, offering valuable implications for molecular characterization, biomarker discovery, and treatment strategies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients and tissue samples

The study included 50 retrospective and 182 prospective patient samples. Normal tissues were collected at first from an area at least 5 cm away from the tumor site. Both tumor and normal tissue samples, each measuring at least 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 cm, were collected during surgery, immediately snap-frozen, and stored at −70°C. Patients providing prospective samples gave written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the institution review board (IRB) of SMC (registration number: 7001988-2024-BR-727-12), conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients included in the study were clinically staged as I, II, III, or IV, with the majority in stages II and III. The following clinical information relevant to surgical outcomes was recorded: sex, age at surgery, tumor location, diagnostic pathology, T-, N-, and M-stage, differentiation, number of lymph nodes (based on a count of 12), lymphovascular invasion, MSI status, IHC results for KRAS, NRAS and BRAF, adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy, survival status, and 5-year overall and disease-free survival outcomes.

RNA extraction and RNA-seq

Tumor and matched normal fresh-frozen tissues were obtained from patients with CRC. The tissues were dissected to 20-40 mg and homogenized 3-4 times for 15 s at a frequency of 30 Hz using Tissue Lyser II (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). RNA was extracted from the tissue samples using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the standard animal tissue RNA extraction protocol. Total RNA concentration was calculated using Quant-IT RiboGreen (R11490; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). To assess the integrity of the total RNA, samples were run on the TapeStation RNA ScreenTape (5067-5576; Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Only high-quality RNA preparations, with RNA integrity number (RIN) greater than 7.0, were used for RNA library construction. The total RNA was subjected to rRNA depletion using the Ribo-Zero Gold rRNA Removal Kit (MRZG12324; Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and then added to 2 μl of 100-fold diluted ERCC Mix 2 solution of ERCC RNA Spike-In Mix (4456740; Ambion, Austin, TX, USA). The RNA-seq library was prepared using the TruSeq RNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The libraries were then submitted to an Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), and the paired-end (2 × 100 bp) sequencing was performed by Macrogen, Inc. (Gangnam-gu, Seoul, Republic of Korea).

RNA pre-processing

The quality of the FASTQ files was determined using MultiQC. The GRCh38 reference genome and the gene annotation GTF file (GENCODEv27) of human were downloaded from GENCODE (https://www.gencodegenes.org/human/), and ERCC sequence FASTA and GTF annotation were downloaded from Thermo Fisher (https://assets.thermofisher.com/TFS-Assets/LSG/manuals/ERCC92.zip). Genome indexing and alignment were performed using STAR (v2.5.3a), and gene expression levels were quantified using RSEM (v1.3.0) (23, 24). The transcripts per million (TPM) value of each gene was scaled with ERCC genes excluded and normalized by adding 1 and log2 for further analysis.

Batch effect removal

Genes with low expression were removed before batch effect correction. Genes with TPM > 1 in more than 70% of the cohort were selected. Log2(TPM ＋ 1) was used to check whether batch effect was distinctive between archival and prospective samples. Batch effect removal between the two sample groups was conducted using ComBat in the R package SVAseq.

Gene selection and pathway analysis between normal and tumor samples

Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for analyzing DEGs selected between normal and tumor samples with FC > 1.5 and adjusted P-value < 0.05. GSEA (25) and overall-representation analysis (ORA) (26) was performed using the Genekitr (27) web server with categories of KEGG-related pathways. Cut-off threshold of minGSSize = 10, maxGSSize = 500, P-value and P-adjusted value < 0.05 (P-adjusted was calculated using Benjamini-Hochberg [BH] algorithm) was used to obtain significantly enriched gene sets.

Transcriptome-based HRD score calculation

expHRD is an individualized, transcriptome-based prediction model for assessing HRD in cancer. It analyzes the transcriptome data of individual patients to predict HRD status, thereby contributing to the optimization of therapeutic strategies. In this study, we utilized the expHRD tool to calculate HRD scores using transcriptome expression data.

CRC-related molecular subtypes classification and pathway activity

TME subtypes were classified using 29 Fges representing four immune signature modules, including anti-TME, pro-TME, angiogenesis fibrosis, and malignant cell properties. The 29 Fges signature scores were calculated using single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) and classified using the classification algorithm provided by Bagaev et al. CMS prediction was performed using the R package CMScaller (v.0.99.2) (28). Colorectal Intrinsic Subgrouping (CRIS) classification was conducted using classifieR (29). Pathway activity scores were calculated using the PROGENy algorithm (30).

Measurement of immune cell infiltration fractions, immune and stromal scores, and tumor purity

The ESTIMATE tool utilizes gene expression data to estimate tumor purity and the proportions of stromal and immune cell infiltration. In this study, it was used to calculate the immune and stromal scores of tumor samples, and the purity score was estimated by converting the calculated results (31). Additionally, CIBERSORTx was employed to calculate 22 immune cell-infiltrating fractions, and only samples with a P-value < 0.05 were included in the analysis (32).

Statistical analysis

Disease-free survival (DFS) was calculated from the date of surgery to the date of the first tumor recurrence. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the date of surgery to the date of death from any cause or last follow-up. The Kaplan–Meier (KM) method was used to estimate survival outcomes, and the log-rank test (pairwise and/or multivariate) was used to compare survival outcomes among subgroups. Statistical significance between two groups was evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, while comparisons among three or more groups were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. In this study, P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using the R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Validation using public data

To confirm the robustness of the results in this study, TCGA-CRC gene expression data from the UCSC Xena platform (IlluminaHiSeq) was utilized, and DEG analysis, gene expression validation, pathway, and subgroup analyses were performed.
