Immune-associated alternative splicing signatures define molecular subtypes in breast cancer
Authors: Yaran Liu, Ping Wang, Bo Yuan, Ping Zhou, Qiang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAlternative splicing (AS) shapes tumor biology by generating mRNA isoforms that regulate oncogenic signaling, immune modulation, and therapeutic response. However, its immune-related role in breast cancer (BRCA) remains insufficiently defined. Using TCGA-BRCA RNA-seq and clinical data, we quantified percent-spliced-in values with SpliceSeq and identified 1,063 differentially expressed AS events across 861 genes. These genes were enriched in cancer- and immune-related pathways, including focal adhesion, VEGF signaling, and cytokine-receptor interactions. Prognostic analyses revealed immune-associated AS events linked to overall survival and progression-free interval. Consensus clustering defined three AS-based subtypes with distinct immune landscapes and outcomes. C3 showed high immune infiltration, immune-activating molecule expression, and favorable prognosis, consistent with an immune-hot phenotype, whereas C1 displayed immunosuppressive features. Genomic alterations in splicing factors were associated with elevated tumor mutation burden, suggesting genomic instability may contribute to immune-related splicing dysregulation in BRCA.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BRCA) is the most commonly diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer-related death among women worldwide. In 2018, it accounted for approximately 2.09 million new cases and 0.63 million deaths, with both incidence and mortality continuing to rise.1 BRCA exhibits substantial clinicopathological and molecular heterogeneity, which underlies differences in treatment response and prognosis across subtypes.2 Despite advances in surgical techniques and adjuvant therapies, outcomes remain poor for certain subtypes. For instance, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), which accounts for ∼15% of breast carcinomas, is highly aggressive and resistant to endocrine or HER2-targeted therapy, resulting in limited treatment options and unfavorable prognosis.3,4 Understanding the molecular characteristics of BRCA subtypes is therefore essential for guiding precision medicine and improving patient survival.

Alternative splicing (AS) is a highly regulated process that allows a single gene to generate multiple messenger RNA isoforms, thereby increasing transcriptomic and proteomic diversity. This process is orchestrated by the spliceosome, which comprises five small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (U1, U2, U4, U5, and U6), and regulates the inclusion or exclusion of specific exons.5 AS occurs in approximately 95% of multi-exon mammalian genes and includes patterns such as alternative promoters, alternative terminators, exon skipping, intron retention, and exon rearrangement.6,7,8 AS plays a critical role in tissue-specific gene regulation, protein-protein interactions, and diverse biological processes including immune response,9,10 brain development,11,12 and erythropoiesis.13

Aberrant AS is increasingly recognized as a hallmark of cancer, contributing to tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis. Dysregulated AS can promote cell proliferation, inhibit apoptosis, enable immune evasion, and facilitate other oncogenic processes.7 Furthermore, alterations in splicing factors (SFs), such as mutations or copy number changes, can drive global splicing dysregulation and generate cancer-specific isoforms that support tumor growth.14,15,16 In BRCA, as well as other cancers including colorectal carcinoma, glioblastoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma, disrupted AS has been shown to play a significant role in disease pathogenesis.17,18,19,20 Emerging evidence also highlights a critical link between AS and tumor immunity. AS modulates the expression of immune checkpoint molecules (e.g., PD-L1 splice variants), neoantigen formation, cytokine signaling, and immune-cell infiltration.21,22 Splicing-derived neoepitopes can shape antigen presentation and influence responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade, while immune-related SFs are frequently altered in cancers with high immune infiltration.23,24 Consequently, AS signatures have begun to attract attention as potential biomarkers for immunotherapy prediction and as targets for splicing-modulating drugs.

The availability of large-scale sequencing resources such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) now allows systematic dissection of AS regulation across tumors.25 Although previous studies have explored AS-based prognostic models in cancers such as glioblastoma, ovarian, and lung cancer,26,27,28 a comprehensive evaluation of AS-mediated molecular heterogeneity and its immune relevance in BRCA remains lacking. In this study, we integrated RNA-seq, genomic, and clinical data from the TCGA-BRCA cohort to systematically characterize the landscape of AS in BRCA. We identified differentially expressed AS (DEAS) events between tumor and adjacent normal tissues, evaluated their prognostic relevance, and defined a splicing-based classification that stratifies tumors into three molecular subtypes with distinct clinical and immune-associated features. To assess the robustness of this classification, we performed extensive internal validation, including resampling-based clustering stability analysis and centroid-based train-test validation. We further evaluated the reproducibility of the subtype structure in an independent BRCA cohort (SCAN-B) using a cross-platform strategy based on shared splicing features. In addition, we investigated the associations between AS patterns and tumor characteristics, including immune cell infiltration, tumor mutation burden (TMB), and molecular subtypes, and explored potential links between splicing regulation and tumor phenotypes. While these analyses provide a comprehensive transcriptomic view of AS-associated heterogeneity in BRCA, we emphasize that the observed relationships are primarily correlative and require further functional validation. Collectively, our findings highlight AS as an additional layer of tumor heterogeneity and suggest its potential utility in patient stratification and therapeutic exploration.

Results

Overview of AS events in breast cancer

We comprehensively profiled AS patterns using RNA-seq, WXS, copy-number variation (CNV), and corresponding clinical data from 1,081 female BRCA patients and 113 paired adjacent-normal tissues obtained from the TCGA database (Figure 1A; Table S1). AS quantification was performed with SpliceSeq, and percent-spliced-in (PSI) values were derived for each splicing junction. Additional genomic and functional annotations were integrated using SpliceAid-F,29 gene ontology (GO),30 and CIBERSORT31 to support downstream analyses of SFs regulation, immune features, and clinical outcomes. Across the BRCA cohort, we detected 115,397 AS events spanning 14,932 genes, categorized into seven canonical types: alternate acceptor (AA), alternate donor (AD), alternate promoter (AP), alternate terminator (AT), exon skip (ES), mutually exclusive exons (ME), and retained intron (RI) (Figure 1B). To ensure robustness, only AS events with an average PSI ≥ 0.05 and detected in over 75% of samples were retained. After stringent filtering within this pipeline (average PSI ≥ 0.05 and detection in ≥75% of samples), 38,889 AS events from 10,368 genes were retained for further analysis (Figure 1C). Among these, exon-skipping events were the most prevalent (∼40%), followed by AP and AT events, together accounting for the majority of observed splicing diversity (Figure 1D). Event totals and type proportions may be influenced by the analytical pipeline and cohort characteristics; accordingly, we performed additional cross-tool (SUPPA2) and independent cohort validation analyses to assess the robustness of these patterns. UpSet analysis demonstrated that more than 44% of genes exhibited multiple AS event types, underscoring the extensive isoform complexity of BRCA (Figure 1E). Genome-wide visualization using Circos plots revealed broad chromosomal distribution of AS events, with clustering in regions containing splicing-active genes (Figure 1F). Collectively, these findings provide a global view of the AS landscape in BRCA and establish a foundational dataset for subsequent analyses of dysregulated and prognostic splicing events.

Identification and characterization of aberrant AS events in BRCA

To identify aberrant AS events associated with breast tumorigenesis, we compared PSI profiles between 113 tumor samples and their matched adjacent-normal tissues from the TCGA BRCA cohort. To ensure robustness, only AS events with an average PSI ≥ 0.05 and detected in over 75% of samples were retained. Differentially expressed AS (DEAS) events between tumor and paired normal tissues (n = 113) were identified using paired Mann-Whitney U tests, with adjusted p < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1 considered significant. We identified 1,063 DEAS events across 861 genes in bulk RNA-seq without purity adjustment, comprising 429 AP, 358 ES, 165 AT, 39 AD, 33 RI, 30 AA, and 9 ME events (Figures 2A–2C; Table S2). Unsupervised hierarchical clustering based on PSI values of these DEAS events clearly distinguished tumor from normal tissues, demonstrating robust detection and subtype-specific splicing signatures (Figure 2A). Representative DEAS events including TANK (AP), JUP (AT), PDP1 (ES), MMP19 (RI), ECM2 (AA), and CCT3 (ME) illustrate diverse exon-intron structures and differential PSI patterns between tumor and normal samples (Figures 2B and 2E). DEAS events were predominantly composed of AP (40.4%) and ES (33.7%) events, followed by AT (15.5%), while the remaining event types (AA, AD, RI, and ME) collectively contributed a relatively minor fraction, suggesting a preferential involvement of transcriptional start and exon-skipping regulation in tumor-associated splicing alterations (Figure 2C). Quantitative comparisons confirmed significant inclusion or exclusion of specific exons in these genes, highlighting the functional variety of AS dysregulation in BRCA (Table S3). Among the DEAS-associated genes, 234 out of 861 (approximately 27%) were also differentially expressed at the mRNA level (Figure 2D), indicating a partial overlap between AS and gene expression changes. To further characterize this overlapping subset, we examined the distribution of splicing event types. AP and AT events were the most prevalent, accounting for 43.9% and 26.3% of events, respectively (Figure S1), suggesting that these event types are relatively enriched among genes showing both splicing and expression alterations. However, this observation reflects the distribution of event types within the overlapping subset and does not imply a direct causal relationship between alternative promoter or terminator usage and overall gene expression changes. This interplay between splicing and expression suggests that promoter and terminator selection contribute substantially to gene expression regulation in BRCA. DEAS parent genes show enrichment in cancer and immune pathways in bulk profiles (Figures S2A and S2B). Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis further revealed that DEAS-associated genes form interconnected modules, emphasizing coordinated regulation of BRCA-related processes (Figure S2C). Overall, these results reveal widespread but selective alterations in splicing regulation in BRCA, underscoring the complexity of transcript isoform remodeling during tumor progression.

Validation of AS patterns across analytical methods and independent datasets

To evaluate the robustness of our findings across analytical frameworks and independent cohorts, we performed both cross-tool and cross-cohort validation analyses. First, we re-analyzed the TCGA cohort using an AS detection method, SUPPA2.32 This analysis reproduced the broad landscape of AS, including the relative distribution of major splicing event types and the proportion of genes affected by different categories (Figures 3A–3D). At the gene level, however, the overlap between tools was limited: 161 genes were shared between SpliceSeq and SUPPA2, whereas 751 genes were detected only by SpliceSeq and 1,361 only by SUPPA2. Thus, shared genes represented 7.1% of the union of genes detected by either tool (161/2,273), 17.7% of SpliceSeq-detected genes, and 10.6% of SUPPA2-detected genes. These results indicate partial agreement in broad event-type distributions but limited gene-level concordance between the two pipelines. To further assess reproducibility across independent patient samples, we analyzed an in-house RNA-seq dataset comprising breast tumors and matched adjacent normal tissues. Consistent with the TCGA cohort, clear differential splicing patterns were observed between tumor and normal samples, and a subset of DEAS-associated genes was reproducibly identified across datasets (Figures 3E and 3F). In addition, representative splicing events showed consistent PSI changes in the in-house cohort (Figure 3J). To provide experimental support at the isoform level, selected events were further validated using qPCR and RT-PCR. For example, the long-to-total isoform ratio of CA6 was significantly reduced in tumor samples, consistent with the predicted isoform switch (Figure 3G). Similarly, differential splicing patterns of representative genes such as BCAS3 and KIF27 were confirmed in breast epithelial and cancer cell lines (Figures 3H and 3I). Prognostic value of differentially expressed AS events in BRCA

To explore the clinical relevance of splicing dysregulation, we evaluated the association between DEAS events and patient survival outcomes in the TCGA BRCA cohort. Clinical validation confirmed that established prognostic variables such as AJCC stage and PAM50 subtype remained significantly associated with both overall survival (OS) and progression-free interval (PFI), confirming the robustness of the dataset for survival modeling (Figure S3; Table S4). Using the median PSI value as the cutoff, univariate Cox regression identified 90 DEAS events significantly correlated with OS and 86 events associated with PFI (p < 0.05), with 29 events overlapping between the two endpoints (Figure 4A; Table S5). The most prognostic events included AT and ES types from genes such as RNF165, MYB, RAB7A, and CA6. Notably, exon-skipping events generally exhibited protective associations, whereas AP and AT events were more frequently linked to adverse prognosis. To determine independent prognostic factors, significant candidates from univariate analysis were further evaluated using multivariate Cox regression. Four DEAS events (AT in RNF165, ES in CLASP1, AT in PCDHA1, and AP in DST) remained independently predictive of OS and/or PFI (Figure 4B). Among them, RNF165-AT consistently associated with favorable outcomes in both survival endpoints (OS HR = 0.37, p = 0.008; PFI HR = 0.42, p = 0.020), while MYB-ES and PCDHA1-AT predicted poorer prognosis. Genomic alterations of SFs in breast cancer

SFs are key regulators of AS, influencing exon inclusion or exclusion by modulating splicing-site selection and spliceosome assembly. To elucidate the regulatory relationships between SFs and aberrant splicing patterns in BRCA, we curated a comprehensive list of 358 SF genes from GO annotations (“RNA splicing,” GO:0008380; “spliceosomal complex,” GO:0005681) and the SpliceAid-F database (Table S6). To elucidate potential genomic drivers of splicing dysregulation in BRCA, we analyzed somatic mutations and CNVs in core SF genes using TCGA whole-exome and CNV data. Among 985 BRCA samples, 120 cases (12.18%) harbored at least one nonsynonymous mutation in splicing-related genes (Figure 5A). The most frequently altered genes included AFF2, SF3B1, SRRM2, KIAA1429, and SNRNP200, with mutation frequencies ranging from 1.5% to 2.5%. Missense mutations predominated, although several nonsense, frameshift, and splicing-site variants were also detected, suggesting potential functional disruption of spliceosome integrity. Representative lollipop plots illustrated recurrent mutation of AFF2 and SF3B1K700E hotspot variants, both occurring within evolutionarily conserved domains (Figure 5B). We also performed a background comparison using randomly sampled gene sets of the same size of SFs to exclude the random effects of mutation. The proportion of samples harboring nonsynonymous mutations in splicing-factor genes was significantly higher than expected by chance (Figure S4), indicating that the observed mutation frequency cannot be solely explained by global mutational burden. In addition, expression analysis showed that the majority of splicing-factor genes are consistently expressed across tumor samples, supporting their transcriptional activity and potential functional relevance (Figure S4). However, we note that mutation enrichment and expression alone do not establish functional impact, and further experimental studies will be required to determine whether these mutations directly contribute to splicing dysregulation. To evaluate the broader impact of these alterations, we compared the mutation burden of SFs with overall TMB. A strong positive correlation was observed between SF mutation load and total TMB (Pearson R = 0.95, p < 0.0001) as well as the MSK-IMPACT panel TMB (Pearson R = 0.93, p < 0.0001), indicating that genomic instability is closely linked to spliceosome gene disruption (Figure 5C). Copy-number analysis revealed widespread structural variation across splicing-related genes (Figure 5D). The top 20 altered SFs showed high frequencies of amplification (e.g., PRPF3, CELF3, SF3B4, ADAR, CLK2, and RBM8A) or deletion (e.g., SF3B3, PRMT7, ESRP2, and NOL3). These CNV events may further modulate splicing activity by changing the dosage of regulatory components. Collectively, these results demonstrate that mutations and CNV alterations in SF genes are frequent in BRCA and likely contribute to the extensive splicing heterogeneity observed across molecular and clinical subtypes.

Correlation between DEAS events and SFs

SFs play critical roles in regulating AS by modulating splice site selection.33 Pearson correlation analysis between DEAS PSI values and SF expression revealed 53 DEAS events significantly correlated with 59 SFs (|Pearson R| > 0.4, p < 0.05) (Figure 6A). Most DEAS correlated with multiple SFs, and conversely, many SFs regulated multiple AS events, indicating collaborative splicing regulation in BRCA. Representative examples include AP of LIMA1, correlated with HNRNPK and HNRNPF, and RBM5, which positively regulated AA of HNRNPA2B1 but negatively regulated AT of TROAP (Figure 6B). Similarly, NOVA1 and TIAL1, two neuronally associated splicing regulators with emerging roles in epithelial cancers, displayed positive correlations with ES events in EPB41 and AT events in SF3B1, respectively. We further investigated potential regulatory mechanisms underlying DEAS events using a publicly available RNA-seq dataset (GSE147505) in which SF3A3 was experimentally perturbed in BRCA cells.34 Using the same SpliceSeq-based pipeline as in our primary analysis, AS changes following SF3A3 knockdown were quantified and compared with DEAS events identified in the TCGA cohort. A partial overlap was observed among DEAS events identified in TCGA, events correlated with SF3A3 expression, and those altered upon SF3A3 perturbation (Figure S5A), with a subset of events consistently detected across analyses. These overlapping events showed concordant splicing patterns between tumor and normal samples in the TCGA cohort, as well as consistent directional changes following SF3A3 knockdown (Figure S5B). In addition, representative loci exhibited concordant exon usage changes at the read coverage level (Figure S5C), supporting responsiveness of specific DEAS events to SF3A3 perturbation. These findings emphasize that dysregulation of specific SFs is tightly linked to aberrant AS in BRCA. The coexistence of positive and negative correlations within the same SF highlights the dual, context-dependent nature of splicing regulation where individual factors may promote exon inclusion in one gene while facilitating exon exclusion in another. Together, these results underscore the multilayered and cooperative network governing AS regulation in BRCA, implicating SFs as critical upstream modulators of transcriptomic heterogeneity and potential therapeutic targets for restoring normal splicing patterns. However, these results do not establish direct causal or one-to-one regulatory interactions, and further mechanistic studies will be required to define the underlying regulatory mechanisms.

Molecular subtypes of BRCA defined by AS signatures

TCGA-BRCA provides a large, heterogeneous cohort supporting discovery-stage AS-based clustering. To explore whether AS patterns reflect molecular heterogeneity in BRCA, we performed unsupervised consensus clustering based on the PSI values of the 1,063 DEAS events identified previously. The consensus cumulative distribution function (CDF) curves and delta area plots indicated that K = 3 achieved optimal stability and discrimination across samples (Figure 7A). The TCGA-BRCA cohort provides a large and heterogeneous dataset suitable for discovery-stage AS-based clustering, and that stability metrics support a robust K = 3 solution within this dataset. Accordingly, the TCGA-BRCA cohort was stratified into three stable AS-based clusters (C1-C3). To assess the robustness of the DEAS-based three-cluster solution, we performed multiple complementary validation analyses. First, a resampling-based stability analysis was conducted by repeatedly clustering 80% of randomly selected samples over 200 iterations. Across all runs, a three-cluster structure was consistently recovered, with moderate but stable concordance to the original clustering (mean ARI = 0.617), indicating a reproducible global structure. To further evaluate reproducibility in unseen samples, we applied a centroid-based training-testing validation framework. In repeated 80%/20% splits, subtype centroids derived from the training set were used to classify samples in the testing set. This approach demonstrated consistently high predictive performance, with mean accuracy of 0.876 and macro-F1 score of 0.879 across 200 iterations. Patient-level prediction stability was also high, with most samples showing near-perfect reproducibility of subtype assignment (Figures S7A–S7D). In addition, we assessed the sensitivity of the clustering results to key preprocessing and analytical parameters, including PSI thresholds, detection frequency cutoffs, and distance metrics. Across all tested parameter combinations, the three-cluster structure was consistently preserved. Clustering concordance remained high for correlation-based distance measures, particularly Pearson and Spearman, while more stringent rank-based metrics yielded lower but consistent agreement. Notably, variations in PSI and detection thresholds had minimal impact on clustering results, indicating that the identified subtype structure is not driven by specific parameter choices (Figure S7E). Together, these analyses demonstrate that the DEAS-based three-subtype classification is robust, reproducible, and stable across resampling, data partitioning, and methodological variations.

To facilitate practical application and improve the usability of the DEAS-defined subtype framework, we next constructed a supervised classification model based on subtype-associated AS features. Feature selection was performed using LASSO regression, followed by training of a multi-class XGBoost model within the TCGA cohort. Using an internal training-validation framework (80% training and 20% validation), the model achieved high predictive performance, with strong concordance between predicted and reference cluster assignments, as demonstrated by confusion matrix analysis and one-vs-rest ROC curves (Figures S7F–S7I). These results indicate that the identified splicing features provide sufficient discriminatory power to accurately classify subtype identity, further supporting the robustness and translational potential of the DEAS-based subtype framework. Visualization of the consensus matrix confirmed clear separation among the three clusters, demonstrating robust subtype-specific splicing signatures. Integration of clinical and genomic characteristics revealed pronounced biological and pathological heterogeneity across subtypes (Figure 7B). The C1 subtype was enriched for luminal A/B and HER2-positive tumors, exhibited lower TMB, and displayed higher tumor purity, consistent with a less immunogenic phenotype. In contrast, C3 was dominated by basal-like and triple-negative tumors and showed markedly higher TMB (both genome-wide and in SF genes), increased prevalence of TP53 mutations, and reduced tumor purity, which were all features associated with aggressive biology and potential immunogenicity. The C2 subtype represented an intermediate group, combining molecular features of both luminal and basal lineages. The splicing-based stratification also demonstrated partial concordance with PAM50 subtypes but revealed additional cross-category distinctions, suggesting that AS signatures capture unique layers of molecular variation not reflected by transcriptional classification alone. These findings indicate that BRCA tumors exhibit diverse and clinically relevant splicing programs, and AS-based clustering provides an alternative molecular framework that complements existing genomic and transcriptomic classifications.

Kaplan-Meier curves provided an initial assessment of different survival outcomes among AS subtypes. They revealed significant differences in both OS and PFI among the subtypes (OS: p = 0.0002; PFI: p = 0.033), with C1 exhibiting the poorest prognosis and C3 the most favorable outcomes (Figures 8A and 8B). These results indicate that AS-defined subtypes possess strong prognostic relevance in BRCA. We next evaluated whether the observed survival associations are independent of these known prognostic factors. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed, adjusting for AJCC pathologic stage, PAM50 subtype, tumor purity, and TMB. In the adjusted OS model, the AS-based subtype classification retained an independent association with OS at the global level (likelihood-ratio test p = 0.027). Compared with C1, C2 showed a significantly lower mortality risk (HR = 0.51, 95% CI = 0.29–0.89, p = 0.018), whereas C3 showed a similar protective direction but did not reach conventional statistical significance after adjustment (HR = 0.42, 95% CI = 0.17–1.06, p = 0.065; Figure 8G). The wider confidence interval for C3 crossed 1.0, indicating a less precise estimate and suggesting that the adjusted prognostic signal for this comparison should be interpreted cautiously. Among covariates, AJCC stage showed the strongest association with OS, TMB showed a modest effect size, and tumor purity was not significantly associated with survival. Repeated 5-fold cross-validation further showed that the direction of the subtype effect was generally stable, with testing-fold HRs below 1.0 for C2 versus C1 in 98.3% of folds and for C3 versus C1 in 83.2% of folds. However, Figure S8A also shows that the HR distribution was not equally precise for the two comparisons: C2 remained centered well below 1.0, whereas C3 showed broader dispersion and several testing folds with HR estimates above 1.0. Consistently, testing-fold global p values for AS-based subtype were broadly distributed, and only 35.5% of testing folds reached p < 0.05 (Figure S8B). We interpret this weaker validation performance as mainly reflecting the smaller sample size and fewer OS events available in each held-out fold, together with unequal subtype group sizes and the less precise C3 estimate. Under these conditions, the direction of effect can remain largely stable while fold-level statistical significance is lost because confidence intervals widen and global tests become underpowered. Thus, cross-validation supports a directionally consistent prognostic signal within TCGA, but also indicates limited precision and reinforces the need for independent external validation.

GSEA further demonstrated that the C3 subtype was significantly enriched in immune-related pathways, including immune system and adaptive immune system modules from the reactome database (Figure 8C),35 suggesting activation of antitumor immune responses in this group. To quantify immune infiltration, we used immune deconvolution algorithms to estimate the fractions of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and leukocytes.36 Both metrics were markedly higher in C3 compared with C1 and C2 (Figure 8D), consistent with its immunologically active phenotype. Detailed immune-cell profiling revealed that C3 tumors show higher estimated levels of CD8+ T cells, M1 macrophages, activated NK cells, and dendritic cells based on CIBERSORT analysis (Figure 8E), together with elevated expression of immune-stimulatory molecules, such as TNFSF4, CD28, and ICOSLG. In contrast, C1 tumors exhibited higher levels of immune-inhibitory checkpoints, including PDCD1 (PD-1), LAG3, HAVCR2 (TIM-3), and TIGIT, as well as enriched M2 macrophages and Treg cells (Figure 8F), reflecting a more immunosuppressive microenvironment. These immune distinctions align with the clinical outcomes observed across subtypes: the immune-cold C1 cluster corresponds to poor prognosis, whereas the immune-hot C3 cluster corresponds to better survival and higher immune activity. Collectively, these findings highlight that AS-based subtypes not only stratify BRCA by molecular splicing features but also reveal distinct immune landscapes, providing potential insight into differential responses to immunotherapy.

Distinct immune infiltration patterns were observed among C1, C2, and C3 (Figure 9A). Quantitative comparisons confirmed statistically significant differences in immune-cell fractions across subtypes for most immune populations (p < 0.05, Kruskal-Wallis test followed by pairwise comparisons) (Figure 9B). In particular, infiltration of effector immune cells such as CD8+ T cells, M1 macrophages, and activated NK cells was highest in C3, while immunosuppressive components such as M2 macrophages and Tregs were enriched in C1. These results reinforce the notion that AS-based subtypes correspond to distinct immune microenvironments in BRCA. The immune-active C3 subtype may represent a population more likely to respond to immunotherapeutic strategies, whereas the immune-quiescent C1 subtype could benefit from combination regimens aimed at reversing immune suppression.

To evaluate whether the CIBERSORT-based immune-hot interpretation was method dependent, we performed an independent TIMER analysis and incorporated the results into Figures 9C and 9D. TIMER showed higher estimated B cell, CD4+ T cell, neutrophil, and myeloid dendritic-cell infiltration in C3 compared with C1, supporting increased immune infiltration in C3. However, TIMER-estimated CD8+ T cell and macrophage were highest in C2 rather than C3. Therefore, the TIMER comparison provides partial independent support for the C3 immune-hot phenotype but also indicates that individual cell-type trends are not identical across algorithms. Accordingly, the C1 immune-cold/immunosuppressive interpretation is presented cautiously and is based primarily on CIBERSORT cell fractions together with checkpoint-gene expression rather than full replication by TIMER (Figures 9C and 9D).

Cross-cohort validation of DEAS-defined subtypes in an independent breast cancer dataset

An independent BRCA cohort from the SCAN-B study was analyzed to evaluate the generalizability of the DEAS-based subtype classification.37 Due to the lack of access to raw RNA-seq data, the original SpliceSeq-based pipeline could not be directly applied. Instead, transcript-level expression data were used to estimate AS events using SUPPA2, enabling a consistent framework for cross-cohort comparison based on shared splicing features. Clustering analysis based on AS profiles in the SCAN-B cohort identified a three-cluster structure consistent with that observed in the TCGA dataset (Figures S9A–S9D). These subtypes exhibited comparable molecular and clinical characteristics, supporting the reproducibility of the splicing-based classification across independent patient populations.

Notably, the identified subtypes in the SCAN-B cohort were significantly associated with OS, recapitulating the prognostic differences observed in TCGA (Figure S9E). In addition, subtype-specific differences were observed in tumor purity, TMB, and PAM50 molecular subtype distribution, consistent with the patterns identified in the TCGA cohort. Immune-related features also varied across subtypes, with differences in estimated immune cell composition reflecting distinct tumor microenvironment characteristics (Figures S9F–S9J). Together, these results demonstrate that the DEAS-based subtype framework captures reproducible biological and clinical heterogeneity across independent datasets, despite differences in data processing and splicing quantification methods. However, we note that cross-platform differences (SpliceSeq vs. SUPPA2) and input data types may introduce variability, and future studies using harmonized pipelines will further improve cross-cohort comparability. To further evaluate whether the SCAN-B survival association was independent of available clinic-molecular features, we performed univariate and multivariable Cox analyses (Figure S10). In univariate analysis, C2 and C3 were both associated with lower mortality risk relative to C1 (C2 vs. C1: HR = 0.52, 95% CI = 0.40–0.67, p < 0.001; C3 vs. C1: HR = 0.58, 95% CI = 0.44–0.75, p < 0.001). However, after adjustment for PAM50 subtype, continuous TMB, and AJCC 8th prognostic stage, the AS-subtype association was no longer statistically significant (C2 vs. C1: HR = 1.24, 95% CI = 0.83–1.86, p = 0.295; C3 vs. C1: HR = 1.08, 95% CI = 0.72–1.63, p = 0.704). Together, these results indicate that the SCAN-B cohort provides external support for survival stratification among the assigned AS subtypes, but the prognostic signal was attenuated after adjustment for established clinic-molecular variables. Thus, the survival association of AS subtypes in SCAN-B should be interpreted as subtype-associated risk stratification rather than evidence for an independent prognostic biomarker.

Discussion

AS plays a critical role in expanding transcriptomic and proteomic diversity, thereby regulating gene expression and cellular function.38 Aberrant AS events have been implicated in BRCA tumorigenesis, progression, and metastasis. For instance, CD44 generates multiple isoforms via exon skipping or retention, and the switch from CD44s to CD44v promotes epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and metastasis.39,40 Similarly, BRCA1 isoforms, such as BRCA1-Δ11q, are associated with carcinogenesis and drug resistance.41 In line with these studies, our data show upregulation of CD44v isoforms in BRCA patients, supporting the biological relevance of our DEAS analysis. Advances in microarray and next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies have enabled systematic profiling of AS in BRCA.42,43,44 Previous studies reported hundreds of DEAS events across different cell lines or EMT models, influencing cell adhesion, cytoskeletal dynamics, and subtype-specific transcriptional programs.44 Consistently, our analysis identified 1,063 DEAS events from 861 genes associated with BRCA carcinogenesis and progression, enriched in pathways such as focal adhesion, VEGF signaling, immune response, and general cancer pathways. Analyses were performed on bulk RNA-seq and event-level adjustment for tumor purity and immune-cell composition was not implemented, which may influence DEAS detection and pathway enrichment. All analyses were conducted using bulk RNA-seq data. We acknowledge that event-level adjustment for tumor purity and immune cell composition was not implemented, which may influence DEAS detection and downstream pathway enrichment. Future studies incorporating cell type-resolved or single-cell approaches will be important to further refine these findings.

In this study, we applied stringent filters (PSI ≥0.05 in ≥75% of samples) to ensure robust detection, resulting in 38,889 AS events from 10,368 genes. ES events were the most common globally, but only accounted for ∼34% of DEAS, consistent with prior studies.27,45,46 AS alterations identified in this study should be interpreted with caution, as they are largely correlative and may be influenced by broader transcriptional reprogramming, genomic instability, and elevated mutation burden in advanced tumors. While a subset of splicing changes appears independent of gene expression and may have functional relevance, we cannot exclude that some events reflect global transcriptomic alterations. Accordingly, this study focuses on characterizing clinically relevant splicing-associated patterns at the tissue level rather than establishing causal relationships. Future studies integrating single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, isoform-specific perturbation experiments, and multivariate modeling incorporating genomic and transcriptional features will be required to more precisely define the functional significance and cellular origin of splicing alterations in BRCA.

The limited overlap between SpliceSeq and SUPPA2 further highlights an important methodological limitation. Although the two pipelines yielded broadly similar distributions of AS event classes, only 161 genes were shared between the two gene-level DEAS sets, corresponding to 7.1% of the union of genes detected by either tool. This indicates that tool-specific event definitions, transcript annotation strategies, and statistical models substantially influence which genes are nominated as differentially spliced. Therefore, the SpliceSeq-derived DEAS set should be interpreted as a discovery set generated under one analytical framework rather than a tool-independent catalog of all BRCA-associated splicing alterations. In this study, all downstream TCGA analyses were based on SpliceSeq-derived DEAS events, whereas SUPPA2 was used for the cross-tool landscape comparison and, in SCAN-B, for external subtype assignment because raw junction-level data were not available. Future work using harmonized annotations, multiple splicing callers, and independent raw RNA-seq cohorts will be needed to define a more consensus set of BRCA-associated AS events.

We also constructed an SF-DEAS correlation network, revealing that individual DEAS events are often regulated by multiple SFs, while most SFs influence multiple AS events. Some SFs, such as RBM5, can play opposing roles depending on the target AS event, reflecting the combinatorial complexity of splicing regulation. In addition to correlation-based analyses, we incorporated perturbation-based evidence using an independent dataset in which SF3A3 was experimentally knocked down. A subset of DEAS events overlapped with those altered upon SF3A3 perturbation and showed consistent directional changes, providing orthogonal support that specific splicing alterations may be responsive to SF activity. Nevertheless, these results do not establish direct causal or one-to-one regulatory relationships, and further mechanistic studies, such as isoform-specific perturbation and binding assays, will be required to elucidate the underlying regulatory mechanisms.

Aberrant AS has been increasingly recognized as a source of diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic biomarkers.27,45 In our cohort, 90 DEAS events were associated with OS and 86 with PFI. Genes harboring survival-associated AS events, including MYB, TANK, and RAB7A, are known to influence tumor initiation and progression.47,48,49,50 For example, skipping of exons 17 and 18 in MYB (MYB-ES) produces an isoform with altered transcriptional activity, correlating with poor prognosis. Similarly, TANK exhibits isoform-specific effects: TANK-AP (exon 1) is downregulated and associated with favorable outcomes, whereas TANK-AP (exon 2.1) is upregulated in tumors and predicts shorter survival. These findings highlight the functional diversity of AS isoforms and their potential as therapeutic targets. The multivariable TCGA survival analysis supports an independent association between the AS-based subtype classification and OS after adjustment for AJCC stage, PAM50 subtype, tumor purity, and TMB. However, the strength and precision of this evidence differed by subtype comparison: C2 retained a statistically significant protective association relative to C1, whereas C3 showed the same protective direction but with a wider confidence interval crossing 1.0. The cross-validation results further temper the interpretation of these findings. Although most testing folds preserved HR estimates below 1.0 for C2 and C3 relative to C1, the effect estimates were more variable in held-out subsets, particularly for C3. This is expected because repeated 5-fold validation divides an already event-limited TCGA-BRCA survival cohort into smaller testing sets; consequently, each fold contains fewer deaths and fewer patients per subtype, which increases uncertainty around HR estimates. The observation that only 35.5% of testing folds reached p < 0.05 should therefore be interpreted as limited statistical power and precision in fold-level testing, rather than a complete loss of prognostic direction. These data support internal consistency of the subtype-survival trend, but they also argue against overstating the prognostic model as fully validated.

Beyond transcriptional effects, our findings highlight a crucial connection between AS regulation and the tumor immune microenvironment. Using unsupervised consensus clustering, we defined three AS-based molecular subtypes (C1–C3) with distinct clinicopathological and immunogenomic profiles. The C3 subtype exhibited robust immune activation characterized by elevated infiltration of CD8+ T cells, M1 macrophages, and activated NK cells, together with increased expression of immune-stimulatory molecules (TNFSF4, CD28, and ICOSLG). In contrast, the C1 subtype displayed an immunosuppressive phenotype with higher abundance of Tregs and M2 macrophages and upregulation of inhibitory checkpoints (PDCD1, LAG3, HAVCR2, and TIGIT). These immune features were closely aligned with survival outcomes, as the immune-hot C3 subtype demonstrated superior OS and PFI compared with the immune-cold C1 cluster. These observations underscore that AS-based molecular stratification captures immunological diversity beyond that reflected by PAM50 or transcriptomic classifications. Splicing regulation may influence immune responsiveness by modulating antigen presentation, cytokine signaling, and expression of immune checkpoint isoforms. For instance, PD-L1 AS generates truncated isoforms lacking transmembrane domains, thereby altering immune escape dynamics.21 Such mechanisms may underlie the divergent immune landscapes observed across our AS-defined clusters.

From a translational perspective, these findings open additional avenues for AS-guided immunotherapy. The immune-hot AS subtype (C3) could represent a patient population more likely to benefit from immune checkpoint blockade, whereas the immune-cold subtype (C1) may require combination regimens with splicing modulators or epigenetic therapies to restore immune visibility. Recent preclinical studies have shown that pharmacologic inhibition of spliceosome components (e.g., SF3B1 and PRPF8) can enhance tumor immunogenicity by increasing neoepitope generation and interferon signaling. Thus, integrating AS biomarkers with immune profiling could refine patient selection and optimize therapeutic outcomes in BRCA. However, AS is highly cell type-specific, and variation in cellular composition represents an inherent limitation of bulk RNA-seq analyses. Our results suggest that the observed splicing landscape reflects contributions from both tumor and microenvironmental cells rather than purely tumor-intrinsic regulation. While cellular composition may influence a subset of DEAS events, it is unlikely to fully explain the observed patterns. Future studies using single-cell and spatial approaches will be required to resolve the cellular origin of splicing alterations. Although the TIMER comparison reduces concern that the C3 immune-hot pattern is specific to CIBERSORT, TIMER, and CIBERSORT do not provide fully identical cell-type estimates and showed discordant trends for selected populations, including CD8+ T cells and macrophages. All deconvolution results remain computational estimates derived from bulk RNA-seq data and should be validated by orthogonal methods such as multiplex immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry, single-cell RNA-seq, or spatial transcriptomics. Importantly, the external SCAN-B analysis should be interpreted as evidence of survival separation rather than definitive external confirmation of independent prognostic value. In SCAN-B, the assigned subtypes were associated with OS in univariate Cox models, but this association was no longer statistically significant after adjustment for PAM50 subtype, TMB, and derived AJCC 8th pathological prognostic stage. Therefore, adjusted prognostic independence of the splicing-based subtypes has been established only within the TCGA cohort. Validation in independent cohorts with raw RNA-seq data, harmonized splicing quantification, complete clinicopathologic annotation, tumor purity estimates, and full multivariable modeling remains an important priority for future work.

In conclusion, our integrative analysis systematically delineates the landscape of AS in BRCA and reveals how splicing dysregulation shapes molecular heterogeneity, immune contexture, and clinical prognosis. We identified 1,063 high-confidence DEAS events regulated by a complex network of SFs and associated with key oncogenic and immune pathways. The discovery of immune-related AS subtypes provides insights into BRCA immunobiology and suggests that targeting aberrant splicing may complement immunotherapy strategies. Together, these results highlight the pivotal role of AS in the interplay between tumor genomics and immunity, offering a framework for splicing-informed precision oncology.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations related to data type and cellular resolution. Most analyses were based on bulk RNA-seq profiles from TCGA and SCAN-B, so PSI values, DEAS detection, pathway enrichment, and immune-cell estimates reflect mixed tumor, stromal, and immune-cell populations rather than purified cell compartments. Event-level adjustment for tumor purity or cellular composition was not implemented, and this may have influenced both differential splicing calls and downstream pathway interpretation. Although TIMER supported the overall immune-hot pattern observed with CIBERSORT, the two deconvolution frameworks define immune populations differently and showed discordant trends for selected cell types. Therefore, the immune-infiltration results should be interpreted as computational estimates from bulk tissue profiles and will require orthogonal validation using multiplex immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry, single-cell RNA-seq, or spatial transcriptomics to confirm cell-type-resolved immune differences.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human cell lines and cell culture

Human breast epithelial and BRCA cell lines (MCF10-A, MCF-7, MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-468, and BT-549) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). MCF-7 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, high glucose; Gibco, USA), and BT-549 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA), while MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA), MDA-MB-468 cells were cultured in Leibovitz’s L-15 medium (Gibco, USA). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, USA). MCF10A cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 5% horse serum (Gibco, USA), 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF; PeproTech), 10 μg/mL insulin (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.5 μg/mL hydrocortisone (Sigma-Aldrich), and 100 ng/mL cholera toxin (Sigma-Aldrich), according to standard culture conditions. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2, except for MDA-MB-468 cells, which were maintained in a CO2-free environment as required for L-15 medium. Culture medium was replaced every 2–3 days. Cells were routinely passaged at 70–80% confluence using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA (Gibco, USA) and seeded at appropriate densities for subsequent experiments. All cell lines were regularly tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

Method details

RNA isolation

Total RNA from cultured cells and tissue samples was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific,15596018CN) or RNAzol RT (Molecular Research Center, RN190), respectively. Briefly, cultured cells were lysed directly in TRIzol. Tissue samples were immediately immersed in liquid nitrogen and ground into a fine powder using a pre-chilled mortar and pestle, then rapidly transferred into tubes containing RNAzol RT to ensure complete lysis and preserve RNA integrity. Phase separation was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C, the aqueous phase containing RNA was transferred to a new tube and mixed with isopropanol to precipitate RNA. The RNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in RNase-free water.

RT-PCR and qRT-PCR

Total RNA (1 μg) was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript IV Ultra All-in-One RT SuperMix (Vazyme, R433-01) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The synthesized cDNA served as the template for subsequent PCR amplification using a standard PCR Master Mix (Yeasen Biotechnology, 10167ES0). The amplified products were separated by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose (Tsingke Biotechnology, TSJ001) gel alongside a DNA marker (Yeasen Biotechnology, DL2000 DNA Marker, 10501ES60; 1 kb DNA ladder, 10501ES80) for size determination. After electrophoresis, the gel was stained with ethidium bromide (Sangon Biotech, A500328-0005) and visualized with a gel imaging system (Bio-Rad). GAPDH or 18s rRNA was used as an internal control gene. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) employing ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, #Q711-02) was performed on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR instrument (Bio-Rad, USA). Quantification of mRNA levels was achieved through qRT-PCR, with each reaction independently repeated at least three times to ensure reproducibility. Data analysis employed the 2−ΔΔCT method. Primer design for qPCR was executed using PerlPrimer software, and the specific primer sequences are indicated in Table S7.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Alternative splicing analysis

RNA sequencing data of BRCA were obtained from the Genomic Data Commons (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). AS profiles were generated for each sample using SpliceSeq, a Java-based tool that quantifies splicing junctions and exon inclusion levels.52 The PSI value, ranging from 0 to 1, was used to quantify AS events. To ensure robustness, only AS events with an average PSI ≥0.05 and detected in over 75% of samples were retained. Overlapping genes among seven types of AS events were visualized using an UpSet plot with UpSetR (version 1.3.3).58 DEAS events between tumor and paired normal tissues (n = 113) were identified using paired Mann–Whitney U tests, with adjusted p < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1 considered significant. Parent genes of DEAS events were analyzed for functional and pathway enrichment using DAVID,56 with adjusted p < 0.05 considered significant. Enriched genes were mapped to the STRING database (version 10.5) and visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.6.0).54,57

AS events were also quantified using SUPPA2 (version 2.3). SUPPA2 defines seven types of AS events, including A3 (alternative 3′ splice site), A5 (alternative 5′ splice site), AF (alternative first exon), AL (alternative last exon), SE (skipping exon), RI (retained intron), and MX (mutually exclusive exon).53 For consistency with the SpliceSeq annotation system used in this study, SUPPA2 event types were mapped to the corresponding categories as follows: A3 to alternative acceptor (AA), A5 to alternative donor (AD), AF to alternative promoter (AP), AL to alternative terminator (AT), SE to exon skipping (ES), RI to retained intron (RI), and MX to mutually exclusive exons (ME). AS event definitions were generated from transcript annotations using the generateEvents function in SUPPA2 with the “–f ioe” option based on the reference GTF file (GENCODE v23). PSI values for each AS event were calculated using the psiPerEvent function based on transcript-level expression matrices. High-confidence AS events were retained by applying filtering criteria of average PSI ≥0.05 and detection in ≥75% of samples. DEAS events between tumor and normal samples were identified using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, followed by Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Events with an adjusted p < 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1 were considered statistically significant. The TCGA SUPPA2 analysis was used as a cross-tool comparison of global AS-event and gene-level patterns and was not used to define the primary DEAS set for downstream TCGA analyses.

Gene expression analysis

Transcript per million (TPM) and raw count data for BRCA were retrieved from Xena Functional Genomics Explorer (https://xenabrowser.net/hub/). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (version 1.6.1) with adjusted p < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1 as significance thresholds.65 Splicing factor genes were curated from Gene Ontology (GO) terms “RNA splicing” (GO:0008380), “spliceosomal complex” (GO:0005681), and related child terms,30 supplemented by SpliceAid-F,29 resulting in 358 SF genes (354 with expression data in TCGA). Pearson correlation analysis between DEAS PSI values and SF expression was performed, with |R| > 0.4 and p < 0.05 considered significant. Correlation networks were visualized in Cytoscape (version 3.6.0).

In-house RNA-seq cohort and data processing

Fresh-frozen BRCA tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues were collected from patients undergoing surgery at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University with written informed consent (PJ2024-09-24). All samples were pathologically confirmed by at least two independent pathologists. A total of 18 primary BRCA cases were included, comprising 8 luminal, 5 HER2-positive, and 5 triple-negative subtypes. All specimens were stored at −80 °C until use.

Total RNA was extracted from fresh-frozen tumor and adjacent normal tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. mRNA was purified using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads (NEB, Cat# S1419S). RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the MGIEasy RNA Library Prep Kit (MGI, Cat# 1000006383) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, mRNA was fragmented, followed by first-strand cDNA synthesis using random hexamer primers and second-strand cDNA synthesis. Libraries were subsequently constructed through end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, size selection, PCR amplification, and purification. Library quality and fragment size distribution were assessed using an Agilent 4200 Bioanalyzer.

Paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was performed on the MGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform. Raw sequencing reads were processed using Trimmomatic (version 0.40)66 and further quality-controlled using fastp (version 0.23.4)67 to remove low-quality reads and adapter sequences. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using HISAT2 (version 2.2.1).68 Transcript assembly and quantification were performed using StringTie (version 2.2.3)69 based on the GENCODE reference annotation (release v23). Transcript expression levels were normalized as TPM. AS events were quantified using the SpliceSeq framework as described above. DEAS events between tumor and normal tissues were identified using the same statistical criteria as applied in the TCGA cohort. The RNA-seq data have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive for Human (GSA-Human) database under accession number HRA008406.

Somatic mutation analysis

Somatic mutation data for 985 BRCA patients were obtained from the Genomic Data Commons. Mutations were filtered to include only those causing amino acid changes, excluding samples with >1000 mutations. Significantly mutated genes were identified using MutSigCV (q < 0.25).70 Tumor mutation burden (TMB) was calculated for all genes and SF genes separately. The maftools R package was used for visualization and analysis of highly altered SF genes.61

Patient survival analysis

High-quality survival data were obtained from the TCGA Clinical Data Resource Outcome (https://gdc.cancer.gov/about-data/publications/pancanatlas). For each DEAS event, patients were stratified into high- and low-PSI groups based on the median PSI value. Univariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess associations with overall survival (OS) and PFI, with p < 0.05 considered significant. DEAS events associated with both OS and PFI were further analyzed using multivariate Cox regression to construct prognostic AS signatures. Risk scores (RS) for each patient were computed using hazard ratios from the multivariate model. For OS signatures, patients were classified into low-, moderate-, and high-risk groups based on RS quartiles. Kaplan–Meier curves, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and prediction curves were used to evaluate prognostic performance. Clinical variables, including PAM50 subtype, tumor purity, ploidy, and ER, PR, HER2 status, were incorporated from previous studies.71

Unsupervised clustering of AS-based subtypes

To identify AS-based molecular subtypes, unsupervised consensus clustering was performed in the TCGA-BRCA cohort using the PSI matrix of 1,063 DEAS events. Clustering analysis was conducted using the R package ConsensusClusterPlus (version 1.62.0) with default parameters.62 Briefly, samples were repeatedly subsampled and clustered across a range of candidate cluster numbers (K = 2–6), and consensus matrices were constructed to evaluate clustering stability. Pearson correlation was used as the similarity metric, and clustering was performed using hierarchical clustering as implemented in the package. For each value of K, consensus clustering results were assessed based on the consensus matrix heatmap, CDF curves, and the relative change in the area under the CDF curve. The optimal number of clusters was determined by jointly considering cluster stability and biological interpretability. Although clustering solutions were evaluated up to K = 6, the most stable and robust structure was observed at K = 3, as indicated by the plateauing of the CDF curve and improved cluster separation in the consensus matrix. Accordingly, the TCGA-BRCA cohort was stratified into three AS-based molecular subtypes, designated C1, C2, and C3. All downstream TCGA analyses, including subtype discovery, survival modeling, genomic and clinical association analyses, and immune characterization, used the SpliceSeq-derived DEAS set described above.

To assess the robustness of the clustering solution, resampling-based validation was performed. In each iteration, 80% of samples were randomly selected and consensus clustering was repeated for a total of 200 iterations. The similarity between clustering solutions obtained from resampled datasets and the reference clustering was quantified using the ARI. In addition, patient-level assignment stability was calculated as the proportion of iterations in which each sample was assigned to the same cluster. To further evaluate reproducibility in unseen samples, a centroid-based train–test validation framework was applied. In each iteration, 80% of samples were randomly assigned to the training set to derive subtype centroids based on DEAS profiles, and the remaining 20% were assigned to the nearest centroid using correlation-based similarity. This procedure was repeated 200 times. Prediction performance was evaluated using classification accuracy and macro-F1 score, and patient-level prediction stability was calculated as the proportion of consistent subtype assignments across iterations. These subtype assignments were subsequently used for downstream analyses, including characterization of clinicopathological features, genomic alterations, immune microenvironment, and survival outcomes.

External SCAN-B cohort for clustering validation

An independent BRCA cohort from the SCAN-B study (GSE96058) was used for external validation of AS-based clustering.37 Transcript-level expression data comprising 3,273 samples were obtained from the publicly available dataset, together with the corresponding UCSC hg38 knownGenes annotation. Due to the lack of raw RNA-seq data and splice junction-level information in this cohort, AS events could not be quantified using the SpliceSeq pipeline. Instead, AS events were estimated using SUPPA2 based on transcript-level expression data, as described above. To ensure cross-cohort comparability, only shared splicing features defined by common parent genes and same event types were retained. Subtype assignment in the SCAN-B cohort was performed using a centroid-based classification approach derived from the TCGA cohort. Samples were assigned to the nearest subtype centroid based on correlation of AS profiles. The sample somatic mutation information was obtained from the MutationExplorer database (http://oncogenomics.bmc.lu.se/MutationExplorer) and integrated with AS-based subtype assignments for downstream analyses.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

Immune cell composition was estimated from bulk RNA-seq data using the CIBERSORT web platform (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/).31 Gene expression matrices were uploaded to the web portal, and immune cell fractions were inferred using the LM22 leukocyte gene signature matrix, which defines 22 immune cell subsets. The algorithm applies support vector regression–based deconvolution to estimate relative cell-type proportions from mixed gene expression profiles. Deconvolution was performed with 100 permutations under default parameters. In addition, stromal and immune components of the tumor microenvironment were quantified using the ESTIMATE algorithm implemented in the R package estimate v2.0.0.60 Stromal score, immune score, and ESTIMATE score were calculated based on gene expression signatures, reflecting the relative presence of stromal cells, immune cells, and the combined tumor microenvironment, respectively. Differences in immune cell fractions and ESTIMATE-derived scores were compared across AS-based subtypes. These measurements were derived from bulk RNA-seq data and may be influenced by tumor purity and cellular composition; therefore, results were interpreted as relative estimates at the tissue level.

For validation using an independent deconvolution method, immune infiltration was additionally evaluated with TIMER.32 TIMER estimates six broad immune-cell categories: B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, neutrophils, macrophages, and myeloid dendritic cells. Because TIMER and CIBERSORT use different model frameworks and immune-cell definitions, we did not treat TIMER as a one-to-one replication of all CIBERSORT cell fractions. Instead, TIMER estimates were Z score scaled and compared among AS-based subtypes to evaluate whether subtype-level immune-infiltration trends were directionally consistent. Heatmaps were generated with samples ordered by AS subtype (C1-C3), and boxplots were used to summarize subtype-level differences.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2). Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t test or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Comparisons among more than two groups were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. For multiple comparisons, p values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Unless otherwise specified, all statistical tests were two-sided. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), as appropriate. For SCAN-B, both unadjusted and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were fitted; the adjusted model included AS-based subtype, TMB, PAM50 subtype, and AJCC stage. For the TCGA subtype-level survival analysis, a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was fitted for OS with AS-based subtype, AJCC pathologic stage, PAM50 subtype, tumor purity, and TMB as covariates. The global contribution of AS-based subtype was evaluated using likelihood-ratio testing. Repeated 5-fold cross-validation was used to assess internal robustness of the subtype-survival association by recording testing-fold global p values and the direction of HR estimates for C2 and C3 relative to C1.
