Plasma EBV DNA dynamics during chemotherapy as a prognostic biomarker in AIDS-related non-Hodgkin lymphoma
Authors: Zhenyan Wang, Li Liu, Jun Chen, Tangkai Qi, Wei Song, Jianjun Sun, Yang Tang, Shuibao Xu, Junyang Yang, Yinzhong Shen, Renfang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the associations of plasma EBV-DNA and tumor EBV status with clinical outcomes in patients with AIDS-related non-Hodgkin lymphoma (ARL).MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed ARL patients diagnosed at Shanghai Public Health Clinical Center between 2013 and 2021. Tumor EBV status was determined by EBV-encoded RNA (EBER) in situ hybridization, and plasma EBV-DNA was quantified using real-time PCR. Survival outcomes were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier analysis and Cox regression. Longitudinal EBV-DNA patterns during chemotherapy were classified into four groups: persistently negative, persistently positive, clearance, and conversion.ResultsAmong 183 ARL patients, 99 underwent tumor EBER testing with 45.5% (45/99) positivity. Survival analysis was performed in 85 ARL patients who received treatment. The 2-year overall survival (OS) did not differ by EBER status (positive: 63.2% vs. negative: 52.5%; HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.41–1.80; p = 0.695). Baseline plasma EBV-DNA was quantified in 52 patients, with 47 (90.4%) undergoing ≥ 4 tests during chemotherapy cycles. Baseline plasma EBV-DNA levels were not predictive of prognosis. However, detectable EBV-DNA during chemotherapy was associated with significantly inferior OS (p = 0.048) and remained an independent prognostic factor after adjustment. Longitudinal analysis showed no statistically significant survival differences among four EBV-DNA patterns (p = 0.074), but patients with persistently positive or conversion patterns exhibited poorer outcomes, while persistently negative or clearance patterns were associated with more favorable prognosis. Pairwise analysis confirmed significantly better survival in persistently negative compared with persistently positive patients (p = 0.016).ConclusionDynamic monitoring of plasma EBV-DNA during chemotherapy provides important prognostic information in ARL. Persistent or newly detectable EBV-DNA identifies patients at higher risk of poor outcomes, supporting the clinical utility of longitudinal EBV-DNA assessment for risk stratification and treatment guidance.

Introduction

The Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) is an oncogenic virus that has been proven to be correlated with multiple types of lymphoma, including diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), Burkitt lymphoma (BL), Hodgkin lymphoma, primary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL), and primary effusion lymphoma [1–3]. The prevalence of EBV infection varies among different regions, populations, and types of lymphoma [4, 5].

Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) results in host immune dysfunction, thereby facilitating EBV infection and the activation of latent EBV. Study showed that the proportion of EBV detected in the plasma of AIDS-related lymphoma (ARL) at the time of lymphoma diagnosis was significantly higher than that of the same type of HIV-negative lymphoma. The continuous detection of EBV in plasma is capable of predicting the occurrence of lymphoma in HIV-infected individuals. Furthermore, the presence of plasma EBV-DNA and high EBV-DNA levels in plasma at the time of lymphoma diagnosis are also risk factors for a poor prognosis of lymphoma [6–8]. Studies have also shown that EBV infection is associated with the clinical and pathological features of lymphoma. Compared with patients with negative serum EBV-DNA, those with positive serum EBV-DNA have higher levels of lactate dehydrogenase and β2-microglobulin, more frequent B symptoms, a higher incidence of secondary hemophagocytic syndrome, and a lower objective response rate to treatment [9]. In immunocompromised individuals, the immunobiological characteristics of EBV-positive primary central nervous system lymphomas vary from those of EBV-negative ones [10]. These studies suggest that EBV infection is not only associated with the occurrence of lymphoma but also with its clinical manifestations and prognosis.

However, the prognostic significance of post-treatment EBV-DNA kinetics in ARL remains unclear. This study aims to compare clinical characteristics by tumor EBV status and assess the prognostic utility of serial EBV-DNA measurements during chemotherapy for ARL.

Methods

Study participants

We conducted an analysis of all patients who were newly diagnosed with ARL at the Shanghai Public Health Clinical Center from August 2013 to August 2021. All the patients were confirmed to be infected with HIV, regardless of whether they were undergoing antiretroviral therapy (ART) at the time of lymphoma diagnosis, and had not received any prior treatment for lymphoma, including chemotherapy, targeted therapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy. The diagnosis of lymphoma was established based on the clinicopathological profiles determined through the expert review of the center’s pathology. The classifications of lymphoma were made in accordance with the 2016 World Health Organization (WHO) criteria. The demographic, clinicopathologic and laboratory data of all patients were retrospectively collected.

In this cohort, first-line chemotherapy for DLBCL consisted of either R-CHOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisone) or dose-adjusted R-EPOCH (rituximab, etoposide, prednisone, vincristine, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin). Patients with BL received either dose-adjusted R-EPOCH or R-HyperCVAD (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, vincristine, doxorubicin, and dexamethasone, alternating with high-dose methotrexate and cytarabine). For PCNSL, the first-line treatment was high-dose methotrexate, with or without rituximab. All participants received concurrent ART. They were followed from study entry until either death or administrative censoring in January 2022.

EBV-encoded RNA (EBER) in situ hybridization

Tumor EBV status was determined by EBER detection using in situ hybridization (ISH). EBER probes were obtained from Beijing Zhongshan Jinqiao Biotechnology Co. (China). The assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nuclear brown staining was considered positive.

Plasma EBV-DNA quantification

Plasma samples were prospectively collected at baseline and during intervals between cycles of lymphoma treatment. Plasma EBV-DNA levels were quantified using real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) with the EBV nucleic acid quantitative detection kit (Sansure Biotech Inc., Hunan, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The ABI 7500 fluorescence PCR system was used for detection. The lower limit of detection was 500 IU/mL; samples below this threshold were considered undetectable.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Continuous variables with non-normal distributions were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Survival rates were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and comparisons were made with the log-rank test. Multivariate analysis was performed using Firth-corrected Cox regression. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Among 183 ARL patients enrolled at Shanghai Public Health Clinical Center between August 2013 and August 2021, tumor tissue was available for EBER testing in 99 cases. Of these, 45 (45.5%) tested positive for EBER, while 54 (54.5%) were negative. Baseline plasma EBV-DNA quantitative results were available for 52 patients, among whom 10 were positive and 42 negative (Fig. 1).

Clinical characteristics were compared according to both EBER status in lymphoma tissues and baseline plasma EBV-DNA status (Table 1). Comparative analysis showed that patients with EBER-positive lymphoma were significantly older than those with EBER-negative lymphoma (median age: 52 vs. 43 years; p = 0.04). Among lymphoma subtypes, PCNSL exhibited the highest EBER-positivity rate (100%), followed by DLBCL (50%), while BL showed the lowest rate (29.7%). A higher proportion of patients in the plasma EBV-DNA positive group had received ART for ≥ 1 year at lymphoma diagnosis compared to those in the negative group (60% vs. 19%, p = 0.016). Other clinical features did not differ significantly between the two groups. See Table 1.

Association between tumor EBER status and prognosis in ARL patients

Among the 99 patients diagnosed with ARL, 85 received anti-lymphoma treatment, whereas 14 did not undergo specific lymphoma therapy. The overall survival (OS) of the 85 treated patients was analyzed according to their EBER status. The 2-year OS rates for ARL patients in the EBER-positive and EBER-negative groups were 63.2% and 52.5%, respectively (HR = 0.862, 95% CI: 0.412–1.804, p = 0.695; Fig. 2a).

Association between plasma EBV-DNA levels and ARL prognosis

Baseline plasma EBV-DNA levels were measured in 52 ARL patients before anti-lymphoma therapy. Ten patients (19.2%, 10/52) had detectable levels (≥ 500 IU/mL). The 2-year overall survival (OS) was comparable between patients with baseline EBV-DNA ≥ 500 IU/mL (70.0%) and < 500 IU/mL (76.4%) (HR 1.32, 95% CI 0.32–5.41; p = 0.675; Fig. 2b).

Among 52 patients with baseline EBV-DNA testing, 47 underwent serial monitoring during chemotherapy (≥ 4 serial measurements during chemotherapy). Kinetic analysis categorized 22 patients (46.8%) as having persistently undetectable EBV-DNA (< 500 IU/mL) and 25 (53.2%) with ≥ 1 detectable result (≥ 500 IU/mL).

Patients with detectable EBV-DNA during treatment had significantly inferior 2-year overall survival compared to those with undetectable levels (68.0% vs. 89.5%; HR 4.13, 95% CI 1.20-14.26; p = 0.048; Fig. 2c). After adjusting for International Prognostic Index (IPI, < 1 vs. ≥ 2), EBV-DNA remained an independent prognostic factor. Patients with EBV positivity had a significantly higher risk of death compared with EBV‑negative patients (HR = 4.66, 95% CI: 1.01–21.58, P = 0.048). In contrast, early achievement of negativity (after 1–2 cycles) was not associated with improved survival outcomes compared to negativity achieved at later time points (Fig. 2d).

We performed an additional exploratory analysis evaluating the association between plasma EBV-DNA status at different treatment time points—after 1–2 cycles, 3–4 cycles, and 5–6 cycles (end of treatment, EOT)—and mortality among patients with ≥ 4 serial measurements during chemotherapy. As shown in the Table 2, mortality rates were numerically higher in EBV-DNA–positive patients compared with EBV-DNA–negative patients after 1–2 cycles (36.4% vs. 18.5%, p = 0.401) and after 3–4 cycles (30.0% vs. 12.5%, p = 0.328), but these differences did not reach statistical significance. At EOT, mortality rates were identical between EBV-DNA–positive and –negative patients (14.3% vs. 14.3%, p > 0.999). Furthermore, no significant trend in mortality across time points was observed within either the EBV-DNA–positive (p = 0.487) or EBV-DNA–negative group (p = 0.712).

Association between longitudinal EBV-DNA patterns and survival

To evaluate the association between dynamic changes in EBV-DNA status during chemotherapy and survival, patients with serial measurements (n = 47) were classified into four longitudinal EBV-DNA patterns: persistently negative (n = 20), persistently positive (n = 6), clearance (positive to negative, n = 5), and conversion (negative to positive, n = 16). No significant differences in overall survival were observed among the four groups (log-rank p = 0.074). Nevertheless, a clear trend was evident: patients with persistently positive EBV-DNA or those who converted from negative to positive during chemotherapy exhibited poorer survival outcomes, whereas those with persistently negative EBV-DNA or viral clearance tended to have more favorable prognoses (Fig. 3).

In pairwise comparisons, patients with persistently negative EBV-DNA had significantly better overall survival than those with persistently positive EBV-DNA (p = 0.016). Additionally, patients with persistently negative EBV-DNA showed a trend toward improved survival compared with those who converted from negative to positive, and patients who achieved viral clearance (positive to negative) also tended to have better outcomes than those with persistent positivity; however, these differences did not reach statistical significance.

Cause of death

In the EBER cohort (n = 99), 85 patients received lymphoma treatment. Among these, 28 deaths occurred: 19 attributed to tumor progression, 7 to infection, and 2 to other causes. In the EBV-DNA cohort (n = 52), all patients received chemotherapy, with 12 deaths reported: 8 due to tumor progression and 4 due to infection.

Discussion

Although the overall incidence of ARL has significantly decreased following the introduction of ART, its effect on the occurrence of EBV-related lymphoma appears to be less pronounced compared to other AIDS-defining malignancies [11]. In the modern ART era, understanding the role of EBV in ARL remains of critical importance. In this study, EBV-related biomarkers were evaluated among a cohort of patients with ARL.

Studies have demonstrated that the prevalence of EBV infection varies across different populations and lymphoma subtypes, and is associated with distinct clinical and pathological characteristics of lymphoma. Patients with EBV-positive lymphomas have a faster clinical progression, a poorer treatment response, and a worse survival prognosis compared to those with EBV-negative lymphomas [12, 13]. The EBER positivity rate in HIV-related DLBCL is higher than that in HIV-negative DLBCL (16% vs. 7%) [7]. Another study reported a higher EBER positivity rate of 31% in HIV-related DLBCL. Accumulating evidence suggests that tumor EBV status may serve as a potentially valuable prognostic marker in HIV-related DLBCL [14]. Our study revealed that approximately 50% of patients with ARL exhibited positive EBER staining in their tumor tissues. Among these subtypes, DLBCL (50%) and PCNSL (100%) demonstrated higher EBER positivity rates compared to Burkitt lymphoma (29.7%). A retrospective study from China reported that the EBER positivity rate in PCNSL among patients with HIV infection was 73.2% [15]. The role of EBV in lymphomagenesis may vary across different lymphoma subtypes. In this study, patients with EBER-positive ARL were older than those with EBER-negative lymphoma, and no association was observed between tumor EBV status and prognosis for ARL patients. These discrepancies in research outcomes may be attributed to differences in study populations, lymphoma subtypes, and other confounding factors, highlighting the need for further investigation with larger cohort studies.

The EBV infection status in tumor tissue does not correspond to the EBV-DNA load in peripheral blood or plasma. Studies in African children suggest that HIV-1 may induce microbial translocation and sustained immune activation, leading to the replication and expansion of EBV in host B cells and an increase in the EBV-DNA load in the blood. However, ART can inhibit HIV-1 replication, microbial translocation, and related immune activation, thereby reducing plasma EBV-DNA and potentially lowering the risk of EBV-related lymphoma [16]. In this study, a higher proportion of patients in the plasma EBV-DNA positive group had received ART for at least one year at lymphoma diagnosis compared to those in the negative group. This finding suggests that EBV may play a more prominent pathogenic role in lymphomas that develop despite prolonged ART. Studies indicate that elevated plasma EBV-DNA levels are associated with both an increased risk of developing ARL and patient prognosis. Specifically, HIV-infected individuals with a peak EBV-DNA load exceeding 100,000 IU/mL face an approximately 13-fold higher risk of developing lymphoma compared to those with a peak load of ≤ 100,000 IU/mL [17]. Furthermore, the proportion of EBV-DNA positivity at lymphoma diagnosis was significantly higher in patients with ARL than in both HIV-negative patients with the same lymphoma type and HIV-positive patients without concurrent malignancies or opportunistic infections. The level of EBV-DNA at the time of lymphoma diagnosis has been identified as a poor prognostic factor for survival in patients with ARL [8, 18]. Furthermore, studies examining disparities in pretreatment total blood EBV-DNA levels and tissue EBER status across different lymphoma types in HIV-negative individuals have explored their impact on prognosis. Additional studies have analyzed disparities in pretreatment total blood EBV-DNA levels and tissue EBER status among different lymphoma types in HIV-negative individuals, exploring their impact on prognosis. These investigations found that pretreatment total blood EBV-DNA level is associated with the prognosis of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, while patients exhibiting positivity for both EBV-DNA in blood and EBER in tissue demonstrate poorer survival in Hodgkin lymphoma [19]. Dynamic monitoring of EBV-DNA levels is valuable for the assessment of treatment response in EBV-associated lymphomas. Following therapy, the proportion of patients with detectable EBV-DNA in whole blood or plasma typically declines [20]. This study analyzed the correlation between plasma EBV-DNA levels and prognosis in patients with ARL. Contrary to previous findings, pretreatment EBV-DNA level did not affect the prognosis of ARL patients. However, detectable plasma EBV-DNA levels ≥ 500 IU/ml during therapy emerged as a significant risk factor for inferior prognosis. A study on HIV-negative lymphoma patients demonstrated that achieving undetectable EBV-DNA levels after treatment is a favorable prognostic factor for NK/T-cell lymphoma. Conversely, pretreatment EBV-DNA positivity acts as both a biomarker of tumor burden and an independent adverse prognostic factor [9].

In the analysis of the association between longitudinal EBV-DNA patterns and survival, although no statistically significant difference in overall survival was observed among the four groups, a clear trend was evident. Patients with persistently positive EBV-DNA or conversion from negative to positive showed poorer outcomes, likely reflecting ongoing viral activity and more aggressive disease. In contrast, persistently negative EBV-DNA or viral clearance may indicate better immune control and treatment response. The lack of statistical significance may be due to the small sample size and subgroup imbalance. However, the significant difference between persistently negative and persistently positive groups supports the prognostic value of sustained EBV-DNA negativity. Longitudinal monitoring may therefore help identify high-risk patients and guide more individualized management strategies.

This study has several limitations, including a relatively small sample size, suboptimal accuracy of quantitative plasma EBV-DNA assays, and the lack of subgroup analyses examining the prognostic impact of EBV infection status according to lymphoma subtypes.

Conclusion

In summary, tumor EBER status and baseline plasma EBV-DNA levels were not significantly associated with survival in patients with ARL. In contrast, detectable EBV-DNA during chemotherapy, particularly persistent positivity or conversion from negative to positive, was associated with inferior outcomes. These findings highlight the potential value of serial EBV-DNA monitoring as a biomarker for prognostic stratification in ARL.
