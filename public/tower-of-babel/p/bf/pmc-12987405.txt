Post-Acute Sequelae of COVID-19 Persist Over 3 Years in Acute Lung Injury/Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome Survivors But Are Not Associated With Persistent Thromboinflammation or Endothelial Dysfunction
Authors: Ansley E. Jones, Zain Khan, Claire F. McGroder, Scarlett O. Murphy, Christopher Depender, Margarita Mira-Sanchez, Charity O. Ogunlusi, Ying Wei, Christine K. Garcia, Matthew R. Baldwin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IMPORTANCE:Inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and complement activation are associated with COVID-19 acute lung injury (ALI) and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).OBJECTIVES:We hypothesized that higher levels of inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and complement activation implicated in more severe COVID-19 ALI/ARDS are associated with post-acute sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC) phenotypes in the 3 years after hospitalization.DESIGN, SETTING, AND PARTICIPANTS:A single-center prospective cohort of 150 adult survivors of severe and critical COVID-19 from the first wave of the pandemic with sampling weighted to include 50% survivors of mechanical ventilation.MAIN OUTCOMES AND MEASURES:Eleven serum biomarkers at hospital discharge, 4 months, 15 months, and 3 years, and symptoms and physical function at 15 months and 3 years. PASC presence was defined using the 12 symptoms and scoring from the Researching COVID to Enhance Recovery (RECOVER) definition. We tested associations of biomarkers with PASC and symptom phenotypes of post-exertional malaise, fatigue, and brain fog while adjusting for age, sex, body mass index, comorbidities, and days since COVID-19 diagnosis.RESULTS:The mean (sd) age of the cohort was 56 years (13); 67% were Hispanic and 25% were Black. PASC was present in 26% of participants at both 15 months and 3 years. PASC and symptom phenotypes at 15 months and 3 years were consistently associated with higher frailty phenotype category, worse short physical performance battery scores, and shorter 6-minute walk distance. Biomarkers of inflammation, including interleukin-6 and soluble tumor necrosis factor receptor-1, endothelial function, including angiopoietin, and complement, including C2, C4b, and C5, were not associated with PASC or symptom phenotypes in cross-sectional or longitudinal analyses.CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE:PASC persists for 3 years after acute COVID-19 ALI/ARDS, is associated with frailty, but not associated with persistently higher levels of inflammatory, endothelial, and complement biomarkers implicated in worse short-term outcomes in acute COVID-19, non-COVID-19 ARDS, and sepsis. Future studies should employ multiomics to elucidate potential mechanisms of PASC.

KEY POINTS

Question: Is elevated inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and complement activation linked to Post-Acute Sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC) up to 3 years after COVID-19-related acute lung injury (ALI)/acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)?

Findings: In a single-center prospective cohort of 150 survivors of severe and critical COVID-19, 26% experienced PASC for up to 3 years. PASC was associated with increased frailty and worse physical function but not with sustained thromboinflammation or endothelial dysfunction.

Meaning: PASC can persist for 3 years post–COVID-19 ALI/ARDS survivors but is not associated with conventional thromboinflammatory or endothelial biomarkers. Multiomics approaches are required to uncover underlying mechanisms.

Acute COVID-19 requiring hospital or ICU admission is a major risk factor for developing long-COVID-19 (1, 2). Approximately 500,000 Americans survived COVID-19 acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) between 2020 and 2022 (3, 4), and at least half are expected to have incident disability 6 months later (5). Yet, fewer than 10% of participants in the largest longitudinal cohorts in the United States dedicated to studying long-COVID-19, the Researching COVID to Enhance Recovery (RECOVER) and C4R cohorts, were hospitalized for acute COVID-19 (6, 7). There remains an unmet need to better characterize and identify mechanisms and therapeutic targets for long-COVID-19 among survivors of severe and critical COVID-19 who had acute lung injury (ALI) or ARDS.

Over 200 symptoms involving 10 organ systems have been associated with long-COVID-19 (8), but the most commonly reported symptoms 6 months after acute COVID-19 are fatigue, post-exertional malaise (PEM), and cognitive dysfunction (8, 9). In the National Institutes of Health-funded RECOVER cohort that consists of approximately 10,000 adults, investigators created a definition of post-acute sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC) as a framework for future clinical investigations. They defined PASC as a score greater than or equal to 12 based on 12 self-reported symptoms with individual scores varying from 1 to 8 (7). The greater than or equal to 12-point threshold for defining PASC identified 23% of severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS)-CoV-2 infected participants as having PASC 6 months post-infection compared with a 3.7% prevalence of PASC in never-infected participants (7).

Although there is progress in clinically characterizing and defining PASC, there are no known treatments for long-COVID-19 because the mechanisms driving physical and mental-health changes remain poorly understood. In this study, we hypothesized that inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and complement activation, which are associated with more severe acute COVID-19 ALI/ARDS, persist after hospital discharge among those with PASC.

Higher levels of interleukin (IL)-6, IL-8, and soluble tumor necrosis factor receptor-1 (sTNFR-1) predict high inflammatory subphenotypes and higher mortality in non-COVID-19 and COVID-19 ARDS (10, 11). In aging studies, higher levels of these inflammatory cytokines are associated with sarcopenia, frailty, and physical function (12, 13), which could underlie PASC symptoms.

Higher levels of circulating growth differentiation factor (GDF)-15, a biomarker released in response to hypoexmia, inflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction, are associated with greater multiple organ failure and higher mortality in non-COVID-19 and COVID-19 ARDS (14, 15). Among older adult acute respiratory failure survivors in the pre-COVID-19 era, higher levels of circulating GDF-15 at hospital discharge were found to be independently associated with muscle weakness and slower rates of physical recovery (16).

Biomarkers of endothelial dysfunction, including angiopoietin, soluble intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (sICAM-1), and soluble vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (sVCAM-1), are associated with more severe and prolonged acute COVID-19 (17), a risk factor for long-COVID-19 (1). COVID-19 ARDS survivors with impaired gas exchange have elevated biomarkers of endothelial inflammation and dysfunction, including ICAM-1 (18). Microthrombosis and hypercoagulable states involving Von Willebrand factor-cleaving protease (ADAMTS13) and complement are associated with more severe and lethal COVID-19 ARDS (19–21). Therefore, we selected these specific biomarkers to test our hypothesis.

METHODS

Study Design and Participants

The Columbia University Irving Medical Center (CUIMC) cohort has been described in detail previously (22–24). We prospectively enrolled ambulatory community-dwelling adults 21 years old or older who were hospitalized with severe or critical COVID-19 at the tertiary-care Milstein or community-level Allen Hospitals between March 1 and May 15, 2020. We weighted sampling to include 50% invasive mechanical ventilation survivors. We excluded patients with preexisting interstitial lung disease or lung transplantation. We enrolled 150 unique participants. We enrolled 76 participants at the initial 4-month follow-up visit and invited them to participate in the 15-month and 3-year follow-up visits. We enrolled 47 and 27 additional participants with the same inclusion criteria at 15 months and 3 years, respectively, to increase and then maintain the sample size at approximately 100 participants. The study was approved by the Columbia University Institutional Review Board (Long-Term Outcomes of COVID-19, AAAT5605 [approval date February 25, 2021] and Columbia University COVID-19 Biobank, AAAS7370 [approval date March 15, 2020]). Procedures were followed in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation (institutional or regional) and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975. All subjects provided informed consent to participate in the study.

Biomarker Analyses

Serum samples from the acute COVID-19 hospitalization were collected from residual daily morning clinical blood samples and cryopreserved at –80°C, usually within 4 hours. Available hospital discharge serum samples were collected a median (interquartile range [IQR] 2 d [4–1]) before hospital discharge. Phlebotomy samples were prospectively collected at all follow-up visits, centrifuged immediately, and serum was cryopreserved at –80°C. Serum biomarker levels were quantified using multiplex assays (see e-Methods, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B611). Biomarkers included IL-6, sTNFR-1, IL-8, GDF-15, ADAMTS13, angiopoietin, sICAM-1, sVCAM-1, and complement components C2, C4b, and C5.

Symptom Questionnaires

At 15 months and 3 years, participants rated the severity and frequency of 43 symptoms on a 7-level ordinal categorical scale and quantified their dyspnea based on the Modified Medical Research Council breathlessness scale. To assess PASC, we used symptom assessments in the study cohort that were identical or similar to the 12 PASC symptoms used to calculate the RECOVER PASC score. We defined symptom presence as moderate, severe, or very severe, and assigned the individual symptom score used in the RECOVER PASC score (e-Table 1, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B611). In primary outcome analyses, we used the initial RECOVER cohort PASC definition of a score greater than or equal to 12. In sensitivity analyses, we re-analyzed PASC scores using the updated 2024 definition (25). We also tested associations of biomarkers across quartiles of PASC, recognizing that the 12-point threshold for PASC positivity may not be valid in our study population. In secondary outcome analyses, we assessed symptom phenotypes of the most prevalent long-COVID-19 symptoms: PEM, fatigue, and brain fog. Patients were phenotypically defined if they reported having these symptoms with moderate-or-greater severity.

Frailty and Physical Function Measurements

We measured the frailty phenotype at 15 months and 3 years. We assessed 4-meter gait speed and grip strength using the Cardiovascular Health Study methodology (26). We measured low activity with the Duke Activity Status Index, which has strong construct validity in acute respiratory failure survivors (27). We calculated weight loss as greater than 10 pounds lost between acute COVID-19 hospital admission and weight measured at the follow-up visit. Each frailty domain was assigned 1 point if present and 0 points if absent (range, 0–5). Patients were categorized as robust (0), pre-frail (1–2), and frail (3–5) (26). We assessed the short performance physical battery (SPPB), defining low as a score of less than or equal to 9, which is associated with disability and mortality (28, 29). We assessed 6-minute walk distance using established guidelines and defined low 6-minute walk distance as less than 80% predicted (30, 31).

Statistical Analyses

We examined cross-sectional and longitudinal unadjusted associations of biomarkers at hospital discharge, 4 months, 15 months, and 3 years with PASC, symptom phenotypes, and frailty categories at 15 months and 3 years using the Mann-Whitney U test and Kruskal-Wallis tests. Biomarkers were natural-log transformed to normalize their distribution. We created generalized additive models to test nonlinear and generalized linear models to test linear adjusted cross-sectional and longitudinal associations. Given the moderate study sample size, we used covariate balancing propensity score to adjust for confounders, as we have done previously (22, 32). We used directed acyclographs to determine plausible confounders and precision variables for inclusion in the covariate balancing propensity score, which were age, sex, race/ethnicity, body-mass-index at follow-up visit, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cardiovascular disease, asthma, estimated glomerular filtration rate (33), pack-years of smoking, and days between positive SARS-CoV2 test and follow-up. We did not adjust for the severity of acute COVID-19 illness or steroid or IL-6 receptor inhibitor use because it would introduce M bias (e-Fig. 1, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B611) (34). We used ordinal logistic regression to test associations of biomarkers with PASC quartiles and frailty categories. We examined unadjusted associations of PASC and symptom phenotypes with frailty and physical function using Chi-square and Kruskal-Wallis tests. We used zero-imputation to account for missing symptom data, which totaled less than 1% (e-Table 2, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B611). We controlled for a false discovery rate of 0.05 based on biomarker class and outcome using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Statistical analyses were done using Stata, Version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX) and RStudio (Version 2022.07.0 + 548; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

The Columbia cohort consisted of 150 adults with 76, 104, and 102 participating at 4-month, 15-month, and 3-year follow-up, respectively (Fig. 1). The mean (sd) age at hospital admission was 56 years (12) and 56% of participants were male. Two-thirds of the cohort were Hispanic and 23% were Black. The median (IQR) hospital length of stay was 18 days (7–42). All participants required oxygen therapy, with 32% requiring nasal cannula, 22% requiring non-rebreather facemask, high-flow nasal oxygen, or noninvasive positive-pressure ventilation, and 46% requiring mechanical ventilation. Those who used non-rebreather facemasks were eligible for high-flow nasal oxygen or noninvasive positive pressure ventilation, but no machines were available at the time. The median (IQR) number of ventilator days was 34 (14–46) (Tables 1 and 2).

The prevalence of PASC in the CUIMC cohort at 15 months and 3 years was similar to that in the RECOVER cohort at 6 months (26% and 26% vs. 21%). Compared with RECOVER participants with PASC, CUIMC cohort participants with PASC had a significantly lower prevalence of palpitations, fatigue, dizziness, and gastrointestinal disturbances at 15 months, and a significantly lower prevalence of palpitations and dizziness and higher prevalence of abnormal movements at 3 years (Table 3). At 15 months, those with PASC were significantly older, more likely to be female, and had a higher body mass index (BMI) than those without PASC. At 3 years, those with PASC were more likely to have a higher BMI than those without PASC. Those with and without PASC had a similar prevalence of comorbidities and severity of acute COVID-19 illness (Tables 1 and 2). In both unadjusted and adjusted analyses, no biomarkers were significantly associated with PASC at 15 months or 3 years (e-Fig. 2, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B611 and Fig. 2). In sensitivity analyses, no biomarkers showed consistent, significant associations across increasing quartiles of PASC nor with the updated PASC score in unadjusted and adjusted analyses (e-Figs. 3 and 4 https://links.lww.com/CCX/B611).

At 15 months and 3 years, PEM prevalence was 43% and 47%, brain fog prevalence was 22% and 27%, and fatigue prevalence was 27% and 41%, respectively. At 15 months, those with PEM were more likely to be older and female compared with those without PEM; those with brain fog were more likely to be female and have asthma than those without brain fog; and those with severe fatigue were more likely to be female and have a higher BMI than those without severe fatigue (e-Table 3, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B611). At 3 years, those with PEM were more likely to be Hispanic than those without PEM; there were no statistically significant differences in demographics, comorbidities, or acute hospitalization characteristics between those with and without brain fog and fatigue (e-Table 4, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B611). In unadjusted and adjusted analyses, no biomarkers were consistently associated with any of the specific symptom phenotypes after adjusting for multiple comparisons. In unadjusted models, IL-8 and sTNFR-1 were associated with brain fog at 3 years cross-sectionally, and only IL-8 remained significant after adjustment for confounders. Additionally, C4b at 4 months and 15 months was significantly associated with PEM at 3 years in unadjusted models but was not significant after adjustment for confounders (Fig. 2; and e-Fig. 2, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B611).

In both unadjusted and adjusted analyses, no biomarkers were consistently associated with frailty phenotype categories. Only GDF-15 was significantly associated with frailty category at 3 years cross-sectionally but did not remain significant when adjusting for confounders (Fig. 2; and e-Fig. 2, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B611). Greater frailty phenotype category, lower short physical performance battery score, and low 6-minute walk distance were significantly associated with PASC, PEM, brain fog, and fatigue at 15 months, and these physical function measures were significantly associated with only PEM at 3 years (Fig. 3).

DISCUSSION

In one of the largest prospective North American cohorts of severe and critical COVID-19 ALI/ARDS survivors with multiyear follow-up, we found that PASC persists in approximately 25% of survivors for up to 3 years post-infection. Our study supports the construct validity of the 2023 and 2024 RECOVER PASC phenotype definitions in a population that was hospitalized for severe and critical COVID-19. PASC was associated with greater frailty, lower SPPB scores, and reduced 6-minute walk distance. However, we found no evidence of thromboinflammation or endothelial dysfunction in those with PASC at 15 months or 3 years post-hospitalization, despite using biomarkers implicated in severe and critical COVID-19, non-COVID-19 ALI/ARDS, sepsis, and frailty. These results challenge the hypothesis that persistent inflammation or endothelial injury drives long-term PASC and suggest alternative mechanisms—possibly distinct immune dysregulation or other molecular pathways—are responsible for its persistence beyond the first year.

The symptom-based data-driven RECOVER cohort PASC definition was created so that future investigations could use a more uniform and validated definition of PASC, but it was derived from a study population consisting of 91% non-hospitalized COVID-19 survivors. The prevalence of composite symptoms differed significantly between the RECOVER cohort and our cohort, with our cohort reporting lower rates of symptoms with lower associated scores (1–2 points). This difference could be due to differences in baseline cohort characteristics, the severity of our cases, differences in measurement of the symptoms, or that our cases all came from the wild-type wave, whereas the RECOVER cohort came from the wild-type and subsequent waves. However, the fact that we observed a similar prevalence of PASC at 15 months and 3 years compared with the RECOVER cohort at 6 months suggests that PASC symptoms persist, particularly in COVID-19 ALI/ARDS survivors beyond the first year following acute COVID-19 illness. Our results are also consistent with the recent meta-analysis finding that initial severity of COVID-19 illness is a major risk factor for the development of PASC (1).

Our findings of a lack of persistent inflammation associated with innate immunity activation in severe and critical COVID-19 ALI/ARDS survivors are consistent with three smaller cohort studies of mostly non-hospitalized COVID-19 survivors with biomarker and symptom assessments done in the first 6 months after acute disease (35–37). In the first study, interferon-d and IL-8 were associated with PASC when compared with never-SARS-CoV-2 infected healthy controls, but not when compared with asymptomatic COVID-19 survivors (35). In the second study, IL-6 was elevated approximately 3 months after acute disease among those with PASC, but analyses did not control for comorbid conditions or multiple comparisons, and there were no observed differences in the levels of 22 other inflammatory analytes between those with and without PASC (36). In the third study, C-reactive protein and d-dimer were not elevated in those with vs without PASC symptoms (37).

Landmark PASC cohort studies with multiomics have enrolled only a minority of hospitalized participants, with few requiring ICU admission. Blood and symptom assessments were done primarily in the first 6 months after acute disease, with PASC defined as the presence of symptoms commonly associated with long-COVID-19, such as fatigue, dyspnea, or brain fog. Although proteomics pathway analyses identified persistent neutrophilic immune activation and nonconventional monocytes in those with PASC (38, 39) and increased expression of IL-6 and IL-8 among those with PASC (39), machine learning models identified other cytokines including epidermal growth factor, low serum cortisol, and elevated immunoregulatory chemokine galactin-1 as being the strongest predictors of PASC (38, 39). Unsupervised cluster analyses of plasma proteomes in PASC study participants suggest that there may be inflammatory and non-inflammatory PASC subphenotypes (39). If such inflammatory heterogeneity exists among the COVID-19 ALI/ARDS survivors with PASC in our study, it could substantially reduce our power to detect associations with inflammatory biomarkers, given our moderate sample size. One multiomics cohort study found terminal complement dysregulation involving the C5bC6/C7 ratio and thromboinflammation involving the Von Willebrand Factor/ADAMTS13 ratio in PASC participants compared with uninfected healthy controls (40). The fact that we did not find evidence of thromboinflammation in our study may be because we chose to measure C2, C4b, and C5, since terminal complement is more unstable and difficult to measure; because we defined controls as COVID-19 ALI/ARDS survivors without PASC symptoms; or because we controlled for potential confounders with adjusted analyses.

One multiomics study enrolled severe and critical COVID-ALI/ARDS survivors similar to our study, but focused on identifying peripheral blood immune signatures associated with residual lung abnormalities in the first year after acute COVID-19 (41). Investigators discovered that persistent neutrophil activation and elevated cytokines not included in our analyses, with the exception of TNFα, are associated with residual lung abnormalities. They also found that the magnitude of the associations between inflammation and residual lung abnormalities declined by 1-year follow-up. This study suggests that post–COVID-19 inflammation might be measured best with inflammatory cytokines other than those that we chose to measure, and that inflammation may be most relevant to the pathogenesis of COVID-19 sequelae only during the first few months following acute disease.

Our study has limitations. It is a single-center prospective cohort enrolled during the wild-type wave of the pandemic, before vaccination was available. Therefore, our findings may not be generalizable to PASC due to subsequent strains of SARS-CoV-2 infection. After the 4-month visit, we enrolled additional participants to increase and then maintain the study sample size at approximately 100 persons for the 15-month and 3-year visits. Demographics, genomics, comorbidities, and acute COVID-19 hospitalization characteristics did not differ between those who did and did not follow up and did not change significantly across follow-up visits, suggesting that cohort attrition and resampling were not affected by differential loss to follow-up or selection bias (23, 24). The wording of some of our PASC symptom questions varied slightly from the RECOVER symptom questions, which could lead to misspecification of the PASC score and 12-point threshold, but our null findings were robust in sensitivity analyses across quartiles of PASC scores. Because this was an inception cohort, we did not assess frailty before acute COVID-19 hospitalization and thus cannot distinguish between preexisting and incident frailty. However, we suspect that most frailty was incident, given that participants were ambulatory, community-dwelling adults with a relatively young mean age of 55 years (sd 11). A priori, we tested only a limited number of biomarkers. Future studies should investigate novel PASC biomarkers with other multiplex biomarker assays, plasma proteomics, and metabolomics.

In conclusion, in a cohort of severe and critical adult COVID-19 ALI/ARDS survivors, we found that PASC persists for at least 3 years in approximately one quarter of participants. Our study advances the field of long-COVID-19 research with the important negative finding that prominent biomarkers of thromboinflammation and endothelial dysfunction implicated in the pathogenesis of acute COVID-19 and non-COVID-19 ARDS, sepsis, and physical frailty do not appear to be elevated in those with PASC for years after acute COVID-19 illness. Given the high prevalence and prolonged duration of PASC in COVID-19 ALI/ARDS survivors, these patients should be prioritized for future multiomics investigations that may elucidate mechanisms as potential treatment targets of their debilitating symptoms.
