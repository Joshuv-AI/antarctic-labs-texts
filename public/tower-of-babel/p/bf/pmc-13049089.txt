An intelligent approach for early smoke/fire detection using vision sensors in smart cities
Authors: Amr Abozeid, Rayan Alanazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early and reliable detection of smoke and fire is essential for minimizing damage and ensuring public safety in smart city environments; however, existing vision-based approaches often suffer from limited contextual understanding, high false-alarm rates, and reduced robustness under complex visual conditions. Conventional convolutional neural network and single-stage object detection models primarily rely on local features, which restrict their ability to detect early-stage or visually diffuse smoke patterns. To address these limitations, this study proposes an intelligent vision-based fire and smoke detection framework that integrates a Vision Transformer (ViT) with the YOLOv8 detection architecture. In the proposed model, ViT is employed as a global feature extractor to capture long-range spatial dependencies and contextual relationships, while YOLOv8 serves as a real-time detection head for precise localization and classification of fire and smoke regions. Experimental evaluation is conducted on the Fire and Smoke Dataset and the Forest Fire Smoke Dataset, comprising over 7000 diverse images collected from urban and rural environments. The proposed approach achieves an accuracy of 99.2%, precision of 98.5%, recall of 97.8%, and an F1-score of 98.1%, representing an improvement of approximately 4.3% in accuracy compared to conventional CNN-based and YOLO-only methods. Additionally, low inference latency demonstrates the suitability of the model for real-time deployment. These results confirm that the proposed ViT–YOLOv8 framework effectively overcomes the limitations of existing approaches and provides a robust, accurate, and scalable solution for early smoke and fire detection in smart city applications.

Introduction

Smart cities are radically altering urbanization, sustainability, and safety. Keeping people and their property secure is becoming increasingly crucial as the globe transitions to smart cities1. A fire may cause significant damage to both persons and property, making it one of the most hazardous and deadly natural disasters. Since the range of these sensors is quite restricted and the outdoor environment requires a wide range of detection systems to cover every region, vision sensors can help. The main limitation of this approach to smoke detection using deep learning and computer vision methods is their reliance on significant, high-quality data that is often difficult to acquire for most of these studies. This is also a common concern among various studies because they claim that the field’s real smoke and fire image data is sparse and lacks the diversity of environmental conditions for generalization. In addition, because such systems rely on large and multi-source data inputs, the computational weight may be quite high, reducing their attractiveness for real-time or resource-limited applications. Further, fixed or pre-existing datasets during training make models poor for novel scenes, as in places where pre-trained models perform significantly worse for unrepresented tasks. Lastly, the fixed camera settings solution would provide blind spots and coverage constraints in monitoring these vast areas. In summary, these indicate more adaptive, resource-effective general wildfire detection systems capable of managing applications in real-time, with the ability to deal with applications in varied environments.

Although there has been an improvement in the existing fire and smoke detection methods, there are several critical gaps remain unresolved. The CNN-based approaches are mostly based on local receptive fields, which restrict their capacity in modelling long-range spatial dependency that is required in the detection of diffuse or early-stage smoke. YOLO-based models, again, despite being computationally efficient, are usually not good at contextual understanding in challenging urban environments, causing false alarms during adverse lighting and background situations. Multi-mode and ensemble solutions are better at detecting, however come at high computational cost thus are not suitable in smart city systems with limited resources in a real-world deployment. Moreover, the fixed and single-view systems limit scalability and coverage. The proposed solution will overcome these shortcomings by combining Vision Transformers, which also use global self-attention to learn contextual and long-range visual relations with YOLOv8, which localizes objects fast and accurately. This system allows powerful fire and smoke detection in a wide range of settings at a low latency, eliminating the accuracy, scalability, and real-time limitations found in previous literature.

Proposed methodology

The proposed methodology integrates ViT with YOLOv8 to create a robust model for accurate fire and smoke detection. The framework consists of several key modules, each with a specific role in enhancing detection performance and precision. Initially, the ViT module leverages self-attention mechanisms to capture complex visual patterns specific to fire and smoke, such as color gradients, intensity, and texture, which are critical for identifying these elements under diverse conditions. ViT’s ability to detention distant dependencies within images enables the model to discern unique spatial relationships, enhancing its capability to recognize subtle fire or smoke features that traditional CNNs might overlook. Following feature extraction by ViT, the YOLOv8 module is introduced for object detection, where it detects and localizes fire and smoke regions with high speed and accuracy. YOLOv8 is selected due to its optimized architecture, which balances detection speed and precision, ensuring the model can operate in real-time with minimal latency. The integration process involves passing the feature-rich embeddings generated by ViT to YOLOv8, enabling it to identify the relevant regions with high accuracy and low false positives. ViT and YOLOv8 form a complementary framework—ViT excels in feature extraction, while YOLOv8 optimally performs localization tasks. The combined framework is trained on the dataset with data augmentation techniques to improve generalization under varied lighting, smoke density, and fire characteristics. Data collection

The dataset for the research is sourced from Kaggle26. Data for the fire and smoke detection system will be collected using a diverse dataset of more than 7000 images from over 400 urban and rural areas. The set was crowdsourced from over 1000 contributors so that varied scenarios were represented, including different lighting conditions (day and night) and distances. All images have been reviewed and verified by human-computer vision experts; thus, their training material is of the highest quality. Images captured under the dataset are mainly taken by mobile devices, emphasizing the real-world applicability of the image. They are formatted in various annotation standards that include COCO and YOLO. Only the 100 public images that are provided in26 were used from the mentioned dataset. Besides the Fire and Smoke Dataset, the Forest Fire Smoke Dataset was also used in order to make the data sets more diverse27. This dataset includes more than 42,000 images collected from heterogeneous sources and varying widely in terms of environmental settings, illumination, and scales of fires and smoke. In order to guarantee the methodological rigor of the model, avoid redundancy, and maintain computational feasibility, a subset of images was systematically extracted from27. The methodology for image extraction and annotation ensured the maintenance of variability with respect to environmental conditions, lighting, and scales of fire/smoke. Moreover, data augmentation methods, which are standard techniques for model generalization, have also been employed. The combined datasets have resulted in a final experimental data pool of 7,720 images, which was further separated into training, validation, and testing subsets. To be able to use the model unbiasedly, a fixed division of 70% to train, 15% to validate and 15% to test was introduced.

Data augmentation

The number of images can be increased significantly to enrich the data and improve the robustness of the model by making rotations, flips, changing brightness and contrast, scaling, and cropping images of the dataset. This involves presenting the model with different orientation images by randomly rotating and flipping images. Similarly, adjusting brightness and contrast simulates different lighting conditions, such as day and night, to ensure the model can identify fire and smoke under different environmental conditions. Scaling and cropping simulate variations in perceived distance and perspective, allowing the model to generalize well to real-world scenarios where smoke or fire might appear closer or farther away in the image. These combined transformations help the model learn to smoke and fire patterns under various conditions, thus making it better equipped to operate in real-time applications.

Vision transformer (ViT) for feature extraction

ViT represents a significant departure from pure CNNs in deep learning architectures applied to pictures, moving instead toward transformers derived from natural language processing28. Using a predetermined size (e.g., 16 × 16 pixels), the ViT architecture splits an input picture into smaller, non-overlapping patches. After that, these patches are compressed into one-dimensional vectors and handled similarly to tokens, representing words in a text sequence. Starting a pre-trained model from large datasets such as ImageNet can make it convenient with transfer learning for the sake of ViT, considering that training time and available resources can be slashed since the model is equipped with base-level visual features by default. Fine-tuning needs preparation of the “Fire and Smoke Dataset,” which modifies the last layers of the ViT in adaptation to the binary classification task of distinguishing between fire and smoke versus non-fire cases. In this stage, learning rate, batch size, and the number of training epochs are to be tuned down, usually at a much lower learning rate than the initial training phase. Position encoding is added after each patch is transferred to a vector using a linear transformation to maintain spatial information from the original image.

YOLOv8 as a fire and Smoke detector

The most recent version of the CNN dynamic object model, YOLOv8, has excellent real-time accuracy. One neural network processes the full picture region, after which it is split up into many parts, and for each part, the probability estimates and possible bounding boxes are estimated29. YOLOv1 to YOLOv7 is continuously improved upon to create the YOLOv8 network. A sequence of convolution layers that pull pixels down at various resolution sizes makes up the YOLO model’s backbone. These features then go via a neck where they are combined and placed into a head, resulting in a loss matrix for object recognition.

YOLOv8 is anchor-free. It predicts the center of an object directly instead of the anchor box offset. Since fewer box predictions are generated with anchor-free detection, Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS), a post-processing mechanism employed for filtering out candidate detections, happens faster. RasNet is the brain of YOLOv8. C2F was employed in the last detection step; f is the number of features in this case. Convolution, batch norm, and the silu function make up the CBS block. Anchor boxes are used in the last detection step to display the likelihood of item detection. Comparing the performance of different versions and switching between them is easy. In the ViT section, the input image is divided into smaller patches, flattened, and assigned position embeddings to maintain spatial relationships. Discussion

The analysis of the performance of the developed ViT in combination with YOLOv8 shows that this model is superior in the early recognition of smoke and fire, with an accuracy of 99.2% compared to traditional models, such as SFDS18, CNN+YOLO22, and FSF Net. A high precision of 98.5% and recall of 97.8% indicate how the model is performing to identify the true fire and smoke with the few false positives and negatives, which is crucial for applications involving safety-critical domains. The F1-score of 98.1% also signifies the fact that the model does well with balanced performance to ensure accurate detection in a real-time scenario. In addition, low latency enables better practical application of the model with timely intervention and proactive emergency response efforts within smart cities. The qualitative examples of detection also show that the model can be used to effectively locate fire and smoke areas to supplement the quantitative evaluation metrics. Also, the overall analysis concluded that the proposed framework is well beyond the current methodologies, as it has shown potential development of public safety through highly advanced detection. Early fire detection, along with response time in smart city environments, is also enhanced through this framework. Enhanced public safety through accurate detection, the model supports proactive measures in emergency services and optimizes resource allocation while minimizing damage. Advanced machine learning and deep learning techniques are important in intelligent, responsive urban safety systems.

In problematic cases where there is visual appearance of fire-like visual indicators but no combustion, e.g. printed flame patterns on garments or fire scenes on displays, the given model proves to be more robust in the sense that it is based not only on colour information but also on learning of contextual and spatial features. The Vision Transformer element identifies relationships in contexts around the world, and this allows distinguishing between fixed flame-like features and dynamically changing areas of fire or smoke which have specific patterns of diffusion, irregularities, and borders, and behaving characteristics. Moreover, the YOLOv8 detection head maintains spatial consistency and object-level localization which minimizes the number of false detections due to localized or artificial patterns. Even though such edge cases are still difficult to handle in purely vision-based systems, the hybrid architecture can be used to counter the false alarms in visually deceptive situations.

Although the suggested ViT-YOLOv8 system records high accuracy and stable performance for real-time smoke and fire detection, there are some limitations that need to be realized. Firstly, the model is tested largely on controlled datasets under laboratory conditions, which might fail to present the complexity of real-world scenarios such as complex weather, lighting, and occlusion. Moreover, the use of visual data only could be seen to restrict the accuracy of detection in low-visibility conditions like darkness or thick fog. To overcome these challenges, future research could include integrating multi-modal data streams, e.g., thermal imaging, humidity, and temperature sensors, to enhance robustness in detection across varying conditions. In addition, deploying the model in real-life urban environments and testing it on streaming video feeds could be used to assess its real-time resilience and adaptability. Future developments can also consider lightweight model pruning for deployment over edge devices, allowing for broader applicability in smart city architectures with constrained computational resources.

Conclusion

The proposed ViT combined with YOLOv8 presents a significant advancement in smoke and fire detection technology, achieving an accuracy of 99.2%, high precision, recall, and a robust F1-score, confirming its reliability and effectiveness for real-time applications in smart city environments. The proposed framework has great performance metrics with low latency and accurate threat localization ability. Therefore, the proposed model will improve public safety measures to a greater extent. This model is better than existing methods and emphasizes the significance of fast and accurate systems in controlling fire hazards. Integrating advanced machine learning techniques such as ViT and YOLOv8 paves the way for proactive emergency responses. It supports the development of intelligent safety infrastructures in urban settings that may eventually transform how fire safety is managed and implemented in smart cities. The future may also involve the integration of more environmental data sources, including temperature and humidity sensors, to enhance detection accuracy. It can be extended further to recognize other hazards beyond fire and smoke, thereby broadening its application in emergency response systems.
