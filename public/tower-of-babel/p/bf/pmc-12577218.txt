Compliance with global action plan on antibiotic prescription and utilization: a multi-facility cross-sectional study in southern Nigeria
Authors: Mary Richard Akpan, Unyime Israel Eshiet, Idongesit Linus Jackson, Ekemini-Obong Etim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntibiotic resistance is a growing concern worldwide. The World Health Organization (WHO) developed the AWaRe classification to categorize antibiotics based on their risk of resistance and to guide appropriate antibiotic prescribing. Understanding the pattern of antibiotics prescribing can help to inform policy and practice.ObjectiveTo assess the extent of compliance with global action plan on antibiotic prescription and utilization in the country by assessing antibiotic prescribing patterns in selected public healthcare facilities using the WHO AWaRe classification of antibiotics.MethodA cross sectional study involving a 3-month review of antibiotic prescription pattern using the WHO AWaRe classification of antibiotics was carried out in 5 selected healthcare facilities in Akwa Ibom State, southern Nigeria, between September 01 and November 30, 2023.ResultsA total of 2,230 antibiotic prescriptions were reviewed across the 5 selected hospitals during the period of the study. The proportion of antibiotics prescribed within the Acess group of the AWaRe classification in the hospitals was 48.7% (n = 1,086), while the proportion of antibiotics prescribed within the “Watch” group was 51.3% (n = 1,144). No antibiotic was prescribed within the Reserve group across the hospitals within the period of the study.ConclusionThere’s a lack of compliance with the WHO global action plan on antibiotic prescription and utilization as the overall prescribing of Access group antibiotics in this study falls short of the WHO recommendations. There is a need for a review of the nationwide strategy to optimize the use of antibiotics in the country.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Bacterial Infections due to pathogenic organisms that are resistant to available antibiotics are usually difficult to treat. Such infections when compared with infections caused by antibiotic sensitive bacteria, are usually associated with higher mortality rates and higher costs of treatment [1]. The emergence of resistant bacteria strains is caused by the administration of suboptimal doses of antibiotics or the irrational selection and use of antibiotics for non-bacterial infections [2–5]. Globally, the development of resistance to first line antibiotics, particularly among hospital and community bacterial infections, has increased over the years [1]. In Nigeria, although a published national surveillance data on antibiotic utilization and associated resistance is lacking, reports indicate a high rate of resistance to cotrimoxazole and ampicillin among pathogenic organisms responsible for urinary tract infections [6]. Also, resistance to the fluoroquinolones and the third generation cephalosporins have also been reported in some parts of the country [6].

In 2015, the World Health Organization (WHO) launched an action plan targeted at curbing the global threat of antibiotic resistance. This initiative emphasized the need to optimize the use of available antibiotics in human and animals through effective antibiotic stewardship, and improving the awareness and understanding of antibiotic resistance among healthcare practitioners through educational interventions [7]. Member states were encouraged to develop and implement antibiotic stewardship programs in line with the global action plan on antibiotic resistance. This led to the development of the National Action Plan for antibiotic resistance by the Nigerian government [8–10].

Furthermore, the Antibiotics Working Group for the 21st WHO Model of Essential Medicines adopted the AWaRe (Access. Watch and Reserve) classification for antibiotics included in the list of essential medicines [8]. In 2018, the AWaRe classification, which now covers 258 antibiotics, was adopted by Health Ministers of the G20 states. The effect of individual antibiotics and their respective classes on the emergence of antibiotic resistant bacteria strains is taken into consideration in this classification, as it focuses on the appropriate use of available antibiotics [9]. The WHO recommends that the utilization of antibiotics at the country level should constitute not less than 60% from the Access group antibiotics [9].

The Nigeria action plan on antibiotic resistance is aligned with the WHO objectives and aims to promote antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) and reduce antibiotic resistance and its impact on health systems and healthcare costs. Antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) can be described as a set of interventions to promote the appropriate use of antimicrobial agents in humans and animals to curb the development of antibiotic resistant strains of microorganisms, preserve the efficacy of available antibiotics and improve therapeutic outcomes [10, 11]. Some of the AMS activities include prospective audit of antibiotic use with interventions to improve the utilization of antibiotics by hospital AMS teams, formulary restriction and preauthorization [11].

The AWaRe classification supports AMS by identifying antibiotics with high resistance potentials and restricting their use to specific infections or confirmed cases of multi-drug resistant organisms. Optimization of antibiotic prescribing by clinicians is a core objective of the WHO global action plan and the Nigerian national action plan on antibiotic resistance [7, 9]. This study was thus aimed at evaluating the extent of compliance with the global action plan on antibiotic prescription and utilization in the country by assessing antibiotic prescribing patterns in outpatient departments of selected public healthcare facilities using the WHO AWaRe classification of antibiotics.

Methods

Study design and setting

It was a cross sectional study involving a 3 month review of antibiotic prescription pattern in five selected public healthcare facilities across the three senatorial districts of Akwa Ibom State, southern Nigeria. The prescription pattern was assessed using the WHO AWaRe classification of antibiotics. One tertiary and four secondary healthcare facilities were purposively selected for the study with at least one facility from each senatorial district of the state. The selected study sites were; The University of Uyo Teaching Hospital (UUTH), General Hospital – Ikot Ekpene (GH-IK), General Hospital – Ikot Okoro (GH-IO), St. Luke’s Hospital – Anua (SLH) and General Hospital – Ibeno (GH-IB), all in Akwa Ibom State.

UUTH, a referral tertiary healthcare facility and an affiliate of the University of Uyo, offers specialized clinical services in various medical specialties and sub-specialties.

SLH is a faith based secondary healthcare facility located in Uyo, Akwa Ibom State. It is one of the foremost missionary hospitals in the southern region of Nigeria. It provides healthcare services to residents of Uyo local government area and the neighboring local government areas within Akwa Ibom state.

GH-Ik, GH-IO and GH-IB are all secondary healthcare facilities managed by the Akwa-Ibom state Hospital Management Board, an agency under the Akwa Ibom State Ministry of Health responsible for managing and overseeing the state’s hospitals, including policy formulation, staff training, and general administration. These hospitals provide secondary healthcare services to residents in the respective local government areas where they are located.

Eligibility criteria

Criteria for selecting prescriptions for inclusion in the study were prescriptions for adult patients and prescriptions that contained at least one antibiotic. Prescriptions longer than 48 h from the time of data collection were excluded from the study.

Data collection and analysis

Antibiotics prescribing data was collected from prescription sheets issued to ambulatory patients by physicians consulting at the outpatient departments of the respective hospitals between September 01 and November 30, 2023, and filled into a data collection form stating the name, the class and the WHO AWaRe classification of the prescribed antibiotic. Collected data were coded and analyzed using the IBM statistical program and service solutions (SPSS) Version 27.0 computer package (IBM Corp., Amonk, NY). Appropriate descriptive statistics including frequencies and percentages were used in the analysis.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this research was obtained from the Health Research Ethics (HREC) Committees of the Akwa Ibom State Ministry of Health and the University of Uyo Teaching Hospital.

Discussion

We assessed the extent of compliance with the global action plan on antibiotic prescription and utilization in public hospitals in Nigeria by evaluating the pattern of prescription of antibiotics based on the WHO AWaRe classification in selected public healthcare facilities. We found the penicillins, floroquinolones and the cephalosporins to be among the most frequently prescribed class of antibiotics. Among the individual antibiotic prescriptions, we found amoxicillin, either alone or in combination with clavulanate, to be the most frequently prescribed, accounting for over a quarter of all the antibiotic prescriptions reviewed.

Our observation is similar to an earlier report in 2015 by Eshiet et al. [12] who found amoxicillin, either singly or as a combination with clavulanic acid, to be the most frequently prescribed antibiotic in a tertiary healthcare facility in southern Nigeria. Similar studies at first and second level hospitals as well as a teaching hospital in Zambia however found the third generation cephalosporin – ceftriaxone, to be the most frequently prescribed antibiotic [13–15]. Our study showed a high prescription rate for metronidazole, a nitroimidazole antibiotic. The use of metronidazole in Nigeria is believed to be high as indicated by previous studies in the country [12, 16]. This may be due to its efficacy, affordability, ready availability and good safety profile.

Although our results showed that in two of the facilities studied (GH-IO and GH-IB) majority of the antibiotics prescribed belonged to the Access group of the WHO AWaRe classification, the overall assessment of the five hospitals studied showed that most of the prescribed antibiotics belonged to the Watch group. A similar study by Ekuma et al. in Uyo - Nigeria also found that the Watch group was the most utilized group of antibiotics [17]. Variations in the utilization of antibiotics classes based on the WHO AWaRe classification has been reported by previous investigators. For instance, similar studies in Zambia, Ghana and India found that the Access group of antibiotics was the most prescribed group [13, 18, 19]. On the other hand, a study in Bangladesh [20] found that almost two-thirds of the prescribed antibiotics were from the Watch group. A worldwide assessment of antibiotic prescribing patterns for adult in-patients based on the WHO AWaRe classification found significant differences in the proportional use of antibiotics in the Access, Watch and Reserve categories [21]. The researchers found that the overall utilization of Watch antibiotics was high. Further analysis by the researchers revealed that healthcare facilities in lower-middle and upper-middle income countries considerably contributed to the percentage of Watch antibiotics utilized. The high utilization of these broad-spectrum antibiotics belonging to the Watch group in these countries is believed to be due to increasing levels of antibiotic resistance, improved access to the antibiotics and rising incidence of infectious disease caused by urbanization [21]. Other worldwide studies also revealed similar variations in antibiotic prescription based on the AWaRe classification [22, 23], with a report indicating a substantial increase in the use of Watch antibiotics in low-and-middle income countries between year 2,000 and 2015 [24]. On the contrary, reports from Togo, Guinea and South Africa show high Access-to Watch ratios with similar trends in Burundi and Burkina Faso [21, 25].

In all the antibiotic prescriptions reviewed in this study, we found no prescribed antibiotic belonging to the Reserve group. A similar finding was reported in a Caribbean study where Reserve antibiotics were not prescribed for the patients [26]. Previous surveys in hospitals in Nigeria, Loas and Guinea also found no reserve antibiotic prescriptions, attributable to the non-availability of these agents in the settings [21]. In contrast, the use of Reserve antibiotics was reported to be high in India and Latin American countries suggesting an increasing burden of antibiotic resistant pathogens in these countries [27, 28].

Our results showed a lack of compliance with the WHO global action plan and the Nigerian national action plan on antibiotic prescription and utilization as the overall prescribing of Access group antibiotics in our study falls short of the WHO recommendations which emphasizes that more than sixty-percent of prescribed antibiotics must be from the Access group [9]. It is in consonance with a 2023 report of three point prevalence surveys (PPS), where underutilization of the Access group antibiotics and overuse of the Watch group antibiotics were found [17]. These findings suggest the need for a review of the nationwide strategy to optimize the use of antibiotics as antibiotic resistance poses a serious threat to public health and the economic growth of any nation [29].

A study of twenty healthcare facilities randomly selected from the six geopolitical zones of Nigeria to evaluate the implementation and practice of AMS in the country found only six facilities with AMS committees, however, with no regular AMS-related activities [30]. Also, a recent study to access the knowledge of AMS and the AWaRe classification of antibiotics among frontline healthcare professionals in Nigeria reported significant knowledge deficit in both AMS and the AWaRe classification of antibiotics among the healthcare professionals [31]. Therefore, measures to improve the awareness and understanding of antibiotic resistance, AMS and the AWaRe classification among healthcare professionals should be developed and implemented. This can be achieved through effective communication, continuous education and training. A Cochrane review of interventions to improve antibiotic prescribing for hospital inpatients established that AMS interventions are effective in increasing compliance with antibiotic stewardship policy as well as reducing antibiotic resistance and healthcare-associated infections [32].

Conclusion

There’s a lack of compliance with the WHO global action plan on antibiotic prescription and utilization as the overall prescribing of Access group antibiotics in this study falls short of the WHO recommendations. There is a need for a review of the nationwide strategy to optimize the use of antibiotics in the country. Sensitization of healthcare workers through educational interventions and the implementation of antimicrobial stewardship programs in hospitals is required.

Limitations of the study

All the study sites were in a single state; thus, the findings may not be a true reflection of the pattern of antibiotic prescription in other states of the country. Hence, the generalization of these findings to other states and practice settings should be done with caution. Furthermore, this study was conducted exclusively in an outpatient setting and thus may underestimate the use of antibiotics, particularly parenteral antibiotics which are commonly prescribed for in-patients.
