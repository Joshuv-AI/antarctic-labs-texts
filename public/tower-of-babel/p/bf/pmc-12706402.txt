Model‐Informed Drug Development for Ligelizumab in Patients With Chronic Spontaneous Urticaria
Authors: Andrzej Bienczak, Aurelie Gautier, Eva Hua, Yan Ji, Emil Scosyrev, Serge Smeets, Thomas Severin, Anton Drollmann, Manmath Patekar, Marina Savelieva
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTModel‐informed drug development (MIDD) has been increasingly applied to guide decision‐making, ameliorate efficiency, and enhance the likelihood of successful trials. The development of ligelizumab, a humanized anti‐IgE monoclonal antibody, in chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) illustrated how MIDD can be applied to support central aspects of drug development, such as dose selection and trial design, pediatric drug development and extrapolation, generation of evidence to support potential labeling, optimizing treatment outcomes, and enhancing patient access. In this manuscript, we provide an overview of the key modeling and simulation analyses that were part of the MIDD approach for the development of ligelizumab in CSU and how they were staggered around the availability of interim and final data from the Phase 2 and Phase 3 studies. Furthermore, we present details of the non‐linear mixed‐effects models characterizing the population pharmacokinetics and exposure‐response relationship of ligelizumab for efficacy in adolescent and adult patients with CSU.Keywords: drug development, exposure‐response, mixed‐effect models, model‐based drug development, modeling, pediatric, simulation

Introduction

Model‐informed drug development (MIDD) refers to various quantitative methods that integrate clinical trial data along with pharmacological, biological, and disease knowledge. MIDD uses exposure‐based, biological and statistical models derived from preclinical and clinical data to help decrease uncertainty and lower failure rates and to develop information that cannot or would not be generated experimentally [1, 2]. The MIDD approach aims to facilitate drug development decision‐making, improve efficiency, and increase the probability of successful trial outcomes, particularly in situations where data are scarce [1, 2]. The MIDD approach follows the “learn and confirm” paradigm, where, upon the emergence of new data, model‐based predictions are verified and models are adjusted accordingly to guide the next step of the development process [3].

Chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) is characterized by the spontaneous appearance of itchy wheals (hives) and/or angioedema lasting for > 6 weeks without an identifiable trigger [4, 5]. Despite treatment, a substantial proportion of patients have uncontrolled CSU, highlighting an unmet need for more effective and novel treatments that lead to complete suppression of urticaria symptoms, as determined using the weekly Urticaria Activity Score (UAS7) [6].

Ligelizumab, a high‐affinity humanized anti‐IgE monoclonal antibody, was being developed for treating patients with CSU whose signs and symptoms are inadequately controlled by H1‐antihistamines (H1‐AH) [6, 7]. The clinical development was based on hypotheses that higher binding affinity and differences in binding epitope would result in a superior clinical response compared to the predecessor anti‐IgE monoclonal antibody, omalizumab [8, 9]. Recently published results of two Phase 3 pivotal trials showed that ligelizumab improved urticaria symptom control and was safe in patients with CSU [10]; however, it was not superior to omalizumab, and a strategic decision was made to discontinue ligelizumab development activities in CSU. Herein, we provide an overview of the key modeling and simulation (M&S) analyses that were part of the MIDD approach for ligelizumab in CSU and how they were staggered around the availability of interim and final data from Phase 2 and Phase 3 studies.

Furthermore, we present details of the non‐linear mixed‐effects models characterizing the population pharmacokinetics (PopPK) and exposure‐response relationship of ligelizumab for efficacy assessed using UAS7 in adolescent and adult patients with CSU, as well as the findings of an exploratory exposure‐response analysis for safety.

The analyses were performed iteratively following the “learn and confirm” paradigm [14, 15] and used data from studies listed in Table 2, as those became available throughout the development of the CSU program.

Data

MIDD analyses were supported through different portions of data in the ligelizumab CSU program (Table 1) comprising individual ligelizumab serum concentrations and efficacy assessed using UAS7 in patients with CSU (Section 1 in Supporting Information S1). The studies and data used in the development of the models presented herein are presented in Table 2.

Ligelizumab pharmacokinetic (PK) concentrations and presence of antidrug antibodies (ADAs) in serum samples were analyzed using validated assays [16]. PK sampling schemes are shown in Table 2. PK concentrations below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) were flagged in the modeling dataset as left‐censored data and used as such in model fitting (Section 1.3b in Supporting Information S1).

UAS7 is a composite score expressing weekly urticaria activity on a scale of 0–42 and is calculated based on itch and hives severity reported by patients twice a day. A patient is classified as a complete responder (CR) if UAS7 = 0 at that time point. Details on UAS7 derivation and imputation rules used for missing scores are presented in Sections 1.1 and 1.3c in Supporting Information S1. The time course of the individual observed UAS7 was used in model fitting. As UAS7 scores are bounded between 0 and 42, a logit‐type transformation was applied to the original scores, which were then used in model fitting (Section 1.2 in Supporting Information S1).

Further details on handling of missing data are presented in Section 1.3 in Supporting Information S1; a summary of excluded data is shown in Section 1.4 in Supporting Information S1.

Model‐Based Analyses

MIDD activities were supported through PK‐IgE binding [11], PopPK, and longitudinal exposure–response modeling. More information on model development and software details is presented in Section 2 in Supporting Information S1.

PK Analyses

PK‐IgE binding and various compartmental models were tested at different stages of clinical development. At each step of development, model structure was confirmed, and accruing data allowed for more precise parameters and covariate effects estimates (Section 2.1 in Supporting Information S1).

Exposure‐Response Analysis for Efficacy

Exposure‐response modeling was conducted using a sequential approach (Section 2.2 in Supporting Information S1). The exposure‐response model for UAS7 comprised a placebo response model and a drug effect model. The mathematical and graphical representations and details of covariate assessment are presented in Sections 2.2.a and 2.2.b in Supporting Information S1 for the placebo and drug response models, respectively.

Simulations

Model‐based simulations were performed to address various clinically relevant questions that were part of the MIDD analyses outlined in the section Flow of MIDD analyses in the ligelizumab CSU program. Details of the simulations conducted with the final models are provided in Section 2.3 in Supporting Information S1.

Exploratory Exposure‐Response Analysis for Safety

Exposure‐response analysis for safety was a descriptive analysis that assessed the exposure‐safety relationship in patients with CSU treated with ligelizumab in studies C2302 and C2303 (Section 2.4 in Supporting Information S1).

Results

High‐level outcomes of MIDD activities depicted in Figure 1 are presented in Table 1. The evolution of the models used to support the activities is described subsequently, with a key focus on the final models.

PK Analyses

Initially, in the absence of data from CSU patients, a PK‐IgE binding model was developed on a pool of Phase 1 studies to guide dose selection for the Phase 2b CSU study C2201 based on the drug's mechanism of action (i.e., predicted depletion of free IgE; Table 1 MIDD activity 1.1) [11]. With the emergence of patient data, and because the PK of ligelizumab was equally well characterized by a simpler two‐compartment PopPK model (which had shorter run‐times and less convergence issues), the PK‐IgE binding model was retired. The PopPK model was initially developed with interim data from study C2201 to support dose selection for pivotal studies (Table 1 MIDD activity 1.2) and adolescent Phase 2 study C2202 (Table 1 MIDD activity 2.1). The model was further updated with emerging data to support dose selection for the pediatric study (Table 1 MIDD activity 2.2) and after completion of pivotal trials to confirm the final posology in adolescents and adults (Table 1 MIDD activities 2.3 and 3.1). The final PopPK model was built on data from 1907 individuals, 113 of whom were adolescent patients with CSU, 1593 were adult patients with CSU, and 201 were healthy adult volunteers. Their baseline demographic characteristics and a summary of the data are presented in Section 3.1 in Supporting Information S1. Population parameters of the final model and the diagnostic plots demonstrated good descriptive and predictive capabilities of the model (Section 3.1.b and Figures S4–S7 in Supporting Information S1, respectively).

Body weight effect was included for all clearance and volume parameters using allometric scaling, centered at 70 kg with estimated exponents of 0.9 and 0.6, respectively. In addition to body weight, the main statistically significant covariate relationships were baseline total IgE on apparent clearance (CL/F) and apparent volume of peripheral compartment (Vp/F) with estimated exponents of 0.106 and −0.0816, respectively, and “ADA‐positive” status (an increase in CL/F by 30% and a decrease of Vp/F by 41% for patients who had at least one ADA‐positive sample [“ADA‐positive”] vs. patients with no ADA‐positive samples [“ADA‐negative”], respectively).

Exposure‐Response Analysis

Placebo Model

Placebo response over time was comparable between the studies and explored covariate subgroups (Section 3.2.b and Figures S9–S11 in Supporting Information S1). Hence, the placebo model structure remained unchanged throughout the development process and did not include covariate effects (Sections 2.2.a and 3.2.c in Supporting Information S1). Placebo response was described as a mono‐exponential decline from a baseline value (U0) to a new steady state value (USS) with a time component of placebo response time (PRT; inverse of rate constant for change). The model's population parameters were estimated with relatively high precision (Section 3.2.c and Table S8 in Supporting Information S1); however, the IIV in all model parameters was large, reflecting the high variability in response due to background H1‐AH treatment. The visual predictive checks for changes in UAS7 indicated overall good descriptive and predictive performance of the placebo model (Section 3.2.b and Figure S12 in Supporting Information S1).

Exposure‐Response UAS7 Model

The drug effect for ligelizumab in CSU was first characterized using an indirect response model where, due to a limited amount of post‐treatment wash‐out data (Table 1 MIDD activities 1.2 and 2.1), the turnover effect was described with a single parameter (drug response time). With the emergence of data in the wash‐out phase (Table 1 MIDD activity 2.2), model parametrization was changed to a classical turnover model (Section 2.2.b and 3.2.e in Supporting Information S1). The advantage of this parametrization is that the rate constant for indirect response production (k in) and loss of response (k out) can have different values. The inhibitory effect of ligelizumab (INHUAS7) on k in was expressed using a sigmoidal (hyperbolic) function with EC50, Hill coefficient, and maximum effect (E max).

The final exposure‐response model for UAS7 was estimated on data from 1808 patients with CSU, 115 of whom were adolescents and 1693 were adults (a summary of the data and baseline patient characteristics, and identified strong correlations between several of them, are presented in Section 3.2.a in Supporting Information S1). Model parameter estimates are presented in Section 3.2.e in Supporting Information Supplement S1. The relative standard error on all estimated parameters was small to moderate; however, similar to the placebo model, the IIV on the structural parameters was high (particularly for EC50 and k out). Overall, the exposure‐response model had good descriptive and predictive performance, especially for data from the pivotal studies that provided the majority of the fitted data (Figure 2). The model described well the onset phase of treatment and its maximum effect; however, it had some limitations in capturing the dynamics of the effect offset after treatment discontinuation.

Visual predictive checks for (a) changes in UAS7 over time and (b) proportion of complete responders (UAS7 = 0) over time split by study and treatment arm for the final exposure–response model. CI, confidence interval; pCR, proportion of complete responders (UAS7 = 0) based on observed data or probability of achieving complete response (UAS7 = 0) based on simulation; PI, prediction interval; q4w, every 4 weeks; UAS7, weekly Urticaria Activity Score.

The exploratory graphical analysis indicated differences in UAS7 response by levels of baseline total IgE, namely an umbrella‐shaped effect with slower onset of action and/or lower maximum response in subgroups with low and high IgE levels (Section 3.2.d and Figures S13–S15 in Supporting Information S1). Based on these distinctly different response patterns and literature [17], the following three subgroups were proposed: low IgE (< 40 IU/mL), moderate IgE (40 to 300 IU/mL), and high IgE (≥ 300 IU/mL). The covariate testing during the drug effect model–building process confirmed observations from the exploratory analysis, identifying IgE as the covariate with the strongest impact on model parameters. EC50 in patients with moderate IgE levels was 747.9 ng/mL and increased in patients with low and high baseline total IgE levels by 75% and 219% to 1309.3 ng/mL and 2385.7 ng/mL, respectively. Reference k out in the model was 0.09 day−1; k out was slower in patients with low IgE (reduced by 31% to 0.062 day−1), adolescents (reduced by 31% to 0.062 day−1), patients who developed ADAs (reduced by 32% to 0.062 day−1), and Asians (reduced by 31% to 0.062 day−1). k out was higher in the study C2201 (39% increase to 0.12 day−1). Baseline UAS7 was higher in patients with CSU who had angioedema at baseline (4% higher) or who previously used cyclosporine (4% higher) and in the study C2201 (13% higher).

Simulations

Simulations were conducted in support of all MIDD activities listed in Table 1. Herein, we present an example of simulations performed to confirm the final posology, evaluation of best treatment options in selected patient subpopulations (Table 1 MIDD activity 3.1), and up‐titration scenarios for non‐responders (Table 1 MIDD activity 3.2).

Simulations of Response to Ligelizumab Under Various Dosing Scenarios

Simulations indicated that an approximately dose‐proportional increase from 2.1 to 3.5 μg/mL and to 6.9 μg/mL in median ligelizumab trough exposures at steady state (C min,SS) for the 72, 120, and 240 mg doses, respectively, would have only a small incremental benefit on UAS7 reduction and increasing probability of achieving UAS7 = 0 (pCR), indicating that the plateau of response was achieved with the 72 mg q4w dose in most patients (Figure 3).

Simulations of Ligelizumab Exposures and Predicted Response in Subgroups of Interest

Simulations were further performed to assess how covariates detected as significant in both PopPK and exposure‐response analyses affected ligelizumab exposure and treatment response. Baseline IgE level was the covariate that had the strongest effect on both ligelizumab PK and exposure‐response relationship. Simulated median ligelizumab C min,SS in patients with moderate baseline IgE levels was 2.2 and 3.6 μg/mL for the 72 and 120 mg q4w regimens, respectively, and it was predicted to increase by approximately 30% in patients with low baseline IgE and to decrease by approximately 25% in patients with high baseline IgE (Figure 4a). The highest treatment response was predicted for patients from the moderate IgE subgroup with pCR (95% prediction interval [PI]) of 50% (46%–54%) and 54% (49%–58%) for the 72 mg dose at Weeks 24 and 52, respectively. Dose increase to 120 mg would result in increases of 4% and 5% in pCR at Weeks 24 and 52, respectively. The model predicted that patients from the low IgE subgroup would have the lowest response in the first 8 weeks after treatment initiation; however, the response would improve over time. By Week 8, patients from this subgroup would have a response similar to that in patients from the high IgE subgroup. After Week 36, their response would be similar to that in patients from the moderate IgE subgroup. Patients from the low IgE subgroup were predicted to have pCR (95% PI) of 45% (40%–50%) and 52% (47%–58%) at Weeks 24 and 52, respectively, from the high IgE subgroup of 39% (33%–45%) and 44% (38%–52%), respectively. Increasing the ligelizumab dose to 120 mg would increase the probabilities (95% PI) to 49% (44%–54%) and 56% (50%–61%) as well as 43% (36%–49%) and 48% (41%–54%) at Weeks 24 and 52 in the low and high IgE subgroups, respectively (Figure 4b).

Simulations of Up‐Titration Scenarios Based on Individual Patient Response

The simulations showed that patients who had a good response to ligelizumab treatment (UAS7 ≤ 6) at time points considered for dose modification responded to the treatment rapidly and were more likely to achieve a rapid and sustained treatment benefit and become symptom‐free (UAS7 = 0) after 1 year of treatment. Conversely, inadequate responders at the same time points (UAS7 > 6) had a lower probability of being symptom‐free after 1 year of treatment. To explore the potential for improvement in inadequate responders through up‐titration, additional simulations were conducted. This assessment was conducted to determine how many of these poor responders could achieve symptom relief by the end of 1 year of treatment if they had switched to a higher dose of ligelizumab, as well as identify the optimal time point for dose adjustment. Based on the simulations conducted, the predicted proportions of patients with inadequate response (UAS7 > 6) with a 72 mg q4w dose were 44%, 41%, and 37% at Weeks 12, 16, and 20, respectively. In the case of up‐titration in these inadequate responders from 72 to 120 mg starting at those respective time points, the probability of them being symptom‐free at the end of year 1 would increase by 5%–6%, from 13% to 19%, 9% to 15%, and 5% to 10%, respectively (Section 3.3 and Figure S16 in Supporting Information S1).

Exposure‐Response Analysis for Safety

The exposure‐response analysis for safety was limited to data from pivotal studies C2302 and C2303. No trends in the rates of adverse events (AEs) or serious AEs (SAEs) were observed in patients treated with ligelizumab with respect to exposure in either of the explored data pools. The proportion of patients with AEs/SAEs was comparable across the exposure quartiles in both placebo‐controlled and entire study duration pools (Section 3.4 and Tables S12–S15 in Supporting Information S1). No difference in the safety profile was observed between patients who developed ADA and those who did not.

Discussion

Over the last decade, the MIDD framework has emerged as an important component of drug development within the pharmaceutical industry and regulatory decision‐making [18, 19]. Development of ligelizumab in CSU is an example of how MIDD and “learn and confirm” paradigm was applied throughout subsequent phases of the clinical development program. The framework guided various aspects of the program (Table 1), including dose selection and design of Phase 2b and Phase 3 studies, supporting the pediatric development and extrapolation, generating evidence for potential labeling, optimization of treatment outcomes, and improving patient access. The M&S analyses were driven by specific questions, which evolved through the drug development process depending on the data and information available. The models used to address those questions were developed following the principles of parsimony (“fit for purpose”) [20] and evolved over time. Initially, in the absence of any clinical data in CSU, and based on the mechanism of action of ligelizumab, a more complex mechanistic PK‐pharmacodynamics (PD) model was used to guide dose selection for the Phase 2 trial in relation to the anticipated IgE biomarker response. With the emergence of patient data, an exposure‐response model for clinical outcome (UAS7) was developed, and the PK‐IgE binding model was retired in favor of a more parsimonious PopPK model.

One of the key topics throughout the development process was dose selection and optimization. Analyses of data from the adult Phase 2b study led to the selection of 72 and 120 mg q4w doses to be tested in the Phase 3 studies, the results of which were recently published [10]. Consistent with the observed data, the final M&S analysis presented in this manuscript indicated little differentiation in UAS7 response between the regimens tested in the pivotal studies (72 mg q4w and 120 mg q4w) and suggested that 72 mg q4w should provide fast and sustained UAS7 suppression in most patients with CSU. Covariates identified as significant in PopPK and exposure–response analyses were considered for dose optimization. Baseline IgE levels emerged as the most influential covariate affecting ligelizumab PK and UAS7 response, demonstrating a noteworthy umbrella‐shaped effect on the exposure–response relationship, which, we speculate, is likely to have a compelling mechanistic explanation. IgE effect on PK can be explained through its mode of action, that is, cytokine neutralization, and an empirical equivalent to the mechanistic description of this process from the PK‐IgE binding model. Concerning efficacy, low IgE levels have been previously associated with a higher likelihood of having Type IIb endotype, where CSU is predominantly driven by IgG autoantibodies (directed against IgE or the IgE receptor), rather than IgE antibodies to autoallergens (Type I endotype) [21]. Given that ligelizumab is an IgE‐neutralizing antibody, a lower response could be expected in patients with CSU with activating factors alternative to IgE. By contrast, very high IgE levels might indicate high disease activity, which could explain the poor treatment response in this subgroup compared with patients with moderate IgE levels at baseline and elucidate the umbrella‐shaped effect of baseline IgE evident in the observed data. The simulations suggest, however, that despite those apparent differences, dose increase in patients with very high IgE would provide only marginal improvements in response. Similar simulations were conducted for other subgroups with lower PK exposures (e.g., patients with high body weight or ADAs) or different response dynamics (e.g., based on difference in the k out estimate). The results indicated that increasing the dose would have limited clinical benefit owing to the high inter‐patient variability in UAS7 response, largely influenced by background and rescue H1‐AH medication.

A challenging aspect that complicated treatment optimization involved high correlations between several baseline patient characteristics, which might have confounded the estimated covariate effects. Furthermore, given the heterogeneous character and not clearly understood pathophysiology of CSU, instead of a priori dose adjustment in selected subgroups of patients, we assessed the benefit of up‐titration based on individual treatment response. Results of this assessment supported discussions on utility, planning, and designing of a possible treatment optimization study. The predicted benefit of up‐titrating poor responders was, however, small and forecasted to have little clinical impact.

MIDD was employed to mitigate the challenges and provide robust support to pediatric drug development for ligelizumab in CSU. We recently published the results of the adolescent PK/PD study C2202, which was designed to support model‐based analysis, rather than a formal between‐group comparison for the primary endpoints [7]. This allowed for the reduction of the overall sample size and exposure to placebo, while maximizing the informativeness of the data to support key questions, that is, comparison of PK and PK‐PD relationships between adolescents and adults. Completed analyses indicated similarity in both PK and PK‐PD between age groups, justifying the use of a PK‐exposure matching approach for dose selection in younger age groups. The planned pediatric study included formal M&S analysis steps to confirm the similarity assumptions and the appropriateness of the proposed dose regimen and to allow for dose adjustment before opening recruitment to younger age cohorts in case generated data suggested those assumptions would no longer hold. Additionally, to streamline the design of pediatric studies and facilitate the transition points between age groups, extrapolation activities were planned to confirm the final posology across all age groups.

An apparent shortcoming of the MIDD strategy for ligelizumab in CSU was that it was not used to facilitate an indirect comparison with omalizumab before the initiation of pivotal trials. While the expectation that the higher binding affinity of ligelizumab would translate to superior efficacy was seemingly confirmed by numerical differences observed in the Phase 2 study, nonetheless, omalizumab response in this trial was visibly worse than that in historical trials. It was speculated that the source of this inconsistency were differences in the study populations rather than it being a chance finding and that head‐to‐head comparison in one trial, even if not sufficiently powered, provided more robust evidence than that from model‐based indirect comparison [9]. Based on the totality of available data, a decision was made to proceed to Phase 3 trials. While the difference in the treatment effect between ligelizumab and omalizumab in pivotal studies was smaller than that expected, the confidence intervals of the observed UAS7 responses overlapped between Phase 2 and Phase 3 studies. Conducting model‐based indirect comparison between two compounds before the initiation of Phase 3 studies could have potentially highlighted this risk.

Conclusion

The development of ligelizumab in CSU is an example of how MIDD and the “learn and confirm” paradigm can be applied in the subsequent steps of drug development, spanning from dose selection and support of trial design, through assisting the pediatric drug development and extrapolation, to evidence generation in support of potential labeling, optimizing treatment outcomes, and improving patient access.
