Non-invasive hair metabolome analysis reveals GERD and thyroid dysfunction in pulmonary fibrosis patients
Authors: Bryce Thomas, Dylan A Goodin, Jefferson Baker, Hunter A Miller, Sally Suliman, Hermann B Frieboes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), the most common fibrotic interstitial lung disease (ILD), is characterized by poor (median ∼3 years) survival. While comorbidities such as gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and thyroid dysfunction are associated with increased IPF occurrence, they are frequently underdiagnosed in ILD patients, hampering timely intervention and effective management. Recent advances suggest that the hair metabolome may serve as a non-invasive biomarker for pulmonary fibrosis diagnosis and progression. Building on these findings, our study explores whether comorbidities such as GERD and thyroid dysfunction can similarly be identified through hair metabolomics, potentially enabling earlier detection and personalized treatment approaches in ILD patients.MethodsILD patient hair metabolome data (n = 53) were obtained from a previous study, in which hair specimens collected were processed for metabolite extraction using 2D-LC/MS-MS. The data were used to train a stacked ensemble machine learning workflow tuned via a rigorous combination of cross-validation, feature selection and testing with a hold-out dataset to diagnose GERD or thyroid dysfunction.ResultsPrediction of GERD achieved AUROCTEST = 0.824 and PRAUCTEST = 0.849 with ten metabolites, with the top five being uracil, porphobilinogen, threonylphenylalanine, L-alanyl-L-proline, and valylvaline. Prediction of thyroid dysfunction achieved AUROCTEST = 0.870 and PRAUCTEST = 0.781 using two metabolites: leucylproline and desthiobiotin. Key metabolic pathways complementary to these metabolites and with GERD or thyroid dysfunction were identified.ConclusionComorbidities such as GERD and thyroid dysfunction can be non-invasively evaluated from the hair metabolome of ILD patients. Longer-term, this approach may help to improve IPF treatment strategies.Keywords: Pulmonary fibrosis, Interstitial lung disease, Hair metabolome, Gastric esophageal reflux disease, Thyroid dysfunction, Machine learning

Introduction

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) describes a group of lung alveolar interstitial disorders with varying median survival from 10 years to 2.2 months [1]. About a third of ILD diagnoses are idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), generally associated with severe symptoms and ∼3-year median survival [1]. IPF is characterized by progressive scarring of the lung parenchyma without an identified cause and by having limited treatment options [2,3]. Certain predisposing factors have been strongly correlated with IPF, such as advanced age and a history of smoking [4]. Comorbidities including gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and thyroid dysfunction have recently been causally associated with increased IPF occurrence [[5], [6], [7]]. These comorbidities are often undiagnosed for IPF patients, preventing improved treatment strategies. Part of the challenge is that GERD is notoriously difficult to diagnose [8] while subclinical hypothyroidism remains highly prevalent [9].

Pharmacological treatment of GERD in GERD-positive IPF patients has been shown to reduce IPF-related mortality [10]. It has also been suggested that GERD itself may act as a vector by which fibrosis is developed in the lungs via aspiration of reflux [11]. On the other hand, undiagnosed thyroid dysfunction may critically affect IPF progression and patient outcomes, with hypothyroidism particularly found to be common in IPF patients [12], for which it may be predictive of mortality [12] and also associated with decreased survival [13]. Hypothyroidism in the UK Biobank genome-wide association study data was identified as a potential causal determinant of IPF via Mendelian randomization [14]. Furthermore, increased survival and resolved fibrosis after thyroid hormone exposure in murine models of IPF suggest that thyroid hormone levels may play a role in the progression of some IPF cases [15]. These findings are supported by observations in humans, in which thyroxine replacement therapy reversed the appearance of lung fibrosis [16].

Given that the diagnostic criteria for IPF depend on the elimination of known primary causes for fibrosis development, and that the progression of ILD where the primary pathology is known typically has a more positive course than IPF [1], identification of GERD or thyroid dysfunction in IPF patients may improve survival and potentially help elucidate diease pathophysiology. Beyond IPF, there exist other forms of ILD, such as connective tissue disease-associated interstitial lung disease (CTD-ILD), which constitutes ∼25 % of ILD cases [1]. In CTD-ILD, the development of ILD is associated with an underlying CTD [17], often autoimmune in nature, and mechanisms that result in connective tissue dysfunction may also result in fibrotic tissue development within the lung [18]. Disease progression can often be attenuated by addressing the underlying autoimmune condition using immunosuppressive drugs [19]. However, ILD survival time varies significantly across acute (days; months) and chronic (months; years) CTD, indicating an ongoing need for improved treatment strategies [20]. Like IPF, CTD-ILD is associated with GERD [21], with some CTD also associated with thyroid dysfunction [22], suggesting that early diagnosis of comorbidities may improve patient outcomes. Recent ongoing calls for standardizing ILD diagnosis and disease definitions [23] and frequent (55 %) initial misdiagnosis of ILD patients [24] highlight the need for improved identification of ILD comorbidities.

Prior studies have engaged metabolomics to predict lung cancer progression [25] as well as pulmonary fibrosis disease diagnosis and progression [26]. A metabolomic analysis could enable more efficient screening of ILD patients for a broad range of possible comorbidities by utilizing a single test, potentially translating to decreased idiopathic diagnoses and improved ILD patient outcomes. Furthermore, a single test could be sufficient to screen for a number of comorbidities, decreasing the latency of diagnosis and the time for treatment initiation, and thus lowering the accrual of irreversible lung damage [27].

In this study, GERD and thyroid dysfurnction were diagnosed using a stacked ensemble machine learning workflow to analyze the hair metabolome data from IPF and CTD-ILD patients obtained in Miller et al. [26]. Identification of hair metabolites associated with ILD comorbidities could provide a robust, non-invasive methodology for personalizing treatment strategies, subcategorizing ILD patients, and potentially elucidating ILD pathophysiology. While previous literature has used metabolomics and machine learning in the diagnosis of GERD (e.g., Refs. [28,29]) and classification of thyroid dysfunction (e.g., Refs. [30,31]), identifying such comorbidities from the hair metabolome of ILD patients has to our knowledge not yet been investigated.

Methods

Patient metabolomic data

Metabolomic data from ILD patient hair samples were obtained from a previous study by Miller et al. [26], where sample processing, metabolite extraction, and 2DLC-MS/MS analysis were performed. As previously reported, MSI Level 1 identification was achieved by matching 2DLC-MS/MS data of unlabeled samples to an in-house database with parent ion m/z, MS/MS spectra, and retention time of authentic standards. MSI Level 2 identification for data without a match was performed with Compound Discoverer software v2.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany).

The study by Miller et al. [26] obtained informed consent for all patients to participate, and collected all specimens following approved Internal Review Board protocols (IRB 19.0971 and 20.0404) from patients with a diagnosis of pulmonary fibrosis. The pulmonary data from these same patients were used in this study, with the PFT readings taken from the same visit as when hair samples were obtained. Here, PFTs of patients were categorized into “Mild” and “Severe” by grouping mild/moderate (>60 % of total lung capacity) and severe/very severe (≤60 %) for FVC [32], mild/moderate (>60 %) and moderately severe/severe/very severe (≤60 %) for FEV1 [32], and normal/mild/moderate (>40 %) and severe (≤40 %) for DLCO [33]. Indication of GERD or thyroid dysfunction was based on the patient established medical history. A total of 53 patients with GERD or thyroid dysfunction data were included for analysis, a number considered sufficient for proof-of-concept testing [34] of the hypothesis that GERD or thyroid dysfunction could be detected from the hair metabolome.

Training and test data split

Patient data were divided into training and test subsets using caret package (≥v6.0-94), with a 70/30 split for GERD and thyroid dysfunction separately. To ensure that proportions of diagnosed to undiagnosed patients were maintained across training and test sets, splitting was performed once for each outcome.

Data pre-processing

To prevent information leakage between training and test sets, pre-processing was performed after data splitting. Data pre-processing was done similarly to Ref. [26], in which metabolites were base 10 log-transformed, normalized by MS Total Useful Signal (MSTUS), imputed, and batch corrected. Briefly, signal intensity peak areas were first base 10 log-transformed to attenuate heteroskedasticity between metabolites. After filtering for metabolites with ≤50 % missing data as in Ref. [26], each sample was normalized by MS Total Useful Signal (MSTUS) to control for sample size. This pre-processing approach has been used in prior work for lung [35] and glioblastoma MS metabolomic data [36]. As metabolites with zero value were assumed to be below the detection limit, normalized metabolites were imputed one-by-one with half the metabolite’s minimum signal intensity. To preserve signal intensity data from the first batch in training and test sets, batch correction was performed prior to autoscaling using the ber statistical model with default arguments as described in the dbnormBer function from the dbnorm (v0.2.2) package [37]. Autoscaling was performed to further lessen heteroskedasticity between metabolites [38]. To consistently transform the data across training and test sets, each test set was autoscaled using mean and standard deviations from its corresponding training set. Finally, because statistical analyses were performed across all samples, the dataset used for relative abundance analysis was preprocessed as described above without splitting into training and test sets. Imputation and effects of batch correction during relative abundance dataset pre-processing are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2, respectively.

Base learner machine learning

To evaluate classification of GERD and thyroid dysfunction, feature selection was performed on the training set for each model and outcome using varImp function from the caret package with useModel enabled. Models performed feature selection either using a model-specific (e.g., random forest), or a generic ROC-based methodology. Features were added one-by-one during model training in the order ranked by varImp from n = 2 to 48. Hyperparameter tuning was performed using the training set with a tune length of 10 and Cohen’s kappa as the optimization metric. Within the training set, k-cross fold validation with 10 folds and 15 iterations was performed for a total of 150 repeats. For validation subsets, AUROC metrics and 95 % DeLong confidence intervals were computed using pROC (v1.18.5). Mean PRAUC and maximum F1 were computed using the PRROC (v1.3.1) package with 95 % quantile bootstrapped confidence intervals computed with 10,000 iterations using the boot (v1.3-31) package.

Ensemble machine learning

To improve the overall predictive capacity of the machine learning workflow, an ensemble machine learning approach was utilized. Ensemble methods describe predictive models that are constructed using the predictions of several base learner models as features. In this manner, they allow for the unique strengths of each base learner model to be leveraged, which can lead to a higher predictive accuracy than any single base learner [25,26,39]. In this study, a stacked generalization technique utilizing the approach inspired by the caretEnsemble package (v4.0.1) was employed. Using the base learner models developed for each outcome, the highest performing models were extracted and stacked into a meta-model (i.e., a meta-learner) whose inputs are base learner predictions on validation subsets during training. Only base learners with AUROCTRAIN ≥0.70 were retained for model stacking. Then, utilizing the variable importance ranking described in Equation (1), the ensemble models were trained and validated using forward feature selection from n = 2 to 25 metabolites. ROC and PR curve data for validation subsets were generated using pROC and PRROC packages, respectively, with PRAUC and maximum F1 confidence intervals computed using bootstrapping with 5000 iterations to yield the respective mean values. Model predictions on the test set were done once using the predict function from the stats package (v4.4.2). All plots were generated using ggplot2 (v3.5.1). To compare to the ensemble machine learning approach, fast and frugal trees were trained using the FFTrees package (v2.0.0) using default arguments.

Ranking of most important predictors

To determine feature rankings in the ensemble models, the feature selection method from Ref. [25] was employed that considered the variable importance ranking and classification performance of each of the base learner models from the training set. For each outcome, the weight of each base learner was scaled linearly such that a perfect AUROC of 1 maps to 1 and an AUROC of ≤0.5 maps to 0. Features with equal values from varImp were ascribed the same rank (tied features n and n+1 for each base-learner k are both ranked at min (rn,k,rn+1,k)).

Ensemble feature rank was calculated as a linear weighted sum of ranks of features identified by all base learners included in each meta learner (Equation (1)). For meta learners using ≤10 features for prediction, a combination of training and test set performance metrics were used to isolate strong learners, including bootstrapped PRAUCTRAIN, PRAUCTEST, maximum F1TRAIN, and maximum F1TEST ≥0.70, and AUROCTRAIN and AUROCTEST ≥0.80. The optimal models were selected by isolating the models with the highest AUROCTRAIN, favoring models with fewer features when DeLong confidence intervals were comparable.

Metabolic pathway analysis

To identify pathways potentially associated with GERD or thyroid dysfunction, the names of the top 20 % of metabolites (GERD n = 27; thyroid dysfunction n = 26) identified in ensemble feature selection were inputted into (Metaboanalyst 6.0, RRID: SCR_015539, https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) with default settings [40]. Metabolite lookup was performed using KEGG database (RRID: SCR_012773, www.kegg.jp).

Statistical analyses

Data analyses were performed using versions of R v4.4.2. T-test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, Chi-square test, and Fisher’s exact tests were performed using the tbl_summary function from the gtsummary package (v2.1.0). Spearman correlations and correlation p-values between metabolites and each outcome were computed using the cor. test function from the stats package (v4.4.2). Using the stats package, relative abundance analyses p-values were computed using t-test and Wilcoxon rank sum test for parametric (Shapiro-Wilks [shapiro.test] p > 0.05) and non-parametric data (Shapiro-Wilks p ≤ 0.05), respectively.

Results

Patient population characteristics

Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Mean age was 66 years (IQR: 54–74 years), and overall gender distribution was 36 % male and 64 % female. Patients were 81 % white, 17 % black, and 2 % other. Patients had a history of smoking 40 % of the time, with the remaining 60 % having never smoked. GERD was diagnosed more often (62 %) than thyroid dysfunction (32 %). Most patients were diagnosed with CTD-ILD (n = 30), with the remaining 23 having IPF. While CTD-ILD trended with GERD diagnosis (Chi square p = 0.058; Spearman correlation p = 0.059), no significant associations were found between pulmonary fibrosis and thyroid dysfunction diagnoses. Mild DLCO, FEV1, and FVC PFT readings occurred in 79 %, 61 %, and 53 % of patients, respectively. DLCO, FEV1, and FVC did not significantly vary by either GERD or thyroid dysfunction diagnoses.

Relative abundance analysis

To quantify changes in metabolite signal intensities by GERD or thyroid dysfunction diagnoses, a relative abundance analysis was performed. Two metabolites significantly differed by GERD (Fig. 1a) and 8 metabolites by thyroid dysfunction (Fig. 1b) (p ≤ 0.05). Interestingly, signal intensities were lower with thyroid dysfunction for most significant metabolites. Statistically significant metabolites for GERD diagnosis included porphobilinogen and L-alanyl-L-proline. Significant metabolites for thyroid dysfunction diagnosis included bis(3,5,5-trimethylhexyl) phthalate, 3-[(3-methylbutyl)nitrosoamino]-2-butanone, urocanic acid, 2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-4-piperidinol, cyclohexylamine, N-(4-hydroxy-3-methoxybenzyl)-6-methylheptanamide, 1-methylimidazole-4-acetaldehyde, and desthiobiotin. Marginally significant metabolites identified in relative abundance analysis for GERD and thyroid dysfunction are in Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2, respectively, showing that among metabolites with highest marginal significance, pro-pro trended higher with GERD (p = 0.052) while triethylamine trended lower with thyroid dysfunction (p = 0.052).

Classification of comorbidities

To elucidate the predictive potential of hair metabolites on ILD comorbidities, a stacked ensemble machine learning workflow (Supplementary Fig. 3) was applied to classify patients by GERD and thyroid dysfunction diagnoses. Clinical characteristics for the patients, split into training (n = 38) and test (n = 15) datasets, are summarized in Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Table 4 for GERD and thyroid dysfunction, respectively, showing that patient age, BMI, race, and gender did not significantly differ between training and test sets (p > 0.05). Heatmaps of training and test sets for each diagnosis are in Supplementary Fig. 4.

Performance of base learners is shown in Fig. 2. Eight base learners for GERD (Fig. 2a) and twelve base learners for thyroid dysfunction (Fig. 2b) were retrained for ensemble models, achieving a maximum AUROCTRAIN ≥0.70 across feature subsets. Ensemble models (meta learners) were stratified by maximum validation subset AUROC performance across all feature subsets (Fig. 2c and d), leading to selection of k-nearest neighbors and random forest as the optimal meta learners (see Methods). Performance characteristics for these meta learners across evaluated feature subsets are given in Supplementary Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 5b, highlighting the stability of performance during model training.

Identification of key metabolites

The top 10 metabolites as ranked by ensemble feature selection (Table 2) were used by k-nearest neighbors for prediction of GERD (uracil, porphobilinogen, threonylphenylalanine, L-alanyl-L-proline, valylvaline, 4-{2-[amino(imino)methyl]hydrazino}-4-oxobut-2-enoic acid, 2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-4-piperidinol, val-leu, phe-ile, and BDB), with AUROCTRAIN = 0.928 [95 % CI: 0.923, 0.933], mean PRAUCTRAIN = 0.957 [95 % bootstrapped CI: 0.953, 0.961], maximum F1TRAIN = 0.887 [95 % bootstrapped CI: 0.880, 0.893], AUROCTEST = 0.824, PRAUCTEST = 0.849, and maximum F1TEST = 0.824 (Fig. 2e). The top 2 metabolites (Table 3) were used by random forest to predict thyroid dysfunction (leucylproline and desthiobiotin) with AUROCTRAIN = 0.990 [95 % CI: 0.989, 0.992], mean PRAUCTRAIN = 0.976 [95 % bootstrapped CI: 0.971, 0.981], maximum F1TRAIN = 0.947 [95 % bootstrapped CI: 0.943, 0.954], AUROCTEST = 0.870, PRAUCTEST = 0.781, and maximum F1TEST = 0.727 (Fig. 2f). Altogether, these results indicate that the hair metabolome of ILD patients can be predictive of GERD and thyroid dysfunction.

To compare ensemble model performance to alternative methods, fast and frugal trees – representing simpler decision-tree machine learning methods – were trained on each training set and evaluated on both training and test set data. Balanced accuracy for fast and frugal tree predictions for GERD and thyroid dysfunction decreased from 0.73 to 0.75 in the training set to 0.56 and 0.70 for the test set, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 7), indicating that simpler machine learning approaches may not match the performance of the optimal ensemble model.

Discussion

This study tested the hypothesis that comorbidities in pulmonary fibrosis patients could be detected via analysis of the hair metabolome. A machine learning workflow was employed to determine key metabolites associated with GERD and thyroid dysfunction in patients diagnosed with IPF or CTD-ILD. Optimal ensemble models were determined by high AUROC and PRAUC performance in both training and test sets, indicating model generalizability. A non-invasive evaluation of GERD or thyroid dysfunction could decrease diagnostic delays in this patient population, with the goal to improve quality of life and more effectively treat pulmonary fibrosis. It is envisioned that such evaluations would be conducted in parallel with current standard-of-care, complementing clinical practice.

GERD is a qualitative diagnosis based on patient observations of specific subjective criteria and can manifest from a variety of root causes [41]. As such, the metabolic profiles of GERD patients may vary to a greater degree than one might expect from a more pathway-specific diagnosis [42]. However, it is possible that downstream manifestations of GERD, such as damage incurred on tissues exposed to the reflux, may converge on conserved metabolic pathways that could be identified through metabolomic analysis, such as the activation of nuclear factor-kappa beta (NF-ΚB) in esophageal mucosa in response to injury of the epithelial barrier function resulting from reflux [43].

Ten metabolites were used for prediction of GERD (Table 2). The highest ranked metabolite was uracil, which has previously been identified as a biomarker for spatial pyrimidine metabolism during oral cancer development [44]. The heme precursor porphobilinogen (PBG), ranking second, was significantly higher for patients with GERD by the relative abundance analysis (Fig. 1). Accumulation of PBG has been associated with neurotoxicity, leading longer-term to comorbidities such as liver disease [45]. The dipeptide threonylphenylalanine, consisting of L-threonine and L-phenylalanine, was the third highest-ranking metabolite. While L-threonine is associated with maintenance of intestinal mucosa and barrier function [46], L-phenylalanine is known to stimulate gastric acid secretion [47]. The fourth-ranking metabolite, L-alanyl-L-proline, was also identified as being significantly lower for patients with GERD by the relative abundance analysis (Fig. 1). L-alanine has been pre-clinically shown to be associated with esophagitis [48].

Valylvaline was the fifth highest-ranking metabolite for GERD prediction. Valine has recently been associated with inflammation in the digestive system [49]. The sixth-ranking metabolite was 4-{2-[amino(imino)methyl]hydrazino}-4-oxobut-2-enoic acid, while the seventh was 2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-4-piperidinol, previously identified in human blood [50]. The latter is not a naturally occurring metabolite and would only be found in individuals exposed to this compound or its derivatives. The dipeptide L-valine-L-leucine (val-leu) ranked eight; leucine has been shown to slow (and valine may slow) gastric emptying [51], The dipeptide L-phenylalanyl-L-isoleucine (phe-ile), ranked ninth; phenylalanine is known to stimulate gastric acid secretion [52]. The tenth-highest ranked metabolite was identified as 1,3-benzodioxolylbutanamine (BDB), which is a natural bromophenol isolated from R. confervoides [53], and can be synthesized as an amphetamine-derived designer drug [54]. Based on the mass/charge ratio, this metabolite could also be identified as 2-oxa-4-azatetracyclo [6.3.1.1–6,10∼.0–1,5∼]tridecan-3-one, which has been associated with the digestive system microbiome in cattle [55]. The presence of metabolites reflecting exogenous exposures or uncommon pathways might not reflect biomarkers of disease but rather represent confounding elements from diet, medications, or the environment. Lastly, the pathway analysis identified histidine metabolism with GERD (Table 4). Histamine, derived from enzymatic decarboxylation of histidine, is well known to regulate gastric acid secretion [56].

Only two metabolites were used for prediction of thyroid dysfunction (Table 3). The highest ranked metabolite was the dipeptide leucylproline. Leucine has recently been associated with the inflammatory response and cellular damage in thyroid cells [57]. Interestingly, a panel of amino acids including leucine distinguished patients with papillary thyroid carcinoma and Hashimoto’s thyroiditis from healthy subjects [58]. The second-ranked metabolite was desthiobiotin, a biotin analogue with less protein-binding affinity than biotin. Here, desbiothin was also found to be significantly higher for patients with thyroid dysfunction by the relative abundance analysis (Fig. 1). Tyrosine metabolism was identified as a significant pathway for thyroid disease (Table 5), highlighting the known role of this amino acid in thyroid hormone synthesis. Another significant pathway associated with thyroid disease was phenylalanine metabolism, potentially reflecting the production of tyrosine from phenylalanine. Complementary to these metabolic pathways, phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis was also found to be significant (Table 5). Furthermore, the arginine and proline metabolism may reflect the impact of hyperthyroidism [59]. Other significant pathways were histidine metabolism, known to be affected by thyroid dysregulation [60], and pyrimidine metabolism. Of note, in this study’s cohort there is no specificity as to whether patients were diagnosed as having hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism, or whether their thyroid dysfunction was primary or secondary. However, the majority of patients (85 %) were at least overweight (BMI ≥25) and 55 % were obese (BMI ≥30) (Table 1), which may imply that hypothyroidism, associated with weight gain, may be more commonly represented in this study than other thyroid conditions [61].

This study has several limitations, including a limited sample size from a single institution. In particular, a small test set could yield unstable model performance metric values. Future studies with larger, multi-center cohorts are necessary to validate the findings and ensure model applicability across diverse patient populations. Moreover, GERD and thyroid dysfunction diagnoses were based on established medical history rather than standardized tests, which could introduce potential misclassification bias. In addition to standardized testing, future work should isolate patients by type of thyroid dysfunction to confirm and refine the model predictions. Thyroid dysfunction describing a variety of thyroid-related conditions could result in increased data heterogeneity. Furthermore, ten patients (18.9 %) were diagnosed with both GERD and thyroid dysfunction, which could confound the findings. Follow-on research should consider stratifying patients based on the specific type and severity of each comorbidity to disentangle overlapping metabolic signatures and improve model specificity. The proposed workflow could be extended to diagnosing other comorbidities associated with ILD [5]. Metabolites from hair could be complemented by metabolites from other sources, such as from serum, which has been recently used to identify succinate as a biomarker for predicting the prognosis and diagnosis of IPF [62]. A multi-omic profiling of comorbidities, e.g., evaluating metabolites from hair, serum and urine, could strengthen machine learning model predictive value and potentially offer further insight into the underlying pathophysiology. Model predictions could be refined on additional ILD patient groups, such as interstitial pneumonia with autoimmune features (IPAF), which has a 11-year median survival time [63]. Longer term, a single-sample-collection multiple-disease screening workflow could enable earlier diagnosis of ILD patient comorbidities and and lead to improved outcomes. We anticipate that the non-invasive nature of evaluating the hair metabolome coupled with classification achievable via machine learning could also facilitate the accurate diagnosis of diseases such as GERD and thyroid dysfunction in non-ILD patients.
