Background

Tenosynovial giant cell tumor (TGCT), previously known as pigmented villonodular synovitis, is a benign but locally aggressive proliferative disorder involving the synovium. 2 It can present in either localized or diffused form, and although the knee is the most commonly affected, the hip accounts for roughly 15% of all cases.6,8 TGCT in the hip tends to affect individuals in their second to fifth decade of life and is commonly destructive to the joint.4,8 It is associated with high rates of cartilage damage and is a known risk factor for early total hip arthroplasty. 4 Traditionally, open synovectomy has been the gold standard, but with modern instrumentation and improved access, hip arthroscopy has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative for appropriately selected patients. 8

Case Presentation

The presented patient is a 56-year-old female who came in with a 1-year history of dull, aching left groin pain aggravated by flexion-based activities of the hip, such as prolonged sitting. She reported associated stiffness of the hip and had failed an intra-articular corticosteroid injection as well as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) medication.

On physical examination, she demonstrated 0° of extension, 100° of flexion, and 20° of both internal and external rotation of the hip. She demonstrated pain with provocative subspine and flexion, adduction, and internal rotation maneuvers. She had preserved strength and an intact distal neurovascular examination.

Preoperative Imaging

Preoperative radiographs demonstrated preserved joint space, a lateral center edge angle of 32.1°, a Tönnis angle of 7.9°, and an alpha angle of 64.4°, consistent with CAM-type femoracetabular impingement. Advanced imaging with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated intermediate signal irregularities surrounding the femoral neck with lobulated and frond-like synovial thickening, most consistent with the appearance of TGCT, with other differential diagnoses including synovial chondromatosis, pannus from inflammatory arthropathy, lipoma arborescens, or other soft-tissue malignancy such as synovial sarcoma.

Indications

Indications for arthroscopic management of TGCT include strong clinical and radiologic suspicion or confirmed diagnosis through interventional radiology (IR) biopsy. Other indications include the absence of arthritis, lesion location that is both accessible and resectable, and availability of a trained hip arthroscopist, as additional portals and extended access may be required.

Technique Description

The patient is positioned supine on a traction table with either postless or posted distraction, with external anatomical landmarks marked out prior to procedure start. Adequate distraction is confirmed with fluoroscopy. Portals are then created in standard fashion. The anterolateral portal is established first using a spinal needle under direct fluoroscopy, ensuring an appropriate trajectory to avoid iatrogenic chondral damage. A modified midanterior portal is then created approximately 4 cm medial and 2 cm distal to the anterolateral portal under direct arthroscopic visualization, seen here being introduced into the arthroscopic triangle.

An interportal capsulotomy is created sharply between the 2 portals. A traction stitch is then placed with the acetabular leaflet utilizing a suture passing device in order to improve visibility of the central compartment. After completion of any central compartment work, a T-capsulotomy is created by adding a vertical limb at the mid-portion of the previously established interportal capsulotomy. Additional traction sutures are placed through the lateral and medial leaflets to elevate the capsule and maintain exposure. A motorized shaver is then used to carefully debride synovitis and loose capsular tissue in order to improve visualization. Care is taken not to over-resect unnecessary capsular tissue.

After the T-capsulotomy is created and the limbs are elevated, the initial lesion can be visualized here, with its classic hemosiderin-laden synovitis appearance. An arthroscopic grasper is introduced to mobilize the initial portion of the lesion. The mass is carefully grasped, rotated, and separated from surrounding attachments, taking care to avoid damage to the adjacent articular cartilage and labrum. This phase requires controlled instrument handling and deliberate extraction to ensure that the lesion is safely and completely removed.

With the initially visible lesion removed, attention is redirected to address the femoral head-neck junction. Using a high-speed bur, the CAM deformity is resected in a systematic fashion to restore a smooth, anatomic concavity to the head-neck offset and eliminate the bony prominence responsible for impingement. The degree of resection is performed according to preoperative planning. Throughout the resection, fluoroscopy and dynamic assessment are used to confirm adequate correction while avoiding over-resection.

After using electrocautery and shaving to improve visualization, a large, multilobulated lesion is now clearly visualized extending along the femoral neck. The TGCT lesion is then mobilized and grabbed with an arthroscopic grasper to initiate removal. Because these lesions are often multilobular and can have firm attachments, a pituitary rongeur can also be used for improved control. The lesion is removed in a deliberate, sequential manner to avoid leaving behind residual nodules that could contribute to recurrence. As the bulk of the tumor is delivered, the working space opens, allowing complete assessment of the remaining synovial tissue.

Following bulk excision of the lesion, a systematic synovectomy is performed using a shaver to remove any residual tissue along the synovial lining. Electrocautery is also used as needed to control any bleeding and to facilitate visualization in areas where the lesion was previously adherent. The combination of appropriate visualization, judicious shaver use, and targeted electrocautery helps ensure thorough lesion clearance while minimizing thermal injury or unnecessary capsule disruption.

To restore stability and minimize postoperative microinstability, it is the author's preference to close the capsule. Simple sutures are placed to close the vertical limb of the T-capsulotomy first before reapproximating and closing the interportal limb.

Results

Postoperative Results

Pathology results confirmed localized-type tenosynovial giant cell tumor, with an overall mass size of 3.5 × 3 × 2 cm. Postoperative radiographs are shown here, demonstrating resection of the CAM-type lesion.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

Our rehabilitation protocol follows a phase progression. 3 During weeks 1 through 6, the patient remains flat foot partial weightbearing initially, with gradual advancement to weightbearing as tolerated. Range of motion is protected utilizing an abduction brace, particularly during the early phases. Phase 2 is weeks 6 through 12 and involves initiating strengthening and gait training. Functional training and return to activities is initiated during weeks 12 through 16 of phase 3. Finally, phase 4 is week 16 and beyond, and includes return to sport protocols.

Technical Pearls

There are several key technical considerations. First, adequate exposure is critical and can be optimized through sufficient traction, careful debridement, and use of a T-type capsulotomy with traction sutures when necessary. Accessory portals, such as a posterolateral portal, may be required for circumferential access, particularly when the synovial proliferation extends inferiorly or posteriorly. Finally, surgeons should maintain a low threshold for recommending a combined or open approach when the disease extends into areas that are not safely reachable arthroscopically.

Discussion

Multiple studies support arthroscopic management of TGCT of the hip. Byrd et al 1 demonstrated significant improvements in the Harris Hip Score, with only 1 conversion to arthroplasty at a minimum 2-year follow-up. Nazal et al 5 similarly reported durable outcomes at 3 years with low recurrence rates. And more recently, in a series of 9 patients, Tang et al 7 showed excellent postoperative patient reported outcomes with no evidence of recurrence on advanced imaging and no need for secondary surgery. Collectively, these data highlight that arthroscopic synovectomy can both be effective and durable when the disease is appropriately localized.
