Gut bacterial dysbiosis in pediatric severe malaria associates with post-discharge mortality
Authors: Olivia J Bednarski, Sawyer B Lehman, David Mzinza, Caroline Kazinga, Ruth Namazzi, Robert O Opoka, Jie Ren, Tuan M Tran, Terrie E Taylor, Karl B Seydel, Chandy C John, Andrea L Conroy, Nathan W Schmidt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGut microbiota have been implicated in severe malaria in murine models, but their contribution to the pathogenesis of severe malaria in children is unknown. Here we show through analysis of gut bacteria in stool samples from two separate African studies enrolling children with severe malaria, and children from local communities, that children with severe malaria have gut bacteria dysbiosis. Among children with severe malaria, there is increased abundance of Enterobacteriaceae that associates with multiple clinical complications of severe malaria. Moreover, increased abundance of Escherichia coli was a predictor of post-discharge mortality. Metagenome analysis identify elevated metabolic pathways and genes supporting the utilization of host-derived molecules in children with severe malaria that have the potential to promote the survival and growth of Enterobacteriaceae. Treatments that target Enterobacteriaceae may have the potential to reduce post-discharge mortality in children with severe malaria.Subject terms: Microbiome, Parasitology

Introduction

Despite advancements in prevention and treatment, severe malaria (SM) continues to be a major global health burden. In 2023, there were 263 million cases resulting in 597,000 deaths with the majority being children <5 years old in sub-Saharan Africa1. These numbers do not include the additional mortality post-discharge, particularly in children with severe malaria anemia (SMA). A recent meta-review revealed there is a greater than 70% increased risk of death in children with SMA in sub-Saharan Africa post-discharge compared to during hospitalization2, with mortality rates reported between 13% to 21% between 2 months to 18 months post-discharge3–5. The pathophysiology of SM in humans is not fully understood6, nor are the underlying mechanisms for post-discharge deaths. Recent findings in mice demonstrate the gut microbiota contributes to the pathogenesis of SM7–15, but the contribution of the gut microbiota to SM in African children is largely unknown.

Mechanistic mouse studies have established that gut microbiota causes susceptibility to SM in mice by dampening humoral immunity to Plasmodium11,12. One study found that Bacteroides species, combined with other bacteria, cause an increased susceptibility to SM in mice14. Additionally, a greater abundance of Bacteroides associated with SMA in Ugandan children compared to those with asymptomatic Plasmodium infections14. Recent work using pre-transmission season stool samples in Malian children demonstrated different bacterial species in the gut of children susceptible to uncomplicated febrile malaria and higher parasitemia in mice with fecal transplants of the same stool16. Interactions between Plasmodium and the intestines have also been observed in mice, where Plasmodium infection results in the accumulation of infected erythrocytes in intestinal microvessels that correlate with inflammatory infiltrates, increased intestinal permeability, and detection of bacteria in blood17–21.

Autopsies of children who died of malaria revealed a high abundance of sequestered infected erythrocytes in intestinal microvessels22,23. Moreover, biomarkers of intestinal injury are elevated during SM and are associated with increased risk of death in children24,25. In African children, malaria has long been known to increase the risk of invasive enteric bacterial infections, particularly Gram-negative bacteria26–30, and one report found that more than half of all bacteremia cases in malaria-endemic areas can be attributed to malaria27.

While most gut commensal bacteria are obligate anaerobes, Enterobacteriaceae are Gram-negative facultative anaerobes, which provides a competitive growth advantage by switching between aerobic respiration, anerobic respiration, or fermentation for energy depending on the environment31–33. Intestinal inflammation and oral antibiotics promote the expansion of pathobionts like facultative-anaerobe Enterobacteriaceae by increasing luminal oxygen and altering luminal nutrients31,34–36. Enterobacteriaceae, including Escherichia coli and non-typhoidal Salmonella, expand in abundance in the intestines of Plasmodium-infected mice17. Furthermore, changes in intestinal immunity and bone marrow hematopoiesis during Plasmodium infection in mice, suppress anti-bacterial immunity resulting in increased susceptibility to invasive Salmonella infection17,37. Together, these data from murine and human studies suggest gut bacteria may lead to susceptibility to, and outcomes associated with, SM in children. However, it remains unknown if gut microbiota contributes to the pathogenesis of acute SM and post-discharge mortality in African children. Here we show gut bacteria dysbiosis, including increase relative abundance of Enterobacteriaceae, associates with SM in African children and that increased abundance of E. coli is a risk factor for post-discharge mortality.

Results

Children with SM have gut bacterial dysbiosis compared to community children

To assess the role of the gut microbiota in children with severe Plasmodium falciparum malaria, we analyzed stool samples collected as part of two separate clinical cohort studies in Africa, one in Uganda and one in Malawi (Supplementary Fig. 1). For the Ugandan study, participants aged 6 months to 4 years old who met the WHO definition for SM were enrolled from two tertiary hospitals along with healthy community children (CC) from the same household or compound area (Supplementary Fig. 1, demographics found in Supplementary Data 1)38. For the Malawian study, participants aged 6 months to 12 years were recruited if they met the WHO definition of cerebral malaria along with CC that sustained asymptomatic P. falciparum infection for 30–60 days (Supplementary Fig. 1, demographics found in Supplementary Data 1). Gut microbiota were characterized from the DNA of stool samples from the Ugandan children at the species level with 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing using MVRSION39 (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Alpha diversity, measured by Shannon index and observed number of species to determine richness and evenness, were decreased in children with SM compared to CC (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. 2D). Beta diversity, assessed by Aitchison’s distance to determine composition similarities, was significantly different between the SM and CC groups at the species-level when controlling for enrollment site, age, receipt of prior antibiotics, and sample collection day (PERMANOVA, p = 0.001; Fig. 1B, Supplementary Data 2).

To validate and extend the findings from MVRSION species-level identification, metagenome sequencing was conducted to enhanced species-level identification, with two taxonomic classifiers, and for assessment of biochemical potential. We assessed a subset of Ugandan samples by metagenomic sequencing that were selected to reduce microbiome cofounders for SM, as well as CC that were PCR positive for P. falciparum (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Consistent with the entire cohort, alpha diversity was significantly lower (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. 2E). Beta diversity was also significantly different when controlling for enrollment site, age, receipt of prior antibiotic, and sample collection day (PERMANOVA, p = 0.001; Fig. 1D, Supplementary Data 2). Changes in bacterial composition between SM and CC groups were further validated with metagenomic sequencing of the separate Malawian cohort (Supplementary Fig. 2C) with significantly lower alpha diversity (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Fig. 2F) and significantly different beta diversity, controlled for age, sex, and prior antibiotic use (PERMANOVA, p = 0.001; Fig. 1F, Supplementary Data 2).

To identify the bacteria responsible for differences in alpha and beta diversity, beta-binomial differential abundance (DA) analysis was completed. DA analysis of 16S rRNA sequencing at the family level demonstrated Ugandan children with SM have increased abundance of six bacterial families including Enterobacteriaceae, Bacteroidaceae, and Enterococcaceae, and six families decreased, including Bifidobacteriaceae, Prevotellaceae, and Clostridiaceae (Fig. 1G, Supplementary Data 3). DA analysis of metagenome sequencing of both cohorts revealed similar findings with increased abundance of Enterobacteriaceae as one of the topmost significant taxa (Fig. 1G, Supplementary Data 4, 5). At the genus level of the Uganda cohort 16S sequencing, there was increased abundance of 10 genera by DA analysis while children with SM had a decrease in 10 genera (Fig. 1H, Supplementary Data 6). The Malawi cohort genus analysis identified a higher number of significant genera, and demonstrated a similar pattern of increased Escherichia, Shigella, Klebsiella, Enterococcus, and Bacteroides with the notable addition of Morganella and Staphylococcus in children with SM compared to CC (Supplementary Fig. 2G, Supplementary Data 7). Species-level analysis with the five most significant species-based q-value between children with SM and CC are shown for both cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 3A–C, Supplementary Data 8–10). Escherichia coli and Bacteroides species were found to be in a high relative abundance across the cohorts for children with SM, with Prevotella and Blautia species across the cohorts for CCs. Collectively, these analyses reveal substantial differences in bacteria communities with a significant higher abundance of facultative pathobionts and lower abundance of typical anaerobes between children with SM and healthy children from the community, demonstrating dysbiosis in children with SM in countries across the African continent.

Plasmodium infection alone minimally alters gut bacteria composition

Differences in bacteria communities between CC and children with SM could be attributed to P. falciparum infection-induced dysbiosis, as mice infected with rodent Plasmodium species exhibit changes in gut bacteria communities8–10. Therefore, we evaluated whether P. falciparum infection itself could induce dysbiosis. Two beneficial attributes of the Ugandan study provide an opportunity to address this possibility. First, CC were screened by PCR for P. falciparum, which demonstrated a subset of CC had asymptomatic P. falciparum parasitemia (PfPos). Of the PfPos children, 16 provided stool samples. Of the CC that were PCR negative for P. falciparum (PfNeg), 54 provided stool samples. Second, stool samples were collected after 12-months in a subset of children, which affords longitudinal assessment of bacteria communities between samples collected at enrollment and 12-months later (n = 4 for PfPos, n = 10 for PfNeg). Assessment of alpha and beta diversity between asymptomatic PfPos and PfNeg CC at enrollment demonstrated no significant differences (Fig. 2A–C).

To further test if P. falciparum infection induces gut bacteria dysbiosis, we conducted beta binomial DA analysis comparing asymptomatic PfPos and PfNeg CC. There were few differences (Supplementary Fig. 4A, Supplementary Data 11), with only the expansion of Enterococcus and decrease in Streptococcus in asymptomatic PfPos CC aligning with the differences between children with SM and CC (Fig. 1F). Further supporting that P. falciparum can lead to an expansion of Enterococcus, this was the only genera significantly lower, with no significant increases, in asymptomatic PfPos CC stool samples at month 12 compared to enrollment samples (Supplementary Fig. 4B, Supplementary Data 12).

These results suggest the observed differences in gut bacteria populations in stool samples provided at enrollment between SM and CC children are likely attributed to disease severity rather than solely to P. falciparum infection. To test this hypothesis, we compared gut bacteria populations over follow-up to evaluate whether bacteria populations normalized towards the CC distribution. A subset of children with SM (n = 35) provided stool samples ~1-month after enrollment instead of during hospitalization. Additionally, a subset of children initially enrolled with SM (n = 109) and CC (n = 14) provided stool samples at 12 months post-enrollment. Assessment of the enrollment, month 1, and month 12 samples demonstrate that baseline gut bacteria composition is largely resolved by one month (Fig. 2D). Comparing SM samples collected at month 1 to CC enrollment samples demonstrate no significant elevations in the previously identified dysbiotic bacteria (Fig. 2D; Supplementary Data 13). Overall, DA analysis of stool collected at 12-month follow-up was comparable between children with SM and CC except for a slight increase in abundance of Anaerostipes in SM survivors (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Data 14). Further, assessing the gut microbiota composition from children with SM at month 12 compared to enrollment demonstrate significant reduction in the relative abundance of taxa that were elevated at enrollment with a concomitant increase in relative abundance of many Firmicutes within the children with SM comparing the enrollment to 12-month sample (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Fig. 4C, Supplementary Data 15). Together, these data suggest SM could lead to gut bacterial dysbiosis.

Multiple factors are associated with gut bacteria dysbiosis during SM

Various factors could contribute to SM-associated gut bacteria dysbiosis including oral antibiotic use40, eating patterns41, host immune responses42–44, and changes in intestine luminal metabolites45. The Ugandan cohort was used for these analyses owing to the detailed collection of environmental and laboratory data. The medical structure of Uganda allows parents to obtain antibiotics for their children at the local pharmacy or lower acuity health centers without the requirement of a doctor-dispensed prescription, which can lead to antibiotic misuse. Antibiotics can increase oxygen tension within the gut lumen providing Enterobacteriaceae a competitive advantage in an aerobic environment46. Within children with SM from Uganda, 45% reported receiving antibiotics for their illness before enrollment, and 61% of those antibiotics were given orally (Supplementary Data 16). Prior oral antibiotic use was associated with an increased abundance of Escherichia and Lactobacillus (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Data 17). Differences in gut bacteria populations were consistent by both 16S and metagenome analyses (Fig. 1E) despite the metagenome analysis excluding participants who reported pre-enrollment any antibiotic use.

Intravenous antibiotics also have the potential to change gut bacteria composition, including intravenous ceftriaxone, which can increase the relative abundance of Enterococcus species47. Enterococcus and Bacteroides can have intrinsic resistance to ceftriaxone48,49 and there are high rates of ceftriaxone-resistant Escherichia and Klebsiella in malaria-endemic countries50,51. A large percentage of Ugandan children with SM (n = 293; 70%), and 100% of Malawian children with SM, received ceftriaxone use during inpatient care. Therefore, we assessed the association between gut bacterial relative abundance and ceftriaxone use during inpatient care, in the Uganda cohort, and found the relative abundance of Enterococcus, Parabacteroides and Escherichia was significantly associated with ceftriaxone (Supplementary Fig. 5A, Supplementary Data 18). To assess if ceftriaxone impacted our initial analysis on all children with SM, we compared CC vs children with SM without ceftriaxone treatment and CC vs SM with ceftriaxone at the family level and found similar findings in both comparisons to our initial analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5B, Supplementary Data 19, 20). Genus-level analysis for the SM group without ceftriaxone had less significant negatively associated Firmicutes but demonstrated a similar pattern of higher Escherichia, Klebsiella, Bacteroides, and Enterococcus relative abundances compared to CC (Supplementary Fig. 5C, Supplementary Data 21). These data suggest that prior and inpatient antibiotic use in children with SM could contribute to bacterial dysbiosis, but that other factors likely also contribute.

Therefore, we looked at additional factors that may contribute to expansion of the identified bacteria. Enterobacteriaceae52, Bacteroides53, and Enterococcus54 can survive in nutrient scarce environments and many children with SM have reduced oral food intake. Using hours since the last meal, children with SM with greatest number of hours since eating had an expansion of Parabacteroides and Enterococcus (Fig. 3, Supplementary Data 22).

Inflammation can provide nitrate and oxygen, which can select for Enterobacteriaceae expansion55. Recruitment of neutrophils and increased nitric oxide from sequestered parasites or the presence of pathogenic bacteria in the intestines will produce reactive oxygen and nitrogen species that confers Enterobacteriaceae survival and growth advantage through respiration via various electron acceptors56. The absolute count of neutrophils from peripheral blood was used as a proxy for neutrophil activity in the colon. As the absolute number of neutrophils increased, so did the abundance of Enterococcus, Bacteroides, Klebsiella, and Flavonifracter (Fig. 3, Supplementary Data 23). Higher peripheral white blood cell counts were also associated with a greater abundance of these same bacteria except for Bacteroides (Fig. 3, Supplementary Data 24).

As previously reported, the obligate destruction of host erythrocytes, as part of the parasite lifecycle, in addition to uninfected erythrocyte destruction, can lead to extensive hemolysis in SM57. Intravascular hemolysis increases the risk of oxidative damage from iron radicals58. Children with SM had increased cell-free hemoglobin and oxidized heme, hemin, the iron containing element of hemoglobin, and the levels correlated with increased abundance of Escherichia, Lactobacillus, and Enterococcus (Fig. 3, Supplementary Data 25, 26). Escherichia and Enterococcus require iron for growth and pathogenicity and can work in a consortium to obtain iron59,60, and previous studies in iron overload syndromes confirm gut microbiota changes61.

To counteract the cytotoxic effects of heme, the host increases expression of heme oxygengase-1 (HO-1)62. Induction of HO-1 and hemolysis has been shown to inhibit bactericidal reactive oxygen species, promoting non-typhoid Salmonella bacteremia in mice infected with Plasmodium37,63. Consistent with these findings, increased plasma HO-1 levels in children with SM correlated with an increase in the abundance of Escherichia and Bacteroides (Fig. 3, Supplementary Data 27). Malaria-associated hemolysis, with release of parasite-driven accumulated hypoxanthine, may lead to hyperuricemia, particularly in the context of acute kidney injury64. We previously reported an increase of Enterobacteriaceae in children with SM and hyperuricemia64. About one-third of blood uric acid is excreted into the intestines and uric acid was previously described to be a growth medium for Enterobacteriaceae65. Consistently, we observed an elevation of Escherichia, Shigella, Erysipelatoclostridium, Bacteroides, and Enterococcus as uric acid levels increase (Fig. 3, Supplementary Data 28). Collectively, this data shows that in SM antibiotic use, fasting, inflammatory cells, hemolysis, HO-1, and hyperuricemia may create an environment and nutrient niche for expansion of Enterobacteriaceae, Enterococcus, and Bacteroides.

Gut bacteria in children with SM have the genomic machinery for Enterobacteriaceae to thrive in an inflamed gut through metabolic and alternative respiration pathways

To better understand the potential functional differences in bacteria in the context of SM, we performed metagenomic profiling with MetaPhlAn4 followed by HUMAnN366 alignment for metabolic pathways and gene families, then compared children with SM vs asymptomatic P. falciparum parasitemia by linear model with MaAsLin267 for both cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 2B). There were 220 total pathways positively associated with SM with 46 unique to Uganda, 19 unique to Malawi and 155 shared between the two cohorts (Fig. 4A). There were 143 pathways negatively associated with SM between the two cohorts with 5 shared. 138 pathways were unique to Malawi, and 0 unique to Uganda, likely due to the fact that Malawi had a larger sample size (Fig. 4A). Based on prior murine gut dysbiosis studies31,34,36,55,68, we hypothesized bacteria in children with SM would have more pathways to support aerobic respiration and responses to inflammation. Indeed, the significantly elevated pathways in the Ugandan (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Data 29) and the Malawian cohorts (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Data 30) could support survival in oxygenated environments, nutrient scarcity, and enhance pathogenicity. The pathways in both cohorts associated with asymptomatic parasitemia demonstrate more general metabolism such as polyamine synthesis, pyrimidine biosynthesis, and glycolysis (Fig. 4B, C). We also sought to assess gene families in both cohorts. The top 5 significant gene families associated with SM included adenosine deaminase and galactosidase while the top 5 asymptomatic parasitemia associated gene families included Firmicutes proteins and RNA polymerases (Supplementary Fig. 6A, B). These data demonstrate there is a large difference in biochemical functional potential between children with SM compared to asymptomatic P. falciparum parasitemia.

Our prior work in mice has demonstrated the presence of specific gut bacteria confer susceptibility to SM rather than resistance12,14; therefore, we further investigated pathways positively associated with SM. The positively associated pathways were categorized for Uganda cohort (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Data 29) and Malawi cohort (Fig. 4E, Supplementary Data 30), with similar numbers. Notably, bacteria in children with SM in both cohorts had elevated pathways for aerobic respiration including terminal electron receptor biosynthesis (ubiquinol and menaquinol biosynthesis), lipid oxidation, TCA cycle, and formate formation. Metabolic pathways included oxidized sugar metabolism, ethanolamine utilization, fucose, and sialic acid degradation. There were also iron related pathways, like heme and aerobactin formation, with further indications of pathogenicity with polymyxin resistance and interestingly glyphosate breakdown, which may implicate different environmental exposures in children that developed SM. Purine breakdown pathways like deoxyribonucleosides degradation pathway and allantoin (byproduct of uric acid degradation) degradation to glyoxylate, support the hypothesis of gut bacteria degradation of intestinal excreted uric acid during hyperuricemia. In the gene family analysis, iron scavenging siderophore pores and genes that support oxidant states (i.e., formate dehydrogenase-N (FDN), ubiquinone biosynthesis protein (Ubiv), superoxide dismutase [Mn]) were also identified in both cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 6). Pathogens, particularly Enterobacteriaceae, are known to use these genes and pathways to survive and outcompete other bacteria, enhance virulence, and alter host immunity60,68–78.

These results support the hypothesis that bacteria in children with SM use iron and degrade purines for growth and survival. As summarized in Fig. 4F, hemolysis during SM can lead to excess uric acid and heme. Bacteria from children with SM had elevated pathways related to siderophore uptake and heme synthesis as well as purine degradation which could account for uric acid breakdown. Glycine a byproduct of uric acid degradation that, when coupled with the glycine cleavage system (GCS) can lead to the byproduct of formate as well as additional purines77,79. Hexitol formation can also contribute to formate formation which was significantly associated with SM increased in both cohorts. Increased formate levels could support respiration as a main electron acceptor during oxygen respiration by Enterobacteriaceae FDN68. Glycine can also contribute to heme b formation in these bacteria further supporting growth as well as function as an alternative electron acceptor during anaerobic respiration60,73,80. Together, these data suggest several metabolic potential pathways, including aerobic/alternate respiration, that may allow for the successful expansion of Enterobacteriaceae in the context of SM-induced changes in the intestinal environment.

The abundance of gut bacteria from the Enterobacteriaceae family were associated with multiple SM complications

We next investigated if increased relative abundance of bacteria in children with SM was associated with complications of SM. The Ugandan cohort was used for these analyses owing to the detailed collection of clinical and laboratory data and 12-month follow-up. SMA was associated with a greater relative abundance of Bacteroides, Klebsiella, and Erysipelatoclostridium (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Data 31). Coma was associated with a greater relative abundance Escherichia, Enterococcus, Shigella, and Lactobacillus (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Data 32). Excess lactic acid, which is possibly produced by the parasite, tissue hypoxia81, or transferred from the gut as microbial metabolism products, was associated with a greater relative abundance Escherichia, Erysipelatoclostridium, Klebsiella, and Bacteroides, while deep acidotic breathing (physical sign of acidosis and a prognostic sign of worse outcomes for children with SM82) was associated with a greater relative abundance Escherichia, Enterococcus, and Lactobacillus (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Data 33, 34). This is of particular interest, as another report found that plasma acids during SM are host and microbial derived83. Increased abundance of Escherichia, Enterococcus, and Lactobacillus were also associated with acute kidney injury (AKI) (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Data 35).

Knowing that changes in eating habits and luminal nutrients may induce bacterial degradation of the intestinal mucus layer84, that parasite sequestration can effect gut epithelial permeability22,23,25, and that intestinal injury markers are elevated in children that died from SM24, we assessed if bacteria were associated with intestinal damage85. These analyses revealed that Escherichia and Bacteroides associated with intestinal mucosal injury marker trefoil factor 3 levels (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Data 36). In contrast, Holdemanella bifomis, which have been shown to help regulate gut barrier integrity86, was associated with decreased intestinal injury (Fig. 5A).

SM has been shown to increase the susceptibility of children to secondary bacterial infections, most commonly pathogenic members of the Enterobacteriaceae family26–30,87. Given the association between abundance of Enterobacteriaceae and intestinal injury, and AKI and lactic acidosis that overlap with sepsis complications17,37,63,88 (Fig. 5A), we investigated if the observed expansion of Enterobacteriaceae in children with SM is associated with translocation and bacteremia. Of the 22 blood samples from children with SM from the entire cohort that grew pathogens on a blood culture, over 50% of the isolated bacteria belong to the Enterobacteriaceae family (Fig. 5B). Of note, over 34% of children with SM received antibiotics prior to enrollment in the study, which could have decreased detection of bacteremia among children in this cohort.

Given the increased abundance of pathogenic Enterobacteriaceae and prolonged immune suppression following malaria89,90, we assessed hospital admissions over the 12-month follow-up period for the entire cohort (SM = 600, CC = 120). Significantly more children with SM (56%) had a post-discharge/enrollment all-cause hospital admission than CC (31%) (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Figure 7A). When evaluating the etiology of post-discharge/enrollment hospital admissions, children with SM had almost a four-fold increase in sepsis-associated admissions (22%) compared to CC (6.7%) (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Figure 7A). We also observed that nearly twice as many children with SM (49%) returned to the hospital with SM requiring hospital admission compared to CC (25%) (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Figure 7A). The proportions of children with SM and CC that returned for an outpatient malaria visit were similar (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Figure 7A). To determine if children with asymptomatic P. falciparum parasitemia were also at greater risk of severe illness throughout the next 12 months, we conducted a similar analysis within the CC based on the presence of P. falciparum. There was no difference in hospitalization for any reason or presumed sepsis between CC based on the presence of P. falciparum (Fig. 5D, Supplementary Figure 7B). These data suggest that SM increases the chances of returning to the hospital for any reason, including sepsis and recurrent SM.

Gut Enterobacteriaceae abundance is associated with post-discharge death

In this cohort, there was 11.2% all cause-mortality from enrollment through the 12-month follow-up, and 5.3% in those that provided stool (Supplementary Fig. 8A). We conducted mortality analysis using DA at the family level between children with SM who died (n = 22; inpatient, n = 6; post-discharge, n = 16) or survived (n = 395) during admission or over 12-month follow-up that provided stool samples (Supplementary Fig. 8B). This analysis identified Enterobacteriaceae as the only family associated with death during admission or post-discharge follow-up (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Data 37). Logistic regression analysis for risk of death from admission to over the 1-year follow-up period showed that genera from the Enterobacteriaceae family (Shigella, Escherichia, and Enterobacter) as well as Bacteroides were associated with overall death (Fig. 6B, Supplementary Figure 7D).

There may be different etiologies for inpatient6,91 compared to post-discharge92 death, so we also assessed them independently. Stool samples were obtained from only 6 of 44 children with SM (Supplementary Fig. 8A) in the cohort who died during initial hospitalization. Consistent with combined in-patient and post-discharge death, the relative abundance of Enterobacteriaceae trended higher in children with SM that died during hospitalization but was not significantly increased, potentially attributed to the small sample size and missing stool samples in children that died during hospitalization (Supplementary Figs. 8A,B, 9A). Using the entire cohort of Ugandan children with SM (n = 600) to assess for potential complications associated with acute death (during initial admission), we identified coma, intestinal injury, deep acidotic breathing, AKI, and hyperuricemia to be highly associated with inpatient death, as previously reported24,38,91 (Supplementary Fig. 9B). We then assessed if relative abundance of Enterobacteriaceae among children with SM was associated with these complications. In children with SM, coma, AKI, intestinal injury, deep acidotic breathing, and elevated uric acid were all associated with a greater abundance of Enterobacteriaceae (Supplementary Fig. 9C). These data indicate that the risk factors for acute death correlate with a greater relative abundance of Enterobacteriaceae.

We further assessed the relationships between clinical complications at enrollment with post-discharge death using survival analysis over 12 months (n = 23 died, n = 533 survived; Supplementary Fig. 8A). SMA, AKI, deep acidotic breathing, and hyperuricemia were significantly related with post-discharge death (Supplementary Fig. 10A). Consistent with previous studies93, the most significant relationship with post-discharge death was SMA (p = 0.0005) (Supplementary Fig. 10A), which was associated with increased Enterobacteriaceae abundance (Fig. 6C). Additionally, relative abundance of Enterobacteriaceae was higher in children that died post-discharge (n = 16 died, n = 395 survived for stool samples) (Fig. 6D). Kaplan–Meier survival curves comparing children with SM who had ≥ median log transformed relative abundance of Enterobacteriaceae to those with < median values show that children with ≥ median values were more likely to die post-discharge (p = 0.01) (Fig. 6E). To better understand the effect of Enterobacteriaceae abundance on post-discharge death, we conducted Cox regression analysis94. There was a significant relationship with post-discharge death and ≥ median log transformed relative abundance of Enterobacteriaceae (Supplementary Fig. 11A), with a 4.30-fold increased hazards of death. This relationship remains significant even after adding known contributors to post-discharge death including SMA, AKI, hyperuricemia, and sickle cell disease (Supplementary Fig. 11B–E). In summary, Enterobacteriaceae abundance may be an independent predictor of post-discharge mortality in this cohort.

Both SMA and hyperuricemia were associated with increased Enterobacteriaceae abundance in children with SM, and alterations in the gut microbiota have also been associated in murine models of hyperuricemia95, preterm anemic infants96, and in adults with iron overload from thalassemia61. Additionally, a recent study identified children with SMA as a top risk factor of death six months post-discharge in malaria endemic areas2. Therefore, we next assessed which gut bacteria were associated with post-discharge mortality in children with hemoglobin < 7 g/dL (defined by the WHO as severe anemia for children 6–59 months)97. With this definition, rather than the previous hemoglobin < 5 g/dL, 15 out of the 16 children that died and provided stool samples were included in the analysis (n = 15 died, n = 240 survived) (Supplementary Fig. 8B). DA analysis at the species-level indicates E. coli as the only species with increased abundance associated with mortality (Supplementary Fig. 12A, Supplementary Data 38). The forest plot shows increased natural log transformed relative abundance E. coli in the children that died with a 1.42-fold increased hazards of death (Supplementary Fig. 12B). These data suggest that the increased risk of post-discharge death in children with SMA is associated with a greater intestinal abundance of Enterobacteriaceae.

Among the samples that were collected at the 1-month timepoint, two were from children who died after that follow-up appointment (n = 2 died, n = 33 survived, Supplementary Fig. 8C). Plotting the Enterobacteriaceae abundance shows a significant increase in relative Enterobacteriaceae abundance in these two children (Fig. 6F). The genus-level barplots show ~50% relative abundance of the gut bacteria were Escherichia, Shigella, and Bacteroides in these two children (Fig. 6G). Plotting the abundances show significant increases for these genera in the two children that died as does DA analysis (Fig. 6H, Supplementary Data 39). Collectively, these data suggest that increased relative abundance of Enterobacteriaceae is an independent contributor to all-cause post-discharge mortality, and that prolonged increases in the abundance of Enterobacteriaceae, in particular E. coli, and Bacteroides, may put children with SM at a higher risk of dying post-discharge.

Discussion

Collectively, our data from pediatric SM cohort studies from Uganda and Malawi demonstrate that SM is associated with gut bacterial dysbiosis. Importantly, survival analysis of post-discharge deaths in the Uganda cohort suggest gut Enterobacteriaceae is an independent predictor of post-discharge mortality, particularly in children with SMA. The association between malaria and susceptibility to bacteremia in children has long been described, though the mechanisms remain elusive. Here, we report that SM may be facilitating intestinal dysbiosis by several mechanisms that promote an increase in the relative abundance of pathogenic Enterobacteriaceae species which may be capable of translocating from the intestine to the blood to increase of risk severe disease and death (Fig. 7).

The Enterobacteriaceae family contains many pathogens capable of epithelial attachment and intestinal injury98. We report elevated markers of intravascular hemolysis and HO-1, a known inhibitor of neutrophils during hemolysis37 and biomarker of sepsis-induced AKI99, are associated with Escherichia. This may explain the association between Enterobacteriaceae and SMA, as hemolytic anemia increases susceptibility to bacteremia through impaired immune response, intestinal injury, and increased free iron100. Furthermore, specific Enterobacteriaceae can produce Shiga toxin, exacerbating hemolysis, endothelial activation, and thrombocytopenia in addition to kidney damage98. Previous reports identified Salmonella as the primary Enterobacteriaceae species responsible for secondary bacterial infections in children with SM, while others demonstrate that Escherichia and Klebsiella are more common101,102. There may also be less exposure to Salmonella in these cohorts as the probability of occurrence of invasive non-typhoidal Salmonella has decreased from 0.65 to 0.23 in Uganda over the past 20 years103. Additionally, Bacteroides can break down the intestinal mucus layer to release nutrients, such as sialic acid, that Escherichia can use for growth, and fucose that increases expression of virulence factors in pathogenic bacteria which may explain the increased abundance of both genera53,84,104,105. In addition to these factors, receipt of ceftriaxone could also be contributing to the increased abundance of Enterobacteriaceae family members in children with SM, owing to the increased prevalence of ceftriaxone-resistant Enterobacteriaceae51. Additionally, given the intrinsic resistance of enterococci to cephalosporins, ceftriaxone could also contribute to the increased relative abundance of Enterococcus among both Ugandan and Malawian children with SM. Although ceftriaxone can be beneficial as empiric treatment for a vast majority of invasive bacterial infections in these children, it could also be contributing to the underlying dysbiosis of potentially pathogenic bacteria.

The contribution of Enterobacteriaceae towards in-patient and post-discharge deaths is likely complex and multi-factorial. Respiratory distress, defined by deep acidotic breathing, during SM is associated with in-patient death91 and is caused by underlying metabolic acidosis106. Enterobacteriaceae could produce acidic molecules that enter blood circulation and contribute to respiratory distress, as plasma acids during SM have been shown to be of microbial origin83. Enterobacteriaceae could also contribute to in-patient mortality through intestinal damage through release of microbial PAMPs that contribute to systemic inflammation and end organ damage, including the kidney. Consistent with this hypothesis, sTREM-1, which is upregulated in response to LPS and other microbial products107, was elevated in children with SM108. Enterobacteriaceae can also contribute towards in-patient deaths through translocation and subsequent bacteremia. Indeed, over 50% of positive blood cultures from children with SM were Enterobacteriaceae. Similarly, Enterobacteriaceae could contribute to post-discharge deaths as we have shown that 30% of children with SM will return with presumed sepsis, similar to prior studies27, which is consistent with sustained increases in Enterobacteriaceae one-month post-SM in two children that subsequently died.

Recent studies have shown that post-discharge malaria chemoprevention can significantly reduce post-discharge readmission or death in children in malaria endemic areas with SM, most of whom also have malaria2. However, the effects of the chemoprevention on readmission or death last only through the period of treatment. Additional interventions to further decrease post-discharge mortality in SM remain a high priority. If the findings of the present study are validated in longitudinal studies that confirm the contribution of gut dysbiosis to SM pathogenesis, they open the possibility of interventions during SM to prevent children from dying after discharge. Patients with inflammatory bowel diseases have higher levels of Enterobacteriaceae and 5-amino sialic acid, an approved therapy for ulcerative colitis, helps maintain an anerobic environment through stimulating PPARγ-induced beta oxidation in colonocytes109. Therefore, 5-amino sialic acid could potentially serve as an adjunct therapy to reduce Gram-negative facultative anaerobe abundances and their contributions to SM pathogenesis. Treatments to reduce intestinal barrier breakdown like citrulline, metformin, or quercetin could help to prevent acute or post-discharge microbial translocation110,111. These measures might reduce in-hospital mortality, though they would need to have rapid action, since most children with SM die in the first 24 h of admission (22/44 (50%) of inpatient deaths occurred on the day of admission in this study) and may, therefore, function better as post-discharge therapies. Oral antibiotic treatment targeting Enterobacteriaceae might also be given during hospitalization and post-discharge to prevent mortality, though such treatment would also run the risk of selecting for resistant bacteria. Targeting Enterobacteriaceae, luminal nutrients, and gut barrier integrity could prevent translocation of gut bacteria and, therefore, its contribution to SM outcomes.

Our associative findings in two separate cohorts of African children strongly suggest that the gut microbiota contributes to SM pathogenesis in children through the expansion of Enterobacteriaceae and the subsequent contribution of these bacteria to SM complications. Our analysis suggests that expansion of pathobionts may be a critical driver of readmission for sepsis syndromes and post-discharge deaths. Metagenome sequencing and pathway analyses outline known genetic potential for pathogens to thrive in an inflamed gut. Further analyses could identify strain-level pathogens, identify antimicrobial resistance genes, tease out fungal or viral contributions, as well as measure bacterial toxins to further explain the contribution of gut dysbiosis to SM pathogenesis. If our findings are validated with further studies, several gut microbiota-based treatments therapies that reduce aerobic respiration, improve gut barrier integrity, improve urinary excretion of uric acid, and oral antibiotics that target Enterobacteriaceae may improve outcomes for children with SM.

Methods

Study population

Uganda

Between 2014 and 2017, 600 children with SM were enrolled in the study designed to assess cognition in children < 5 years of age at 12 months follow-up as described previously38. Children were eligible if they: (1) were between 6 months and 4 years of age; (2) had diagnostic evidence of malaria with either a positive rapid diagnostic test for Plasmodium falciparum histidine-rich protein-2 or direct visualization of parasites by Giemsa microscopy; (3) required hospitalization; and (4) had 1 or more of the following WHO SM criteria: coma (Blantyre coma score < 3), respiratory distress (deep acidotic breathing or lower chest wall retractions), multiple seizures (≥ 2 generalized seizures in 24 h or a seizure > 30 min in duration), severe anemia (hemoglobin < 5 g/dL), or prostration (≥ 1 year, unable to sit unsupported or stand; < 1 year, unable to breastfeed). Children were recruited at 2 referral hospitals in Central and Eastern Uganda: Mulago National Referral Hospital in Kampala and Jinja Regional Referral Hospital in Jinja. Exclusion criteria included the following: known chronic illness requiring medical care, history of coma, head trauma, known developmental delay, cerebral palsy, or prior hospitalization for malnutrition. Delayed exclusion criteria included an elevated cerebrospinal fluid white blood cell count in a child with coma.

Community children (120 total children, with 70 providing stool samples) were enrolled as controls recruited from the nuclear family, extended relatives, and nearby households and were commonly enrolled during the one-month follow-up appointments for the children that had SM. Exclusion criteria included no illness requiring medical care within the previous four weeks and no findings of neurological abnormalities on physical exam in addition to the other exclusion criteria previously stated. Malaria was evaluated in CC by either rapid diagnostic test for Plasmodium falciparum histidine-rich protein-2 or direct visualization of parasites by Giemsa microscopy followed by nested PCR targeting the 18S rRNA gene of Plasmodium spp., followed by amplification of Plasmodium falciparum using species-specific primers and visualization of amplicons though agarose gel electrophoresis, as previously described112, from genomic DNA isolation from whole blood using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Among enrolled participants, 487 children provided stool samples at enrollment, and 35 provided stool samples at the month 1 follow-up. 123 children provided stool samples at the 12-month follow-up appointment. Enrollment stool samples from children with SM were collected between admission and discharge (Supplementary Fig. 1). For the post-discharge mortality analysis of children with severe malaria, 411 provided stool samples at enrollment and 16 children died after discharge from the hospital. Of the 35 children that provided stool samples at the month 1 follow-up, 2 children died within the year.

Malawi

Between 2021 and 2024, 142 children between the ages of 6 months and 12 years were recruited at the Pediatric Research Ward (PRW) at Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital in Blantyre, Malawi into a study investigating the pathogenesis of cerebral malaria. Children were eligible if they met the WHO clinical case definition of cerebral malaria (Blantyre Coma Score of ≤ 2, peripheral parasitemia on thick smear, and no other discernable cause of coma). Exclusion criteria included known developmental delay and chronic illness requiring previous hospitalization.

Asymptomatic children were recruited from neighboring villages chosen from areas with recent CM admissions. Healthy children from the community were asked to donate a blood sample on a filter paper. These dried blood spots were subsequently analyzed for the presence of P. falciparum by qPCR detecting the 18S rRNA gene113. Participants with positive qPCR results were asked to travel to the PRW four weeks after the first encounter to donate a blood and stool sample. Exclusion criteria included any febrile illness between the encounters as well as the use of anti-malarial medication for two weeks prior to the initial visit.

Clinical care and participant follow-up

Uganda

Clinical care was supervised by study pediatricians as previously described38. On enrollment, a medical history and physical examination were recorded. Children were managed according to the Uganda National Guidelines for the treatment of SM and all complications were cared for per standard-of-care. Fluids were administered intravenously as clinically indicated based on the degree of hypovolemia with fluid administered as a slow infusion over 1 h unless a bolus was indicated (shock with fluid loss). Children requiring blood transfusion were given packed red cells at a dose of 10 mL/kg over 4 h or whole blood at 20 mL/kg if packed red blood cells were not available. Indications for blood transfusion included a hemoglobin ≤ 5 g/dL or < 6 g/dL with signs of respiratory distress. Malaria was treated with intravenous artesunate at a dose of 2.4 mg/kg if ≥ 20-kg body weight or 3 mg/kg for children < 20 kg at 0, 12, and 24 h after enrollment followed by once-daily intravenous artesunate until the child was able tolerate oral artemether-lumefantrine. Participants with suspected concomitant bacteriemia received intravenous antibiotics unless blood culture results were negative, or the patient improved clinically. All children in coma received intravenous ceftriaxone at 100 mg/kg until the cerebrospinal fluid analysis results revealed no laboratory evidence of meningitis. A pediatric nephrologist was consulted for children with AKI for symptomatic management and referral for dialysis as indicated. When the study was conducted, hemodialysis was available as a fee-based service on adult wards on a limited basis because of availability. None of the children received dialysis.

Malawi

Clinical care for children with CM has been previously described114. On enrollment, a medical history and physical examination were recorded. Malaria was treated with intravenous artesunate at a dose of 2.4 mg/kg if ≥ 20-kg body weight or 3 mg/kg for children < 20 kg at 0, 12, and 24 h after enrollment at which point treatment was switched to oral artemether-lumefantrine. A nasogastric tube was placed if the patient was unable to tolerate oral meds at this point. All children received intravenous ceftriaxone at 100 mg/kg. Children requiring blood transfusion were given packed red cells at a dose of 10 mL/kg over 4 h or whole blood at 20 mL/kg if packed red blood cells were not available. Indications for blood transfusion included a hemoglobin ≤ 5 g/dL or < 6 g/dL with signs of respiratory distress. All children underwent EEG analysis. Children with overt seizures as well as those in non-convulsive status epilepticus were treated with an increasingly aggressive anticonvulsant regimen including diazepam, phenobarbitone, levetiracetam, and ketamine.

Assessment of clinical complications

Features of SM were defined according to the World Health Organization SM criteria115. Acute kidney injury (AKI) was defined using the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) global consensus criteria using serum creatinine alone as urine output monitoring was not routinely conducted for all participants. AKI was defined as a 1.5-fold increase in serum creatinine over estimated baseline116. AKI staging was as follows: stage 1, 1.5- to 1.9-fold increase in creatinine over baseline; stage 2, 2.0- to 2.9-fold increase over baseline; and stage 3, ≥ 3.0-fold increase over baseline116. Baseline creatinine was estimated using a validated height-independent approach117. Additional complications assessed included hyperuricemia (uric acid > 7.0 mg/dL), lactic acidosis (lactate > 5.0 mmol/L), intestinal injury (trefoil factor 3 > 4.1 ng/mL), and Kussmaul breathing (physical exam finding of deep acidotic breaths). Sepsis was defined clinically as fever above 38°C with evidence of dysfunction of at least one organ system (i.e. AKI, cardiac failure, etc.) with negative blood smears for malaria or persistent fever despite antimalarial medication.

Clinical laboratory assessments

Peripheral blood smears were used to quantify parasite density using Giemsa staining using the participants white blood cell count. Malaria parasite biomass was assessed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay quantifying plasma histidine-rich protein 2 levels (Malaria Ag Cellabs, Brookvale, Australia). All children had a complete blood count at enrollment. Lithium heparin whole blood was used for an i-STAT handheld blood analyzer to assess a basic metabolic panel and blood gas values (Abbott Point of Care Inc., Princeton, New Jersey). Lactate and glucose levels were assessed using point-of-care analyzers (Accutrend Plus, Roche Diagnostics). Creatinine was assessed using a Beckman Coulter AU5822 chemistry analyzer using the modified Jaffe colorimetric method (Beckman Coulter, Brea, California), which is traceable to an isotope dilution mass spectrometry reference method using the US National Institute of Standards and Technology Standard Reference material SRM967. To assess for bacteremia, 1–3 mL of whole blood was inoculated into pediatric blood culture bottles (Peds Plus/F), which were incubated in an automatic BACTEC 9050 Blood culture system for up to 5 days. Positive samples were Gram stained and subcultured on blood agar, chocolate agar, or MacConkey agar plates. Human immunodeficiency virus serology was performed using antibody rapid diagnostic kits (Determine Alere, STAT-PAK, and Uni-Gold) using standard diagnostic criteria, with all seropositive samples in children <18 months and indeterminate samples in children of any age confirmed by polymerase chain reaction (Abbott Laboratories Ltd.). Plasma TFF3 and sTREM-1 levels were measured in Uganda using custom MagPix Luminex panel. Uric acid levels were tested from serum and tested at the Indiana University Pathological clinical laboratory using the Timed Endpoint Diazo Method.

Stool sample processing

Uganda

Enrollment stool samples from children with SM were collected between admission and discharge and stored at −80°C (Supplementary Fig. 1). CC provided stool samples in the outpatient clinic (within the hospital compound) on the day of the initial enrollment. Stool samples were processed with the same methodology for both SM and CC. Full stool samples were collected, typically by the parent, into sterilized collection cups. Part of sample was place into a barcode labeled cryovial by the laboratory staff and transferred to a −80°C freezer. Note that samples collected at the Jinja site were stored in a −20°C freezer for up to two weeks prior to being transferred to a −80°C freezer. Stool samples were also collected after 12 months during a follow-up visit to the enrollment site. These samples were processed similarly. At the completion of the study, the samples were shipped to Indiana University School of Medicine on dry ice and stored at −80°C until 2021 when the DNA was extracted. DNA from ~100 mg of stool was extracted from frozen partial stool samples using QIAmp PowerFecal DNA kits (Qiagen) specific protocol and DNA was measured using Qubit 3 Fluorometer (Invitrogen).

Malawi

Enrollment stool samples from children with SM were collected between admission and discharge and stored at −80°C (Supplementary Fig. 1). CC provided stool samples in the outpatient clinic (within the hospital compound) on the day of the initial enrollment. Stool samples were processed with the same methodology for both SM and CC. Total DNA was extracted from stool samples using the QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (Qiagen), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, ~250 g of stool was aliquoted into the provided bead-beating tubes containing garnet beads. To enhance microbial cell lysis, 800 µl of lysis buffer was added, and the samples were homogenized using a TissueLyser II bead beater (Qiagen) for 2–3 min at 30 f/s. After centrifugation to pellet debris, the supernatant was transferred and processed through a series of inhibitor and DNA binding steps using silica membrane spin columns. The DNA was eluted in 50 µl of elution buffer and the concentration and purity were assessed using the Qubit 4 fluorometer (Invitrogen). The DNA was stored at −20°C until transfer to Indiana University School of Medicine on dry ice and stored at −80°C prior to sequencing.

16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing

The DNA library preparation and sequencing was completed at Washington University in St. Louis using the Multiple 16S Variable Region Species-level Identification (MVRSION) approach, as described39, using HiSeq4000. Briefly, all nine hypervariable regions (V1–V9) of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified using 14 primer pairs via high-throughput microfluidic PCR to enhance species-level taxonomic identification. The MVRSION algorithm processed the resulting short-read amplicon sequences by mapping them to a curated full-length 16S rRNA gene reference database derived from SILVA. A list of candidate species was generated for species with amplicon reads mapping to at least four variable regions. For each candidate species, “discriminatory variable regions”—those with sequences most unique to the species—were identified. The amplicon reads were then re-aligned to the candidate species list, and species was considered present if the requisite number of reads mapped aligned to the candidate species discriminatory variables regions. Relative abundance was estimated based on the proportion of reads mapping to a species relative to the total number of reads mapping to all species. The final output was a feature table containing species-level taxa and their relative abundances constructed by the Genome Technology Access Center, Washington University, Saint Louis. The feature table was further analyzed by importing into R and converting into a phyloseq118 object that was assessed using linear or logistic modeling, beta-binomial analysis through the package corncob119, and visualized using MicroViz120. Scatter plots were made in GraphPad Prism (v10.2.1) from natural log-transformed relative abundance of the bacteria.

Whole metagenomic sequencing

Uganda

WGS samples from the Ugandan cohort were selected to reduce microbiome cofounders (no prior antibiotic use, age > 2 years old, no severe malnutrition, no HIV) and then randomly subsetted for a final count of 39 samples. DNA from the initial extraction was tested for purity and sent to Indiana University Center for Medical Genomics for whole genome shotgun metagenomics sequencing and library preparation on NovaSeq 6000 with 150 bp paired-end sequencing. Quality control and host sequence removal was performed using KneadData (v0.12.0). Genome assembly was created using Kraken2 (v2.1.3)121 followed by Bracken (v2.5.1)122 with pre-built standard Kraken2 database. Confidence score was increased to 0.10. Genome assembly was also conducted with Metaphlan4 (v4.0.6)123 using a 1.01 M prokaryotic reference and metagenome-assembled genomes.

Malawi

All samples from the Malawian cohort were sent for WGS. DNA from the initial extraction was tested for purity and sent to Indiana University Center for Medical Genomics for whole genome shotgun metagenomics sequencing and library preparation on NovaSeq 6000 with 150 bp paired-end sequencing. Quality control and host sequence removal was performed using KneadData (v0.12.0). Genome assembly was created using Kraken2 (v2.1.3)121 followed by Bracken (v2.9)122 with pre-built standard Kraken2 database. Confidence score was increased to 0.10. Genome assembly was also conducted with Metaphlan4(v4.1.1)123 using a 1.01 M prokaryotic reference and metagenome-assembled genomes.

Alpha diversity

Diversity assessed through richness/observed species was calculated summing the number of species from the feature table for each sample, and Shannon index was calculated with the equation (index=−∑i−1Spi*ln(pi), where S = species count and pi is the proportion of S found in the ith species) to estimate both richness and evenness. Comparisons between children with SM and CC were completed with a non-parametric two-sided Mann–Whitney test in GraphPad Prism (v10.2.1).

Beta diversity

Beta Diversity was calculated using Aitchison’s distance with a center log ratio transformation124 with a minimum prevalence of 1/100. PERMANOVA testing was completed to assesses for differences while controlling for child age, sex, enrollment site, the day of stool collection (if reported), and a history of prior antibiotics.

Differential abundance analysis

Beta binomial modeling using the corncob package through MicroViz was used to assess covariates against gut composition119,120, which is inherently two sided. A minimum of 10% prevalence in samples was required. Corncob package controls the false discovery rate to account for multiple comparisons119. Taxonomic tree produced via MicroViz was used to represent the results120. Prevalence was shown through the size of the branch and a transparent circle indicates a p value < 0.05. Output of beta binomial model was also used to create the heatmap in Figs. 3 and 5. Represented color was based on t statistic. See Supplementary Data 3–15 & 17–39 for full statistical output.

Biochemical potential analysis

The Humann3 (v3.6)66 pipeline was used to profile the pathways and gene families from the metagenomic sequencing data. Briefly, the forward and reverse reads were combined into a single file after Kneaddata and output of Metaphlan4 was used for taxonomic profiling. Pangenome profiling was completed with UniRef90 for gene families and MetaCyc for pathways. Remaining unmapped reads are matched to a protein database with DIAMOND (v2.0.15). Output gene families and pathways were combined and renormalized to “copies per million” without the removal of final remaining unmatched reads. Output tables were loaded into R. Unmapped and unmatched pathways were removed and the output underwent wrench normalization125. MaAsLin267 was used for differential abundance analysis between children with SM vs PfPos CC as the fixed effects. The default log transformation was done with the linear model, and no additional normalization was done beyond the Wrench normalization. Significant pathways were only considered if q < 0.05 (multiple corrections applied) for pathways and if q < 0.01 and −1 < coefficient < 1 for the gene families of both cohorts. Category of pathways were hand curated based MetaCyc descriptions and are found in Supplementary Data 25, 26.

Remaining statistical analysis

Demographic tables were analyzed by two-sided Fisher’s exact test, two-sided Wilcoxon rank sum exact test, or one-sided Pearson’s Chi-squared test using the package gtsummary (v2.0.0)126. Two-sided Fisher’s test was performed for proportional data representing return visits for comparison between CCs and SM as well as PfPos versus PfNeg using the package gtsummary (v2.0.0)126. Individually assessed logistic regressions using continuous compositionally natural log transformed bacterial abundance was visualized with ggplot2 (v.3.5.1)127 packages in R displayed as forest plots between genera/species and death. Univariate logistic regression was used to assess associations of clinical complications and in-patient mortality using the package gtsummary (v2.0.0)126. Enterobacteriaceae abundance were presented as median with interquartile range and analyzed using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test using GraphPad Prism. Survival analysis of post-discharge deaths was visualized with Kaplan–Meier curves and assessed with log-rank analysis in R using the survival package in R128,129. Cox regression analysis was used to assess the relationship between median relative abundance of Enterobacteriaceae abundance from children with SM and time-to-death after hospital discharge in the following year using the survival package in R128,129. Linearity and proportional hazards assumptions were assessed by inspection of standard diagnostic plots. This relationship was also assessed when including clinical complications (severe anemia, hyperuricemia, acute kidney injury, and hemoglobin genotype), and all global assumptions were met. For logistic regression models, diagnostic plots confirmed that the models met assumptions and were not driven by influential data points. Supplementary Data tables were produced with packages gt130 and gtsummary126.

All study procedures and the schedule of sample collection was outlined in consent forms and consent was obtained to store samples.
