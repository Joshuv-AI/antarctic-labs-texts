Combined sequence and copy number analysis improves diagnosis of limb girdle and other myopathies
Authors: Babi R. R. Nallamilli, Yinghong Pan, Lisa Sniderman King, Lakshmanan Jagannathan, Vinish Ramachander, Ann Lucas, Jan Markind, Raffaella Colzani, Madhuri Hegde
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveClinical and genetic heterogeneities make diagnosis of limb‐girdle muscular dystrophy (LGMD) and other overlapping disorders of muscle weakness complicated and expensive. We aimed to develop a comprehensive next generation sequence‐based multi‐gene panel (“The Lantern Focused Neuromuscular Panel”) to detect both sequence variants and copy number variants in one assay.MethodsPatients with clinical diagnosis of LGMD or other overlapping muscular dystrophies in the United States were tested by PerkinElmer Genomics in 2018–2021 via “The Lantern Project,” a sponsored diagnostic testing program. Sixty‐six genes related to LGMD subtypes‐ and other myopathies were investigated. Main outcomes were diagnostic yield, gene‐variant spectrum, and LGMD subtypes' prevalence.ResultsMolecular diagnosis was established in 19.6% (1266) of 6473 cases. Major genes contributing to LGMD were identified including CAPN3 (5.4%, 68), DYSF (4.0%, 51), GAA (3.7%, 47), ANO5 (3.6%, 45), and FKRP (2.7%, 34). Genes of other overlapping MD subtypes identified included PABPN1 (10.5%, 133), VCP (2.2%, 28), MYOT (1.2% 15), LDB3 (1.0%, 13), COL6A1 (1.5%, 19), FLNC (1.1%, 14), and DNAJB6 (0.8%, 10). Different sizes of copy number variants including single exon, multi‐exon, and whole genes were identified in 7.5% (95) cases in genes including DMD, EMD, CAPN3, ANO5, SGCG, COL6A2, DOK7, and LAMA2.Interpretation “The Lantern Focused Neuromuscular Panel” enables identification of LGMD subtypes and other myopathies with overlapping clinical features. Prevalence of some MD subtypes was higher than previously reported. Widespread deployment of this comprehensive NGS panel has the potential to ensure early, accurate diagnosis as well as re‐define MD epidemiology.

Introduction

Muscular dystrophies (MDs) are a clinically and genetically heterogeneous group of inherited disorders caused by pathogenic variants in several genes. 1 MDs encompass a wide range of disorders characterized by progressive degeneration and weakness of muscles, which impose difficulties with physical activity and walking, and can be a heavy burden on affected individuals and their families. 1 , 2 Limb‐girdle MDs (LGMDs) are a subset of MDs predominantly involving proximal muscle weakness with an autosomal recessive or dominant mode of inheritance. 1 More than 30 LGMD types are linked to specific gene loci, 3 , 4 manifesting in broadly overlapping and heterogeneous phenotypes. 3 , 5 , 6 , 7

Comprehensive guidelines to help neurologists in providing clinical diagnosis of LGMDs have been published. 8 Since clinical evaluation alone may not be sufficient to accurately diagnose a specific LGMD subtype, other testing may be needed. 9 , 10 Muscle biopsy studies with immunohistochemistry and immunoblotting help in identifying a few MD subtypes. Most neurologists, however, prefer molecular testing rather than an invasive muscle biopsy to identify underlying specific MDs. While some genes are well characterized, 1 , 11 the genotype–phenotype correlation of other rare MDs still gives a challenge when providing an accurate clinical diagnosis and prognosis.

While definitive molecular diagnosis helps to pinpoint specific LGMD subtypes, delays in obtaining definitive molecular diagnosis occur due to the time and cost burden of the multiple tests required to cover the complex genetic variant spectrum. Traditional molecular testing strategies include initial Sanger sequencing, next‐generation sequencing (NGS), and follow‐up copy number variation (CNV) analysis by microarray and additional reflex testing depending on the suspected MD subtype. Around 40% of patients with MD remain without a definitive molecular diagnosis, 12 therefore missing out on targeted medical management.

Studies of NGS for molecular analysis of LGMDs and other overlapping MDs 1 , 11 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 include 180 and 35 patients in China and Korea, respectively, 17 , 20 a targeted NGS panel study in 58 German patients with genetically unclassified LGMDs, 15 and a whole exome sequencing study in 60 Australian families with a clinical diagnosis of LGMDs. 14 These studies were limited by small sample size and lack of CNV analysis. Much larger genetic studies of patients with suspected MDs are needed to understand better the rare forms of MDs.

As part of a sponsored‐testing program designed to overcome financial barriers to diagnosis, we developed a comprehensive NGS‐based multi‐gene panel, “The Lantern Focused Neuromuscular Panel,” to detect sequence variants and CNVs in one assay. Use of this panel has subsequently allowed for an expanded understanding of the genetic spectrum underlying LGMDs and other overlapping MDs. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first large‐scale report of diagnostic MD panel testing using a single NGS assay to detect all variant types. Specifically, our assay includes 66 genes associated with LGMDs and other overlapping MDs and comprises sequence variant detection as well as CNV analysis. In a cohort of 6473 patients, we identified genetic variants in different genes related to MDs and demonstrated how integrating CNV analysis into routine NGS‐based panel testing is instrumental in identifying rare LGMDs and other overlapping MD subtypes.

Materials and Methods

Patient enrollment

Testing for the Sanofi‐sponsored “Lantern Focused Neuromuscular Panel” was performed at PerkinElmer Genomics, which is certified by the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments and College of American Pathologists (CLIA‐CAP). The project encompassed data collected from 6473 patients who underwent molecular testing from October 2018 to December 2021. Informed consent, a routine clinical testing procedure at PerkinElmer Genomics, was obtained from each patient. The consent encompasses testing as well as collecting and using patients' clinical data for research or publication. The criterion to access sponsored testing is clinical suspicion of LGMD or other overlapping MD based on clinical features and symptoms.

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted using a PerkinElmer Chemagic DNA CS200 DNA Extraction Kit with a Chemagic™ 360 (PerkinElmer) instrument. DNA quality was checked with a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and quantified using PicoGreen reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a Wallac 1420 Victor™ 2D microplate reader (PerkinElmer). 21

Focused neuromuscular panel sequencing

Multi‐gene NGS panel testing was performed with 105 genes related to LGMDs and other overlapping myopathies from October 2018 to March 2019. The panel size was reduced to 99 genes from April 2019 to January 2020. Following suggestions from neurologists, the panel size was reduced further to 66 genes from February 2020 to December 2021 to focus on the subtypes of LGMDs and to reduce the burden of variants of uncertain significance (VUS). A constant set of 64 genes was shared amongst all iterations. The genes included in the panels are listed in Table S1. Here, we report all identified cases with pathogenic variants in all genes included in the panel testing except for the GAA gene. All identified GAA variants diagnosed through “The Lantern Project” are reported in a separate article. 22

A custom Agilent SureSelect targeted sequence capture was used to enrich all genes included in the panel, followed by NGS on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 with 150‐base‐pair paired‐end reads. A base was considered to have sufficient coverage at 20X, and an exon was considered fully covered if all coding bases plus three nucleotides of flanking sequence on either side were covered at 20X or more. Alignment to the human reference genome (hg19) was performed, and annotated variants were identified in the targeted region. Sequence variants reviewed had a minimum coverage of 8X and an alternate allele frequency of 20% or higher. This assay could not detect variants in regions of the exome that were not covered, such as deep intronic, promoter and enhancer regions, and areas containing large numbers of tandem repeats. Primary data analysis was performed using an Illumina bcl2fastq converter v2.19. Secondary analysis was performed using an Illumina DRAGEN Bio‐IT Platform v.3.4.12. Tertiary data analysis was performed using an SNPEff v5.0 and PerkinElmer's internal ODIN v.1.01 software.

Sequence variant analysis using NGS panel data

Sequence variants were assessed by PerkinElmer Genomics' proprietary analysis and interpretation pipeline, Ordered Data Interpretation Network (ODIN). ODIN aggregates and displays sequence variants data for each patient tested. The variants were annotated as per Human Genome Variation Society nomenclature. Variants were evaluated by their frequency, as reported in public databases (gnomAD, dbSNP, EVS, and Leiden LOVD). Variants having a frequency greater than expected given the prevalence of disease were considered benign. Variant interpretations and classifications were performed using American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) standards and guidelines for the interpretation of sequence variants. 23 Curated known deep intronic variants were tagged in the ODIN database using the ACMG interpretation guidelines. The variants were automatically detected and flagged in ODIN for review if identified by sequencing. Other deep intronic variants were not analyzed.

Copy number variation (CNV) analysis using NGS panel data

CNV was assessed using Biodiscovery's NxClinical software (BioDiscovery, El Segundo, CA). Male and female reference sets were created from healthy donors using NGS data with the help of a BAM Multiscale Reference Builder module. CNVs were detected using the Hidden Markov Model‐based fast adaptive states segmentation technique algorithm. 21 CNV analysis was designed to detect deletions and duplications of three exons or more; in some instances, due to the size of the exons or other factors, not all CNVs were analyzed.

Results

Patients

Of the 6473 patients referred to the clinical molecular diagnostic laboratory between 2018 and 2021 with a clinical suspicion of LGMD or other overlapping MDs, 5357 (82.8%) patients enrolled in the project were aged 18 years or older and 1116 (17.2%) were aged less than 18 years (Fig. 1A). The average age of patients during participation was 44 years old. The cohort included 2823 (43.6%) females, 3520 (54.4%) males, and 130 (2.0%) patients with unreported sex. Most frequent ethnicity categories included 3718 (57.4%) Caucasian, 635 (9.8%) Hispanic, 609 (9.4%) African American, 190 (2.9%) Asian, and 78 (1.2%) Middle Eastern (Fig. 1B). Pathogenic variants were identified in 1266 (19.6%) patients (Fig. 2). This diagnostic yield was calculated based on patients harboring pathogenic variants in autosomal recessive, autosomal dominant, and X‐linked genes, as well as patients with one pathogenic variant and one VUS in the same gene. The prevalence of each identified LGMD and other MD subtypes identified by “The Lantern Focused Neuromuscular Panel” was established (Fig. 2). The majority of patients have pathogenic variants in one of the following: PABPN1, 10.5% (133/1266); DMD, 9.2% (116/1266); CAPN3, 5.4% (68/1266); DYSF, 4.0% (51/1266); GAA, 3.7% (47/1266); ANO5, 3.6% (45/1266); FKRP, 2.7% (34/1266); and PYGM, 2.6% (33/1266) (Tables S2–S5). Rare autosomal dominant MD subtypes identified by “The Lantern Focused Neuromuscular Panel” are listed in Table 1. Forty patients with two pathogenic variants and seven with one pathogenic variant and one VUS in the GAA gene were identified in the project; these are reported separately along with patients' clinical features and biochemical results of GAA enzyme testing. 22

PABPN1: Encoding poly(A) binding protein nuclear 1

Oculopharyngeal MD (OPMD), an autosomal dominant or autosomal recessive disorder associated with GCN repeat expansion in exon 1 of PABPN1, is characterized by ptosis, dysphagia, and selective involvement of the muscles of the eyelids and pharynx, with an onset usually in the fourth to sixth decade of life. Normal alleles have 10 GCN repeats encoding 10 alanines. Autosomal dominant alleles have 12 to 17 GCN repeats, and autosomal recessive alleles have 11 GCN repeats. 24 Autosomal recessive forms of OPMD have later age of onset along with milder clinical features. PABPN1 is included in “The Lantern Focused Neuromuscular Panel.”

Definitive molecular diagnosis was established in 133 of 1266 (10.5%) patients by identifying the pathogenic GCN repeat allele in PABPN1 using NGS‐based panel testing (Fig. 3, Table S2). Carrier status was also established in 35 patients by identifying the heterozygous 11 GCN repeat allele.

Expansion of a GCN trinucleotide repeat in exon 1 of the PABPN1 gene is associated with OPMD. It is inherited in either an autosomal dominant or an autosomal recessive manner. Normal alleles have 10 GCN repeats encoding 10 alanine amino acids. Autosomal dominant alleles have 12 to 17 GCN repeats and autosomal recessive alleles have 11 GCN repeats. Heterozygous 11‐GCN repeats have been observed in some individuals with mild adult‐onset OPMD. (B) Number of identified cases with different GCN trinucleotide repeat in exon 1 of the PABPN1 gene.

CAPN3: Encoding calpain‐3

Calpainopathy (LGMD2A), characterized by symmetric progressive weakness of proximal LG muscles with age at onset ranging from 2 to 40 years, 25 is the most common LGMD subtype (~30% of all LGMD cases). 26 It was long considered an autosomal recessive disorder. Recently, in‐frame deletions in the CAPN3 gene including c.643_663del21 and c.598_612del15 were reported to be associated with an autosomal dominant form of calpainopathy. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 “The Lantern Focused Neuromuscular Panel” identified 60 of 1266 (4.7%) patients with two CAPN3 variants (Fig. 2): 17 of the 60 (28.3%) had homozygous pathogenic variants and thus autosomal recessive calpainopathy. Pathogenic CAPN3 variants along with VUS were identified in 12 of the 60 (20.0%), and pathogenic CNV were identified in 3 of the 60 (5.0%) (Table 2).

Additional patients with CAPN3 deletions exhibited complex inheritance patterns: nine had the 15‐base‐pair in‐frame deletion c.598_612del15 and six more had the 21‐base‐pair in‐frame deletion (c.643_663del21; Tables S3 and S4). In 8 of these 15 (53.3%) patients with in‐frame deletions, no second reportable CAPN3 variant was detected, indicating autosomal dominant inheritance (Table S3); 7 of the 15 (46.7%; 4 with c.598_612del15 and 3 with c.643_663del21) had second reportable CAPN3 variants and thus autosomal recessive inheritance (Table S4). One further patient, a 12‐year‐old boy with clinical suspicion of LGMD (ARMD108; Table 2), had c.643_663del21 compound‐heterozygous with deletion of the whole CAPN3 gene.

DYSF: Encoding dysferlin

Pathogenic DYSF variants are associated with autosomal recessive LGMD2B, Miyoshi MD, and distal myopathy with anterior tibial onset. Miyoshi MD is characterized by muscle weakness and atrophy in the distal parts of the legs with age of onset around 19 years. LGMD2B is characterized by weakness and atrophy of the pelvic and shoulder girdle muscles in adolescence or young adulthood, with slow progression. 31 Pathogenic DYSF variants with second reportable variants were identified in 51 (4.0% of the total 1266 cases) patients with autosomal recessive dysferlinopathy (Fig. 2). Homozygous pathogenic variants were identified in 13 of the 51 (25.4%) patients with dysferlinopathy. Pathogenic DYSF variants along with second reportable VUS were identified in 16 of the 51 (31.4%) patients (Table S4). The 51 patients identified included 16 Caucasian, 14 African American, 12 Hispanic, 2 Asian, 2 Middle Eastern, and 5 of unreported ethnicity/race (Fig. 4A).

ANO5: Encoding anoctamin‐5

Pathogenic ANO5 variants are associated with autosomal recessive LGMD2L, Miyoshi MD, and autosomal dominant gnathodiaphyseal dysplasia. LGMD2L is characterized by proximal lower‐limb weakness with age of onset ranging from 15 to 70 years. 32 “The Lantern Focused Neuromuscular Panel” data identified 45 (3.6% of the total 1266 cases) patients with two reportable ANO5 variants and thus autosomal recessive LGMD2L (Fig. 2); 6 of the 45 (13.3%) had homozygous pathogenic ANO5 variants and 14 of the 45 (31.1%) had pathogenic ANO5 variants along with second reportable VUS. The founder mutation c.191dup in exon 5 occurred in 27 of the 45 (60.0%) along with other reportable ANO5 variants, indicating that it is the most common variant (Table S4). Pathogenic CNVs were identified in 3 of the 45 (6.7%) patients with LGMD2L (Table 2). One of these had a whole‐gene ANO5 deletion (ARMD45), and intragenic deletions were identified in two patients (Fig. 5A,B; Cases: ARMD44 and ARMD46, respectively). All three had a second reportable ANO5 sequence variant. Ninety‐one patients had a single pathogenic ANO5 variant without any reportable second ANO5 variant (Table S3).

(A) Copy number loss of exon 1 to 22 and sequence variant c.191dup in exon 5 were detected in ANO5 of a 52‐year‐old male with proximal and distal muscle weakness, exercise intolerance, and muscle pain (ARMD44; Table 2). FKRP: Encoding fukutin‐related protein

FKRP‐related muscle disorders include highly variable clinical phenotypes, ranging from severe congenital MD to mild LGMD2I. 33 Pathogenic FKRP variants along with a second reportable variant were identified in 34 of 1266 (2.7%) patients (Fig. 2). A homozygous pathogenic variant was identified in 22 of these 34 (64.7%) patients with LGMD2I. The most common founder mutation c.826C>A (p.Leu276Ile) in exon 4 was identified in 33 of the 34 (97.1%) patients in the homozygous state or with a second reportable FKRP variant (Table S4); all LGMD2I cases except one. Two other reportable variants, c.266C>T (p.Pro89Leu) and c.1441C>G (p.Pro481Ala), were identified in the exon 4 of the FRKP gene in a 66‐year‐old female with LGMD2I (ARMD234).

PYGM: Encoding myophosphorylase

McArdle's disease (glycogen storage disease V) is an autosomal‐recessive disorder characterized by exercise intolerance and sometimes myoglobinuria, resulting from pathogenic PYGM variants. Thirty‐three patients (2.6% of 1266 cases) in our cohort received molecular diagnosis of McArdle's disease. Less allelic heterogenicity was observed for PYGM than other disease genes; the c.148C>T (p.Arg50Ter) variant was identified in most cases (25 of 33 [75.8%] patients). Twenty of twenty‐five patients with the c.148C>T variant were Caucasian, two Hispanic, two of unreported ethnicity/race, and one was African American and Caucasian. This variant was detected in the homozygous state in 14 of 33 (42.4%) patients (Table S4).

Classic autosomal dominant MDs

A total of 13 genes in “The Lantern Focused Neuromuscular Panel” were related only to classic autosomal dominant MDs and not to autosomal recessive MD subtypes. We identified 108 of 1266 (8.5%) patients with pathogenic variants in these classic autosomal dominant genes, including VCP (28, 2.2%), MYOT (15, 1.2%), FLNC (14, 1.1%), LDB3 (13, 1.0%), DNAJB6 (10, 0.8%), SMCHD1 (10, 0.8%), CAV3 (6, 0.5%), SGCE (3, 0.2%), BAG3 (2, 0.16%), HNRNPA1 (2, 0.16%), TNPO3 (2, 0.16%), TPM2 (2, 0.16%), and MATR3 (1, 0.08%) (Table 1, Table S3).

X‐linked MDs

A total of, 125 patients with pathogenic variants were identified in the following four X‐linked genes including DMD (116, 9.3%), EMD (5, 0.4%), FHL1 (2, 0.16%), and MTM1 (2, 0.16%) (Table S5). A total of, 116 patients with a definitive diagnosis of DMD/BMD were identified. A total of, 83 of the 116 (71.6%) patients were identified with large DMD deletions and duplications. A patient (XLMD124) was identified with monosomy X, and a patient (XLMD125) was identified with Klinefelter syndrome (XXY), as chromosomal abnormality. A total of, 34 of the 116 (29.3%) patients with DMD/BMD were identified with sequence variants. Out of the five patients with EMD pathogenic variants, two had whole gene deletions.

Discussion

LGMDs and other MDs are clinically heterogeneous and sometimes difficult to distinguish via clinical evaluations and muscle biopsy. The same gene variants may show different clinical presentations within a family; also, similar clinical phenotypes can arise from different genes. NGS panel results from previous testing programs were limited to 35 genes related to LGMD subtypes, and CNV analysis was not included. 1 To expand our understanding of the genetic spectrum underlying LGMDs and other MDs with overlapping phenotypes, we developed a larger, comprehensive NGS‐based 66‐gene panel, to detect sequence variants and CNVs in one assay.

Individuals from the United States with clinical suspicion of LGMD or other overlapping muscular dystrophies are included in this study. However, for some cases very limited information regarding the associated clinical features was provided. Both sequence variants and copy number variants are analyzed in this panel testing, with the following limitations. Smaller CNVs are difficult to detect when compared to larger ones. Some CNVs may not be detected due to the size of the exons or other factors such as sequence complexity.

Consistent with our previous publication, 1 our findings confirm CAPN3, DYSF, ANO5, and FKRP as major contributing genes related to LGMD recessive subtypes (Fig. 2). Calpainopathy (LGMD2A) was the most common LGMD in our cohort, with definitive molecular diagnosis established in 68 of 1266 (5.4%) patients; 60 had an autosomal recessive subtype and 8 had an autosomal dominant subtype (Fig. 2). A diverse variant spectrum was observed, with 40 different pathogenic variants distributed across the entire CAPN3 gene covering exon regions 1, 2, 4–11, 16, 18–23 (Table S4). CNV analysis helped identify 3 of the 68 patients (Table 2). A homozygous intragenic deletion of CAPN3 exons 22–23 was identified in an 11‐year‐old male (ARMD107; Table 2) with an onset age of 9 years, along with elevated creatine kinase (CK), proximal muscle weakness, and toe walking. Another intragenic deletion of CAPN3 exons 2–9 was identified with a second reportable pathogenic variant c.2318_2321dup in exon 22 in an 18‐year‐old male (ARMD109; Table 2, Fig. 6A) with elevated CK, hypotonia, and proximal muscle weakness. A whole CAPN3 gene deletion was identified with a second reportable pathogenic variant c.643_663del in exon 5 in a 12‐year‐old male with clinical suspicion of LGMD (ARMD108; Table 2). Further detailed genotype–phenotype correlation studies in multiple generations of large families with clinical suspicion of calpainopathy are required to understand better this complex LGMD.

CNV analysis also identified a case of gamma‐sarcoglycanopathy (LGMD2C): copy number loss of the entire SGCG gene and sequence variant c.702G>A (p.Met234Ile) in exon 7 were identified in a 38‐year‐old male with hypotonia and proximal and distal muscle weakness (ARMD462; Table 2, Fig. 6B).

Dysferlinopathy, one of the most common autosomal recessive LGMD subtypes, is caused by pathogenic DYSF variants. Although the 51 patients spanned five ethnic groups, less DYSF allelic heterogeneity was observed in African Americans and Hispanics. The c.2643+1G>A DYSF variant was the most common pathogenic variant in African Americans, observed in 9 of 14 (Fig. 4B). The c.4253G>A (p.Gly1418Asp) DYSF pathogenic variant was identified in five patients with dysferlinopathy; all are Hispanic, indicating this variant is more common in this group (Fig. 4C).

Our findings confirmed autosomal recessive LGMD2L (ANO5) as another common LGMD subtype and indicated less allelic heterogeneity in the ANO5 gene than in genes related to other LGMD subtypes. Further parental segregation studies are required in these families to identify any underlying autosomal subtype.

Fukutinopathy (FKRP) was also common in our cohort, with variants indicating high homozygosity relative to other LGMD subtypes.

The results also illuminate the genetic basis of overlapping MDs. The highest prevalence of OPMD is reported in Bukhara Jews in Israel (1:600) and French Canadians (1:1000). 24 , 34 Prevalence of OPMD is 1:100,000–1,000,000 in the European population. Most affected US individuals are of French Canadian or Ashkenazi Jewish descent, or Spanish Americans residing in Texas and California; however, large‐scale studies have not provided in‐depth knowledge regarding OPMD in other populations. 24 , 34 , 35 , 36 We identified 133 patients with a definitive molecular diagnosis of OPMD (Fig. 3). To our knowledge, this is the first large‐scale report of diagnostic testing of the PABPN1 gene using NGS‐based testing. Seventy of the patients were Caucasian, indicating a higher prevalence of OPMD in United States Caucasians than previously thought. Other patients with OPMD included 42 Hispanic, 10 French Canadian, 2 Asian, 1 African American, 1 Native American, and 7 with unknown ethnicity/race (Table S2). We observed different combinations of autosomal dominant and autosomal recessive alleles in this cohort. The 13 GCN repeat allele was identified in 111 of 133 (83.5%) patients with an OPMD diagnosis, indicating it to be the most common autosomal dominant pathogenic allele (Fig. 3). The homozygous 11 GCN repeat allele was identified in only one patient (0.8%; OP1) indicating that the autosomal recessive OPMD subtype is very rare relative to the autosomal dominant subtype. Other dominant alleles including 12 GCN, 14 GCN, and 15 GCN were identified in 21 of 133 (15.7%) patients, suggesting these dominant alleles are rare compared with the 13 GCN allele (Fig. 3). It is interesting that the homozygous 12 GCN repeat allele was identified in one patient with OPMD (OP2). The heterozygous 11 GCN repeat allele was identified in 35 individuals and OPMD‐related clinical features were not observed in the majority of these individuals; only two individuals had some clinical features related to mild OPMD (Table S6). These large‐scale definitive molecular diagnostic results directly facilitate further genotype–phenotype correlation studies, specifically to observe correlations between alanine‐repeat length and onset age, disease severity, and progression, and will help to fill research gaps on the genetic basis and complex inheritance mechanisms of OPMD.

McArdle disease is a metabolic myopathy characterized by exercise intolerance manifested by rapid fatigue, myalgia, and muscle cramps. 37 Pathogenic variants in the PYGM gene were identified in 33 (2.6%) patients. The results demonstrated that McArdle disease is a common MD, which is difficult to distinguish from other LGMD subtypes without molecular confirmation.

To avoid separate reflex testing for CNV analysis, we integrated NGS‐based CNV analysis in our program. This reduces costs and saves time when providing a definitive molecular diagnosis. CNVs were identified in 95 of 1266 (7.5%) cases (Table 2, Tables S2–S4). Different CNVs, including single‐exon, multi‐exon, and whole genes, were successfully identified using this NGS panel in genes including DMD, EMD, CAPN3, ANO5, SGCG, COL6A2, DOK7, and LAMA2. Thus, integration of CNV analysis into routine NGS‐based panel testing is instrumental in identifying rare LGMDs and other overlapping MD subtypes.

Results from our cohort provide new evidence illuminating oligogenic or multigenic inheritance in muscular dystrophies. Most patients with MD of known etiology have a single‐gene change explaining the observed Mendelian inheritance; however, some show exceptions to Mendelian inheritance patterns. Definitive molecular diagnosis increasingly suggests roles for multiple genes in some patients with MDs. Pathogenic variants are identified in multiple genes in these patients, leading to multigenic inheritance patterns and an unusual complex clinical presentation. Our large‐scale project indicated the possible role of multigenic inheritance by identifying 26 cases (26/1266, 2.1%) with pathogenic variants in multiple genes (Table S7). Further genotype–phenotype correlation studies are required in other affected family members and healthy control individuals to understand completely the possible digenic or multigenic inheritance in these families.

In conclusion, this comprehensive NGS panel that included CNV analysis helped identify LGMD subtypes as well as other myopathies with overlapping clinical features. Greater prevalence was observed for genetically confirmed OPMD, VCP‐related inclusion body myopathy, and other dominant overlapping MDs, eg, MYOT‐related, LDB3‐related, or FLNC‐related autosomal dominant myofibrillar myopathy and cardiomyopathy. Expanding beyond the classic 35 LGMD genes and including CNV analysis in parallel with sequencing is an efficient and cost‐effective approach to optimize diagnostic yield. Based on the experience gained from this large‐scale study, the following important genes such as PABPN1, VCP, LDB3, FLNC, CAV3, PYGM, LAMA2, GMPPB, NEB, GNE, RYR1, CLCN1, and DMD should be included along with classic LGMD genes in routine panel testing. NGS‐based CNV analysis should be included to reduce the cost associated with separate reflex testing for CNV detection, especially muscular dystrophies related to DMD, EMD, CAPN3, ANO5, DYSF, LAMA2, SGCG, and COL6A2.
