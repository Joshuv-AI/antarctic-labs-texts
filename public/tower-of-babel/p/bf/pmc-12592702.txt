A two-stage architecture for soundscape classification and preservation
Authors: Samantha Di Loreto, Sergio Montelpare
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSound classification is a fundamental task in audio signal processing with applications ranging from environmental monitoring to urban planning. We present a novel two-stage system for open set audio classification that integrates deep learning techniques with Schafer’s soundscape theory. The two-stage architecture consists of: (1) a variational autoencoder (VAE) that first learns compressed representations of acoustic features and identifies distinctive sounds through reconstruction error analysis, and (2) a convolutional neural network (CNN) that then classifies these sounds into Schafer’s theoretical categories (keynotes, sound signals, and soundmarks) using the learned features. This sequential approach allows the system to first understand what makes sounds unique before categorizing their role in the soundscape. The VAE effectively learns the latent representation of acoustic features from mel-spectrograms, while the CNN leverages these representations for classification. We evaluated the system on standard datasets including UrbanSound8K, ESC-50, URBAN-SED, and TUT urban acoustic scenes, achieving an average accuracy of 80.7% across all datasets. Additionally, we tested our approach on a dataset composed of binaural recordings collected in the university neighborhood of Pescara, Italy. Our research provides empirical validation of Schafer’s theoretical framework through quantitative metrics, demonstrating strong alignment between computational classifications and theoretical descriptions. The proposed methodology advances soundscape analysis by objectively quantifying previously qualitative categories, enabling automated classification while maintaining theoretical fidelity, and creating a foundation for soundscape preservation policies that protect acoustic identity as a form of intangible cultural heritage.Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

When sound waves propagate through an environment, they create what R. Murray Schafer termed a “soundscape” - the acoustic environment as perceived and understood by individuals and society1.

Schafer’s pioneering work in acoustic ecology2 introduced a comprehensive framework for analyzing environmental sounds based on their perceptual, cultural, and functional characteristics. This framework, further developed by Truax3 and Wrightson4, distinguishes three fundamental categories: keynotes - persistent background sounds that may not be consciously perceived but define the acoustic character of a place (such as traffic hum or ventilation systems); sound signals - foreground sounds that capture conscious attention and often convey specific information (like sirens, bells, or announcements); and soundmarks - sounds unique to a particular location that possess cultural significance and contribute to the acoustic identity of a place (such as Big Ben in London or distinctive church bells in a community). This categorization has been influential in soundscape studies5–7 and urban planning8.

While Schafer’s theory has been influential in fields ranging from urban planning to acoustic ecology, efforts to quantitatively validate and automate classification according to this framework have been limited. Traditional sound event classification typically focuses on identifying the sound source (e.g., car horn, bird song) rather than its function within the soundscape. Brown et al.9 attempted to develop objective measures for soundscape quality based partially on Schafer’s work, but did not directly address the classification problem.

In urban sound classification, researchers have explored different deep learning architectures including ANNs, CNNs, RNNs, and LSTMs to categorize diverse soundscapes10–12.

Recent advances in deep learning have revolutionized audio classification, with convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and variational autoencoders (VAEs) demonstrating exceptional performance in extracting meaningful features from audio data13. However, these approaches have rarely been applied to theoretical frameworks from acoustic ecology.

The open set recognition task aims to detect unexpected samples while learning only from known samples. It has primarily been applied to image classification problems14, but few works exist in the audio domain. A key challenge in open-set audio recognition is the collection of appropriate datasets. Some research, such as rhythm pattern recognition, requires professional experts to annotate the audio data.

Recent work has made significant progress in addressing unknown sound event classification through deep learning methods. This approach creates compact cluster structures in the feature space for known classes, which helps recognize unknown classes by allowing sufficient space to locate unknown samples in the embedded feature space. By applying both center loss to minimize intra-class distance (pulling embedded features toward their cluster centers) and supervised contrastive loss to increase inter-class separation, researchers have developed more robust classification systems. Additionally, explorations into self-supervised learning for detecting unknown sound events have shown promising results, with experimental evidence demonstrating sustained performance improvements across various datasets15.

In the early 2000s, researchers focused on generating powerful audio descriptors. These were usually divided into three sets: low-level features, score features, and mid-level features. Hand-created music features such as spectral centroid16 and pitch salience17 were commonly used. Score features like mode and pitch density were calculated directly from musical scores. Mid-level features were more intuitively understandable by typical learners.

With the advancement of deep learning, algorithms now integrate feature extraction and decision-making for classification. With many trainable parameters, networks can directly learn to map extracted features from input to target labels. As the number of labeled samples increases, these networks become more robust and reliable. Researchers have adapted traditional convolutional neural networks from the image domain to the audio domain, building sound event classification systems that perform well even in noisy environments18.

For evaluation of audio classification systems, several benchmark datasets have been established in the literature. Salamon et al.19 introduced a taxonomy and dataset for urban sound research, providing a foundation for audio classification in urban environments. Piczak20 contributed to the field with an environmental sound classification dataset, enabling standardized evaluation across multiple acoustic categories. Mesaros et al.21 presented a framework for sound event detection and scene analysis, along with corresponding evaluation datasets that have become important benchmarks in the acoustic community.

Some researchers have explored transformer structures to detect sound events based on audio tagging and temporal location information22. However, approaches that incorporate theoretical frameworks from acoustic ecology, particularly Schafer’s soundscape categorization, have been notably absent from the computational literature.

This paper aims at addressing this gap by developing a novel two-stage audio classification system that seamlessly integrates deep learning techniques with Schafer’s soundscape theory.

Our two-stage architecture operates sequentially: first, a VAE learns what makes sounds acoustically distinctive through reconstruction analysis; second, a CNN classifies these characterized sounds into Schafer’s categories. This separation enhances classification accuracy by building upon learned acoustic distinctiveness.

Our system comprises:

A variational autoencoder (VAE) that learns to extract and compress meaningful acoustic features from audio mel-spectrograms, where “meaningful” refers to features that capture the essential acoustic characteristics needed for classification while discarding noise and redundancy, providing a robust latent representation of the soundscape.

This approach not only provides practical tools for automated soundscape analyses but also offers empirical validation of Schafer’s theoretical framework through quantitative metrics. By demonstrating the alignment between computational classifications and theoretical descriptions, we establish a much-needed bridge between qualitative acoustic ecology and quantitative machine learning methodologies.

This ambitious objective is achieved by employing four synergistically related techniques: (1) development of a specialized two-stage neural network architecture designed specifically for soundscape classification according to Schafer’s theoretical framework; (2) empirical validation of Schafer’s soundscape categories through objective computational metrics, verifying the practical applicability of this seminal theory; (3) comprehensive evaluation across multiple standard audio datasets (UrbanSound8K, ESC-50, URBAN-SED, TUT Urban Acoustic Scenes); (4) validation of previously qualitative descriptors in soundscape analysis, enabling systematic preservation of acoustic environments.

It is important to note that while our system provides automatic classification based on acoustic features, we acknowledge that true cultural significance cannot be determined by computational means alone. The approach identifies acoustically distinctive candidates that often correlate with cultural importance, as validated through our community studies following ISO 12913-223 protocols, but final soundmark designation requires human interpretation and community consensus.

Characteristic analysis of Schafer’s categories via CNN (convolutional neural network)

To classify sounds according to Schafer’s theoretical framework, we developed a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) architecture with five convolutional layers followed by three fully connected layers. Our approach uses the VAE to obtain initial classifications and then trains a CNN to refine them. While the initial system uses only audio energy as a simple criterion to distinguish between keynote, sound signal, and soundmark (using percentile thresholds), the CNN can consider more complex patterns in spectrograms. Discussion

The present study proposes a computational approach to soundscape analysis grounded in Schafer’s theoretical framework1, with the primary goal of preserving the acoustic identity of places. By combining variational autoencoders and convolutional neural networks, this work attempts to translate qualitative soundscape categories into quantifiable metrics that can identify and protect the characteristic acoustic elements of urban environments. This discussion examines how the current approach relates to existing literature, acknowledges methodological limitations, and suggests potential applications for acoustic heritage preservation. The preservation of soundscapes as cultural heritage has gained increasing recognition in recent years6. As noted by Schafer1, distinctive sounds significantly contribute to a place’s identity and deserve protection similar to visual landmarks. Our computational approach offers a systematic method to identify these acoustically and culturally significant elements, addressing a gap in current preservation practices that often prioritize visual and architectural elements over acoustic ones26.

Previous computational approaches to soundscape classification have typically relied on acoustic parameters without explicitly connecting to established theoretical frameworks or preservation goals. Cain et al.27 identified two principal dimensions of soundscape perception (calmness and vibrancy) through principal component analysis but did not address the identification of distinctive sounds for preservation. Similarly, Davies et al.28 focused on positive and negative soundscape evaluations using semantic differential analysis without specific attention to cultural heritage aspects. The perceptual dimensions identified in these studies, while valuable, do not directly facilitate the identification of sounds that constitute a place’s acoustic identity.

More recent studies have employed machine learning for soundscape classification, though rarely with preservation as an explicit aim. Boes et al.29 used supervised learning to predict human perceptual responses to soundscapes, while Madanian et al.30 applied deep learning to classify urban sounds according to their source types. However, these approaches generally focus on sound source identification rather than on the functional and cultural roles that sounds play within environments, which is central to Schafer’s framework and crucial for heritage preservation.

The two-stage architecture used in the current study for learning audio representation follows approaches such as those of31,32, who demonstrated the effectiveness of variational methods to capture perceptually relevant acoustic features. Our integration of VAEs with CNNs extends this work by specifically tailoring the architecture to identify Schafer’s categories, particularly soundmarks as candidates for preservation, showing reasonable performance across multiple datasets (average accuracy of 80.7%).

The results suggest some alignment between computational metrics and Schafer’s theoretical descriptions, particularly in the distinctive patterns observed for soundmarks (high cultural significance and uniqueness). This appears consistent with Schafer’s characterisation of soundmarks as sounds that are especially regarded or noticed by people in that community and with subsequent interpretations by researchers such as Truax33 in the context of acoustic communication. The high classification accuracy for soundmarks across datasets (ranging from 83.5% to 86.3%) suggests that these culturally significant sounds may indeed possess distinctive acoustic properties that can be computationally identified and subsequently preserved.

The case study of the university district in Pescara offers preliminary evidence of the method’s applicability to real-world acoustic preservation challenges. Identifying specific university environment soundmarks including distinctive bell tower, department-specific equipment sounds, and sounds from recurring cultural events demonstrates how the system can isolate elements that contribute to the acoustic identity of a place. This aligns with Amphoux’s concept of sonic identity34 and responds to concerns raised by researchers such as Westerkamp35 about the homogenization of acoustic environments and loss of distinctive local sounds.

Several methodological limitations should be acknowledged regarding the preservation objective. First, the current implementation may not fully capture the historical and cultural associations that make certain sounds significant to communities, an aspect emphasized by Smith36 in her work on intangible cultural heritage. Second, the validation has been performed primarily on relatively short audio segments, which may not fully capture the temporal dynamics of soundmarks that might evolve over daily, seasonal, or annual cycles, as discussed by Botteldooren et al.37. Third, community involvement in identifying valued soundmarks is limited in the current approach, despite the importance of community perspectives in heritage identification emphasized by heritage scholars such as Parker38.

The approach presented here has specific applications for acoustic heritage preservation. By identifying distinctive soundmarks, the system could support the development of “sound preservation plans” analogous to architectural preservation plans, as proposed by O’Connor39. The quantitative metrics could potentially inform decisions about which sounds deserve protection in urban development processes, addressing gaps in current planning practices noted by Aletta and Kang40. Moreover, the approach could facilitate the creation of acoustic archives that document distinctive sounds before they disappear due to urbanization, technological change, or cultural shifts, responding to urgent documentation needs identified by UNESCO41 in its Convention for the Safeguarding of Intangible Cultural Heritage.

Our system automatically identifies acoustically distinctive sounds that may serve as soundmark candidates according to Schafer’s framework. However, we must address the fundamental distinction between acoustic distinctiveness and cultural significance. The CNN excels at identifying statistical uniqueness - unusual spectral patterns, high reconstruction errors, temporal anomalies - but these acoustic features do not inherently carry cultural meaning. A church bell, for instance, may be detected as acoustically unique due to its harmonic structure and temporal pattern, but its significance as a soundmark derives from its role in community life, historical associations, and symbolic value that no algorithm can directly assess. Our approach operates on an empirical observation: sounds that become culturally significant often possess distinctive acoustic properties that make them memorable and recognizable. This correlation, validated in our case study through ISO 12913-223 listening tests with the university community, enables the system to identify likely candidates for soundmarks. The validation showed strong agreement between automatic acoustic classification and community-identified significant sounds, suggesting that acoustic distinctiveness can serve as a useful proxy for cultural importance within specific contexts. This relationship parallels approaches in ecoacoustics, where systems like BirdNET42 automatically identify potential species calls that require ornithologist verification. Similarly, our system provides efficient pre-screening of acoustically distinctive sounds that merit further investigation through participatory methods. The key contribution is demonstrating that Schafer’s theoretical categories can be computationally approximated, providing a systematic tool for initial soundscape analysis. We emphasize that our system is designed for automatic classification to assist, not replace, human judgment in heritage preservation. Different communities will value different sounds based on their unique cultural contexts, historical experiences, and collective memories. The computational approach provides reproducible, systematic identification of acoustic candidates, while the determination of true cultural significance requires community engagement, ethnographic understanding, and human interpretation.

Future research focusing on preservation might explore several directions. Integration of historical recordings could enable the identification of soundmarks that have disappeared but might be candidates for reintroduction, addressing the concept of “acoustic restoration” discussed by Bijsterveld43. Community participatory methods could be incorporated to validate computationally identified soundmarks against community perceptions of acoustic heritage, following approaches suggested by Feld44 in acoustic ethnography. Longitudinal studies could examine how soundscapes and their distinctive elements evolve over time, addressing preservation challenges related to gradual change.

Conclusion

This study has demonstrated the effectiveness of a two-way deep learning-based approach for classifying and preserving urban soundscapes. Our results highlight how the combination of a variational autoencoder (VAE) for latent feature extraction and a convolutional neural network (CNN) for classification according to Schafer’s categories provides a robust framework for soundscape analysis. The proposed approach achieved an overall accuracy of 87.3% in sound classification, with precision and recall rates of 82.2% and 91.3% respectively (F1-score: 90.2%). Particularly significant was the system’s ability to identify soundmarks with a precision of 92.1%, which are crucial elements for defining a place’s sonic identity. The distribution of Schafer’s categories in Pescara’s university district revealed a characteristic balance: 47.2% keynotes, 32.5% sound signals, and 20.3% soundmarks. This distribution, together with the analysis of seasonal and daily variations, provides important insights into the temporal dynamics of the soundscape and its resilience. The analysis of sound properties through the radar chart confirmed the validity of Schafer’s theoretical distinctions: keynotes are characterized by high persistence (0.9) but low attention (0.2); sound signals show high communicative function (0.9) and attention (0.8); soundmarks present high cultural significance (0.9) and uniqueness (0.9). Comparing classifications based solely on sound energy with those refined by the CNN revealed an accuracy increase of 18.7%, demonstrating the added value of the deep learning approach in identifying complex patterns not captured by simple energy metrics. Future research could extend the framework to comparisons between different urban realities, analysis of long-term temporal evolution of soundscapes, and integration of psychoacoustic parameters for better correlation with human perception.

Our automatic classifier demonstrates that Schafer’s categories can be computationally approximated through acoustic analysis, providing a systematic tool for soundscape documentation. However, the transition from acoustic distinctiveness to cultural significance ultimately requires human validation. Future work should focus on integrating automatic classification with standardized participatory protocols to ensure preservation efforts reflect both acoustic uniqueness and community values.

In conclusion, this study offers an innovative methodological contribution at the intersection of acoustic ecology and artificial intelligence, providing computational tools to preserve and document the sonic identity of places. The proposed two-way approach represents a significant step toward the systematic conservation of cultural sonic heritage, particularly relevant in an era of rapid transformation of urban environments. The results suggest the importance of integrating computational analysis of soundscapes into urban planning and intangible cultural heritage conservation policies. The developed methodology could support continuous monitoring initiatives, contributing to the creation of dynamic sound archives and the design of acoustically aware urban spaces.

Methods

Terminology and definitions

In this work, we employ several machine learning concepts that we define here for clarity. Classes refer to the categories into which we organize sounds - in our case, Schafer’s three categories: keynotes, sound signals, and soundmarks. Each audio sample is assigned to one of these classes based on its characteristics. Intra-class distance measures how similar sounds are within the same category - for example, how similar different keynote sounds are to each other. A smaller intra-class distance indicates that sounds in that category share more common features, making them easier to identify as a group. The reconstruction error represents the difference between an original audio signal and its reconstructed version after compression by the VAE. When the VAE processes an audio file, it first compresses it into a simplified representation (encoding), then attempts to recreate the original from this compression (decoding). The reconstruction error measures how much information was lost in this process - unusual or distinctive sounds typically have higher reconstruction errors because they are harder for the model to compress and recreate accurately. This two-stage architecture was specifically chosen to operationalize Schafer’s theoretical framework computationally. The VAE enables unsupervised discovery of acoustic patterns without labeled data, learning what makes sounds distinctive - crucial given the subjective nature of soundscape perception. The CNN then maps these learned features to Schafer’s categories, benefiting from pre-extracted relevant patterns rather than raw spectrograms. This separation provides interpretability through reconstruction error as a novel distinctiveness metric. We selected PyTorch for reproducibility, mel-spectrograms for their perceptual relevance matching human auditory processing, and Head Acoustics binaural recording to capture spatial characteristics essential to soundscape perception. The 70th/30th percentile thresholds were empirically determined from energy distributions, aligning with psychoacoustic principles of auditory stream segregation where sounds emerging 6-10 dB above background become perceptually distinct.

Note that while soundscape studies typically employ continuous metrics (LAeq, loudness, roughness), our approach necessarily discretizes sounds into Schafer’s three categories. This discretization parallels established practices like categorizing noise levels (quiet/moderate/loud) or the ISO 12913-3 quadrants (pleasant/unpleasant eventful/uneventful), making complex acoustic environments interpretable for preservation decisions.

System architecture overview

The audio classification system operates as a two-stage pipeline, as illustrated in Fig. 5. The first stage utilizes a Variational Autoencoder (VAE)45,46 for feature learning and dimensionality reduction of audio data. The second stage employs a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) classifier47 that categorizes sounds according to Schafer’s taxonomy based on the features extracted by the VAE.

This architecture leverages the strengths of both models: the VAE’s ability to learn compact, informative representations and the CNN’s discriminative power for classification tasks.

This preprocessing stage converts raw audio waveforms into mel-spectrograms, which capture the frequency content of audio signals in a way that approximates human auditory perception. The mel-spectrogram representation is particularly suitable for audio classification tasks as it emphasizes perceptually relevant frequency bands. The study conducts spectral analysis of binaural audio recordings from the Pescara University District using mel-scale frequency analysis, which provides a perceptually-relevant representation by mimicking human auditory perception. The researchers compute mel spectrograms using a 2048-point FFT with 512-sample hop length, generating 128 mel frequency bins that capture spectral evolution over time. Through a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) architecture, the analysis examines reconstruction error of these spectral representations to distinguish between different acoustic conditions in the university district environment. The VAE learns to encode complex spectral patterns present in binaural recordings into a lower-dimensional latent space and reconstruct them, with reconstruction error serving as a discriminative feature for audio classification. This spectral-based methodology enables effective characterization of the acoustic environment by focusing on frequency domain properties and their temporal variations.

The Adam optimizer50 was used the VAE with an adaptive learning rate schedule. For the VAE, the learning rate was started at 1e-4 and reduced by a factor of 10 at epochs 100 and 150.

The VAE compresses mel-spectrograms from 1282756 dimensions (approximately 353,000 values) to a 256-dimensional latent vector, achieving a 1,400 compression ratio. This “meaningful representation” preserves perceptually relevant features (temporal patterns, spectral envelopes, harmonic structures) while discarding redundancy. The reconstruction error serves as a distinctiveness metric: keynotes (common sounds) have low reconstruction error, sound signals moderate error, and soundmarks (unique sounds) high error. This compressed representation provides the CNN with pre-extracted relevant features rather than raw spectrograms, improving classification efficiency and accuracy.

The model processes acoustic features as follows: mel-spectrograms provide time-frequency representations analogous to psychoacoustic analysis; the VAE compresses these into latent features similar to principal components in soundscape studies; reconstruction error serves as an acoustic distinctiveness metric; the CNN maps these features to Schafer’s categories based on learned patterns corresponding to L90-like levels for keynotes, L10-like peaks for sound signals, and unique spectral signatures for soundmarks.

Data acquisition in situ

It was conducted a real-world case study using binaural monitoring of the university district in Pescara, Italy, to create an authentic neighborhood dataset. We applied the two-stage neural network architecture system to analyze the binaural recordings from the university campus. Located on the Adriatic coast of central Italy, Pescara is a vibrant city with approximately 120,000 inhabitants. The university district is situated in the southern part of the city, where the campus of “G. d’Annunzio” University creates a distinctive urban environment characterized by a mix of academic buildings, student gathering spaces, and surrounding residential and commercial areas. Figure 7 illustrates the study area.

Map of the study area showing the university district of Pescara with the G. d’Annunzio University campus and surrounding urban context. Map created using Adobe Illustrator 2023 (Adobe Inc., https://www.adobe.com/products/illustrator.html) based on AutoCAD base map files.

The university district was selected as validation site for several reasons: (1) it provides acoustic diversity with clear examples of all three Schafer categories - keynotes (ventilation systems, ambient movement), sound signals (bells, announcements), and soundmarks (distinctive bell tower, cultural events); (2) it offers a semi-controlled environment with accessible community for validation studies; (3) it enabled systematic temporal sampling across different schedules (lectures/breaks, weekdays/weekends). The site naturally includes many sound categories from the training datasets (air conditioner, car horns, sirens) while also presenting unique sounds not in the training data, testing the model’s ability to generalize to new acoustic environments.

The acoustic monitoring was conducted over a six-week period using a Head Acoustics GmbH Herzogenrath (Type BSU; S/N 1580028; T/N A01) binaural recording system, which accurately replicates human hearing perception through its anatomically correct ear simulator. As illustrated in Fig. 8, the recording equipment was strategically placed at four different locations across the campus, capturing the soundscape during various time segments (morning, afternoon, evening) and on different days of the week (including weekdays and weekends) to ensure comprehensive temporal coverage of the acoustic environment.
