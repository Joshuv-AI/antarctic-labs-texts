Barriers to the implementation of epilepsy genetic testing and counseling guidelines
Authors: Sophie Melly, Amanda L. Bergner, Tristan Sands, Natalie Lippa, Maureen Mulhern
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIn 2022, the National Society of Genetic Counselors (NSGC) published a practice guideline for genetic testing and counseling for unexplained epilepsy. It is endorsed by the American Epilepsy Society and recommends genetic testing for all patients with unexplained epilepsy (prioritizing exome/genome sequencing [ES/GS] or multi‐gene panel [MGP] as first‐tier testing) and providing professional pre‐/post‐test counseling. This study investigates the alignment of neurologists' practice with this guideline and identifies barriers to implementation.MethodsNeurologists who treat epilepsy in the United States completed a survey about guideline awareness, current practices, and barriers to genetic testing/counseling. Recruitment occurred through newsletters/listservs of professional organizations and public contact information. A total of, 91 participants accessed the survey, and 84 completed enough for inclusion in data analysis.ResultsApproximately half (43.9%, 36/82) of participants offer genetic testing to most/all their patients with unexplained epilepsy, while the other half (46.3%, 38/82) offer genetic testing only to patients with epilepsy for whom they suspect a genetic etiology. Neurologists caring for adult patients were significantly more likely than those caring for pediatric patients to fall into the latter category (χ 2[1, N = 69] = 22.66, p < 0.001). When ordering genetic testing, 75.5% (n = 40/53) of participants choose MGP most often, while 20.8% (n = 11/53) choose ES/GS and 3.8% (n = 2/53) choose microarray. Barriers to genetic testing and pre‐/post‐test counseling included insurance coverage, test coordination, limited expertise, and access to a genetic counselor (GC). Barriers were more significant for adult providers. Over half of participants (56.1%, n = 46/82) were aware of the NSGC guideline, but almost all (91.3%, n = 36/82) thought it was not broadly publicized.SignificanceIncreased insurance coverage, access to GCs, and awareness of guidelines are needed to improve access to genetic testing and counseling for unexplained epilepsies, particularly for the adult population.Plain Language SummaryIn 2022, the National Society of Genetic Counselors published a practice guideline recommending genetic testing and counseling for all patients with unexplained epilepsy, using exome/genome sequencing or a multi‐gene panel as the first‐tier test. A total of, 44% of participants offer genetic testing to most/all their patients, while 46% test only when they suspect a genetic cause. Adult neurologists rate most barriers higher than pediatric neurologists. Better insurance coverage and increased access to genetic counselors are key to improving access to epilepsy genetic testing and counseling.

INTRODUCTION

Genetic testing is increasingly used in clinical practice for patients with unexplained epilepsy. Current genetic testing methodologies provide variable outcomes in terms of diagnostic yield for patients with unexplained epilepsy, with a 17% yield across all types of genetic testing and genome sequencing. 1 Whole genome sequencing provides the highest diagnostic yield, with up to 48% of people receiving a positive result. 1 While the diagnostic yield of genetic testing for unexplained epilepsy has been shown to be higher in the setting of infantile or childhood onset of seizures, 2 , 3 past studies examining the diagnostic yield of exome sequencing have demonstrated that even those with the onset of seizures in adulthood had a diagnostic yield between 3.7% and 20%, showing the utility of testing regardless of age. 1 , 4 , 5

Genetic testing is a powerful tool for guiding healthcare decisions in the setting of epilepsy, with changes in clinical management for 12%–80% of people with epilepsy who receive a genetic diagnosis. 1 Potential positive outcomes of receiving a genetic diagnosis for unexplained epilepsy include more informed selection of anti‐seizure medication, initiation or avoidance of treatment options such as ketogenic diet or surgery, referrals to specialists for screening of organ systems based on gene associations, ability to participate in clinical trials, access to prognostic information and gene‐specific resources, more accurate estimation of recurrence risk, and potential for prenatal diagnosis. 1 , 6

Pre‐ and post‐test counseling is crucial to the genetic testing process. Genetic counselors (GCs) are able to provide quality pre‐/post‐test counseling and offer valuable expertise in selecting cost‐effective and efficient tests, choosing the most appropriate person to test, and interpreting test results. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Also, genetic counseling is associated with enhanced patient satisfaction, empowerment, knowledge, experience, and psychosocial support, as well as decreased anxiety. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 When possible, GCs should be utilized to help those considering genetic testing make informed decisions. 9 , 14

The National Society of Genetic Counselors (NSGC) recently published an evidence‐based practice guideline for genetic testing and counseling for unexplained epilepsies. 10 This guideline was endorsed by the American Epilepsy Society (AES) and makes several key recommendations: (1) genetic testing should be offered to anyone with unexplained epilepsy; (2) first‐tier testing should utilize either exome/genome sequencing (ES/GS) or a multi‐gene panel (MGP) with >25 genes; and (3) genetic counseling should be provided by a qualified healthcare professional both before and after testing is completed. 10 ES/GS is conditionally recommended over MGP, except when a defined epilepsy syndrome is suspected, when rapid testing is necessary and rapid ES/GS is unavailable, or when other barriers prevent ES/GS from being ordered. 10

Previous research from 2017 and prior revealed barriers to epilepsy genetic testing, including a large time commitment for adequate consenting and follow‐up procedures; limited clinician knowledge about genetic testing and counseling, results interpretation, and guidelines; unclear clinical utility; and difficult insurance processes. 14 , 15 , 16 However, the NSGC guideline was published in October 2022, and to date no studies have assessed the extent to which these recommendations have been implemented, or assessed barriers to implementation.

Our study was designed to fill this gap by surveying neurologists in the United States who provide care for people with epilepsy. We aimed to assess awareness of the NSGC guideline, how current practices compare to the guideline's recommendations, and what barriers to genetic testing and counseling exist so that we may identify how to increase access to these services for all patients with unexplained epilepsy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This was an observational, cross‐sectional online survey study conducted between October 2023 and February 2024 and approved by the Columbia University Institutional Review Board (IRB Protocol AAAU7189). The survey was distributed to neurologists who treat patients with epilepsy in the United States. Subjects were contacted through the AES email newsletter, the New York State Neurological Society (NYSNS) newsletter, the National Association of Epilepsy Centers (NAEC) listserv, the pediatric Status Epilepticus Research Group (pSERG) listserv, the Citizen's United for Research in Epilepsy (CURE) Researcher Update, the National Society of Genetic Counselors Neurogenetics Special Interest Group and epilepsy subgroup, and through email addresses found through colleagues, Internet searches, and PubMed. Participants who completed the survey were eligible to enter a raffle for one of five $100 Visa gift cards.

Survey design

The survey was developed by the study team, which consisted of a genetic counseling master's student, three American Board of Genetic Counseling‐certified GCs, and a physician who is American Board of Psychiatry and Neurology‐certified in child neurology and epileptology. The survey included 28 items across four domains: demographics, awareness of practice guidelines, current practice and perceived barriers to genetic testing, and current practice and perceived barriers to pre‐ and post‐test counseling (see Supporting Information: Document 5). Some items were written by the study team while others, including various demographic questions and questions relating to barriers to ordering genetic testing, were modified from pre‐existing measures. 17 , 18 , 19

Data collection and analysis

Survey responses were collected and stored using Qualtrics software, descriptive statistics were gathered using Excel, and statistical analysis of quantitative data was done using Statistics Kingdom and Social Science Statistics to determine p‐values. An independent sample t‐test was used to assess if there was a significant difference between adult and pediatric neurologists for their average ratings of barriers to genetic testing and their average ratings of barriers to pre‐ and post‐test counseling. A two‐by‐two chi‐square goodness of fit test was used to compare the responses of adult neurologists and pediatric neurologists for two multiple choice questions. An alpha value of 0.05 was used to indicate the threshold for significant results.

RESULTS

Participants

A total of 91 neurologists responded to the survey, of whom three did not meet eligibility criteria and six did not respond to any questions other than eligibility. Eighty‐two responses were included in the analysis. Of the 79 respondents that answered the demographic questions, 42 (53.2%) primarily treated pediatric populations, 35 (44.3%) primarily treated adult populations, and 2 (2.5%) treated both. Awareness of 2022 NSGC practice guideline

In this sample, 73.8% of pediatric neurologists were aware of the guideline (n = 31/42) as compared with 40.0% of adult neurologists (n = 14/35), a statistically significant difference (χ 2 [1, N = 77] = 8.99, p = 0.003). Additionally, participants who graduated from medical school 21 years ago or more were significantly more likely to be aware of the guideline (n = 28/41, 68.3%) than those who had been out of medical school for 20 years or less (n = 17/38, 44.7%) (χ 2 [1, N = 79] = 4.46, p = 0.035). There was no significant difference in guideline awareness between participants who have 50% or fewer patients with epilepsy (n = 38/64, 59.4%) and those whose patients with epilepsy make up more than 50% of their patient population (n = 7/15, 46.7%) (χ 2 [1, N = 79] = 0.80, p = 0.371). Participants reported that they most frequently learn about new practice guidelines through professional conferences (n = 69/82; 84.2%) or their own research (n = 51/82; 62.2%). For those aware of the 2022 NSGC guideline (n = 46/82, 56.1%), the two most common ways in which they learned about the guideline were through a colleague (n = 14/46; 30.4%) or at a professional conference (n = 12/46; 26.1%) (notably, the 2022 NSGC guideline was highlighted at the 2022 AES Annual Meeting). However, almost all of those who were aware of the guideline did not think it had been broadly publicized (91.3%; n = 42/46).

Genetic testing for the unexplained epilepsies

Current practices

Participants were split in terms of how they currently practice, with approximately half offering genetic testing to most or all of their patients with unexplained epilepsy (n = 36/82; 43.9%) and approximately half offering genetic testing only to those for whom they suspect a genetic etiology (n = 38/82; 46.3%). Only 2.4% (n = 2/82) of participants do not offer genetic testing to any patients with unexplained epilepsy. The remaining 7.3% (n = 6/82) wrote in another option, mostly that they would offer testing if it was free. A significant difference was seen when comparing practice areas, with the majority of pediatric neurologists offering genetic testing to all or most of their patients with unexplained epilepsy (n = 28/42; 66.7%) and the majority of adult neurologists offering genetic testing only to those for whom they suspect a genetic etiology (n = 26/35; 74.3%) (χ 2[1, N = 69] = 22.66, p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in current genetic testing practices between participants with 50% or fewer patients with epilepsy and those with more than 50% of their patients with epilepsy (χ 2[1, N = 79] = 1.85, p = 0.173), nor between participants who graduated medical school more than 20 years ago and those who graduated 20 years ago or less (χ 2[1, N = 79] = 0.06, p = 0.035).

When selecting and ordering genetic tests, 41.25% (n = 33/80) select and order the test themselves, 30% (n = 24/80) of participants refer patients to see a genetics specialist at a separate appointment, 21.25% (n = 17/80) select and order the test with the involvement of a GC, and 7.5% (n = 6/80) select and order the test with the involvement of a provider other than a GC. The overwhelming majority of participants involved in testing reported ordering either a multi‐gene panel (n = 40/53; 75.5%), exome sequencing (n = 7/53; 13.2%), or genome sequencing (n = 4/53; 7.5%) most often. The remainder (n = 2/53; 3.8%) reported ordering chromosomal microarray most often.

Barriers

Participants rated potential barriers to genetic testing on a scale from 1, Not a Barrier, to 5, Significant Barrier. Almost all participants (n = 78/80; 97.5%) reported billing and insurance as a barrier. This was the barrier with the highest average rating. Lack of access to a GC was reported as a barrier to offering testing by 66.3% of respondents (n = 53/80), most of whom (n = 33/53; 62.3%) rated this barrier highly as a 4 or a 5. Logistics of test coordination was also a reported barrier for most participants (n = 64/80; 83.8%), with adult neurologists rating this barrier significantly higher (adult mean [am] = 3.49) than pediatric neurologists (pediatric mean [pm] = 2.5) (t[75] = −3.60, p < 0.001). Notably, adult neurologists also rated the barriers of time involvement (t[75] = −3.92, p < 0.001), lack of practice or institutional guidelines (t[75] = −3.09, p = 0.003), possibility of incidental or uncertain findings (t[75] = −2.02; p = 0.05), lack of experience with the consenting process (t[75] = −3.22, p = 0.002), genetic test availability (t[75] = −2.70, p = 0.009), lack of understanding of test limitations (t[75] = −3.15, p = 0.002), and limited clinical utility (t[75] = −5.15, p < 0.001) significantly higher than pediatric neurologists. Pre‐ and post‐test counseling for the unexplained epilepsies

Current practices

Over half of participants indicated that pre‐test counseling was always provided to patients with unexplained epilepsy who were considering genetic testing (n = 45/79; 57%). Slightly more indicated that post‐test counseling was always provided to patients who had received genetic testing (n = 50/79; 63.3%). For pre‐ and post‐test counseling respectively, participants reported that counseling was performed most often by neurologists (n = 39/75, 52%; n = 42/76, 55.26%), GCs (n = 29/75, 38.67%; n = 26/76, 34.21%), and clinical geneticists (n = 6/75, 8%; n = 7/76, 9.21%).

Barriers

The most frequently reported barriers to offering pre‐ and post‐test counseling were related to lack of access to a GC. This included 81% of respondents (n = 64/79) reporting a long wait time for an appointment with a GC (m = 3.33) and the same number reporting a lack of insurance coverage for GC appointments (m = 3.23). The third most frequently cited barrier was lack of access to a GC (m = 3.1). This was reported by 70.9% of respondents (n = 56/79), the majority of whom (n = 39/56; 69.6%) rated this barrier as a 4 or a 5.

When considering neurologist involvement in pre‐ and post‐test counseling, adult neurologists rated the barriers of limited expertise (t[75] = −3.23, p = 0.002) and inadequate time (t[75] = −2.38, p = 0.02) significantly higher than pediatric neurologists. DISCUSSION

In 2022, the NSGC published the first evidence‐based practice guideline for genetic testing and counseling for patients with unexplained epilepsy. This guideline recommends that every patient with unexplained epilepsy receive pre‐ and post‐test counseling—including test selection, ordering, and interpretation—from a qualified health care professional. 10 Participants reported that counseling is offered to patients with unexplained epilepsy most of the time, with post‐test counseling more likely to be offered than pre‐test counseling. Although most participants receive counseling, the importance of these counseling sessions should continue to be emphasized to facilitate informed decision making, ensure result understanding, and provide emotional support during the pre‐and post‐testing period. 14

The 2022 NSGC guideline also recommends that genetic testing be offered to all patients with unexplained epilepsy. 10 This recommendation to offer testing to all age groups is a shift from previous research, which emphasized the utility of testing in infancy and childhood and when comorbidities were present. 2 , 20 Physicians may judge the likelihood of a genetic etiology for epilepsy based on factors such as positive family history, dysmorphic features, age of onset, and presence of a neurodevelopmental disability (NDD) or developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE); however, a literature review was only able to associate DEE and NDD with increased diagnostic yield due to a lack of reported clinical data for the other factors. 1 Additionally, as one participant commented and in the experience of the authors, adults may be less likely to be offered genetic testing if their epilepsy is being managed successfully. Our study indicates that adult neurologists are significantly more likely than pediatric neurologists to only offer genetic testing to patients with epilepsy for whom they suspect a genetic etiology. However, this practice may lead to missed genetic diagnoses in the adult population. Many adults did not have genetic testing available when they were children, and symptoms can evolve so that adults who once had the characteristic features of a genetic epilepsy syndrome no longer do, and many past publications do not include enough phenotypic data to help ascertain which features lead to a higher yield. 1 , 21

Adult neurologists rated the barrier of limited clinical utility significantly higher than pediatric neurologists. However, studies have noted gene‐specific treatment changes or other clinically actionable findings in 17%–55.5% of adult patients with a genetic diagnosis. 5 , 21 While earlier age of seizure onset is correlated with a higher yield of genetic testing, 2 , 3 past studies examining the diagnostic yield of exome sequencing have demonstrated that even those with the onset of seizures in adulthood had a diagnostic yield between 3.7% and 20%, although for the latter the sample size was small. 4 , 5

Furthermore, in addition to the barrier of limited clinical utility, adult neurologists also rated almost every other barrier to genetic testing significantly higher than pediatric neurologists. These barriers include logistics of test coordination, time involvement, possibility of incidental or uncertain findings, lack of experience with the consenting process, lack of institutional and practice guidelines, lack of understanding of test limitations, and unavailable/inaccessible genetic tests. These barriers are consistent with the challenges noted by other studies and in other specialties. 14 , 16 , 17 , 19 , 22 , 23 , 24 The difference between adult neurologist practices for genetic testing and the 2022 NSGC guideline recommendation appears to be due to a combination of lack of awareness of the NSGC guideline and existing barriers to implementation. As the barriers that were more significant for adult neurologists centered around lack of genetic counseling and logistical knowledge, greater integration of GCs and relevant education into adult neurology clinics may help mitigate this discrepancy.

The biggest barrier to genetic testing reported was billing and insurance logistics. While insurance policies and level of coverage for genetic testing vary widely, participants' responses indicated a clear disconnect between the coverage offered and the coverage needed to allow testing to align with evidence‐based recommendations. In the authors' experience, many providers are uncertain about what coverage is offered in advance of ordering testing, and often a claim needs to be submitted to determine which cases are covered. In the United States, genetic counselors are not currently recognized by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, so genetic counseling services from genetic counselors are not reimbursed. 25 Previous research noted that child neurologists find it more difficult to get epilepsy genetic testing covered for pediatric beneficiaries of Medicaid compared to those with commercial insurance, and that Medicaid is less likely to pay for an epilepsy panel or exome sequencing than for microarray or single gene testing. 16 This difficulty is seen in the oncology space as well, with 23% of cancer‐related claims for next‐generation sequencing for patients on Medicare denied. 26 However, one participant commented that in their pediatric neurology space, there are “minimal barriers for Medicaid patients” and “difficulty for commercially insured patients.” The current limited public and private insurance coverage is a barrier to guideline implementation and illustrates inconsistency in patient care which could lead to healthcare disparities.

Another component of the 2022 NSGC guideline is the recommendation that ES/GS or MGP be offered as the first‐tier test to patients with unexplained epilepsy. 10 ES/GS is conditionally recommended over MGP in epilepsy cases due to its higher diagnostic yield and ability to include informative relatives. 10 Nearly all participants indicated that they order MGP or ES/GS most often. However, those who offered MGP most often far outweighed those who offered ES/GS most often, which may indicate the existence of barriers specific to ES/GS. When genetic testing is offered, comprehensive testing at the level of at least an MGP is available, but it is still necessary to increase the availability of ES/GS.

Lack of access to a GC was a highly rated barrier for both pediatric and adult neurologists for both genetic testing and counseling. In fact, most of those who rated access to a GC as a barrier to testing or counseling rated it as a 4 or 5 (the two highest ratings), indicating that this is a major obstacle for a subset of respondents. In addition to lack of access to a GC, the barriers to counseling included a long wait time for a GC, lack of insurance coverage for GC appointments, limited expertise, and limited time to provide counseling. Considering that most participants indicated that they either order testing without a GC or refer to a genetics specialist for a separate appointment, and that neurologists are the ones to most often provide genetic counseling, increasing the number of GCs in the neurology space could be one method of streamlining patient care while making genetic testing and counseling more accessible and comprehensive, particularly for adult patients. 16 , 24

Study limitations

The sample size of this study was small, with only 82 respondents included in the data analysis. These responses may not be representative of the entirety of the epileptology community, but they offer a valuable first glimpse into neurologists' current practices since the publication of the 2022 NSGC guideline. It is also possible that the NSGC guideline has simply not yet had enough time to be effectively implemented, and we recommend regularly reassessing barriers to epilepsy genetic testing and counseling.

Most participants practiced at academic medical centers in urban areas, and some participants were recruited through GC colleagues. Therefore, our study population is likely made up of the neurologists most likely to have access to GCs and to genetic testing. Additionally, an ascertainment bias is likely present, with those most interested in genetic testing (and therefore more likely to be up to date on guidelines) also being most likely to respond. However, we did not ask whether in‐house genetic testing was offered at participants' workplaces, which could be a factor in accessibility. We also have no way of knowing how many participants work at the same institution and therefore have access to similar information or practice similar protocols, potentially skewing the results.

Also, participants were provided with background on the 2022 NSGC guideline at the beginning of the survey, which could have influenced their responses to align more with the guideline. Finally, adult neurologists were asked about their current practices for “patients with unexplained epilepsy.” This did not differentiate between patients with pediatric‐onset epilepsy and adult‐onset epilepsy, which could impact interpretation of our results. For example, it is possible that genetic testing offered by adult neurologists is primarily offered to those with pediatric‐onset epilepsy, in which case those with adult‐onset epilepsy have even less access to testing than it seems.

Barriers to genetic testing and counseling for patients with unexplained epilepsy are likely even more significant than reported in our study.

Future directions

Future directions for similar research include assessing barriers and facilitators to incorporating more GCs into neurology clinics and investigating ways of integrating support tools or other methods to achieve efficient yet thorough patient care. Additionally, we can continue to spread awareness of the 2022 NSGC guideline by promoting it at conferences (particularly those with a high adult neurologist attendance), discussing it with colleagues, adding it to society websites, and/or sending out a summary within the newsletter or listserv of professional organizations, among other strategies.

CONCLUSION

When genetic testing is able to be offered to patients with unexplained epilepsy, the level of testing is broad and aligns with the 2022 NSGC guideline. However, ES/GS is not offered as frequently as MGP. Barriers like insurance coverage and lack of access to GCs prevent genetic testing from being offered to every patient with unexplained epilepsy. Barriers to testing and counseling were found to be more significant for adult neurologists than pediatric neurologists, indicating a need for increased networks between adult neurologists and genetics providers. Additionally, only slightly more than half of respondents were aware of the 2022 NSGC guideline.

Pre‐ and post‐test counseling is almost universally offered to patients with unexplained epilepsy and is most often provided by neurologists, followed by GCs. However, notable barriers included access to and wait time for a GC, which demonstrates a need for greater availability of GCs who specialize in epilepsy.
