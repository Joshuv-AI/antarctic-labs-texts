Comparative Analysis of Impact of Different Skill-training Methods among Medical Students: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Charushila Rukadikar, Shaista Saiyad, Neeraj Mahajan, Sangeeta Gupta, Arani Das, Anil Gangwar, Avinash Singh, Atul Rukadikar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:The development of practical skills is crucial in medical education, with various teaching-learning (T/L) methods such as Demonstration-Observation-Assistance-Performance (DOAP), Peyton’s four-step approach, and See One Do One Teach One (SO-DO-TO) being employed to enhance students’ competencies in handling medical devices.Objective:This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of DOAP, Peyton’s approach and SO-DO-TO methods on skill performance of 1st-year MBBS students in using a sphygmomanometer, audiometer, and electrocardiogram (ECG).Methodology:A cross-sectional study was conducted among 126 1st-year MBBS students, who were randomly divided into three groups (n = 42). Each group was trained using one of the three T/L methods on specified competencies. Skill acquisition was assessed through an Objective Structured Practical Examination (OSPE), and perceptions were gauged through a Likert Scale questionnaire.Results:Students trained using Peyton’s method scored the highest in OSPE with a mean score of 7.35 (standard deviation [SD] = 2.19), compared to 5.72 (SD = 1.62) for DOAP and 5.83 (SD = 1.37) for SO-DO-TO. The differences in scores were statistically significant (P < 0.01) between the groups. Perception data indicated that both students and faculty rated Peyton’s method highest in overall satisfaction, effectiveness, and comfort, despite its slightly higher time consumption.Conclusion:Peyton’s four-step approach significantly enhances medical students’ skill acquisition and satisfaction compared to DOAP and SO-DO-TO methods. Integrating multiple teaching strategies, as seen in Peyton’s method, can offer a more comprehensive and effective learning experience. Future studies should investigate the long-term impacts and applicability of these methods across various medical disciplines.

Introduction

The development of skills is an essential component of the medical education system and is the most effective method for evaluating the overall performance of MBBS students.[1] Despite the fact that interaction between teachers and students is one of the most effective ways to improve students’ performance, there remains a significant variation in students’ ability to perform common bedside clinical procedures.[2] These procedures include the handling of sphygmomanometers, audiometers, and electrocardiographs.[3] To enhance the learning experience of MBBS students within the current framework of the medical education system, a variety of instructional strategies can be utilized.[4]

Among these strategies are the Demonstrate, Observe, Assist, and Perform (DOAP) method, Peyton’s four-step strategy, and the See One Do One Teach One (SO-DO-TO) technique. The DOAP method involves the trainer first demonstrating the skill, then having the trainee observe it, followed by assisting the trainee in executing the skill.[5] Walker and Peyton introduced a modern method for the acquisition of functional skills, which includes four stages: demonstration, deconstruction, comprehension, and performance.[6] Recent studies have highlighted Peyton’s approach as particularly effective in medical training, demonstrating superior skill acquisition and retention compared to traditional methods. This is largely due to its systematic breakdown of tasks, which reduces cognitive load and enhances learning outcomes.[67]

The SO-DO-TO method is the model designed to progress trainees from beginner practitioners to competent professionals through graded responsibility. This teaching system, exemplified by the phrase “SO-DO-TO,” aims to facilitate skill acquisition through incremental steps.[8] Recent evaluations have shown that the SO-DO-TO method is particularly effective in surgical education, where it enhances both technical skills and learner confidence by integrating teaching as a final step. The continuous engagement with the material through teaching helps to reinforce knowledge and skills, making it an efficient model for long-term retention.[910]

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of various teaching-learning (T/L) tools (DOAP, Peyton’s Approach, and the SO-DO-TO Model) on the skill performance of students in handling a sphygmomanometer, audiometer, and electrocardiogram (ECG) device. In addition, this research investigated the effectiveness of these teaching and learning strategies in terms of students’ knowledge, skill acquisition, and perception of the methods. The study also assessed the perceptions of faculty members and students regarding the three different approaches.

Methodology

The current study was conducted among 1st-year MBBS students. The Institutional Ethical Committee approved this cross-sectional study, ensuring that ethical standards were maintained throughout the research process. Participants were included and excluded based on the specific criteria, and their written consent was obtained after individual counseling regarding the study. Confidentiality was strictly upheld throughout the study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study included 1st-year MBBS students who were selected as participants. However, students who were either unwilling or unavailable to participate in the study were excluded. In addition, those who had previously received formal skill training in the use of medical instruments such as an electrocardiogram, audiometer, and sphygmomanometer were also excluded to ensure that the study results were not influenced by prior knowledge or experience in these areas. This approach ensured a more accurate assessment of the training’s effectiveness among students without prior exposure to these specific skills.

Randomization

A total of 126 1st-year MBBS students, including both sexes, were selected and randomly divided into three groups (A, B, and C; n = 42 each) using a computer-generated randomization list. This ensured that each group had an equal number of participants and that the assignment of teaching methods was unbiased.

A total of 126 1st-year MBBS students, including both sexes, who fulfilled the inclusion criteria were selected and divided into three groups (A, B, and C; n = 42). Following informed consent, three different competencies were taught: handling a sphygmomanometer, audiometer, and electrocardiograph, and interpreting an electrocardiogram. These competencies were taught using three different T/L tools: the DOAP method, Peyton’s approach, and the SO-DO-TO T/L tool technique. The distribution of these tools across the groups is outlined in Tables 1 and 2. The study utilized a cross-sectional design, gathering data from a wide range of participants at a single point in time.

Training duration and session frequency

Each group underwent training for a total period of 3 weeks. With one session per week, this resulted in a total of three sessions per method. Each session, lasting 90 min, was carefully designed to provide a balanced mix of theoretical knowledge, demonstration, and hands-on practice, tailored to the specific teaching method being utilized.

Assessment of training effectiveness

After teaching, the students were assessed for the effectiveness of training in terms of knowledge, skill acquisition, and perception of the three T/L methods in the following ways:

Skill acquisition assessment

After the training, students were assessed on their skill acquisition through an Objective Structured Practical Examination (OSPE). The OSPE checklist used for the assessment was developed by subject matter experts in medical education. To ensure its validity, the checklist was subjected to a Delphi process involving a panel of three experts who reviewed and refined the items based on relevance, clarity, and comprehensiveness. This iterative process continued until consensus was achieved among the experts. The checklist was further validated through a pilot study involving a small group of students, where inter-rater reliability was tested, showing a high degree of agreement (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.85), confirming the checklist’s reliability and consistency in assessing the students’ performance.

Perception of students and faculty toward teaching-learning methods

The perception questionnaire was prepared in the form of a Likert scale to gauge the students’ and faculty members’ perceptions of the three different T/L methods.[1234567]

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS software version 25.0. (IBM Corp. Released 2017. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations, were calculated to summarize the data. The effectiveness of the different T/L methods was compared using the one-way ANOVA, which was chosen due to its ability to assess differences in means across multiple groups. For the analysis of perception data collected through the Likert scale questionnaire, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used, as this nonparametric test is appropriate for comparing ordinal data across more than two independent groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests. These statistical methods were selected to ensure robust and accurate comparisons across the different teaching methods, taking into account the nature of the data and the study design.

Results

Three groups of 42 students each were randomly assigned to a total of 126 students for this study. The students were skilled by three T/L methods for handling of medical devices, and at end of training, they were evaluated for skill acquisition through OSPE, and for perception of student and faculty toward T/L method by filling 10 Liker Scale questionnaire.

Statistical significance

The P values for the comparisons between the groups were as follows:

DOAP versus PEYTON: P =0.032

DOAP versus SO-DO-TO: P =0.045

PEYTON versus SO-DO-TO: P =0.058

The results indicate that the PEYTON method resulted in significantly higher scores compared to the DOAP method (P < 0.05). The effect size, measured using Cohen’s d, was 0.65, suggesting a moderate to large effect size. The SO-DO-TO technique also showed a significant improvement over DOAP, with a small to moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.43).

Perception of the students toward different teaching-learning method

Students demonstrated a strong preference for Peyton’s four-step method, giving it the highest ratings in terms of overall satisfaction, efficiency, and ease of use. Although it required more time to complete, 37% of students reported being highly satisfied with Peyton’s approach, compared to 36% for DOAP and only 20% for SO-DO-TO. Students found Peyton’s method to be particularly effective for skill development because of its clear structure and step-by-step breakdown, which facilitated their understanding of more complicated tasks. While the DOAP method was appreciated for its thoroughness, students felt it took too long, and the SO-DO-TO method, although practical, was rated lower in terms of overall satisfaction.

Perception of faculties toward different teaching learning tools

Faculty members also preferred Peyton’s four-step method, giving it the highest scores for engagement, effectiveness, and overall satisfaction. They found that this method promoted greater student comprehension and interaction, making teaching sessions more dynamic and effective. However, they noted that the longer time commitment required for Peyton’s method presented a challenge. Despite the benefits of the DOAP and SO-DO-TO methods, the faculty valued Peyton’s structured and interactive approach, finding it more successful in helping students acquire and master complex skills.

Discussion

The findings from our study demonstrate the effectiveness of different T/L methods in enhancing the practical skills of medical students, particularly in handling medical devices. The results suggest that the Peyton, which combines the elements of both SO-TO-DO teaching method and Demonstration, Observation, Assistance, and Practice (DOAP) methods, is superior in terms of skill acquisition and student satisfaction compared to using either method alone.

Skill acquisition

As shown in Table 3, the skill assessments using various teaching-learning tools demonstrate differences in performance among the groups. Students trained using the Peyton method scored significantly higher on the OSPE than those trained with either the SO-TO-DO or DOAP methods alone. The Peyton method group had an average score of 7.35, compared to 5.72 for DOAP and 5.83 for SO-TO-DO indicating that the integration of both methods provides a more comprehensive learning experience. This finding aligns with previous research indicating that mixed-method teaching approaches can enhance the learning outcomes by addressing various learning styles and providing multiple contexts for practice and application.[811]

Moreover, the Peyton method demonstrated a lower coefficient of variation (30%) compared to DOAP (28%) and SO-TO-DO (23%), suggesting more consistent performance among students. This consistency could be attributed to the Peyton method’s structured yet flexible approach, which allows students to grasp complex concepts and procedures more effectively.[12]

Confounding factors and control measures

Potential confounding factors in this study include variability in student prior knowledge, motivation, and differences in faculty teaching styles. To control for these factors, randomization was used to assign students to the different T/L methods, thereby minimizing the impact of individual differences. In addition, the same faculty members taught each method across different groups to reduce variability due to teaching styles. Despite these measures, it is acknowledged that complete elimination of all confounding factors is challenging in educational research.

Student perceptions

Figure 1 illustrates the student perceptions toward the different teaching-learning methods. The student perception data further support the effectiveness of the Peyton method. Students rated this method highest in terms of overall satisfaction, effectiveness, and comfort. Notably, 37% of students expressed high satisfaction with the Peyton method, compared to 20% for SO-TO-DO and 36% for DOAP. This positive feedback is critical as it correlates with higher engagement and motivation, which are essential for deep learning and skill retention.[13]

However, it is important to note that students found the DOAP method to be more time-consuming than the other methods. This perception could be due to the detailed and comprehensive nature of the DOAP approach, which requires more time for thorough understanding and practice. Balancing the depth of content with time efficiency will be a crucial consideration for future implementations.[14]

Faculty perceptions

Figure 2 presents the faculty perceptions towards different teaching Learning methodologies. Faculty feedback also favored the Peyton method, rating it highest in terms of interest, effectiveness, and overall satisfaction. Faculty members noted that this method facilitated better student understanding and engagement, allowing for more interactive and responsive teaching sessions. This finding is consistent with the constructivist learning theory, which emphasizes the importance of active learning and teacher-student interaction in constructing knowledge.[15]

Faculty also highlighted the need to manage the additional time required for the peyton method. Efficient scheduling and potentially integrating time-management strategies within the framework could help mitigate this concern without compromising the quality of education.[8]

Our study’s outcomes are consistent with the existing literature advocating for blended learning approaches in medical education. Blended learning, which combines traditional and modern teaching methods, has been shown to improve knowledge retention, practical skills, and learner satisfaction.[11] The Peyton’s method in our study represents a form of blended learning, leveraging the strengths of SO-TO-DO and DOAP’s methodologies.

The positive outcomes associated with the Peyton method also resonate with the findings from previous studies that emphasize the benefits of integrating multiple teaching strategies to cater to diverse learning needs and enhance overall educational outcomes.[14]

Limitations and future directions

While our study provides valuable insights into the effectiveness of different T/L methods, it has some limitations. The sample size, although sufficient to detect significant differences, may not fully represent the diversity of medical student populations. In addition, the controlled environment of the study may not fully capture the variability of real-world clinical settings. The short duration of the intervention and follow-up are another limitation, as it does not allow for the assessment of long-term retention of skills. Future research should explore the Peyton method’s applicability across various medical disciplines, with a focus on its long-term impact on clinical competence and its effectiveness in more diverse and less controlled settings. In addition, investigating the impact of integrating technology with these teaching methods could offer new insights into enhancing medical education.

Practical implications for medical educators

The outcomes of this study suggest that the Peyton method can be a highly effective tool in medical education, particularly in skill-intensive courses. Medical educators should consider integrating this method into their curriculum to improve student outcomes. The structured nature of the Peyton method, which combines demonstration, deconstruction, comprehension, and practice, provides a balanced approach that caters to various learning styles and ensures a deeper understanding of complex procedures. However, educators must also be mindful of the time demands of this method and explore the ways to integrate it efficiently into the existing curriculum without overloading students or faculty. In conclusion, our study demonstrates that the Peyton teaching method significantly enhances medical students’ skill acquisition and satisfaction. By integrating the best aspects of SO-DO-TO and DOAP methods, the Peyton model provides a comprehensive and effective learning experience, preparing students for the complexities of clinical practice.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that the Peyton teaching method significantly enhances medical students’ skill acquisition and satisfaction. By integrating the best aspects of SO-DO-TO and DOAP methods, the Peyton model provides a comprehensive and effective learning experience, preparing students for the complexities of clinical practice.
