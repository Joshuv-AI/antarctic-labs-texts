The relationship between public health emergencies and suicidal ideation: the mediating role of fear of missing out and the moderating role of conscientiousness
Authors: Zhenjing Tan, Bofeng He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPublic health emergencies have been found to be significantly associated with mental health outcomes, yet their association with suicidal ideation remains insufficiently explored. The present study aimed to examine the cross-sectional association between public health emergencies and suicidal ideation among college students, with a particular focus on the potential mediating role of fear of missing out (FoMO) and the moderating role of conscientiousness.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 1,012 college students from a university in Guangdong Province, China. Participants completed questionnaires assessing exposure to information about public health emergencies, FoMO, suicidal ideation, and the Big Five Personality Traits Inventory. The PROCESS macro in SPSS was used to test the mediation and moderation effects.ResultsThe results revealed that (1) public health emergencies were positively associated with suicidal ideation; (2) FoMO mediated the relationship between public health emergencies and suicidal ideation; and (3) The moderating effect of conscientiousness was significant, such that the positive association between public health emergencies and FoMO was stronger at higher levels of conscientiousness, whereas the positive association between FoMO and suicidal ideation was weaker at higher levels of conscientiousness.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that public health emergencies and suicidal ideation are associated through FoMO. Conscientiousness exhibits a two-stage moderating effect in the pathways from public health emergencies to FoMO and subsequently from FoMO to suicidal ideation. The study highlights that addressing FoMO is associated with lower levels of suicidal ideation among college students, and suggests integrating conscientiousness into mental health interventions during public health emergencies. Future longitudinal or experimental research is needed to establish causal directions.

Introduction

Public health emergencies refer to sudden outbreaks of major infectious diseases, unexpected public health crises, mass illnesses of unknown causes, major food and occupational poisonings, and other events that seriously affect public health [67]. In recent years, there has been an increase in the frequency of public health emergencies.Beginning in January 2020, the novel coronavirus (COVID-19) spread globally and was declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern by the World Health Organization. Over the following three years, the COVID-19 virus exerted a profound impact on global public health. According to WHO statistics, as of May 2023, there had been over 760 million confirmed cases and 6.9 million deaths worldwide [73]. From May 2022 onward, the emergence and spread of the mpox (formerly monkeypox) outbreak across multiple countries also drew significant public attention and concern. In addition to such acute public health crises as COVID-19 and mpox, seasonal epidemics including influenza and dengue fever continue to cause clustered outbreaks and fatalities each year.These emergencies pose a significant threat to people's lives and health, and have a considerable impact on their daily life and socio-economic aspects.

The rapid dissemination of information about public health emergencies via the internet and social media has enabled individuals to easily access and track developments in real-time. This has led to a rise in anxiety and fear among individuals concerned about their safety and well-being. During the global COVID-19 pandemic, the general population experienced increased mental health symptoms, notably anxiety and depression [57, 78].

Public health emergencies and suicidal ideation

Public health emergencies pose a significant threat to health and have a profound impact on individuals' physical and mental well-being [7, 11, 62]. During the outbreak of public health emergencies, a wide range of information is rapidly disseminated online. As the amount of information about the public health emergencies to which people are exposed increases, their perceptions of the health risks posed by the emergency will deepen, resulting in heightened anxiety and fear [60].

Suicidal ideation is an early psychological phenomenon observed in individuals who have attempted suicide or died by suicide. It is a critical indicator for assessing suicide risk, which refers to thoughts and plans of suicide without actual attempts [5, 28]. Suicidal ideation is prevalent among college students [48]. This population also exhibits a higher suicide risk relative to other age groups [5, 36]. During public health emergencies, the mental health of college students deteriorates, with increased symptoms of depression and anxiety, and even the emergence of suicidal ideation [82].

The Stressor-Strain-Outcome (SSO) model is a theoretical framework that explains how external stressors lead to psychological or physical strain, which in turn influences individual and organizational outcomes [44]. In the SSO model, a stressor refers to external or environmental demands that elicit a stress response; strain denotes the resulting negative psychological or physiological state experienced by the individual; and the outcome represents behavioral, emotional, or organizational consequences that follow from prolonged strain [44]. In other words, the source of the stressful stimulus creates a corresponding burden on the individual, which in turn leads to the individual adopting the corresponding behavior or performance. In accordance with the SSO model, public health emergencies pose a significant health risks. As stressors, they can lead to significant psychological burdens and trigger fear. Suicidal ideation may emerge as a behavioral manifestation when individuals face the threat of public health emergencies.

Repeated exposure to crisis-related narratives has been shown to heighten fear and increase the risk of suicidal ideation [25]. When individuals are exposed to extensive information about public health emergencies, their psychological distress and fear may intensify, potentially increasing the risk of suicidal ideation.

Based on the aforementioned evidence, it was hypothesized that there was a positive relationship between public health emergencies and suicidal ideation.

The role of FoMO in the relationship between public health emergencies and suicidal ideation

Public health emergencies pose a significant threat to individuals' health and well-being, leading to increased public attention and a surge in demand for health risk information [8, 26, 63]. Research has shown that individuals are more likely to experience fear of missing out when exposed to major events [33]. The health risks linked to public health emergencies, combined with heightened public concern, increase individuals' tendency to worry and fear losing access to relevant and useful information. This anxiety and fear drive individuals to spend more time seeking relevant information. If sufficient information about health risks is not readily available, individuals may experience anxiety about missing critical information, further exacerbating their fear of missing out.

Fear of missing out (FoMO) is defined as a pervasive apprehension that others are having rewarding experiences from which one is absent, and a desire to stay connected to what others are doing [56]. Self-determination theory suggests that individuals make decisions based on experience when they are well informed about their environment and information, highlighting the dynamic role of intrinsic needs [14]. FoMO stems from the absence of basic psychological needs [15, 71, 77]. During public health emergencies, individuals spend more time seeking relevant information to address their health and safety concerns and reduce anxiety and fear. However, excessive exposure to public health emergency information may intensify the FoMO about critical updates, particularly due to the constant accessibility of digital media [43].

Individuals exhibiting high levels of FoMO are more likely to experience heightened anxiety and depression [1, 15, 17, 19]. Fear of missing out may indirectly contribute to suicidal ideation by exacerbating social comparison stress, loneliness, and dissatisfaction with one’s current circumstances. The social comparison theory posits that individuals engage in a process of comparison with others, which may culminate in either positive or negative emotional experiences [21]. FoMO reinforces this comparative process, leading individuals to focus excessively on the positive experiences of others. This focus often results in dissatisfaction and frustration with one’s own life. When such feelings become overwhelming, individuals may develop suicidal ideation as an extreme coping mechanism [40, 42, 68].

Based on the preceding analysis, it can be inferred that public health emergencies may impact suicidal ideation via the following pathway: public health emergencies → FoMO → suicidal ideation. In light of these findings, we propose that FoMO serves as a mediating variable in the relationship between public health emergencies and suicidal ideation among college students.

The role of personality trait in the relationship between public health emergencies and suicidal ideation

Public health emergencies are well-documented to induce anxiety and even panic. However, research indicates that individuals with varying personality traits may respond differently to major events. For example, a study found that post-traumatic symptoms experienced by individuals during the COVID-19 pandemic varied by personality traits [27]. Individuals with high neuroticism levels tend to exhibit stronger emotional reactions to stressful situations, which may increase their risk of mental health issues.With regard to personality traits, individuals with neurotic personalities tend to exhibit heightened sensitivity, a proclivity for pessimism, and pronounced mood swings [38, 65, 81]. This group is more prone to experiencing negative emotions, such as anxiety and unease, in relation to the potential health risks associated with public health emergencies. Individuals prone to psychological crises exhibit specific personality traits, including superficiality and negativity, excessive introversion, indecisiveness and hesitancy, emotional instability, low self-confidence, excessive dependence on others, and impulsive behaviour [18].

It has been proposed that rational individuals can avoid suicidal impulses by altering their cognition, regulating emotions, maintaining rationality, optimism, and bravery, and taking proactive measures to seek help [79]. Rationality can intercept the progression from suicidal ideation to suicidal behavior, acting as a protective factor against suicidal acts, while impulsive traits serve as risk factors for suicidal behavior. The stress-diathesis model of suicidal behaviours posits that an individual's likelihood of suicidal ideation and suicidal behaviours is contingent upon their level of impulsivity [49]. Specifically, individuals exhibiting a high level of impulsivity are at a greater risk of engaging in suicidal behaviours than those with a low level of impulsivity.

Studies have shown that positive personality traits can mitigate the impact of events on individuals and serve as a negative predictor of suicidal ideation [42, 52, 69]. Conscientiousness moderates the presence of suicidal ideation. Specifically, among individuals with low levels of conscientiousness, there is a notable positive correlation between Suicide Crisis Syndrome and short-term suicidal behaviors [22]. Conversely, for those with moderate to high levels of conscientiousness, this correlation is insignificant. These traits are linked to the employment of mature defense mechanisms, such as emotional support and proactive coping strategies, which may help to alleviate stress and traumatic symptoms [27]. However, a recent study has found that higher conscientiousness is significantly associated with increased risk of recent suicidal behaviors among older adults with depression [64]. In contrast, some research has found no significant association between conscientiousness and suicidal ideation [34]. Given these inconsistent findings, the role of conscientiousness in suicide risk remains uncertain and warrants further investigation.

As discussed above, evidence suggests that an individual's personality traits considerably influence their ability to cope with situational stress associated with major events. Furthermore, research suggests a strong correlation between public health emergencies and personality traits, which also play a significant role in the development of suicidal thoughts. Therefore, it is proposed that different personality traits will have markedly different impacts on the effect of public health emergencies on suicidal ideation.

We hypothesize that conscientiousness, one of the Big Five personality traits, may mediate the relationship between exposure to information about sudden public health events and suicidal ideation, thereby reducing the negative impact of sudden public health events on suicidal ideation.

Aims

During the summer and autumn months each year, southern China enters a high-incidence period for intestinal infectious diseases, such as hand, foot, and mouth disease and norovirus infection, as well as for dengue fever and seasonal influenza. These diseases are particularly prone to clustered transmission in densely populated school settings. Meanwhile, sporadic cases of COVID-19 have also been reported in the region recently. Information about these emergencies spreads widely and rapidly through the internet and social media. Existing research has confirmed the negative impact of public health emergencies on individuals, including suicidal ideation [30, 41, 61]. When a public health emergency occurs, people have a strong need to seek information and widely collect and share relevant information through the internet and social media [3, 66]. Individuals who frequently use the internet and social media are prone to FoMO. A literature review revealed that the relationship between public health emergencies, FoMO, and suicidal ideation remains unclear.

Research indicates that personality traits may play a role in suicidal ideation, but the findings are not conclusive. Some studies suggest that conscientiousness moderates suicidal ideation [22] and negatively predicts suicidal ideation [69]; others find that conscientiousness is positively correlated with suicidal ideation [64] or that there is no association between conscientiousness and suicidal ideation [34].

Therefore, this study will explore the relationship between public health emergencies, FoMO, suicidal ideation, and conscientiousness, providing empirical evidence for college student support and mental health education.

Hypothesis of the current study

This study employs the Stressor-Strain-Outcome (SSO) model as a framework to establish a moderated mediation model to explore the relationship between public health emergencies, FoMO, suicidal ideation, and conscientiousness. Public health emergencies, as stressors, may be associated with suicidal ideation through FoMO, with conscientiousness playing a moderating role.

Method

Participants and procedure

In the autumn of 2023, a survey was conducted among 1,136 college students aged 19–24 at Guangdong Medical University in Guangdong Province, China. The public health emergencies examined in this study encompass concentrated outbreaks of COVID-19, influenza, dengue fever, and other health-threatening events. Students self-reported their behaviors and feelings during these public health emergencies in the past 3 months through a survey questionnaire. A total of 1,136 questionnaires were collected, with those containing more than three unanswered questions or more than 20 responses deemed invalid. Based on this criterion, a total of 124 invalid questionnaires were excluded, resulting in 1012 valid questionnaires, with a questionnaire validity rate of 89.08%. Among the valid samples, there were 395 male students (39.03%) and 617 female students (60.97%).

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Medical University (#202,310,002). The researcher provided students with an informed consent form that explained the purpose of the study, the basis for voluntary participation, and a commitment to data protection. Participation in the study was subject to the students' consent.

Measure

Public health emergencies

In this study, suicidal ideation, fear of missing out (FoMO), and conscientiousness represent individual psychological traits, whereas public health emergencies are objective external events, serving as environmental stimuli or stressors encountered by individuals. Previous research in psychology on public health emergencies has primarily investigated the relationships between individual psychology and behavior within the context of such events, without treating public health emergencies themselves as measurable variables [7, 41, 50, 62]. This study, however, treats public health emergencies as a variable to explore their relationship with other variables and the underlying mechanisms. To examine the interrelationships among variables within a unified psychometric framework, it is necessary to transform this external event into a psychological representation that can be perceived and evaluated by individuals, thereby maintaining structural homogeneity with the other psychological variables. Consequently, a psychological-behavioral indicator is required to serve as a proxy for the public health emergency.

According to cognitive appraisal theory, an external event must undergo individual cognitive appraisal to become a psychologically meaningful stressor [46]. In the context of a public health emergency, information serves as the primary medium for this appraisal. If an individual is entirely unexposed to information related to the event, the event, despite its objective existence, does not enter their cognitive system and is therefore unlikely to trigger psychological or behavioral responses. Hence, using information exposure as the measurement indicator essentially captures the psychological impact of the event after it has been processed cognitively by the individual.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the WHO introduced the concept of an "infodemic," highlighting that during a disease outbreak, an overabundance of information – including false or misleading content – spreads rapidly and widely in both virtual cyberspace and the physical environment [74]. Individuals' experience of a public health emergency is largely shaped by their perception of this infodemic. The impact on people often stems not directly from the event itself, but from the information they are exposed to, process, and internalize. Therefore, using information exposure as a measurement indicator can better reflect the actual psychological impact of public health emergencies on individuals within a highly mediatized social context.

Currently, information regarding public health emergencies (such as COVID-19) is predominantly disseminated through the internet and social media, characterized by faster speed and broader reach [80]. Individuals' access to and the impact of public health emergency information are also highly dependent on these channels. Therefore, this study employs the degree of an individual's exposure to public health emergency information as the quantitative indicator for the public health emergency within this research framework. Drawing on relevant scales such as the revised Impact of Event Scale (IES-R) [29, 83] and scales related to mobile social media dependency [45, 47, 70, 75], a Public Health Emergency Information Exposure Questionnaire was developed.

This study operationally defines information exposure during public health emergencies as the behavior of individuals actively or passively acquiring, processing, and disseminating information through various media during the occurrence of public health emergencies, as well as the physiological and psychological impacts of such information on individuals. It encompasses two dimensions: information exposure behavior and information exposure impact. Survey items

Using a five-point scoring system, where "1" represents "strongly disagree" and "5" represents "strongly agree," each question is a single-choice forced response. Participants were asked to report the extent to which they had been exposed to news or information about public emergencies on social media (WeChat, QQ, Sina Weibo, Tiktok, Zhihu, or Baidu Tieba) and online platforms in the past three months. These public emergencies include COVID-19, influenza, dengue fever, and other health-threatening events. The total score obtained by adding up the 13 items represents the overall exposure to public health emergency information, with a higher score indicating a higher degree of exposure to such information for an individual. This score serves as the quantitative indicator for public health emergencies in this study.

We employed JASP 0.95 to assess the reliability and validity of the questionnaire, using Average Variance Extracted (AVE), Composite Reliability (CR), and Cronbach's α coefficients to evaluate the questionnaire's validity and reliability. The results indicated that the AVE values for Factor 1 and Factor 2 were 0.503 and 0.496, respectively, which are close to or exceed the critical value of 0.5, suggesting that the questionnaire's validity is acceptable [23, 31]. The CR values for Factor 1, Factor 2, and the overall scale were 0.882, 0.775, and 0.930, respectively, all exceeding 0.7 [23]. The Cronbach's α coefficient for this study was 0.863. The results of the reliability and validity assessment demonstrate that the questionnaire meets measurement standards in terms of both reliability and validity.

Fear of missing out

The Fear of Missing Out scale developed by Przybylski (2013) was used to measure the level of fear of missing out. The scale consists of 10 items, including questions such as 'I am afraid that other people have more exciting experiences and rewards than I do' and 'I feel anxious when I don't know what my friends are up to'. The scale is scored on a five-point scale, where 1 is 'not at all' and 5 is 'completely', and the scores for each of the 10 questions are added together to give an overall fear of missing out score, where the higher the score, the greater the fear of missing out. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the Scale was 0.878.

Suicidal ideation

Suicidal ideation was measured using the Suicidal Ideation Self-Assessment Scale [76]. The full scale consists of four factors: despair, optimism, sleep, and masking, and includes 26 items such as 'My daily life is full of things that interest me' and 'I am convinced that life is cruel to me', to which subjects are asked to answer 'yes' or 'no'. Subjects were asked to answer 'yes' or 'no', with 'yes' scoring 1 and 'no' scoring 0. The total score for suicidal ideation was calculated by summing the scores of the despair, optimism and sleep factors. The masking factor was not included in the total suicidal ideation score. The higher the total score, the more suicidal ideation and the higher the risk of suicide. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the Suicidal Ideation scale was 0.786.

Conscientiousness

The Chinese Big Five Personality Inventory brief version (CBF-PI-B; [72]) was used to assess personality traits. The questionnaire consists of 40 items, including five dimensions, namely neuroticism, conscientiousness, agreeableness, openness to experience and extraversion, with eight questions in each dimension. The questions include 'I often worry that something bad will happen' and 'I often make decisions only after careful consideration'. A 6-point scale was used, with 1 being 'very unconventional' and 6 being 'very conventional'. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the conscientiousness subscale from the Chinese Big Five Personality Inventory was 0.811.

Statistical approach

Descriptive statistics and correlation analyses were performed using SPSS 23.0. Moderated mediation analysis was applied using the SPSS-PROCESS program developed [32], and the bias-corrected percentile bootstrap method was used to test the moderated mediation model.

Results

Common method bias examination

To assess the presence of common method bias (CMB) in the current study, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted. The results show that there are 12 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1. The first unrotated factor accounted for 14.26% of the total variance, which is significantly below the 40% threshold commonly used as an indicator of severe CMB [55]. Thus, the results suggest that the data are not substantially affected by common method bias.

Clinical significance of conscientiousness in relation to suicidal ideation

Discussion

Public health emergency affects suicidal ideation

The results indicate that greater exposure to information about public health emergencies among college students was associated with higher levels of suicidal ideation (Eq. 1 in Table 3), thereby supporting Hypothesis 1. The panic and anxiety triggered by the constant exposure to information about public health emergencies are amplified, and the more information people are exposed to, the greater the perceived threat from the public health emergencies, and therefore, the more stress they will experience [53].

The model shows that by incorporating FoMO as a mediator in the pathway connecting public health emergencies to suicidal ideation, the mediation model is established. FoMO functions as a mediator between public health emergencies and suicidal ideation, thus supporting Hypothesis 2. In this context, the direct effect of public health emergencies on suicidal ideation is insignificant. This finding underscores the potential importance of FoMO as a key stress transmission mechanism linking public health emergencies and suicidal ideation.

The potential health risks associated with public health emergencies are associated with increased anxiety about missing out on pertinent information, which in turn is associated with a heightened sense of FoMO. When a public health emergency strikes, diverse information rapidly disseminates through mediums such as the internet, allowing individuals to access this information at any time and from any location. Fearing the loss of critical updates on public health emergencies, individuals continually seek out information online. The utilization of the internet and social media further amplifies the likelihood of experiencing FoMO [17, 24, 35]. Notably, there exists a significant correlation between excessive social media usage and suicidal thoughts [20, 54].

The dynamic transmission mechanism of the SSO model elucidates the psychological and behavioral response process extending from public health emergencies to suicidal ideation. Essentially, public health emergencies (Stressor) are linked to suicidal ideation (Outcome) via the phenomenon of FoMO (Strain). Specifically, information related to public health emergencies, functioning as a stressor, imposes considerable psychological pressure on individuals due to its suddenness, unpredictability, and health risks. Upon encountering such information, individuals, motivated by the need for self-protection, tend to continuously monitor the unfolding situation of public health emergencies and harbor anxieties about missing out on crucial details, thereby readily experiencing FoMO. If this persistent emotional strain remains unaddressed, it will exacerbate both psychological and physiological stressors for individuals, potentially culminating in extreme psychological and behavioral outcomes, such as suicidal ideation.

Conscientiousness moderate the relationship between public health emergencies and suicidal ideation

The model (Fig. 4) demonstrated that conscientiousness moderated both the relationship between public health emergencies and FoMO, and the relationship between FoMO and suicidal ideation, thereby supporting Hypothesis 3. Personality traits have been demonstrated to moderate the conversion from stressors to strain [12, 51]. Our model reveals a two-stage moderating role of conscientiousness in the pathways from public health emergencies to FoMO and subsequently from FoMO to suicidal ideation. Specifically, in the first pathway, high conscientiousness amplifies the link between public health emergencies and FoMO. Conversely, in the second pathway, high conscientiousness attenuates the link between FoMO and suicidal ideation. This apparent contradiction in the two-stage moderating effect of conscientiousness actually underscores the complexity of this personality trait. Indeed, within the Big Five personality model, conscientiousness encompasses various sub-factors, including dutifulness, reliability, orderliness, self-discipline, competence, industriousness, and self-control [10, 13, 58]. Studies in managerial psychology have shown that these different sub-factors within the conscientiousness construct exert diverse influences on job performance and academic achievement [6, 16, 37].

We believe that the complexity inherent in the rigorous structure of conscientiousness gives rise to varied moderating effects across different stages, spanning from public health emergencies, through FoMO, to suicidal ideation. Individuals with high conscientiousness, particularly those characterized by dutifulness and competence, tend to perceive it as their responsibility to thoroughly comprehend the information when confronted with the health threats posed by public health emergencies, empowering themselves to confront these threats and safeguard themselves and their loved ones. Consequently, they harbor concerns about missing any crucial updates regarding health risks and preventive measures [2]. Moreover, the uncertainty and lack of control associated with public health emergencies pose challenges to the inherent needs of these high conscientiousness individuals for orderliness and reliability. In an attempt to dispel this uncertainty, they engage in excessive information gathering to reestablish a sense of control and mitigate the uncertainty [4]. This anxiety over "missing crucial health information" and the tendency to over-collect information are fundamentally expressions of FoMO in health crisis situations.These factors may all increase their susceptibility to experiencing a heightened sense of FoMO when exposed to information about sudden public health events, compared to those with lower conscientiousness.

Although individuals with high conscientiousness are more prone to experiencing FoMO due to exposure to information about public health emergencies, they also possess robust psychological resources that can mitigate the impact of FoMO on suicidal ideation. The factors of self-discipline and self-control, which are integral to conscientiousness, are closely related to self-regulation ability. When individuals experience FoMO in the context of public health emergencies, those with high conscientiousness, due to their stronger self-regulation and self-control abilities, are more adept at using emotional regulation strategies to suppress negative emotions such as anxiety and fear brought on by FoMO, thereby reducing its impact on suicidal ideation. Additionally, individuals with high conscientiousness exhibit greater planning and goal pursuit. Even when FoMO triggers anxiety and fear, their plans and goals for the future constitute a strong psychological defense, preventing fleeting thoughts of despair from turning into suicidal ideation [39, 40].

At the input end of stress (public health emergencies → FoMO), factors such as conscientiousness, competence, self-discipline, and organization cause individuals to overreact when dealing with the threats posed by public health emergencies, thereby amplifying the association between public health emergencies and FoMO. At the output end of stress (FoMO → suicidal ideation), factors such as conscientiousness, self-discipline, and self-control enable individuals to alleviate negative emotions more effectively. The regulatory process of conscientiousness highly aligns with the classic stress-coping theory. When confronted with stress, individuals typically engage in two sequential cognitive appraisal processes: primary appraisal and secondary appraisal [46]. Individuals with high conscientiousness may be more likely to assess the situation as high-threat and high-risk during the primary appraisal stage (public health emergencies → FoMO), leading to a stronger emotional response (FoMO). However, during the secondary appraisal stage, individuals with high conscientiousness evaluate their own coping resources more highly and tend to adopt more adaptive coping strategies, resulting in better outcomes.

In the aforementioned discussion, we analyzed the moderating role of conscientiousness and its potential underlying mechanisms. However, in the present study, the effect size of conscientiousness in the pathway from public health emergencies to FoMO was Cohen’s f2 = 0.011, which is below the threshold for a small effect (f2 = 0.02) [9]. Moreover, the effect size of conscientiousness in the pathway from FoMO to suicidal ideation was Cohen’s f2 = 0.004, substantially lower than the small-effect benchmark. These relatively modest effect sizes indicate that although conscientiousness significantly moderated the aforementioned pathways from a statistical standpoint, the magnitude of its moderating effect was limited, contributing only marginally to the incremental explained variance in the outcome variables. This finding suggests that, in the context of psychological crises triggered by public health emergencies, the moderating influence of conscientiousness may be partially overshadowed by more potent stress-related factors.

Despite the small effect size of the moderation, its practical significance and value should be interpreted within the specific contextual framework [59]. Public health emergencies are macro-level events affecting millions of individuals. Even a variable associated with an incremental f2 of 0.011—which corresponds to approximately 1.1% additional explained variance—may carry non-negligible practical implications at the population level. Furthermore, although conscientiousness demonstrated a limited alteration in the strength of the association between FoMO and suicidal ideation in our sample (Cohen’s f2 = 0.004), from the perspective of suicide prevention, an identified protective factor—even with a very small effect size—remains meaningful and worthy of consideration.

In examining the clinical relevance of conscientiousness to suicidal ideation, the results indicated that the high-conscientiousness group exhibited a significantly lower prevalence of suicidal ideation compared to the low-conscientiousness group (OR = 0.156). From a translational perspective, these findings suggest that suicide prevention intervention programs could consider incorporating behavioral strategies designed to enhance conscientiousness.

Implications and limitation

Implications

This study employed the SSO model to elucidate the relationship between public health emergencies and suicidal ideation, uncovering FoMO as a pivotal mediating mechanism in this connection. By integrating FoMO into the SSO model, we gained a clearer understanding of the interplay between public health emergencies and suicidal ideation.

More crucially, our findings revealed a dual-stage moderating role of conscientiousness within the SSO model, indicating that conscientiousness exerts distinct moderating effects at various stages of the same psychological process. Although the effect size of this moderating effect is small, this discovery broadens the scope of previous research, which posited that conscientiousness solely exhibited a unidirectional protective or risk effect. This intriguing finding offers empirical support for establishing a context- and path-specific moderation model of personality traits.

Our research offers a more profound insight into the psychological transformations undergone by college students in the stressful context of public health emergencies, providing valuable implications for mental health education and student management among them. For example, when public health emergency strikes, college students extensively seek information on the risks associated with such events to fully comprehend and evaluate the health risks. During this period, schools can establish a scientific information dissemination mechanism to prevent information overload or a vacuum, thereby fulfilling students' information needs regarding the risks posed by public health emergencies. Moreover, given the dynamic moderating role of personality in college students' psychological crises, it implies that when conducting mental health education and management, schools can establish an early warning model for psychological crises grounded in the interaction between personality and situation. Currently, campus psychological crisis early warning systems primarily focus on identifying abnormal psychology or behavior that has already manifested. By incorporating personality factors, the early warning model will become more comprehensive.

Limitations

The study has several limitations: (1) The cross-sectional study design limits the inference of causality. Future studies utilizing a longitudinal design are warranted to establish temporal precedence. Specifically, data should be collected at three waves: T1 (initial outbreak), T2 (1–2 months post-outbreak), and T3 (3–6 months post-containment). This multi-wave approach would enable the observation of variable trajectories over time, allowing for a more precise elucidation of the pathway from public health emergency information exposure to suicidal ideation through FoMO. Additionally, it would facilitate the analysis of conscientiousness as a moderator of this mediation process across different phases, offering stronger evidence for causal mechanisms. (2) The measurement of information exposure to public health emergencies was conducted using a self-designed questionnaire. Although the reliability and validity of the questionnaire generally meet the requirements, further in-depth verification is needed in the future to ensure higher reliability and validity of the questionnaire; (3) Conscientiousness has been proven to have multiple sub-factors, and different sub-factors may have different effects on psychological processes. Due to the limitations of the measurement scales used in this study, which were unable to assess the subdimensions of conscientiousness, this research could not provide a mechanism for the moderating effects of these subdimensions. Consequently, the findings rely on inferences and speculations drawn from prior studies. Future research should incorporate measurements of the subdimensions of conscientiousness to further explore their underlying mechanisms. Future research should delve deeper into exploring the mechanisms of action of different sub-factors of conscientiousness. (4) Some potential confounding variables that may influence the research results—such as depression, anxiety, and level of social support—were not included in this study, which may affect the robustness of the model. Future research should control for these potential variables. (5) In this study, although the moderating effect of conscientiousness was statistically significant, its effect size (Cohen’s f2) fell below the threshold for a small effect, indicating a relatively limited absolute contribution in explaining additional variance. The small effect size of the moderating effect of conscientiousness may be attributed, on one hand, to its potential masking by other more dominant stress factors, and on the other hand, to its complex and yet-to-be-clarified subdimensional structure. Therefore, future research should consider incorporating additional factors and measuring the subdimensions of conscientiousness to clarify the relationships among these subdimensions, as well as their associations with variables such as public health emergencies, FoMO, and suicidal ideation.

Conclusion

In summary, the study found that there is a positive correlation between public health emergencies and suicidal ideation. The results indicate that greater exposure to information about public health emergencies is associated with higher levels of FoMO, and FoMO is further associated with stronger suicidal ideation.

The present research also indicates that conscientiousness moderated the associations between public health emergencies and FoMO, as well as between FoMO and suicidal ideation. Specifically, the association between public health emergencies and FoMO was stronger at higher levels of conscientiousness, whereas the association between FoMO and suicidal ideation was weaker at higher levels of conscientiousness. While the moderating effect of conscientiousness is modest in magnitude, it remains noteworthy for highlighting the conditional nature of these relationships.
