Peiminine suppresses prostate cancer progression by regulating cell proliferation, motility, apoptosis, and mitochondrial dysfunction
Authors: Yang Yu, Xiaobo Chen, Fei Xiong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeiminine, an active alkaloid, has been reported to exhibit antitumor properties in several malignancies. This study aims to examine the role and potential mechanism of peiminine in prostate cancer (PCa).MethodsCCK-8, wound healing, colony formation, and Transwell assays were employed to evaluate PCa cell phenotypes. Cell cycle progression and apoptosis were examined by flow cytometry. Mitochondrial ROS, mitochondrial membrane potential, and ATP levels were measured to evaluate mitochondrial function in PCa cells. Western blotting was used to assess protein levels associated with apoptosis, EMT, and Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect β-catenin expression. The in vivo effects of peiminine were evaluated using a xenograft mouse model.ResultsPeiminine dose-dependently impaired PCa cell viability without significantly affecting non-tumor cells. Peiminine inhibited PCa cell growth and motion and triggered apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, and mitochondrial dysfunction in vitro. Peiminine reduced PCa cell-derived tumor growth in the xenograft mouse model. Peiminine inhibited Wnt/β-catenin signal transduction.ConclusionPeiminine exhibits an antitumor role in PCa by targeting Wnt/β-catenin signaling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03455-6.Keywords: Aggressiveness, Peiminine, Prostate cancer, Wnt/β-catenin pathway

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the second most common malignancy in men worldwide, with a predilection for the elderly, particularly those over the age of 65 [1]. In 2022, the global incidence of PCa in men is estimated to be 14.2%, second only to lung cancer (15.3%) [2]. In addition, as reported by the American Cancer Society, its incidence rate has been increasing by 3% per year since 2014 [3]. Recent advances in the diagnosis and treatment of PCa have led to significant improvements in patient survival rates over the past decade [4]. For patients diagnosed at early stages, their life expectancy can reach up to 99% for more than 10 years. However, patients with metastatic PCa have a poor overall survival rate of merely 30% for 5 years [5]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to find new and effective drugs against PCa proliferation and metastasis.

Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a complicated cellular process whereby epithelial cells acquire mesenchymal phenotypes [6]. During EMT, cells gain the capacity for migration/invasion, contributing to cancer progression [7]. Evidence suggests that EMT functions as a pivotal modulator of tumor metastasis and drug resistance in PCa [8]. Multiple signal transduction pathways are involved in inducing EMT, including the canonical Wnt pathway [9]. In response to stimuli, the ligand Wnt binds to the receptors on the cell surface, thereby activating the canonical pathway. This process subsequently recruits the Disheveled protein and inactivates the destruction complex (Axin2, APC, GSK-3β), allowing the nuclear translocation of β-catenin and subsequently promoting the transcription of target genes [9]. Accumulating evidence has indicated that Wnt/β-catenin signaling plays a carcinogenic role in many cancers, including PCa [10, 11]. Multiple compounds have been reported to exert antitumor effects in PCa by targeting the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. For example, Gastrodin suppresses PCa cell proliferation and inhibits tumorigenesis in a xenograft mouse model by inhibiting the canonical Wnt/β-Catenin signaling pathway [12]. Fang et al. demonstrated that β-ionone inhibits the migration, invasion, and EMT of PCa cells via negative regulation of this pathway [13]. Thus, targeting this pathway is considered a promising strategy for tumor therapy.

Plant-derived natural products have been regarded as a valuable source of anticancer drugs due to their lower toxicity and higher effectiveness compared to conventional pharmaceuticals [14]. Peiminine, an active alkaloid extracted from Fritillaria thunbergii Miq., possesses multiple pharmacological properties, such as anti-inflammation and antioxidation [15]. Moreover, peiminine exhibits a tumor-suppressive role in several malignancies. For example, Yi et al. proposed that peiminine suppresses breast cancer progression by triggering ferroptosis via the Nrf2 pathway [16]. Li et al. revealed its tumor-suppressive role in colorectal cancer by targeting the LINC00659/miR-760 axis [17]. Nevertheless, the precise function of peiminine in PCa remains unclear.

Herein, we intend to explore whether peiminine functions as a tumor suppressor in PCa. The potential mechanism was also investigated. It was hypothesized that peiminine might impede PCa cell aggressiveness by modulating Wnt/β-catenin signal transduction.

Materials and methods

Preparation of peiminine

Peiminine (99.94% purity) was purchased from MedChemExpress (HY-N0213, Shanghai, China). Peiminine was dissolved in 0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide and diluted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to the desired concentrations. Its chemical structure is shown in Fig. 1.

The chemical structure of peiminine

Cell culture

The nontumor cell line (RWPE-1) and PCa cell lines (PC-3, DU-145) were obtained from WheLab (Shanghai, China). RWPE-1 cells were incubated in the defined K-SFM (Gibco, Grand Island, NY) containing 5 ng/mL EGF and 0.05 mg/mL BPE. PCa cells were grown in an F-12 K medium (Gibco) or minimal essential medium (MEM; Gibco) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (F-12 K for PC-3, MEM for DU-145) in a humidified environment (5% CO2, 37 ℃).

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

RWPE-1 and PCa cells were inoculated into 96-well plates (5 × 103/well) and exposed to different concentrations of peiminine (0–40 µM). After the indicated periods (24, 48, or 72 h), cells were incubated with CCK-8 reagent (10 µL; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for an additional 2 h. Next, a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) was used to evaluate the optical density at 450 nm.

Colony formation assay

Tumor cells were plated in 6-well plates (600 cells/well) and exposed to different concentrations of peiminine for 2 weeks, followed by fixing in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min and staining with 0.5% crystal violet for 1 h. The stained cells were imaged using a light microscope (Leica Microsystems, Shanghai, China), and the number of colonies (> 50 cells) was counted.

Flow cytometry

For cell cycle analysis, cells were fixed in 70% ice-cold ethanol at − 20 ℃ overnight. Next, cells were stained with propidium iodide solution (0.5 mL; Beyotime) at 37℃ for 30 min in the dark. For cell apoptosis analysis, the collected cells were resuspended in the binding buffer (100 µL) and then mixed with Annexin V-FITC (5 µL) and propidium iodide (10 µL) for 15 min in the dark. Cell cycle distribution and apoptosis were evaluated using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). The results were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Wound healing assay

PCa cells were plated in 6-well plates to 90% confluence. Then, the cell monolayer was scratched with a 200 µL pipette tip and washed twice with PBS to remove detached cells. The cells were subsequently incubated in the serum-free medium containing different concentrations of peiminine. Wounds were captured at 0 h and 24 h after peiminine treatment using a light microscope (Leica Microsystems), and the migration distance was measured with ImageJ software. The cell migration rate was calculated as (wound width at 0 h − wound width at 24 h)/wound width at 0 h.

Transwell assay

PCa cell invasion was tested using Transwell chambers (24-well, 8-µm pore size; Corning Inc., Corning, NY), with the upper chamber pre-coated with diluted Matrigel (Beyotime). Cells (4 × 105) in a serum-free medium (200 µL) containing different concentrations of peiminine (0–40 µM) were placed in the upper plates, and a complete medium (800 µL) containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After 24 h, cells that invaded the lower chamber were fixed with 4% PFA and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min. The cells were photographed under a light microscope (Leica Microsystems), and 5 fields were randomly selected to quantify the invasive cells.

Western blotting

RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was employed for protein isolation from cells, followed by quantifying protein concentration using a bicinchoninic acid assay kit (Beyotime). Then, equal amounts of protein samples (20 µg) were separated on 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, blotted onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Beyotime), and blocked with 5% non-fat milk. Afterward, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (detailed in Table 1) at 4℃ overnight, washed three times with TBST, and then incubated with the horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:1000, ab7090, Abcam, Shanghai, China) for 1 h. Lastly, protein bands were visualized using BeyoECL Plus (Beyotime) and detected using a gel imaging system. Relative band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Measurement of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS)

The Mitochondrial Superoxide Assay Kit with MitoSOX Red (S0061S, Beyotime) was used to detect mtROS as per the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, after the indicated treatment, cells were treated with MitoSOX staining solution (5 µM) at 37℃ for 30 min. After incubation, cells were washed twice with PBS. The results were observed under a fluorescent microscope (Leica Microsystems) and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) assay

The MMP was examined using the JC-1 fluorescent probe (C2006, Beyotime). After the indicated treatment, tumor cells were harvested and treated with JC-1 solution (5 µg/mL) at 37℃ for 20 min, followed by washing twice with the buffer solution. The staining results were observed under a fluorescent microscope (Leica Microsystems), and the MMP was determined by calculating the ratio of JC-1 aggregate/monomer (red/green) using ImageJ software.

ATP assay

Cellular ATP levels were examined with an ATP assay kit (S0026, Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, PCa cells were seeded in 96-well plates (1 × 104 cells/well). After treatment with different concentrations of peiminine for 24 h, cells were lysed using 100 µL of lysis buffer, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. The supernatant was collected and mixed with 100 µL of ATP detection reagent. Luminescence was immediately measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific).

Immunofluorescence staining

Tumor cells were plated in 6-well plates and exposed to peiminine (40 µM) for 24 h. Then, cells were collected, fixed in 100% methanol, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min, and treated with 10% normal goat serum for 1 h. Next, the cells were incubated with the anti-β-catenin primary antibody (1:250, ab32572, Abcam) overnight at 4 ℃. The Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated secondary antibody (Abcam) was used for subsequent incubation for 1 h. DAPI was utilized for nuclear labeling. The staining results were observed and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems), and relative fluorescence intensity was determined using ImageJ software.

Xenograft mouse model

Ten male BALB/c nude mice (4 weeks old) were obtained from Vital River (Beijing, China) and maintained under SPF conditions. After one-week acclimatization, the mice were injected subcutaneously with PC-3 cells (4 × 106) in the right flank. One week after implantation, the mice were randomly assigned to the control and peiminine groups (n = 5/group) and were intraperitoneally injected with either normal saline or peiminine (2 mg/kg) every 3 days. The dose of peiminine was selected according to previous reports [17, 18]. The tumor volume was calculated as: tumor volume (mm3) = width2 × length × 0.5. On day 22, all mice were euthanized by decapitation under anesthesia, and tumors were collected for further analysis. In addition, six normal tumor-free nude mice were randomly divided into the control and peiminine treatment groups to evaluate drug toxicity (n = 3/group). At the end of the experiment, mice were euthanized, and prostate tissue was collected for hematoxylin-eosin staining (G1120, Solarbio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All experimental procedures were conducted following the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, with approval from the Ethics Committee of Yichang Central People’s Hospital. The maximum tumor diameter was less than 15 mm, and the tumor burden was less than 10% of normal body weight. The tumor size/burden was not exceeded in this study.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% PFA, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned (4-µm-thick). The tumor sections were then deparaffined and rehydrated, followed by heat-mediated antigen retrieval with a Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). After treatment with the blocking buffer (P0102, Beyotime) for 1 h, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies against Ki67 (1:500, ab15580, Abcam) or β-catenin (1 µg/mL, ab35272, Abcam) overnight at 4 ℃ and then incubated in 0.3% H2O2 in Tris-buffered saline for 15 min, followed by incubation with the HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:300, ab7090, Abcam) for 1 h. DAB (P0202, Beyotime) was used for color development. The results were photographed using an inverted phase contrast microscope (Leica Microsystems), and the percentage of Ki67- and β-catenin-positive cells were quantified using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.2 software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation from three independent experiments. Differences were compared by Student’s t-test (for two groups) or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc analysis (for multiple groups). p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Peiminine inhibits PCa cell proliferation

We first examined the cytotoxicity of peiminine to the normal human prostate epithelial cell line (RWPE-1) and PCa cell lines (DU-145, PC-3). The cells were treated with different concentrations of peiminine (0, 5, 10, 20, and 40 µM) for the indicated periods. As a result, peiminine at concentrations below 40 µM had no significant impact on RWPE-1 cell viability after 24 h of incubation (Fig. 2A). By contrast, peiminine time- and dose-dependently impaired the viability of PCa cell lines (Fig. 2B). Subsequently, 10, 20, and 40 µM of peiminine, which showed significant suppressive effects on PCa cell viability at 24 h, were selected for further use. Moreover, peiminine dose-dependently decreased the number of colonies formed by PCa cells (Fig. 2C, D), indicating the inhibitory impact of peiminine on PCa cell proliferation. In parallel, the percentage of cells in the G0/G1 phase was increased in the peiminine-treated groups (Fig. 2E, F), suggesting that peiminine arrested PCa cells in the G0/G1 phase.

Peiminine inhibits PCa cell proliferation. A CCK-8 assay showing the viability of RWPE-1 cells exposed to various concentrations (0–40 µM) of peiminine for 24 h. B CCK-8 assay for evaluating the viability of PCa cells treated with various concentrations of peiminine for 24, 48, or 72 h. C, D Colony formation assay for assessing PCa cell proliferation under peiminine treatment. E, F Flow cytometry analysis of cell cycle distribution of PCa cells treated with peiminine. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001

Peiminine impedes PCa cell motion

Subsequently, we evaluated the effect of peiminine on the migratory and invasive abilities of PCa cells. As depicted in Fig. 3A–D, peiminine dose-dependently suppressed the migration rate and reduced the number of invaded PCa cells. EMT is a key determinant of tumor cell metastasis. We then examined whether peiminine affected the EMT process of PCa cells by detecting EMT-related protein expression. As displayed by Western blotting, the protein levels of E-cadherin (an epithelial marker) were elevated while those of Snail and Vimentin (mesenchymal markers) were reduced in the peiminine-treated groups (Fig. 3E–G). Collectively, peiminine treatment dose-dependently impedes PCa cell migration, invasiveness, and EMT in vitro.

Peiminine impedes PCa cell motion. A, B Wound healing assay for evaluating PCa cell migration. C, D Transwell assay for assessing the invasive ability of PCa cells. E Representative images of Western blotting showing protein levels of EMT-related markers. F, G Quantitative results of Western blotting. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001

Peiminine induces PCa cell apoptosis

As many anticancer drugs exert their effects through activating the apoptotic pathway, we then estimated whether the suppressive effect of peiminine on PCa was associated with apoptosis. As displayed by flow cytometry, the cell apoptosis rate was prominently increased after peiminine treatment in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4A, B). Consistently, Western blotting revealed that peiminine suppressed Bcl-2 (anti-apoptotic protein) expression and elevated Bax and cleaved-caspase3 (pro-apoptotic proteins) expression in PCa cells (Fig. 4C–E), indicating that peiminine treatment contributed to PCa cell apoptosis.

Peiminine induces PCa cell apoptosis. A, B Flow cytometry analysis for detecting PCa cell apoptosis. C Representative images of Western blotting showing protein levels of apoptosis-related markers. D, E Quantitative results of Western blotting. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001

Peiminine induces mitochondrial dysfunction in PCa cells

The mtROS levels were detected using the MitoSOX fluorescent probe. The results demonstrated that peiminine treatment dose-dependently elevated mtROS production in PCa cells (Fig. 5A, B). In parallel, we examined the MMP using the JC-1 fluorescent probe. The transition from red fluorescence (aggregate) to green fluorescence (monomer) with JC-1 can easily detect the decrease in cell membrane potential. As shown in Fig. 5C, D, the JC-1 aggregate/monomer ratio was reduced as peiminine concentration increased in PCa cells, indicating that peiminine induced the reduction in the MMP. In addition, decreased ATP levels were observed in PCa cells as peiminine concentration increased (Fig. 5E). These results indicated that peiminine induces mitochondrial dysfunction in PCa cells dose-dependently.

Peiminine induces mitochondrial dysfunction in PCa cells. A, B Detection of mitochondrial ROS in PCa cells using the MitoSOX fluorescent probe. C Representative images of JC-1 assay for assessing mitochondrial membrane potential in PCa cells. D Quantification of mitochondrial membrane potential (indicated by the ratio of JC-1 aggregate/monomer). E Measurement of ATP levels in PCa cells. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001

Peiminine suppresses Wnt/β-catenin signal transduction

To explore the possible mechanism by which peiminine suppresses PCa cell aggressiveness, we estimated its impact on the canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which plays a critical role in PCa progression. Peiminine treatment dose-dependently inhibited GSK-3β phosphorylation and induced β-catenin degradation in PCa cells (Fig. 6A−C). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining revealed that nuclear β-catenin expression was reduced in peiminine-treated PC-3 cells (Fig. 6D, E), suggesting the suppressive impact on the Wnt/β-catenin signal transduction.

Peiminine suppresses Wnt/β-catenin signal transduction. A Representative images of Western blotting showing (p)-GSK-3β and β-catenin protein expression in PCa cells. B, C Quantification of protein expression. D Representative immunofluorescence staining images showing β-catenin expression in PC-3 cells treated with or without peiminine (40 µM). E Quantification of immunofluorescence staining for nuclear β-catenin expression. ***p < 0.001

Peiminine exerts anti-PCa effects in vivo

To further validate the tumor-suppressive role of peiminine in PCa, a xenograft mouse model was constructed via subcutaneous injection of PC-3 cells into nude mice. As displayed in Fig. 7A–C, peiminine administration markedly inhibited PCa tumor growth in tumor-bearing mice, as evidenced by smaller tumor size and lower tumor weight in the peiminine-treated group. There was no significant difference in mouse body weight between the two groups (Fig. 7D), indicating that peiminine at the selected dose (2 mg/kg) had no significant toxicity to the mice. Additionally, we included normal tumor-free nude mice to evaluate the effect of peiminine on normal prostate tissue. As shown by hematoxylin-eosin staining, peiminine had no significant effect on normal prostate tissue from tumor-free mice (Fig. S1), indicating its selective action on PCa cells. Moreover, the tumor tissues from the peiminine group exhibited decreased expression of Ki67 (a proliferation marker) and β-catenin in comparison to those from the control group, as depicted by IHC staining (Fig. 7E, F), suggesting that peiminine suppresses PCa tumor growth in vivo possibly by suppressing Wnt/β-catenin signal transduction.

Peiminine inhibits tumor growth of PCa in vivo. A Images of tumors in the peiminine and control groups. B Tumor volume was monitored every 3 days. C Tumor weight was measured on the 22nd day. D Measurement of mouse body weight changes. E Representative images of IHC staining for evaluating Ki67 and β-catenin expression in murine tumor tissues of each group. n = 5/group. F Quantification of IHC staining for Ki67 and β-catenin expression. ***p < 0.001

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that peiminine dose-dependently suppressed PCa cell proliferation and motion while inducing cell apoptosis, G0/G1 cell cycle arrest, and mitochondrial dysfunction in vitro. Moreover, peiminine treatment inhibited the tumor growth of PCa in mice. Mechanistically, peiminine inhibited Wnt/β-catenin signal transduction in both PCa cells and tumor-bearing mice, indicating that the anti-PCa effect of peiminine may be attributable to its inhibitory effect on Wnt/β-catenin signaling.

Androgen deprivation therapy remains the primary treatment for advanced PCa. Despite initial remission with this therapy, the development of the castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) becomes inevitable in a majority of the cases [19]. Conventional chemotherapeutic agents for PCa, including docetaxel, are associated with limitations such as hematopoietic system toxicity, repeated withdrawal reactions, and drug resistance [20]. These observations highlight the critical need to identify novel pharmacotherapeutic agents that effectively target the malignant behavior of PCa cells while minimizing adverse effects. In recent decades, traditional Chinese medicine has demonstrated its effectiveness as a therapeutic alternative and chemosensitizer for various malignancies, including PCa [21]. Peiminine is a bioactive natural compound that exhibits antitumor effects on several malignancies. Previous evidence indicated that peiminine regulates the EGFR/FAK pathway to enhance gastric cancer cell sensitivity to adriamycin [22]. Yang et al. revealed that peiminine inhibits cell growth and induces apoptosis in urothelial bladder cancer [23]. Similar to these reports, our study revealed that peiminine reduced PCa cell proliferative ability, arrested cell cycle in the G0/G1 phase, and enhanced apoptosis in vitro. Moreover, animal experiments showed that peiminine suppressed PCa cell-derived tumor growth in vivo, indicating the suppressive effect of peiminine against tumorigenesis in PCa. Additionally, we found that peiminine treatment did not affect mouse body weight and had no significant impact on normal prostate tissue from tumor-free mice. Previous evidence has also demonstrated that peiminine can suppress tumor growth in an osteosarcoma xenograft mouse model without causing toxicity to major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) [24], indicating that peiminine has strong antitumor activity with low toxicity in vivo.

Metastasis is a primary cause of cancer-related mortality in various cancers, including PCa [25]. It was reported that peiminine inhibited the migratory and invasive capacities of colorectal cancer cells and suppressed the EMT process [17]. EMT plays a pivotal role in tumor progression and metastasis, characterized by downregulated epithelial markers and upregulated mesenchymal markers [26]. Previous studies have demonstrated the potential of various natural compounds to inhibit metastasis in PCa by restraining the EMT process, including liquiritigenin and resveratrol [27, 28]. This study revealed the inhibitory impacts of peiminine on PCa cell migration, invasiveness, and EMT, as evidenced by upregulated E-cadherin and downregulated Snail and Vimentin, indicating that peiminine treatment could suppress PCa cell metastasis.

Mitochondria are dynamic organelles that generate ATP to fulfill the energy needs for cell survival through the mitochondrial respiratory chain and play a critical role in both health regulation and disease development [29]. Notably, dysfunctional mitochondria have been observed in cancer [30]. Mitochondrial dysfunction is characterized by mtROS production, MMP depolarization, and ATP decrease [31]. Dysfunctional mitochondria produce excessive ROS, which induces oxidative stress and subsequently cell apoptosis, and the decline of MMP is considered a hallmark of apoptosis [32]. Our results showed that peiminine treatment dose-dependently induced mtROS production, MMP decline, and ATP reduction in PCa cells, suggesting that peiminine contributed to mitochondrial dysfunction. A previous report indicated that peiminine induced apoptosis and MMP reduction in hepatocellular carcinoma cells [33], supporting the findings in our study.

Emerging evidence suggests that aberrant activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling is closely associated with tumor onset and progression [34]. The activation of this pathway has been demonstrated to promote the malignant behavior of PCa cells [11]. Consequently, drugs targeting this pathway hold great potential for the treatment of PCa [12]. A previous study demonstrated that peiminine attenuates osteoporosis in a rat model by modulating Wnt/β-catenin signaling [35]. Our study showed that peiminine inhibited GSK-3β phosphorylation and β-catenin nuclear translocation in PCa cells. Additionally, peiminine also reduced β-catenin expression in tumors from the tumor-bearing mice, suggesting its role in suppressing Wnt/β-catenin signal transduction.

In conclusion, this study is the first to reveal the antitumor function of peiminine in PCa. Our results demonstrate that peiminine suppresses cell aggressiveness and reduces tumor growth of PCa possibly by inactivating Wnt/β-catenin signaling. These findings indicate that peiminine may serve as a therapeutic agent for PCa treatment.
