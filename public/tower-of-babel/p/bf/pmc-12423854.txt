Biogenic Synthesis of Gold Nanoparticles with Cyanobacterium Lyngbya confervoides and In Vitro Antimicrobial Assessment
Authors: Surendra Swain, Shuvasree Bej, Ajit Kumar Bishoyi, Bigyan Ranjan Jali, Chita Ranjan Sahoo, Rabindra Nath Padhy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Green nanotechnology is a suitable method, where natural reductants synthesize nanoparticles in an eco-friendly, safe, less hazardous, and less toxic manner. Nowadays, multidrug-resistant bacteria cause severe infections in public health; thus, new eco-friendly and less toxic antimicrobials are needed. An aliquot of 10 mL aqueous extract of nonnitrogen fixing cyanobacterium Lyngbya confervoides was added to a 40 mL 1 mm solution of HAuCl4 to obtain L. confervoides nanoparticles (Lc-AuNPs). Furthermore, the synthesized Lc-AuNPs were analyzed with UV–vis spectroscopy; the peak was obtained at 537 nm, at 350–650 nm. FTIR analysis at 4 cm–1 resolution at 450–4000 cm–1 showed the obtained bands at 2867.55, 2163.13, 2050.57, 1981.15, 1618.32, 1509.26, and 1011.64 cm–1. DLS analysis of Lc-AuNPs showed peaks between 10 and 100 d·nm and zeta potential values obtained between 50 and −50 mV. FESEM analysis of Lc-AuNPs confirmed the spherical-shaped nanoparticles within 100 nm size. EDX analysis of Lc-AuNPs presents the elements C, O, Au, Na, and K. Subsequently, the biosynthesized Lc-AuNPs were monitored against multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogenic bacterial strains, Gram +ve Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pyogenes and Gram −ve Escherichia coli and Acinetobacter baumannii, proving excellent activities with obtained zone of inhibition of 14, 16, 14, and 14 mm, respectively. Furthermore, Lc-AuNPs were tested against fungal strains Trichophyton rubrum and Candida tropicalis, which had zones of inhibition of 12 and 12 mm, respectively. The studied MIC values of Lc-AuNPs against bacteria S. aureus, S. pyogenes, E. coli, and A. baumannii were 100, 25, 100, and 50 μg/mL, respectively. Furthermore, the MIC values against fungal strains T. rubrum and C. tropicalis were 3.12 and 6.25 μg/mL, respectively. This revealed potent antimicrobial properties of Lc-AuNPs and could be used as future antibacterial agents and antifungals.

Introduction

Nanotechnology is an emerging area of science and engineering where phenomena occur in nanoscale dimensions, and the sizes of nanoparticles (NPs) range 1–100 nm. This involves characterizations, designs, materials, manufacture, systems, devices, and applications of nanomaterials that have extensive promising medical applications, such as therapeutic product developments, diagnostic imaging, biomedical implants, drug delivery, tissue engineering, and diagnostic methodologies. The NPs are produced through two approaches, basically, bottom-up and top-down synthesis: in bottom-up methods, chemical reactions occur with the assembly of molecules, atoms, and ions to produce NPs, with examples of chemical vapor deposition, sol–gel method, chemical reduction, pyrolysis, electrochemical, etching, and hydrothermal method of synthesis. Where the biological synthesis of nanoparticles with plants, bacteria, fungi, and algae involves bottom-up approaches, in the top-down approaches, the physical method occurred where bulky metals were reduced to smaller particles or ions and typically less than 100 nm in size, with some examples such as laser ablation, mechanical milling, sputtering, engraving, and electroexplosion. , The eco-friendly and sustainable green synthesis route of NPs was an alternative and fascinating method, where nontoxic secondary metabolites from the extracts of plant parts, microbes, and algae were used as capping and reducing agents for NPs biosynthesis. Gold is precious, colloidal, and biocompatible and has been used since ancient times for its curative applications in several diseases. AuNPs have various applications in biosensors, catalysts, imaging, cancer treatment, and HIV treatment. Biosynthesis using gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) has received considerable attention due to its ease, quickness, eco-friendliness, cost-effectiveness, low toxicity, and ease of scale. It has potential medical applications in treating antimicrobial drugs, arthritis, and anticancer properties.

Overuse and misuse of antibiotics are the leading causes of the development of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria; some examples are Gram −ve bacilli, with carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales Acinetobacter baumannii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Gram +ve methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus. Nowadays, microorganisms like bacteria, fungi, molds, etc., cause several infections in humans and other animals; to fight this infection, several antimicrobials and antibiotics were developed, but with time, the microorganisms become resistant to most antibiotics, which originate problems in treating of diseases; to combat these diseases, new antimicrobials need to be developed. Thus, researchers focus on alternative, cost-effective, and eco-friendly antimicrobial agents that can overcome MDR strains. The infections caused by the colonization of MDR bacteria are a healthcare burden; the incidence and carriers of resistant bacteria are not known, which causes diseases such as cellulitis, pneumonia, and urinary tract infections. , Developing existing and new drugs with promising nanocarriers can overcome infections like lower respiratory infections, malaria, tuberculosis, and diarrhea, which are dominant drivers of morbidity and mortality everywhere. The biological synthesis of NPs has recently gained attention due to their biological, physical, and chemical properties with an eco-friendly nature. Indeed, physical and chemical nanomaterial synthesis methods produce toxic NPs and are costly. Thus, the green synthesis method is gaining more attention in the area of nanotechnology. The biological entities, including algae, fungi, and bacteria, are taken in NPs synthesis; however, cyanobacteria (blue-green algae) are the promising candidates for NPs synthesis due to unique characteristics like high growth rate, accumulation of toxic heavy metals, no toxic byproducts, less energy required, presence of biomolecules, and phycochemicals. Since 2.6–3.5 billion years ago, photosynthetic prokaryotes named cyanobacteria have evolved, making them a rich source of phycocompounds due to their distinct adaptive properties in various diverse habitats on Earth. The phycochemicals likely lipopeptides, enzymes, polysaccharides, and cellular pigments found in cyanobacteria help in AuNPs synthesis, such as Lyngbya, Phormidium, Nostoc, Spirulina, and Scytonema, have anticancer, antifungal, and antiviral properties. The cyanobacteria are easy to culture, rapidly grow biomass that can produce AuNPs with a size within 100 nm with spherical, cylindrical, cubical, ellipsoidal, hexagonal, and irregular shapes, as seen using several reported cyanobacterial species. The nanoparticles produced using cyanobacteria have unique physicochemical properties, including small size, large surface area, and surface functional properties that battle against microorganisms. Cyanobacteria have capping and reducing agents that take part in NPs synthesis and produce 1–100 nm sized NPs. Indeed, these are biocompatible in nature and have antimicrobial properties. The overuse of antibiotics leads to resistance to antimicrobials, causing antimicrobial resistance, where green-synthesized metallic NPs can combat antimicrobial resistance with unique microbe cell lysis properties. Photosynthetic cyanobacteria have various advantages, such as the presence of bioactive compounds, cultivability, scalability, fabrication of metals, and high metal uptake capabilities. Many metals, such as gold, silver, platinum, zinc, palladium, copper, and iron, are synthesized with cyanobacteria, which is the green route of synthesis of nanoparticles with an environmentally friendly nature as an alternative to physical synthesis routes. Various spectral analyses were performed to determine the presence of functional groups, nanoparticle stability, and morphological characteristics. The cyanobacteria-mediated nanoparticles had distinctive antibacterial, antiviral, antifungal, anticancer, antidiabetic, antioxidant, drug delivery, diagnosis, heavy metal removal, catalytic dye degradation, and biosensing properties. Cyanobacteria have several bioactive compounds and enzymes that have the potential to reduce metal ions into nanoparticles. The green synthesis of AuNPs with marine alga Gelidiella acerosa has been reported to have potential antidiabetic, antioxidant, and antibacterial properties. The cyanobacterium Streptomyces platensis protein-mediated AuNPs biosynthesis and its antibacterial properties against Gram +ve S. aureus and Bacillus subtilis are reported. The cyanobacterium Nepenthes calcicola had bioactive chemicals with reducing and stabilizing properties for the biosynthesis of AuNPs, and the biosynthesized AuNPs showed potential antibacterial, antifungal, and antioxidant properties. The nonnitrogen fixing cyanobacterium S. platensis and Oscillatoria sp. mediated AuNPs biosynthesis had effective anticancer properties against human HeLa cervical and CaCo-2 colon cancer cells; AuNPs biosynthesis with S. platensis and Oscillatoria sp. had antibacterial properties against S. aureus, B. subtilis, MRSA, Salmonella typhi,P. aeruginosa, and Klebsiella pneumoniae and antifungal activities against Candida albicans and C. tropicalis in vitro. , AuNPs biosynthesized with ethanol extracts of microalga Synechococcus moorigangae had antibacterial efficiencies against pathogenic bacteria, Escherichia coli, S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, and K. pneumoniae.

The cyanobacterium Lyngbya sp. had several phycocompounds, amino acids, lipids, proteins, peptides, apramide, majusculamide, apratoxin, lyngbyastatin, cocosamides, etc., which had potent antimicrobial applications, anticancer, antifungal, antibacterial, and antioxidant properties; Lyngbya sp. mediated biosynthesis of nanoparticles had several industrial and pharmaceutical applications. The cyanobacterium Lyngbya sp. had a capacity for bioreduction, stabilization, and heavy metal accumulation properties for the biosynthesis of AgNPs, which showed potent antibacterial properties against human common pathogenic bacteria, E. coli, S. aureus, and P. aeruginosa with the zone of inhibition (ZOI) of 16, 14, and 12 mm, respectively.

Materials and Methods

Materials and Reagents

Reagent-grade HAuCl4·3H2O (gold chloride trihydrate, purity ≥ 99%, number RM4291-1G, Himedia), Muller Hinton agar (Himedia) for pathogenic bacteria culture, Muller Hinton broth (Himedia), and triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (TTC) (Himedia) for MIC and MBC study were used. The marine cyanobacterium Lyngbya confervoides (strain no. BDU142001) was maintained in the laboratory for mass cultivation. After biosynthesis of AuNPs with the cyanobacterium L. confervoides, the synthesized Lc-AuNPs size was seen in FESEM (JEOL, JSM 7610F plus model), FTIR (Spectrum two, 104789), UV–visible spectrometry (Type, 2203, double beam, Systronics), and DLS (Malvern instrument, ZEN-1600).

Maintenance of Microorganisms

Previously procured human pathogenic bacteria, Gram +ve Streptococcus pyogenes (MTCC 1982) and S. aureus (MTCC 7443) and Gram −ve A. baumannii (MTCC 1425) and E. coli (MTCC 443), were cultured in the Central Research Laboratory, IMS, and Sum Hospital, Bhubaneswar. Pathogenic fungi, Trichophyton rubrum (MCC 1598) and Candida tropicalis (MCC 1559), were procured previously and cultured in the laboratory for antifungal assay.

Lyngbya confervoides Biomass Growth and Harvest

The cyanobacterium L. confervoides was axenically cultured in 5 L capacity trays with ASN III media for 6 months while maintaining 12 h of light–dark cycles at 25–30 °C in aseptic laboratory conditions. After visible growth of biomass, it was filtered in a Whatman No. 1 filter paper, and the filtered biomass was shade-dried for 2–3 days. The fully dried biomass was ground in a mortar and pestle into biomass powders and stored in a refrigerator at 14 °C in an airtight centrifuge tube.

AuNPs Biosynthesis

1 g of aqueous dried powder of L. confervoides extract was dissolved in 50 mL of sterile Milli-Q water and kept for 24 h. After that, it was stirred and heated at 60 °C for 1 h. Then, the solution was filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper for cell-free extracts, and 1 mM gold chloride of 100 mL was prepared as a stock solution. Then, 60 mL of 1 mM gold chloride was mixed with 20 mL of filtered extract and stirred for 1–2 h, where the change in color of the solution was observed as light brown and some tiny particles were seen in the naked eye in the solution, which signifies the reduction of NPs into AuNPs, and the reduction occurred due to the phycocompounds present in the L. confervoides extract. Biosynthesized Lc-AuNPs were analyzed in a UV–vis spectrophotometer at a range of 350–650 nm to confirm the successful biosynthesis of AuNPs. After that, the synthesized Lc-AuNPs were centrifuged at 9000 rpm for 30 min, the pellet was collected and dried in an oven at 60 °C for 1–6 h, and the dried powder and the supernatant were stored in an airtight sterile container wrapped in an aluminum foil to reduce photoactivation. These were characterized by several spectral techniques and monitored for in vitro antimicrobial properties.

Spectral Analysis of Lc-AuNPs

The first indication of successful bioreduction of HAuCl4 was known by the visual observation of color change from light green to light brown. Furthermore, the biosynthesized Lc-AuNPs were analyzed in a UV–visible spectrophotometer, and the absorbance peak was seen at 537 nm. FTIR analysis of Lc-AuNPs was done to characterize possible phycocompounds in synthesized NPs, which helps reduce metallic ions into possible NPs. The FTIR analysis of Lc-AuNPs was found as wavenumber peaks at 2867.55, 2163.13, 2050.57, 1981.15, 1618.32, 1509.26, and 1011.64 cm–1, which indicated the presence of functional biomolecules in L. confervoides, which were responsible for the reduction of Au+ ions into possible Lc-AuNPs. DLS analysis of Lc-AuNPs determines the hydrodynamic diameter of NPs and particle size distributions, where one peak between 10 and 100 nm was obtained and a second peak was between 100 and 1000 d·nm. The analyzed zeta potential value was obtained between −50 and 50 mV, which indicated the surface charge of NPs. The FESEM analysis of Lc-AuNPs obtained sizes of NPs within 100 nm, and EDX analysis showed the presence of various elements in biosynthesized Lc-AuNPs.

Monitoring In Vitro Antimicrobial Properties of Biosynthesized Lc-AuNPs

Pathogenic MDR bacteria, S. aureus, S. pyogenes, E. coli, and A. baumannii, were cultured in Muller Hinton broth in 15 mL centrifuge tubes for 24 h; Muller Hinton agar (MHA) containing Petri plates were prepared in a sterile Laminar airflow chamber with semisolid MHA plates with the bacterial strains being spread and cultured. Furthermore, wells were done using puncher, and the biosynthesized Lc-AuNPs, gold chloride stock solution, aqueous L. confervoides extract, and antibiotic gentamycin 50 μg/mL were a positive control. These Petri plates were stored and incubated for 12–24 h at 37 °C, and the ZOI were recorded using the Himedia zone scale. Similarly, fungi T. rubrum and C. tropicalis were cultured in sabouraud dextrose broth in a 15 mL centrifuge tube; after 12–24 h, the fungal strains were cultured in Petri plates with Sabouraud dextrose agar, and solidifying these strains cultured using the spread plate method; accordingly, the wells were prepared using the agar well diffusion method by puncher, the aqueous extract of L. confervoides, Lc-AuNPs, AuCl3 stock solution, and positive control gentamycin was put in wells using the well diffusion method. Thereafter, the Petri plates were incubated in a BOD incubator for 12–24 h, and the observed ZOI was recorded using a zone scale. Additionally, the Muller Hinton broth and Sabouraud dextrose broth were prepared for bacteria and fungi culture in 96-well plates, respectively, and 2,3,5-triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (TTC) was used for MIC analysis.

Results and Discussion

AuNPs Biosynthesis and UV–Vis

The aqueous extract of L. confervoides was added to a 1 mM gold chloride stock solution, and the solution was stirred using a magnetic stirrer maintained at 60–70 °C. After 2–3 h, the solution color changes from light green to brown, indicating the reduction of metallic ions due to the extract containing phycocompounds. The reduced AuNPs solution was analyzed with UV–visible spectroscopy at 350–650 nm, and the peak was found at 537 nm (Figure ).

UV–vis analysis of Lc-AuNPs showed an absorbance peak at 537 nm.

The marine bacterium Vibrio alginolyticus was grown in a nutrient broth medium; after incubation, the culture medium was centrifuged, and the supernatant was mixed with a 1 mM aqueous solution of HAuCl4·3H2O; after 24 h of incubation, the solution color was changed from light yellow to reddish brown, which was the visual indication of the reduction of metallic ions to AuNPs. The synthesized AuNPs were analyzed in a UV–vis spectrum at a range of 300–600 nm, and the SPR bands were observed at a peak of 530 nm after 24 h incubation, which confirms the successful formation of AuNPs. The aqueous 2 mM HAuCl4 colloidal stock solution was added to the cyanobacterium Nostoc calcicola extract and stirred for 2 h. The solution changed color, which confirmed the reduction of Au3+ ions to Au0 and the formation of AuNPs. The UV–vis spectrum peak was obtained at 527 nm; a similar peak was observed in this present study, and the UV–vis spectrum analysis of AuNPs determines the reduction of Au3+ to nanoforms due to the AuNPs having typical surface plasmon resonance (SPR) absorption peaks. Cyanobacterium S. platensis mediated AuNPs biosynthesis was confirmed by the change in color of the solution from green to ruby red upon 3 h of continuous stirring and after 48 h of incubation. The successful formation of AuNPs was confirmed by UV–vis monitoring at a range of 300–700 nm, and the SPR peaks were seen at 385, 524, and 685 nm. This indicates the reduction of Au3+ to Au0 with oscillations of electrons in NPs and the formation of SPR in the NPs. Green alga Dunaliella salina mediated AuNPs biosynthesis was visually confirmed by the color change of the solution from watery yellow to dark purple in the biosynthesis process where an aqueous extract of D. salina was as a capping and reducing agent. Furthermore, the UV–vis analysis of the biosynthesized AuNPs was confirmed by the obtained SPR band at 560 nm. The green synthesis of AuNPs with cyanobacteria S. platensis and Oscillatoria sp. was determined with visual color change due to bioreduction of Au3+ and UV–vis spectra analysis at a range of 300–700 nm and the obtained peak at 545 nm.

FTIR Analysis of AuNPs

The FTIR analysis of Lc-AuNPs showed the obtained spectrum peaks at 2867.55, 2163.13, 2050.57, 1981.15, 1618.32, 1509.26, and 1011.64 cm–1 at a wavenumber between 450 and 4000 cm–1; where the functional groups in mid-infrared region indicated alkene groups in the C–H stretching (2867.55 cm–1), CC stretching alkynes group (2163.13 cm–1), R-ONO2 stretch nitrites group (1509.26 cm–1), and C–O stretch esters (1011.64 cm–1) (Figure ).

FTIR analysis image of Lc-AuNPs showing various absorbance bands.

Cyanobacterium Lyngbya majuscula mediated AuNP biosynthesis through FTIR spectra analysis, which indicated amide 1 and 11 bands at 1582 and 1665 cm–1. Elsewhere, FTIR analysis of the green alga Chlorella sp. exopolysaccharides had indicated FTIR spectra, with the obtained peak at 3090 cm–1 showing the presence of the −OH group and at peak 1622 cm–1 indicating the presence of C–N (amide) and COO– stretching. Additionally, the obtained peaks at 1467, 2854, and 2935 cm–1 indicated C–H stretching with methylene and methyl groups. The unicellular cyanobacterium S. moorigangae mediated AuNPs biosynthesis, being characterized in FTIR at a range of 400–4000 cm–1, while the obtained bands were at 3288, 2921, 1736, and 1549 cm–1, identified as stretching O–H, C–H, C–O, and N–O, respectively. Moreover, the phytoconstituents, polyphenols, polysaccharides, phycobilin, carbohydrates, pigments, chlorophyll, polyunsaturated fatty acids, vitamins, and minerals facilitate the reduction of metal ions into AuNPs. The obtained absorbance peaks at 2867.55, 2163.13, 2050.57, 1981.15, 1618.32, 1509.26, and 1011.64 cm–1 indicated the presence of various functional groups in biosynthesized Lc-AuNPs.

Previously, the filamentous N2-fixing Anabaena spiroides produced biosynthesized AuNPs, which on characterization with FTIR yielded the absorption peak 602.6 cm–1, representing C–Br stretching halo compound, second, 1376.9 cm–1 representing O–H bending, phenol and alcohol, 1536.9 representing nitro compound, N–O stretching, 1632.4 indicating alkene and CC stretching, 2917.7 indicating amine salt and N–H stretching, 3275.5 indicating alcohol and O–H stretching, blithely.

The obtained particles were spherical-shaped 22.3, 22.6, 23.4, 33.5, 36.8, 39.2, and 40.3 nm sized nanoparticles, whereas the EDX result defined the elements C, O, Au, Na, and K in AuNPs (Figures and ).

The analysis of biosynthesized gold nanoparticles used Spirulina maxima polysaccharides against the fungus C. albicans, where FESEM analysis of the treated sample confirmed the antifungal properties. Additionally, the EDX analysis represented the presence of Au in the sample, which was responsible for the anticandidal properties. The biological synthesis of AuNPs was characterized using FESEM-EDX analysis, confirming a successful size range between 0 and 100 nm, and several shapes were identified. Furthermore, the elemental compositions produced by AuNPs were revealed and had potential antimicrobial activities against pathogenic bacteria and fungi. AuNPs were basically of spherical shape, but gold nanorods were a significant antimicrobial agent used in the biomedical sector. The biological synthesis of zinc NPs with the cyanobacterium Desertiflum sp. was seen in FESEM for structural and morphological analysis, where the star-shaped particles with a size of 94.80 nm were obtained.

Antimicrobial Properties of Biosynthesized Lc-AuNPs and MIC Study

The cyanobacterium L. confervoides had assorted phycochemicals that reduced gold particles into successful production of phycosynthesized Lc-AuNPs, which had potential antimicrobial agents against Gram +ve bacteria S. aureus and S. pyogenes and Gram −ve bacteria E. coli and A. baumannii with obtained zone of inhibition values of 14, 16, 14, and 14 mm, respectively (Figure ). Likewise, Lc-AuNPs were monitored against fungal strains T. rubrum and C. tropicalis, with zone of inhibition values of 12 and 12 mm, respectively. The obtained MIC values of Lc-AuNPs against bacteria S. aureus, S. pyogenes, E. coli, and A. baumannii were 100, 25, 100, and 50 μg/mL, respectively. Moreover, the MIC values against fungal strains T. rubrum and C. tropicalis were 3.12 and 6.25 μg/mL, respectively (Figures and ).

Mechanism of action behind the AuNPs on bacterial and fungal cells.

The cyanobacterium Leptolyngbya JSC-1 had been reducing and stabilizing properties for AuNPs production, which had been seen to have antibacterial activities against pathogenic bacteria, S. aureus and E. coli. Cyanobacterium S. maxima had green reducing agents that produced silver nanoparticles, and several spectroscopic techniques, such as FTIR, SEM, and XRD, confirmed the characteristics of NPs. Indeed, the confirmed NPs had notable antibacterial properties against pathogenic bacteria S. aureus and Enterococcus faecalis, with zone of inhibition at 2.5 and 3.2 mm, respectively. The cyanobacterium A. spiroides had several phycochemicals that reduce and cap gold particles during AuNPs synthesis. Notably, the AuNPs had antibacterial agents against MDR pathogenic bacteria, S. pyogenes, MRSA, and Klebsiella oxytoca.

The antibacterial activity of silver nanoparticles containing Lyngbya sp. was evaluated against a range of pathogenic bacterial species using the agar well diffusion method. Since the gold nanoparticles displayed significant inhibitory zones, 14–16 mm, especially for S. aureus and E. coli, the previous results demonstrated their strong antibacterial properties. The nanoparticles can interact with bacterial membranes, rupturing them and producing oxidative stress because of their small size, vast surface area, and crystal structure. This explains the nanoparticles purported antibacterial action. Because AuNPs are tiny, they can cause bacterial cell death after oxidative stress and membrane rupture by the nanoparticles contact with the bacterial cell membrane. Gold nanoparticles made by Lettres majuscule biosynthesis have antibacterial qualities that are on par with or superior to those made chemically. Biosynthesized AuNPs are often favored over chemically generated AuNPs, which may contain hazardous ingredients and produce particles with irregular sizes or reduced stability, due to their lower toxicity, ecologically friendly manufacture, and enhanced biocompatibility.

Conclusions

Cyanobacteria have the unique capabilities of rapid growth, heavy metal accumulation, and environmental adaptation properties, making them distinctive, which fascinate researchers for green synthesis of metallic nanoparticles. The cyanobacterium L. confervoides had several phycochemical metabolites facilitating the synthesis of AuNPs; in addition, spectral analyses such as FTIR, XRD, DLS, and FESEM-EDX of Lc-AuNPs confirmed the successful formation of AuNPs, where the nature, compounds, structure, shape, and composition of particles were unveiled. Today, MDR bacteria and fungi are developing at an alarming rate, posing a significant threat to the health sector. They are rapidly adapting to contemporary antibiotics due to the overuse of antibiotics as well as changes in lifestyles, which require eco-friendly drugs to combat the ever-developing MDR strains that are gaining strength with each passing generation. The biosynthesized AuNPs were monitored for antimicrobial activity, where they had potent action against the MDR bacteria Gram +ve S. aureus and S. pyogenes and Gram −ve E. coli and A. baumannii, and similarly against pathogenic fungi T. rubrum and C. tropicalis. Thus, this work could facilitate research in future drug-development cascades against pathogenic bacteria and fungi.
