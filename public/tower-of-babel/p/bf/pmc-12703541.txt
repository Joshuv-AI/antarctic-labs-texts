Comparative Effectiveness of Text + Video vs Text Alone to Prompt Stool-Based Testing for Colorectal Cancer Screening
Authors: Amanda F Petrik, David Mosen, Christine Singh, Priyanka Gautom, John F Dickerson, Elizabeth Shuster, Michalah K Tandy, Jessica K Gonzalez, Annie Thibault, Jamie H Thompson, Gloria D Coronado
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionColorectal cancer (CRC) is the second-leading cause of cancer-related deaths in the United States. Screening for CRC can be accomplished by mailed fecal immunochemical testing (FIT) outreach. Text-based mailed FIT outreach reminders have been shown to improve FIT return rates, but research is lacking in determining if the addition of instructional videos to text messages could boost FIT return.MethodsThe study took place at Kaiser Permanente Northwest. Eligible patients included those 45–75 years old who were due or becoming due for CRC screening. Patients were randomized into 2 groups: 1) text alone: received a standard written text message only, and 2) text + video: received a standard written text and a link to a short instructional video. Patient demographic data, including age, sex, CRC screening status, language, race and ethnicity, and length of Health Plan enrollment, were collected. Cox regression analysis was used to compare completion of FIT 90 and 180 days after randomization between both groups.ResultsA total of 12,346 patients were included in this study, with 6156 randomized in the text alone group and 6190 in the text + video group. Patient demographic data were nearly identical in both groups. No differences were found in the FIT return rates between the text alone group (reference) vs text + video group at 90 days (hazard ratio, 1.02; 95% CI, 0.96–1.07; P = .54) and 180 days (hazard ratio, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.96–1.06; P = .78).ConclusionsFuture research should determine ways of assessing patients’ receipt of video + text messages to optimize the effectiveness of text message outreach for CRC.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second-leading cause of cancer-related deaths in the United States. 1 Screening for CRC and early detection of the disease can be accomplished by various screening options, including stool-based testing like fecal immunochemical tests (FITs) and follow-up colonoscopy among those with atypical stool test results. 2 FITs are a common, noninvasive way to screen average-risk, asymptomatic patients for CRC and can be completed in the comfort of one’s own home. 3,4 Research highlights how FIT and other types of CRC screening have contributed to the decline in CRC incidence and mortality as they can prevent cancer (through the removal of precancerous polyps) or detect it at early, treatable stages. 5–8 As an annual screening tool, FIT is inexpensive and scalable across large populations, making it practical for both low-income and insured populations. 4,9

One approach to increasing FIT completion rates is to mail tests directly to patients who are due. Previous studies have shown that mailed FIT outreach is effective in increasing CRC screenings across populations, leading to the possibility of reducing disparities in CRC screening, incidence, and mortality. 9–12 To optimize CRC screening, the Community Preventive Services Task Force recommends using patient reminders. 13 Reminders, in the form of letters or postcards, automated phone calls, and text messages, can boost stool test return rates. 12,14,15

Utilizing digital communication tools has shown promise in increasing cancer screening. For example, Uy and colleagues conducted a systematic review of text messaging interventions in cancer prevention and found that text messaging interventions increased screening by 0.6% to 15% (based on 9 studies). 16 Further, several studies have found increased CRC screening rates among patients in interventions that included a video component, delivered by various modes like DVDs or portable personal digital video display devices, compared with control groups receiving usual care. 17–19

Despite evidence showing that patient reminders using text messages or videos improve FIT test return rates, to the authors’ knowledge, no prior studies have assessed the effectiveness of pairing instructional videos with text messages. This study compares FIT return rates among 2 groups of patients: one group who received a standard text message and a second group who received a standard text message with a link to a short, animated video explaining how to complete the FIT. These findings may inform efforts to optimize mailed FIT outreach programs.

Methods

Setting

This study took place at Kaiser Permanente Northwest in Portland, Oregon. Kaiser Permanente Northwest is an integrated health care system, serving approximately 650,000 medical members in Oregon and southwest Washington. Patients were eligible to participate in this study if they were aged 45–75 years old, spoke English or Spanish, and were due or due soon (within 30 days) for CRC screening. Consistent with standard care and prior to randomization, the authors excluded members with disqualifying medical conditions (such as hospice, palliative care, CRC, Crohn’s disease, and kidney transplant, among other conditions), previous CRC screening complications, and members outside the Kaiser Permanente Northwest network. Eligible patients were randomized into 2 groups: the text alone group vs the text + video reminder group. Randomization was stratified by 4 factors: age (45 < 55, 55 < 65, 65 and over), sex (male, female, other), language (English, Spanish), and CRC screening status (screening due soon: had a prior screening and previous screening coverage is about to end; screening overdue: had a prior screening and previous screening coverage period has passed; never screened, no screening documented in patient chart).

Study procedures

In March and April 2023, eligible patients were randomized into 1 of the 2 groups. Eligible patients were identified at 2-week intervals, and FIT kits were mailed to patients in 4 separate batches on March 16, March 30, April 13, and April 27, 2023, with each patient receiving a single kit. The mailings included a FIT kit, a letter describing how to complete the kit, and instructions for returning the completed kit. The same day, they were notified by either text message or interactive voice response (IVR) informing them they will receive a FIT kit in the next several days, and they should complete and return the kit within 2 weeks. Those randomized in the text + video reminder group received an instructional, animated video embedded into the text message. The video explained how to complete the FIT kit and was approximately 2 minutes long in English and 2 minutes and 20 seconds in Spanish. An IVR was sent to patients if they were not eligible to receive a text message due to having an invalid or missing mobile phone number or opting out of text messaging, and accordingly, were not sent a link to the instructional video. Patients were given the option to either mail back the FIT kit or drop it off at a Kaiser Permanente clinic. Based on the patient’s language preference indicated in the medical chart, text messages and videos were sent in English or Spanish. A description of the text messages is listed in Table 1.

Results

Outreach summary

Based on FIT screening eligibility, a total of 13,596 patients were eligible for the outreach efforts. Of these patients, 6795 were allocated to text alone messages and 6801 were allocated to text + video messages. Of those initially allocated to receive text alone messages, 639 (9.4%) did not receive a text message and/or FIT kit (identified by the text message vendor), leaving a total of 6156 available for analysis. Similarly, of those initially allocated to text + video messages, 611 (9.0%) did not receive their messages (identified by the text message vendor) and/or FIT kit, leaving a total of 6190 for analysis. In summary, the final analytic sample included 12,346 patients (see Figure 1).

Population description

Both the text alone and text + video groups were similar in terms of reported variables (see Table 2). Nearly 80% of patients in both groups were between the ages of 45–65 years old, and about 20% were 65 years or older. A majority in both groups were female (52%), almost half (48%) had never been screened, and 96% were English-speaking. About 73% of participants were White, and over 90% maintained continuous health insurance coverage during the 12 months prior to randomization.

Data from the CCPN website indicate that from February 1, 2023, through May 31, 2023 (during the evaluation interval), there were 146 views and 90 unique viewers for the FIT video. The video received 85 views from mobile phones, 60 from desktops, and 1 from a tablet. The location for 20 of the viewers was Portland, Oregon.

Kaplan–Meier statistics

The Kaplan–Meier graph (Figure 2) visually compares the FIT completion rates over the 180-day follow-up period among both the text alone group and text + video group. Approximately 60% in both groups had completed FIT tests by 180 days after randomization.

Cox regression results

Language, sex, and prior screening status violated the assumption of proportional hazards and thus were included in the model as stratification variables rather than covariates. Overall, there was no difference in completion of FIT testing between the text alone (reference group) vs text + video groups at 90 days (HR, 1.02; 95% CI, 0.96–1.07; P = .54) and 180 days (HR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.96–1.06; P = .78) after randomization (see Table 3).

Of the covariate measures, age was associated with completion of FIT. Compared with those aged 45 < 55, patients between the ages of 55 and 65 were less likely to complete the FIT testing at 90 days (HR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.86–0.98; P = .01) and at 180 days (HR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.86–0.97; P = .004) after randomization. However, those aged 65+ years old were more likely to complete the FIT at 90 days (HR, 1.23; 95% CI, 1.15–1.32; P < .0001) and 180 days (HR, 1.22; 95% CI, 1.14–1.31; P < .0001) after randomization.

Discussion

This study examined whether embedding an instructional video on how to complete FIT testing into a text message would increase FIT return rates among patients due or becoming due for CRC screening. This analysis included 12,346 English- or Spanish-speaking patients aged 45–75 years old and found the instructional video did not further boost FIT completion compared with patients who received messages that were text alone. Further, the findings indicated few views of the video during the study’s evaluation interval.

This study makes a unique contribution to the minimal available research that isolates the impact of adding videos to text-based mailed FIT outreach reminders. Although this approach was scalable across a large sample, differences within the 2 groups were not noted in this study. These findings suggest that a single text message with an embedded video may not be sufficient to influence screening uptake. Future research should consider additional ways of engaging with patients, like sending multiple text messages with videos. For example, Huf and colleagues compared the effectiveness of a single text message reminder vs a series of text messages to patients at an urban community health center who were mailed a FIT kit. The authors reported an absolute 17.7% increase in FIT return among patients who received a series of text messages vs patients who received only one. 20 As such, greater frequency in outreach may increase screening.

Although this study examines changes in FIT screening following the delivery of an animated instructional video and text message, it highlights the uncertainty for researchers to assess whether patients received or accessed the text messages or embedded video. Although the use of text messaging can be recorded, many text message vendors are unable to track delivery of text messages or receipt of the video. The authors were unable to confirm whether patients allocated to the text + video group clicked on the video link or could view the video at all. As text message interventions continue to progress, developing data systems to track outcomes from digital technologies will be important to test the effectiveness of digital interventions.

This study has a few limitations. First, it was conducted in a single large, integrated delivery system in the Pacific Northwest during a short period. Further, the kit included clear instructions on how to complete the FIT. It is possible patients relied on the written instructions as a primary resource and thus did not need to use the video. Additionally, the authors were unable to receive information regarding the receipt of the text messages or videos. In the absence of vendor data on receipt of text messaging, future research might use bidirectional texting information or qualitative data to ascertain patients’ receipt of text messages and ability to access and open embedded videos.

Conclusions

The authors’ analysis found that among patients 45–75 years old who were due or coming due for CRC, the addition of a video to a text message notification to complete the FIT did not increase FIT completion compared with patients who received messages with text alone. However, the proportion of patients who successfully viewed the video could not be determined. Further research is needed to understand what factors may augment or reduce viewing of video reminders to increase completion of FIT testing. Moreover, further investigation is warranted to determine patients’ receipt and ability to access videos sent in text messages.
