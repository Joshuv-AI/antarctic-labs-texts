Lived Experience and Clinician Perspectives on the Priorities and Challenges of Integrating Peer Support in Acute Psychiatric Services for People With BPD
Authors: Brianna J Turner, Baylie McKnight, Zack Senay, Skye Barbic
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPeople with borderline personality disorder (BPD) are high utilisers of acute psychiatric services (i.e., psychiatric emergency and inpatient services). Yet, people with BPD often encounter significant stigma in these settings, which can result in demoralisation and reluctance to seek further care. Peer support services are increasingly adopted to align mental health services with recovery‐oriented principles. This study aimed to understand the perceived acceptability, opportunities and challenges of integrating peer support for people with BPD in acute psychiatric services. We invited people with lived experience (i.e., people with symptoms of BPD and their loved ones) and mental health professionals who provide care for people with BPD to participate in an online survey. We received 41 quantitative responses and 25 responses to open‐ended questions. Peer support was rated as important by people with lived experience and providers. Themes extracted via thematic analysis from the open‐text responses considered how peer support would (or would not) meet patients' needs, including: feeling seen, heard and understood; fostering a sense of connection and belonging; reducing negative or stigmatising experiences; accessing instrumental and informational support; experiencing a sense of psychological safety; and enhancing a sense of hope. Most responses saw peer support as well matched to these needs; however, some expressed possible implementation challenges, including staffing consistency, role confusion and incompatibility of crisis stabilisation versus recovery goals. Overall, results underscored the potential of peer support to enhance alignment with recovery‐oriented practice, which is an organisational priority for many mental health services.Keywords: borderline personality disorder, mental health consumers, mental health recovery, peer support, recovery‐oriented practice

Introduction

Peer support services (PSS), defined as organised support by and for people with mental health challenges (Cyr et al. 2016), are increasingly valued in psychiatric care as an opportunity to align services with recovery‐oriented principles of increasing empowerment, autonomy and hope for people living with mental health challenges (Leamy et al. 2011). Peer supporters are uniquely able to provide empathy, acceptance and connection with patients because of their shared lived experiences, instil hope because they embody recovery and support patients' self‐determination in identifying and working towards recovery goals (MacLellan et al. 2015). Integrating peer support with usual mental health services has been shown to reduce the severity of depression (Pfeiffer et al. 2011), improve treatment engagement and retention (Chinman et al. 2014), and increase hope and empowerment (Lloyd‐Evans et al. 2014), though evidence of its efficacy in reducing functional impairment, improving overall quality of life and reducing hospitalisations is mixed (Lloyd‐Evans et al. 2014). Moreover, the integration of peer support can benefit systems of care by improving the awareness and knowledge of meanings and experiences of personal recovery among healthcare professionals, increasing the extent to which psychiatric services are psychologically safe and aligned with consumers' recovery goals and increasing accountability of services to meeting system‐level recovery‐oriented goals. Despite growing adoption in outpatient mental health services (Shalaby and Agyapong 2020), the acceptability and feasibility of integrating PSS in acute psychiatric settings such as inpatient and emergency units remain uncertain (Rogers et al. 2016; Waldemar et al. 2016).

Background

Borderline personality disorder (BPD) is a psychiatric diagnosis characterised by pervasive emotional, interpersonal and behavioural dysregulation (American Psychiatric Association 2022). People with BPD are high utilisers of acute psychiatric services (Broadbear et al. 2022; Gregory et al. 2021; Sveen et al. 2024; Álvarez‐Tomás et al. 2024). Yet, these patients encounter frequent and significant stigma and negative care experiences in these settings (Bodner et al. 2015; Lawn and McMahon 2015; Rogers and Dunne 2011; Stapleton and Wright 2019; Miller et al. 2024). Specific stigmatising attitudes include low expectations that people with BPD can recover, a belief that patients' problems are manipulative or not genuine, and a belief that patients with BPD are ‘wasting’ resources (Stapleton and Wright 2019). It is imperative to understand how to make acute psychiatric services psychologically safer for people with BPD so that long‐term harm, including self‐stigma, hopelessness or demoralisation, avoidance of treatment when it would be in the person's interests to engage with care, and escalating self‐harm, does not occur when patients require care in these settings.

This qualitative study aimed to understand the needs, opportunities and potential obstacles for integrating PSS within acute psychiatric services, namely inpatient and emergency psychiatry services, in one health service delivery area in Western Canada. At the outset, we wish to acknowledge critical perspectives that question whether formal integration of peer support into psychiatric systems of care undermines the radical potential of peer support (Rose 2014). Likewise, some advocates call for radical revisioning of how we think about and treat BPD (Duff et al. 2020). An assumption underlying our work is that, as long as psychiatric services are a major care setting serving people with BPD, they must work to meet the ethical mandates of beneficience (i.e., benefitting consumers and promoting their wellbeing) and non‐maleficence (i.e., avoiding injury or harm). Beginning from this position, we sought to explore whether peer support would make these settings more or less psychologically safe for people with lived experience, their carers and the health professionals working in these settings.

Qualitative methods were deemed appropriate as our goal was to understand whether and why integrated PSS would be acceptable to people who would interact with this service. We aimed to include multiple stakeholder perspectives, including: (1) people with lived experience of BPD, (2) loved ones of people with BPD, given they are often substantially involved in supporting the daily and treatment needs of the person with BPD (Bailey and Grenyer 2013), and (3) mental health providers who work with BPD, especially in acute psychiatric settings. We used an open‐ended, online survey to answer the following questions: (1) How would integrated PSS change your experiences of acute psychiatric services, if at all? (2) What types of trainings, education and structure would support effective peer support? (3) What do you see as some of the challenges or obstacles that would need to be addressed or overcome before PSS could be effectively integrated in acute psychiatric services?

Methods

Participants

A total of 84 people began the online survey, of whom 49 consented to participate, 41 provided demographic and closed‐ended responses, and 25 responded to open‐ended questions. Most respondents self‐identified as women (78% [n = 32]; 14% [n = 6] transgender, gender queer or non‐binary, 7% [n = 3] men) and White (93%, [n = 38]; 7% [n = 3] Indigenous, 5% [n = 2] Hispanic/Latinx). Thirty respondents reported a personal history of BPD (73%), 7 identified as loved ones and 13 had professional experience working with BPD (note: lived/professional experience categories were not mutually exclusive). Many respondents had received (56% [n = 23]) or provided (63% [n = 26]) peer support.

Measures

Survey content was informed by the experiences of the second author in providing support to people with BPD and by two initial community listening sessions we held, one with mental health service users and a second with clinicians. From these experiences, we developed nine open‐ended questions to elicit patient and provider experiences in acute psychiatric (i.e., inpatient and emergency psychiatry) services and their perspectives on the acceptability of integrating peer support for BPD in acute psychiatric services (see Table 1).

Procedures

Procedures were approved by the Research Ethics Board of the local Health Authority (H18‐02606). To recruit people with lived experience of BPD and their loved ones, study invitations were announced at in‐person meetings and on social media of a provincial charitable organisation that provides peer support and advocacy for people with BPD. To recruit health providers, we advertised the survey on the regional public Health Authority's Mental Health and Substance Use email listserv and at meetings of their internal quality council groups. Participants were offered a $10 e‐gift card. The survey was open from 05/2019 to 10/2019.

Analyses

We used a thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2006) to examine responses to the open‐ended survey questions. Free‐text responses were coded by two authors (Author 1, Author 3). To facilitate close engagement with the data, coders: (1) independently read the set of responses several times, (2) generated initial open codes and potential themes, (3) met to discuss their initial codes and elaborate on areas of agreement or divergence, (4) reviewed the responses again to refine the codes and themes, and (5) defined and named the themes and organised excerpts within each theme. Responses to a quantitative rating item were summarised descriptively by respondent experience (lived vs. professional).

Results

Qualitative Results

Participant responses varied in length, with an average of 222 words (SD = 184 words), and ranging from 19 words (i.e., one respondent who provided one word or short phrase responses to all prompts) to 762 words. While these responses were almost certainly briefer than we likely would have obtained had we used an interview, participants expressed their ideas succinctly in written format.

Theme 1: Feeling Seen, Heard and Understood (To Counter Shame)

The first theme focused on the benefits of finding a sense of shared experience and understanding in mitigating prominent experiences of shame related to the diagnosis and experience of living with BPD, which were often triggered or intensified when people seek care from acute psychiatric services. Responses described that people often felt a sense of foreignness or incomprehensibility regarding their experiences—a sense, both implied and explicitly stated, that how they felt and acted was strange, crazy, bizarre or made no sense. Authentic understanding was perceived as profoundly healing of this shame, and respondents described peer workers as uniquely equipped to offer this understanding given their similar lived experience. As several respondents put it:

While accepting care can be difficult for those with BPD who struggle with shame, peer support from someone who is well‐trained in validation and recognizing when someone is going into a shame spiral can be pivotal towards encouraging someone with BPD to accept help and be motivated to make changes in their life.

Learning that you aren't crazy because of having intense survival tactics when you needed them. Having someone or people to relate to, to help say “that might not be normal, but I do it too. It might not be okay right now, but we will both get there.” A lot of us just need a helping hand.

Several respondents also reflected on the confusion and overwhelm that is felt following initial diagnosis of BPD, which is often made during contact with acute psychiatric services, and how helpful it would be to have an opportunity to hear from and ask questions of a person who shared the diagnosis. For example,

Being surrounded by doctors can be overwhelming… Finding out you have or may have BPD is frightening—it can feel like your world is crashing down on you and there is nothing that can help you. In my experience it felt like the beginning of the end. Now I know that it was the beginning of a new start at life but the way the current system works certainly does not make it feel that way. Nothing compares to relating to someone else who has lived experience with BPD.

Theme 2: Connection and Belonging (To Counter Isolation)

The second theme described feelings of isolation and aloneness related to the hospital experience (e.g., being left alone for several hours while waiting to speak with a doctor), which amplified feelings of alienation from the community in general. Several respondents wrote that they believed they must be the only person struggling in the way they did. Peer workers were viewed as powerful counters to this narrative, providing concrete embodiment of a community of people with similar experiences. As respondents explained:

Relating to someone else with lived experience is critical in reducing the isolation people with BPD often feel because their thoughts, feelings and behaviours are so different from folks without BPD.

A sense of belonging is so important as folks with BPD often feel like outcasts at home, work and school, and when involved in sports and social activities, as well as every other aspect of life that involves people.

One respondent also wrote that peer support could create:

A sense of community, and being able to hear from other people that these thoughts we think and the big emotions we feel are not limited to us alone.

At the same time, some respondents expressed concern that emotional connection and relationships can be difficult for people with BPD to navigate when they are in crisis, and that a sense of community is best engendered when it is consistent and dependable. Sudden unavailability of a peer worker could trigger feelings of abandonment and exacerbate distress. Several respondents emphasised the need for stable and predictable support and consistent staffing. One respondent suggested: ‘Have backups for peer supporters in case someone has to cancel. Consistency is so important for folks with BPD.’ Another elaborated: ‘I've seen many people through my times at the hospital, and no one is consistent, thus I don't trust the support they offer.’ These responses seemed to underscore how one‐off, ad‐hoc interactions might undermine a sense of meaningful connection to a larger group or shared identity.

Provider respondents were concerned about possible transference reactions between patients and peers, the possibility for ‘splitting’ in which polarised views of medical staff versus peer workers could create therapeutic challenges, and difficulty maintaining a professional atmosphere if too many personal experiences were shared.

Finally, several respondents wrote about the importance of care transitions, especially being discharged from acute psychiatric services back to the community, and how unsuccessful transitions contribute to a sense of abandonment and lack of connection. One respondent said that they would find peer support helpful ‘only if they were able to offer regular, meaningful and routine follow‐up.’ Another person wrote:

If the peer support stops abruptly upon being discharged then it could have negative and potentially severe consequences for the person with BPD. There needs to be a resource package of ALL support in the community (MH services, private services, food services, housing supports, special interest groups/sports/clubs, etc.). There needs to be a more holistic approach to helping people with BPD.

Theme 3: Reducing Negative or Stigmatising Care Experiences (To Counter Detachment and Anger)

The third identified theme focused on negative and stigmatising experiences that people with BPD encounter in acute psychiatric settings, particularly from hospital staff. As one respondent wrote, ‘We are the most stigmatized folks in the hospital setting and don't get treated fairly or kindly.’ Many respondents reported that they felt misunderstood and judged by hospital staff, and this often led to a sense that the care they received was ineffective or, in some cases, harmful, and subsequent avoidance of acute psychiatry services. Increasing empathic and destigmatising care was viewed as necessary to re‐engage patients who had been harmed. Others described wanting greater accountability to ensure hospital staff are providing patients with BPD accurate and current information on the disorder, its treatment and its prognosis, underscoring the mistrust many respondents felt towards staff.

Respondents hoped that integrated PSS would reduce these experiences by providing direct counterexamples to stigmatising beliefs and by educating hospital staff about BPD (for instance, discussing the role of trauma in BPD). Others spoke about the complementary role of peer support workers as patient advocates. One person wrote:

Well, the last time I was hospitalized the emergency room doctor and the social worker I saw both treated me like I was faking my illness. If there were peer support workers with lived experience of BPD there to offer support and advocacy, it may have helped. Stigmatization by mental health professionals can be traumatic and make symptoms worse.

Others described the hospital environment and potential impact of peer support as follows:

There tends to be a ‘me versus them’ dichotomy. Adding peer support helps bridge the gap and changes the singular ‘me’ to ‘we’ and even helps breakdown the presence of a dichotomy.

Another benefit would be that doctors and nurses treating the patient with BPD will see the peer support worker and see what they've overcome, and maybe that will create less stigma.

Within this theme, several respondents expressed apprehension about how stigma could impact the peer workers. For instance, some responses worried that hospital staff would be resistant to peer support workers, refusing them access to patients even when it was clinically indicated and within the patient's rights to have an advocate present. Others worried about the potential for miscommunication arising from the distinct roles of hospital staff and peer workers interfering with treatment. Respondents emphasised that both peer workers and hospital staff would require orientation and ongoing training to support effective collaboration.

This theme also included several responses that emphasised that hospital staff are overburdened given the intense demands of acute psychiatric services, and often do not have the time they wish to spend counselling patients. While some responses viewed peer workers as potentially complementary to staff roles, for instance by being able to spend more time directly with patients providing coaching on de‐escalation and listening to patients' stories, others expressed concerns about role confusion. A few provider responses indicated that peer workers would be unwelcome in acute psychiatry settings, and would be viewed as ‘inappropriate’ and could introduce ‘an unwanted dynamic into an already busy work place.’

Theme 4: Instrumental and Informational Support (To Counter Bewilderment)

The fourth theme focused on the potential for peer support workers to offer instrumental and informational support to support crisis stabilisation and community care planning. For instance, respondents suggested that peer workers may be uniquely effective in teaching de‐escalation strategies to patients because they have experience using these skills themselves, potentially increasing patients' receptiveness to teaching. Additionally, respondents highlighted the complexity of effective discharge planning as a concern and indicated peer workers could complement the clinical team in suggesting local and online supports that could meet patients' goals and needs. Finally, some respondents indicated peer workers might be helpful in explaining the role and expectations of acute psychiatric services based on their experiences and in providing information for loved ones who may be impacted by BPD. For instance, clinician respondents described that peer support could result in ‘improved understanding of the continuum of care’, ‘knowledge of the condition’, ‘support with coping strategies’ and providing patients with information about online or community resources and referrals.

At the same time, at least one respondent felt that informational or instrumental support from peers would not meet their needs, given the acuity and crisis‐driven nature of acute psychiatric admissions. They explained:

At least for myself, when I am in hospital, a peer support group is a bit advanced for the kind of help I need. When I go to the hospital, I'm in crisis, so I need crisis support, not management support.

Theme 5: Psychological Safety (To Counter Traumatic Re‐Experiencing)

A fifth theme focused on the role of trauma and the need to incorporate a trauma‐informed approach in acute psychiatric settings. As one respondent wrote:

Many people with BPD, myself included, are complex trauma survivors. These behaviors are something our brain has adapted to survive child abuse, hostage situations, or other ACES [adverse childhood experiences] that last a prolonged amount of time.

To align services with a trauma‐informed approach, respondents emphasised the necessity of trust, mutual respect, healthy boundaries and that members of the care team, whether in peer or clinical roles, not take behaviours that may arise from traumatic histories, such as angry outbursts or dissociation, personally. One clinician viewed peer support as supportive in that it would:

Respect the client in a safe environment in order to gain trust towards making incremental positive changes in the client's personality.

Respondents expressed that peer workers should have good self‐awareness of their own emotional wellbeing to avoid detrimental reactions to patients' behaviours. Self‐care, training in effective peer support and crisis management, and availability of ongoing mental health support, debriefing and supervision for peer workers were deemed essential. Finally, respondents emphasised the importance of patient autonomy in directing peer support, including having a choice regarding with which peer worker they would like to work and when they would receive support, as trauma‐informed practice.

Theme 6: Hope (To Counter Despair)

The sixth and final theme related to hope, which was described as a powerful antidote to patients' and staff's feelings of despair. One person said:

Knowing that if other people with BPD can get through hard times, and come full circle to helping others, maybe we can too. Also hearing that someone else has been through what we're going through and has come out okay.

Another explained:

Hearing from someone else who has lived it (and come out the other side) gives hope to those who feel utterly hopeless. I had no idea BPD was treatable and that folks with BPD could have successful careers, families, and healthy social relationships. Telling people these things are possible is meaningless. Showing them is powerful.

Likewise, several clinician respondents highlighted that the availability of peer support could help ‘put [patients] at ease’, ‘provide a positive perspective for the client to assist in personality changes’ and result in ‘installation of hope for patients with BPD’.

Perceived Barriers or Limitations to Peer Support

Several respondents, particularly clinicians, expressed potential concerns about peer support. These included the potential for incongruency between the goals of peer workers and clinical staff, challenges in interprofessional communication, the requirement for adequate training and coping skills for peer workers to maintain their own mental health, and the potential for stigmatising attitudes among staff to impact peer workers (e.g., ‘resistance from staff due to fear of change and concerns regarding possible splitting behaviours between workers and [clinical staff]’). Illustrating this point, one provider expressed that they would ‘never’ support the integration of peer workers in an acute psychiatric setting, noting that a ‘…health care provider does not need to have a broken leg in order to provide care/diagnose a patient with a broken leg.’ Conversely, another provider wrote that ‘… anything that supports a positive interaction with the BPD client has got to be beneficial to me and my work.’

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the acceptability of integrating peer support for people with BPD with usual treatment in acute psychiatric settings. Given the frequency with which people with BPD access acute psychiatric services (Broadbear et al. 2022; Gregory et al. 2021; Sveen et al. 2024; Álvarez‐Tomás et al. 2024; Pasic et al. 2005), and the significant stigma and negative care experiences they report encountering there (Bodner et al. 2015; Lawn and McMahon 2015; Rogers and Dunne 2011; Stapleton and Wright 2019), there is a need to explore how these services could better align with recovery‐oriented care principles. Before discussing the implications of our findings, we acknowledge that our respondents were predominantly White, cis‐gender females; thus, our results should be interpreted with the limitations of this sample in mind. Namely, the potential for additional harms that occur when persons with racialised and gender minority identities access care to impact perceptions of the appropriateness, benefits and limitations of peer support were not well captured in this sample. Further work acknowledging the possibility for intersectional harms and synergistic benefits is crucial prior to moving forward.

Overall, our results suggested that integrated peer support was acceptable to people with BPD, their loved ones and the clinical staff who work with them. Respondents highlighted the potential value of peer workers in being able to counter stigma for both patients and staff by providing a living example of recovery and by advocating against stigmatising attitudes and practices, which, in turn, would increase knowledge of and hope for positive outcomes. Peer workers were also viewed as uniquely equipped to demystify the diagnosis and its prognosis, provide tangible support and information to help patients learn and practice de‐escalation skills and access appropriate community supports following discharge, and offer a sense of community and shared experience. Each of these competencies was highly valued by patients, loved ones and providers as contributing to clinical stabilisation, effective treatment planning and long‐term outcomes. At the same time, several potential implementation obstacles were highlighted, including perceived incompatibility of the goals of peer support (i.e., empowerment, autonomy and hope) with immediate and clinical goals of acute psychiatric services (i.e., crisis stabilisation and acute symptom relief), potential for role confusion between clinical and peer staff, and the logistical challenges associated with ensuring that peer support workers are consistently and predictably available so that trusting, trauma‐informed relationships with patients can be built. Generally, our results support further exploration of the feasibility, operational challenges and needs, and implementation of integrated peer support for people with BPD in acute psychiatric services.

While previous studies have examined clinical staff's perceptions, knowledge and practice of recovery‐oriented care in acute psychiatric settings (Cleary et al. 2013; Tsai and Salyers 2010), fewer have jointly examined acceptability from both patient and provider perspectives (Chen et al. 2013). Our results suggest overall alignment between people with lived experience (i.e., patients and loved ones) and clinical staff in their support for integrated peer support, albeit with slightly less favourable views expressed by clinical staff. We note that, of the minority of responses that expressed significant reservations about or rejection of integrated peer support services in acute psychiatric settings, the strongest opposition was expressed by providers. Adopting a recovery‐oriented approach requires listening to, prioritising and finding ways to be responsive to the wants and needs of people with lived experience. This suggests that successful implementation will require education, training and collaboration with clinical staff to increase their knowledge and understanding of the role and value of peer support in recovery‐oriented practice. Indeed, a recent meta‐analysis identified staff attitudes as a key facilitator or barrier to the successful implementation of peer support (Ibrahim et al. 2020). Identifying and exploring reasons for hesitancy may also help organisations anticipate and plan for potential operational challenges, supporting a smoother transition.

Mental health services increasingly affirm principles of recovery‐oriented care, which include person‐centeredness, patient empowerment, strengths‐(versus deficits‐)based conceptualisation and partnership between mental health clinicians and patients (Kidd et al. 2014; Lorien et al. 2020; Waldemar et al. 2016). Previous analysis has suggested that recovery‐oriented practice and evidence‐based medicine can be considered complementary, particularly when mental health providers and patients are able to engage in genuine collaboration towards the patient's goals (Davidson et al. 2009; see, however, Rose 2014, for an opposing view). However, uptake of these principles has been deeper in outpatient services relative to acute and inpatient settings (Shalaby and Agyapong 2020; Tsai and Salyers 2010), and acute psychiatric clinical staff report that adoption of recovery‐oriented principles remains aspirational (or, more cynically, rhetorical) rather than realised (Cleary et al. 2013). Indeed, misunderstandings of the recovery‐oriented framework, including a belief that engagement with treatment is necessary and sufficient for recovery, persist (Slade et al. 2014). Recent reviews have documented organisational, structural and professional barriers to implementing recovery‐oriented care in acute psychiatric services and suggested that most hospital psychiatry services remain problem‐ or deficits‐focused and medically oriented with limited adoption of recovery‐oriented practices (Gwinner and Ward 2013; Waldemar et al. 2016). Common constraints to implementing recovery‐oriented practice in acute psychiatric settings include high patient turnover, staffing challenges, perceived incompatibility of crisis stabilisation versus recovery goals, operational and logistical challenges, and space issues (Gwinner and Ward 2013; Waldemar et al. 2016). Additional facilitators and barriers to implementing peer support arise from the organisational culture, peer worker training, role definitions, staff attitudes, resources and financial arrangements (Ibrahim et al. 2020).

It is noteworthy that many previous studies have focused on the capacity of clinical staff to adopt recovery‐oriented practices (Gwinner and Ward 2013; Waldemar et al. 2016). Integrated peer support may provide an opportunity to expand the care team in ways that could complement existing roles and responsibilities. Additionally, peer support workers may not face the same set of constraints as medical staff. Indeed, a recent study of peer support workers in German psychiatric hospitals found that their role was well integrated and complementary to the medical team, with unique knowledge and capacities to offer one‐on‐one support to patients and continuity of care following discharge (Nossek et al. 2021). However, a randomised trial that compared 4 months of one‐on‐one peer support plus treatment as usual (TAU) to TAU alone for psychiatric inpatients with at least one previous psychiatric admission did not find evidence of incremental benefits to peer support with respect to psychiatric readmission, nor serious adverse events (Gillard et al. 2022). A limitation of this trial was that personal recovery outcomes, such as life satisfaction and meaning, hope and empowerment, were not assessed, limiting the ability to determine whether peer support provided tangible benefits to the outcomes it is arguably most aligned with. Of course, peer support is not the only means of aligning services with recovery‐oriented principles, and the goal of recovery‐oriented care is to develop a vision that is shared by all members of the clinical team and service users.

This study contributes to an ongoing conversation regarding ways to reimagine acute psychiatric care for BPD (Enoksson et al. 2022; Fowler et al. 2018; Paris 2015; Webb et al. 2023; Besch et al. 2020). There is a decades‐long disagreement about the value of inpatient psychiatric admissions for people with BPD, fuelled by concerns that such admissions may precipitate clinical deterioration, engender dependence or coercion in therapeutic relationships and limit opportunities to learn necessary problem‐solving and crisis survival skills, while having limited demonstrated efficacy in preventing suicide (Besch et al. 2020; Paris 2004; Webb et al. 2023). Moreover, people with BPD have reported significant harms resulting from their interactions with psychiatric care systems. Still, given that people with BPD experience high rates of self‐harm, suicide and emotional crisis, the prevention and management of which is a primary goal of acute psychiatric services, and that access to specialised psychotherapies that support longer‐term stabilisation remains extremely limited (Tomko et al. 2014; Tusiani‐Eng and Yeomans 2018), it is likely that people with BPD will continue to be high utilisers of acute psychiatric services. Recent evidence contradicts the assumption that inpatient admissions are iatrogenic for patients with BPD (Fowler et al. 2018). Moreover, innovative models of care, including stepped care (Choi‐Kain et al. 2016; Paris 2015) and planned brief admissions (Eckerström et al. 2019; Enoksson et al. 2022), provide alternatives that may increase patient agency and autonomy and decrease negative care experiences. We note that integrating peer support is being trialled in innovative ways as well. For instance, researchers recently evaluated a tablet‐based app that, in addition to delivering evidence‐based components of suicide prevention and care planning, offered acutely suicidal patients in the emergency department opportunities to engage with psychoeducational videos by people with lived experience concerning what to expect during the emergency department visit and after discharge, coping with shame and messages of hope (Dimeff et al. 2021). This demonstrates the potential for creative solutions to increasing access to peers and peer support, even in settings where availability of an in‐person peer worker may not be feasible.

Limitations

Our results should be interpreted with several limitations in mind. First, there are a number of limitations related to the convenience sampling approach used in this study that may limit the generalisability of these results. Although we aimed to recruit both people with lived experience and mental health providers, we received many more responses from people with lived experience. Thus, our results may not fully appreciate the scope of positive or negative attitudes of clinical staff towards integrated peer support given this limited sample. Only 30% of people who opened the survey provided responses to the set of open‐ended questions, likely pointing to self‐selection in completion. On the one hand, we likely heard from stakeholders who had strong opinions, either in favour or against peer support. On the other hand, there may be additional points of view that were not captured because we used an open‐ended, online survey method. Moreover, we did not restrict recruitment to clinical staff who work in acute psychiatric settings as we aimed to include a breadth of perspectives, including from providers who work in outpatient settings, in this initial study. Future work engaging staff from acute psychiatric settings in particular will be needed to identify potential implementation barriers and facilitators. We also note that our sample is composed predominantly of White women. The gender breakdown is not unexpected, as BPD is more commonly diagnosed in women (American Psychiatric Association 2022). However, additional work is needed to understand the needs and priorities of men, gender diverse folks and racialized people. This is particularly important as it pertains to both improving psychological and cultural safety of psychiatric services, and as it impacts the practice of peer support, given consumers from different backgrounds may have vastly different experiences, beliefs and expectations surrounding diagnosis and treatment (Corrigan 2024). Second, there are some limitations associated with the scope of this study. Namely, we focused this work on the question of acceptability to patients, caregivers and providers, and as a result, we did not explore operational and organisational practices that may impact feasibility. Future studies should incorporate healthcare decision‐makers, administrators and leaders, as their perspectives are key to identifying and addressing implementation barriers (Piat et al. 2010).

Conclusions

Despite being touted as an aspirational direction in many mental health systems, implementation of recovery‐oriented practice in acute psychiatric settings has been challenging (Gwinner and Ward 2013; Waldemar et al. 2016). This study provides support for the idea that integrating peer support in acute psychiatric services could improve both patient and provider experiences by aligning services with BPD patients' personal recovery needs, offering a practical way to increase recovery orientation. Peer support workers have unique skills and experiences that allow them to offer a sense of understanding, connection, trust and hope, which are essential to recovery‐oriented practice. More broadly, considering ways in which personal recovery goals can complement crisis stabilisation goals may increase health systems' readiness to adopt new practices that would ultimately improve patient and provider experiences.

Relevance for Clinical Practice

People with BPD are frequent utilisers of acute psychiatry services, but the full scope of their recovery needs cannot be met in such services given their time‐limited, stabilisation‐oriented nature. Integrated peer support nonetheless represents an opportunity to align acute services with recovery‐oriented principles, and is viewed as broadly acceptable by patients, loved ones and clinicians. By modelling recovery, providing in‐the‐moment validation and skills coaching, and sharing knowledge gained by virtue of their shared lived experience, peer workers are uniquely suited to helping patients and staff develop the knowledge and skills to promote recovery.
