Introduction

Mirizzi syndrome (MS) is a rare complication of long-standing gallstone disease, first described by Pablo Luis Mirizzi in 1948[1]. It is characterized by extrinsic compression of the common hepatic duct (CHD) due to an impacted gallstone in the cystic duct or gallbladder neck, often leading to obstructive jaundice. Over time, chronic inflammation and pressure necrosis can result in the formation of a cholecystobiliary fistula, as seen in advanced cases like Type IV MS. Despite advances in imaging techniques, preoperative diagnosis remains challenging[2]

We present a case of Type IV MS, as classified by Csendes (2008), in a 69-year-old woman diagnosed preoperatively and successfully managed through an open surgical approach.

Case presentation

A 69-year-old woman, with no significant medical history, presented to the emergency department with generalized pruritus, jaundice, fever, and upper-right quadrant abdominal pain. Examination showed abdominal guarding. Laboratory investigations revealed an inflammatory syndrome (C-reactive protein: 80 mg/L, white blood cells: 15 000/μL). Also, they indicated cholestasis with gamma-glutamyl transferase levels at 538 IU/L, alkaline phosphatase at 800 IU/L, total bilirubin at 135 μmol/L, with a predominance of conjugated bilirubin at 116 μmol/L. Hepatic cytolysis was four times the normal level. Abdominal ultrasonography showed dilatation of intrahepatic ducts upstream of an obstruction at the CHD, with an atrophic gallbladder and a thickened wall. A subsequent abdominal computed tomography (CT) scan revealed dilated bile ducts upstream of a 17-mm stone located in the distal common bile duct (CBD). The gallbladder appeared atrophic containing 2 macro calculi. The cystic duct and the main bile duct were inseparable at the level of the lower biliary confluence, where a 25-mm stone was also identified. These findings are consistent with acute cholecystitis complicated by MS Type IV, with migration of a stone into the distal CBD (Fig. 1). To confirm the diagnosis, a biliary magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed, revealing a fistula between the gallbladder and the CBD and an obstructive gallstone in the distal bile duct. Notably, no pancreatic mass syndrome or duodenal papillary syndrome was detected. These findings strongly suggested MS, classified as Type IV (Fig. 2). The patient was put on wide specter antibiotics. Endoscopic treatment was deemed difficult due to the size of the gallstones. The decision was to operate the patient via laparotomy. The patient underwent general anesthesia for surgery through a right subcostal approach. Exploration revealed a scleroatrophic gallbladder adherent to the CBD. A cholecystotomy was performed, allowing for the extraction of two gallstones from the gallbladder and one stone from the distal CBD via the cholecystobiliary fistula using stone forceps. The bile duct clearance was verified using a choledoscope. The cholecystotomy was then closed over a T-tube (Kehr drain) (Fig. 3). A cholangiography was conducted, confirming the clearance of the main bile duct (Fig. 4). The procedure was completed with subhepatic drainage using a Salem tube. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, with the T-tube drain evacuating approximately 300 cc daily. The patient was discharged on the 6th postoperative day, with normalization of blood tests after 10 days during outpatient follow-up. At 3 months postoperative, the T-tube drain was removed after a cholangiography confirming the vacuity of the CBD. The patient was in good health and asymptomatic at 1-year follow-up. Discussion

MS, first described by Pablo Luis Mirizzi in 1948[4], is a rare complication of chronic cholelithiasis, occurring in 0.05%–4% of patients undergoing cholecystectomy. It results from an impacted gallstone in the cystic duct or gallbladder neck, leading to external compression of the CHD, which can progress to cholecystocholedochal fistula formation in advanced cases. The rarity and variable presentation of MS contribute to significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, often requiring a combination of imaging modalities and intraoperative findings to confirm the diagnosis and determine the appropriate treatment strategy[5].

The syndrome was first described by Argentinean surgeon Pablo Luis Mirizzi in 1948, and since then, it has been classified into various types, with Type IV being the most complex and challenging to treat. Type IV MS represents the most advanced form, where there is complete destruction of the bile duct wall and a cholecystocholedochal fistula[6].

MS often presents with jaundice, right hypochondrium pain, anorexia, and vomiting. However, in some cases, it remains asymptomatic, leading to delayed diagnosis and an increased risk of complications. If left untreated, MS can progress to severe conditions such as cholecystocholedochal fistula formation, cholangitis, gallbladder empyema, gallstone ileus, bile duct perforation, biliary strictures, and hepatic abscess.

In our case, the patient presented with angiocholitis due to the blockage of the CBD.

The classification of MS has evolved over time to facilitate better diagnosis and treatment planning. The most commonly used systems are those by Csendes and Beltran[1,7], which categorize MS based on the degree of biliary involvement:

Accurate diagnosis and classification of MS require a comprehensive evaluation using both radiological and endoscopic imaging techniques.

Ultrasonography (US) remains a commonly used initial diagnostic tool, though its sensitivity varies significantly across studies, ranging from just a few percent to nearly 50% in most cases[8]. Given its accessibility and noninvasive nature, US serves as a useful first-line investigation, guiding subsequent diagnostic steps. Some authors have suggested that modern advancements in ultrasonography technology could further improve its diagnostic accuracy[9].

CT imaging has demonstrated a diagnostic sensitivity comparable to US[9] but is particularly valuable in differentiating MS from malignant strictures. This distinction is critical, as MS can mimic cholangiocarcinoma, and, in some cases, it coexists with gallbladder carcinoma (GBC)[10]. Notably, patients with concurrent MS and GBC tend to be a decade older and exhibit a longer history of symptoms than those with MS alone, emphasizing the need for thorough preoperative evaluation.

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) are often compared for their diagnostic efficacy in MS. Both modalities exhibit high sensitivity, typically ranging from 63% to 89% for MRCP and from 63% to 72% for ERCP[5]. While MRCP offers the advantage of being noninvasive, its availability and high-cost limit widespread use. Additionally, MRCP alone may sometimes struggle to distinguish benign from malignant biliary strictures without concurrent conventional MRI imaging. On the other hand, ERCP remains the gold standard for diagnosing MS, owing to its high sensitivity and therapeutic capabilities. Beyond confirming the diagnosis, ERCP allows for interventions such as biliary stenting, which can be critical in selected cases[11].

Given the diagnostic complexities of MS, a multimodal imaging approach – combining US, CT, MRCP, and ERCP – can optimize detection rates and facilitate more effective management strategies. Some studies have suggested that integrating MRCP with CT might further improve diagnostic accuracy, ensuring a more reliable classification of MS cases[11]. These findings reinforce the need for a tailored, patient-specific approach to imaging, particularly when differentiating MS from malignancies or planning appropriate surgical interventions.

A significant number of MS cases are only identified intraoperatively, as preoperative imaging may fail to provide a definitive diagnosis. During surgery, several characteristic findings may indicate the presence of MS, including an edematous or atrophic gallbladder, severe fibrosis and adhesions around Calot’s triangle, and an impacted gallstone within the infundibulum or cystic duct neck[12]. Distortion of Calot’s triangle often complicates dissection and increases the risk of bile duct injury. The presence of a cholecystobiliary fistula should be strongly suspected if bile leakage occurs following the extraction of an impacted stone[13]. In such cases, intraoperative cholangiography is a valuable tool for determining the exact location and size of the fistula, identifying residual stones, and assessing the integrity of the bile duct wall. However, this technique can be technically demanding due to the altered anatomy and the dense inflammatory process, increasing the risk of iatrogenic bile duct injury.

Surgical intervention remains the primary treatment for MS; however, it presents multiple challenges. First, due to its rarity, many surgeons have a low level of suspicion for this condition, as gallbladder surgeries are often performed earlier in the disease course, before MS fully develops. Second, the condition is frequently undiagnosed preoperatively, which complicates intraoperative management. Additionally, chronic inflammation leads to dense adhesions, anatomical distortion, and the potential formation of cholecystobiliary or cholecystoenteric fistulas, increasing the risk of bile duct injury or severe hemorrhage during dissection of Calot’s triangle.

Historically, laparotomy has been the preferred approach for managing MS due to its relative safety compared to laparoscopy, which has high conversion rates (31%–100%) and an increased risk of bile duct injury[2]. While laparotomy is more invasive and associated with higher complication rates and prolonged hospital stays, it offers advantages such as superior visualization, tactile feedback, and the ability to remove gallbladder calculi before performing cholecystectomy, thereby facilitating a more controlled surgical procedure.

In our case, we opted for a minimally invasive approach using a cholecystotomy, an extraction of the stones, and cholecystoplasty using a T-tube drain.

Various diagnostic and treatment algorithms have been proposed for MS[2,9,12,14,15]; however, there remains no universally accepted consensus on its classification or the optimal management approach for each type. Given the complexity and variability of this condition, establishing a standardized set of guidelines through broader consensus is crucial to improving diagnostic accuracy and surgical outcomes.

Conclusion

MS, though uncommon, poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges that demand heightened clinical awareness to prevent misdiagnosis and delayed treatment. Early recognition is crucial, as untreated cases can progress to life-threatening complications such as bile duct strictures, secondary biliary cirrhosis, and GBC. Despite advances in imaging modalities, no single diagnostic tool guarantees absolute accuracy, necessitating a multimodal approach that combines US, CT, MRCP, and ERCP for optimal evaluation. The absence of a universally accepted classification system and standardized treatment protocol further complicates decision-making. Therefore, establishing consensus guidelines for diagnosis and management is essential to improve patient outcomes. A multidisciplinary approach, integrating hepatobiliary surgeons, gastroenterologists, and radiologists, is fundamental in tailoring individualized treatment plans and reducing surgical morbidity. Our case underscores the importance of a thorough preoperative evaluation, the role of combined imaging strategies, and the necessity of surgical expertise in managing advanced MS effectively.
