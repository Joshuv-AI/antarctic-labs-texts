Adaptive responses to environmental variability in rotifers: Integrating plasticity and bet hedging in dormancy exit
Authors: Carlota Solano‐Udina, María José Carmona, Eduardo M García‐Roger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnvironmental variability imposes strong selective pressures that may favor adaptive responses such as phenotypic plasticity and bet hedging. Plasticity is generally favored under predictable conditions, whereas bet hedging is advantageous against environmental uncertainty. Plastic responses rely on reliable cues to adjust phenotypes to future conditions, whereas bet hedging spreads risks when cues are unreliable. However, because environments often consist of both predictable and unpredictable components, combined responses may be selected. Here, plasticity and bet hedging were examined in diapause‐exit traits of Brachionus plicatilis clones sampled along a natural gradient of environmental predictability regarding hydroperiod length. Diapausing egg hatching was experimentally assessed under six salinity treatments, with salinity considered as a potential cue for diapause termination in predictable habitats. Because salinity integrates water volume and evaporation, it can act as an informative cue of the expected hydroperiod duration. For each clone, hatching fraction and timing of hatching were quantified across salinities, from which three key traits were derived: (1) a plasticity index of hatching fraction, capturing sensitivity to salinity; (2) the magnitude of hatching fraction across the studied salinity range, reflecting risk spreading among growing seasons through incomplete hatching (i.e., among‐season bet hedging); and (3) the variation in hatching time, spreading risk within a growing season through asynchronous hatching (i.e., within‐season bet hedging). Across clones, hatching fractions were higher and hatching occurred faster at low salinities, whereas hatching was delayed or even inhibited at higher salinities. Such a peaked response suggests that salinity is a reliable cue of favorable future conditions. Moreover, clones showed intermediate hatching fractions, indicative of bet hedging. Clones with higher hatching fractions also showed more asynchronous hatching, indicating potential functional redundancy between among‐season and within‐season bet hedging. Additionally, clones exhibiting stronger plastic responses showed higher hatching fractions, supporting the interpretation of phenotypic plasticity and bet hedging as alternative yet potentially co‐occurring adaptive responses. Finally, plasticity was greater in clones originating from predictable habitats, whereas bet‐hedging traits were stronger in clones from less predictable environments. Overall, these findings highlight how the reliability of environmental cues can shape the evolution of combined adaptive responses in fluctuating environments.Keywords: bet hedging, Brachionus plicatilis, diapause‐exit strategies, mixed strategies, phenotypic plasticity, predictability, time‐varying environments

INTRODUCTION

Organisms inhabiting time‐varying environments are challenged to schedule the timing of life history traits, such as birth, growth, or reproduction, with the occurrence of favorable conditions. To cope with this variability, life histories may evolve to accommodate conditions via phenotypic plasticity (Lande, 2009; Langerhans & Dewitt, 2004; Pigliucci, 2002) or to spread the risk of uncertainty in time through bet hedging (Cohen, 1966; Seger & Brockman, 1987; Simons, 2011).

Adaptive phenotypic plasticity—the capacity of a single genotype to exhibit different phenotypes in different environments—is expected to evolve in a trait when organisms have accurate information on forthcoming environmental conditions (i.e., reliable cues about selection on the expressed plastic phenotypes; Botero et al., 2015; Reed et al., 2010). On the other hand, bet hedging enables organisms to persist under unpredictable conditions, that is, when cues about future conditions are unreliable or lacking. It is defined as an adaptive response that reduces the variance in fitness across generations at the expense of reducing the arithmetic mean fitness (Simons, 2011; Starrfelt & Kokko, 2012), by spreading the risk of encountering an unfavorable environment over time typically through the production of a variety of phenotypes in the offspring of a genotype (i.e., the so‐called diversifying bet hedging). While phenotypic plasticity and bet hedging are often studied separately, it is increasingly recognized that environmental variability consists of both predictable and unpredictable components (Crean & Marshall, 2009; Donaldson‐Matasci et al., 2013; Simons, 2014). Thus, organisms may evolve combinations of both forms of evolutionary responses (Joschinski & Bonte, 2021, 2020; Simons, 2014).

Adaptive phenotypic plasticity is well documented in the scientific literature (for review, see Fox et al., 2019 and references therein), and rigorous empirical tests of bet hedging are growing too in the last years for a variety of organisms (Franch‐Gras, García‐Roger, Serra, & Carmona, 2017; Graham et al., 2014; Gremer & Venable, 2014; Pinceel et al., 2021; Solano‐Udina, Serra, et al., 2025; Tarazona et al., 2017). However, only a few studies exist assessing both phenotypic plasticity and bet hedging simultaneously (Furness et al., 2015; Grantham et al., 2016; Gremer et al., 2016; Mejbel & Simons, 2018; Simons, 2014; Van Den Elzen & Emery, 2026). In this sense, whether bet hedging or phenotypic plasticity is expressed to a lesser or greater degree depends on the reliability of the cues for environmental change (Botero et al., 2015; Joschinski & Bonte, 2021; Leung et al., 2020).

In this study, we focus on the reactivation of organisms after a period of dormancy, a crucial ecological transition in time‐varying environments. Our aim is to provide an empirical test for the joint expression of phenotypic plasticity and bet hedging in traits related to this transition, and to assess the circumstances under which each response mode is more frequently expressed. To this end, we take advantage of a study system which includes a collection of natural habitats covering a gradient of predictability, and an organism whose ecology has been widely studied, and which has been shown to be useful for empirically testing theoretical predictions in relation to environmental unpredictability. Water bodies in the Mediterranean region serve as good examples of time‐varying environments (Blondel & Aronson, 1999), spanning a range from nearly permanent to highly seasonal and temporally unpredictable systems, driven mainly by rainfall patterns (Franch‐Gras, García‐Roger, Franch, et al., 2017; Solano‐Udina, Serra, et al., 2025). In non‐permanent ponds, resident organisms, such as zooplankton, are strongly reliant on pond inundation, so they undergo life cycles at annual or even shorter time scales and require a dormant phase for surviving between hydroperiods.

Monogonont rotifers are a major component of continental zooplankton and common inhabitants of temporary ponds. They are cyclical parthenogens combining proliferation by clonal reproduction with occasional bouts of sexual reproduction. Brachionus plicatilis Müller 1786 sensu stricto is the best‐known monogonont and a widely used model in evolutionary ecology studies (Declerck & Papakostas, 2017; Serra et al., 2019). In temperate climates, natural populations of B. plicatilis are active in the water column only during a fraction of the year, the so‐called planktonic growing season (hereafter, growing season), which begins with the hatching of diapausing eggs from the pond sediment. Hatchlings are asexual females that produce genetically identical daughters, so populations are composed of a collection of clones (genotypes), each originating from a single diapausing egg. After several asexual generations, sexual reproduction is initiated, typically resulting in the production of a new cohort of diapausing eggs (e.g., Gilbert, 1974; Schröder, 2005). Diapausing eggs settle in the sediment, where they can resist desiccation and other adverse biotic and abiotic conditions, and—after an obligatory dormant period (Hagiwara & Hino, 1989; Martínez‐Ruiz & García‐Roger, 2015)—can hatch when suitable conditions return and start a new growing season. However, not all diapausing eggs hatch in the season following their production; some remain dormant forming long‐lived banks (García‐Roger et al., 2006a; Kotani et al., 2005). Since diapause is the only way to survive unsuitable conditions between growing seasons, the number of diapausing eggs produced by a genotype during a growing season is a major component of its between‐year fitness (Serra & King, 1999).

The exit from diapause is a critical transition at which a rotifer leaves the relative safety of the dormant stage and enters a life stage that is more vulnerable to the variability in ecological conditions (García‐Roger et al., 2019). Therefore, the timing of exiting diapause can have important consequences on fitness and is expected to be under strong selective pressure. Variation in the timing of exiting diapause has been observed at the within‐genotype level in rotifers (Martínez‐Ruiz & García‐Roger, 2015; Schröder, 2005), which may be underpinned by phenotypic plasticity, bet hedging, or a combination of both (García‐Roger et al., 2014, 2017). For adaptive phenotypic plasticity to evolve, diapausing eggs must perceive cues that reliably predict conditions leading to successful colonization of the water column. Water presence, temperature, or salinity may act as cues for diapausing egg hatching, but they are not equally reliable. For instance, water presence may be a first cue of habitat suitability, but successful clonal proliferation requires appropriate conditions beyond pond refilling. One key source of uncertainty that rotifers face is for how long the water will persist in the pond. Interestingly, fluctuations of the water level in the shallow, endorheic ponds that serve as habitat for rotifers are typically accompanied by variation in water salinity. In these habitats, salt concentration rises because of water evaporation, whereas rainfall causes a decrease in water salinity through dilution. Thus, salinity might provide reliable information about the phase of the flood–drought cycle at which the pond is, but also about the likely duration of the hydroperiod, offering diapausing rotifer eggs a cue to time their exit from diapause. Low salinity, occurring when a pond has recently been filled by rainfall, would indicate the early stages of the hydroperiod and a higher probability that water will persist long enough to allow hatching, clonal proliferation, and the production of a new cohort of diapausing eggs. In contrast, high salinity may reflect evaporation and the approach of desiccation, signaling a shorter remaining hydroperiod and a reduced likelihood that the reproductive cycle can be completed successfully.

Variation in the timing of exiting diapause within a genotype may also arise via bet hedging if the environment is somewhat unpredictable, for example, when salinity is not a reliable cue (for review, see García‐Roger et al., 2014). According to bet‐hedging theory, spreading diapausing egg hatching among or within growing seasons reduces the risk that all hatchlings will face an unexpectedly adverse environment that could lead to a zero‐fitness event. Among‐season bet hedging is supported by experimental evidence in B. plicatilis, where, under an experimentally manipulated regime of unpredictable growing season lengths—conditions in which rotifers could not anticipate the end of the growing season—diapausing eggs evolved intermediate hatching fractions—that is, only a subset of diapausing eggs hatched under favorable conditions while others remain dormant. This result demonstrates the evolution of bet hedging in response to unpredictability in the season length (Tarazona et al., 2017). Within‐season bet hedging, in contrast, involves spreading hatching events over time within a single growing season, thereby reducing the risk that all hatchlings will experience adverse short‐term initial conditions (Gremer et al., 2016; Solano‐Udina, Serra, et al., 2025; ten Brink et al., 2020).

Here, we conducted an empirical study on the joint expression of phenotypic plasticity and bet hedging in traits related to the exit from diapause in wild populations of B. plicatilis. Assessing phenotypic plasticity requires quantifying how a genotype responds to variation in an environmental factor—in this case, salinity. However, strong sensitivity to salinity does not necessarily imply that a genotype evolved in a predictable environment, because plasticity alone cannot reveal whether salinity is a reliable environmental cue. Demonstrating cue reliability is inherently challenging (Bonamour et al., 2019), and in our system, it would require measuring salinity at the time of pond refilling and testing its correlation with subsequent hydroperiod length—an approach that is scientifically appealing but logistically unfeasible. Instead, we employed an external metric of environmental predictability derived from temporal variation in hydroperiod duration. By combining genotype‐specific sensitivity to salinity with pond‐specific predictability, we evaluated both the potential for salinity to act as an informative cue and whether the evolution of plasticity and bet hedging aligns with the predictable structure of each habitat, thereby empirically addressing their joint expression. Overall, we focused on three traits related to the duration of diapause in rotifers that allow us to unravel the role of phenotypic plasticity when cues are potentially reliable, and the role of bet hedging in coping with environmental unpredictability. The first trait of interest is the reaction norm of the hatching fraction of diapausing eggs across a salinity gradient. This trait captures each clone's sensitivity to this environmental cue and thus describes the plastic response of the genotype. The second trait is the magnitude of the hatching fraction—that is, an analogue of the subject of Cohen's (1966) classical model of among‐season bet hedging in which the optimum hatching fraction for a genotype equals the probability of encountering a “good” growing season—which accounts for the degree of predictability in the length of the growing season (for a review on rotifers, see García‐Roger et al., 2014). Lower diapausing egg hatching fractions would be selected for under unpredictable conditions, as a means to hedge against the risk of hatching during an adverse growing season. The third trait of interest is within‐season variation in the timing of hatching. This trait is expected to manifest with varying degrees of synchrony, depending on the degree of predictability regarding the onset of the growing season, and is therefore also considered a diversifying bet‐hedging trait. If phenotypic plasticity (high sensitivity of hatching fraction to salinity) and bet‐hedging traits (low hatching fraction and high asynchrony at hatching time) are alternative responses within a gradual continuum, their optimal balance should depend on the relative reliability of the environmental cue.

Specifically, we hypothesize that (1) salinity serves as a key environmental cue for rotifers by signaling the phase of the hydroperiod. If this is the case, we expect (1.1) the hatching fraction of diapausing eggs to vary across a salinity gradient—that is, genotypes will differ in their sensitivity to salinity with maximum hatching fractions at low salinity, potentially because it anticipates long hydroperiods. We also expect (1.2) the timing of hatching to depend on salinity, with delayed hatching occurring at higher salinities. We further hypothesize that (2) both phenotypic plasticity and bet hedging may evolve in traits related to the exit from diapause. If so, we expect (2.1) a within‐clone positive relationship between sensitivity to salinity and the magnitude of the hatching fraction. This expectation follows from the premise that greater sensitivity to salinity reflects higher plasticity, while a high hatching fraction (regardless of salinity) indicates a lower bet‐hedging response, and vice versa. We also expect (2.2) a negative relationship between sensitivity to salinity in hatching and variation in hatching time. Taken together, these expectations imply a continuum of strategies, from highly responsive plastic genotypes to risk‐spreading bet hedgers. Additionally, we expect (2.3) both addressed bet‐hedging traits—hatching fraction and variation in hatching time—to act as complementary risk‐spreading strategies, showing a positive relationship. It has been suggested that risk minimization can be achieved through different modes of bet hedging (García‐Roger et al., 2014; Simons, 2011), so once a genotype adopts a particular risk‐spreading strategy, additional strategies may not be necessary (Childs et al., 2010; Starrfelt & Kokko, 2012; ten Brink et al., 2020). Finally, we hypothesize that (3) the response mode that shapes the expression of diapausing egg hatching traits—plasticity versus bet hedging—varies with the reliability of the environmental cues. If this is the case, we expect (3.1) higher sensitivity of hatching fraction to salinity (i.e., phenotypic plasticity) in more predictable habitats, where salinity could act as a reliable cue allowing for this type of adaptive response, and both (3.2) lower diapausing egg hatching fractions and (3.3) higher variation in hatching time in unpredictable habitats, where salinity may not reliably predict the duration of the hydroperiod and spreading risk across time provides a selective advantage.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study system and environmental predictability quantification

This study focuses on B. plicatilis clones coming from wild populations inhabiting six shallow, brackish endorheic and non‐permanent ponds in eastern Iberian Peninsula (Figure 1). These water bodies span a 27‐km2 area, with distances between them ranging 0.6–14 km. Despite their geographical proximity, they are hydrologically isolated, lacking surface water connections, and exhibit marked variation in size and flooding dynamics (Table 1). Previous studies (e.g., Franch‐Gras, García‐Roger, Serra, & Carmona, 2017; Montero‐Pau et al., 2017; Ortells et al., 2000; Solano‐Udina, Serra, et al., 2025) have characterized these ponds, highlighting a substantial variation in salinity (both among ponds and within ponds across time) and a gradient of environmental predictability. Quantifying this gradient allowed us to test how clone sensitivity to salinity and the expression of bet hedging in hatching traits varied with pond predictability.

Previous studies used Landsat remote sensing imagery to quantify pond predictability in this system by applying a hydrology‐based approach. Here, we build on this work by reassessing the degree of environmental predictability in the original habitats of the studied rotifer clones at a finer temporal resolution. For this purpose, we compiled a dataset of 1073 multispectral satellite images from the European Space Agency's (ESA's) Sentinel‐2 mission (hereafter, scenes), covering the period 2015–2023. Scenes (100 m2 resolution, and approximately 3‐day revisit) were obtained via the Application Programming Interface (API) from the Sentinel Hub Catalog (https://services.sentinel-hub.com/api/v1/catalog/1.0.0/.). We retained an average of ca. 1000 cloud‐free scenes per pond (for details on the procedure, see Solano‐Udina, García Roger, & Carmona, 2025) on which water presence was assessed using the Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI; Gao, 1996; McFeeters, 1996). This index was chosen over others for its better performance after validation with randomly selected true‐color scenes in which the ponds had water or were completely dry. From the time‐series of water presence in each pond, we calculated the coefficient of variation of hydroperiod (CVH) length as a proxy of habitat unpredictability. This metric has proven adequate as an inverse measure of the degree of habitat stability in our system and for the organism under study (Solano‐Udina, Serra, et al., 2025).

Sampling and isolation of rotifer clones

Obtaining multiple clonal lines from each studied pond allowed us to capture within‐population genetic variation in plasticity and bet‐hedging hatching traits. To generate this set of clonal lines, sediment samples were collected in July 2022 from the six ponds. The upper sediment layer (approx. the top 10 cm) was sampled at three randomly selected points in each water body using a Van Veen grab. The three samples from each water body were pooled into a single container prior to further processing, as a way of ensuring the recovery of a sufficient number of diapausing eggs from the natural populations, given the patchy distribution of egg banks and the relatively low spatial heterogeneity of ecological condition within these ponds (Franch‐Gras, García‐Roger, Serra, & Carmona, 2017; García‐Roger et al., 2006b). The pooled samples were then stored at 4°C in darkness for at least 1 month and until further processing. Diapausing eggs were isolated from the sediment samples using a saccharose flotation technique (Gómez & Carvalho, 2000) and transferred individually into 96‐well plates containing 250 μL of 6 g L−1 artificial saline water (Instant Ocean, Aquarium Systems). These eggs were incubated at 25°C and constant light (photosynthetically active radiation, PAR: 35 μmol m−2 s−1) conditions (García‐Roger et al., 2005, 2006a). Once hatched, neonate females were transferred individually into 13‐mL tubes with culture medium and allowed to proliferate asexually, thereby establishing clonal lines. The rotifer culture medium consisted of 12 g L−1 saline water enriched with f/2 medium (Guillard & Ryther, 1962) and containing 500,000 cells mL−1 of Tetraselmis suecica (Kylin) Butcher 1959 (Microalgae Culture Collection of Instituto de Acuicultura Torre de la Sal‐Consejo Superior de Investigaciones Científicas, IATS‐CSIC, Spain). This algal density represents ad libitum feeding levels. Stock cultures of rotifer clones were maintained at 20°C and constant light (35 μmol m−2 s−1) and renewed weekly by replacing half of the culture volume with fresh medium.

B. plicatilis belongs to a cryptic species complex (Mills et al., 2017) and often co‐occurs in sympatry with its sibling species B. manjavacas in many of the natural populations studied (e.g., Gómez et al., 2000, 2002, 2007; Montero‐Pau et al., 2011; Ortells et al., 2003). For this reason, the established clones were identified as B. plicatilis using PCR‐RFLP analysis targeting a fragment of the cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) mitochondrial gene (see details in Campillo et al., 2005; Montero‐Pau et al., 2011). The final number of B. plicatilis clones ranged between 5 and 19 among populations.

Mass production of diapausing eggs and hatching bioassays

A subset of five to seven clones per population was randomly selected for subsequent characterization of traits related to diapausing egg hatching. Each clone was individually mass cultured to produce cohorts of diapausing eggs, which were then used to carry out hatching bioassays. For each clone, three replicate mass cultures were established each consisting of at least 100 asexual females taken from the stock culture and inoculated in 100 mL of culture medium with an initial microalgae concentration of 500,000 cells mL−1. This food concentration was maintained in the mass cultures throughout a growth period of approximately 21 days under standard conditions. This time period was long enough as to allow clonal proliferation, the occurrence of sexual reproduction, and the production of diapausing eggs. Diapausing eggs were collected within the first 5 days after observing diapausing‐egg producing females in the mass cultures and pooled for the three replicates of a clone. This procedure yielded cohorts of diapausing eggs of comparable age and produced under similar conditions across clones, for use in the bioassays.

Mass‐produced diapausing eggs from each clone were individually distributed into multiwell plates across six salinity treatments: 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, and 36 g L−1. These salinity levels fall within the physiological tolerance range of B. plicatilis (Gabaldón, Carmona, et al., 2015; Gabaldón, Serra, et al., 2015; Lowe et al., 2007), and cover the values observed in the natural habitats (see Table 1). Wells were incubated under standard hatching conditions in terms of temperature and light (see above), and inspected every 24 h over a 21‐day period in which it was recorded (1) whether each egg hatched (or not), (2) when it hatched (in days from the start of incubation in the hatching bioassay), and (3) whether it died or was lost. This timeframe was long enough for cumulative hatching curves to level off in most treatments and clones (see Appendix S1: Figure S1). Every 11 days, the diapausing eggs were transferred to fresh medium to minimize medium deterioration and water evaporation. In total, we conducted nearly 17,000 assays (average of 76 eggs per salinity and clone × 6 salinities × 5–7 clones per population × 6 populations).

Characterization of traits related to diapause exit

Hatching bioassays allowed us to assess the three traits related to either phenotypic plasticity of bet hedging in the exit from diapause for each clone studied across the salinity gradient. The hatching fraction was calculated as the proportion of hatched eggs (excluding deteriorated ones) by the end of the bioassay for each clone and salinity treatment. These data were used to determine the salinity level yielding the highest hatching fraction (HFmax). This value was used to compare among‐season bet hedging among clones, with lower HFmax indicating greater bet hedging.

From the records of the days it took each diapausing egg to hatch, we calculated the interquartile range of hatching time for each clone, as a proxy for hatching asynchrony capturing within‐season bet hedging (Solano‐Udina, Serra, et al., 2025). The interquartile range was chosen instead of other measures of dispersion because it is less sensitive to outliers and does not require data to be normally distributed. For the sake of simplicity in further analyses, we used the interquartile range of hatching time at the salinity at which maximum hatching occurred (hereafter, simply IQRHT).

Joint regression analysis (Finlay & Wilkinson, 1963) was used to quantify phenotypic plasticity by analyzing how the hatching phenotype of a given clone changes across salinity treatments. This approach involves regressing the hatching fraction of each rotifer clone onto the average hatching fraction across all clones in each salinity treatment (i.e., environmental value). The resulting regression slope represents the sensitivity of hatching fraction to salinity and serves as an index of phenotypic plasticity for each clone (hereafter, PIHF), with steeper slopes indicating greater plasticity.

Data analyses

We fitted a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) to evaluate the effect of experimental salinity on the hatching fraction of the diapausing eggs. Hatching data were analyzed on an egg‐by‐egg basis as a binary response (hatching probability), assuming a binomial error distribution and a logit link function. The model considered salinity as a covariate and included a quadratic term to account for potential nonlinearity in the response. Population and clone within population were included as random‐effect factors, as well as their interaction with salinity. The GLMM was run as a random slope, random intercept model, allowing both the intercept and the effect of salinity to vary across populations and clones. The significance of effects was evaluated using likelihood ratio tests (LRTs) comparing the full GLMM to reduced models, each with the factor of interest removed.

To assess the effect of experimental salinity on the hatching time, we used a Cox proportional hazards mixed‐effects model, which is a time‐to‐event model, with the day of hatching as the time‐to‐event response variable. This model considered the same explanatory variables, and their significance was tested as follows: the same approach as for the hatching fraction.

Linear models (LMs) were used to assess differences in IQRHT and PIHF among populations, considering clones as replicates. Note that IQRHT and PIHF values can be computed only for each clone‐by‐salinity combination and for each clone, respectively.

To test the hypotheses concerning associations among the three traits related to diapause exit, we first performed pairwise correlations between HFmax, IQRHT, and PIHF of the studied clones. However, a common issue when studying life history traits is the risk of statistical artifacts, such as part–whole effects, which may generate spurious or inflated correlations. Therefore, we additionally performed partial correlations as a complementary approach to distinguish true functional relationships from those arising through mathematical construction.

Finally, to assess whether the degree of environmental predictability influenced the extent of phenotypic plasticity or diversification traits in the hatching traits, we ran robust linear models (RLMs) on each trait against CVH as an explanatory variable. RLMs were implemented due to the lack of normality of the residuals.

All analyses were run in R v. 4.3.2 (R Core Team, 2021). GLMMs were applied using the glmer function from the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015). Cox proportional hazards mixed‐effects model on the timing of hatching was performed using the coxme function from the coxme package (Therneau, 2015). LRTs for the significance of effects in the above‐mentioned models were implemented using the anova function from the base package in R. LMs were implemented using the lm function from the stats package (R Core Team, 2021). Correlations between pairs of the three traits related to diapause exit were computed using the cor.test function from the stats package (R Core Team, 2021). RLMs were performed using the lm_robust function in the estimatr package (Blair et al., 2025). Plots were built using ggplot2 package (Wickham, 2016).

RESULTS

Our results suggested that salinity may act as a reliable environmental cue for diapause exit, with both hatching fraction and hatching time of diapausing eggs showing substantial variation in response to salinity among populations and among clones within the same population (Figure 2). Overall, the highest hatching fractions were generally observed at low salinities (3–6 g L−1), conditions under which diapausing egg hatching also occurred more rapidly, whereas increasing salinity resulted in reduced hatching fractions and delayed hatching. Notably, the diapausing eggs of some clones—particularly those from CAM and HYC populations—failed to hatch at the highest experimental salinity tested (36 g L−1).

Raw hatching data allowed us to assess, for each clone studied across the salinity gradient, the three traits related to either bet hedging or phenotypic plasticity in the exit from diapause in rotifers. Firstly, the highest hatching fraction observed across the studied salinity range, HFmax, was considered as a measure of among‐season bet hedging. Maximum hatching generally occurred at low salinities, although the salinity at which HFmax was reached varied notably among clones, spanning from 3 to 24 g L−1. HFmax showed substantial variation among clones, with an overall mean of 17% (range: 1.1%–70.5%).

The second trait studied was the asynchrony of hatching events, measured as IQRHT, which accounted for within‐season bet hedging. IQRHT ranged from 0 to 7.25 days among clones, with a mean of 2.41 days. No significant differences among populations were detected for IQRHT (F 5,31 = 1.254, p = 0.308). Finally, phenotypic plasticity in the hatching fraction, as measured by PIHF from joint regression analysis, showed marginally significant differences among populations (F 5,31 = 2.374, p = 0.062; Figure 3) and considerable variation among clones (ranging from −0.268 in ATA250 to 5.013 in ATA210; see Appendix S1: Figure S2 for within‐population variability).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we provide empirical evidence for the joint expression of phenotypic plasticity and bet hedging in traits related to the exit from diapause in the rotifer B. plicatilis. Our findings support the theoretical prediction that phenotypic plasticity and bet hedging are not mutually exclusive adaptive strategies, but can evolve together in environments that include both predictable and unpredictable components, with plastic responses allowing organisms to track reliable cues of environmental change, while bet‐hedging traits spread risk under uncertainty (Joschinski & Bonte, 2021, 2020; Simons, 2014).

Salinity appeared to act as a reliable environmental cue for diapausing egg hatching, as most rotifer clones reached their highest hatching fractions at low salinities (3–6 g L−1), with hatching success declining and even being inhibited at higher salinities, conditions known to be within the range of tolerance in the euryhaline B. plicatilis (Gabaldón, Carmona, et al., 2015; Gabaldón, Serra, et al., 2015; Lowe et al., 2007). The fact that hatching was maximized at a lower salinity than the optimum for asexual reproduction of B. plicatilis (10–20 g L−1; Tran‐Nguyen et al., 2025) suggests that the observed pattern is not merely the result of a physiological inhibition caused by salt, but instead that salinity is potentially informing embryos about the current phase of the hydroperiod and thus anticipating habitat persistence. These findings support the idea that selection could favor sensitivity to low salinity as a general and reliable cue of the onset of a sufficiently long hydroperiod (i.e., a true growing season for rotifer populations). Nevertheless, salinity alone cannot fully anticipate future environmental circumstances that may suddenly and unexpectedly shorten the hydroperiod. Consistent with this, no clone showed complete (100%) hatching fraction of diapausing eggs, a signature of bet hedging. Together, these results evidence the combined expression of both adaptive responses within rotifer genotypes.

Our results revealed substantial genotypic variability in the hatching responses to salinity within populations. This variability was reflected in significant clone × salinity interactions that provide evidence of genetic variation for phenotypic plasticity in diapause‐exit traits, and highlight the role of within‐population diversity in shaping adaptive responses to fluctuating environments. No strong differentiation in the timing of hatching and the hatching fraction was observed among populations in relation to salinity, likely reflecting ecological similarities among the studied ponds. Indeed, these ponds are geographically close, similar in size, and host comparable assemblages of resident taxa. While these features make them suitable for studying the adaptation of populations in a natural setting—and thus, for the application of correlational approaches (Levins, 1968)—they may also limit ecological differentiation. Additionally, the temporary nature of these habitats and their frequent desiccations may facilitate the passive dispersal of diapausing eggs across the landscape (Moreno et al., 2016). Given the geographical proximity and seasonal synchronicity of the ponds, such dispersal is expected to enhance connectivity and sustain gene flow among populations. In line with this, Franch‐Gras et al. (2019) found moderate geographic genetic structure in this system (F ST = 0.11 ± 0.01 SE). Gene flow can homogenize allele frequencies across populations while simultaneously increasing within‐population genetic variation through the introduction of novel genotypes. In this way, the continuous exchange of diapausing eggs among populations may enrich the genetic pool of each pond, favoring a variety of hatching strategies well suited to the broader environmental mosaic, rather than to pond‐specific conditions.

The phenotypic plasticity observed in the hatching fraction was summarized in the form of a plasticity index (PIHF). To do this, we used the slope of joint regression analysis (Finlay & Wilkinson, 1963). This approach allowed us to measure the response of the hatching fraction to the variation in salinity in a standardized way across rotifer clones and populations (Diouf et al., 2020; Lacaze et al., 2009). This is a widely applied method in quantitative genetics (e.g., Baptistella et al., 2024; Diouf et al., 2020; Lacaze et al., 2009) that provides a dimensionless measure of phenotypic plasticity—allowing further comparisons among clones, traits, or studies—while integrating the whole range of responses rather than only extreme or mean values. Since multiple alternative metrics for quantifying phenotypic plasticity have been proposed (see e.g., Simons, 2014; Valladares et al., 2006), we tested the robustness of our results by also calculating the Environmentally Standardized Plasticity Index (ESPI; Valladares et al., 2006) for the hatching fraction. Repeating the same analysis as for PIHF, we obtained qualitatively consistent results (see Appendix S1: Figure S4), with both indices being strongly correlated (Pearson's r 35 = 0.743, p < 0.001), providing additional confirmation of the robustness of our approach. An additional advantage of PIHF is that it directly indicates how sensitive a clone is to salinity changes. Therefore, in those clones where the plastic response was stronger (i.e., PIHF took larger values), we inferred that salinity has a greater reliance as a cue for hatching. A pending test to conclude whether salinity is a reliable cue for rotifer embryos would assess if it consistently correlates with favorable environmental conditions (i.e., lower salinities at the timing of pond flooding being associated with longer enough hydroperiods).

Among‐ and within‐season bet hedging were assessed via HFmax and IQRHT, respectively. On the one hand, HFmax accounted as an inverse estimator of delayed hatching, which is a classic example of bet hedging among growing seasons. Recall that even at the salinity at which the maximum hatching was reported, the hatching fraction at saturation of any clone was never 100%, but instead took intermediate values. This suggests that diapausing eggs of the same cohort produced by a clone differ in programmed diapause duration, with a fraction remaining dormant until the following growing season. Such a delayed hatching hedges against interannual environmental uncertainty as, for instance, in the length of the growing season. Although rotifer clones can diversify the hatching of their diapausing eggs across a wider range of salinities, HFmax—or, alternatively, the hatching fraction under conditions considered to be optimal—is a commonly used metric in previous bet‐hedging studies on rotifers (Franch‐Gras, García‐Roger, Serra, & Carmona, 2017; Tarazona et al., 2017) and facilitates comparisons. Furthermore, HFmax was highly correlated with the overall proportion of hatchlings (i.e., the total fraction of eggs hatched across all salinities relative to total eggs incubated per clone; Pearson's r 154 = 0.81, p < 0.001), and the results of our analyses held up qualitatively when this alternative metric was used. For reference, average values for HFmax were within a similar range yet slightly lower than the hatching fractions previously reported by Franch‐Gras, García‐Roger, Serra, and Carmona (2017) for the same study populations under similar conditions (i.e., 6 g L−1 salinity and comparable incubation time). In addition, we used IQRHT to describe potential bet hedging at the within‐season level following previous studies (Solano‐Udina, Serra, et al., 2025; Tarazona et al., 2020). As mentioned above, it is an inverse measure of the degree of synchrony in the hatching of diapausing eggs from the same cohort and is more robust to outliers than alternative metrics (e.g., SD in hatching time; see, for instance, Simons, 2014, but for seed germination). Clones with high IQRHT, showing staggered hatching throughout the growing season can buffer against the risk of a false start of the growing season (Vanoverbeke & De Meester, 2009).

In this study, we focused on the relationships among these three traits and, by extension, on the adaptive response modes they represent. We found that genotypes with higher PIHF (i.e., stronger plastic responses to salinity) tended to show higher HFmax, which implies less bet hedging among seasons. These findings align with the idea that phenotypic plasticity and bet hedging may vary along a continuum, rather than falling into discrete categories (Joschinski & Bonte, 2020). Interestingly, we also found that rotifer clones showing high HFmax (i.e., low among‐season bet hedging) showed high IQRHT (i.e., high within‐season bet hedging). This suggests that delaying the hatching of a portion of diapausing eggs and desynchronising the hatching of the responding fraction may serve as alternative risk‐spreading responses. Since these two forms of bet hedging are thought to be adaptations to different types of unpredictability—among growing seasons and within a growing season, respectively—our findings suggest that selection may favor one form over the other depending on the dominant temporal scale of environmental unpredictability in a given habitat. Furthermore, the observed relationship between the hatching fraction and the dispersion in time of hatching implies potential functional redundancy between the two forms of bet hedging. In this sense, diversification across seasons (i.e., reduced hatching fraction) may also provide protection against within‐season uncertainty, rendering additional within‐season risk‐spreading (i.e., increased variation in hatching time) unnecessary or even maladaptive. Therefore, our results provide empirical support for the general prediction in life history theory that investment in one adaptive strategy may reduce the need to invest in another strategy that serves the same purpose.

To address how phenotypic plasticity and bet hedging vary along a gradient of environmental unpredictability, we correlated the studied traits against the CVH length, used here as an inverse estimator of environmental stability. Our premise was that salinity would act as a more reliable cue in habitats that fluctuate in a more predictable manner. In line with our predictions, PIHF and HFmax were negatively correlated with CVH, indicating respectively that phenotypic plasticity was more prevalent in predictable ponds, whereas among‐season bet hedging predominated in ponds with more variable hydroperiods. In contrast, the expected positive relationship between IQRHT and CVH—that is, greater diversification in hatching time within the growing season in more unstable habitats—was not observed. In this regard, it is important to recall that CVH is related to unpredictability regarding the duration of the growing season, whereas increased IQRHT would be expected to evolve primarily in response to unpredictability at the beginning of the growing season, for example, due to the existence of false starts (ten Brink et al., 2020; Vanoverbeke & De Meester, 2009).

Despite providing new insights into the mechanisms underlying variation in diapause exit, our results should be interpreted in light of several considerations that may pave the way for promising lines of future research. First of all, here we only evaluated the hatching response to salinity, without considering other environmental cues that could anticipate the onset or progression of a favorable growing season. While our results indicated that salinity is an ecologically meaningful and often reliable cue in temporary aquatic systems, it may not fully capture the complexity of environmental cues involved in diapause exit. Other factors—such as temperature or photoperiod—could also act independently or in interaction with salinity, modulating the response of diapausing eggs in more complex environmental scenarios.

Future work should therefore explore whether the observed relationships between plasticity and bet hedging persist when incorporating these cue combinations, thereby testing the generality of our findings. Given the multicomponent nature of environmental predictability (Hernández‐Carrasco et al., 2025; Sabo & Post, 2008; Solano‐Udina, Serra, et al., 2025; ten Brink et al., 2020), plasticity to unmeasured cues may also contribute to variation in diapause exit and could partially overlap with what we interpret as bet‐hedging responses. Although we consider this effect to be negligible under our controlled laboratory experiment, we acknowledge that it may become relevant under natural conditions and should therefore be considered in field studies. Furthermore, as mentioned above, our proxy for environmental predictability may overlook other relevant dimensions related to hydrological conditions, such as the probability of false starts in the growing season. Quantifying the frequency of such events in nature could help to explain how within‐season bet hedging evolves and how it relates to the other adaptive responses in fluctuating environments. Finally, a critical next step towards confirming the adaptive value of the phenotypic plasticity and of any of the two modes of bet hedging in the exit from diapause in rotifers would be to determine whether the observed phenotypic variability due to each adaptive mechanism translates into an increase in the geometric mean of genotype fitness across contrasting environmental regimes (e.g., predictable vs. unpredictable). While this type of test lies beyond the scope of this study, it provides a promising direction for future research using complementary approaches, such as experimental evolution.
