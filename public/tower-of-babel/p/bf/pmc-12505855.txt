LncNFYC-AS1 ameliorates Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia via regulating miR-1323
Authors: Cai Chen, Jianping Hu, Mao Guo, Li Li, Qihong Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) is a common respiratory infection, yet its molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) play critical roles in various diseases, but their regulatory functions in MPP require further investigation. This study aimed to investigate the expression profile and clinical significance of NFYC-AS1 in MPP and analyze its interaction with miR-1323 in modulating cellular functions.MethodsIn vitro cell culture methods were used to establish MPP cell models, and A549 cells were transfected to introduce or inhibit lncRNAs and miRNAs. Real-time quantitative PCR was used to detect the expression levels of NFYC-AS1 and miR-1323. The direct binding relationship between NFYC-AS1 and miR-1323 was verified using a luciferase reporter gene assay. Elisa was used to quantify the levels of cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. CCK-8 assay was employed to assess cell growth capacity. Transwell analysis was used to study cell migration. Cell apoptosis rates were detected by flow cytometry. Finally, statistical analysis methods were used to process and interpret the data.ResultsNFYC-AS1 was significantly upregulated in patients and significantly associated with MPP risk, showing a negative correlation with miR-1323. Mechanistically, NFYC-AS1 directly targeted and suppressed miR-1323. In MP-infected A549 cells, NFYC-AS1 knockdown reduced inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α/IL-1β/IL-6), enhanced proliferation/migration, and inhibited apoptosis—effects abolished by miR-1323 inhibition.ConclusionNFYC-AS1 is highly expressed in MPP and regulates inflammatory responses, cell proliferation, migration, and apoptosis by targeting miR-1323. The NFYC-AS1/miR-1323 axis may serve as a potential therapeutic target and show preliminary discriminatory value for MPP.

Background

Mycoplasma pneumoniae (M. pneumoniae, MP) is widely recognized as a principal causative agent of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) [1, 2], exhibiting persistent prevalence throughout the year. This epidemiological pattern contributes significantly to increased healthcare utilization and substantial economic burdens on healthcare systems globally [3]. While the majority of MP pneumonia cases present with relatively mild and self-limiting symptoms [4], a subset of individuals may develop protracted cough, recurrent wheezing [5], or evolve into more severe manifestations such as lobar pneumonia [6] or bronchiolitis [7]. In rare, severe instances, MP infection can precipitate acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [8], posing a serious threat to life.

Current clinical management predominantly relies on macrolide antibiotics [9, 10]. However, the escalating prevalence of antibiotic-resistant Mycoplasma pneumoniae strains, coupled with suboptimal responses to standard therapies in a subset of patients, therefore necessitates an investigation into its pathophysiological basis for refining therapeutic approaches [11, 12]. Although preliminary insights have been gained into the role of the host immune system in combating MP infection [13], particularly concerning the aberrant activation of inflammatory responses, the intricate molecular regulatory networks that govern these processes remain largely unexplored and represent a research gap.

In recent years, the regulatory roles of long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) in infectious diseases have garnered increasing attention [14]. Particularly in respiratory infections, lncRNAs have emerged as modulators of host-pathogen interactions and inflammatory responses, as evidenced by their involvement in influenza, COVID-19, and other pulmonary pathologies. Accumulating evidence suggests that lncRNAs participate in host immune responses against pathogens through diverse molecular mechanisms [15]. In viral infections, lnc-Cxcl2 has been shown to specifically bind to the Cxcl2 gene promoter region, where it recruits ribonucleoprotein La to reduce chromatin accessibility, thereby cis-inhibiting CXCL2 expression in lung epithelial cells and effectively attenuating neutrophil-mediated pulmonary inflammation during influenza virus infection [16]. In SARS-CoV-2 infection, distinct lncRNA expression profiles have been observed, with lnc-MALAT1 showing positive correlation and lnc-MEG3 demonstrating negative correlation with disease severity; their combined detection exhibits high predictive value for COVID-19 progression [17]. Further investigations revealed significant upregulation of lnc ISR, lnc ATV, and lnc HEAL in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of COVID-19 patients, among which lnc ATV displayed an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.762, indicating its potential as a diagnostic biomarker [18]. In Mycoplasma pneumoniae infections, rRNA-depleted RNA sequencing identified 17 differentially expressed lncRNAs in refractory M. pneumoniae pneumonia (RMPP) patients, which may contribute to disease progression through modulation of the IL-17 signaling pathway [19]. Additionally, in pneumonic inflammation models, lnc NKILA was found to alleviate TNF-α-induced apoptosis and inflammatory cytokine release in lung fibroblasts by upregulating miR-21 to inactivate NF-κB and JNK signaling pathways [20]. These findings not only elucidate the multi-layered regulatory networks of lncRNAs in respiratory infections but also provide a theoretical foundation for developing novel diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets.

This investigation centers on NFYC-AS1, a novel lncRNA potentially involved in MPP pathogenesis. Integrating clinical sample analysis with in vitro experimentation, we systematically examined its interaction with miR-1323. The experimental framework encompassed three critical components: clinical correlation validation, mechanistic exploration, and functional characterization.

Methods

Bioinformatics analysis

In the bioinformatics analysis section, the GSE103119 dataset was obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), providing transcriptomic sequencing data of lncRNA expression profiles from patients with Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection and healthy controls. The log2FPKM matrix was extracted, and differential expression analysis was performed, focusing on annotations containing “lnc” and “-AS1” to identify relevant lncRNAs with significant expression changes. Subsequently, the lncRNASNP2 database (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/lncRNASNP/#!/) was used to predict potential miRNA binding partners for the candidate lncRNAs. During the screening process, miRNAs unrelated to the pathogenesis of Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia or those showing elevated expression levels in patients were systematically excluded, ensuring the selection of highly relevant miRNAs with potential regulatory roles.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was estimated using PASS 21 software with the One-Sample Sensitivity and Specificity method. The parameters were set as follows: Power = 0.9, significance level (α) = 0.05, and disease prevalence = 0.3. Under the null hypothesis, both sensitivity (Se0) and specificity (Sp0) were set to 0.6. Under the alternative hypothesis, both sensitivity (Se1) and specificity (Sp1) were set to 0.9. The minimum required sample size for this study was then calculated based on these predefined parameters.

Sample collection and processing

This study enrolled 77 adult patients with MPP confirmed at People’s Hospital of Chongqing LiangJiang New Area, along with 77 age- and gender-matched healthy volunteers who underwent physical examinations during the same period serving as the control group. The diagnosis of all patients was confirmed based on clinical symptoms, imaging examinations, and etiological detection, strictly adhering to the inclusion criteria which included adult subjects over 18 years of age, absence of other severe underlying diseases, and no prior treatment. Individuals with recent antibiotic use or immunosuppressive therapy were excluded. The Ethics Committee approved this study protocol, and all participants provided written informed consent before participation. The collected serum samples were processed by low-speed centrifugation to separate serum, which was then immediately stored at −80 °C.

During the study, we collected detailed baseline clinical information for all participants, including age, gender, Body Mass Index (BMI), history of alcohol consumption, and smoking status. This was done to facilitate a comprehensive analysis of baseline characteristics and to minimize the impact of potential confounding factors on the research outcomes. Routine clinical biomarkers including C-reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin (PCT) were measured in all participants at enrollment using standardized hospital laboratory protocols.

Cell culture

The human lung adenocarcinoma cell line A549 (ATCC CCL-185) was cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Corning) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (HyClone) at 37 °C under 5% CO2. The reference strain Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) (ATCC 29342) was maintained in PPLO broth supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher) at 37 °C. Bacterial suspensions were standardized to 1 × 107 CCU/mL (color-changing units), aliquoted, and cryopreserved at −80 °C prior to use.

A549 cells are widely used in M. pneumoniae infection studies to establish in vitro models of bacterial pneumonia [21–23].

Cell transfection

Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) when A549 cells reached 70% confluence in 6-well plates (1.5 × 105/well). Constructs included: miRNA mimics (miR-1323 mimics, NC mimics) and inhibitors (miR-1323 inhibitor, NC inhibitor) synthesized by RiboBio; overexpression plasmids (oe-NFYC-AS1) and empty vectors (vector) from GenePharma; and knockdown vectors (sh-NFYC-AS1) with controls (sh-nc) in pLKO.1 backbone (Addgene). Transfection complexes were incubated in serum-free medium for 20 min, added to cells for 5 h, and then replaced with complete medium. Cells were harvested after 48 h for analysis.

Real time quantitative PCR (RT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen), and cDNA was synthesized with the PrimeScript RT kit (Takara). Amplification was performed using SYBR Green Premix (Roche) on a QuantStudio 6 system (Applied Biosystems), with GAPDH and U6 as endogenous controls for NFYC-AS1 and miR-1323, respectively. Primers were designed and synthesized by Sangon Biotech. Reactions (20 µL) followed a standard three-step protocol. Relative expression was calculated by the 2⁻ΔΔCt method, with four independent replicates per group.

Luciferase reporter assay

The binding sites between NFYC-AS1 and miR-1323 predicted by lncRNASNP2 were cloned into pmirGLO vectors (wild-type, WT; mutant-type, MUT) by GenePharma. WT/MUT constructs were cotransfected with miR-1323 mimics or inhibitors into A549 cells. After 48 h, luciferase activity was measured using a Dual-Luciferase Assay Kit (Promega), with firefly/renilla ratios normalized to controls. Four independent replicates were performed.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) in cell supernatants were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (R&D Systems). Absorbance at 450 nm was measured (BioTek microplate reader), with concentrations calculated from standard curves. Quadruplicate independent experiments were performed.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using a CCK-8 kit (Dojindo). A549 cells in logarithmic phase were seeded into 96-well plates (3 × 103/well) and subjected to six treatments: control (PBS), MP-infected, MP + sh-NC, MP + sh-NFYC-AS1, MP + sh-NFYC-AS1 + nc inhibitor, and MP + sh-NFYC-AS1 + miR-1323 inhibitor. At 0, 24, 48, and 72 h, 10 µL CCK-8 reagent was added per well. After 2 h incubation at 37 °C, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek). Quadruplicate independent experiments yielded data expressed as mean ± SD.

Cell migration analysis

Cell migration was analyzed using Transwell chambers (8 μm pore, Corning). Treated A549 cells were resuspended in serum-free DMEM/F12 and seeded in the upper chamber (5 × 104/chamber), while the lower chamber contained 10% FBS medium as a chemoattractant. After 24 h at 37 °C, non-migrated cells were removed. Migrated cells were methanol-fixed, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted in five random fields under a microscope. Four independent replicates were performed.

Apoptosis detection

Cell apoptosis was quantified by flow cytometry using an Annexin V-FITC/PI kit (BD Biosciences). Harvested cells were washed with PBS and resuspended in binding buffer. Cells were stained with 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL PI for 15 min in the dark. Apoptotic rates were analyzed on a flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). Quadruplicate experiments were conducted.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 10.1 and SPSS 27.0. Continuous variables are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD); categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Inter-group comparisons used independent t-tests; multi-group comparisons used one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test. Spearman’s correlation assessed miR-1323-NFYC-AS1 links. Chi-square tested baseline homogeneity. Binary logistic regression identified independent predictors of MPP. ROC curves evaluated diagnostic performance. Statistical significance was defined as two-tailed p < 0.05.

Results

Clinical characteristics and baseline demographics

Sample size estimation using PASS 21.0 with α = 0.05 and power = 0.9 indicated a minimum requirement of 54 subjects. Accounting for potential attrition, 77 hospitalized MPP patients and 77 age-/gender-matched healthy controls were ultimately enrolled. As detailed in Table 1, the cohorts showed comparable baseline parameters including age (33.06 ± 9.20 vs. 32.34 ± 9.68 years), gender distribution (female: 41.6% vs. 42.9%), BMI (22.10 ± 1.75 vs. 22.56 ± 1.90 kg/m²), drinking (22.1% vs. 26.0%) and smoking status (7.8% vs. 10.4%), minimizing confounding bias. Multivariate logistic regression (Table 2) showed that high NFYC-AS1 expression was significantly associated with MPP risk (OR = 20.984, 95% CI: 8.819–49.928, p < 0.001), with predictive capacity significantly surpassing other clinical variables.

Identification and diagnostic value of NFYC-AS1 in MPP

Analysis of the GEO dataset GSE103119 (Fig. 1A) identified NFYC-AS1 as a significantly upregulated lncRNA among 34 differentially expressed candidates. Clinical validation revealed markedly elevated expression of NFYC-AS1 in MPP patients compared to healthy controls (Fig. 1B). Its diagnostic potency was corroborated by ROC curve analysis (AUC = 0.871, p < 0.001), outperforming procalcitonin (AUC = 0.748) and CRP (AUC = 0.792) (Fig. 1C).

Molecular interaction profile between NFYC-AS1 and miR-1323

Clinical specimen analysis revealed decreased miR-1323 expression in MPP patients relative to healthy controls, showing an inverse correlation with NFYC-AS1 levels (r = −0.754; Fig. 2A-B). Bioinformatic screening via the lncRNASNP2 database identified miR-1323 as a putative target of NFYC-AS1 (Fig. 2C).

Dual-luciferase assays demonstrated diminished luciferase activity when miR-1323 mimics were cotransfected with wild-type NFYC-AS1 constructs, whereas mutant constructs abolished this effect; conversely, miR-1323 inhibitors enhanced luciferase activity in wild-type groups (Fig. 2D-E). Molecular intervention experiments demonstrated that NFYC-AS1 overexpression reduced miR-1323 expression, whereas shRNA-mediated knockdown of NFYC-AS1 elevated its abundance (Fig. 2F). In A549 cellular models induced by Mycoplasma pneumoniae, NFYC-AS1 expression was upregulated while miR-1323 levels were downregulated (Fig. 2G-H).

Regulatory effects of NFYC-AS1/miR-1323 on cellular functions

Transfection efficacy validation confirmed elevated miR-1323 levels in sh-NFYC-AS1-treated cells versus sh-NC controls, which was reversed by combined miR-1323 inhibition (Fig. 3A). In MP-infected A549 models, NFYC-AS1 knockdown significantly reduced TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 concentrations compared to sh-NC groups, an anti-inflammatory effect abolished upon miR-1323 suppression (Fig. 3B-D). Enhanced proliferative capacity in sh-NFYC-AS1 groups was eliminated by miR-1323 inhibitors in CCK-8 assays (Fig. 3E). Transwell analysis demonstrated increased cell migration following shRNA-mediated knockdown of NFYC-AS1, an advantage attenuated through miR-1323 inhibition (Fig. 3F). Flow cytometry revealed decreased apoptotic rates in sh-NFYC-AS1-treated cells, while miR-1323 inhibitors restored apoptosis (Fig. 3G). These data collectively demonstrate that NFYC-AS1 directly binds to and represses miR-1323 activity, thereby modulating inflammatory responses, cell proliferation, migration, and apoptosis in MPP.

Discussion

This study delves into the role of the NFYC-AS1 in the pathogenesis of MPP, elucidating its mechanism of action through the regulation of miR-1323 in influencing cellular functions. Our findings unequivocally demonstrate that NFYC-AS1 is significantly upregulated in MPP patients and exhibits a strong correlation with disease onset, serving as a significant risk factor and diagnostic marker. Notably, its diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.871) exceeded procalcitonin (0.748) and CRP (0.792), highlighting clinical relevance.

This study indicates that NFYC-AS1 might be involved in disease-related processes. Previous studies have reported that NFYC-AS1 is involved in regulating cellular processes such as apoptosis, autophagy, and oncogenic signaling pathways in various pathological conditions. In lung adenocarcinoma, NFYC-AS1 was found to suppress the expression of apoptosis-related proteins p53 and PARP, modulate autophagic activity, and downregulate the oncogenes MET and c-Myc, thereby promoting tumor progression [24]. Furthermore, in the same cancer type, NFYC-AS1 was shown to facilitate tumor development by regulating the autophagy-related gene BIRC6, reinforcing its role in modulating cellular survival pathways [25].

Beyond cancer, NFYC-AS1 has also been implicated in non-neoplastic diseases, highlighting its broader biological significance. In diabetic foot ulcers, NFYC-AS1 was reported to aggravate tissue damage by downregulating miR-199a-5p, leading to the upregulation of pro-inflammatory factors S100A8, S100A7, and XDH [26]. This suggests that NFYC-AS1 may function as a regulatory molecule in inflammatory responses and tissue repair processes, potentially contributing to chronic wound pathogenesis. Additionally, NFYC-AS1 exhibits pan-cancer overexpression, particularly in tumors harboring RB1 mutations. Functional studies demonstrated that knockdown or transcriptional inhibition of NFYC-AS1 impairs cell growth in lung squamous cell carcinoma and small cell lung cancer, with cells exhibiting mitotic defects [27].

Prognostically, NFYC-AS1 has emerged as a potential biomarker in multiple malignancies. In glioblastoma, high NFYC-AS1 expression was significantly associated with poor patient survival as part of an m6A modification- and autophagy-related lncRNA signature [28]. Similarly, in lung adenocarcinoma, NFYC-AS1 was included in an RNA methylation-related lncRNA prognostic model alongside other signature molecules such as OGFRP1 [29]. These findings highlight the clinical relevance of NFYC-AS1 as a predictive biomarker and suggest its potential utility in risk assessment and therapeutic decision-making.

While our study demonstrates that NFYC-AS1 directly binds and represses miR-1323 activity in MPP, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the association between NFYC-AS1 and MPP risk (OR = 20.984) requires validation in cohorts adjusted for clinical confounders (e.g., age, smoking, comorbidities). Second, although NFYC-AS1 exhibits comparable discriminatory ability to CRP and procalcitonin in our preliminary analysis, further large-scale studies are needed to comprehensively evaluate its diagnostic performance against established and emerging biomarkers. Third, the mechanistic link between NFYC-AS1 and miR-1323, particularly whether NFYC-AS1 functions as a ceRNA by competing for shared mRNA targets, requires identification and validation of downstream miR-1323-regulated mRNAs. Finally, while A549 cells provided a tractable model for epithelial responses, future studies should employ primary cells or vivo models to better recapitulate the immune-pathophysiological complexity of MPP.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that NFYC-AS1 is markedly upregulated in MPP and exerts its effects by directly binding to and suppressing miR-1323 activity, leading to enhanced inflammation, enhancing cellular migration, and inhibiting apoptosis. Modulating the NFYC-AS1/miR-1323 axis alleviates pathological changes associated with MPP, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target and its preliminary discriminatory value, which requires further validation. These findings offer new insights into the molecular mechanisms of MPP and potential avenues for clinical intervention.
