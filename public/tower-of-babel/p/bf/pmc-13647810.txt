Gobal, regional, and national burden of hepato-pancreato-biliary cancers in older populations, 1992–2021, and prediction to 2040: analysis of data from the Global Burden of Disease study, 2021
Authors: Heng Tian, Yipin Yang, Xinyi Wang, Shuxing Chen, Yafen Wang, Siqing Yue, Yuhan Liu, Ran Wei, Congjun Zhang, Fan Wang, Kangsheng Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Liver, pancreatic, gallbladder, and biliary tract cancers are among the most prevalent malignancies with increasing annual incidence rates [1, 2]. Noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) account for over 70% of global deaths, with cancer being the leading cause, particularly in older adults [3]. By 2040, cancer is projected to be a major cause of illness and death worldwide, as the global population ages due to improvements in nutrition, health, and economic conditions [4]. Old age is a major cancer risk factor, and the prevalence of cancer in older adults increases, requiring adaptations in healthcare systems [5]. Patients with cancer often experience symptoms, such as fatigue, discomfort, and weight loss. Advancements in vaccines and treatments have improved the prognosis of liver cancer [6]. The prognosis of pancreatic and biliary tract cancers remains poor, with a five-year survival rate of approximately 10% [7]. Surgery is the primary treatment for gallbladder cancer, but only a few patients qualify, and the recurrence rate is high (up to 80%) within three years [8, 9]. These cancers severely affect the health-related quality of life [10]. The complex anatomy of the liver, gallbladder, and pancreas complicates early detection and surgery [2]. The incidence and mortality rates of these malignancies are expected to increase with population growth. However, global research on the disease burden of these cancers, particularly gallbladder cancer, is limited, especially in low- and middle-income countries, with a notable lack of epidemiological studies focusing on older adults [11–13].

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 provides valuable data for epidemiological research. This study used GBD data to assess the burden of hepato-pancreato-biliary (HPB) cancers, and examined trends and regional variations to inform medical practices and health policies. We examined three HPB cancers (liver cancer (LC), gallbladder and biliary tract cancer (GBTC), and pancreatic cancer (PC) in individuals aged 60–89 years and presented the latest global, continental, and country-level incidence and mortality data for older adults. The age group of 60–89 was chosen to capture the majority of cases in older adults, ensuring that the findings reflect the specific burden on populations that are most likely to face the debilitating effects of these cancers. This age group also aligns with previous studies using GBD data, which typically categorized older adults into this specific range to understand cancer trends accurately [14]. This study offers a detailed evaluation using trend analysis, decomposition, and risk-factor projections.

Methodology

Data source

The GBD 2021 provides data on the incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) of a wide range of diseases and injuries worldwide. These statistics were extracted from the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) results platform (query ID:112c0d609ca88d5c2e19c8a6712bb710; accessed January 6, 2025). Positioned as a fundamental tool for epidemiological research, the GBD 2021 presented a comprehensive assessment of health losses due to 369 diseases, injuries, disabilities, and 87 risk factors across 204 nations and territories [15]. The study population was defined as individuals aged 60–89 years [16]. The 25th to 75th percentiles of the 1,000 posterior estimates generated through the GBD analytical framework were used to construct the 95% confidence intervals (CIs), expressed per 100,000 individuals, based on data from the GHDx platform [17]. Furthermore, the sociodemographic index (SDI), which functions as an extensive evaluation of health-associated social and economic determinants, was computed using financial positions, educational attainment, and fertility statistics of 204 countries and territories. According to the estimates, all nations are classified into five quintiles: high, high-middle, middle, low-middle, and low SDI regions [18].

Descriptive analysis

A descriptive study was conducted at the global, regional, and national levels to elucidate the burdens of three HPB cancers (LC, GBTC, and PC). Global trends in case numbers, age-standardized rates of incidence, and mortality rates for the three HPB cancers from 1992 to 2021 are visually illustrated for various sexes, age groups, SDI levels, regions, and nations. In this study, the six GBD age categories were divided into five-year intervals for individuals aged 60–89 years [19].

Trend analysis

To examine the global trends in the incidence and mortality rates of the three HPB cancers among senior individuals aged 60–89 years from 1992 to 2021, we calculated the average annual percentage change (AAPC) by computing the weighted average of the annual percentage change (APC) across segmented time intervals. The APC estimates were weighted by variance to account for the differences in the precision of estimates across various periods. Additionally, age-standardized rates were calculated using the GBD standard population to account for differences in age structures across regions and over time. These adjustments ensured that the trends reflected true changes in disease burden independent of demographic shifts. The specific time periods analyzed were 1990–2001, 2002–2011, 2011–2021, 1992–2021, and 2019–2021 [20, 21]. The epidemiological changes in the incidence and mortality of HPB cancers at the global, continental, and national levels were assessed using joinpoint regression analysis, which can detect locations with significant trend alterations (i.e., joinpoints) and split the entire trend into several subsegments depending on the detected joinpoints (https://surveillanc.cancer.gov/joinpoint) [22]. Joinpoint regression analysis was performed using the Joinpoint Regression Software, Version 4.9.1.0 (National Cancer Institute, USA). Significance testing was conducted at p < 0.05, and a log-linear model was used for trend analysis. A log-linear model was fitted, assuming a Poisson distribution for cancer incidence and mortality rates owing to the discrete count nature of the data. This distributional assumption appropriately reflects the independent and infrequent occurrence of events (new cases and deaths) over time.

The APC was computed as the geometric means of the annual percentage changes within each segment. The AAPC was then derived as a weighted average of the segment-specific APCs weighted by the segment length. The AAPC values are presented together with their 95% CIs to convey the precision of the estimates. Trends were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Cross-validation was performed to ensure that all reported trend statements were consistent with the AAPC confidence intervals. Specifically, a trend was labeled as increasing if both the AAPC estimate and lower CI bound were greater than zero, decreasing if both the AAPC and upper CI bound were below zero, and stabilizing otherwise. To ensure numerical consistency, all AAPC values were rechecked against the original model output, and any discrepancies owing to rounding or segmentation differences were recomputed.

Age-standardized rates were calculated using the GBD standard population, allowing valid comparisons across regions and times. The trends were subsequently stratified by sex, age group, and SDI quintiles and visualized on global, regional, and national scales. This approach ensured the accurate interpretation of statistically validated temporal patterns in the HPB cancer burden.

Decomposition analysis

A decomposition analysis was conducted to investigate the extent to which demographic and epidemiologic factors contributed to variations in new cases and deaths from HPB cancers over the past 30 years. Specifically, the analysis aimed to break down the contributions of the changes in the total population, age distribution, and epidemiological shifts within each country. To ensure the robustness of this decomposition, we applied the Das–Gupta method, a widely recognized approach in global health studies that partitions the contributions of demographic (e.g., population growth and aging) and epidemiological changes (e.g., incidence or mortality rate shifts) on the overall disease burden. This method provided a comprehensive understanding of how various factors interact and influence health outcomes over time [23]. In our analysis, contributions were partitioned into three key components: population growth, aging, and epidemiological changes. Population growth refers to an increase in the total number of people in a region, which leads to an increase in the absolute number of disease cases. Aging refers to changes in the age structure of the population, which can affect disease incidence, owing to a higher risk of certain diseases among older individuals. Finally, epidemiological changes capture shifts in disease rates themselves, including both increases and decreases in incidence or mortality, often due to changes in risk factors, healthcare access, or disease prevention efforts.

The formula ay, py, ey = ai,y × py × ei,y was used to calculate the number of disease episodes (i.e., incidence or mortality counts) at each location. The incidence ay, py, ey denotes the incidence count affected by age distribution, total population, and incidence in a given year y. Specifically, ai,y represents the percentage of persons within age group i among n age groups in year y, py is the general population in year y, and ei,y represent the incidence for age group i in year y. Changing one variable at a time while keeping the rest constant enabled the evaluation of each component influence on fluctuations in incidence rates. The approach for estimating mortality was the same as that used for incidence.

Risk factors analysis

In GBD 2021, we identified the risk factors associated with the three HPB cancers, including alcohol use, behavioral risks, drug use, high BMI, high fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels, metabolic risks, and tobacco consumption/smoking status. These were analyzed based on the attributable DALYs and age-standardized mortality rates (ASMR). Metabolic risk and tobacco consumption/smoking status were the two consistently matched risk factors across three HPB cancers. Moreover, these were the only risk factors that could be matched for gallbladder and biliary tract cancers using the GBD of Disease 2021 dataset. A detailed description of these risk factors and methodologies for assessing their impact on the incidence and mortality of HPB cancers can be found in previous studies [24, 25].

The ggplot2 package in R (version 4.2.1) was used to illustrate the influence of these risk factors on the DALY trends for HPB cancers from 1992 to 2021. The association between the DALYs of HPB cancers in older adults and SDI was determined using smoothing spline models implemented with the smoothing spline function in R. Spearman’s correlation was employed to obtain the r indices and p-values for the relationship between SDI and DALYs. Statistical evaluations and visualizations were performed using R (version 4.2.1) with the ggplot2 package for plotting and the cor.test function for correlation analysis.

Predictive analysis

A comprehensive predictive analysis was conducted to support public health policy development and healthcare resource distribution by forecasting the future disease burden of three HPB cancers from 2022 to 2040. Bayesian forecasting is employed as the primary prediction method to ensure robustness and provide accurate projections under various assumptions. The Bayesian age-period-cohort model was used to account for the complexity of data and uncertainty in projections. This model integrates prior knowledge through Bayesian inference, allowing for the flexible and reliable forecasting of future disease burden [26]. The core method used for forecasting was Bayesian forecasting with a focus on the Bayesian age-period-cohort model. This model incorporates demographic, epidemiological, and temporal factors to predict the disease burden. The Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) method was applied to fit the model, which is particularly effective for handling complex nonlinear relationships in data. Using this model, we accounted for the uncertainty in predictions, providing a range of possible outcomes along with their associated confidence intervals. This approach allows better handling of sparse data or situations with high uncertainty, making it an ideal choice for long-term projections.

The model also included adjustments for expected demographic changes such as population growth, aging, and shifts in disease incidence due to changes in risk factors and healthcare interventions. These factors were incorporated to ensure that the projections reflected realistic scenarios.

For clarity, the model focuses on age-standardized rates and total incidence and mortality of HPB cancers from 2022 to 2040. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the projections and evaluate how changes in assumptions could influence the forecasted outcomes. In particular, the Bayesian model produced confidence intervals for the predictions, providing a transparent representation of the uncertainty around the disease burden projections for 2040. This ensured that the predictions accounted for the inherent uncertainty in a long-term forecasting, thereby providing a comprehensive set of forecasts for the public health planning. The Bayesian model was implemented using the R package "INLA" (Version 21.09.22).

Results

Global trends

In 2021, among individuals aged 60–89 years, the global age-standardized incidence rates (per 100,000) of LC, GBTC, and PC are 31.34, 15.46, and 36.81, respectively. The corresponding numbers of new cases of LC, GBTC, and PC were 31, 15, and 36 per 100,000, respectively. The global number of deaths and age-standardized mortality rates for these cancers were 160,783 (30.16), 12 (12.49), and 36 (36.81), respectively. From 1992 to 2021, the age-standardized incidence and mortality rates of the three HPB cancers showed divergent trends. LC exhibited an overall increase in incidence, rising by 0.28% per year (95% Confidence Interval, CI 0.21–0.35)%, while mortality showed a non-significant upward trend (AAPC = 0.13%, p = 0.181). However, a decline in these rates was observed from 2012 to 2021, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic (2019–2021), with incidence and mortality decreasing by -1.04% and -1.07%, respectively. From 1992–2021, pancreatic cancer incidence increased by 0.35 (95% CI 0.19–0.50)% annually, while gallbladder and biliary tract cancer declined by 0.48 (95% CI –0.64 to –0.32)%. GBTC showed a consistent decline in both incidence (-0.48%) and mortality (-0.98%) over 1992–2021, with the most significant decline occurring from 1992 to 2001 (incidence: -0.74%; mortality: -1.11%), as identified by joinpoint analysis. PC showed a long-term increase; however, the increase was not statistically significant from 2012 to 2021 for incidence (AAPC = 0.15%, p = 0.48), and a short-term decline in mortality was observed from 2019 to 2021 (AAPC = -0.69%, p = 0.014). Global trends by sex

Among the three cancers, only LC showed a distinctly higher burden in older males than females. In 2021, men will account for approximately 65% of all LC cases and deaths worldwide. Both sexes experienced increasing incidence and mortality, but these changes were not statistically different (male AAPC: 0.27% and 0.10%; female AAPC: 0.21% and 0.08%). For GBTC, females had a slight excess (≈53% of incident cases; 55% of deaths), with a significantly steeper decline in incidence among women (AAPC − 1.35%, 95% CI − 1.51 to − 1.18) than men (− 0.44%, 95% CI − 0.59 to − 0.28; p < 0.05). Mortality decreased in females (− 0.93%) but rose slightly in males (0.20%). For PC, males had a modestly higher burden (≈52% of incident cases and deaths), with faster and statistically significant increases in men (incidence AAPC 0.45%, 95% CI 0.36–0.55; mortality 0.35%, 95% CI 0.24–0.46) than in women (incidence 0.25%, 95% CI 0.16–0.33; mortality 0.09%, 95% CI 0.02–0.17) (Tables 2–4; Supplementary Figure S1).

Joinpoint regression analysis revealed the years marked by substantial shifts in global incidence and mortality rates when classified by age (Table 4).

Global trends by SDI quintiles

In 2021, the total age-standardized incidence and mortality rates of the three HPB cancers increased with rising SDI, with PC showing the most pronounced trend. The highest age-standardized incidence rates were observed in the high-SDI regions for LC (40.19, 95% CI: 43.32 to 36.02), GBTC (21.65, 95% CI: 23.95 to 18.62), and PC (62.34, 95% CI: 66.77 to 55.54). The highest age-standardized mortality rates were also seen in high-SDI regions for LC (33.33, 95% CI: 35.74 to 29.94), GBTC (14.18, 95% CI: 15.71 to 12.10), and PC (59.25, 95% CI: 63.42 to 53.00).

From 1990 to 2021, GBTC showed a clear negative gradient with the rising SDI, whereas LC showed a transition from a decreasing to an increasing trend as SDI rose. The incidence and mortality of PC showed the highest growth rates in the low-middle SDI regions, with non-monotonic trends overall. Notably, high-SDI areas saw the most significant reductions in incidence and mortality for GBTC (incidence, AAPC: − 0.8%; mortality, AAPC: − 1.64%), while low-SDI regions showed the steepest declines in LC incidence (AAPC: − 0.8%) and mortality (AAPC: − 0.78%). In contrast, the most pronounced increase in PC incidence and mortality occurred in the low-middle SDI regions (incidence, AAPC: 1.6%; mortality, AAPC: 1.57%) (Tables 2–3).

Joinpoint regression analysis indicated that particular years demonstrated the most significant alterations in worldwide incidence and death, as classified by the SDI quintiles (Supplementary Table S1).

Regional trends

Overall, the 2021 burden peaked for LC in Africa, GBTC in Asia, and PC in Europe. Over 1992–2021, LC mortality increased the fastest in the America, while that for GBTC declined in most regions but rose in Africa, and PC mortality grew most rapidly in Africa.

In 2021, Africa had the highest age-standardized incidence and mortality rates for LC (incidence: 38.21, [95% CI: 44.47 to 32.37]; mortality: 42.77, [95% CI: 49.77 to 36.23]). Asia had the highest rates for GBTC (incidence: 17.24, [95% CI: 20.39 to 13.17]; mortality: 14.56, [95% CI: 17.26 to 11.18]). Europe recorded the highest rates for PC (incidence: 55.85, [95% CI: 59.64 to 50.82]; mortality: 56.90, [95% CI: 60.74 to 51.90]). From 1992 to 2021, the most substantial increase in age-standardized incidence and mortality rates of LC was observed in America (incidence, AAPC: 1.96%; mortality: 1.66%). In contrast, for LC, the highest decrease in age-standardized incidence and mortality was recorded in Africa (AAPC: -0.34%) and Asia (AAPC: -0.42%). For GBTC, the largest relative decreases in incidence and mortality were recorded in America (AAPC: -1.16%) and Europe (AAPC: -1.82%). Although the age-standardized rates of GBTC showed a clear decreasing global trend, significant increases were observed in Africa (incidence, AAPC: 0.48%; mortality: 0.37%). The highest increase in the age-standardized incidence and mortality of PC occurred in Africa (incidence, AAPC: 1.66%; mortality: 1.61%) (Tables 2–3).

National trend

The age-standardized incidence and mortality rates of older adults with the three HPB cancers showed considerable variation among the 204 nations and territories at the national level (Fig. 2; Supplementary Figures S5–S7). Decomposition analysis

Over the past three decades, there has been a notable increase in the incidence frequency and deaths worldwide. Decomposition analyses revealed the relative effects of population aging and variations in the incidence and mortality of the three HPB cancers across different locations. Between 1992 and 2021, the increase in the incidence and deaths from the three HPB cancers was largely attributed to population growth (Supplementary Figures S8–S9; Supplementary Tables S11–S13). Among the World Health Organization (WHO) regions, the effects of population aging and growth on HPB cancers were more pronounced in the Western Pacific compared to those in other regions (Supplementary Figure S9).

Predictions of incidence and deaths

The incidence and number of deaths from the three HPB cancers are projected to continue to increase until 2040 in both females and males (Supplementary Figure S1). Our projections show that by 2040, the number of incidences of LC, GBTC, and PC will be 504,287 (152,517 to 856,057), 273,060 (131,659 to 414,460), and 629,164 (310,697 to 947,630), and the number of deaths due to LC, GBTC and PC will be 486,529 (93,273 to 881,482), 209,562 (103,622 to 315,503), and 638,285 (316,971 to 959,599), respectively (Supplementary Table S15). However, the age-standardized incidence and mortality rates for LC (incidence, AAPC: -0.59%; mortality: -0.59%), GBTC (incidence, AAPC: -0.19%; mortality: -0.47%), and PC (incidence, AAPC: -0.28%; mortality: -0.28%) are anticipated to decline until 2040.

Discussion

This study comprehensively investigated the three components of LC, GBTC, and PC in individuals aged 60–89 years. Prior research was focused on specific aspects of cancer epidemiology, such as incidence trends in certain countries or regional risk factor analyses [27–29]. In current study, we assessed the incidence and mortality rates of each cancer type globally, incorporating time trends, decomposition analysis, risk factor analysis, and projections.

We found diverging long-term trends: GBTC incidence and mortality declined, whereas LC and PC generally increased. Burdens intensified with advancing age, peaking at 85–89 years of age. China contributed the largest absolute counts in 2021, whereas the regional and SDI gradients varied by cancer type.

Linking trends to development, GBTC showed a negative correlation with SDI, which is consistent with improved cholelithiasis management, safer cholecystectomy, and broader metabolic risk control in the elevated SDI settings. The high SDI paradox is evident in LC, where despite advanced healthcare systems, the incidence continues to rise. In the regions with elevated SDI, LC incidence shifted from a decreasing to an increasing trend, primarily due to non-viral factors, such as alcohol use and metabolic dysfunction (e.g., obesity and diabetes), which counteract the benefits of improved hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV) control. Although better healthcare and viral hepatitis control are expected to reduce the LC burden, these rising trends suggest that NCD risk factors in these regions may be driving the increase, highlighting the need for integrated metabolic risk management along with HBV/HCV vaccination and treatment. PC exhibited non-monotonic behavior, with the fastest growth in low- to middle-SDI settings, aligning with rising obesity/diabetes and limited early detection. These patterns argue for tiered policies: sustained viral hepatitis immunization and treatment, alcohol control (LC) in lower-SDI settings, strengthening metabolic risk reduction across all SDI levels (all three cancers, especially PC), and maintaining gallstone prevention and perioperative safety (GBTC) where surgical access is high. Additionally, health systems should prioritize screening for metabolic risk factors in aging populations, particularly in those with obesity and diabetes, to reduce the burden of LC, GBTC, and PC.

As populations continue to age, particularly in low- and middle-SDI regions, health systems should focus on age-specific prevention strategies such as catch-up vaccination and antiviral therapy for older adults at risk of LC, along with comprehensive metabolic risk screening and management. These SDI-related differences underscore the need for global cancer control to integrate infectious disease prevention and chronic disease management frameworks. Age amplifies the cancer burden: as overall trends increase, age-specific rates steepen in older age groups; when trends decline, reductions are less pronounced with age, occasionally reversing at 85–89 years. Health systems should prioritize age-specific prevention and care such as catch-up vaccination and antiviral therapy for HBV/HCV (LC), comprehensive management of cardiometabolic risks (for all three HPB cancers), and equitable access to high-quality imaging and perioperative care (for GBTC). Additionally, improving palliative care pathways for PC is essential as survival remains limited.

Early symptoms are often nonspecific, contributing to the late presentation and limited curative options [30–32]. Etiologic drivers include viral hepatitis/cirrhosis (LC), metabolic dysfunction and adiposity, tobacco and alcohol use, and environmental exposures [33–41]. Increasing obesity/diabetes and persistent tobacco use plausibly underlie some of these observed trends. The WHO’s objectives for NCD prevention include eradicating viral hepatitis, reducing harmful alcohol and tobacco use, and addressing diabetes and obesity by 2030 [42, 43]. These strategies are crucial to address the rising burden of HPB cancers, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.

For LC, sustained impact is likely from HBV vaccination, HCV testing/treatment, alcohol harm reduction, and metabolic risk control, alongside access to treatment [44–49]. For PC, survival remains poor, with rising incidence and mortality driven by metabolic risks like high BMI and FPG, especially in low- and medium-SDI countries [50–53]. Although incidence rates have been consistently minimal in low-SDI countries, high-quality mortality data are limited due to diagnostic challenges [52]. PC survival remains poor. While population-wide screening is not indicated, targeted surveillance for selected high-risk groups is supported by expert consensus [54].

This GBD-based study provides valuable insights into the worldwide incidence and mortality rates of HPB cancers in individuals aged 60–89 years, investigates the most pertinent risk variables, and forecasts the future burden of these cancers in this population. However, our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, our analysis relies on GBD modelled estimates that may underestimate cancer burden in low-SDI regions with incomplete vital registration. Variations in diagnostic coding, under-reporting, and lack of histologic subtyping (e.g., intrahepatic vs. extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma) could affect comparability. Nevertheless, our study leverages the latest GBD 2021 data, incorporates comprehensive Joinpoint and decomposition analyses, and provides age-specific projections to 2040. Additionally, not all countries, particularly low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), have complete population-based cancer registries, leading to gaps in data for estimating the cancer burden. These biases, particularly in LMICs, may lead to the underreporting of cancer incidence and mortality, affecting the comparability of data across regions. Therefore, certain small island nations were excluded from the GBD dataset because of limited data availability and challenges in gathering comprehensive cancer statistics for these regions. Although the GBD 2021 incorporates some behavioral risk factors, especially for GBTC, it lacks data on several other relevant risk factors, making the direct comparisons across cancer types difficult. Despite these limitations, this study utilized the GBD 2021 data and incorporated joinpoint regression and decomposition analyses, offering a comprehensive understanding of temporal trends. Age-specific projections for 2040 are included to suggest future research and policy development.

Conclusion

There is strong heterogeneity in the age-standardized incidence and mortality rates of HPB cancers among individuals aged 60–89 years across 204 countries. We identified several temporal patterns of HPB malignancies across older populations worldwide, indicating possible deficiencies in prevention, management, and treatment approaches. This study tends to enhance our understanding of the HPB cancers epidemiology, optimize healthcare resource allocation, and guide the development of relevant health policies.
