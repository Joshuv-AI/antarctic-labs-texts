Development and validation of a single-nephron estimated glomerular filtration rate model to predict disease progression in IgA nephropathy
Authors: Chen Yang, Shuang Liang, Zhi-Yu Duan, Shu-Wei Duan, Jie Wu, Zhe Feng, Pu Chen, Xiang-Mei Chen, Yong Wang, Guang-Yan Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe highly variable clinical progression of IgA nephropathy (IgAN) makes it challenging to accurately predict the risk of disease deterioration in patients. Although elevated single-nephron estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is implicated in disease progression, its prognostic utility remains underexplored due to methodological limitations in nephron quantification. This study aims to fill this research gap by establishing single-nephron eGFR as a prognostic factor and developing a predictive nomogram for assessing kidney disease progression in IgAN patients.MethodsWe included 190 patients with biopsy-proven IgAN undergoing kidney biopsy and CT imaging. Single-nephron eGFR was calculated by dividing eGFR by nephron number, derived from cortical volume and glomerular density. A Cox model incorporating clinical, pathological, and single-nephron eGFR parameters was developed (training cohort: n = 133) and validated (validation cohort: n = 57). Kidney function decline was defined as an annual eGFR decrease ≥5 ml/min/1·73 m2, ≥40% eGFR reduction, or end-stage renal disease. Model performance was assessed using Harrell’s C-index, time-dependent AUC, and calibration curves.ResultsOf the 410 patients screened, 190 (46%) met the eligibility criteria. The cohort was 55% male patients, with a median age of 36 years (IQR, 30–47). The median follow-up duration was 37 months (IQR, 22–48). The nomogram included smoking history, eGFR, use of calcium channel blockers, and single-nephron eGFR. It demonstrated good discrimination (C-index 0.76 [0.69–0.82] in training; 0.75 [0.65–0.85] in validation) with good calibration. Low-risk patients had significantly longer survival compared to high-risk patients in both the development (P < 0.001) and validation (P = 0.04) cohorts. An interactive Shiny app was developed for clinical use (https://yangchenkay.shinyapps.io/IgAN/).ConclusionsThis study develops a clinically applicable prediction model for IgAN that underscores the prognostic value of single-nephron eGFR in renal outcome and enables effective risk stratification of kidney function decline in patients with IgAN.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04534-0.

Introduction

IgA nephropathy (IgAN), one of the most prevalent primary glomerulonephritis worldwide, represents a leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) in Asian populations [1, 2]. A critical challenge in its clinical management stems from the marked heterogeneity in disease progression risk, with 10-year cumulative ESRD incidence ranging from 5% to 60% across reported cohorts [3, 4]. This striking prognostic variability underscores the urgent need for mechanistically informed prognostic indicators. Enhanced risk stratification through such biomarkers could enable personalized therapeutic strategies aimed at managing renal outcomes.

Multiple prognostic risk factors have been reported for IgAN. Since the 1980s, single-nephron glomerular filtration rate (GFR) has been widely recognized as a key predictor of renal functional deterioration [5–7]. However, technical challenges in direct single-nephron GFR measurement have led to widespread use of estimated GFR (eGFR) as a surrogate parameter in clinical studies [8–12]. This substitution introduces significant bias due to interindividual variability in nephron numbers [13]. Furthermore, emerging evidence questions the clinical relevance of elevated eGFR, as current measurement protocols cannot reliably distinguish compensatory hyperfiltration from preserved renal function [6, 8]. These methodological limitations undermine the validity of existing single-nephron eGFR prognostic assessments in IgAN research. They also underscore the need for accurate nephron quantification methods to enable reliable assessment of single-nephron eGFR.

In this study, we adopted a new nephron quantification method in IgAN patients using combined glomerular density and cortical volumetry. Subsequent single-nephron eGFR calculations were derived by dividing the eGFR by total nephron count. Through longitudinal analysis of our cohort, we systematically evaluated the prognostic value of single-nephron eGFR [14, 15]. While single-nephron eGFR demonstrated significant association with renal functional decline, its isolated application showed limited clinical utility in capturing disease complexity. That is, the parameter alone exhibited insufficient precision for reliable high-risk patient identification. To address this issue, we constructed a multidimensional prediction model integrating single-nephron eGFR with key clinical parameters.

Recent advances have proposed multiple prognostic models for IgAN [16–20]. However, these models demonstrate limited clinical utility in early-stage patients due to IgAN’s characteristically chronic progression. They predominantly predict long-term renal failure risk, yet over 40% of clinical trials targeting early-stage cohorts have failed to achieve primary endpoints [21–23].

This discrepancy is a result of the current models’ inability to detect early functional deterioration. Such diagnostic deficiency results in inadequate risk stratification, which in turn leads to an inadvertent enrollment of low-risk individuals. Persistent deficiencies in prognostic accuracy not only compromise trial design validity but also delay time-sensitive interventions. Notably, early immunosuppressive therapy shows potential to preserve nephron function before irreversible structural damage occurs in patients with preserved renal architecture [24].

To address the above issues, we developed a prognostic model predicting kidney function decline in IgAN. This model synergistically combines established clinical parameters with histopathological features, incorporating single-nephron eGFR as a key predictor. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first prognostic model predicting kidney function decline that integrates single-nephron eGFR with routinely available clinical parameters collected during diagnostic biopsy. The integration of single-nephron eGFR enables precise risk stratification through direct quantification of nephron functional capacity.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study included patients with biopsy-confirmed IgAN at the Chinese PLA General Hospital between January 2016 and October 2022. During hospitalization, these patients underwent both kidneys computed tomography (CT) scan and kidney biopsy. The diagnosis of IgAN was established by immunofluorescence that showed dominant or co-dominant mesangial IgA deposition, light microscopy that revealed mesangial hypercellularity or matrix expansion, and electron microscopy that demonstrated mesangial electron-dense deposits. Exclusion criteria included:(i)patients under 18 years of age;(ii)patients with any other glomerular diseases, such as lupus nephritis, membranous nephropathy, or minimal change disease;(iii)patients with secondary IgAN based on comprehensive clinical assessment, or those with pathological evidence of diabetic nephropathy, obesity-related glomerulopathy, or hypertensive nephropathy;(iv)patients with missing CT images or biopsy images;(v)patients with an eGFR < 30 ml/min/1.73 m2, or those who experienced acute kidney injury or required temporary dialysis before or during hospitalization;(vi)patients with a cortical area < 2 mm2 on biopsy images or < 5 intact non-sclerotic glomeruli;(vii)patients whose follow-up information was unavailable.

Eligible patients (n = 190) were randomly assigned in a 7:3 ratio into a model development cohort (n = 133) and a validation cohort (n = 57), ensuring comparability between the groups. This study was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (S2023-467–01).

Data

We collected clinical data, CT, and biopsy images for all patients. Laboratory values were collected at the time of biopsy to synchronize with pathological assessments. GFR was estimated using the serum creatinine-based 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation, which does not include race in its calculation [25]. Medication use was defined as prescriptions actively maintained at discharge following biopsy. This approach captured the first-line therapy based on pathological risk stratification. Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors use was documented in 5 patients (2.6%), which was below the threshold for meaningful subgroup analysis. Therefore, SGLT2 inhibitors use was not included as a covariate in the primary analysis. Hypertension was defined as blood pressure greater than 140/90 mmHg or the use of antihypertensive medications. Kidney biopsies were scored according to established criteria for the Oxford MEST scoring system [26–28]. Missing data ( < 5%) were imputed using the mode for categorical variables, and no continuous variables had missing data. Outcome data were obtained through medical records or telephone interviews with the patients or the hospitals they visited.

Single-nephron eGFR assessment

CT and biopsy images were independently reviewed in a blinded manner with respect to the clinical characteristics. Parenchymal volume was derived from non-contrast CT scans to avoid potential nephrotoxicity risks associated with iodinated contrast agents in chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients. The parenchymal volume was measured using the Pair annotation software package (https://www.aipair.com.cn/en/, Version 2.9, RayShape, Shenzhen, China). Cortical volume was then calculated using a validated formula with parenchymal volume as input. This formula was derived from a cohort of 49 kidney donors and 58 subjects with renal disease, demonstrating strong correlation with contrast-enhanced CT measurements (r = 0.92, P < 0.001) (Supplemental Methods) [29].

Kidney biopsy samples were scanned into high-resolution digital images using an Olympus VS200 slide scanner (Tokyo, Japan). Biopsy slides with severe structural artifacts were excluded. The scanned digital images were magnified using Olympus OlyVIA software v3.2. The cortex, as well as each complete or partial non-sclerotic glomeruli and cross-sectional profile, were manually traced. To standardize cortical depth bias, an additional 1.77 glomeruli/mm3 was added to needle biopsy samples. Using stereological models, the mean non-sclerotic glomerular volume and non-sclerotic glomerular density were calculated from these measurements [14, 15]. Non-sclerotic glomerular density was further corrected for technical biases by applying a three-dimensional shrinkage factor combined with a correction for perfusion pressure loss. Partial glomeruli bisected by biopsy needles were counted as 0.675 glomeruli based on the mean tuft area ratio [15]. All missing glomerular tufts were counted. Additional calculated details are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

To mitigate potential biases in cortical volume assessments associated with structural renal abnormalities, the study excluded patients with secondary glomerular pathologies, including diabetic nephropathy, obesity-related glomerulopathy, and hypertensive nephropathy. Only seven (3.7%) participants had a history of diabetes mellitus and five (2.6%) patients with severe interstitial fibrosis (Oxford T2 lesions) were included, which further reduces potential confounding from cortical structural damage.

The nephron number was calculated by multiplying the non-sclerotic glomerular density in the biopsy sample by the cortical volume of both kidneys and rounding to the nearest 10,000 nephrons. The body-surface-area-unadjusted total eGFR (units: ml/min) was derived by rescaling the standard eGFR (ml/min/1.73 m2) according to individual body surface area (BSA). BSA was calculated using the Mosteller formula [30]. Single-nephron eGFR was then calculated by dividing this unadjusted total eGFR by the nephron number and rounding to the nearest 1 nl/min [14].

To evaluate the reproducibility of the nephron counting methodology, we assessed inter-observer variability. A randomly selected subset of 30 biopsy images was independently analyzed by two trained investigators who were blinded to each other’s results. Agreement was quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for absolute agreement with a two-way random-effects model. An ICC value greater than 0.75 was considered indicative of good reliability.

Outcome measures

The primary composite outcome, progressive decline in kidney function, included the following: an annual eGFR decrease ≥5 ml/min/1.73 m2, ≥40% eGFR reduction, or ESRD [31]. The annual rate of eGFR decline was estimated using linear mixed-effects models. This metric is a recommended surrogate endpoint for clinical trials in CKD and demonstrates strong predictive validity for ESRD risk [32]. Sustained ≥40% eGFR decline is defined as a permanent ≥40% reduction in eGFR relative to the value at biopsy. Moreover, ESRD was defined as an eGFR < 15 ml/min/1.73 m2, need for dialysis or kidney transplantation.

Among patients receiving renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (RASI) therapy, 76% had initiated these agents prior to renal biopsy. Of these, 88% maintained post-biopsy treatment, which mitigated potential eGFR fluctuations attributable to RASI introduction during follow-up periods.

Statistical analyses

The sample size was determined based on the available data, and no prior power calculation was conducted. Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies and proportions for categorical variables and as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables. The correlation between single-nephron eGFR and other variables was analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Concordance between eGFR and single-nephron eGFR was assessed using Bland-Altman analysis. eGFR slope was evaluated with linear mixed models.

The Cox regression model was used to develop the prognostic model, employing stepwise backward elimination with a threshold of P < 0.1 for variable inclusion. Cox regression analyses were also applied to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) or odds ratios, along with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) or p-values. Survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method.

To assess potential multicollinearity between eGFR and single-nephron eGFR, we calculated variance inflation factors (VIFs) for all variables in the Cox model and generated a Spearman correlation matrix. A VIF value below 5 was considered indicative of no significant multicollinearity.

The prediction accuracy of the survival models was evaluated using two methods. First, the discriminative ability of the models was assessed by Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) and the area under the time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curve (time-dependent AUC). Second, model calibration was evaluated using calibration plots that compared predicted probabilities of outcomes at 24 and 36 months with observed probabilities. These time points were selected both for their clinical relevance to mid-term risk and to ensure statistical reliability, as the median follow-up was 37 months. The nomogram was derived from the model, and individual risk scores were calculated by applying the nomogram to the development cohort. The cutoff value, determined based on the tertile of the risk score in the training cohort, was used to stratify patients into low-, moderate- and high-risk groups. To facilitate the accessibility and usability of the model, the final model based on the optimal feature subset was deployed as an interactive web-based Shiny app that enables individualized survival prediction, personalized interpretation, and explanation of the model.

Model validation was conducted in two steps. First, a bootstrap resampling process (500 times) was performed to provide an unbiased estimate of model performance, specifically the C-index. Second, to assess the validity of the model, prediction accuracy was determined in the validation cohort by computing the C-indices and generating calibration plots. The nomogram was applied to the validation cohort to obtain individual risk scores, and the optimal cutoff determined in the development cohort was used to stratify patients in the validation cohort into low-, moderate- and high-risk groups [33]. The clinical utility of the nomogram was evaluated using the log-rank test to determine whether the survival distributions differed significantly between the groups. A two-sided P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Data analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.1 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria), PCPM (V4.25, Jingding Medical Technology Co., Ltd.), and GraphPad Prism version 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA).

Among the included patients, 104 (55%) were male, with a median age of 36 years (IQR, 30–47) (Table 1). Immunosuppressive therapy was administered to 103 patients (54.2%) based on the treating nephrologist’s assessment of clinical and pathological risk factors. The reproducibility of the underlying nephron counting method was excellent, with an inter-observer ICC of 0.94 (95% CI, 0.88–0.97). At the last follow-up, 59 (31%) of the 190 patients exhibited kidney function decline. Patients were randomly divided into a training group (n = 133) and a validation group (n = 57) at a 7:3 ratio. The median follow-up times after kidney biopsy were 37.1 months and 36.6 months in the derivation and validation cohorts, respectively. Single-nephron eGFR and other factors

We also conducted a correlation analysis of the baseline clinical data from these 190 samples. As shown in Fig. 2C, single-nephron eGFR was positively correlated with hypertension, proteinuria, and glomerular volume. Conversely, it was negatively associated with endocapillary hypercellularity (E1), crescents, and the number of nephrons. To elucidate the interplay between single-nephron eGFR and RASI therapy, we stratified patients by RASI use (Figure S2). In patients without RASI therapy, single-nephron eGFR was significantly elevated in CKD Stage 3. By contrast, in RASI-treated patients, single-nephron eGFR was effectively normalized and remained stable across all CKD stages.

Nomogram

Nomogram variable screening

In the training cohort, univariate Cox regression analysis identified several baseline risk factors significantly associated with kidney function decline (Table 3): a history of smoking (HR 2.33, 95% CI 1.17–4.65; P = 0.02), higher baseline eGFR (HR, 1.02; 95% CI, 1.00–1.03; P = 0.008), and higher single-nephron eGFR (HR, 1.02; 95% CI, 1.01–1.03; P = 0.01). Nomogram construction and validation

To identify independent predictors in the training group, we conducted a multivariate Cox regression analysis. Stepwise regression results indicated that a model including smoking history, eGFR, single-nephron eGFR, and CCBs use had the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) in the training group (Table 3). VIF values were all less than two, indicating no multicollinearity among the selected variables. In particular, the correlation between eGFR and single-nephron eGFR was weak (Spearman’s correlation P = 0.52), suggesting that these variables capture distinct aspects of renal physiology. We constructed a nomogram based on these selected variables (Fig. 3). On the scoring scale, a corresponding score was assigned to each variable. The points for each selected variable were summed to calculate the total score, which was then used to estimate the probability of rapid progression at 24 and 36 months. In this study, the total risk points for most patients ranged from 13 to 187 (median: 89, IQR: 70–114). The C-index in the training group was 0.76 (0.69–0.82), while in the validation cohort it was 0.75 (0.65–0.85). To assess the model’s stability, we conducted 500 bootstrap resamples. The bootstrap-corrected C-indexes in the training group (0.73 [0.65–0.80]) and validation group (0.75 [0.63–0.86]) were similar to the uncorrected C-indexes. Sensitivity analyses demonstrated the independent prognostic value of both eGFR and single-nephron eGFR (Table S2). In Model 1 (excluding eGFR), single-nephron eGFR remained significantly associated with kidney function decline (HR = 1.02, 95% CI 1.00–1.03, P = 0.01). In Model 2 (excluding single-nephron eGFR), eGFR also retained significance (HR = 1.02, 95% CI 1.00–1.03, P = 0.004). Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis using only rapid eGFR decline as the endpoint yielded consistent results, confirming the robustness of our model (Supplementary Table S3). These analyses demonstrated the independent prognostic value of both eGFR and single-nephron eGFR. To address potential overfitting and validate the independent contribution of single-nephron eGFR, we developed a parsimonious base model that included well-established risk factors with the strongest prognostic evidence in IgAN: sex, eGFR, proteinuria, Oxford S lesion, and Oxford T lesion. As shown in Table S4, the addition of single-nephron eGFR remained statistically significant. Furthermore, including the use of CCBs did not attenuate the association between single-nephron eGFR and outcomes. A sensitivity analysis, which excluded patients with advanced CKD (eGFR < 45 ml/min/1.73 m2), was conducted to assess the robustness of the prediction model. The model retained its predictive accuracy with a C-index of 0.73 (95% CI 0.67–0.8).

Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the consistency of the model’s performance across key clinical strata defined by baseline eGFR, proteinuria levels, RASI and immunosuppression treatment status. The model demonstrated generally consistent discriminative performance across these subgroups, as evidenced by overlapping confidence intervals in point estimates of the C-index(Table S4).

Web-based application development

To enhance the accessibility and practical utility of the final model with the optimal feature subset, an interactive web-based application was developed using Shiny (Figure S3). The web interfaces allow users to input clinical characteristics of a new sample and then the web application can help predict probabilities of kidney function decline, which offers personalized survival forecasts. The web application is accessible at https://yangchenkay.shinyapps.io/IgAN/.

Discussion

We developed and validated a risk-prediction model for kidney function decline in biopsy-proven IgAN. The model achieves discriminative accuracy comparable to established tools, while its primary contribution lies in quantifying nephron-level stress—a key pathophysiological driver—enabling mechanistically informed risk stratification beyond conventional parameters.

Beyond its comparable discriminative power, the integration of single-nephron eGFR represents a key advance: First, our findings provide mechanistic support for current therapeutic strategies that target single-nephron hyperfiltration. Evidence from RASI trials demonstrates that agents mitigating hyperfiltration, such as RASI which lower intraglomerular pressure, can slow down the progression of CKD [34, 35]. For example, Holtkamp et al. observed that an acute hemodynamic decline in eGFR during losartan initiation is associated with a slower long-term renal function deterioration [36]. This reflects effective intraglomerular pressure reduction. Our identification of elevated single-nephron eGFR as a central pathogenic driver directly corroborates the mechanistic rationale for this therapeutic approach.

Furthermore, our model’s ability to stratify risk translates into direct treatment implications. By identifying patients with high single-nephron eGFR—indicating significant glomerular stress—our tool enables clinicians to move beyond passive prognosis towards targeted therapeutic intervention. High-risk patients, as defined by the model, could be prioritized for more aggressive hemodynamic management. This includes optimizing RASI therapy to maximal tolerated doses to reduce intraglomerular pressure, or considering combination therapies with SGLT2 inhibitors, which have demonstrated efficacy in mitigating hyperfiltration injury. This approach aligns with the model’s mechanistic basis: targeting the primary pathophysiological driver it identifies.

Second, our findings offer a plausible explanation for the significant inter-individual variability observed in RASI-mediated renal protection. While many patients benefit from RASI therapy, a substantial subset experiences limited renal improvement despite adequate treatment [37]. We conjecture that this variability is driven, at least in part, by baseline differences in single-nephron eGFR. This hypothesis can be supported by established pathophysiology: rodent models have shown that sustained glomerular stress from adaptive hyperfiltration can directly induce podocyte injury and glomerulosclerosis [38, 39]. Thus, similar hemodynamic stressors are likely to operate in human IgAN. Accordingly, the baseline single-nephron eGFR load represents a critical determinant of therapeutic response.

Third, our multivariable regression analysis confirmed that elevated single-nephron eGFR is an independent prognostic factor, alongside established variables such as smoking history, baseline eGFR, and CCBs use. Although the per-unit hazard ratios for these continuous variables appear modest, their cumulative effects across clinically observed ranges translate into substantial risk differences, as evidenced by the effective separation of Kaplan-Meier curves in our risk stratification. The association between CCB use and increased risk of progression is intriguing and warrants discussion. This association persisted after adjustment for hypertension status and blood pressure, suggesting it may not solely reflect underlying hypertension severity. However, residual confounding by indication (e.g., preferential CCBs use in patients with more severe or resistant hypertension) cannot be entirely ruled out in our observational design. Mechanistically, it is plausible that CCBs, by predominantly dilating the afferent arteriole, may provide less optimal reduction of intraglomerular pressure compared to RASI in the setting of pre-existing hyperfiltration stress.

Notably, the observed association between higher baseline eGFR and increased progression risk, while seemingly counterintuitive in the context of CKD prognosis, is mechanistically consistent with the concept of compensatory hyperfiltration: it reflects that a preserved or elevated overall filtration rate in IgAN often signifies significant nephron loss being compensated for by increased filtration workload per nephron(i.e., elevated single-nephron eGFR). This heightened single-nephron stress, rather than preserved intrinsic function, drives progression risk.

Furthermore, sensitivity analyses demonstrated that while eGFR and single-nephron eGFR are mathematically linked, they capture distinct physiological mechanisms contributing to IgAN progression. The observed ‘poor agreement’ between these measures in coincidence analyses is not a limitation but rather a fundamental and instructive finding. It underscores that a preserved or even elevated total eGFR can mask a profoundly stressed nephron population. This discordance is the very essence of compensatory hyperfiltration: the kidney maintains overall function by driving remaining nephrons to work harder. Therefore, the lack of close agreement is expected and clinically meaningful—it allows us to identify those patients whose relatively good overall kidney function (by eGFR alone) is paradoxically at high risk because it is sustained by maladaptive hyperfiltration. This finding serves two key purposes. First, it provides strong evidence that hyperfiltration-induced injury mechanisms—well established in rodent models—are relevant to human IgAN. Second, it highlights that elevated single-nephron eGFR conveys prognostic information beyond conventional clinical or histological markers, thereby reinforcing its value as a mechanistically distinct predictor of disease progression.

Interestingly, proteinuria did not retain its predictive significance in our fully adjusted model. This contradicts the findings in previous studies and may stem from the high prevalence of pre-biopsy (64.2%) and post-biopsy (74.7%) RASI use in our cohort. Experimental data indicate that RASI preferentially reduces proteinuria, which may attenuate the association between proteinuria and disease progression in treated populations [40, 41]. Collectively, these observations regarding the diminished prognostic value of proteinuria under widespread RASI use, coupled with our earlier finding that baseline single-nephron eGFR is a key determinant of therapeutic response variability. This raises a conjecture that warrants further exploration: RASI efficacy in mitigating progression may depend on the baseline single-nephron eGFR. If validated, this would position baseline single-nephron eGFR not only as a prognostic marker but also as a potential predictive biomarker for guiding RASI therapy intensity or identifying patients most likely to benefit from it.

This mechanistic value—complementing rather than competing with existing models—justifies its clinical utility despite similar C-index ranges. Despite the central role single-nephron eGFR plays in modulating RASI response, the observed stability of single-nephron eGFR across CKD stages in our cohort underscores a complex interplay of intrinsic renal adaptation and extrinsic pharmacologic modulation. Notably, our analysis revealed no significant differences in single-nephron eGFR between stages, suggesting the presence of robust homeostatic mechanisms. This apparent paradox reflects the dual-phase nature of compensatory hyperfiltration: in early CKD, nephron loss induces adaptive increases in single-nephron eGFR among remaining nephrons, whereas in advanced stages, structural constraints limit further increases despite continued nephron attrition [42]. Importantly, the widespread use of RASI therapy in our cohort may have contributed to this observed stability. By attenuating glomerular hypertension and suppressing hyperfiltration, RASI may blunt early compensatory rises in single-nephron eGFR and thus masking stage-related differences. Critically, this stability demonstrates the limited compensatory capacity of single-nephron eGFR—a finding that aligns with our observation that nephron number significantly declines across CKD stages while single-nephron function remains static. As such, the preserved single-nephron eGFR homeostasis likely results from the combined influence of endogenous compensatory responses and exogenous hemodynamic modulation. It should be noted that this stability does not imply absence of injury. Our model effectively identified patients at high risk of disease progression, underscoring the prognostic value of incorporating single-nephron eGFR into risk stratification frameworks.

Beyond model validation, our findings also offer translational and conceptual implications. First, the model facilitates proactive clinical management. By design, it targets early intervention within a validated timeframe: our 37-month follow-up period (approximating 3 years) corresponds to the evidence-based window in which eGFR slope most strongly predicts long-term outcomes. By identifying high-risk IgAN patients earlier than conventional models, it creates a window for timely therapeutic escalation. Therefore, this model serves as a powerful supplement to the existing clinical tools. Specifically, patients with elevated predicted risk scores could benefit from intensified renin-angiotensin system inhibition, or combination therapy with SGLT2 inhibitors, or emerging therapies such as endothelin receptor antagonists, which have shown promise in reducing proteinuria and slowing disease progression in recent clinical trials [43]. These strategies directly mitigate the hemodynamic stress quantified by single-nephron eGFR. Early intervention at this stage may prevent irreversible hyperfiltration-induced injury before significant functional decline occurs.

Second, the model provides a framework for optimizing clinical trial design. Current IgAN trials face challenges due to heterogeneous patient populations with variable progression rates. Our risk stratification tool enables standardized enrollment of high-risk patients based on objective risk scores, particularly those incorporating single-nephron eGFR. This targeted selection reduces outcome variability, enhances statistical power, and increases the likelihood of detecting true therapeutic effects. As a result, trials evaluating novel interventions may achieve more conclusive results with smaller sample sizes and improved efficiency.

Third, our methodology has broader applicability across glomerular diseases. The central role of hemodynamic stress in IgAN progression suggests similar mechanisms may operate in other proteinuric nephropathies. Quantification of single-nephron eGFR could thus be adapted for risk prediction in conditions like focal segmental glomerulosclerosis or diabetic kidney disease, where compensatory hyperfiltration similarly contributes to progression [44, 45]. This approach offers a unified framework for assessing nephron-level stress across diverse etiologies, enabling cross-disease comparisons of therapeutic strategies aimed at mitigating glomerular hyperfiltration. We acknowledge that the current reliance on CT imaging and biopsy-based stereology for nephron quantification poses practical and economic barriers to widespread clinical implementation. However, the primary goal of this study was to establish the foundational prognostic valueof single-nephron eGFR using the most accurate methods available. This proof-of-concept provides a critical benchmark against which future, more feasible approaches to quantifying nephron-level stress can be developed and validated for clinical translation.

Study limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, technical factors may cause discrepancies between calculated and actual glomerular density. Although we corrected for cortical depth bias and excluded patients with insufficient glomeruli or small cortical areas, such errors cannot be entirely eliminated [15]. Second, the use of stepwise variable selection in model development may introduce instability in the final set of predictors. Third, we derived single-nephron eGFR from estimated GFR rather than measured GFR. While rigorous covariate adjustments were applied to enhance reliability, residual errors inherent to eGFR equations may persist, such as variability in tubular secretion and biases related to muscle mass estimation. Fourth, due to the relatively modest sample size, rapid kidney function decline was used as one of the primary endpoints. Although this surrogate measure has been validated for predicting long-term outcomes in CKD, it may occasionally reflect transient functional fluctuations rather than irreversible parenchymal damage. To mitigate this limitation, we conducted strict case reviews to exclude events attributable to temporary, non-progressive changes in renal function. Fifth, the model was developed and validated in a single-center Asian cohort. Although internal validation through bootstrapping showed robust performance, the generalizability of our findings to other ethnicities and healthcare settings remains to be established. Future external validation should compare performance against existing models, accounting for endpoint variability. Sixth, nephron quantification relied on a single biopsy slide. As glomerulosclerosis may exhibit focal distribution, consecutive section analysis could enhance accuracy. Future studies should validate our stereological method against multi-slice assessments when sufficient tissue is available. Seventh, the requirement for both contrast-free CT volumetry and biopsy-based stereological analysis to quantify nephron number poses practical and economic barriers to widespread clinical implementation. Eigth, medication data were collected at discharge following biopsy, representing the initial post-biopsy treatment strategy. We lacked detailed longitudinal data on medication adherence and adjustments during the follow-up period. Changes in treatment regimen over time represent a potential unmeasured confounder for the observed kidney outcomes.

Conclusions

We developed and validated a prognostic model for kidney function decline in IgAN patients, presented as an intuitive nomogram that balances predictive accuracy with clinical applicability. This tool enables rapid point-of-care risk stratification and prognosis prediction, thereby facilitating personalized treatment decisions. To enhance clinical integration, we also provide a web-based Shiny application that supports real-time survival probability calculations. Taken together, the model and application support precision hemodynamic management by quantifying nephron-level stress, marking a shift from reactive care toward proactive prevention of disease progression.
