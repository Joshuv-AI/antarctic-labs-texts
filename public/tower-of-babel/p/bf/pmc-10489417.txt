Efficacy of Flap-based Posterior Trunk Reconstruction after Vertebrectomy and Radiation Therapy: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Francesco Amendola, Gaia Ghiringhelli, Alessandro Mela, Sergio Miranda, Elisa Dolfato, Eleonora Bulgarelli, Michele Diomedi, Stefano Bonomi, Luca Vaienti, Alessandro Luzzati
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Adjuvant radiation therapy following vertebrectomy is a major risk factor for local wound complications such as dehiscence, infection, and skin necrosis. In selected cases, well-vascularized coverage and modification of tension forces on the wound might reduce the risk of postoperative complications and reoperations. We aimed to demonstrate a reduction in general and specific complications in patients undergoing vertebral resection and flap coverage compared with vertebral resection alone.Methods:We retrospectively analyzed and collected data from patients diagnosed with a tumor involving the spine and requiring a total or partial posterior vertebrectomy between January 2012 and October 2022, referred to a single tertiary-level orthopedic and trauma center. We included only patients in whom primary closure of the wound was possible but judged to be under excessive tension.Results:A total of 145 patients underwent partial or total vertebrectomy for oncological reasons at our tertiary-level trauma hospital. Among these, 73 patients were eventually included according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria: 53 in the orthopedic group and 20 in the orthoplastic group. Considering only patients undergoing radiation therapy, the orthoplastic group showed significantly lower rates of overall complications (33% versus 69%) than the orthopedic group.Conclusions:Primary flap coverage, especially in patients receiving RT, reduces the risk of postoperative complications and avoids a second reconstructive operation, consequently reducing patient discomfort, length of hospital stay, and healthcare costs.

Takeaways

Question: Does coverage with pedicled flaps performed contextually to vertebrectomy reduce the rate of postoperative complications?

Findings: Preventive coverage with pedicled flaps, performed during the first vertebral resection and for wounds that might have been closed primarily under tension, reduced the postoperative complication rate in patients receiving adjuvant radiation therapy.

Meaning: The viable, well-vascularized tissues of a pedicled flap offer a protective tool against wound dehiscence or breakdowns in patients undergoing vertebrectomy and adjuvant radiation therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Vertebral involvement accounts for only a minority of soft tissue and bone malignant tumors. However, this treatment requires complex multidisciplinary management.1 In primary malignant tumors of the spine, surgical excision with negative margins is the landmark treatment,2 leading to an increase in overall survival and disease-free interval. Surgery has proven efficacy in the treatment of vertebral metastasis.3 Chemotherapy and radiation therapy (RT) mainly act as adjuvant therapies for local and distant tumor control.4

Spinal cord decompression is usually achieved via posterior laminectomy with rod and screw stabilization, whereas total vertebrectomy might require anterior column stabilization with a cage system.2,5–7 When indicated, postoperative RT allows better tumor control and increases survival rate.9–10 In normal cases, the paraspinal muscles are the only coverage interposed between synthetic fixation devices and the skin.11

Soft-tissue defects resulting from radiation-induced damage often require reconstructive surgical intervention.12 Although several reconstructive options exist for posterior trunk defects,13 they markedly affect the length of hospital stay and overall patient discomfort. In selected cases, well-vascularized coverage and modification of tension forces on the wound might reduce the risk of both postoperative complications and reoperations in patients undergoing total or partial posterior vertebrectomy after tumor excision.

The aim of our study was to investigate the preventive use of locoregional skin or muscle flaps after total vertebrectomy in reducing the risk of postoperative complications in patients undergoing total or partial posterior vertebrectomy after tumor excision.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study Design

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Upon approval by the institutional review board, we retrospectively analyzed and collected data from patients diagnosed with a tumor involving the spine and requiring a total or partial posterior vertebrectomy, between January 2012 and October 2022, referring to a single tertiary-level orthopedic and trauma center. All vertebral resections were performed by the same spine surgeon (A.L.), whereas all reconstructions were performed by board-certified plastic surgeons (A.B. and F.A.). Retrospectively collected data included age; sex; type of tumor; localization of the tumor; chemotherapy; RT; treatment data, including surgical approach and procedure details; postoperative complications, including wound dehiscence; infection; reoperation; and survival.

Aim and Selection Criteria

We aimed to demonstrate a reduction in general and specific complications in patients undergoing vertebral resection and flap coverage compared with vertebral resection alone. To avoid selection bias, we included only patients in whom primary closure of the wound was possible but judged to be under excessive tension. Patients presenting with soft-tissue defects who were not candidates for primary closure were excluded from this study. The rationale of our study was that excessive intraoperative tension on the skin margins of the wound or poor soft-tissue coverage of the fixation devices could jeopardize the success of the surgery and increase the risk of complications. Other inclusion criteria were age older than 18 years, histological diagnosis of a malignant tumor involving the vertebra, primary vertebral resection, and stabilization. Exclusion criteria were a diagnosis other than malignant tumor, previous operations performed on the same vertebrae, and flap coverage not performed during the primary resection.

Patient Evaluation and Surgical Technique

An expert spine surgeon initially examined each patient. A systemic workup for staging was performed in each patient, including a bone scan, plain radiography, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging scans of the spinal column and abdomen. Histopathological examination was always performed before the primary vertebral resection. All stabilization procedures were performed via posterior column stabilization with rods and screws as well as in the case of total vertebrectomy. All patients underwent a median longitudinal incision as the surgical approach for vertebrectomy. A decision regarding whether a flap was needed during the first resection operation was made by a senior orthopedic surgeon (A.L.), considering the intraoperative tension of the tissues after stabilization. There were two indications for primary flap coverage: if the wound was larger than 8 cm at rest after stabilization, due to the high tension expected on closure, and if the paraspinal muscles did not represent a good coverage of the rods, with subcutaneous tissue directly overlying the fixation devices. Drains were placed in every patient and removed when producing less than 30 mL of serous secretion per day. Chemotherapy was administered both preoperatively and postoperatively with the doxorubicin/cisplatin/HD-MTX (MAP) regimen following the European Society of Medical Oncology guidelines. The intensity modulated RT was administered after the operation for tumors judged to be at high risk of local recurrence.

Grouping and Statistical Analysis

Each patient was then enrolled in one of the two groups depending on the type of surgery performed. Patients included in the orthopedic (OT) group received a partial or total vertebrectomy and posterior stabilization with rods and screws, without any type of muscular or cutaneous plasty performed during primary tumor resection; patients in the orthoplastic (OP) group underwent primary resection and stabilization, followed by a muscle or cutaneous flap to obtain the final closure. Each patient was followed up postoperatively until the end of ambulation and clinical stability. De-identified data were managed using Microsoft Excel data analysis pack (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, Wash.). Normal distribution was not assumed; frequencies were compared with the chi-square test and means with the Mann–Whitney U test. We performed a post-hoc power analysis for chi-square tests, considering a ratio between variances of 2, an alpha of 0.05, and a beta of 0.2, estimating a global sample size of 32 patients.

RESULTS

Between 2012 and 2022, 145 patients underwent partial or total vertebrectomy for oncological reasons in our tertiary-level trauma hospital. Among these, 73 patients were eventually included according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria: 53 in the OT group and 20 in the OP group. There was no difference in the mean age between the OT and OP groups (54 versus 48 years, P > 0.05). Thirty-six patients (68%) in the OT group and 12 patients (60%) in the OP group presented with relevant comorbidities (P < 0.05), whereas primary malignant tumors were the cause of vertebral resection in 26 patients (49%) in the OT group and 18 (90%) in the OP group. The mean number of excised vertebrae was 1.5 for each of the groups, with a mean operative time of 8 hours and 52 minutes in the OT group and 7 hours and 4 minutes in the OP group. Although the relative frequency of patients receiving adjuvant chemotherapy did not differ significantly between the two groups (28% versus 40%), the number and frequency of patients receiving postoperative RT were significantly different between the OT and OP groups (n = 16, 30% versus n = 18, 90%, respectively). Drains were removed at an average 9 days following the operation, without a significant difference between the two groups (8.3 versus 9.7 days). The mean follow-up duration was 22 months in the OT group and 18 months in the OP group.

Among the 20 patients who underwent flap-based reconstruction, four presented with a cervicothoracic defect (range C7–T6) and were reconstructed with an inferior trapezius pure muscle flap; eight presented with a mid-low thoracolumbar defect (range T6–L2) and were reconstructed with a pedicled latissimus dorsi pure muscular flap, based on the main thoraco-dorsal pedicle; eight presented with a lumbosacral defect (range L3–S4) and were reconstructed with a V-Y advancement flap based on the lumbar artery or superior gluteal artery perforators. Complication and Reoperation Rates in Patients Receiving RT

The mean time interval between operation and RT was not significantly different among the two techniques (3.3 months for the OT group and 3.7 months for the OP group; IQR 2–4.1 and 1.9–3.9, respectively). Considering only patients undergoing RT, 11% of total complications (n. 2) occurred before the initiation of RT, and 89% (n. 15), after or during the RT. Among the complications occurring before the RT, one was registered in the OT group and one in the OP group, and they did not delay the start of the RT, being minor dehiscences. The OT group showed significantly higher rates of overall complications (n. 11, 69% versus n. 6, 33%, respectively), dehiscence (n. 5, 31% versus n. 1, 6%, respectively), infections (n. 4, 25% versus n. 0, respectively), and reoperations (n. 5, 31% versus n. 1, 6%, respectively). Among the reoperations, the OT group registered three revisions of infected fixation devices and two soft tissue debridements and closures; in the OP group, the single reoperation was a minor soft-tissue debridement and closure. Complications other than infection and dehiscence were observed in six patients in the OT group (three para-paresis, two perioperative deaths, and one cerebrovascular accident) and four in the OP group (two para-paresis and two perioperative deaths). DISCUSSION

Primary malignant tumors or metastatic diseases involving the spine require partial or total vertebrectomies for complete resection,5,6 and surgical excision with negative margins is the most important prognostic factor in the treatment of sarcomas.11 Adjuvant RT has been shown to improve local tumor control and survival.8 Wide resections, synthetic stabilization devices, and RT carry a risk for complications of overlying soft tissue coverage, such as skin necrosis, wound dehiscence, and infection. Intraoperative edema of the soft tissues and/or inadequate trophism of the paraspinal muscles might be an additional factor that increases the risk of postoperative complications. In our retrospective cohort, we demonstrated the advantage of primary flap closure in patients with excessive retraction of the tissues or inadequate soft-tissue coverage of the stabilization devices.

Direct comparison of patients who received only vertebrectomy (OT group) with those receiving primary wound closure with local flaps showed no significant differences between overall complications, infections, dehiscence, and reoperations. In this comparison, preventive flap coverage did not show any advantages. This result was already clinically significant considering that the OP group included patients considered to have a higher risk of postoperative complications. The overall rate of complications and reoperations in the OP group (40% and 25%, respectively) was comparable to that of the OT group (30% and 23%, respectively), showing that the high-risk population with preventive coverage achieved outcomes comparable to those of the low-risk population. Even if the OP group showed a significant higher number of defects partially involving the cervical (50%) and sacral (35%) column, compared to the OT group (11% and 9%, respectively), we do not consider this difference to represent a bias in the outcomes comparison.

The role of adjuvant therapies in osteosarcoma of the spine has been well addressed by the systematic review performed by Shankar et al,2 where they showed a limited, yet clear advantage of en bloc resection followed by chemotherapy and RT. The poor quality of available evidence on the topic was notable, mainly consisting of case series including fewer than 30 patients. In this setting, our cohort study emerged as one of the largest studies in the literature concerning surgical treatment of osteosarcoma of the spine.

The rates of adjuvant RT differed significantly between the two groups (30% versus 90%, OT and OP, respectively), representing a possible confounding bias. Thus, we performed a bias-reduced analysis, including only patients diagnosed with a primary malignant tumor and receiving adjuvant RT. Sixteen patients were extracted from 53 patients in the OT group, and 18 of 20 patients in the OP group. Considering only this restricted group, the frequency of complications, dehiscence, infections, and reoperations dramatically increased in the OT group (30%, 11%, 8%, and 23%, respectively) and decreased in the OP group (33%, 6%, 0%, and 6%, respectively), showing a significant difference between the two groups. These results confirm the impact of RT on postoperative complications and the efficacy of preventive coverage with a vascularized flap in their reduction. Moreover, the difference between the overall patients and RT-only patients outlines the significance of RT as a confounding bias in all studies that did not perform a bias-reduced analysis.

Fisher et al14 reported a consecutive series of 26 patients who underwent en bloc resection of primary tumors of the spine and subsequent reconstruction. They showed a similar rate of postoperative wound infection (n = 4, 15%) compared to our OT group, yet higher than that in the OP group. The similar reported rate of infection strengthens our results, bolstering the impact of flap coverage in reducing the risk of infection.

Flap coverage has shown efficacy and safety15–17 in complex defect reconstruction, offering a more viable solution compared to the use of other products such as dermal matrices.18 Although different studies are available in the literature reporting the efficacy of flap coverage in defects of the posterior trunk and spine,19–27 they all report the coverage of defects that cannot be closed primarily. In such cases, reconstruction using a local or distant flap is mandatory. Our study focused on the role of preventive flaps in minimizing the risk of postoperative complications in patients undergoing total or partial posterior vertebrectomy after malignant tumor excision.

The patients included in the OP group presented with postexcisional wounds that could be closed primarily but under greater tension or with ineffective coverage of fixation devices. In these patients, flap coverage can be reserved as a secondary treatment for further potential complications. Nevertheless, our study demonstrates that primary flap coverage, especially in patients receiving RT, is able not only to reduce the risk of complications but also to avoid a second reconstructive operation, consequently reducing patient discomfort, hospital stay, and healthcare costs.

This study has some limitations. The main limitation was the subjective criterion for the decision to perform a flap during the first vertebrectomy, which might represent a bias. We consider, though, that comparing flap reconstructions of already complicated wounds represents itself a selection bias, which hinder, the validity of the comparison. If common objective criteria were used to select whether to perform a flap (ie, evidence of soft-tissue defects, infection of fixation devices, etc.), the same criteria would have selected two very different samples. The retrospective nature of this study weakens its level of evidence, although sarcomas of the spine are rare, and a prospective study is laborious and expensive. The relatively low number of patients may have hindered the detection of statistical significance in the above-mentioned outcomes. Future studies are advised, analyzing the possible survival impact of flap coverage, an analysis that we did not carry out due to the limited number of patients. We do not believe that flap coverage may have a direct impact on survival, but that the preventive use of flaps may shorten the average time to adjuvant therapy and indirectly favor local tumor control and/or survival.

CONCLUSIONS

The immediate, preventive coverage of fixation devices during the first intervention of partial or total vertebrectomy in oncological patients undergoing adjuvant RT improves outcomes, reducing the overall complication rate, infections, and wound dehiscence.
