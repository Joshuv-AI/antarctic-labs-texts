Metabolic Tagging of Tumour Extracellular Vesicles for Targeted Modulation of Dendritic Cells
Authors: Rimsha Bhatta, Wenhao Xu, Ruike Dai, Yusheng Liu, Jiadiao Zhou, Yixin Xue, Hua Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExtracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as a promising alternative to cell‐based therapies due to their benign safety profiles and favourable pharmacokinetic properties. Tumour EVs, in particular, have shown promise to function as a therapeutic cancer vaccine for various cancer types. However, the efficient and targeted delivery of EVs to dendritic cells, the prominent type of antigen‐presenting cells in the body, remains a challenge. Here we report the metabolic tagging of tumour EVs with azido groups for subsequent conjugation of anti‐DEC205 via efficient click chemistry. We showed that azido‐sugars can metabolically label cancer cells with azido groups and that azido‐labelled cells can secrete azido‐tagged EVs. Anti‐DEC205, upon facile modification with dibenzocyclooctyne, was successfully conjugated onto azido‐tagged EVs. Anti‐DEC205‐conjugated EVs were preferentially internalized by dendritic cells over other types of immune cells, and improved the processing and presentation of EV‐encased antigens by dendritic cells. We further demonstrated the ability of anti‐DEC205‐conjugated EVs to induce enhanced cytotoxic T lymphocyte responses and antitumor efficacy in comparison with control EVs. Our study provides a facile and universal approach to chemically tagging EVs and conjugating targeting moieties to EVs, for targeted modulation of cells of interest.

Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy has shifted the paradigm for clinical cancer treatment over the past two decades, especially with the success of checkpoint blockade therapies and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapies. [Mellman et al. 2011, Lesterhuis et al. 2011, Martin et al. 2020] However, the limited patient response rate, unsatisfactory therapeutic benefit against various solid tumours, and occasionally severe side effects have limited the utility of existing immunotherapies. Therapeutic cancer vaccines, despite showing less clinical success as checkpoint blockades and CAR T therapies, have the promise to induce long‐term control of tumours with a benign safety profile. [Melief et al. 2015, Mullard 2016, Banchereau and Palucka 2018] Cancer vaccines function by delivering tumour antigens and adjuvants to dendritic cells (DCs), the prominent type of antigen‐presenting cells in the body, for subsequent priming of tumour‐specific T cells. To date, various types of cancer vaccines including DC vaccine, nanovaccine, biomaterial scaffold vaccine, tumour extracellular vesicle (EV) vaccine, and mRNA vaccine have been developed. [Gilboa 2007, Cheever and Higano 2011, Zhao et al. 2022] Among them, tumour EV vaccines have demonstrated favourable safety and the ability to elicit cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses in clinical trials, but are still limited by the modest therapeutic efficacy. [Gu et al. 2015, Cully 2021, Zhao et al. 2024]

Tumour EV vaccines function by administering tumour‐derived EVs into the body, followed by the uptake and processing of EV‐encased tumour antigens by DCs. However, the uptake efficiency of administered tumour EVs by DCs is often low, limiting the potency of resultant CTL responses. These issues have motivated the development of DC‐targeting strategies that can improve the delivery of tumour EVs to DCs in the body. To this end, DC surface receptors such as DEC205 [Bonifaz et al. 2004, Bozzacco et al. 2007], Langerin [Idoyaga et al. 2011, Wamhoff et al. 2019, Bursch et al. 2007], and DC‐SIGN [Engering et al. 2002, Švajger et al. 2010, Unger et al. 2012] have been leveraged to achieve DC‐targeted delivery of cargos. For example, surface functionalization with anti‐DEC205 was shown to improve the uptake of molecules and nanoparticles by DCs. [Zheng et al. 2024, Cruz et al. 2014, Saluja et al. 2014] However, efficient functionalization of tumour EVs with DC‐targeting ligands such as anti‐DEC205 remains a challenge. Conventional EV modification methods rely on the covalent conjugation of carboxyl‐bearing agents to amine‐bearing EVs or the binding of antibodies to proteins present on the surface of EVs, which are limited by the minimal amount of amino groups or receptors on each EV. [Armstrong et al. 2017, Liu et al. 2023, Piffoux et al. 2018, Zhang et al. 2020]

We recently reported an EV metabolic tagging technology that can introduce unique chemical tags (e.g., azido groups) to the surface of EVs. [Bhatta et al. 2023, Bhatta et al. 2025] Azido‐sugars can metabolically label tumour cells with azido groups via the metabolic glycoengineering pathways, [Wang et al. 2017, Wang and Mooney 2020, Laughlin et al. 2006] and the azido‐labelled tumour cells can secrete azido‐tagged EVs. This approach can introduce >3,000 azido groups per EV for various types of cells and thus enables the conjugation of a high number of dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO)‐bearing cargos to EVs via efficient click chemistry. [Baskin et al. 2007, Chang et al. 2010] Here we show that anti‐DEC205 (𝛼DEC205) can be conjugated to tumour EVs via the metabolic tagging method for the development of DC‐targeted tumour EV vaccines (Figure 1a). 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated tumour EVs, upon administration to the body, are expected to target and become processed by DCs. Tumour antigen‐presenting DCs can then prime antigen‐specific CD8+ T cells that can find and kill tumour cells (Figure 1b).

Fabrication of Azido‐Tagged EVs and 𝛼DEC205‐Conjugated EVs

E.G7‐OVA cells were treated with tetraacetyl‐N‐azidoacetylmannosamine (Ac4ManAz), a common metabolic labelling agent, at 37°C for 3–5 days. EVs were isolated from the culture media by centrifugation at 350 rcf for 5 min to remove cell debris, centrifugation with an Amicon filter (100 kDa molecular weight cutoff) at 4,347 rcf for 20 min, and purification with a qEV size exclusion chromatography column. To confirm the presence of azido groups on EVs, EVs collected from Ac4ManAz‐ or PBS‐treated E.G7‐OVA cells were mixed with DBCO‐Cy5 for 30 min and purified. Compared to EVs harvested from unlabelled cells, EVs from Ac4ManAz‐treated cells showed a significantly higher Cy5 fluorescence intensity (Figure 2a), demonstrating the presence of azido groups on EVs secreted by azido‐labelled cells. The morphology of EVs was characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging, which showed negligible differences between azido‐tagged EVs and unlabelled EVs (Figure 2b). The diameter of azido‐tagged EVs and unlabelled EVs also showed negligible differences, as confirmed by Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA) (Figure 2c). To synthesize 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs, we first synthesized DBCO‐functionalized 𝛼DEC205 by reacting 𝛼DEC205 with DBCO‐NHS. 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs were then synthesized by conjugating azido‐labelled EVs with DBCO‐𝛼DEC205 (Figure 2d). For quantification purposes, 𝛼DEC205/Cy5‐conjugated EVs were also synthesized by incubating azido‐labelled EVs with a mixture of DBCO‐𝛼DEC205 and DBCO‐Cy5 (Figure 2e). We also compared the DC‐activation effect of EV‐N3 versus EV by incubating them with DCs and analysing the DC surface activation markers including CD40, CD80, CD86, and MHCII via flow cytometry (Figure S1), which showed negligible differences between EV‐N3 and EV groups. These results align with our prior report on the minimal immunogenicity of EV‐N3 in mice. [Bhatta et al. 2023]

Targeting of 𝛼DEC205‐Conjugated EVs Towards DCs

To confirm the DC targeting capability of 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs, bone marrow‐derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were incubated with 𝛼DEC205/Cy5‐conjugated EVs or Cy5‐conjugated EVs for 16 h, followed by flow cytometry analysis of Cy5+ BMDCs. Compared to DCs treated with Cy5‐conjugated EVs, cells treated with 𝛼DEC205/Cy5‐conjugated EVs showed a significantly higher Cy5 fluorescence intensity (Figure 2f). The enhanced uptake of 𝛼DEC205/Cy5‐EVs by DCs in comparison with Cy5‐EVs occurred consistently across different concentrations of EVs (Figure 2f‐h). We also tested the DC targeting effect of 𝛼DEC205/Cy5‐EVs in a three‐cell type co‐culture system. A mixture of DCs, T cells, and E.G7‐OVA cancer cells were incubated with 𝛼DEC205/Cy5‐EVs or Cy5‐EVs for 16 h. 𝛼DEC205/Cy5‐EVs still exhibited a significantly higher uptake efficiency by DCs than Cy5‐EVs (Figure 2i‐j). Compared to T cells or E.G7‐OVA cells, 𝛼DEC205/Cy5‐EVs were preferentially endocytosed by DCs over T cells and E.G7‐OVA cells (Figure 2i‐j). In vivo, C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously injected with 𝛼DEC205/Cy5‐EVs or Cy5‐EVs, and Cy5+ DCs and macrophages were analysed. Compared to Cy5‐EVs, 𝛼DEC205/Cy5‐EVs led to a higher number of Cy5+ DCs in the draining lymph nodes (Figure 2k). In contrast, negligible differences in the population of Cy5+ macrophages (CD11b+F4/80+) in draining lymph nodes were detected between 𝛼DEC205/Cy5‐EV and Cy5‐EV groups (Figure 2l). These results show that αDEC205‐conjugated EVs exhibit preferential uptake by DCs over the other tested cell types in vitro and enhance EV delivery to DCs in vivo.

𝛼DEC205‐Conjugated EVs Improve Activation and Antigen Presentation of DCs

We next studied whether 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs can induce the enhanced activation and antigen presentation efficiency of DCs. BMDCs were incubated with 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated E.G7‐OVA‐derived EVs, unmodified EVs, or 𝛼DEC205 for 16 h. Compared to unmodified EVs or 𝛼DEC205, 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs resulted in a higher expression level of CD86 and MHCII activation markers on BMDCs (Figure 3a‐c). BMDCs treated with 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs also managed to express a higher level of SIINFEKL, the CD8 epitope of OVA protein, via MHCI (Figure 3d). We further confirmed the presentation of SIINFEKL antigen on BMDCs by co‐culturing these pretreated BMDCs with carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE)‐stained SIINFEKL‐specific CD8+ T cells (OT‐I) for 3 and 6 days, respectively. At 3 or 6 days, BMDCs pretreated with 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs consistently resulted in an enhanced proliferation of OT‐I cells in comparison with non‐pretreated BMDCs (Figure 3e‐f). At 6 days, compared to BMDCs pretreated with 𝛼DEC205 or unmodified EVs, BMDCs pretreated with 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs induced significantly improved proliferation of OT‐I cells (Figure 3g‐h).

We also explored the dose‐dependent activation effect of 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs towards DCs. For this, we fabricated 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs by mixing azido‐tagged E.G7‐OVA EVs with varying input concentrations of DBCO‐𝛼DEC205 (1, 3, 9, and 18 µg/mL) during the conjugation step, and cultured them with BMDCs for 16 h. BMDCs treated with EV alone or the mixtures of azido‐tagged EVs and 𝛼DEC205 were used as the controls. Compared to the mixture of EVs and 𝛼DEC205, 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs consistently resulted in a higher expression level of CD86 and MHCII on BMDCs (Figure 4a‐d). For 𝛼DEC205‐EVs group, the expression levels of CD86 and MHCII on BMDCs overall increased with the input concentration of 𝛼DEC205 (Figure 4a‐d). In the following co‐culture study of BMDCs and OT‐I cells, the proliferation rate of OT‐I cells also increased with the concentration of 𝛼DEC205 for BMDCs pretreated with 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs (Figure 4e‐f). In most conditions, the non‐conjugated mixture of 𝛼DEC205 and EVs resulted in minimal activation of DCs and negligible improvement in the stimulation of OT‐I cells, in comparison with EVs alone (Figure 4e‐f). These experiments demonstrated the ability of 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs to target DCs, induce the enhanced activation of DCs, and improve the processing and presentation of EV‐encased antigens by DCs.

𝛼DEC205‐Conjugated EVs Show Enhanced CTL Response

We next studied the CTL response and antitumor efficacy of 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs. C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously injected with 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs, EV only, or PBS on day 1 (Figure 5a). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated on days 6, 9, and 15 to assess SIINFEKL tetramer+ CD8+ T cells and IFN‐γ + CD8+ T cells upon ex vivo stimulation with SIINFEKL peptide. On day 6, compared to EVs, 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs resulted in a significant increase in the percentages of SIINFEKL tetramer+ and IFN‐γ + cells within the CD3+CD8+ T cell population (Figure 5b‐g, Figure S2a‐g). The increased level of SIINFEKL‐specific CD8+ T cells in PBMCs in the 𝛼DEC205‐EV treatment group was consistently observed on days 9 and 15. In the following prophylactic tumour study where E.G7‐OVA tumour cells were inoculated on day 29, mice treated with 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs showed a reduced tumour growth rate and prolonged survival in comparison with the control groups (Figure 5h‐i, Figure S3a‐e). In a therapeutic setting, E.G7‐OVA cells were first inoculated on day 1, followed by the subcutaneous injections of 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs, EVs, 𝛼DEC205, or PBS on days 10, 12, and 14 (Figure 5j). While showing a limited therapeutic benefit, mice treated with 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs showed a reduced tumour growth rate and prolonged survival time in comparison with EV alone, 𝛼DEC205 alone, or untreated groups (Figure 5k‐l, Figure S4a‐e). To further assess antigen‐specific CTL activation, we pretreated BMDCs with αDEC205‐conjugated EVs or OVA or PBS, followed by the co‐culture of BMDCs with SIINFEKL‐specific OT‐I cells. Compared to the PBS group, BMDCs pretreated with αDEC205‐conjugated EVs or OVA resulted in the upregulated surface expression of CD25 and CD69 activation markers on OT‐I cells, intracellular expression of IFN‐γ and TNF‐α cytokines in OT‐I cells, and enhanced proliferation of OT‐I cells (Figure S5a‐j), demonstrating the capability of αDEC205‐conjugated tumour EVs to prime tumour‐specific CD8+ T cells. In the subsequent tumour cell killing assay, after co‐cultures with the abovementioned BMDCs, OT‐I cells were incubated with GFP‐expressing E.G7‐OVA cells for 12 h. As a result, a reduced tumour cell viability was observed in the αDEC205‐EV group than in the PBS group (Figure S6a‐c), demonstrating that CD8+ T cells primed by EV vaccines can kill the corresponding tumour cells. These results demonstrate that the conjugation of 𝛼DEC205 to tumour EVs via our metabolic tagging approach can improve the delivery of EV‐encased antigens to DCs, enhance the activation status of DCs, and improve the overall antigen‐specific CTL response and antitumor efficacy of tumour EV vaccines.

𝛼DEC205/CpG‐Conjugated EVs Induce Robust CTL Response and Antitumor Efficacy

To further improve the antitumor efficacy of EV vaccines, we explored the co‐conjugation of 𝛼DEC205 and CpG, a commonly used toll like receptor 9 (TLR9) agonist, to tumour EVs. 𝛼DEC205/CpG‐conjugated EVs (𝛼DEC205/CpG‐EV) were synthesized by mixing azido‐tagged E.G7‐OVA‐derived EVs, DBCO‐𝛼DEC205, and DBCO‐CpG. CpG‐conjugated EVs (CpG‐EV) without 𝛼DEC205 conjugation were used as controls. Both 𝛼DEC205/CpG‐EV and CpG‐EV were able to induce the activation of DCs (Figure S7a‐c) and lead to successful presentation of MHCI‐SIINFEKL on DCs (Figure S8a‐c). Compared to CpG‐EV, the pretreatment of DCs with 𝛼DEC205/CpG‐EV resulted in enhanced long‐term proliferation of OT‐I cells (Figure S8b‐c). In vivo, subcutaneous injections of 𝛼DEC205/CpG‐EV and CpG‐EV elicited the production of SIINFEKL‐specific CD8+ T cells, and 𝛼DEC205/CpG‐EV resulted in a slightly higher systemic level of SIINFEKL‐specific CD8+ T cells than CpG‐EV (Figure 6a‐d, Figure S9–S10). In the subsequent prophylactic tumour study, compared to the abovementioned 𝛼DEC205‐ EV or unmodified EV (Figure 5h‐i), both 𝛼DEC205/CpG‐EV and CpG‐EV showed dramatically improved tumour control and animal survival (Figure 6e‐f). In comparison with CpG‐EV, 𝛼DEC205/CpG‐EV showed a slightly reduced tumour growth rate and prolonged animal survival (Figure 6e‐f). In the therapeutic setting, 𝛼DEC205/CpG‐EV and CpG‐EV also exhibited significantly slowed tumour growth and prolonged animal survival in comparison with 𝛼DEC205‐EV or unmodified EV described above (Figure 6g‐h, Figure 5k‐l). These experiments demonstrated that the co‐conjugation of CpG adjuvant can further improve the CTL response and antitumor efficacy of 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated tumour EVs.

Discussion

EVs have gained significant interest due to their promising therapeutic and diagnostic applications. In our previous studies [Bhatta et al. 2023, Bhatta et al. 2025], we successfully leveraged metabolic glycoengineering to introduce clickable azido tags to the surface of EVs, enabling facile and precise functionalization of EVs with various types of agents through click chemistry. Based on this technology, in this work, we demonstrated the facile conjugation of DBCO‐𝛼DEC205 to azido‐tagged EVs. The surface functionalization with 𝛼DEC205 conferred the DC‐targeting ability to EVs, by significantly improving the uptake efficiency of EVs by DCs. Our results showed that 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs were preferentially internalized by DCs than other tested cell types, including T cells and cancer cells. Compared to conventional EV modification methods, which typically rely on amine‐carboxyl conjugation or antibody binding to surface proteins [Armstrong et al. 2017, Liu et al. 2023, Piffoux et al. 2018, Zhang et al. 2020], our metabolic tagging and conjugation approach can be universally applied to EVs derived from different types of cells. This approach overcomes the low abundance of functional groups or specific protein molecules on native EVs and provides a platform for developing enhanced EV‐based diagnostics and therapeutics in the context of cancer, autoimmune disorders, inflammatory diseases, and other diseases. [Wiklander et al. 2019, Kumar et al. 2024]

In addition to the enhanced cellular uptake, 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs also induced the activation of DCs and improved the processing and presentation of EV‐encased antigens by DCs in comparison with control EVs. This confers an enhanced CTL response and a modestly improved tumour control in both prophylactic and therapeutic settings (Figure 5). It appears that 𝛼DEC205 functionalization alone, through the metabolic labelling and click conjugation, can enhance the uptake of EVs by DCs and increase the activation status of DCs to some extent. However, 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs are not potent enough to yield a significant therapeutic benefit. To this end, we showed that the co‐conjugation of 𝛼DEC205 and CpG adjuvant can further improve the CTL response and therapeutic efficacy of EV vaccines (Figure 6). While CpG‐conjugated EVs have demonstrated promise to elicit decent CTL response and antitumor efficacy, the concern on the systemic toxicity of adjuvants such as CpG remains a hurdle for potential clinical translation. The co‐conjugation of αDEC205 onto EVs, as introduced in this study, provides a viable option to improve the CTL response of EV vaccines with a reduced amount of adjuvants. The optimization of αDEC205‐to‐CpG ratio for αDEC205/CpG‐conjugated EVs in future efforts would be valuable. The combination of EV vaccines and existing immunotherapies including checkpoint blockades also warrants further investigation

To summarize, we report a facile method to develop 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated tumour EVs, by metabolically tagging EVs with azido groups and then conjugating DBCO‐𝛼DEC205 onto azido‐tagged EVs via click chemistry. We show that 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated tumour EVs can be preferentially taken up by DCs over other types of cells. Compared to control EVs, 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated tumour EVs can induce the activation of DCs and improve the processing of EV‐encased antigens by DCs, leading to enhanced CTL responses. The co‐conjugation of 𝛼DEC205 and CpG adjuvant can further improve the CTL response and antitumor efficacy of tumour EV vaccines. Our study introduces a facile approach to improving the antitumor efficacy of conventional tumour EV vaccines, and offers a universal platform to display targeting ligands on EVs to achieve targeted delivery of EVs to cell types of interest.

Methods

Materials and Instrumentation

D‐Mannosamine hydrochloride, DBCO‐Cy5, DBCO‐Cy3, sodium azide, bromoacetic acid, dicyclohexyl carbodiimide, N‐hydroxysuccinimide, and other chemical reagents were acquired from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) unless stated otherwise. Recombinant murine GM‐CSF was procured from PeproTech, Inc. (Cranbury, NJ, USA). The primary antibodies used in this study include fluorophore‐conjugated anti‐CD11b (Invitrogen, Catalog #14‐0112‐82), anti‐CD11c (Invitrogen, Catalog #12‐0114‐82), anti‐CD86 (Invitrogen, Catalog #12‐0862‐82), anti‐MHCII (Invitrogen, Catalog #11‐0920‐82), anti‐CD3 (Invitrogen, Catalog #17‐0031‐82), anti‐CD8 (Invitrogen, Catalog #45‐0081‐82), anti‐CD4 (Invitrogen, Catalog #12‐0041‐82), anti‐MHCI‐SIINFEKL (Invitrogen, Catalog #12‐5743‐82), and anti‐IFN‐γ (Invitrogen, Catalog #17‐7311‐82). The fixable viability dye efluor780 (Catalog #65‐0865‐14) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. All antibodies were diluted 1:100, except for the fixability dye, which was diluted 1:1000. SIINFEKL‐MHC I tetramer was obtained from the NIH Tetramer Core. Mouse T cell isolation kits, Dynabeads, and LS separation columns were sourced from Miltenyi Biotec (Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). qEV isolation columns were purchased from IZON Science (Christchurch, New Zealand). Flow cytometry analyses were performed using Attune NxT or BD LSR Fortessa flow cytometers and analysed with FlowJo v7.6 and FCS Express v6 and v7 software. Fluorescence measurements of DBCO‐Cy3 and DBCO‐Cy5 were performed on a plate reader. Small compounds were analysed using the Agilent 1290/6140 ultra high‐performance liquid chromatography/mass spectrometer. Proton nuclear magnetic resonance spectra were collected on the Agilent DD2 600. Matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectra were obtained using the Bruker Ultraflextreme MALDI‐TOF/TOF Mass Spectrometer. The size and distribution of EVs were measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS) and NTA instruments. Transmission electron microscopic images of EVs were captured with a JEOL 2100 TEM.

Cell Line and Animals

The E.G7‐OVA (ATCC CRL‐2113) cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA) and was authenticated to confirm the absence of any contamination. Cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 units/mL Penicillin G, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and G418 at 37°C in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere. Female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). They were provided with feed and water ad libitum and were kept under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. All animal procedures were performed in compliance with National Institutes of Health and Institutional guidelines, with approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Illinois at Urbana‐Champaign.

Synthesis of Ac4ManAz

D‐Mannosamine hydrochloride (1.0 mmol) and triethylamine (1.0 mmol) were dissolved in methanol, after which N‐(2‐azidoacetyl) succinimide (1.2 mmol) was added. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h. Following this, the solvent was removed under reduced pressure, and the residue was re‐dissolved in pyridine. Acetic anhydride was then added, and the reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for an additional 24 h. Isolation of Metabolically Labelled EVs

E.G7‐OVA cells were cultured in T75 flask in the presence of Ac4ManAz (50 µM) or PBS for 3–5 days. On day 3–5, cell culture media containing EV were collected followed by centrifugation at 350 rcf for 5 min to remove cells and debris. After the removal of any cell or debris, the medium was collected and subjected to centrifugation with an Amicon filter (100 kDa cutoff molecular weight) at a speed of 4,347 rcf for 15 min. The centrifugation washing steps were repeated for at least 4 times to collect EVs. qEV size exclusion column was then used to further purify EVs based on their size. Dynamic Light scattering measurement of the collected EVs was performed to measure their size and size distribution. The NTA was used to measure the concentration of EVs.

Conjugation of DBCO‐Fluorophore to EVs

Azido‐labelled E.G7‐OVA EVs were incubated with DBCO‐Cy5 or DBCO‐Cy3 at room temperature for 30 or 60 min, followed by the removal of unbound fluorophores using centrifugation. The fluorescence intensity of fluorophore‐conjugated EVs was measured on a plate reader.

Synthesis of DBCO‐𝛼DEC205

𝛼DEC205 and DBCO‐sulfo‐NHS (1:5 molar ratio) were mixed in PBS and shaken for overnight at 4°C. The unreacted DBCO‐sulfo‐NHS was removed by centrifugation with an Amicon filter (3,000 Da molecular weight cutoff).

Conjugation of 𝛼DEC205 to EVs

DBCO‐𝛼DEC205 in PBS was mixed with the solution of azido‐tagged EVs at 4°C for overnight. The unreacted DBCO‐𝛼DEC205 was removed by centrifugation with an Amicon filter (100 kDa molecular weight cutoff). Similarly, 𝛼DEC205/Cy5‐conjugated EVs were synthesized by co‐conjugating DBCO‐Cy5 and DBCO‐𝛼DEC205 to azido‐tagged EVs.

Uptake of 𝛼DEC205‐EV by DCs

BMDCs were isolated by following a previously established protocol. Briefly, bone marrow cells were isolated from the tibia and femur of C57BL/6 mice and cultured in RPMI media in the presence of 20 ng/ml GM‐CSF for 7 days. On day 7, the medium was replaced with fresh media with 10 ng/ml of GM‐CSF. To study the uptake of EVs by BMDCs, day‐7 BMDCs were incubated with 𝛼DEC205/Cy5‐conjugated EVs or Cy5‐conjugated EVs for 16 h, followed by flow cytometry analysis.

In Vitro Activation of DCs by 𝛼DEC205‐EV

Day‐7 BMDCs (50 k) in 100 µL of culture medium were incubated with 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs, EVs alone, 𝛼DEC205 alone, or PBS for 16 h. For most experiments, the input concentrations used during EV conjugation were 9 µg/mL for anti‐DEC205 and 1×107/mL for EVs. To study the effect of αDEC205 input during conjugation, in some experiments, the input concentration of αDEC205 was set at 1, 3, 9, and 18 µg/mL, respectively. These values refer to the concentration of αDEC205 added during the conjugation step, rather than the absolute amount conjugated onto each EV. After incubation, DCs were stained with fluorophore‐conjugated anti‐CD11c and anti‐CD86, along with a live/dead viability dye, for 20 min at 4°C prior to flow cytometry analysis. For experiments involving the analysis of antigen presentation efficiency, an additional antibody, APC‐conjugated anti‐MHCI‐SIINFEKL, was used for flow cytometry analysis.

Co‐culture of DCs and OT‐I Cells

BMDCs were pretreated with 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs, EVs alone, 𝛼DEC205 alone, or PBS for 16 h. In some experiments, BMDCs were pretreated with 𝛼DEC205/CpG‐conjugated EVs or CpG‐conjugated EVs. BMDCs were then co‐cultured with CFSE‐stained OT‐I cells for 3 or 6 days, followed by flow cytometry analysis of OT‐I cell proliferation. Unless otherwise noted, the concentration of EVs was set at 1 × 107/mL. The input concentrations used during conjugation were 9 µg/mL for αDEC205 and 5 nM for CpG. These values indicate the amounts added during the conjugation reaction and do not represent the absolute quantity conjugated per EV. The CpG dose was selected as a moderate adjuvant input to induce detectable immune activation without introducing excessive toxicity, per our prior study. [Bhatta et al. 2023]

OT‐I Activation Assay

BMDCs were treated with αDEC205‐conjugated EVs or OVA or PBS for 16 h and then co‐cultured with OT‐I cells for 24 h. After co‐culture, cells were stained with fluorophore‐conjugated antibodies against CD8, CD25, CD69, IFN‐γ, and TNF‐α, together with a live/dead viability dye, followed by flow cytometry analysis. In separate experiments, CFSE‐stained OT‐I cells were co‐cultured with treated BMDCs for 3 days, and CFSE dilution was analysed by flow cytometry.

Tumour Killing Assay

BMDCs were treated with αDEC205‐conjugated EVs or OVA or PBS for 16 h and then co‐cultured with OT‐I cells for 24 h. The primed OT‐I cells were subsequently co‐cultured with E.G7‐OVA‐GFP cells for 12 h. Tumour cell viability was then analysed by flow cytometry.

In Vivo Tracking of 𝛼DEC205‐Conjugated EVs

𝛼DEC205/Cy5‐conjugated EVs or control Cy5‐EVs were subcutaneously injected into the flank of C57BL/6 mice. After 16 h, lymph nodes were isolated for analysis. Single cell suspensions from lymph nodes were stained with fluorophore‐conjugated anti‐CD11b, anti‐CD11c, anti‐F4/80, and live/dead stain prior to flow cytometry analysis.

Vaccination and Prophylactic Tumour Study

C57BL/6 mice (5–7 weeks) were subcutaneously injected with 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs, EVs alone, 𝛼DEC205 alone, or PBS, 𝛼DEC205/CpG‐conjugated EVs, or CpG‐conjugated EVs (100 µL) on day 1. The concentration of EVs was set at 1×107/mL. The input concentrations of αDEC205 and CpG used during EV conjugation were 9 µg/mL and 5 nM, respectively. These values refer to the amounts added in the conjugation step, rather than the absolute quantity conjugated per EV. The blood was drawn on days 6, 9, 15, and 25, respectively to analyse SIINFEKL‐specific CD8+ T cells in PBMCs. For the tetramer analysis, PBMCs were stained with dye‐conjugated anti‐CD3, anti‐CD8, anti‐CD4, and SIINFEKL tetramer and fixable viability dye for 20 min, followed by multiple washing steps and flow cytometry analysis. To analyse IFN‐γ expression in CD8+ T cells, PBMCs were stimulated with OVA peptide ex vivo for 1.5 h, treated with Golgi plug for 2.5 h, stained with FITC‐conjugated anti‐CD3, PE‐conjugated anti‐CD8, and e780 fixable viability dye, treated with the fixation & permeabilization buffer, stained the APC‐conjugated anti‐IFN‐γ, and analysed on a flow cytometer. For the following prophylactic tumour study, 2 × 105 E.G7‐OVA cells in 50 µL of HBSS were injected subcutaneously on day 29, and the tumour volume was measured every 3–4 days. The tumour volume was calculated by taking (length) x (width)2/2, where the long axis diameter is the length and the short axis diameter is the width.

Therapeutic Tumour Study with E.G7‐OVA Model

Mice were subcutaneously injected with 2 × 105 E.G7‐OVA cells in 50 µL of HBSS on day 1. On days 10, 12, and 14, respectively, mice were subcutaneously injected with 𝛼DEC205‐conjugated EVs, EVs alone, DEC205 alone, PBS, 𝛼DEC205/CpG‐conjugated EVs, or CpG‐conjugated EVs. The concentration of EVs, 𝛼DEC205, and CpG were set at 1×107/mL, 9 µg/mL, and 5 nM, respectively. Tumour volume was monitored every 3–4 days. The tumour volume was calculated by taking (length) × (width)2/2, where the long axis diameter is the length, and the short axis diameter is the width.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism v6 and v8. Sample variance was tested using the F test. For samples with equal variance, the significance between the groups was analysed by a two‐tailed student's t test. For samples with unequal variance, a two‐tailed Welch's t‐test was performed. For multiple comparisons, a one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post hoc Fisher's LSD test was used. The results were deemed significant at 0.01< * P ≤ 0.05, 0.001< ** P ≤ 0.01, highly significant at 0.0001< *** P ≤ 0.001, and extremely significant at **** P ≤ 0.0001.
