From prescribing indicators to rational drug use: a medication safety perspective
Authors: Muhammad Farooq Anwar, Nur Aizati Athirah Daud, Rabia Hussain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOver the past few decades, the emergence of irrational medicine use has become a significant global health challenge. It has contributed to medication errors, adverse drug reactions, higher treatment costs, increased morbidity, and mortality. Problems with irrational prescribing are a matter of concern in low and middle-income countries, where regulatory control is underdeveloped, healthcare systems are constrained by economic pressures, and there is a shortage of trained personnel. The World Health Organization (WHO) developed core prescribing indicators to assess and promote rational medication use for healthcare settings. These include indicators measuring polypharmacy, antibiotic use, injectable use, generic prescribing, and adherence to essential medicine lists. This narrative commentary explores the potential of WHO prescribing indicators in promoting the rational use of medicines, highlighting their role in patient safety and supporting evidence-based prescribing practices. The five key areas of these indicators provide valuable insights into prescribing practices in healthcare settings of low and middle-income countries. These indicators also support the WHO’s initiative of Medication without Harm. Despite their potential strengths, there are some potential limitations, such as failing to capture the data behind the prescribing practices of prescribers and their utility in specialised healthcare settings. The future of rational prescribing lies in adapting these tools through qualitative assessments, comprehensive data collection methods that include qualitative study design approaches, and targeted training interventions to ensure safe, effective, and equitable medication practices across healthcare systems globally.

Introduction

In the last three decades, the global challenge of irrational medication use has become a significant issue in clinical practices. The World Health Organization (WHO) has reported that more than 50% of medicines are used irrationally, whether in prescribing, selling, dispensing, or administering them to patients. The WHO emphasises the importance of using medications rationally by ensuring that it is prescribed according to clinical needs and at an affordable price (Meena et al., 2022; Ofori-Asenso & Agyeman, 2016). The increasing global incidence of irrational medication use is a significant concern for healthcare systems and providers. Irrational prescribing occurs whenever the principles of rational medication prescribing are ignored or overlooked. This often arises due to various circumstances, which lead to failure of treatment, adverse drug events, an increase in morbidity, high mortality rates, and increased financial burden on patients as well as on healthcare entities (Sri Ranganathan, 2008).

Poor adherence to good prescribing practices or irrational use of medicines is always a public health issue in developing countries, whereby Irrational medicine use refers to excessive, incorrect, or inappropriate use of medicines (Zairina et al., 2024). This results in emerging resistance to antibiotics, the use of multiple drugs, and irrational use of medicine by patients in the prevention or treatment of disease, which contribute to ongoing public health challenges. Poor prescribing occurs due to economic constraints, inadequate regulatory control, insufficient knowledge, and a shortage of experienced and trained staff (Melku et al., 2021).

As the world is aging, healthcare expenses are also increasing. According to the WHO 2021 report, global expenditures in the health sector doubled from USD 4.2 trillion in 2000 to USD 8.9 trillion in 2019, while primary healthcare contributes to more than half of the total expenditures (Global Expenditure on Health: Public Spending on the Rise? 2021). The WHO published data on medication errors, indicating that if these errors are preventable, approximately USD 42 billion could be saved (Medication without Harm: Policy Brief. Geneva:WHO, 2023). The Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) report on medication safety in 2022 states that poor medication safety and sub-optimal prescribing lead to 1 in 10 hospitalisations and 1 in 5 inpatients experiencing medication-related harms during hospital stay (Katherine et al., 2022).

The problem of rational prescribing is emerging as a serious concern for low and middle-income countries (LMICs), where regulatory control is underdeveloped, healthcare systems are constrained by economic pressures, and there is a shortage of medicines. Now, it is also emerging as a problem in high-income countries. Inappropriate prescribing is one of the leading factors contributing to the irrational use of medicines (Albarqouni et al., 2023; Choonara, 2013). This narrative commentary aims to provide a brief overview of the WHO prescribing indicators for assessing rational prescribing. It will explore the insights gained from utilising prescribing indicators, considering their advantages, and examining their limitations in healthcare settings.

Understanding the need for prescribing indicators

Medication safety is a global health issue affecting all age groups and the overall population. A systematic review by Panagioti et al. (2023) reported the global prevalence of medication errors, indicating that 1 in 20 patients is affected by these errors. This study also highlighted that in LMICs, about 80% of the medication errors occur at the stage of prescribing (Panagioti et al., 2023). On average, 125 children die every day because of the irrational use of medicines (Li et al., 2022). A retrospective study based on a prescription audit in a tertiary care hospital in India reported that 22.3% of the prescribing errors were related to medicines usage among 1,451 prescriptions (Mulkalwar et al., 2024). A prospective observational study in Ethiopia reported that in the older adult population, out of 813 medication errors, 32% were due to prescribing errors by the prescribers (Gebre et al., 2021). A systematic review focusing on medication errors in outpatient and ambulatory settings reported that the majority of studies highlighted prescribing errors as the most common type of error (Naseralallah et al., 2023). Such errors often arise due to the lack of experience, inadequate awareness of up-to-date literature, informational asymmetry, and the physician-patient relationship (Duman & Karadağ, 2024; Mohamadloo et al., 2017). Studies from African countries have reported that poor prescribing practices, such as deviations from guideline-directed medical therapy and the overuse of medicines, pose a potential threat to the rational use of medicines. Such practices mainly impact the overall patient safety and may lead to drug–drug interactions, adverse drug events, and antimicrobial resistance (Amaha et al., 2019; Melku et al., 2021; Okoye et al., 2022). These findings raise serious concerns about patient safety within the public healthcare system and highlight the need to enhance prescribing practices and enforce strict medication safety protocols to reduce preventable harm (Fox et al., 2016).

The WHO launched the Global Patient Safety Challenge (GPSC), Medication Without Harm, in 2017, aiming to reduce the overall burden of severe and preventable drug-related harm by 50% in the coming five years (Khawagi et al., 2021). At the 72nd World Health Assembly (WHA) in 2019, a resolution was adopted on GPSC, focusing on the implementation of strategic patient safety interventions and policies to eliminate avoidable harm in healthcare settings. This reflects the clear commitment of the WHA and WHO to improving medication management for reducing the prevalence of medication errors.

In 2024, the WHO released a policy brief on Medication Without Harm, aiming to enhance systems and practices for medication use, improve medication literacy, increase the availability of medicines, and ensure healthcare professionals have adequate knowledge (Medication Without Harm : Policy Brief, 2024). In the light of this global perspective of safer medication practices, rational pharmacotherapy is the only way out for the treatment of communicable and non-communicable diseases worldwide (Rieder, 2015). According to the WHO definition, rational pharmacotherapy requires that patients receive medications appropriate to their clinical needs, in doses that meet their requirements, for an adequate period, and at the lowest possible cost. This process follows the comprehensive six-step prescribing framework for physicians, outlined in the ‘Guide to Good Prescribing’, which promotes the rational use of medicines (Figure 1) (de Vries et al., 1994). Prescribing is about making better decisions by utilising evidence-based guidelines, focusing on the patient's needs, and using helpful tools to ensure the right choice of medicine for the right duration, thereby promoting the rational use of medicine (Akici & Oktay, 2007; Panagioti et al., 2023). The rational use of medicine helps improve treatment outcomes and contributes to the overall improvement of public health initiatives by promoting patient safety, ensuring equitable access to effective treatments, and reducing medication errors and adverse events (AL Qamariat, 2021). Establishing effective monitoring mechanisms is a crucial step in reducing or preventing medication errors. This process not only helps to reduce the risk of misuse, overuse, and potential adverse drug events, but also promotes the rational use of medicines (Thomas et al., 2013).

Types of prescribing assessment tools

Over time, many implicit (judgment-based) and explicit (criterion-based) tools have been developed to improve medication use. These tools enable healthcare institutions to make informed strategic decisions, assess the quality of prescriptions, and guide prescribers in promoting the rational use of medications (Kaufmann et al., 2014; Spackman et al., 2019). In implicit or judgment-based evaluations, clinicians evaluate a patient’s treatment regimen. However, the findings may lack validity, reliability, and generalisation, depending on the user experience, knowledge, and attitude. Issues of validity and reliability can be addressed through the use of clearly specified data collection instruments and training for data collectors, such as the Medication Appropriateness Index (MAI). In geriatric patients, MAI is a helpful tool to determine whether a medication is appropriate or inappropriate (Li et al., 2022; Shelton et al., 2000; Spinewine et al., 2007). Explicit tools, on the other hand, are developed from literature reviews, consensus methods, and expert opinions. Examples include Beers Criteria, Pediatric, Omission of Prescription and Inappropriateness (POPI), and WHO prescribing Indicators. These tools provide strict criteria and an equitable methodology for patient care, with a primary focus on medication safety. However, they fail to account for the tailored needs of patients and the multiplicity of treatment regimens. Depending on each county's guidelines and essential medicine list, these tools require regular updates to remain in context (Kaufmann et al., 2014; Prot-Labarthe et al., 2014; Spinewine et al., 2007).

All of these implicit and explicit tools are evident, although the study settings for their development and implementation differ significantly; for example, the availability of advanced drugs, disease burden and prevalence, and healthcare infrastructure may contribute towards the selection of a particular tool. The majority of these tools originate from high-income countries and cannot be generalised to LMICs, as healthcare settings and resources differ significantly. Among these tools, the WHO core prescribing indicators are a cornerstone for assessing prescribing practices, offering an adaptable framework that supports rational medicine use in all healthcare settings.

WHO prescribing indicators

During the early 1980s, the WHO and the International Network for Rational Use of Drugs (INRUD) developed a list of key indicators to assess drug use in healthcare facilities (Kilipamwambu et al., 2021; Ofori-Asenso, 2016). These indicators significantly contributed to providing the necessary data for the quantitative assessment of rational medication use (Wendie et al., 2021). In 1993, the WHO convened a session in Geneva and proposed core drug use indicators, publishing the guidelines on ‘How to Investigate Drug Use in Health Facilities’ in collaboration with INRUD (Figure 2) (WHO, How to Investigate Drug Use in Health Facilities?, 1993). They are widely recognised and used as a global standard for assessing drug prescribing, especially in outpatient settings at the primary healthcare level (Tefera et al., 2021). Measuring polypharmacy using the WHO indicator

This indicator serves to quantify the degree of polypharmacy, which is defined as the ‘concurrent use of multiple medications’ (Medication Safety in Polypharmacy. Geneva: WHO, 2019). Polypharmacy may be clinically necessary and appropriate in comorbid patients who often require the concurrent use of several medications. It mainly contributes to drug–drug interactions, therapeutic duplications, and the incidence of adverse drug events (Mariya Davis et al., 2021). Polypharmacy can be quantified when a combination of different drugs is considered as a single entity, and this can be computed by dividing the total number of prescribed drugs by the number of patient encounters (Ofori-Asenso, 2016; WHO, How to Investigate Drug Use in Health Facilities?, 1993).

Assessing antibiotic prescription frequency

The indicator assesses the frequency with which healthcare service providers prescribe antibiotics. This helps in understanding the rate of antibiotic use, which in turn aids in recognising the misuse of antibiotics and their contribution to antibiotic resistance (Gujar et al., 2021). In areas with a significant prevalence of infectious diseases, an increase in antibiotic prescribing appears to be a clinically justified response, whether administered prophylactically or for treatment. The percentage of antibiotics prescribed is calculated by dividing the number of encounters with antibiotics prescribed by the total number of encounters (Ofori-Asenso, 2016; WHO, How to Investigate Drug Use in Health Facilities?, 1993).

Monitoring injectable medication use

Administration of medication by injection form has the potential for serious consequences like anaphylactic reactions, necrosis, infections, etc. However, in the case of admitted patients and ambulatory settings, the administration of medicines through injections is often a medical necessity to save lives (A. Gujar et al., 2021; Rohitha et al., 2024). This indicator is calculated by dividing the number of medicines prescribed as injectable in an encounter by the total number of encounters, and then multiplying it by 100 to show the result as a percentage (WHO, How to Investigate Drug Use in Health Facilities?, 1993).

Evaluating generic prescribing practices

The WHO prescribing indicator is used to measure the prescriber's habit of prescribing using generic or international non-proprietary names of medicines. In this indicator, prescriptions are carefully examined, and only the generic name of medicines is considered (Ofori-Asenso, 2016). This can also indicate the prescriber's familiarity with the generic name of the drug in contrast to its brand name. This can help determine the impact of pharmaceutical companies on physicians’ prescribing behaviour in promoting brand-name medicines (Mariya Davis et al., 2021). It is measured in percentages by dividing the number of drugs prescribed by generic names by the total number of drugs prescribed and then multiplying by 100 (WHO, How to Investigate Drug Use in Health Facilities?, 1993).

Compliance with essential medicine list

This indicator emphasises conformity with national medicine use policies, such as the Essential Medicines List (EML). EML is a list of medicines that fulfil the requirements of a country's population in a controlled, cost-effective manner, with a limited number of medicines. Healthcare institutions prepare local formularies based on the EML. In LMICs, compliance is quite challenging due to issues related to the supply chain and patent rights of pharmaceutical companies for specific medications. The percentage of medicines prescribed from the EML is measured by dividing the number of medicines prescribed from the EML by the total number of medications prescribed (Ofori-Asenso, 2016; WHO, How to Investigate Drug Use in Health Facilities?, 1993).

Applications of WHO prescribing indicators

The WHO prescribing indicators help in identifying potential medicine use problems, especially in outpatient settings. These indicators identify potential adverse drug events and medication errors by assessing the extent of key aspects such as polypharmacy, antibiotic usage, and the prescribing of injectable drugs. For instance, a systematic review based on twenty-one studies focused on prescribing patterns at primary healthcare facilities in developing countries, concluded that the WHO prescribing standards were not fully met in these countries. The main reasons include polypharmacy, overprescribing of antibiotics, brand preference, and low compliance with EML. This study also found that WHO prescribing indicators can have a significant impact in promoting patient safety and rational medicine use (Kristina & Wulandari, 2020). These findings highlighted the importance of systematically analysing prescribing patterns by regulating the monitoring of prescribing practices through the WHO core indicators, which promote the rational use of medicines. Drug utilisation assessments in hospitals, based on WHO prescribing indicators, can assist in policy-making decisions and regulatory frameworks. Furthermore, it helps to evaluate the impact of pharmaceutical companies on the prescribers and ensure the availability of EML medicines in healthcare facilities (A. Rohitha et al., 2024).

Polypharmacy is always an issue while managing patients with morbidities. Such patients are at risk of having potential drug–drug interactions, side effects, and adverse drug reactions. Among special populations (geriatrics, pediatrics, and pregnant females), polypharmacy is a leading factor associated with poor treatment adherence, especially in LMICs. Thus, establishing polypharmacy stewardship helps reduce the potential risks of irrational medication use and promotes cost-effectiveness (Barlow et al., 2021; Daunt et al., 2023).

In outpatient settings, antibiotics are widely prescribed either for prophylaxis or for the treatment of different diseases, especially in LMICs. The use of antibiotics beyond the optimal range may alert healthcare professionals to the increased rate of infections, or it may be a sign of poor prescribing practice. Such practices contribute to the emergence of multidrug resistance, resulting in increased morbidity, mortality, and treatment costs (Medication without Harm: Policy Brief. Geneva:WHO, 2023; Ofori-Asenso, 2016). Multiple factors contribute to the unnecessary use of antibiotics, including irrational prescriptions, promotional activities by pharmaceutical companies, limited access to healthcare, inadequate knowledge of antibiotics, and their improper use (Ndaki et al., 2025). Irrational use of antibiotics is considered a waste of resources, as bacteria and viruses are emerging as superbugs, and the world is running short of effective antibiotics. To avoid this, the WHO and INRUD considered including antibiotics in the prescribing indicators to promote their rational use (Hailesilase et al., 2024; Sartelli et al., 2020). Targeted intervention, following antimicrobial stewardship, reduces the irrational use of antibiotics; for example, the implementation of the AWaRe classification for antibiotic use has shown success by reducing the overprescribing of antibiotics (Mudenda et al., 2023; Sartelli et al., 2020).

There were many crucial aspects of intravenous medicine use reported, which harm patient safety as well as the safety of healthcare workers. A systematic review by Kuitunen et al. (2020) reported that a lack of knowledge about medicine and communication errors, both in prescribing, can impact the safe administration of medication in patients (Kuitunen et al., 2020). Patients are at high risk of getting infections like Hepatitis B, C, catheter-related bloodstream infections, and nosocomial infections if aseptic techniques of injection preparation and administration are not followed. Medication overdose is also a significant concern that affects the overall morbidity of the patients (Alhumaid et al., 2021; Levengood et al., 2021). Safe injection practices are the biggest concern in saving patients from the risk of blood-borne diseases and healthcare workers from needle stick injuries (WHO, 2016). Due to these concerns, the WHO recommends prioritising the prescribing of oral medications as the primary mode of medicine administration. The WHO prescribing indicators enable healthcare facilities to monitor and minimise unnecessary injection prescribing, ultimately improving patient and healthcare worker safety (Desalegn, 2013; Tefera et al., 2021).

Multiple clinical and economic benefits have been observed when drugs are prescribed using their generic names. Among them, facilitating the communication of drug information and reducing medication errors are at the top, as both improve patient safety. The WHO emphasises generic prescribing as it facilitates lowering treatment costs and improving research and development at the industrial level (Agrawal et al., 2025). The former organisations, IMS Healthcare and Quintile, now known as IQVIA, have published a report on the utilisation of generic medicines. The report stated that the availability of generic medication has led to a twofold increase in access to medicine for patients who suffer from chronic illnesses. It highlighted that medications used to treat hyperlipidemia and cardiovascular diseases are available at cheap prices all across the world. Therefore, the use of generic medicines or generic substitution in LMICs can help reduce the disease burden among underserved patient categories (IQVIA, 2024).

Training and educational programmes for healthcare professionals are essential to ensure a comprehensive understanding and effective application of rational medication use (de Vries et al., 1994; Kristina & Wulandari, 2020). The WHO prescribing indicators support healthcare institutions in evaluating and optimising their prescribing practices, promoting the rational use of medicines (Ofori-Asenso, 2016). In particular, healthcare facilities in LMICs face multiple challenges, including insufficient human resources, weak governance, inadequate infrastructure, and limited public awareness regarding medication literacy, which further contributes to the additional burden on the healthcare system (Medication Without Harm: Policy Brief, 2024). Therefore, these indicators have the potential to provide valuable insights into prescribing behaviour and to identify areas for improvement in medication usage (Agrawal et al., 2025; Holloway, 2011).

Limitations of WHO core prescribing indicators

WHO core prescribing indicators are valuable tool for assessing medicine prescription patterns in primary healthcare settings; however, there are limitations in inpatient and specialist outpatient settings due to the complexity of medicine use (WHO, How to Investigate Drug Use in Health Facilities?, 1993). Furthermore, data collection methods and the key areas within healthcare settings significantly influence how these indicators are utilised. Such as retrospective data collection can lead to overestimation of polypharmacy, antibiotic use, and injection use because patients without prescriptions are often excluded from primary health care settings. Conversely, prospective data collection can introduce observer bias, as health facility staff may alter prescribing behaviours when aware of being observed. This phenomenon is known as the Hawthorne effect, where individuals change their behaviour in response to being observed (Ofori-Asenso, 2016).

WHO drug prescribing indicators do not sufficiently capture the underlying reasons for prescribing practices. Hence, there is a need for qualitative methods, such as focus group discussions or in-depth interviews, to understand the reasons behind such prescriptions that do not conform to the prescribing indicators. Additionally, these indicators do not assess the suitability of prescribed medications for specific diagnoses, nor analyse inpatient prescribing patterns or the influencing factors surrounding these prescriptions. Moreover, numerous medications administered to special populations are utilised off-label, a circumstance that standard indicators may not adequately reflect, thereby complicating the assessment of their safety and efficacy.

The future of rational prescribing

Rational use of medicines ensures safe and effective use, ultimately improving public health outcomes. Moreover, some of the issues, like a lack of regulatory controls, economic challenges, and irrational prescribing practices, mainly affect the vulnerable population, such as children, pregnant women, and elderly patients. Several areas require exploration, including the use of prescribing indicators to implement evidence-based practices and fostering global collaborations to promote rational prescribing. Such measures can help to reduce the incidence of ADRs and promote optimal medication use. By continuously monitoring prescriptions and adhering to guideline-directed medical therapy, this approach can create a safe and effective environment tailored to the patient’s needs, while addressing challenges such as economic constraints and knowledge gaps.

Rational prescribing assessments depend on the continual evolution and adaptation of tools, such as implicit and explicit assessments. The future of rational prescribing, as guided by WHO prescribing indicators, can be redefined by refining data collection methods and incorporating qualitative approaches. These efforts are essential in evaluating drug use and improving prescribing practices, particularly in primary healthcare settings. However, there are limitations to admitted patient care, qualitative assessments, and regional adaptability, highlighting the need for complementary approaches to ensure the safe and effective use of medication.

Conclusion

The WHO prescribing indicators include measures of polypharmacy, antibiotic use, and injectable drugs, aiming to reduce medication errors, promote medication safety, and improve overall prescribing practices. These indicators also promote the economic and equitable use of medicines across various healthcare sectors by monitoring prescribers’ adherence to the use of generic medicines and compliance with the country's essential medicines list. By following these indicators, healthcare institutions can effectively assess prescribing practices and gain insight into prescribing patterns for sustainable, patient-centered healthcare services.
