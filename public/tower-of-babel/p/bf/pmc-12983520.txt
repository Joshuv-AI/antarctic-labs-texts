Butyrate-producing Eubacterium rectale inhibits gastric carcinogenesis and augments the efficacy of immunotherapy
Authors: Wei Gao, Jiani Xiong, Guangfeng Wu, Yuhuang Chen, Xiaoyan Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC), ranking as the fifth most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally, remains a formidable public health challenge [1]. Despite advancements in surgical resection and adjuvant therapies, the 5-year overall survival rate for advanced GC remains dismal (<10%), underscoring the urgent need for innovative therapeutic paradigms [2]. The emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly anti-PD-1/PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies (MAB), has revolutionized oncology by harnessing the host’s immune system to combat tumors. Notably, the phase III CheckMate 649 trial demonstrated that combining nivolumab with chemotherapy significantly improved overall survival and progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with HER2-negative advanced GC, establishing ICIs as a cornerstone of modern GC management [3]. However, clinical responses remain heterogeneous: only ~40% of patients achieve durable benefit, while the majority exhibit primary or acquired resistance [4, 5]. Unraveling the mechanisms underlying ICI resistance and identifying strategies to broaden therapeutic efficacy have thus become critical priorities in GC research.

In recent years, the gut microbiota has emerged as a pivotal “hidden player” in modulating anticancer immunity and ICI efficacy across multiple malignancies [6–10]. Accumulating evidence indicates that commensal bacteria can shape systemic and tumor microenvironment (TME)-specific immune responses by regulating T cell activation, dendritic cell maturation, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) function thereby influencing ICI sensitivity [6, 11, 12]. Dysbiosis, characterized by reduced microbial diversity and altered composition, has been consistently associated with poor ICI outcomes in melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and hepatocellular carcinoma [13–16]. In GC, pioneering studies have linked specific bacterial taxa (e.g., Fusobacterium nucleatum, Akkermansia muciniphila) and microbial metabolites (e.g., short-chain fatty acids [SCFAs]) to tumor progression and immune evasion [17–19]. However, the precise microbial drivers of ICI response/resistance in GC, as well as their mechanistic interplay with chemotherapy and immunotherapy, remain poorly characterized. This knowledge gap hinders the translation of microbiome-based strategies into clinical practice for GC patients.

Among SCFAs, butyrate produced by gut commensals such as Eubacterium rectale (E. rectale) and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii (F. prausnitzii), has garnered significant attention for its pleiotropic antitumor properties [20–23]. Butyrate acts as a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor, epigenetically modulating gene expression in immune and cancer cells, and serves as a primary energy source for colonocytes, reinforcing gut barrier integrity [24, 25]. Preclinical studies have shown that butyrate enhances CD8+ T cell infiltration, induces MDSCs function, and downregulates PD-L1 expression in tumor cells, thereby sensitizing tumors to ICIs [26–28]. Despite these findings, whether E. rectale—a dominant butyrate-producing bacterium in the human gut and its metabolic product butyrate directly contribute to ICI efficacy in GC remain unexplored.

To address these questions, we conducted a prospective cohort study integrating clinical data, multi-omics profiling, and preclinical validation. Here, we report that E. rectale abundance is positively correlated with improved response to tislelizumab plus chemotherapy and prolonged progression-free survival (PFS) in GC patients. Mechanistically, E. rectale and exogenous butyrate inhibit tumor growth, promote apoptosis, reduce PD-L1 expression, and augment CD8+ T cell infiltration in murine GC models. Targeted metabolomics of gut, serum, and tumor revealed that SCFAs in the gut drive systemic immune modulation despite intercompartmental diffusion. Furthermore, E. rectale or butyrate administration reshaped gut microbiota networks toward deterministic assembly, enhancing ecological stability and functional synergy. Collectively, these findings identify E. rectale and its butyrate metabolite as key mediators of immunotherapy efficacy in GC, providing a rationale for developing microbiome-targeted adjuvants to overcome ICI resistance.

Methods

Collection of in house GC cohort

A total of 30 patients histologically confirmed with GC were recruited in this study at the Department of Abdominal Oncology, Fujian Cancer Hospital, from July 2020 to June 2023. Detailed clinical features of all patients were summarized in Supplementary Table 1. All patients were HER2-negative and had received chemotherapy in combination with PD-1 monoclonal antibody (MAB). The specific treatment regimen is as follows: 200 mg tislelizumab, intravenous injection once every three weeks. Oral administration of capecitabine at a dose of 1.0 g/m2 and oxaliplatin at a dose of 130 mg/m2. Every 21 days is a cycle. According to clinical guidelines, in order to reduce the risk of cumulative neurotoxicity and allergy to oxaliplatin, patients discontinued oxaliplatin after completing 6 cycles of chemotherapy and continued maintenance treatment with capecitabine monotherapy combined with MAB (Fig. 1a).

Treatment responses were assessed every two cycles according to enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan and the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) 1.1 standard (Supplementary Table 2).

Fecal sample collection

Baseline fecal samples were collected within seven days before the start of treatment. The second fecal samples were collected within three days of the fourth week after the start of the treatment. The third fecal samples was collected within three days after the end of the treatment. Fresh fecal specimens were immediately placed in sterilized containers and stored at a temperature of −80 °C within a 24-hour period, remaining preserved in this state until the subsequent DNA extraction process was initiated.

16S rRNA sequencing

Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted using the OMGEA Soil DNA Kit (M5635-02) (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The bacterial community was amplified by PCR using specific primers targeting the V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene. The forward primer was 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′), and the reverse primer was 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). To facilitate multiple sequencing, a sample-specific 7-bp barcode sequence was introduced at the 5′ end of the primers. The PCR amplification products were purified using Vazyme VAHTSTM DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) to remove primer dimers and other impurities. The purified amplification products were quantified using the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) to ensure consistent DNA concentrations for each sample. Using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in conjunction with the NovaSeq 6000 SP Reagent Kit (500 cycles), dual-end sequencing (2 × 250 bp) was conducted. This read length design ensures that the overlapping sequences in the V3–V4 region are ≥50 bp, meeting the quality standard of Q30 > 80%. The details of quality control and taxonomic annotation were available in Supplementary Text 1.

Rarefaction curve analysis was performed to assess whether the sequencing depth was sufficient to capture the microbial diversity within each sample (Supplementary Fig. S1). The curves were generated by progressively subsampling the sequence data and calculating two commonly used alpha diversity indices, the Shannon and Simpson indices. For all samples, both the Shannon and Simpson curves plateaued as sequencing depth increased, indicating that the observed microbial diversity had reached saturation and that the sequencing effort was adequate to represent the bacterial community diversity present. As part of our data preprocessing steps, we applied a prevalence filter to eliminate taxa likely representing sequencing errors or transient contaminants. This was achieved by computing the average relative abundance for each microbial taxon across the entire sample set. We then established a cutoff of 0.01% mean relative abundance. All taxa falling below this cutoff were excluded from further ecological and statistical analyses.

Mouse models and animal treatment

Specific pathogen free (SPF)-grade female BALB/c mice (aged six weeks) were purchased from Hangzhou Ziyuan Laboratory Animal Science and Technology Co., Ltd. After one week of adaptive feeding, 100 μL of cultured and counted mouse forestomach carcinoma (MFC) were injected subcutaneously into the right axilla (containing 1 × 106 cells). On the 5th day after inoculation, tumor formation was observed. On the 7th day, when the tumor volume grew to about 60–80 mm3, all mice were weighed and the tumor volume was measured. 25 mice with similar weight and tumor volume were selected, then they were randomly divided into 5 groups (5 mice in each group): E. rectale+MAB, E. rectale, Butyrate+MAB, MAB and Control group.

E. rectale+MAB group: From the first day to the 14th day, E. rectale was administered by gavage to each mouse at a dose of 200 μL (1 × 1010 CFU/mL; colony-forming units, CFU), once a day. From the 7th day to the 21st day, PD-1 MAB was administered by intraperitoneal injection, 200 μg per mouse, once every three days.

E. rectale group: From the first day to the 14th day, E. rectale was administered by gavage to each mouse at a dose of 200 μL, once a day. From the 7th day to the 21st day, sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was administered by intraperitoneal injection, 200 μL per mouse, once every three days.

Butyrate+MAB group: From the first day to the 14th day, sodium butyrate (150 mg/kg) was administered by gavage once a day. From the 7th day to the 21st day, PD-1 MAB was administered by intraperitoneal injection, 200 μg per mouse, once every three days.

MAB group: From the first day to the 14th day, sterile PBS was administered by gavage to each mouse at a dose of 200 μL. From the 7th day to the 21st day, PD-1 MAB was administered by intraperitoneal injection, 200 μg per mouse, once every three days.

Control group: From the first day to the 14th day, sterile PBS was administered by gavage to each mouse at a dose of 200 μL. From the 7th day to the 21st day, sterile PBS was administered by intraperitoneal injection, 200 μL per mouse, once every three days.

Sample collection and processing

Tumor tissues, fecal samples, and colonic tissues were collected from mice as follows:

Tumor tissues: Tumor samples were divided into two portions. One portion was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for subsequent histological analyses, including hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, immunofluorescence (IF), and immunohistochemistry (IHC). The remaining portion was snap-frozen and stored at −80 °C for 16S rRNA gene sequencing and metabolomic analysis.

Fecal samples: Colonic contents (feces) were collected directly from the colon lumen. Samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for 16S rRNA gene sequencing and metabolomic analysis.

Colonic Tissues: Two segments of colonic tissue (approximately 1 cm in length each) were harvested. One segment was fixed in 4% PFA for histological evaluation via H&E staining and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay. The other segment was snap-frozen and archived at −80 °C for future applications.

Targeted metabolomics analysis

All of the 11 SCFA standards were obtained from ZZ Standards Co., LTD. (Shanghai, China). Methanol (Optima LC-MS), acetonitrile (Optima LC-MS), ammonium acetate, isopropanol (Optima LC-MS) were purchased from Thermo-Fisher Scientific (FairLawn, NJ, USA). Ultrapure water was purchased from Millipore (MA, USA). An ultra-high performance liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) system (Vanquish™ Flex UHPLC-TSQ Altis™, Thermo Scientific Corp., Germany) was used to quantitate SCFA in Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The mass spectrometer was operated in negative multiple reaction mode (MRM) mode. Parameters were as follows: IonSpray Voltage (−4500 V), Sheath Gas (35psi), Ion Source Temp (550°C), Auxiliary Gas (50psi), Collision Gas (55psi).

Western Blotting (WB) analysis

Tissue lysates were prepared by homogenizing samples in RIPA buffer (with protease inhibitors), followed by centrifugation to isolate soluble proteins. Protein concentration was determined via BCA assay. Equal amounts (20–60 μg) were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gels, transferred to NC membranes, and blocked with 5% BSA. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (4 °C, overnight) and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (RT, 1 h). Protein bands were visualized using ECL substrate and imaged with a chemiluminescence detector.

IF assays

Tissue sections were deparaffinized/rehydrated, permeabilized (0.3% Triton X-100), and subjected to antigen retrieval (microwave/heat). Endogenous peroxidase was blocked (3% H₂O₂, 15 min), followed by non-specific blocking (3% BSA/serum, 30 min). Primary antibodies (species-specific) were incubated overnight (4°C). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied (50 min, RT), followed by TSA-fluorophore reaction (1–15 min). Sequential labeling cycles (up to 4 rounds) included antibody elution (95°C/heat or specialized buffer) and repeated antibody/TSA steps with distinct fluorophores. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and slides were mounted with anti-fade medium. Images were acquired using spectral/fluorophore-specific filters.

IHC assays

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized (xylene) and rehydrated (graded ethanol). Heat-induced antigen retrieval (citrate buffer) preceded blocking of endogenous peroxidase (3% H₂O₂). Primary antibodies were incubated (60 min, RT), followed by polymer-linked secondary antibodies (15 min, RT). DAB developed signals (5–8 min), nuclei were counterstained (hematoxylin), and sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted for microscopy. Standard IHC workflow ensured robust antigen detection.

TUNEL assays

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized (xylene), rehydrated (graded ethanol), and digested with proteinase K (37 °C, 20 min). After permeabilization (20 min, RT), endogenous peroxidase was blocked (3% H₂O₂, 20 min). Sections were incubated with TdT reaction mix (1:5:50, TdT enzyme:Biotin-dUTP:Equilibration Buffer, 37 °C, 1 h) and streptavidin-HRP (37 °C, 30 min). DAB developed apoptotic nuclei (brown), counterstained with hematoxylin (blue). Slides were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Apoptotic cells were identified by brown nuclear staining.

Study approval

All experiments involving patients were approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Provincial Cancer Hospital (approval SQ2019-072–01). All patients signed written informed consent forms. All animal experiments were performed according to the ethical guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Fuzhou Cold-Spring Biology Co., LTD (approval IACUCBWS-PZ202507).

Statistical analysis

All analytical procedures were implemented in R software (version 4.2.1). For continuous variables, non-parametric comparisons were conducted using a two-tailed Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Differential abundance analysis of microbial taxa was performed using the Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) algorithm via the Galaxy-based web platform (http://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/galaxy/), with taxa selected based on a Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) score threshold of 2.0. Correlation patterns in SCFA abundance profiles across gut, serum, and tumor compartments were evaluated using Mantel tests. Beta-diversity analyses, including Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) and Nonmetric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS), were applied to Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrices generated from compositional data. Visualization of ordination results was accomplished with the ggplot2 package. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA), implemented through the vegan package, was used to assess group differences in community composition. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and significance was defined at p value < 0.05.

Results

E. rectale is associated with favorable response to combination therapy and prolonged survival

Thirty patients with GC were enrolled in this study and were divided into partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD) according to the RECIST 1.1 (Fig. 1a; Supplementary Table 3). To identify bacterial taxa that improve the efficacy of patients with GC receiving combination therapy, we analyzed the gut microbiota of this cohort. The results showed that 9, 5, and 8 enriched taxa were detected in PR, PD, and SD, respectively (linear discriminant analysis [LDA] >2; Fig. 1b and c). At the species level, we observed that E. rectale was overabundant in PR and the relative abundance of E. rectale in PR was significantly higher than that in PD and SD (Fig. 1d; Wilcoxon test, p < 0.01), suggesting an association between E. rectale abundance and treatment outcome. Immunohistochemical analysis showed that PD-L1 expression was significantly higher in PR patients than in SD and PD patients (Fig. 1e; Wilcoxon test, p < 0.05). Additionally, we divided patients into three groups according to the progression-free survival (PFS), including the Long, Middle, and Short group (Fig. 1f). LEfSe analysis revealed that E. rectale was enriched in the Long group (LDA > 2; Fig. 1g). The relative abundance of E. rectale in the Long and Middle group was significantly higher that in Middle (Fig. 1h; Wilcoxon test, p < 0.05). Grouping patients based on the median PFS also confirmed this result (Supplementary Fig. S2). In summary, these results suggest that E. rectale is associated with prolonged PFS and enhanced responsiveness to combination therapy.

E. rectale and its derived butyrate exert anti-tumor effects by modulating the immune microenvironment

Previous study has confirmed the association of E. rectale and its butyrate metabolite with anti-tumor immunity [29]. Thus, we speculate that E. rectale could potentially enhance the efficacy by affecting ICIs in GC. To address this, we conducted GC tumor-bearing BALB/c mouse models. Twenty-five mice were divided into five groups: MAB (n = 5), E. rectale+MAB (n = 5), E. rectale (n = 5), Butyrate+MAB (n = 5), and control (n = 5) (Fig. 2a). As shown in Fig. 2b, administration of E. rectale and butyrate profoundly enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-1 MAB, while the anti-tumor effect of anti-PD1 MAB alone was limited (Fig. 2b). We performed H&E staining on the colon tissue of mice, and the results showed that the U-shaped crypts of the colon in the Control, MAB, and E. rectale group were deformed and incomplete. However, after intervention with butyrate or bacteria combined with MAB, the colon damage was relieved and the U-shaped crypts were intact (Fig. 2c). Additionally, H&E staining of tumor tissues of five groups also confirmed the anti-tumor effect of E. rectale and butyrate (Fig. 2c). Using the TUNEL staining, we observed more TUNEL-positive cells in the Butyrate+MAB and E. rectale+MAB group, suggesting active role of E. rectale and butyrate in cell apoptosis.

Moreover, we detected significantly depleted expression level of PD-L1 in Butyrate+MAB and E. rectale+MAB group, while MAB and control exhibited the highest PD-L1 expression (Fig. 2d and e; Wilcoxon test, p < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. S3 and S4; Supplementary Table 4). To further investigate the effects of E. rectale and butyrate on the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), we used multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) techniques to detect various markers on the surface of T cells (Fig. 2f). The results showed that IFN-γ positive signals showed stronger fluorescence expression in MAB, E. rectale+MAB and Butyrate+MAB, suggesting enhanced cytokine secretion associated with immune activation. Besides, the fluorescence labeling brightness of CD8 and CD3 in these three groups was markedly higher than that of the control group, indicating an increase in cytotoxic T cells and total T cell infiltration levels. Consequently, these results demonstrate that E. rectale and butyrate can augment the efficacy of anti-PD-1 MAB via modulation of the TIME.

SCFAs in the gut, rather than serum or tumor tissues, exert anti-tumor effects

Next, we sought to explore the location where butyrate exerts anti-tumor effects after gastric lavage. We conducted metabolomics analysis targeting multiple SCFAs in the gut, serum, and tumors (Fig. 3a). Significant difference in the abundance profile of SCFAs among gut, serum, and tumors was observed (Fig. 3b; PERMANOVA test, p = 0.001). The abundance of SCFAs was highest in the gut, followed by tumors, and lowest in serum (Fig. 3c) and butyrate also exhibited this trend (Fig. 3d; Wilcoxon test, p < 0.0001). Subsequently, we used Mantel test to elucidate the correlation of these SCFAs among the three compartments. The results showed significant correlation between gut and serum, and serum and tumor (Fig. 3e; p < 0.05). However, we did not detect significant correlation between gut and tumors (p = 0.698).

Subsequently, we dissected the SCFAs spectrum of five groups in the three compartments. In gut, we observed significant difference in SCAFs abundance between the E. rectale+MAB and MAB group (Fig. 3f and g; PERMANOVA test, p = 0.007). However, we did not detected significant differences in the abundance of these SCFAs across the five groups in either gut or tumors (Fig. 3h and i). Therefore, combined with the high abundance of SCFAs in the gut, we speculate that it should be butyrate located in the gut, rather than in serum or tumor tissue, that exerts antitumor effects.

Gut microbiota is not the main source of intratumoral microbiota in gastric cancer

Furthermore, we performed 16S rRNA sequencing on fecal and tumor samples of the five groups of mice to dissect the gut microbiota and intratumoral microbiota profiles (Fig. 4a). First, we observed significant difference in microbiota structure between gut and tumors (Fig. 4b; PERMANOVA test, p = 0.001). The samples of gut were clustered into close cluster in principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) diagram, which indicated that the composition of gut microbiota were highly similar. However, the tumor samples showed a discrete distribution pattern, suggesting that there was significant inter-individual heterogeneity in the microbiota structure in the TME. Except the Shannon index (Fig. 4c), multiple microbial diversity indices were distinct between gut and tumors. Specifically, gut microbiota exhibited significantly higher Pielou’s and Simpson indices, while intratumoral microbiota harbored elevated Richness and beta diversity (Fig. 4d–g).

At the phylum level, Bacillota and Bacteroidota constituted the core microbial community in gut, while Bacillota and Pseudomonadota were the dominant taxa in tumors (Supplementary Fig. S5; Fig. 4h and i). At the genus level, unclassified_Muribaculaceae was the most abundant genus in gut, while Pigmentiphaga and unclassified_Chloroplast were the dominant taxa in tumors. These results suggest profound discrepancy in microbiota structure between gut and tumors. Subsequently, using sample-matched gut and tumor samples, we utilized the SourceTracker tool [30] to explore the proportion of the intratumoral microbiota derived from the gut microbiota, using the gut as a source and the tumor as a sink. The results suggest that the proportion of direct contribution from gut microbiota was low (average proportion < 9%). Additionally, the alpha diversity of gut and intratumoral microbiota was not significantly correlated (Supplementary Fig. S6). Thus, we speculated that the composition of intratumoral microbiota was more derived from local microenvironment specific amplification or non-intestinal microbial colonization, rather than direct migration or systematic dissemination of gut microbiota.

Adaptive adjustments of microbial networks are facilitated by E. rectale and butyrate stress

To further elucidate the microbial network perturbation of the five groups, we constructed the microbial correlation network of gut for each group (Fig. 5a–e). The results showed that microbial networks of the E. rectale+MAB, E. rectale, and Butyrate+MAB group exhibited higher modularity compared with the MAB and control group (Fig. 5a–f). This result indicates that the gut microbiota of E. rectale or butyrate treated mice formed clearer functional modules or ecological communities. Additionally, relatively lower clustering efficient, density, and average degree were detected in these three groups (Fig. 5f). The changes in these topological parameters collectively point to the structural transformation of the processed microbial community network from “high connectivity, high aggregation” to “modular, low redundancy”. Combined with the enhanced signal of CD8+ T cells in the previous immunofluorescence results, it is speculated that this reconstruction of the microbiota network may be coordinated by optimizing the spatial distribution and function of the immune microenvironment, the specific molecular mechanisms and long-term effects on host health are worth further exploration to provide a more favorable basis for microbiota-immune interaction for host immune activation.

Then, we compared the alpha diversity of gut microbiota and intratumoral microbiota across the five groups. The results showed similar alpha- and beta-diversity among the five groups either in gut or tumor (Supplementary Fig. S7–S9; Supplementary Table 5). However, in gut, we found that compared with other four groups, the Butyrate+MAB group exhibited relatively higher alpha diversity, particularly the Pielou’s and Simpson indices (Fig. 5g–j). Subsequently, LEfSe analysis showed that seven genera were overabundant in the Butyrate+MAB group (Fig. 5k). Notably, Prevotellaceae_UCG-001 and Lachnospiraceae_UCG-010 were reported to be beneficial bacteria. Besides, Acetatifactor and Monoglobus were known butyrate-producing bacteria. The expansion of these microbiota not only directly enhances intestinal barrier function (for example, butyrate acts as a SCFA to promote intestinal epithelial cell proliferation and tight junction protein expression), it also indirectly optimizes the host immune microenvironment through metabolite interactions and immunomodulation.

Enhanced deterministic assembly of gut microbiota in mice administered with E. rectale or butyrate

Next, we sought to investigate the community assembly pattern of mice administered with E. rectale or butyrate. First, we compared the community assembly between gut microbiota and intratumoral microbiota using the βNTI values. The results showed that the gut microbiota assembly of mice was dominated by deterministic processes (deterministic accounted for 80%), while the intratumoral microbiota showed a stronger random feature (stochastic accounted for 62%) (Fig. 6a; Wilcoxon test, p < 0.0001). Consistently, the pNST analysis also revealed significantly depleted proportion of stochastic processes in gut compared with tumor (Supplementary Fig. S10; Wilcoxon test, p < 0.0001). Furthermore, compared with the control group, we found that the deterministic process of gut microbiota was significantly enhanced after the intervention, and the deterministic process of intratumoral microbiota was also elevated (Fig. 6b and c; p < 0.05).

We further revealed the essential differences in assembly mechanisms between gut microbiota and intratumoral microbiota. According to the βNTI values, we divided the assembly process into heterogeneous selection (HoS), homogeneous selection (HeS), dispersal limitation (DL), and homogenizing dispersal (HD). Compared with gut microbiota, intratumoral microbiota exhibited elevated proportion of DL and HD, while enhanced HoS was observed in gut microbiota (Fig. 6d and e). We also observed increased proportion of HoS in mice treated with E. rectale or butyrate in gut and tumors (Fig. 6f and g). In summary, we have confirmed that treatment with E. rectale or butyrate can significantly enhance the deterministic process of gut microbiota and intratumoral microbiota, mainly manifested as the enhancement of HoS. Based on the low proportion of direct contribution from gut microbiota in intratumoral microbiota tracing analysis, we speculate that the enhanced local determinism of tumors may be due to intervention measures that indirectly screen for microbial subgroups that are more suitable for ecological niches by regulating the TME, forming a bidirectional regulatory model of “systemic homogenization of gut microbiota-local homogenization selection of intratumoral microbiota”.

Discussion

Our study combines prospective clinical observations showing an association between E. rectale and enhanced efficacy with preclinical mechanistic evidence from mouse models. By integrating clinical cohort analysis, multi-compartment metabolomics, and preclinical validation, we uncover a critical role for E. rectale-derived butyrate in modulating tumor immunity and overcoming immunotherapy resistance, with implications for precision microbiome-based interventions in GC.

ICIs have transformed oncology, but their utility in GC is constrained by variable response rates [31, 32]. While prior studies have identified microbiome signatures associated with ICI outcomes in colorectal cancer, melanoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma [17, 29, 33, 34], the specific bacterial drivers of response in GC remain poorly defined. Our prospective cohort study addresses this gap by demonstrating that higher baseline abundance of E. rectale correlates strongly with improved PFS and objective response to tislelizumab-chemotherapy. This aligns with emerging evidence that suggests an association between butyrate-producing taxa and improved ICI outcomes. For example, one study suggested that E. rectale can enhance the efficacy of anti-PD1 immunotherapy in melanoma [29]. In addition, E. rectale treatment has been reported to reduce tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and the incidence of lymphoma in mice [20]. F. prausnitzii, another major butyrate generator, has been associated with improved antitumor activity of immunotherapy [35]. Notably, our work extends these observations to GC, emphasizing that E. rectale may serve as a clinically actionable biomarker for selecting patients likely to benefit from ICI-based regimens.

Mechanistically, we demonstrate in mouse models that both E. rectale and exogenous butyrate can enhance anti-PD-1 efficacy, mirroring some of the clinical associations observed in human responders, including reduced tumor growth, increased CD8+ T cell infiltration, and downregulated PD-L1 expression. These findings converge with preclinical studies highlighting butyrate’s dual roles in reshaping the TME and systemic immunity: as an HDAC inhibitor, butyrate epigenetically modulates gene expression in tumor cells to suppress PD-L1 [36], while also acting on immune cells—for instance, by promoting naive CD4+ T cell differentiation into Th1 effectors and enhancing CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity [37]. Intriguingly, our targeted metabolomics revealed that SCFAs exert tissue-specific antitumor effects despite intercompartmental diffusion, with gut-resident butyrate driving systemic immune modulation. This pattern of metabolite distribution leads us to hypothesize that the gut may serve as a major site for butyrate-mediated immunomodulation, where microbial metabolites prime immune cells (e.g., dendritic cells, T cells) that then traffic to distant tumors, a concept supported by studies showing that oral butyrate supplementation enhances anti-tumor immunity in murine models [38, 39].

Beyond direct metabolic effects, our observation that E. rectale or butyrate administration induces deterministic assembly of gut microbiota networks is noteworthy. Deterministic processes, driven by species interactions and environmental selection, contrast with stochastic assembly, which is often associated with dysbiosis [40, 41]. The shift toward deterministic networks following butyrate supplementation may reflect the restoration of a functionally cohesive microbial community, characterized by increased abundance of cross-feeding taxa (e.g., primary degraders and butyrate producers) and reduced opportunistic pathogens. This ecological stabilization could enhance the resilience of the gut barrier and sustain long-term production of beneficial metabolites, thereby maintaining immunotherapy sensitivity. Future longitudinal studies tracking microbiome dynamics during treatment will be critical to validate this hypothesis and explore whether microbiota network signatures predict sustained response.

Our findings open several avenues for clinical translation. First, if validated in larger, independent cohorts, E. rectale abundance could potentially inform clinical decision-making as a predictive biomarker to stratify GC patients for tislelizumab-chemotherapy, potentially avoiding ineffective treatment in non-responders. Second, E. rectale-based probiotics or butyrate supplementation (e.g., sodium butyrate, tributyrin) represent promising adjuvants to boost ICI efficacy, particularly in patients with low baseline E. rectale levels. Notably, butyrate delivery via engineered probiotics or targeted nanoparticles could circumvent limitations of oral administration (e.g., first-pass metabolism) and maximize local gut effects [42].

However, several questions remain. While we establish correlation between E. rectale and response, causal validation in humanized gnotobiotic mice colonized with patient-derived E. rectale strains would strengthen mechanistic proof. Additionally, the interplay between E. rectale, other butyrate producers, and the broader microbiome (e.g., SCFA-consuming taxa) requires deeper exploration, as synergistic or competitive interactions may modulate overall efficacy. Besides, we acknowledge that potential confounders, including prior lines of therapy, antibiotic or probiotic use, and dietary habits were not systematically controlled for in our analyses. The possible influence of these unmeasured variables on the outcomes should be considered when interpreting the results of this study. Finally, whether these findings extend to other GC subtypes or combination therapies warrants investigation.

In summary, our study provides converging clinical and preclinical evidence supporting a potential key role for E. rectale-derived butyrate in modulating immunotherapy efficacy in GC, bridging the gap between gut microbiota composition, metabolic activity, and antitumor immunity. By linking clinical outcomes to mechanistic insights into metabolite action and microbiome reprogramming, we provide a foundation for developing microbiome-targeted strategies to overcome ICI resistance. As the field moves toward personalized oncology, harnessing the gut microbiome may prove indispensable to maximizing the benefits of immunotherapy for GC patients worldwide.
