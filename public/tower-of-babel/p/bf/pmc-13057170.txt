A deep reinforcement learning framework for influence maximization problem on large-scale social networks
Authors: Fang Yang, Yifan Wang, Nina Shu, Xingkui Du, Xiangyi Teng, Jing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recently, the Influence Maximization (IM) problem has gained significant prominence in both industrial applications and academic research. The dissemination of information on social influence maximization has attracted significant attention from both industries and academia. Since the exponential expansion of network data and the increasing intricacy of application scenarios, traditional approximation algorithms face substantial challenges, including insufficient theoretical guarantees, suboptimal empirical performance, and prohibitive computational costs. Although several deep learning-based algorithms have emerged recently, they often exhibit limited generalizability when applied to heterogeneous large-scale networks. To tackle this issue, in this paper we introduce a Deep Reinforcement Learning framework, MaDGNN, to solve the Influence Maximization problem on large-scale social networks via graph neural network and reinforcement learning. Hence, our algorithm can be applied to different types of networks. Extensive empirical experiments in real-world scenarios demonstrate that our model surpass baseline methods by a significant margin across diverse datasets. Furthermore, the proposed algorithm trained on smaller graphs exhibits excellent scalability to larger graphs.

Introduction

Online Social Networks (OSNs) serve as highly efficient conduits for interpersonal communication and information dissemination. Furthermore, social networks serve as multifunctional platforms beyond mere communication and have huge economic value through practical applications such as recommendation systems1 and online marketing2. Driven by these economic and social imperatives, the IM problem has received extensive scholarly attention over the past two decades.

Technically speaking, Kempe et al.3 initially formalized the influence maximization (IM) problem as a combinatorial optimization (CO) problem, and illustrated the information diffusion process via introducing the independent cascade (IC) model4 and linear threshold (LT) model5. It has been proved that the IM problem is NP-hard and the influence diffusion is submodular and monotone under the IC and LT models. Based on this, a greedy algorithm was proposed to solve IM problem by selecting the node with the largest marginal gain in influence diffusion. For maximizing a monotone submodular function under budget constraint, the greedy approach can achieve (1 - 1/e) approximation ratio. Nevertheless, it is challenging to compute the influence spread of a seed set under both IC and LT model. The difficulty lies in estimating the influence propagation arises from the randomness inherent in probabilistic diffusion models. Afterwards, Borgs et al.6 initially proposed a novel Reverse Influence Sampling (RIS) technique to reduce the running time. The objective of this method is to generate solutions that are approximately accurate with a reduced number of RIS samples. However, it is evident that RIS still needs a considerable amount of computational overhead in practice for the generation of a satisfactory solution, especially in large-scale networks. Subsequently, a series of RIS-based algorithms have been proposed7–10, which are recognized as the state-of-the-art IM solving methods. Despite their theoretical merits, these algorithms still encounter significant scalability bottlenecks when applied to large-scale networks.

In parallel, the rapid advancement of deep learning and reinforcement learning (RL) has spurred alternative approaches to IM, which aim to approximate influence spread via learnable parametric functions. Several pioneering works have explored this direction. S2V-DQN11 combined structure2vec embeddings with Deep Q-Networks for general combinatorial optimization; however, it lacks explicit modeling of complex social diffusion dynamics. GCOMB12 introduced a hybrid approach using GCNs for node pruning followed by a greedy heuristic, which, while efficient, does not constitute a fully end-to-end learning paradigm. More specialized models like PIANO13 further refined the DRL framework, yet its generalization remains limited when transitioning from small-scale training subgraphs to large real-world networks. Additionally, frameworks such as ToupleGDD14 focus primarily on predicting influence spread through GNNs, which differs from the strategic sequential decision-making required for optimal seed selection. While these methods avoid time-consuming sampling, they often struggle with policy stability or fail to capture the high-order recursive nature of cascades.

To address the aforementioned limitations, this paper introduces MaDGNN, a novel Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) framework specifically engineered for the Influence Maximization (IM) problem on large-scale social networks. The core innovation of MaDGNN lies in its integrated ”Perception-Decision” optimization mechanism. On the perception level, we design a Cascade-aware Graph Neural Network (GNN) that, unlike conventional embedding methods (e.g., those in S2V-DQN or GCOMB), explicitly captures high-order topological features and cascade effects inherent in information diffusion. On the decision level, we are the first to introduce the Munchausen Reinforcement Learning15 mechanism into the IM domain. By incorporating entropy-regularized Munchausen terms, MaDGNN effectively mitigates the policy instability and over-estimation issues common in traditional DRL methods like PIANO, significantly enhancing the model’s exploration capabilities and convergence stability in complex social environments. Diffusion Model. MaDGNN can be easily adapted to different diffusion models by simply modifying the definition of its reward function. In this paper, we report the results on the IC model. In the IC model, we use a recommended setting that has been widely used in IM problems3,6,7, which is to set the edge weights to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1/N_{\textrm{in}}(v)$$\end{document} (in-degree setting). In order to better and fairly assess the performance of various methods, we record the seed sets obtained by them and then run 10,000 times in Monte Carlo simulations to estimate the expected spread of influence. Furthermore, all experiments are conducted independently for 10 times and we recorded the average value of the measured metrics.

Training Data. To train our model, we generated 20 random Erd os-Renyi (ER) graphs with node sizes ranging from 15-50. Specifically, we first randomly and uniformly sample the number of nodes from a range of 15 to 50. Then we generate an ER graph with an edge probability of 0.15. Finally, the number of graphs out of the generated ones for training is set as 15.

Baselines. We compare MaDGNN with two state-of-the-art reinforcement-learning IM methods, ToupleGDD14 and S2V-DQN14. In addition, we include two classical heuristic baselines–CELF-deepis18,18 and the IMM algorithm7–in our experiments. CELF-deepis extends the classic CELF++ algorithm18 by replacing its expensive Monte Carlo influence oracle with the DeepIS neural estimator18. Experimental results

Influence spread

In this section, we evaluate the performance of MaDGNN against four baselines–ToupleGDD, S2V-DQN, CELF-deepis and IMM–on six datasets (douban, soc-epinions, ia-email-EU, fb-pages-media, facebook-Amherst41 and Metabolic.edgelist). Table 2 reports each method’s influence spread and running time. For visual comparison, Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, and 10 plot the spread curves and runtime profiles across all datasets. Following the recommendation of14, we incorporate the initial embedding during training (rather than at test time) to further enhance performance.

As Figs. 1, 3 and 5 illustrate, MaDGNN consistently attains the highest spread on every dataset. IMM yields very stable influence gains–outperforming S2V-DQN and CELF-deepis in most scenarios–but remains clearly sub-optimal compared to MaDGNN. By contrast, ToupleGDD and CELF-deepis exhibit greater variability across datasets, and S2V-DQN delivers the poorest results overall. These observations underscore both the superior effectiveness and the robustness of MaDGNN.

Running time

Figures 2, 4, and 8 plot the corresponding running times for different models on the Douban, Soc-epinions, and ia-email-EU datasets. For a fair comparison, we measure the time each method requires to output the seed set given the budget. We observe that CELF-deepis, accelerated by DeepIS, achieves roughly two orders of magnitude speedup over the original Monte Carlo–based CELF++ implementation, yet remains slightly slower than MaDGNN. Among the other methods, S2V-DQN achieves the fastest runtime, followed by MaDGNN. IMM exhibits moderate efficiency–its overall running time is slower than MaDGNN and S2V-DQN but still slightly faster than CELF-deepis. Generalization ability

To better validate the generalization performance of our model, we first trained MaDGNN in the in-degree setting and then tested it under both the 0.1 and 0.5 propagation-probability regimes. Figures 6 and 9 report results for the 0.1 setting, while Figs. 7 and 10 correspond to the 0.5 setting.

Under the 0.5 setting, all five methods (MaDGNN, ToupleGDD, S2V-DQN, CELF-deepis and IMM) achieve very similar influence spreads, indicating that none gains a clear advantage when propagation probabilities are uniformly high. In contrast, in the 0.1 setting, ToupleGDD maintains a slight lead, with MaDGNN closely trailing and both substantially outperforming S2V-DQN, CELF-deepis and IMM.

Regarding runtime, the DeepIS-accelerated CELF-deepis remains marginally slower than the rest; by comparison, MaDGNN and S2V-DQN exhibit nearly identical, fastest execution times. IMM occupies an intermediate position–faster than CELF-deepis but slower than MaDGNN and S2V-DQN. These experiments confirm that MaDGNN not only generalizes robustly across propagation regimes but also delivers state-of-the-art influence spread with minimal runtime overhead.

Parameters sensitivity analysis

During training, we employ an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon$$\end{document}-greedy exploration strategy in which \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon$$\end{document} is decayed linearly from 1.0 to 0.05. Such schedules are well established in the deep Q-network literature: for example, Mnih et al. (2013) initially set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon$$\end{document}=1.0, reduced it to 0.1 during early training, and then continued its decay to 0.01 to balance exploration and exploitation and promote convergence in high-dimensional state spaces. Likewise, Tian et al. (2022) applied a linear \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon$$\end{document} decay from 1.0 to 0.05 in a deep RL framework for influence maximization, demonstrating that this scheme effectively drives sufficient exploration on large-scale graphs while preventing premature convergence to suboptimal policies. In the ToupleGDD framework (Chen et al., 2022), which integrates three GNN modules with a dual-DQN architecture, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon$$\end{document} was similarly decayed from 1.0 to 0.05; experiments on both synthetic and real social networks showed that this strategy maintained robust early exploration and rapid late-stage convergence, yielding performance on par with or superior to IMM and OPIM-C. More recently, Liu et al. (2023) introduced the DREIM model–combining GNNs and DQN–and again adopted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon : 1.0 \rightarrow 0.05$$\end{document}, validating on small synthetic and large real-world networks that this decay schedule substantially improves influence spread relative to traditional greedy or heuristic baselines. Guided by these empirical precedents, we fix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon$$\end{document}final at 0.05 to ensure minimal residual exploration in later training, thereby refining policy learning without inducing instability from excessive randomness. In this study, we investigate the key parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} in the Munchausen Q-learning algorithm. The parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} controls the weight of the Munchausen regularity term, which improves the update of the value function by increasing the log probability of the strategy. To evaluate the effect of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} on the performance of the algorithm, we conducted experiments on different values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} and recorded the performance of the algorithm on the datasets.

To prevent interference from other factors, the model is trained as before. Discussion

Our study introduces MaDGNN, a novel framework that addresses influence maximization by modeling it as a reinforcement learning problem. By integrating graph neural networks for effective node embedding with the innovative Munchausen DQN for parameter training, we are the first to apply Munchausen-DQN in influence maximization problem. Extensive experiments performed on diverse social network datasets demonstrate that MaDGNN not only outperforms baseline algorithms in performance but also exhibits robust generalization ability across various network with different scales and structures. These results underline the value of combining advanced deep reinforcement learning techniques with graph-based approaches to tackle complex combinatorial optimization challenges. Overall, our findings indicate that MaDGNN sets a promising new baseline for future research in network optimization, while also suggesting the combination of RL with network embedding could be applied to more application scenarios. In future work, we plan to make our proposed algorithm adaptable to different types of models and investigate the use of large language models to explore more complex types of network data19,20.

Adaptability to the linear threshold model

While the primary evaluations in this study utilize the Independent Cascade (IC) model, the MaDGNN architecture is inherently adaptable to the Linear Threshold (LT) model via a simple redefinition of its reward function. In the LT model, a node v is activated when the weighted sum of influences from its active neighbors exceeds a threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _v \sim U[0,1]$$\end{document}. Critical discussion and model constraints

While MaDGNN exhibits robust performance across various datasets and diffusion models, we acknowledge several limitations that provide avenues for future research.

Sensitivity to Entropy Temperature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document}: The performance of the Munchausen RL framework is contingent upon the entropy temperature parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} to balance policy stability and exploration. Our empirical observations indicate that the agent’s performance is sensitive to this parameter; a value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} that is too high may lead to over-smoothing of the action-value distribution, resulting in sub-optimal seed selection. Conversely, a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} that is too low may diminish the regularization benefits of the Munchausen effect, leading to slower convergence. While we found \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau \in [0.01, 0.1]$$\end{document} to be generally effective, adaptive tuning of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} based on graph density remains an open challenge.

Scalability Bottlenecks: Although our cascade-aware GNN captures high-order topological features efficiently, it faces scalability constraints when applied to ultra-large-scale networks. In this study, we evaluated datasets up to the scale of Douban and Soc-epinions (approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^5$$\end{document} nodes and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^6$$\end{document} edges). However, extending MaDGNN to networks of even larger magnitude (e.g., tens of millions of edges) presents significant practical challenges.

Firstly, the recursive message-passing mechanism in GNNs incurs prohibitive memory overhead during the aggregation phase on standard GPU hardware, as storing and processing high-order embeddings for ultra-large graphs often exceeds available VRAM. Secondly, from an RL perspective, the state-action space expands exponentially with the network size. As the number of candidate nodes increases, the exploration efficiency of the RL agent diminishes, making it increasingly difficult to achieve convergence within reasonable training cycles. Consequently, while MaDGNN maintains a superior Prediction Efficiency Ratio (PER) on current benchmarks, its direct application to ultra-large-scale environments would require additional strategies such as graph partitioning or hierarchical reinforcement learning, which are currently beyond the scope of this framework.

Methods

Problem definition

A social network is typically represented by a digraph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G = (V, E)$$\end{document}, where V denotes the node (user) set and E denotes the relationships between nodes. For an edge \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(u,v)\in E$$\end{document}, v is regarded as the inner neighbor of u, and u is regarded as the outer neighbor of v. In this paper, we use the IC model as the diffusion model, which is one of the most commonly used propagation models.

Definition 3.1 (IC model): Given a graph G and a set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S \subseteq V$$\end{document}, every node in the IC model has two states: active and inactive. The nodes in seed S are first activated by the IC model and then begin to propagate in discrete timestamp order according to the spreading rules. When a node u is activated at timestamp t, it has the ability to activate an inactive node v in its neighborhood with a success probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{uv}$$\end{document}. If v is successfully activated, then v becomes active at the next timestamp \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t+1$$\end{document} and cannot be activated or activate any other node in subsequent timestamps. The propagation continues until no new nodes can be activated. Given a seed set S, denote by I(S) the number of nodes activated at the end of the diffusion. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta (S)$$\end{document} be the expected number of nodes that can be activated by S. Then \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {E}[I(S)] = \delta (S)$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta (S)$$\end{document} is called the influence spread of S.

Definition 3.2 (Influence Maximization): Given a social network \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G=(V, E)$$\end{document}, integer k, and a diffusion model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varphi$$\end{document}. The aim of the IM problem is to select k nodes as the seed set S under the propagation model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varphi$$\end{document} such that the expected number of nodes that S can influence is maximized, i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta (S)$$\end{document} is maximized.

Define \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma (v; S) = \sigma \bigl (S \cup \{v\}\bigr ) - \sigma (S)$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma (\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the influence spread function, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S \cup \{v\}$$\end{document} represents adding node v to the current seed set S. The marginal gain obtained by adding node v to S is thus \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma (v; S)$$\end{document}. Assuming that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_t$$\end{document} is the currently partial solution set, the greedy algorithm will select the node that maximizes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma \bigl (v; S_t\bigr )$$\end{document} as the next seed. However, computing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma \bigl (v; S_t\bigr )$$\end{document} for every v and every partial solution set is #P-hard under the IC model or other probabilistic diffusion models. In this paper, we adopt a different approach to the IM problem. We consider the IM problem as a reinforcement learning (RL) task, aiming to find a strategy that selects k nodes to maximize the final influence spread. We formulate the marginal gain as the value function in RL, which is also hard to compute directly. Therefore, we utilize a deep reinforcement learning (DRL) framework to approximate this value function by a parameterized function.

Definition 3.3 (Learning-based IM Problem): This problem can be divided into two phases, the learning phase and the inference phase. During the learning phase, given a set of training networks \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G =\{G_1,G_2...G_i\}$$\end{document} and a diffusion model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varphi$$\end{document}, we train a set of parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document} such that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y = \tilde{\sigma }(v; S)$$\end{document} is continuously approximated by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma \bigl (v; S)$$\end{document}. During the inference phase, given a network G, an integer k, and the learned parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document} for approximating the marginal gain, we solve the IM problem in G for k budgets under the same propagation model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varphi$$\end{document}.

Our proposed algorithm

A. Framework of our model

We will show the framework of our algorithm in this section, which combines the node embedding module and DRL algorithm to solve the IM problem. Our framework is presented in Fig. 18. Given a set of training graph, we first use the GraphSAGE21 method to obtain the initial node embedding, since previous studies and experiments have shown that the vectors obtained through the initial embedding are more effective than randomly initialized embedding for subsequent DQN training. Then we use the GNNs with attention mechanism to learn node embeddings in all directions. After the node embeddings of GNNs, we use the obtained vectors to construct the parameterized function and use the RL framework to learn the parameters. Instead of using other deep Q-networks such as DQN or DDQN, we utilize MDQN to learn parameters to approximate the marginal gain , after which a greedy strategy is used to select seed nodes.Figure 18The framework of MaDGNN.

The framework of MaDGNN.

B. Node Embedding module

B.1 Initial Embedding method

Prior to applying the DQN model, we initialize node representations using GraphSAGE. GraphSAGE is chosen for its versatility and robustness, as it efficiently aggregates information from a node’s local neighborhood through sampling. In our case, GraphSAGE is more appropriate than existing methods such as DeepWalk22, node2vec23, and DeepInf24. This process yields enriched embeddings that capture both local and global structural features, providing a solid foundation for subsequent deep reinforcement learning.

In addition, it consists of three components: the state of the in-neighbors, the ability of the in-neighbors to influence, and the tendency to be influenced by the in-neighbors. They can be denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_v$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_u$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_u$$\end{document}. We use these three components to represent \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_v^k$$\end{document}.

It is assumed that the algorithm has already been trained and that the parameters are fixed; we will later describe how these parameters are learned. We also assume that the parameters of the aggregator function have been learned (denoted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{AGGREGATE}_k, \, k \in \{1, 2, \ldots , K\}$$\end{document}), which aggregates information about the neighbors from each node, along with a set of weight matrices \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{W}^k$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k \in \{1, 2, \ldots , K\}$$\end{document}, used to pass information between different layers of the model.

B.2 The GNNs module

After obtaining the initial node embeddings, we refine them with a multi-layer GNN that captures inter-node state dependencies, influence capacity, and susceptibility to influence. Our model comprises three coupled submodules: (1) State GNN, which models the activation state; (2) Source GNN, which quantifies a node’s influence capacity; and (3) Target GNN, which reflects its susceptibility to influence. The overall structure will be detailed next.

The State GNN models the activation state of each node during the cascade effect by aggregating signals from active in-neighbors. Specifically, we assign an attention coefficient to each in-neighbor, reflecting how strongly it influences the node’s activation. Unlike previous implementations that employ a standard ReLU activation, we replace it with an eLU function25 to better handle negative inputs and ensure smoother gradient flow. Formally, we calculate the attention coefficients using a softmax over eLU-based transformations of neighbor features and then update the node’s state representation by aggregating these weighted signals. Empirically, this modification stabilizes training and yields improved performance compared to ReLU-based approaches.

The Source GNN models a node’s capacity to influence others by aggregating signals from its out-neighbors. It computes dynamic influence weights via an attention-like mechanism, similar to the State GNN, but focuses on how effectively a node can propagate activation. As with our modifications to the State GNN, we replace the traditional ReLU activation with eLU to improve gradient flow and training stability. After assigning influence scores and normalizing them, the node’s source representation is updated through a multi-layer MLP. Empirical results indicate that the use of eLU in Source GNN yields more robust performance compared to ReLU-based approaches.

The Target GNN captures a node’s susceptibility to being influenced by its neighbors. It introduces a dynamic weight for each in-neighbor, reflecting how likely the node is to become activated. Consistent with our modifications in the State and Source GNNs, we replace the standard ReLU with an eLU activation to enhance training stability and effectively handle negative inputs. After calculating influence scores and normalizing them, the node’s target representation is updated through a multi-layer MLP. Empirical comparisons show that this eLU-based design offers smoother gradient updates and yields better performance than ReLU-based implementations.

Training Process of MaDGNN

We adopt Munchausen Reinforcement learning method to train our parameterized Q-function. The central observation of this method is that the Qvalue need not be the only bootstrap target:if the optimal deterministic policy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi ^*$$\end{document} were available, then the log policy for the optimal action is 0 and the suboptimal action is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-\infty$$\end{document},yielding a potent shaping signal that does not alter the true optimum. Since \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi ^*$$\end{document} is inaccessible, we instead use the agent’s current stochastic policy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi$$\end{document}, which we assume to be numerically stable. In practice, Munchausen Reinforcement learning simply augments the immediate reward \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r(s_t,u)$$\end{document} with a ”Munchausen term” \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha \ln \pi (a_t \mid s_t)$$\end{document} in the TD update, thereby bootstrapping the agent’s own estimates of action quality without changing the optimal control.

Theoretical analysis

Convergence.

Under the standard Q-learning framework, if the state-action space is finite, the learning rate schedule satisfies the Robbins–Monro conditions, and the exploration policy ensures sufficient visitation of all state-action pairs, then the Q-learning update defines a strict contraction mapping that converges to the optimal action-value function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q^*$$\end{document}29. Munchausen-DQN augments the DQN loss with a Munchausen reward term based on the current policy; this term does not violate the contraction property of the Bellman operator. Consequently, with experience replay and target-network stabilization, the algorithm still provably converges almost surely to a fixed point corresponding to an approximate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q$$\end{document} function.

Although deep function approximation introduces nonlinearity and bias, prior work has shown that under assumptions of “sufficient network width” or “adequate fitting capacity,” DQN-based methods can still achieve approximate convergence30,31. Therefore, we have reason to believe that MaDGNN also converges stably given adequate training and a balanced exploration–exploitation schedule.

Approximation Quality. Since the influence spread function under both IC and LT models is monotone and submodular, the classical greedy algorithm achieves a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1 - 1/e)$$\end{document}-approximation32. MaDGNN parameterizes and learns an estimate of the greedy marginal gain; if the approximation error is bounded by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon$$\end{document}, then the seed set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_{\textrm{MaDGNN}}$$\end{document} found by MaDGNN satisfies11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \sigma \bigl (S_{\textrm{MaDGNN}}\bigr ) \;\ge \; \Bigl (1 - \tfrac{1}{e} - \epsilon \Bigr )\,\sigma \bigl (S^*\bigr ). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Empirically, we observe \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Vert Q_\theta - Q^*\Vert _\infty \rightarrow 0$$\end{document} as training progresses, indicating that MaDGNN can approximate the optimal greedy solution within a controllable error margin.
