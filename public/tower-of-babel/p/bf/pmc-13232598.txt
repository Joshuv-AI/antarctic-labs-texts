Surgical resection of longitudinal vaginal septum improves fertility and reproductive capacity in mice
Authors: Abigail E Solomon, Yotam Ashkenazi, M Natalia Chaves Rivera, Sydney Bell, Chase A Burzynski, Harvey J Kliman, Aya Tal, Reshef Tal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLongitudinal vaginal septum is a fibrous tissue band that bisects the vaginal canal and is associated with poor breeding in mice, leading to considerable waste of research time and resources in affected colonies. We observed a very high incidence (34.2%) of longitudinal vaginal septum in one of our mixed backgrounds inbred mice colonies that suffered from poor breeding. We hypothesized that vaginal septum resection would lead to normalization of fertility and reproductive outcomes in affected mice. Female mice were evaluated for vaginal septum and divided into three groups: septum surgery, septum no surgery, and no septum control. Surgical septum excision was done under general anesthesia using microscissors. Breeding began 7 days following septum surgery, and reproductive outcomes were assessed. Histopathological analysis of septate and non-septate reproductive tracts revealed no other abnormalities apart from the vaginal septum. Septum surgery mice had increased copulatory plug rates (82 vs 46%, P = 0.036), pregnancy rates after 30 days (95.2 vs 36.3%, P = 0.0001), pregnancy rates after 75 days (100 vs 72.7%, P = 0.01), and delivery rates (100 vs 72.7%, P = 0.01), as well as shorter time to pregnancy (P < 0.01) compared to septum mice without surgery. There were no differences in litter size, viability, or pup weight between the groups. In conclusion, our findings indicate that the reproductive capacity of mice is adversely affected by the presence of a vaginal septum. Surgical resection of longitudinal vaginal septum can normalize reproductive outcomes in mice. This simple procedure can save substantial time and financial resources in laboratory mice research impacted by poor breeding.Lay summaryA vaginal septum is a birth defect consisting of a tissue band that crosses the vaginal canal, dividing it. Introduction

Vaginal septum is a congenital abnormality that is characterized by a band of fibrous connective tissue bisecting the vaginal canal (Ludwin et al. 2020). The septum may either be transverse, oblique, or longitudinal, depending on the orientation of the tissue (Ludwin et al. 2020). The septum may also be classified as partial or complete (Ludwin et al. 2020). Vaginal septum has been described in various mammalian species, including humans, horses, cats, dogs, and mice (Root et al. 1995, Nomura et al. 1997, Chang et al. 2013, Ludwin et al. 2020, Volkmann et al. 2021).

In humans, vaginal septum is a rare phenomenon, affecting approximately 1 in every 70,000 women (El Abbassi et al. 2023). Vaginal septum may either be asymptomatic or present with symptoms that could disrupt reproductive health, including painful intercourse (dyspareunia), retention of menstrual blood behind the septum (hematocolpos), difficulty using feminine hygiene products such as tampons, leucorrhea, and labor dystocia (Ludwin et al. 2020). Diagnosis of vaginal septum can be made by pelvic exam, ultrasound, or MRI (Akcaoglu & Atilgan 2025). Treatment of symptomatic women with septum is usually surgical with vaginal septum resection (Ludwin et al. 2020). Septum resection surgery typically leads to elimination of symptoms and optimization of reproductive outcomes in women (Ludwin et al. 2020, Akcaoglu & Atilgan 2025). While this procedure is commonly performed in women affected by vaginal septum, there has been no exploration of similar remedies for several other species impacted by vaginal septum to date.

Laboratory mice (Mus musculus) have been used for decades as model organisms for human health and disease research (Rydell-Tormanen & Johnson 2019). Their genetic sequence homology to humans, small size, and rapid reproductive turnover provide practical and financial advantages, making them one of the most widely used animal species for laboratory biomedical research (Rydell-Tormanen & Johnson 2019). The reported incidence of a longitudinal vaginal septum can vary widely across mice strains. In a study of mice in Jackson Laboratories in 1976, the incidence of longitudinal vaginal septum was reported to be highest (38.1%) in BALB/c strain, while it was only 4.0% in C57BL/6J mice (Cunliffe-Beamer & Feldman 1976). In another study, increased incidence of longitudinal vaginal septum was observed in C57BL6/J mice (11.3%), while much lower incidence was reported in C57BL/6N mice (1.0%) (Gearhart et al. 2004). Importantly, the presence of longitudinal vaginal septum in mice was previously shown to be associated with markedly decreased reproductive performance, including decreased fertility, copulatory plugs, and pregnancy rates (Chang et al. 2013). Having high rates of vaginal septum in a mouse colony may be very detrimental to biomedical research. It can lead to increased breeding times and/or infertility, with a negative impact on financial resources related to increased housing and maintenance costs of the breeding colony, as well as a delay in research project timelines. Moreover, poor reproductive performance in septate mice can lead to mice wastage and increase mice replacement. It can present unique challenges to reproductive sciences research where achievement of pregnancy is often an essential part of the scientific inquiry.

In our lab, when evaluating mice for copulatory plugs we noticed an unusually high incidence (34.2%) of longitudinal vaginal septum in our colony of mixed background (Swiss Webster/C57BL/6J), which was associated with poor breeding. Previous studies have suggested eliminating female mice with septum from an affected colony in an attempt to reduce transmission of this trait (Gearhart et al. 2004, Chang et al. 2013). While this approach may help to alleviate some financial and time detriments in the long term, it is associated with significant animal waste. Moreover, this strategy may not always be feasible when individual septate mice are of a highly desired transgenic lineage and/or phenotype. To reduce waste of time, financial, and animal resources in our mouse colony affected by high rates of vaginal septum, we sought to explore a novel approach of performing surgical septum resection in mice. We hypothesized that septum resection surgery would restore reproductive outcomes in affected mice, similar to its beneficial effects in women.

Materials and methods

Animals

Our study colony consisted of mice of mixed backgrounds (Swiss Webster, C57BL/6J) derived from parent lines received from Jackson Laboratories (USA). The mice were bred and maintained at the Yale School of Medicine Animal Facility under approved Yale University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) protocol (#2023-20496). Mice were kept in a facility with temperatures between 20 and 26°C and a 12 h light:12 h darkness cycle (07:00–19:00 h). Mice were assessed daily by Yale Animal Resources Center (YARC) technicians to ensure the availability of food, water, and clean bedding.

Evaluation for the presence of vaginal septa and experimental groups

Females in our colony were examined at age 6–7 weeks old for the presence of vaginal septa. For this assessment, the mouse tail was lifted, and the vaginal opening was inspected by applying gentle external pressure on the vaginal orifice with the aid of a glass Pasteur pipette. When present, a longitudinal vaginal septum was easily visualized as a band of tissue bisecting the vaginal orifice. All females within a particular litter were examined and divided into one of the following three experimental groups: septum surgery, septum no surgery, and no septum control.

Reproductive tract histopathology

To evaluate for other reproductive tract abnormalities in septate mice and characterize the histopathology of the septum, a reproductive tract dissection was performed. Septate mice were euthanized using isoflurane (100%), and an abdominal incision was made to expose the reproductive tract. The entire reproductive tract was excised en bloc including ovaries, uterine horns, cervix, and vaginal canal. The tissue was fixed using 4% PFA in PBS, processed, and embedded in paraffin. Tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Microscopic images of histological sections were acquired using the ECHO Revolve K(2) R3/R4 Microscope and processed with the accompanying ECHO Pro Application (Discover Echo Inc., USA).

Septum resection surgery

Female septate mice between 8 and 12 weeks of age were anesthetized using isoflurane. The mouse was secured to a nose cone in a prone position. Refresh P.M. Lubricant Eye Ointment was applied to prevent eye dryness. The mouse was positioned under a stereo microscope (3078 Binocular Zoom Stereo Microscope, ACCU-SCOPE) for clear visualization of the septum. Lidocaine ointment (5%) was then applied to the vagina. At the beginning of the surgical procedure, the septum was grasped using forceps, and an incision was made at the dorsal aspect of the septum using microscissors (Fig. 1). Further incisions were made along the septal planes dorsally and ventrally toward the cervix until the septum tissue was completely excised (Fig. 1). Occasionally, a small amount of bleeding was encountered during dissection and was wiped from the surgical field with a sterile gauze to maintain clear visualization.

(C) Excision of septum tissue continued along the septal planes. (D) Further incisions were made along the ventral aspect of the septum along the septal plane until reaching the cervix. (E) The vaginal orifice is shown following completion of septum excision, and (F) the excised septum tissue is shown.

Following septum resection, the mouse was injected subcutaneously with meloxicam (1 mg/kg body weight) immediately and again at 24 h postoperatively. The mouse was monitored postoperatively for a clean surgical site, normal activity, and well-being for a duration of 48 h. The postoperative recovery period was 7 days prior to mating. Photographs of vaginal septum resection surgery were captured using ACCU-SCOPE 3078 Binocular Zoom Stereo Microscope with mounted ACCU-SCOPE Excelis HD Camera (Accu-Scope, Inc., USA).

Vaginal cytology

Septate and non-septate mice were placed in a cage with male bedding and allowed to cycle for 2 weeks. Vaginal cytology was performed daily to assess estrous cyclicity as previously described (Tal et al. 2016). Briefly, 20 μL PBS was pipetted into the vaginal opening and aspirated back to obtain vaginal cells. Next, the cells were smeared onto a slide mounted with cover slips for visualization of the vaginal cells using a light microscope.

Breeding, copulatory plugs checks, and reproductive characterization

To evaluate the effect of vaginal septum surgery on breeding and reproductive performance, virgin female mice aged 8–12 weeks were divided into the following experimental groups: septum surgery (n = 21), septum no surgery (n = 22), and no septum control (n = 38). All females were mated with proven male breeder mice. Females were introduced to the cage of the male breeder at 16:00 h and examined every morning at 08:00 h for copulatory plugs for a total of 10 days. Females with plugs were isolated and housed individually. Copulatory plug rates were calculated as the number of mice with positive plugs divided by the total number of mice evaluated for plugs.

Upon breeding initiation, female mice weights were monitored every 2 days for a period of 75 days, or until all mice had delivered. Pregnancy was defined as an increase of >8 g from baseline, at which point females were separated to be housed individually and observed for delivery. Litter size and viability were recorded on the day of delivery, and neonatal weights were recorded from all live pups. Weights of live pups were also collected 7 and 21 days following delivery. Pregnancy rates were calculated as the number of pregnant mice divided by the number of mice bred. Delivery rates were calculated as the number of mice delivered divided by the total number of mice bred.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 Software (GraphPad Software, Inc., USA). An unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was used for comparison between two groups. A comparison of Kaplan–Meier curves of time to pregnancy and time to delivery was made using the Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. A one-tailed Z-test was used for the comparison of copulatory plug proportions. Two-tailed chi-square tests were used for comparison of pregnancy and delivery proportions. P-values < 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Gross anatomical and histopathological evaluation of the female mouse reproductive tract

Throughout the study duration, 34.2% (51 of 149) of virgin female mice born in our Swiss Webster/C57BL6/J mixed background mouse colony were found to have vaginal septum (Fig. 2A). All vaginal septa were of longitudinal orientation. To examine the histopathological characteristics of longitudinal vaginal septum and to assess for any associated anatomical abnormalities between septate and non-septate female mice, a comprehensive dissection of the entire reproductive tract was performed (Fig. 2B and C). Careful examination of the reproductive tract did not reveal any other gross macroscopic abnormalities in septate mice. Notably, the longitudinal septum extended caudally from the vaginal orifice to about two-thirds of the cervix cephalad (Fig. 2D and E). Histological analysis of the septal tissue showed that it consisted of fibrous connective tissue and muscle fibers encased in a mucosa of stratified squamous epithelium with occasional evidence of vascular infiltration (Fig. 2E). Histological evaluation of the ovaries, uterine horns, endocervix, ectocervix, and vagina revealed otherwise normal histology in septate mice, comparable to non-septate animals (Fig. 2E, F, G).

Vaginal cytology of septate and non-septate mice

To determine whether estrus cyclicity is different between septate and non-septate mice, female mice from both groups were housed in cages containing male bedding and allowed to cycle for a duration of two weeks. Cycle stage was assessed daily by vaginal cytology. Septate and non-septate mice did not show differences in the proportion of time spent in the following estrus cycle stages: estrus, metestrus, proestrus, and diestrus (Fig. 3A and B). In addition, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of their estrus cycle length, which was assessed throughout the duration of three full estrus cycles (Fig. 3C).

(C) Mean cycle length assessed across three consecutive estrus cycles in vaginal septum and no septum control mice. n = 5/group. Data are shown as mean ± SEM.

Septum resection surgery results in increased copulatory plug rates in septate females

To evaluate the effects of septum resection surgery on reproductive performance, virgin septate mice were divided into two groups. The first group underwent septum surgery, while the second group did not undergo surgery. A third group of littermate non-septate mice served as additional controls. One week following surgery, mice were housed with proven male breeders and checked for copulatory plugs every morning for 10 days or until the entire cohort was observed with a copulatory plug. The percentage of mice with copulatory plugs was significantly greater in septum surgery mice compared to septum no surgery mice (82 vs 46% respectively, P = 0.036) (Fig. 4A). There were no significant differences found in copulatory plug rates between the septum surgery and no septum control groups, or the septum no surgery and no septum control group (Fig. 4A). A Kaplan–Meier curve, reflecting the percentage of mice achieving copulatory plugs over a period of 10 days, is shown in Fig. 4B.

Effects of vaginal septum resection on copulatory plug rates. (A) Copulatory plug rates in septum surgery (n = 11), septum no surgery (n = 13), and no septum control (n = 35) groups during the 10-day follow-up period. (B) Kaplan–Meier survival curve showing percentage of mice obtaining copulatory plug over time. Data are shown as mean ± SEM. *P < 0.05.

Septum resection surgery results in increased pregnancy and delivery rates compared to intact septate females

To investigate the impact of septum resection surgery on mice fertility, septum surgery, septum no surgery, and no septum control mice underwent a breeding trial and were monitored for weights every 2 days following the start of mating. Pregnancy was defined as an 8 g increase in weight from baseline, at which point the pregnant mouse was separated and checked daily for delivery. The breeding trial lasted for a total of 75 days. After one month of breeding, the pregnancy rate in the septum surgery group (95.2%) was significantly greater than that of the septum no surgery mice (36.3%) (Fig. 5A, P < 0.0001). Pregnancy rates were also significantly increased in the no septum control group (63.2%) compared to the septum no surgery group (36.3%) at this timepoint (Fig. 5A, P = 0.045). In addition, the pregnancy rate was significantly increased in the septum surgery group (100%) compared to septum no surgery group (72.7%) at the end of the breeding trial period (75-day timepoint) (Fig. 5B, P < 0.01). Significantly increased pregnancy rates were also found in no septum control females (100%) compared to the septum no surgery group (72.7%) at this 75-day timepoint (Fig. 5B, P < 0.001). A Kaplan–Meier curve analyzing the time to pregnancy over the duration of the breeding trial revealed significantly shorter time to conception in the septate mice that underwent septum resection vs septate mice that did not undergo surgery (Fig. 5C, P < 0.01). Time to conception was also significantly shorter in the no septum control group as compared to the septum no surgery group (Fig. 5C, P < 0.01). Moreover, delivery rates in the septum surgery mice (100%) and in no septum control mice (100%) were significantly greater than the septum no surgery group (72.7%) at the conclusion of the breeding trial (Fig. 5D, P < 0.01 and P < 0.001, respectively). There were no significant differences in delivery rates found between septum surgery and no septum control groups (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, the Kaplan–Meier curve demonstrates the time from start of mating to delivery was significantly shorter for the septum resection group and the no septum control group when compared to septum no surgery mice (Fig. 5E, P < 0.01 and P < 0.01, respectively).

Litter size, viability, and neonatal weights following septum resection surgery

To evaluate the impact of septum surgery on litter size, pup viability, neonatal weights, and well-being, pregnant mice from the septum surgery, septum no surgery, and no septum control groups were monitored for delivery every day for the duration of the study. On the day of delivery, the litter size, number of live pups, litter viability (% live pups), and mean litter weight (day 0) were recorded. Pups were also weighed on days 7 and 21 of age. Three dams, one from each of the study groups, delivered pups that were all deceased upon delivery and were not weighed. No significant differences were found between the three study groups in terms of litter size, the number of live pups, or percentage of pup viability (Fig. 6A, B, C, and Supplementary Data File 1 (see section on Supplementary materials given at the end of the article)). There were also no significant differences between the three groups in mean litter weight on day 0 (Fig. 6D), or on neonatal days 7 and 21 (data not shown).

Discussion

In this study, we show for the first time that surgical resection of longitudinal vaginal septum normalizes reproductive capacity and outcomes in affected mice, leading to increased copulatory plug rates, pregnancy rates, and delivery rates, as well as significantly shorter time from mating to pregnancy and delivery rates as compared to septate mice without this intervention.

The incidence of longitudinal vaginal septum in our affected mouse colony of mixed background (Swiss Webster, C57BL/6J) was 34.2%, which is relatively high compared to other studies. Previous studies in C57BL/6J mice reported the incidence of longitudinal vaginal septum occurring at 4.0 and 11.3% (Cunliffe-Beamer & Feldman 1976, Gearhart et al. 2004). In BALB/C mice, the rates described were as high as 38.1 and 14.2% (Gearhart et al. 2004, Chang et al. 2013). Despite several reports on colonies affected by longitudinal vaginal septum, the genetic origin of this anatomical abnormality is yet to be determined. A study led by Shire in 1984 conducted inbred breeding schemes with BALB/C and C57BL/6J mice, revealing that vaginal septum may be inherited from either male or female parent, and there may be several genetic loci involved in the manifestation of this phenotype (Shire 1984). These findings were later confirmed by Chang et al. as attempts at breeding non-septate females in their affected subline did reduce the frequency of the trait, but did not eliminate it entirely (Chang et al. 2013). Both studies conclude that either parent may be responsible for the transmission of vaginal septum, as the trait may be of a polygenic recessive nature (Shire 1984, Chang et al. 2013). In our study, we similarly observed transmission of this phenotype from either male or female parent from the affected colony. Several studies have interrogated a genetic basis to vaginal septum in mice. One study investigated ADAMTS18, a secreted metalloproteinase with roles in development and degradation of extracellular matrix (ECM). While 41% of Adamts18−/− knockout females of mixed genetic background exhibited vaginal atresia, 6% exhibited vaginal septum resulting from reduced fusion of Müllerian ducts and reduced apoptosis in vaginal epithelial cells (Lin et al. 2021). The authors, however, alluded to a previous study that generated the same genetic knockout in a different mixed background which exhibited an incidence of 10% vaginal atresia and 36% vaginal septum (Ataca et al. 2016). These strain-specific discrepancies point to a modifier effect by other loci in the parental strain on septum penetrance, leading to variable expressivity of putative disease-causing genes. Other investigations focused specifically on vaginal closure resulting from failure of vaginal epithelial apoptosis at pubertal onset, an estrogen-driven process, rather than septum formation per se. Not surprisingly, some genes implicated in this process are pro-apoptotic (i.e. Bax, Bak) and anti-apoptotic (Bcl2), as well as their upstream regulators, including retinoic-acid receptor α (RARα) and β-catenin (Rodriguez et al. 1997, Lindsten et al. 2000, Ren et al. 2010, Ito et al. 2014, 2015, Li et al. 2015, Zheng et al. 2023).

Our findings support those of previous studies of longitudinal vaginal septum in mice, indicating that this congenital defect is associated with detrimental effects on breeding and fertility. Chang et al. revealed a significantly lower copulatory plug rate observed in mice with longitudinal vaginal septum (51%) as compared to non-septate littermates (72%), suggesting that a longitudinal vaginal septum may serve as a physical barrier for copulatory activity, resulting in negative reproductive outcomes in affected laboratory mice (Chang et al. 2013). We sought to confirm these observations, as well as investigate whether septum resection surgery could restore copulatory activity in affected mice. In our study, analysis of copulatory plug rates between the three groups similarly showed that septate mice had decreased copulatory plug rates compared to non-septate control mice. Importantly, a significant increase in plugs was detected in the septum surgery group as compared to the septum no surgery group, indicating normalization of copulatory activity in septate females that underwent septum resection surgery.

Previous studies reported significantly decreased pregnancy rates in mice with vaginal septum as compared to non-septate controls (Gearhart et al. 2004, Chang et al. 2013). Chang et al. reported pregnancy rates of 54% and 27% for non-septate and septate mice, respectively (Chang et al. 2013). Similarly, Gearhart et al. found pregnancy rates of 75% and 50% for non-septate and septate mice, respectively (Gearhart et al. 2004). In their study, it was noted that if a septum had ruptured during copulation, the affected female demonstrated reproductive capacity equivalent to a non-septate female, yielding an 86% pregnancy rate for females with post-breeding ruptured vaginal septum (Gearhart et al. 2004). This supports the notion that the septum acts as a physical barrier. In our study, septum resection surgery greatly improved the reproductive capacity of mice affected by a longitudinal vaginal septum. By removing the abnormal septal tissue, we observed significantly increased pregnancy rates and decreased time from mating to pregnancy in the septum surgery group compared to septate mice that did not undergo surgical intervention. In addition, septum surgery mice had significantly increased delivery rates and decreased time-to-delivery compared to septum no surgery mice. These findings establish septum resection surgery as an effective treatment for restoring the reproductive capacity of septate mice. Importantly, to exclude the possibility that deficits in reproductive capacity and fertility are related to hormonal and/or ovulatory factors, we evaluated estrus cyclicity in septate and non-septate mice and found no difference in their estrus cycle duration or phases.

While a longitudinal vaginal septum serves as a physical barrier to copulation, it may also impede parturition. Previous publications reported observations of labor dystocia among septate mice, although they did not describe any adverse outcomes conferred to pups delivered from septate mothers compared to non-septate littermates (Gearhart et al. 2004, Chang et al. 2013). In our study, we similarly found no significant differences between septate mice with intact septum, septate mice following septum resection or non-septate controls in terms of litter outcomes, including total litter size, live litter size, percentage of live pups, or pup weights.

It is firmly established that the mammalian vagina arises during prenatal development from two distinct organ rudiments: the endoderm-derived urogenital sinus, which gives rise to the caudal two-fifths of the vagina (as well as the urethra and bladder), and the mesoderm-derived Müllerian ducts, which give rise to the cranial portion of the vagina (as well as the oviducts, uterine horns, and cervix). The Müllerian duct epithelium also gives rise to the adult vaginal epithelium that responds to cyclic changes in the levels of estrogens and progestins (Boutin et al. 1992). A study in human utero-vaginal development reported that the upper two-thirds of vaginal epithelium originates from Müllerian epithelium, while the lower third develops from the urogenital sinus (Fritsch et al. 2013). In contrast, studies performed in mice reveal that the vaginal epithelium in its entirety is derived from Müllerian epithelium (Kurita 2010). Studies in humans have shown that vaginal septa may be associated with other Müllerian duct malformations such as didelphic uterus, bicornuate uterus, and septate uterus (Ludwin et al. 2020). However, a study conducted by Gearhart et al. found no coexisting reproductive anatomic abnormalities in mice affected by longitudinal vaginal septum (Gearhart et al. 2004). Our findings of the anatomic restriction of septal tissue to the caudal aspect of the vagina, together with absence of anatomical and histological abnormalities to Müllerian duct derivatives including the uterus, ecto- and endo-cervix, argue in favor of urogenital sinus derivation of the vaginal septum in these mice, and against a Müllerian-derived anomaly. A caveat to this assertion is that our study did not look at developmental parameters in these mice, such as embryonic Müllerian duct fusion. Nevertheless, our results corroborate the observations that developmental defects in caudal regions of the urogenital tract often result in congenital anomalies such as an imperforate vagina (Schlafer & Foster 2016).

Although research on longitudinal vaginal septum in mice is limited, there have been several proposed methods for reducing the incidence of longitudinal vaginal septum to date. One strategy may involve implementation of a structured breeding scheme to reduce the frequency of vaginal septum. However, without knowing which genes are responsible and how they interact to give rise to the development of this anomaly, it is challenging to eliminate or reduce the frequency of the phenotype (Gearhart et al. 2004, Chang et al. 2013). A study conducted by Chang et al. attempted to decrease the frequency of vaginal septum in their colony through selective breeding, although they report genetic drift of sublines despite following a brother-sister mating scheme (Chang et al. 2013). Since structured breeding has proved unsuccessful in reducing the frequency of vaginal septum, several studies have recommended that affected females be culled from the breeding colony (Gearhart et al. 2004, Chang et al. 2013). By removing these septate females, the incidence of septum will be limited or extinguished entirely, which would relieve the burdens of time and finances that could arise from continued propagation of septate mice. While this is an effective approach, it may not always be a feasible option. If vaginal septum has a high occurrence within a particular colony, as is observed in our study and several others, removing affected females will result in significant animal waste. This conflicts with the 3Rs principle of animal research: replacement, refinement, and reduction of animal use (Russell & Burch 1960). In cases of high septum incidence in a colony, performing septum resection surgery presents several major advantages. First, it allows for the continued involvement of septate females in breeding colonies to avoid significant animal waste, consistent with the principle of ‘reduction’ in the 3Rs (Russell & Burch 1960). Second, sometimes it may not be possible to remove a particular animal from a breeding colony if it is of a highly desired transgenic lineage or phenotype. Moreover, in certain research fields, such as reproductive sciences, pregnancy can be an integral part or focus of the research investigation, and the presence of a vaginal septum can delay or skew reproductive outcome measurements. Thus, performing septum resection surgery can serve as a highly effective strategy for reducing animal waste, research time, and costs while optimizing the reproductive capacity of mice afflicted by longitudinal vaginal septum.

In conclusion, we have shown the feasibility of performing septum resection surgery in mice affected by longitudinal vaginal septum and that this surgery leads to normalization of their fertility and reproductive capacity. Septum surgery results in increased copulatory plug rates, decreased time to pregnancy and delivery, as well as increased pregnancy and delivery rates compared to septate mice that did not undergo this surgical intervention. Vaginal septum surgery is a quick and relatively easy procedure without complications that can be employed by investigators using mice as preclinical disease models when dealing with affected colonies as a strategy to save on breeding time, financial costs, while also reducing animal waste. Further studies investigating the genetic origins of vaginal septum may inform investigators to anticipate and potentially eliminate the inheritance of vaginal septum in an impacted colony.
