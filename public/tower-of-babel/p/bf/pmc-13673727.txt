Subcutaneous transplantation of lung organoids ameliorates vascular dementia via the IGFBP7-Ago2 mediated lung-brain axis
Authors: Qihang Luo, Jiajia Yang, Jiabao Huang, Nanhe Lin, Wenqing Liu, Ziyan Huang, Hongjiang Wang, Jiahui Yao, Jiayi Xiao, Haiyue Zhu, Xianshen Sha, Guangyao Liang, Yun Xie, Zhenwen Liang, Qiuhua Yu, Chuhuai Wang, Shengwei Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vascular dementia (VaD), whose leading cause is chronic cerebral hypoperfusion (CCH), currently has no approved effective disease-modifying therapies. The lung-brain axis mediates crosstalk between the lung and the nervous system, yet the specific mediators underlying its role in VaD remain unclear. This study demonstrated that subcutaneous transplantation of lung organoids (LO) ameliorated cognitive dysfunction in a mouse model of bilateral common carotid artery stenosis (BCAS). We showed that BCAS-induced CCH downregulated pulmonary expression of insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 7 (IGFBP7), a key mediator of the lung-brain axis. LO transplantation restored​circulating IGFBP7, which translocated to the brain and acted directly on microglia by binding to Argonaute2 (Ago2), thereby inhibiting pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, reducing neurovascular damage and neuroinflammation, and alleviating​peripheral lung injury caused by CCH. Notably, LO transplantation concurrently restores cerebral function and pulmonary homeostasis, with concurrent recovery of endogenous pulmonary IGFBP7 expression in BCAS mice, indicating concurrent improvement of both brain and lung pathology through lung-brain axis crosstalk. This study confirmed that LO transplantation represented an effective biotherapy for VaD and identified the IGFBP7-Ago2 axis as a core regulatory pathway of the lung-brain axis. These findings provide mechanistic insights into the pathogenesis of VaD and highlight that IGFBP7 is a potential therapeutic target for restoring inter-organ crosstalk in dementia.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12974-026-03957-2.

Introduction

Vascular dementia (VaD) is the second most common form of dementia worldwide, with chronic cerebral hypoperfusion (CCH) as its core pathological driver [1–3]. Despite its high prevalence and substantial socioeconomic burden, no disease-modifying therapy has yet been approved for VaD [4–6]. Almost all investigational strategies focus on mitigating damage within the central nervous system (CNS), yet this CNS-centric approach has failed to yield clinical benefits, highlighting the need to shift research toward systemic regulatory networks and identify novel translatable targets for VaD [7]. Neuroinflammation driven by excessive pro-inflammatory microglial activation acts as a central and persistent mediator of CCH-induced cognitive impairment [8, 9]. Aberrantly activated microglia trigger a cascade of amplified inflammatory damage, resulting in blood–brain barrier (BBB) disruption, white matter demyelination, synaptic loss, and neuronal death [10]. Although microglia represent a highly promising therapeutic target for VaD, decades of anti-inflammatory drug development have not achieved successful clinical translation [11, 12]. Major limitations include nonspecific systemic anti-inflammatory approaches associated with severe off-target risks due to lack of cell-type selectivity, and strategies restricted to local CNS intervention that overlook regulatory inputs from the periphery [10]. Traditionally, microglial activation was believed to be governed solely by the local central microenvironment with little influence from circulating factors [13]. However, emerging studies have challenged this view by demonstrating that secretory factors from peripheral organs directly shape microglial inflammatory phenotypes [14, 15]. Thus, this concept provides a novel research framework for VaD treatment by targeting the lung-brain axis, a key peripheral-central communication pathway.

Among these peripheral regulatory networks, the lung-brain axis serves as a critical bidirectional communication system that links pulmonary homeostasis to CNS function via neural, immune, and humoral routes [16, 17]. Most studies have focused on the well-established lung-to-brain pathological cascade, in which primary lung disorders such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease activate brain microglia, impair cerebral perfusion, and promote cognitive decline through chronic hypoxia, systemic inflammation, and lung-derived circulating factors [18]. By contrast, the reverse brain-to-lung pathway, in which primary cerebral hypoperfusion drives secondary pulmonary injury, remains severely understudied, especially in the context of CCH-induced VaD. Recent preclinical studies have confirmed that cerebral injury alone is sufficient to induce pulmonary inflammation, alveolar barrier dysfunction, and respiratory mechanical abnormalities [16, 17]. Neuroinflammation triggered by CCH promotes systemic release of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-1β, and HMGB1, which cross the damaged BBB to disrupt pulmonary barrier function and elicit local lung inflammation [19, 20]. The injured lung then amplifies systemic inflammatory signals that feed back to the brain, further exacerbating microglial activation and cognitive decline, forming a self-amplifying pathological brain-lung inflammatory cycle [17]. This cycle has long been overlooked in VaD research and represents a key knowledge gap addressed in the present study.

Here, we posit that the lung is not only a direct target of CCH-induced injury, but also a major source of circulating signals that sustain the pathological brain-lung inflammatory cycle, making it an ideal therapeutic target to break this vicious cycle. Organoids are three-dimensional stem cell-derived structures that faithfully recapitulate the cellular composition, secretory function, and physiological activity of native organs, with significantly stronger paracrine capacity than conventional two-dimensional cultures [21]. We focus on IGFBP7, a core secretory product of lung organoids and a key regulator of inflammation and vascular homeostasis [22]. Existing evidence demonstrates that pulmonary endothelial cells represent an important source of circulating IGFBP7, particularly under lung injury conditions [22]. Moreover, elevated IGFBP7 levels are independently associated with favorable outcomes in patients with acute ischemic stroke and cerebral small vessel disease [23]. On the other hand, Ago2, the catalytic core of the RNA-induced silencing complex, has been shown to maintain microglial quiescence and restrain excessive neuroinflammation through non-canonical protein–protein interactions [24]. However, whether lung-derived IGFBP7 serves as a protective mediator of the lung-brain axis via its interaction with Ago2 to regulate microglial activation has remained entirely unelucidated to date.

Based on the above evidence, we hypothesize that subcutaneous transplantation of lung organoids delivers IGFBP7, a key protective mediator of the lung-brain axis, which binds directly to Ago2 to suppress pro-inflammatory microglial activation, break the pathological brain-lung inflammatory cycle, and ameliorate pathology in both organs through lung-brain axis crosstalk, thereby alleviating CCH-induced cognitive impairment. To test this hypothesis, we used the classic BCAS mouse model of VaD to systematically evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of subcutaneous LO transplantation, dissect the underlying molecular mechanism centered on the IGFBP7-Ago2 axis, and characterize bidirectional lung-brain crosstalk under CCH [25, 26]. This study not only establishes a novel organoid-based biotherapeutic strategy for VaD, but also reveals, for the first time, a protective regulatory pathway in the lung-brain axis whereby lung-derived IGFBP7 targets Ago2 to repair brain injury, providing new mechanistic insights into the pathogenesis of VaD.

Methods

Animals and bilateral common carotid artery stenosis (BCAS) model

Male C57BL/6 mice aged 9–11 weeks (body weight 28–30 g) were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of Sun Yat-sen University. All animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility under controlled temperature (22 ± 2 ℃), relative humidity (50 ± 10%), and a 12 h light/dark cycle (lights on at 7:00 AM), with ad libitum access to sterilized food and water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-sen University (SYSU-IACUC-2025-B1921). All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering, maintain animal welfare, and reduce the number of animals used throughout the experiment.

To mimic CCH, the core pathological feature of VaD, we established the BCAS mouse model. After 7 days of adaptive feeding, mice were randomly divided into 3 groups in a blinded manner: sham operation group (Sham), BCAS model group (BCAS), and BCAS with Lung organoid transplantation group (LOG). For surgery, mice were anesthetized with 1–2% isoflurane in oxygen, and a 1 cm midline neck incision was made. Submandibular glands and sternocleidomastoid muscles were gently separated to expose bilateral common carotid arteries (CCAs). The right CCA was carefully isolated from the adjacent vagus nerve, and a microcoil (0.18 mm internal diameter, WUXI SAMINI SPRING Co., Ltd.) was placed around the artery. The same procedure was performed on the left CCA, followed by layered skin suture and disinfection with povidone-iodine. A thermostatic heating pad was used to maintain mouse core body temperature at 36.5 ± 0.5 ℃ during and 2 h after surgery. Meloxicam was administered for postoperative analgesia. For the Sham group, the same surgical procedure was performed without microcoil placement.

Cerebral blood flow (CBF) measurement

Three days after surgery, CBF was measured in randomly selected Sham and BCAS mice (n = 6 per group) using a two-dimensional laser speckle contrast imaging system (PeriCam PSI High Resolution with PIMSoft, Perimed, Sweden). Mice were anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane and placed prone on a heating pad. A midline scalp incision was made to fully expose the skull, which was cleaned with sterile saline to remove soft tissue debris. The imaging camera was positioned vertically 10 cm above the skull to collect CBF data from the bilateral parietal lobes. After data acquisition, the scalp was sutured and disinfected. Perfusion data were visualized as pseudocolor heatmaps for quantitative regional analysis.

Lung organoid (LO) preparation and subcutaneous transplantation

Primary lung cells were isolated from the lung tissue of neonatal C57BL/6 mice and resuspended in growth factor-reduced Matrigel (Corning, USA) at a density of 2.0 × 107 cells/mL. Notably, our high-density seeding protocol accelerates organoid maturation to 7 days compared with conventional 10–21 day protocols, while retaining key pulmonary lineages and paracrine function. Twenty microliters of cell-loaded Matrigel droplets (organoid precursors) were plated in each well of a 24-well plate, and 200 μL of complete LO culture medium was added after Matrigel solidification. The complete medium consisted of DMEM/F12 supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin–streptomycin, 100 ng/mL noggin (MedChemExpress, MCE), 100 ng/mL R-spondin 1 (MCE), 200 ng/mL fibroblast growth factor 4 (FGF4, MCE), 10 ng/mL basic FGF (FGF-basic, MCE), and 5 μM Y-27632 (Abmole). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator, and the medium was changed every 3 days.

After 7 days of culture, mature LOs were harvested and resuspended in 100 μL of fresh serum-free DMEM/F12 medium containing approximately 200–250 organoids, supplemented with recombinant VEGF164 protein (HY-P7312, MCE)​ to promote graft vascularization. The mixture was subcutaneously injected into the left hindlimb of mice 3 days after surgery. Mice in the Sham and BCAS groups received an equal volume of serum-free medium containing the same concentration of VEGF164 as a vehicle control.

Successful engraftment of lung organoids was confirmed by manual palpation. A firm, movable subcutaneous nodule (3–5 mm in diameter) became palpable at the injection site by Day 10 and persisted throughout the experimental period. Mice without a palpable nodule by Day 10 were excluded from further analysis. At the endpoint (Day 39), gross examination upon dissection confirmed the presence of a vascularized nodule, which was subsequently processed for histological analysis. The general status, body weight, and wound healing of mice were monitored daily after administration.

Experimental design and blinding

Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a computer-generated random number table. All behavioral tests, histological analyses, and data quantifications were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation. Group codes were revealed only after data collection was complete.

Pre-established exclusion criteria included: (1) death during or after surgery; (2) failure to develop a palpable subcutaneous nodule by Day 7 in the LOG group; (3) severe postoperative complications (e.g., wound infection, bleeding); and (4) extreme outliers (> 3 standard deviations from the group mean) in behavioral tests. A total of 8 mice were excluded from the final analysis. Specifically, 4 mice died during or shortly after surgery, 2 mice in the LOG group failed to develop a palpable nodule by Day 10, and 2 mice were identified as outliers in behavioral tests.

Sex as a biological variable: Only male C57BL/6 mice were used in this study to avoid potential confounding effects of estrous cycle fluctuations on cognitive and behavioral outcomes. Future studies incorporating both sexes are warranted to evaluate the generalizability of our findings.

Neurological behavioral function tests

All behavioral tests were performed during the light phase (between 10:00 AM and 5:00 PM) to minimize intra-day variability. One hour before each test, mice were transferred to the testing room to acclimate to the environment and eliminate transfer-induced stress. Between tests and individual animals, the equipment was thoroughly cleaned with 75% ethanol to eliminate odor residues. All behavioral data were recorded and analyzed using the TopScan video tracking system (Clever Sys., Inc., USA). Mice were housed individually 1 day before the first test to acclimate to single housing. All tests were performed by investigators blinded to group assignments, in the order of least to most stressful: nesting test → open field test → Y maze test → novel object recognition test → Morris water maze.

Nesting test

At 5:00 PM on the test day, 5 g of sterile cotton nesting material was placed in a fixed corner of the home cage. At 10:00 AM the next day, mice were gently removed from the cage, and photos of the nest were taken from a fixed vertical position. Nesting quality was scored in a blinded manner using a validated 5-point scale: 0: Nesting material was completely untouched and unshredded, with no nest formation; 1: Material was slightly shredded but not piled, with no defined nest shape; 2: Material was largely shredded, with partial piling but no closed/semi-closed structure; 3: A clear semi-closed nest was formed, which could accommodate most of the mouse body but had thin, incomplete walls; 4: A complete closed/semi-closed nest was formed, with thick, compact walls and a defined cavity.

Open field test (OFT)

Spontaneous locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior were assessed using a 40 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm white acrylic open field box. Mice were gently placed in the center of the box and allowed to explore freely for 10 min. Total distance traveled, average speed, and time spent in the central (20 cm × 20 cm) and peripheral areas were recorded.

Novel object recognition test (NOR)

The test was performed in the same 40 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm open field box, using two pairs of objects with distinct colors and shapes, with no inherent preference for mice. The test consisted of a training phase and a test phase, with a 24 h inter-trial interval. During the training phase, two identical objects (Object A) were fixed in two opposite corners of the box, 5 cm away from the walls. Mice were placed in the center of the box with their back to the objects, and allowed to explore freely for 10 min. During the test phase, one familiar object (A) was replaced with a novel object (B), and mice were allowed to explore for another 10 min. Activity within a 2 cm radius around the object, with the nose pointing toward the object, was defined as exploratory behavior. The novel object preference index was calculated as: (exploration time for novel object/total exploration time for both objects) × 100%.

Y maze test (YMZ)

Spatial working memory was assessed using a Y-maze apparatus consisting of three identical white acrylic arms (30 cm × 6 cm × 15 cm each) and a triangular central connecting area, with a 120° angle between each arm. Mice were placed at the end of one fixed start arm and allowed to explore freely for 10 min. Entry into an arm was defined as all four limbs fully entering the arm. The sequence of arm entries was recorded to calculate the spontaneous alternation rate:Spontaneous alternation (%) = (Number of correct alternations/(Total arm entries—2)) × 100%. Where correct alternations are defined as consecutive entries into all three different arms without repetition.

Morris water maze (MWM)

Spatial learning and reference memory were assessed using the MWM test. The test was performed in a 120 cm diameter circular stainless steel tank filled with water (23 ± 1℃) opacified with food-grade titanium dioxide. A 10 cm diameter transparent acrylic platform was placed 1.5 cm below the water surface in the target quadrant, with fixed visual cues around the tank throughout the test. First, a 5-day hidden platform training was performed, with 4 trials per day and a 30 min inter-trial interval. For each trial, mice were placed into the water facing the tank wall from one of four pseudo-randomized start quadrants, with a maximum trial duration of 60 s. If the mouse found the platform within 60 s, it was allowed to stay on the platform for 15 s; if not, it was gently guided to the platform and allowed to stay for 15 s. On day 6, the platform was removed for the probe trial. Mice were placed into the water from the quadrant farthest from the original platform position and allowed to swim freely for 60 s. Average swimming speed, swimming trajectory, time spent in the target quadrant, and number of crossings over the original platform position were recorded.

Sample collection and quantitative proteomic analysis

At the end of the experiment, mice were deeply anesthetized with 3% isoflurane. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was collected via cisterna magna puncture using a glass capillary. CSF samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4℃ to remove cell debris, and the supernatant was collected. Blood was collected via orbital venous puncture, incubated at room temperature for 30 min for coagulation, and serum was isolated by centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 15 min at 4℃. All samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80℃ until analysis. Due to the limited volume of CSF obtainable from individual mice, samples from multiple animals were pooled for proteomic analysis: CSF from 8 BCAS mice was pooled into one sample, and CSF from 9 LOG mice was pooled into one sample. Serum samples were analyzed individually (n = 3 per group). Due to the limited CSF volume, CSF proteomic analysis was performed as an exploratory screening approach, and the results were subsequently validated by ELISA in serum samples.Quantitative proteomic analysis of serum and CSF samples was performed by LC-Bio Technologies Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China) using an Orbitrap Astral data-independent acquisition (DIA) proteomic platform. Briefly, low-abundance proteins in 50 μL serum were enriched using Proteonano™ nanobeads. Enriched proteins were digested with trypsin via on-bead digestion, desalted with C18 tips, and vacuum-dried to obtain purified peptides. Peptides were reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid, diluted to 0.05 μg/μL, and spiked with iRT retention time standards.

Peptide separation was performed on a Vanquish Neo ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system coupled with an Orbitrap Astral Zoom mass spectrometer in DIA mode. Protein identification and quantification were processed using DIA-NN software (v1.9.2) in DirectDIA mode, with peptide-spectrum match (PSM) and protein-level false discovery rate (FDR) both controlled below 0.01. Bioinformatic analysis was performed using R software (v4.0). Protein intensities were normalized by median normalization. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were defined as proteins with P < 0.05 and fold change > 1.2. Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment, and protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analyses were conducted for DEPs. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed with significance thresholds of |normalized enrichment score (NES)|> 1, nominal P (NOM p) < 0.05, and FDR q < 0.25.

Histological and immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis

After deep anesthesia, mice were transcardially perfused with 20 mL ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by 30 mL ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for fixation. Brain and lung tissues were harvested, post-fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h at 4℃, dehydrated in gradient ethanol, cleared with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Serial 5 μm thick sections were prepared for subsequent staining.

For IHC staining, sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated in gradient ethanol, and subjected to antigen retrieval in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95℃ for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H₂O₂ for 10 min at room temperature. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min, sections were incubated with primary antibodies against TNF-α (1:200, GB153968, Servicebio) and IL-1β (1:200, ab283818, Abcam) overnight at 4℃. After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were developed using 3,3'-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. The percentage of positive staining area was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA) in a blinded manner.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining and quantitative analysis

For brain IF staining, mice were transcardially perfused with PBS and 4% PFA, and brain tissues were post-fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h, then dehydrated in 30% sucrose solution for 48 h at 4℃. 30 μm thick coronal brain sections were prepared using a Leica CM1950 cryostat. Sections were blocked and permeabilized with blocking buffer (5% BSA + 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer overnight at 4℃.

The primary antibodies used were as follows: Rabbit monoclonal anti-NeuN (1:500, R381075; Zenbio), Rabbit monoclonal anti-MBP (1:1000, R25054; Zenbio),Guinea pig anti-Iba-1 (1:500, #HA601368; HUABIO), Rabbit anti-GFAP (1:1000, #HA601483; HUABIO), Rabbit anti-PSD95 (1:500, #ET1602-20; HUABIO), Rat anti-VGluT1 (1:500, #HA601392; HUABIO), Rabbit anti-CD68 (1:500, #HA722737; HUABIO), Mouse anti-CD31 (1:800, GB15063; Servicebio), Rabbit anti-ZO-1 (1:300, sc-33725; Santa Cruz), Rabbit anti-IGFBP7 (1:200, 83292-5-RR; Proteintech), Rabbit anti-Claudin 5 (1:200, GB11290; Servicebio), Mouse anti-Ago2 (1:300, 67934-1-Ig; Proteintech). After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with corresponding species-specific fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500, Alexa Fluor 488/594, Invitrogen) diluted in blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 5 min.

For lung tissue multiplex IF staining of CD31, E-cadherin, Collagen I, and IGFBP7, the tyramide signal amplification (TSA) method was used with a TSA Fluorescent Staining Kit (Seville, Cat. No. G1255-50T), strictly following the manufacturer’s protocol with minor modifications. Briefly, 5 μm paraffin-embedded lung sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H₂O₂ for 10 min at room temperature, followed by blocking with 5% BSA for 30 min. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD31 (1:1000, GB15063; Servicebio), E-cadherin (1:1000, GB12083; Servicebio), Collagen I (1:1000, GB11022-3; Servicebio), and IGFBP7 (1:200, 83292-5-RR; Proteintech), respectively. After three washes with PBS, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 50 min at room temperature. TSA signal amplification was conducted by incubating sections with tyramide working solution (prepared according to the kit instructions) for 10 min. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min.

Images were captured using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal laser scanning microscope and Leica DM6B upright fluorescence microscope. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of target proteins was quantified using ImageJ software, and 3D reconstruction was performed using Imaris software. For quantitative analysis, images were acquired and analyzed by an investigator blinded to the experimental groups. For each region of interest, 3–5 non-overlapping fields were randomly selected from 3 serial sections per animal.

Microglial morphological analysis and CD68 immunoreactivity scoring

Microglial morphological analysis and CD68 immunoreactivity scoring were performed following established protocols [27, 28]. Confocal images of target brain regions (hippocampal CA1; medial prefrontal cortex, mPFC) were acquired using the Zeiss LSM 880 with a sequential scanning mode to avoid channel crosstalk. For each region, 3–5 non-overlapping fields were randomly selected from 3 serial sections per animal. Images were captured using a 40 × objective with constant laser intensity across all channels, at a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels and a z-step of 1 μm. Blinded quantitative analysis was conducted by an investigator naive to the experimental group allocation via ImageJ software (NIH, USA). For each animal, 20 randomly selected Iba1-positive microglia with visible cell bodies were analyzed for three core morphological parameters. CD68, a lysosomal/phagocytic activity marker, was semi-quantified using a validated 0–3 scoring system following published criteria [28, 29], where: 0 = no or minimal CD68 staining signal in the Iba1 + cell soma; 1 = punctate, dispersed CD68-positive signals; 2 = CD68 staining covering 1/3 to 2/3 of the total Iba1 + cell soma area; 3 = CD68 staining covering more than 2/3 of the total Iba1 + cell soma area. All data are presented as mean ± SEM, with n = 5 mice per group.

Cell culture and oxygen–glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) model

Mouse hippocampal neuronal HT22 cell line and mouse microglial BV2 cell line were purchased from Procell Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Both cell lines were authenticated via STR profiling, confirmed to be mycoplasma-free, and maintained in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, in a 37 °C humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

To establish the OGD/R model, cells at 70–80% confluence were washed twice with glucose-free DMEM. The culture medium was then replaced with glucose-free DMEM, and cells were transferred to a hypoxia chamber filled with 95% N₂ and 5% CO₂ (oxygen concentration < 0.1%) for 3 h (BV2 cells) or 6 h (HT22 cells) to induce OGD. After the hypoxia phase, the glucose-free medium was replaced with complete growth medium, and cells were returned to the normoxic 5% CO₂ incubator for 8 h (BV2 cells) or 24 h (HT22 cells) for reoxygenation.

Cell intervention and conditioned medium (CM) preparation

For co-culture experiments, a 0.4 μm pore size Transwell system was used. BV2 cells after OGD were seeded in the lower chamber, and mature LOs were placed in the upper chamber, and co-cultured for 8 h in complete medium. For drug intervention, recombinant mouse IGFBP7 protein (MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-P70858) was used at 50 ng/mL. Because the protein carries a His tag, cellular uptake was detected by fixing cells and staining with an anti-His antibody (YA4624) and fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibody.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for IGFBP7

Serum and LO culture supernatant samples were collected and stored at −80 °C until analysis. The concentration of mouse insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 7 (IGFBP7) was measured using a commercially available mouse IGFBP7 ELISA kit (CUSABIO, CSB-EL011101MO), strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, all samples and standards were diluted to the linear working range and added to the pre-coated 96-well microplates, followed by incubation at room temperature for 2 h. After washing with the provided wash buffer to remove unbound substances, the biotinylated detection antibody was added and incubated for 1 h. Subsequently, streptavidin-HRP conjugate was added, and the plates were incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. After a final wash, the substrate solution was added to initiate the color reaction, which was stopped with the stop solution after 20 min. The optical density of each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The concentration of IGFBP7 in each sample was calculated based on the standard curve generated from recombinant IGFBP7 standards, and all samples were assayed in technical duplicates to ensure reproducibility.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from BV2 cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of RNA were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using a reverse transcription kit (Takara). qPCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Takara) on a QuantStudio 5 real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher). The relative mRNA expression levels of IL-6, iNOS, TNF-α, IL-1β and Ago2 (primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 1) were calculated using the 2^(− ΔΔCt) method, with β-actin as the internal reference gene.

Cell viability and proliferation assays

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay: HT22 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and treated with different conditioned media for 24 h. After treatment, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo, Japan) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 1 h at 37℃. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader to assess cell viability.

5-ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay: Cell proliferation was detected using a Click-iT EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (Invitrogen, USA). HT22 cells were incubated with 10 μM EdU for 2 h after treatment with conditioned media. Cells were fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and stained according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope, and the percentage of EdU-positive cells was quantified in a blinded manner.

Immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry (IP-MS) and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) validation

To identify interacting partners of IGFBP7 and validate the interaction with Ago2, BV2 cells subjected to OGD/R and treated with IGFBP7 were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Total protein extracts were pre-cleared with Protein A/G magnetic beads for 1 h at 4 °C, then incubated with 5 μg of anti-IGFBP7 antibody (83292-5-RR; Proteintech) or normal rabbit IgG (as negative control) overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Immune complexes were captured by incubation with Protein A/G magnetic beads for 2 h at 4 °C. After extensive washing with lysis buffer (5 times, 5 min each), the bound proteins were processed differently for IP-MS and Co-IP:

For IP-MS, proteins were eluted and digested with trypsin. The resulting peptides were separated by UHPLC and analyzed using an Orbitrap Astral Zoom mass spectrometer in DIA mode. MS/MS data were searched against the UniProt mouse database using DIA-NN software (v1.9.2), with valid protein identification set at an FDR of < 0.01.

For Co-IP validation, bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 2 × SDS loading buffer for 5 min. Eluates were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies against Ago2 (1:1000; Proteintech) and IGFBP7 (1:1000; Proteintech) overnight at 4 °C. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate and imaged with a chemiluminescence detection system. Input samples (2% of total lysate) were included as loading controls.

Protein–protein docking and analysis

The full-length protein structures of mouse IGFBP7 and Ago2 were obtained from the UniProtKB database. Rigid protein–protein docking was performed using the GRAMM platform with default parameters, and the top 10 optimal conformations were collected. Binding energy, interacting surface area, hydrogen bond formation, and key interacting residues were analyzed. Binding free energy was calculated via PDBePISA, and 3D visualization of the docking complex was conducted using PyMOL 3.1.

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection for Ago2 knockdown

To knock down Ago2 expression, BV2 cells were transfected with a pool of two specific siRNAs targeting mouse Ago2 (siAgo2) or a negative control siRNA (siNC). The siRNA sequences targeting Ago2 are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 2 × 105 cells/well and cultured overnight to 60–70% confluence. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 50 pmol of each siRNA (total 100 pmol per well) was mixed with 5μL Lipofectamine 3000 in Opti-MEM, incubated for 15 min at room temperature, and added to cells. After 6 h, transfection medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. At 48 h post-transfection, cells were subjected to OGD treatment. Knockdown efficiency was validated by qPCR and Western blot.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Sample sizes were determined based on power analysis (α = 0.05, power = 0.8) from preliminary experiments. For in vivo experiments, n = 10 mice per group were used for behavioral tests, and n = 5 mice per group were used for histological and molecular analyses. For in vitro experiments, at least 5 independent biological replicates were performed for each assay.

The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. For data that violated the assumption of normality, non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U test for two-group comparisons; Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc correction for multiple groups) were applied.

Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test (or Mann–Whitney U test when appropriate). Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. The data from the MWM test (escape latency over training days) were analyzed using two-way repeated measures ANOVA with group as the between-subject factor and day as the within-subject factor, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA) and R software (v4.0).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that LO transplantation significantly reversed cognitive deficits in BCAS mice. Mechanistically, LO transplantation inhibited microglial pro-inflammatory polarization via the IGFBP7-Ago2 signaling axis, thereby alleviating neuroinflammation, reducing neuronal and synaptic loss, mitigating white matter demyelination, and preserving BBB integrity. Notably, CCH caused secondary pulmonary damage alongside central injury, and LO transplantation improved both. Although these data are consistent with a model wherein central improvement may alleviate secondary peripheral damage, the directional causality remains to be established by time-course and targeted intervention studies. Collectively, this study provides novel insights into lung-brain axis crosstalk under CCH conditions and identifies LO transplantation as a candidate organoid-based strategy for VaD that merits further translational evaluation.

The lung-brain axis is a critical bidirectional communication network linking pulmonary function and central homeostasis [30, 31]. Pulmonary dysfunction is an independent risk factor for cognitive decline, and conversely, cognitive impairment exacerbates lung tissue damage, forming a mutually reinforcing pathological vicious cycle [32, 33]. Currently, the pathological cascade by which lung injury induces brain damage has been extensively reported [27, 34, 35], whereas the role of brain injury in reversely regulating lung tissue under CCH remains poorly understood. Early studies using a rabbit carotid artery ligation model showed that reduced cerebral blood flow triggered severe pulmonary pathological damage [36, 37]; however, this model involves excessive ischemic insult and fails to mimic the mild-to-moderate CCH pathological features in the BCAS model. Our study confirmed that BCAS induced mild lung injury and pulmonary inflammation in mice, indicating that CCH alone is sufficient to cause peripheral lung tissue damage. These findings suggest that LO intervention represents a promising approach to break the pathological vicious cycle of the lung-brain axis.

In addition to ameliorating cognitive dysfunction, LO transplantation targeted the full spectrum of core neuropathological lesions driving VaD progression, including neuronal loss, synaptic damage, white matter demyelination, and BBB disruption. The BBB is a critical structural foundation for maintaining central nervous system homeostasis, and the loss of tight junction proteins is a core initiating event of BBB breakdown in CCH-induced VaD [38]. Previous studies have demonstrated that IGFBP7 exerts endothelial barrier protective effects via specific upregulation of the tight junction protein ZO-1, while exhibiting no regulatory effect on other tight junction components including Claudin-5 [39, 40]. In line with these established findings, we observed that LO transplantation exerted selective modulation of tight junction proteins in the cerebral microvasculature: it specifically upregulated ZO-1 expression without significantly altering Claudin-5 levels. This finding not only provides direct structural and functional evidence for LO transplantation-mediated BBB repair, but also functionally validates IGFBP7 as the key downstream effector mediating the vascular protective effects of LO transplantation [38]. We further identified that the synaptic-preserving and neuroprotective effects of LO transplantation relied on potent inhibition of aberrant activation, pro-inflammatory polarization, and excessive phagocytosis of microglia in the hippocampus and mPFC. Compared with direct neuronal protection, targeting neuroinflammation—the core driver of disease progression—more fundamentally blocks disease development [41]. This provides critical cellular evidence linking peripheral LO transplantation and central neuroprotection within the lung-brain axis framework. GSEA further confirmed that LO transplantation intervention corrected widespread signaling pathway dysregulation induced by CCH and reshaped systemic homeostasis in BCAS mice. Aberrant activation of hypoxia-related signaling, particularly the HIF-1 pathway, coupled with dysregulated inflammatory and metabolic cascades, represents the core mechanism driving the self-amplifying pathological vicious cycle along the lung-brain axis [42, 43]. Of note, the lung is highly susceptible to CCH-mediated injury due to its abundant ECM and dense microvascular network, serving as a key peripheral hub integrating systemic inflammatory and metabolic signals [44]. Consistent with this, our study showed that dysregulation of ECM and vascular homeostasis pathways identified by GSEA closely matched the histological phenotypes of pulmonary microvascular endothelial injury and interstitial collagen degradation, supporting that lung injury is an integral component of the systemic pathological cascade triggered by CCH. These findings demonstrate that LO transplantation concurrently ameliorates CCH-induced CNS injury and its secondary pulmonary damage, and highlight the existence of lung-brain axis crosstalk under CCH conditions. LO transplantation serves as an effective intervention to interrupt this pathological crosstalk, simultaneously restoring central and peripheral homeostasis. Collectively, these proteomic and bioinformatic findings refine the theoretical framework of bidirectional lung-brain axis regulation under CCH and highlight the lung as a key peripheral hub integrating systemic pathological signals under CCH.

We identified IGFBP7 as the key secretory factor mediating the protective effects of LO transplantation. IGFBP7 is a unique subtype of the IGFBP superfamily with low affinity for IGF-1/IGF-2, and extensively regulates cell proliferation, metabolism, inflammatory responses, and vascular homeostasis through both IGF-dependent and -independent pathways [45, 46]. IGFBP7 exerts context-dependent bidirectional effects on inflammation: endothelial-derived IGFBP7 exacerbates alveolar barrier disruption in acute lung injury and sepsis, whereas it inhibits pro-inflammatory cascades and alleviates excessive inflammation in acute kidney injury and tissue repair models [22, 47, 48]. This functional duality depends on tissue type, disease stage, and local microenvironment. Consistent with this characteristic, our in vivo and in vitro experiments confirmed that under CCH pathological conditions, IGFBP7 directly targets microglia, inhibits OGD/R-induced pro-inflammatory polarization, and exerts anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects, which align highly with its reparative function in chronic tissue injury. We next investigated the cellular sources of IGFBP7 in lung tissue and lung organoids. In native lung, immunofluorescence and single-cell transcriptomic analysis consistently showed that IGFBP7 was predominantly expressed by endothelial cells, with fibroblasts contributing to a lesser extent. In striking contrast, mature lung organoids displayed a fundamentally different IGFBP7 expression landscape: scRNA-seq revealed that IGFBP7 was most prominently expressed in fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells, and epithelial cells, whereas endothelial cells—the dominant source in native lung—contributed to a much lesser extent. This shift from an endothelial-dominated pattern in native lung to a mesenchyme/epithelial-dominated pattern in organoids indicates that the organoid platform fundamentally remodels the cellular sources of IGFBP7. This broader secretory profile distinguishes lung organoids from both native lung tissue and lung cell suspensions, and may underlie their superior therapeutic efficacy as a multifunctional secretory unit. Consistent with this selective secretory profile, we observed that although LO transplantation significantly ameliorated central pathological injury induced by CCH, it only restored pulmonary endothelial CD31 expression without obvious effects on interstitial collagen deposition. This selective protection arises from differential repair kinetics of distinct cellular components: pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells exhibit strong self-renewal capacity and rapid repair following removal of injurious stimuli [49]. However, interstitial ECM remodeling involves complex metabolic networks with inherently slower repair kinetics, consistent with the lack of detectable collagen restoration within the 39-day observation period [50].

Previous studies have shown that IGFBP7 exerts biological functions via intracellular internalization [51]. Consistent with this, we observed that OGD stress significantly enhanced the uptake of IGFBP7 by BV2 microglia, indicating that activated microglia are key target cells of IGFBP7 and that intracellular internalization is a prerequisite for its regulatory function. Using co-immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry, we screened and identified Ago2 as a core interacting protein of IGFBP7; in vivo and in vitro experiments jointly verified the direct binding between IGFBP7 and Ago2 in microglia. Ago2 is the only member of the Argonaute family with endonuclease activity, serving as a core component of the RNA interference pathway and the catalytic center of the RNA-induced silencing complex(RISC) [52]. In addition to canonical miRNA-mediated gene silencing, Ago2 regulates mRNA stability, translation activation, DNA damage repair, and chromatin remodeling through non-canonical pathways [53]. Ago2 is essential for CNS development, synaptic plasticity, cognitive homeostasis, and maintenance of BBB integrity [54, 55]. Moreover, Ago2, as the catalytic core of the RISC, mediates the anti-inflammatory effects of miRNAs such as miR-124 and miR-146a [56]. These miRNAs suppress excessive activation of pro-inflammatory pathways including NF-κB: miR-146a targets key signaling molecules IRAK1 and TRAF6, while miR-124 regulates microglial activation and inflammatory responses [57–59]. More importantly, previous studies have shown that exogenous supplementation of Ago2 also alleviates LPS-induced neuroinflammation [24]. Notably, our study was the first to confirm that IGFBP7 directly binds Ago2 in microglia and inhibits their pro-inflammatory polarization, establishing the IGFBP7-Ago2 axis as a regulator of microglial inflammatory phenotypes.

We also observed that LO transplantation reduced the number of GFAP-positive astrocytes in the hippocampus of BCAS mice (Figure S4). However, GFAP only labels the cytoskeletal structure of astrocytes, which limits its suitability for studying the interaction between IGFBP7 and astrocytes. Given that microglia are the primary drivers of neuroinflammation in CCH-induced VaD, the present study was designed to focus on microglial polarization as the core mechanism. The functional significance of the astrocytic changes observed here therefore remains unclear and warrants further investigation using more specific astrocyte markers.

In future research, patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) can be used to generate personalized lung organoids for autologous transplantation therapy in VaD. iPSC-based regenerative therapies have already demonstrated prominent clinical translational potential: the first clinically approved iPSC-derived therapeutics are now available for Parkinson’s disease and severe heart failure, and multiple clinical trials have further validated the safety and feasibility of personalized iPSC-derived cell therapies [60, 61]. This strategy not only minimizes the risk of allogeneic immune rejection and avoids adverse events associated with systemic immunosuppression, but also enables truly individualized precision therapy [62]. Compared with conventional systemic drugs, which are often limited by short half-lives and off-target effects, lung organoids physiologically secrete a variety of bioactive molecules into the systemic circulation. Within the 39-day observation period of this study, a single subcutaneous transplantation of lung organoids effectively ameliorated cognitive dysfunction and neuropathology in BCAS mice. Whether this therapeutic effect persists beyond this timeframe and whether repeat administration is required warrant further investigation with extended observation periods and systematic assessment of graft immunogenicity. In summary, this lung organoid-based biotherapeutic strategy offers a novel paradigm for VaD treatment that extends beyond the traditional CNS-centered approach, and merits further investigation for its clinical translational potential. Nevertheless, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the observation period was limited to 39 days; long-term efficacy, graft immunogenicity, and the potential need for repeated administration require future evaluation. Second, while our data support the IGFBP7-Ago2 axis as a key mechanism, direct in vivo causal evidence via genetic manipulation of IGFBP7 within the graft was not obtained due to technical challenges in intact 3D organoids. Future studies utilizing conditional knockout models or optimized gene-editing approaches, as well as extended time-course experiments, are warranted to further validate the causal role of this axis and to dissect the directionality of lung-brain crosstalk under CCH conditions.

Conclusion

In summary, our study provides the first preclinical evidence that LO transplantation exerts multi-domain cognitive protection in the BCAS mouse model of VaD. Mechanistically, LO-derived IGFBP7 is secreted into the circulation and physically interacts with microglial Ago2, suppressing aberrant microglial activation and neuroinflammation, thereby rescuing neuronal loss, synaptic damage, white matter injury, and BBB disruption. Furthermore, we observed concurrent improvement of brain pathology and secondary pulmonary injury following LO transplantation intervention, revealing lung-brain crosstalk as an important aspect of CCH pathology. Together, these findings support LO-based biotherapy as a potential therapeutic strategy for VaD, and identify the IGFBP7-Ago2 axis as a key regulatory pathway through which a peripheral organ-derived factor modulates central immune cell function. This study expands our understanding of VaD pathogenesis beyond the central nervous system to encompass systemic interorgan crosstalk. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the downstream molecular mechanisms of the IGFBP7-Ago2 axis and to evaluate LO transplantation in large animal models.
