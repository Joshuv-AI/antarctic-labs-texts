Tail spike protein evolution enables phage Psm140 to target Salmonella ShdA and overcome host resistance
Authors: Yukun Zeng, Shuhong Han, Xiaofu Wan, Zixun Lin, Xiangke Duan, Xin Zhou, Guoliang Zhang, Jiayin Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe growing challenge of bacterial antibiotic resistance has heightened attention in phage therapy. However, the rapid evolution of phage resistance in bacteria poses a major obstacle to its clinical application, making the development of strategies to overcome bacterial resistance a critical research priority. In this study, we investigated the coevolutionary dynamics between Salmonella enterica sm140 and its lytic phage, Psm140. Our previous work revealed that bacterial resistance to Psm140 arises through mutations in the rfbD gene, disrupting O-antigen synthesis and preventing phage recognition. Building on this finding, we employed laboratory-directed evolution to generate an evolved phage variant, PRsm1-20, capable of reinfecting resistant bacteria. Genomic analysis identified 18 nonsynonymous mutations in the tail spike protein gene (ORF63), enabling PRsm1-20 to utilize the outer membrane protein (OMP) ShdA as an alternative receptor while retaining lipopolysaccharide (LPS) binding capability. Genetic knockout and complementation assays confirmed ShdA as the essential receptor for PRsm1-20 infection. These findings elucidate a molecular mechanism by which phage tail spike protein evolution overcomes host resistance, providing new insights for the development of engineered phage-based antimicrobial therapies.Keywords: Salmonella enteritidis, Phage-resistance, Fibronectin-binding adhesin ShdA, Tail spike

Introduction

Salmonella, a Gram-negative facultative intracellular pathogen, currently comprises over 2600 identified serotypes (Esmael et al., 2021). Recognized as one of the four leading global causes of diarrheal diseases (Karama et al., 2024), Salmonella poses significant public health threats worldwide. According to estimates from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), this pathogen causes approximately 1.35 million intestinal infections and 420 deaths annually (Jeon et al., 2025). Notably, contaminated food products - including meat, poultry, eggs, and dairy-account for 85% of all salmonellosis cases (Ip et al., 2024; Paphitis et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025). These epidemiological data not only demonstrate the global prevalence of Salmonella but also underscore its severe impact on both human and animal health. The increasing reliance on antibiotics to combatSalmonella infections has led to the emergence and spread of antimicrobial-resistant strains (Swaggerty et al., 2022), creating substantial challenges for clinical treatment and exacerbating public health risks. Consequently, controlling Salmonella transmission and preventing the development of antimicrobial resistance have become critical priorities in global public health. Given this escalating crisis, conventional antibiotic therapy may no longer represent a sustainable solution. There is an urgent need to develop novel antimicrobial agents to address the growing threat of antibiotic resistance and safeguard human and animal health.

Phages are viruses capable of self-replication within bacterial hosts, ubiquitously distributed in natural environments (Strange et al., 2021). These bacterial viruses exhibit remarkable host specificity, selectively targeting and lysing their intended bacterial strains without disrupting the host's normal microbiota (Fang et al., 2024). Phage therapy offers several distinct advantages: straightforward isolation protocols; relatively short development cycles; and cost-effective production (Wang and Zhao, 2022). Therapeutically administered phages require low dosages yet demonstrate exceptional biodistribution, capable of crossing biological barriers (including the blood-brain barrier) and achieving systemic dissemination with high efficiency (Arumugam et al., 2022). In recent years, accumulating clinical evidence has documented successful applications of phage therapy against resistant bacterial infections (Weber-Dąbrowska et al., 2023). However, a significant challenge persists: bacterial populations frequently develop resistance through rapid evolutionary mutations that impair phage infectivity, representing a major cause of therapeutic failure (Gaborieau and Debarbieux, 2023). To address this critical limitation in phage applications, it is imperative to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying bacterial anti-phage defense systems, and develop effective strategies to counteract these resistance mechanisms.

Extensive research has demonstrated that bacteria employ diverse defense strategies against phage infection, including but not limited to blocking phage adsorption (Kintz et al., 2015), superinfection exclusion (Sie) systems (Safari et al., 2020), restriction-modification (RM) systems (Vasu and Nagaraja, 2013), abortive infection mechanisms (Lopatina et al., 2020), assembly interference systems, and CRISPR-Cas adaptive immunity (Lander, 2016). While these mechanisms confer effective protection against phage infection, they often impose significant fitness costs on bacterial hosts, such as reduced growth rates and attenuated virulence, since many targeted receptors simultaneously function in bacterial metabolism and immune evasion (Hyman and Abedon, 2010 ). In response to these sophisticated host defenses, phages have evolved remarkable counteradaptation strategies through continuous genetic variation and natural selection, such as receptor switching to alternative surface molecules (Meyer et al., 2012), enzymatic degradation of receptor-masking structures (Latka et al., 2017), promiscuous recognition of multiple receptors (Doulatov et al., 2004), modification of restriction enzyme cleavage sites to evade RM systems (Lacks and Greenberg, 1975 ; Avrani et al., 2011), targeted mutations in viral genomic loci (Deveau et al., 2008), production of anti-CRISPR proteins to inhibit Cas nuclease activity (Guo et al., 2017), and encoding of antiviral factors to counteract abortive infection systems (Hinton, 2010; Otsuka and Yonesaki, 2012).

In Salmonella, the fibronectin-binding adhesin ShdA represents a high-molecular-weight OMP initially characterized by Kingsley et al., who demonstrated its surface localization and functional role in fibronectin binding during intestinal colonization (Kingsley et al., 2002). Subsequent studies have established ShdA as a pleiotropic virulence factor that not only mediates bacterial adhesion and gut colonization, but also orchestrates critical pathogenic processes including host migration, signal transduction, and pathogen-host interactions (Urrutia et al., 2014). As a significant OMP, ShdA may potentially influence host-pathogen interactions by modulating bacterial surface characteristics. However, the current state of research on the functional role of ShdA in bacterial-phage interactions remains limited. OMPs frequently function as pivotal targets for phage recognition and adsorption. Consequently, elucidating the ShdA-phage interaction could reveal new mechanisms of Salmonella resistance and inform the development of phage-based antimicrobials.

Currently, systematic studies on the complex co-evolutionary relationship between Salmonella and phages remain relatively scarce. Most studies predominantly focus on elucidating bacterial resistance mechanisms, while largely neglecting the phage's adaptive strategies to overcome host defenses. Our preliminary work revealed that Salmonella enteritidis sm140 develops phage resistance through mutations in the rfbD gene, which disrupts O-antigen synthesis and alters LPS structure, consequently reducing adsorption efficiency of phage Psm140 (Zeng et al., 2024a). Building upon this discovery, we evolved Psm140 on resistant host, successfully generating phage variants capable of reinfecting rfbD mutants. The present study aims to illustrate the adaptive evolutionary strategies of phage Psm140 under LPS-deficient selective pressure and to determine whether it overcomes host defense through receptor recognition shifts, thereby exploring the Salmonella-phage coevolution. Furthermore, it hopefully will provide innovative ideas and strategies for developing new phage therapies and effectively addressing the increasingly serious problem of bacterial resistance.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, plasmids and phages

Salmonella enteritidis sm140 was originally isolated from the liver of diseased broiler chicken. The complete genome sequence of this strain has been deposited in the NCBI GenBank database under accession number CP125220.1. The phage-resistant strain Rsm1 was derived from sm140 through mutation of the rfbD gene. Additionally, we constructed sm140-mCherry, a derivative strain stably expressing red fluorescent protein for experimental tracking. The lytic phage Psm140 was isolated from sewage samples and demonstrates specific infectivity against strain sm140, but cannot lyse the resistant variant Rsm1. Plasmids pET28a (kanamycin resistant), pCas (kanamycin resistant), pTarget (spectinomycin resistant), and pYA3334 (chloramphenicol resistant) are all maintained in our laboratory.

Genome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis of phage Psm140

Phage Psm140 was concentrated from lysates using PEG8000 (Qingdao Hope Bio Technology Co., Ltd. China) precipitation, resuspended in PBS buffer, and sterilized by 0.22 μm filtration before genomic DNA extraction with the Viral DNA/RNA Extraction Kit (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China, RC311-01). The purified DNA was subjected to whole-genome sequencing on the DNBSEQ-T7RS platform (MGI Tech) following library preparation with the VAHTS Universal Plus DNA Library Prep Kit. Raw sequencing data were quality-filtered using Trimmomatic (v0.39) and assembled de novo with Platanus. Functional annotation of predicted genes was performed through BLASTp analysis (Diamond v2.0) against multiple databases, including NR, SwissProt, COG, KEGG, CARD, PHI, VFDB, TCDB, and REBASE, to characterize potential antibiotic resistance genes, virulence factors, host-interaction proteins, transporters, and restriction-modification systems. The assembled genome was visualized using Easyfig (v2.2.3) and deposited in GenBank for accession number assignment.

Phage genomic evolutionary analysis

In accordance with the classification criteria established by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) (Turner et al., 2023), we performed comprehensive phylogenetic analysis of phage Psm140 based on its terminase large subunit and major capsid protein sequences. Following BLAST alignment against the NCBI database, we retrieved highly homologous sequences and constructed phylogenetic trees using the Neighbor-Joining algorithm in MEGA 7.0 to elucidate evolutionary relationships among target gene sequences, with detailed genomic comparison data provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Identification of phage receptor-binding protein

To identify the receptor-binding protein of phage Psm140, we performed comparative genomic analysis against NCBI databases and selected two candidate proteins for functional characterization: the tail fiber protein (TF, ORF62) and tail spike protein (TS, ORF63). Using Psm140 genomic DNA as template, we amplified target genes with pET28a-homologous primers (Supplementary Table S2) and cloned them into BamHI-digested pET28a vector via homologous recombination. After transforming DH5α competent cells and verifying by sequencing, the recombinant plasmids pET28a-TF and pET28a-TS were expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) with 1 mM IPTG induction at 16°C overnight. The recombinant proteins were purified by nickel affinity chromatography (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, P2229S) and validated by SDS-PAGE.

To preliminarily screen for potential receptor-binding proteins, a competitive adsorption assay was performed. Gradient concentrations (10, 30, 60, and 90 μg/mL) of TF or TS proteins were separately mixed with 200 μL of sm140. An equal volume of LB liquid medium (Qingdao Hope Bio Technology Co., Ltd. China) served as the negative control. After incubation at 37°C for 5, 10, or 20 min, 100 μL of Psm140 (10^10 PFU/mL) was added to each mixture. After 15 min incubation, unbound phages in supernatant were quantified by double-layer plaque assay. The number of adsorbed phages was determined by subtracting the PFU in the supernatant from the PFU of the initial inoculum.

To visualize host-binding specificity, purified TS protein was fluorescently labeled via fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) conjugation. Briefly, 1 mL TS protein (60 μg/mL) was reacted with 50 μL 10^−5 M FITC in PBS: DMSO (4:1) under dark conditions, followed by 48 h dialysis. The resulting FITC-labeled TS was then incubated with sm140-mCherry for 5, 10, or 20 min. After incubation, the samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM, TCS SP8, Leica, Germany).

Laboratory evolution of phage Psm140

To generate phage variants capable of infecting the resistant strain Rsm1, we performed directed evolution of Psm140 through serial passage experiments. The Rsm1 strain was inoculated (1% v/v) into 5 mL LB medium and cultured at 37°C with 220 rpm shaking until reaching mid-log phase (OD600 ≈ 0.6). Subsequently, 200 µL of the parental Psm140 lysate (10^10 PFU/mL) was added and co-cultured for 24 h. After incubation, the culture was centrifuged (10,000 × g, 10 min, 4°C) and supernatant filtered through 0.22 µm membranes to obtain the first-generation evolved phage PRsm1-1. To maintain experimental consistency, all passages utilized aliquots from the same frozen stock of Rsm1. This serial passage protocol was repeated for 20 rounds, ultimately yielding a series of evolved phages (PRsm1-1 through PRsm1-20).

Assessment of phage infectivity using spot assay

The infectivity of evolved phage variants was evaluated using a standardized spot assay protocol. Briefly, 100 µL aliquots of mid-log phase bacterial cultures (sm140, Rsm1, sm140ΔrfbD, and Rsm1::rfbD) were individually mixed with 6 mL soft agar (0.5% w/v) and overlaid onto LB agar plates to create double-layer assay plates. After solidification, 2 µL of each evolved phage lysate (PRsm1-1 through PRsm1-20) was spotted onto the bacterial lawns. The plates were air-dried for 15 min and subsequently incubated at 37°C for 16–18 h. Phage infectivity was determined by observing the presence or absence of clear plaques on each bacterial strain.

Impact of evolved phage on resistant strain growth

To evaluate the growth inhibition efficacy of the 20th-passage evolved phage (PRsm1-20), we conducted bacterial growth curve analyses using both the original (sm140) and resistant (Rsm1) host strains at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1. Each experimental tube contained 3 mL LB broth inoculated with 1 mL bacterial culture (10^9 CFU/mL) and 1 mL phage lysate (10^9 PFU/mL), with 5 mL sterile LB medium serving as the blank control. Cultures were incubated with continuous shaking at 37°C, and bacterial growth was monitored hourly by measuring OD600 absorbance for 24 h using UV spectrophotometry. Each experiment was replicated three times.

Morphological and biological characterization of evolved phage

The evolved phage variants were comprehensively characterized for their morphological and biological properties according to established methods (Zeng et al., 2024b), including analyses of plaque morphology, optimal multiplicity of infection, one-step growth kinetics, thermal stability (4–70°C), pH tolerance (pH 3–11), and host range specificity. The host range analysis evaluated lytic activity against 15 reference strains comprising Salmonella enteritidis (C50041, J06, J15), Salmonella typhimurium (J002, S002), Salmonella pullorum (C7913, S98104, S9876), Escherichia coli (AA5, ER2738, YS2), Klebsiella pneumoniae (AD3, AE2, Y2), and Shigella castellani (2-2, Y6-1), all maintained in our laboratory collection at −80°C.

Genome resequencing and PCR validation of evolved phage

Whole-genome sequencing of the 20th-passage evolved phage PRsm1-20 was performed using the previously described methodology. To validate the mutation profiles identified through resequencing, we performed targeted PCR amplification and sequencing of all genes found to carry mutations (lysozyme and tail spike genes). Using specifically designed primers (Supplementary Table S2) that extended 50–80 bp beyond the coding sequences of both genes, we amplified these regions from both the ancestral Psm140 and evolved PRsm1-20 phage genomes for comparative analysis. Following electrophoretic verification, all amplicons were sequenced by TSINGKE Biological Technology Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China), and the resulting sequences were aligned using MegAlign software with focus on the complete coding regions for accurate mutation profiling. The evolutionary conservation of the mutated residues was evaluated using ConSurf.

Identification of evolved phage receptor components

To determine whether phage adsorption receptors on host were proteinaceous or LPS-based, we systematically treated Salmonella strains sm140 and Rsm1 with either proteinase K (20 mg/mL) or sodium periodate (20 mM) for 1 h at 37°C. Parallel PBS-treated samples served as controls. After thorough washing, we quantified the adsorption efficiency of phages Psm140 and PRsm1-20 to these pretreated cells using standard plaque assays. Briefly, 100 μL of bacterial culture was mixed with 6 mL of molten soft agar (0.5% agar) and poured onto a LB agar base plate. After solidification, 2 μL of phage suspensions were spotted onto the bacterial lawn. The plates were allowed to dry, then incubated at 37°C for 12–16 h, after which the presence or absence of clear lysis zones (plaques) was recorded. For direct receptor characterization, we isolated OMPs and LPS from both bacterial strains. Phage-receptor interactions were examined by pre-incubating phage particles with isolated OMPs or LPS, followed by infectivity assays. Each experiment was replicated three times.

Isolation and characterization of phage-resistant bacterial mutants

To isolate phage-resistant variants, we performed secondary infection assays with Rsm1. Bacterial cultures were grown to mid-log phase (OD600=0.6) in LB medium at 37°C with 220 rpm shaking, then challenged with phage PRsm1-20 at an MOI of 0.001 for 12 h. Five distinct single colonies, designated as Rsm1-1 to Rsm1-5, were purified through three consecutive streak plates on LB agar. Phage resistance was confirmed by triple-replicated plaque assays, where the absence of plaque formation or significantly reduced plaque numbers indicated phage resistance. To ensure strain authenticity, all resistant isolates were verified using Salmonella enteritidis specific primers (sdfI, Supplementary Table S2). Whole-genome sequencing of resistant mutant Rsm1-1 was performed (Tiangen DNA extraction kit, sequenced by Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. China) followed by alignment using BWA-MEM with default parameters. Candidate mutations identified through sequencing were validated by PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing of the complete shdA gene (primers in Supplementary Table S2, sequenced by TSINGKE Biological Technology Co., Ltd. China) with sequence alignments performed using MegAlign software.

Construction of shdA knockout mutant using λ-Red and CRISPR-Cas9 systems

TheshdA gene knockout was engineered through a combination of λ-Red recombination and CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing. First, we identified an optimal sgRNA target sequence (gacgctgaaccttagctcaa) using CRISPOR design tools. The 20 bp sgRNA was then cloned into the pTarget vector via PCR amplification using specific primers (sgRNA-CRISPR-F/R, Supplementary Table S2). For homologous recombination, we designed shdA-specific primers (AF/R and BF/R) using Primer Premier 5.0 and generated an AB fusion fragment through overlap PCR. Competent Rsm1 cells were transformed with pCas plasmid by electroporation, with successful transformants selected on kanamycin plates. Four Rsm1-pCas competent cells were subsequently co-transformed with 100 ng of verified pTarget-sgRNA plasmid and 400 ng of AB fusion fragment. Double-resistant colonies (Rsm1ΔshdA) were isolated and validated by PCR using the primers listed in Supplementary Table S2.

shdA complementation in phage-resistant strain

To functionally complement the shdA gene in phage-resistant strain Rsm1-1, we employed homologous recombination to reintroduce an intact shdA coding sequence. Using Primer Premier 5.0, we designed specific primers (3334-shdA F/R and pYA3334 F/R, Supplementary Table S2) to amplify both the shdA gene and homologous regions of plasmid pYA3334. The purified PCR fragments containing shdA and pYA3334 homologous arms were ligated into the linearized vector using a seamless cloning kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, D7010M), generating the recombinant plasmid pYA3334-shdA. This construct was then introduced into Rsm1-1 competent cells via electroporation to create the complemented strain Rsm1-1::shdA. To confirm functional complementation of shdA, spot assays were performed using both the ancestral phage Psm140 and evolved variant PRsm1-20 against the shdA knockout mutant (Rsm1ΔshdA) and complemented strain (Rsm1-1::shdA).

Determination of the adsorption capacity of evolved phage PRsm1-20

Strains (Rsm1, Rsm1-1, Rsm1ΔshdA and Rsm1-1::shdA) were cultured until reaching OD600=0.6. Then, 200 μL of each bacterial culture was combined with 100 μL of phage suspension at a titer 10^10 PFU/mL (MOI=100). The mixture was supplemented with 200 μL of fresh LB broth. A control sample was prepared by mixing 100 μL of the phage solution with 400 μL of LB medium. All samples were incubated at 37 °C for 7 min and subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 1 min. The supernatant, which contained unabsorbed phage particles, was collected, and phage titers in the supernatant were measured to quantify the non-adsorbed fraction. All assays were conducted with three replicates. The resulting bacterial pellets were resuspended in 1 × PBS and visualized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, HT7800, Hitachi, Japan) to assess phage adsorption.

Results

Genomic characterization and phylogenetic analysis of phage Psm140

Phage Psm140 possesses a linear double-stranded DNA genome of 41,913 bp with a GC content of 49.5%, encoding 64 predicted open reading frames (ORFs) (GenBank accession: PP437543). Genomic annotation revealed 18 antisense and 46 sense-oriented ORFs, including 36 (56.25%) with predicted functions and 28 (43.75%) hypothetical proteins (Fig. 1A). Functional analysis identified genes involved in host lysis (holin, endolysin), DNA replication/modification (helicase, HNH endonuclease, DNA polymerase), structural components (capsid, tail fiber), and viral assembly (portal protein, terminase). CAZy database analysis detected two glycoside hydrolases: ORF22 (endolysin) and ORF63 (tail spike with endorhamnosidase activity for O-antigen cleavage), while TCDB annotation suggested ORF54 may participate in transmembrane transport. The compact genome lacks tRNA, rRNA, mobile elements, and virulence/resistance genes, consistent with an obligately lytic lifestyle. Complete genome annotation data are available in Supplementary Table S3. Phylogenetic analyses based on the large terminase subunit (Fig. 1B) and major capsid protein (Fig. 1C) placed Psm140 within the Jerseyvirus genus Guernseyvirinae subfamily, Caudoviricetes class), showing closest relationships to phages F12013/vB_SenS_phi135 and VT223/blauehaus, respectively, supporting its taxonomic classification per ICTV standards.

Tail spike protein ORF63 functions as the receptor-binding protein of phage Psm140

The tail fiber and tail spike proteins were successfully expressed and purified by nickel-affinity chromatography, as confirmed by SDS-PAGE analysis (Supplementary Figure S1). UV quantification revealed concentrations of 1.417 mg/mL and 1.356 mg/mL for the tail fiber and tail spike proteins, respectively. Competitive binding assays demonstrated that only ORF63 could effectively compete with Psm140 for host (sm140) binding. At 10 μg/mL, ORF63 reduced phage adsorption approximately 3-fold across all incubation periods (5, 10, or 20 min), while higher concentrations (30, 60, or 90 μg/mL) caused more dramatic 10–24 fold reductions, with maximal inhibition occurring after 20 min incubation (Fig. 2A).

Tail spike protein ORF63 functions as the receptor-binding protein of phage Psm140. A: Adsorption activity of the tail spike protein. *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01. B: Binding of ORF63 to sm140-mCherry observed by LSM.

To further confirm ORF63's receptor-binding function, we performed fluorescence-based binding assays using FITC-labeled ORF63 (60 μg/mL). Confocal microscopy revealed robust and time-stable binding of ORF63 to host (sm140) across all tested incubation periods (5, 10, and 20 min) (Fig. 2B). These results provide direct visual evidence that ORF63 acts as the primary receptor-binding protein, with its consistent spatial distribution on the bacterial surface supporting its essential role in the initial stages of phage infection.

Adaptive evolution of phage lytic activity against resistant hosts

Through serial passaging under laboratory conditions, phage Psm140 evolved the ability to infect its previously resistant host Rsm1 while maintaining lytic activity against the original host sm140. Early evolutionary stages (passages 1–3) showed inconsistent lysis patterns against Rsm1, with some phage populations failing to establish productive infections. Stable and efficient lysis of resistant hosts was consistently achieved after 16 passages (Fig. 3A). Time-course analysis of bacterial lysis revealed distinct infection dynamics between the ancestral and evolved phages. The ancestral Psm140 exhibited rapid lysis of sm140 within 2 h post-infection, followed by bacterial regrowth indicating resistance development at 6 h. In contrast, the evolved PRsm1-20 showed delayed but sustained lytic activity, initiating bacterial clearance at 3 h and maintaining effective suppression of both sm140 and Rsm1 until 18 h post-infection (Fig. 3B). This extension of effective bacterial control demonstrates the improved therapeutic potential achieved through directed evolution.

Morphological and biological characterization of evolved phage PRsm1-20

Transmission electron microscopy revealed that the evolved phage PRsm1-20 maintained morphological characteristics similar to its ancestral counterpart Psm140, with comparable head diameter (∼50 nm) and tail length (∼105 nm) (Fig. 4A). However, plaque morphology analysis demonstrated significantly smaller plaque formation by PRsm1-20 on sm140 hosts (pinpoint vs moderate-sized plaques) (Fig. 4B). Quantitative titration showed titers of 1.5 × 10^11 PFU/mL for Psm140 and 1.6 × 10^10 PFU/mL for PRsm1-20.

Biological characteristics of the phages. A: Morphological characterization by transmission electron microscopy. B: Plaque morphology on host bacterial lawns. C: Optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI) determination. D: One-step growth curve analysis. E: Thermal stability profile after 1 h incubation at various temperatures. F: pH stability assessment after 1 h exposure to different pH conditions.

Host range analysis against 17 bacterial strains revealed identical lysis profiles between PRsm1-20 and Psm140 (Supplementary Table S4). Both phages shared similar infection parameters: optimal MOI of 10^−5 (Fig. 4C), 5-min latent periods, 75-min rise periods, and burst sizes of 255 PFU/cell (Psm140) versus 249 PFU/cell (PRsm1-20) (Fig. 4D).

Stability assessments showed both phages were maintained activity between 4 and 50°C, with significant inactivation at 60°C (1 h) and complete loss of viability above 70°C (Fig. 4E). They demonstrated pH stability from 3.0 to 12.0 but were completely inactivated under extreme pH conditions (≤2 or ≥13) (Fig. 4F). Collectively, aside from reduced plaque size, PRsm1-20 preserved the fundamental characteristics of Psm140 in morphology, host range, infection dynamics, and environmental stability.

Genomic analysis of evolved phage PRsm1-20

Quality control of the sequencing data for phage PRsm1-20 (NCBI SRA accession: SRP497024) indicated high data quality, enabling reliable downstream bioinformatic analysis. Alignment against the reference genome Psm140 revealed a total of 42 mutation sites in PRsm1-20, distributed across two genes: one mutation was located in the lysozyme gene (ORF22), while the remaining 41 mutations were identified in the tail spike gene (ORF63) (Fig. 5A). To validate the sequencing data, PCR amplification of both genes was performed using specific primers. The products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis, confirming the expected fragment sizes for both the lysozyme and tail spike genes (Supplementary Figure S2). Although initial resequencing data suggested a putative mutation (A222T) in the lysozyme gene, subsequent Sanger sequencing confirmed this to be a sequencing artifact. Sequence alignment of the tail spike gene, following translation of nucleotide sequences into amino acids, revealed that the mutations included 23 synonymous (T81G, T90C, A96G, C108G, C111T, T114C, A117C, C132T, G165C, T168G, C171T, A174G, C177T, T282G, T291G, C297T, G387T, C390T, A393G, T396C, C405T, G432A, T450C) and 18 nonsynonymous substitutions (A46G, G118A, C140A, G185A, C188T, T268C, G284C, A285C, G286C, A292G, C307A, G311C, T313C, G383T, C384A, A788G, A1241G, A1255G) (Fig. 5B). Among the 41 mutations, 26 were located in conserved regions, 4 in average regions, and 11 in variable regions of the protein (Supplementary Figure S3). These findings demonstrate strong selective pressure on the tail spike protein during phage adaptation to resistant hosts, while genomic stability was largely maintained in other functional regions.

Acquisition of dual receptor recognition enables phage adaptation to host resistance

Comprehensive receptor characterization revealed distinct host recognition patterns between the ancestral Psm140 and evolved PRsm1-20 phages. The wild-type Psm140 exhibited exclusive dependence on LPS for host adsorption, showing complete resistance to proteinase K treatment (P > 0.05) but significant 60% reduction after KIO4-mediated LPS oxidation (P < 0.001) (Fig. 6A). This LPS specificity was further confirmed by competitive binding assays in which purified LPS caused an 8-log reduction in phage titer (4.63 × 10^9 to 20 PFU/mL), while OMPs showed minimal effect (3.83 × 10^9 PFU/mL) (Fig. 6B).

Determination of binding receptor of phage. A: Adsorption properties of phage Psm140 and PRsm1-20 on sm140 and Rsm1 treated with proteinase K or KIO4. B: Receptor competition assay.

In contrast, the evolved PRsm1-20 developed dual receptor recognition capability. For wild-type sm140, it maintained LPS binding (60% adsorption reduction by KIO4) while acquiring new OMP recognition (70% reduction by proteinase K; both P < 0.001). For the LPS-deficient Rsm1 strain, PRsm1-20 relied exclusively on OMP binding (70% proteinase K-sensitive adsorption) (Fig. 6A). These results demonstrate that phage evolution expanded receptor recognition from strict LPS dependence to a dual-specificity system incorporating OMP binding, thereby overcoming host resistance while preserving ancestral infection pathways (Fig. 6B). The evolutionary acquisition of OMP recognition represents a sophisticated adaptation mechanism that broadens host range without sacrificing original infection capabilities.

OMP ShdA serves as a novel receptor for evolved phage PRsm1-20

To precisely characterize the receptor components recognized by the evolved phage PRsm1-20, we performed sequential resistance selection using the first-generation resistant strain Rsm1 and phage PRsm1-20. Through double-layer plaque assays, we isolated second-generation phage-resistant bacterial clones. All five monoclonal isolates were confirmed as Salmonella enteritidis by species-specificsdfI primer detection, qualifying them for subsequent analyses. Phage resistance validation revealed that only strain Rsm1-1 maintained consistent resistance to PRsm1-20 after three rounds of purification (Supplementary Figure S4), and was therefore selected for whole-genome resequencing (NCBI SRA accession: SRR28553065). Genomic analysis identified a C→T point mutation at position 1,445 in the OMP geneshdA, resulting in a Ser482Leu amino acid substitution.

As shown in Supplementary Figure S5A, fragments A, B, and the AB fusion product were successfully amplified. The 20 bp gRNA sequence in the pTarget vector was replaced by PCR (Supplementary Figure S5B). For scarless knockout of shdA, the pCas plasmid was introduced into Rsm1, and successful integration was confirmed by PCR amplification of an 888 bp fragment from four randomly selected clones using pCasF/R primers (Supplementary Figure S5C). The AB fusion fragment and pTarget-gRNA were then co-transformed into competent Rsm1-pCas cells. Dual-resistant colonies were selected on kanamycin and spectinomycin plates, and the shdA knockout was confirmed by the absence of a PCR product using shdA primers (Supplementary Figure S5D). Using λ-Red recombination and CRISPR/Cas9 systems, we constructed an shdA knockout mutant (Rsm1ΔshdA,) and genetic complementation strain (Rsm1-1::shdA). Phage susceptibility assays demonstrated complete resistance in both the natural shdA mutant (Rsm1-1) and knockout strain (Rsm1ΔshdA), with phage adsorption rates showing a 4.5-fold reduction (from 93 to 21%). In contrast, the complemented strain (Rsm1-1::shdA) restored wild-type susceptibility, with adsorption rates returning to normal levels (81%) (Fig. 7A, B). TEM provided visual confirmation that Rsm1 cells showed dense arrays of phage particles bound to their surfaces (Fig. 7C), while Rsm1-1 and Rsm1ΔshdA exhibited nearly complete absence of phage adsorption (Fig. 7D, E). Notably, phage binding was fully restored in the complemented strain Rsm1-1::shdA (Fig. 7F). These comprehensive results demonstrate that the OMP ShdA serves as a novel receptor enabling PRsm1-20 to reinfect previously resistant host bacteria (Rsm1).

Discussion

As naturally occurring viruses that specifically infect and lyse bacteria, phages represent one of the most abundant and diverse biological entities in nature and represent a promising alternative to conventional antibiotics (Adriaenssens and Brister, 2017; Huss and Raman, 2020). To address the phenomenon of phage resistance in bacteria, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying phage-host interactions represents a fundamental scientific issue for optimizing phage-based therapeutics.

Our previous work demonstrated that Salmonella sm140 escapes phage Psm140 recognition through mutations in the rfbD gene (encoding dTDP-4-dehydrorhamnose reductase), which disrupts O-antigen synthesis and leads to defective LPS structure (Zeng et al., 2024a). This finding exemplifies the canonical bacterial resistance mechanism of phage evasion through surface polysaccharide modification. However, the arms race between phages and their hosts has never ceased in nature (Safari et al., 2020). Therefore, this study investigated a fundamental question in phage-host coevolution: whether phages can evolutionarily acquire alternative infection pathways when bacterial hosts develop resistance through LPS loss.

Through 20 rounds of laboratory-directed evolution, we obtained the evolved phage variant PRsm1-20, which is capable of reinfecting resistant hosts. Whole-genome sequencing revealed 18 nonsynonymous mutations exclusively clustered in the tail spike protein gene (ORF63). In vitro competitive binding assays and LSM observations indicate that the tail spike protein ORF63 of ancestral phage Psm140 functions as its receptor-binding protein, specifically recognizing LPS as its receptor. In the event of a mutation in the LPS receptor of the host bacterium sm140, the original phage becomes non-infective. Strikingly, the evolved PRsm1-20 variant overcame this limitation through targeted mutations in ORF63, acquiring the capacity to recognize the novel receptor ShdA while remarkably preserving its ancestral LPS-binding capability. This 'receptor switching' strategy not only confirms that phages can break through host resistance barriers through key protein mutations but also reveals the remarkable plasticity of the receptor recognition system in phage-host interactions, thereby corroborating previous reports that phages can evolve their receptor-binding proteins to recognize new receptors (Chang et al., 2010; Meyer et al., 2012; Cai et al., 2019). Despite the extensive mutations in its tail spike gene, PRsm1-20 maintained key functional properties including optimal infectivity (MOI=10^−5) and environmental stability (4–50°C, pH 3-12). Most notably, it exhibited threefold longer antibacterial activity than its ancestral phage (18 h vs 6 h), significantly delaying resistance emergence. Whole-genome analysis confirmed the absence of virulence or lysogeny genes, supporting its clinical potential. These results collectively demonstrate that phage genomes can accommodate substantial adaptive mutations in key proteins without compromising core functions or safety, underscoring a feasible evolutionary route toward enhancing therapeutic efficacy.

Previous studies have established that tailed phages primarily rely on tail spike proteins to recognize host surface receptors and initiate infection (Buth et al., 2015; Simpson et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2019). While most dsDNA phages employ the holin-lysozyme system for host lysis (Frias et al., 2009; Heineman et al., 2009; Bordenstein and Bordenstein, 2016), our genomic analysis revealed no mutations in these lytic genes of the evolved PRsm1-20 phage. This observation implies that during coevolution with host sm140, selective pressure predominantly targeted the adsorption process rather than the subsequent lysis mechanism. These findings underscore the critical role of adsorption as the first step in phage infection (Chapot-Chartier, 2014; Xiong et al., 2019) and demonstrate that adaptive evolution of tail spike proteins serves as the primary determinant for overcoming host resistance.

Genomic analysis in this study identified a critical C1445T mutation (Ser482Leu) in the shdA gene of resistant strain Rsm1-1, with subsequent knockout and complementation experiments demonstrating its direct causal relationship to phage susceptibility. While shdA has been extensively characterized as a fibronectin-binding adhesin involved in bacterial colonization and pathogenesis (Kingsley et al., 2002; Urrutia et al., 2014), our work provides the first experimental evidence establishing its additional function as a phage receptor during infection. TEM offered compelling visual confirmation: Rsm1 exhibited dense arrays of adsorbed phage particles, whereas shdA mutants showed near-complete absence of phage binding, and complementation restored wild-type adsorption patterns. These findings not only definitively identify ShdA as the novel receptor recognized by PRsm1-20, but more importantly reveal the dual functionality of virulence factors in mediating both pathogenicity and phage susceptibility-suggesting an evolutionary trade-off between bacterial infectivity and phage resistance.

Allosteric effects can modulate the structure and function of binding sites through long-range interactions (Xu et al., 2016). Moreover, misfolding or destabilization of membrane proteins has been shown to impair their role as phage receptors (Bertozzi Silva et al., 2016; Azam and Tanji, 2019). In this study, the residue at position 482 of ShdA was substituted from a polar and hydrophilic Ser to a nonpolar and hydrophobic Leu. This substitution not only introduces substantial hydrophobicity and steric hindrance, potentially disrupting the proper folding or stability of ShdA in the bacterial outer membrane, but also shifts the local electrostatic potential from negative toward neutral. These changes may allosterically alter or perturb the overall conformation of ShdA, thereby indirectly impairing its binding capacity to the tail spike protein ORF63.

Although this study provides an investigation into the mechanism by which phage Psm140 evolves to recognize the host OMP ShdA through tail spike protein adaptation, several limitations remain. First, the laboratory-based directed evolution process may not fully recapitulate the long-term co-evolutionary dynamics between phages and their hosts in natural environments. Consequently, the practical efficacy of the evolved phage PRsm1-20 requires further validation under more physiologically relevant conditions. Secondly, among the 18 nonsynonymous substitutions identified in the phage tail spike protein, the critical mutations and their precise mechanistic roles require further elucidation through more refined molecular docking analyses and functional experiments.

Conclusion

This study elucidates the dynamic coevolution between Salmonella sm140 and phage Psm140, demonstrating how phages overcome host resistance through receptor-switching adaptations. When host developed resistance through LPS loss, the evolved phage PRsm1-20 overcame this barrier by acquiring dual receptor specificity (LPS/OMP) via tail spike mutations while maintaining infectivity and environmental stability. These findings reveal the adaptive strategy of phage receptor-switching in response to host resistance and establish the outer membrane protein ShdA as a functional phage receptor. We hope these discoveries will enhance our understanding of phage-host interactions and provide insights for developing phage therapeutics against resistant pathogens.
