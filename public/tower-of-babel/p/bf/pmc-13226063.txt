Leveraging electronic health record data for precision medicine insights: the precision medicine registry at NYU Langone Health
Authors: Carina M. Flaherty, Krutika Pandit, Eduardo Iturrate, Aditya Surapaneni, Amyn Majbri, Sneha Mehta, Saul B. Blecker, Leora Horwitz, Jelle Veraart, Aristotelis Tsirigos, Morgan E. Grams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Integrated electronic health record databases provide an unprecedented opportunity to enhance knowledge of disease prediction, prevention, and management in real-world settings. The Precision Medicine (PMED) Registry is a cohort of approximately 2 million patients seen at NYU Langone Health inpatient and outpatient centers, capturing data generated during clinical care from January 1, 2010, to the present, with regular data updates. Data have been used for several research investigations, including international meta-analyses, validation of disease identification algorithms, local evaluation of risk tools, testing analytical pipelines for imaging data, and the investigation of novel correlates of established risk prediction models. Additionally, the assessment of local practice has provided insights into clinical practice patterns and aided quality improvement efforts to assess and promote the uptake of guideline-directed therapies at the system and provider level. This study illustrates how real-world integrated electronic health record data with multi-modal clinical information can be leveraged to support research in prediction, diagnosis, prevention, and treatment optimization across health systems.

Introduction

Electronic health records (EHRs) have emerged as a powerful tool in the modern healthcare landscape in the United States, offering an unprecedented opportunity to evaluate and enhance personalized approaches to patient care. Federal legislative efforts, including the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act (HITECH Act) and the Affordable Care Act1,2, have spurred the widespread use of EHRs in medical centers. The vast amount of data generated through routine medical care enables the identification of potential disparities, risks, and opportunities for intervention. EHR data can be leveraged to assess risk and aid in clinical decision-making support (e.g., identifying pharmacotherapy prescription patterns3, predicting morbidities in preterm birth infants4, and improving population health through chronic and infectious disease surveillance5–7), and to bolster research in fields with little quantitative data such as psychiatry8 (e.g., coupling information from clinical encounter notes with EHR-derived biomarkers to produce risk profiles for suicide prevention9). Comparing EHR data with health insurance claims data10 can enhance understanding of real-world behaviors, such as medication adherence. Research registries that link genetic data to electronic health records, such as the All of US Research Program11 and the UK Biobank12 allow global meta-analyses of genome-wide association studies13 that lead to a better understanding of the genetic basis of a wide range of diseases.

The advent of precision medicine14 and the rise of artificial intelligence15 in healthcare have also created new opportunities to transform raw EHR data into actionable insights that directly enhance patient care and outcomes. Fundamental to the derivation of insights from EHR data is the ability to curate and synthesize large amounts of data. Tools like artificial intelligence (AI) powered imaging analytics16, machine learning algorithms17, large-language models, and predictive modeling18 can facilitate sifting through the vast amount of data contained in EHRs. By leveraging sophisticated data processing methods, such as dimensionality reduction techniques19, researchers can derive usable, detailed, and informative representations from this data. Integration with other large-scale data sources, such as genomic and wearable device data, can enable the construction of comprehensive phenotypic and biological profiles of participants’ health. Together, these multimodal data resources form the foundation for precision medicine and motivate research efforts both locally20 and nationally11 to advance patient care and outcomes through large-scale, system-integrated research infrastructures.

The Precision Medicine (PMED) Registry at NYU Langone Health (NYULH) was created to help promote data-driven approaches to improving disease prediction, prevention, diagnosis, and treatment through the collection of electronic health record data at NYULH. The PMED Registry is uniquely embedded within the NYULH system, capturing longitudinal, prospective, and retrospective data across the full continuum of care, from outpatient and specialty clinics to emergency and inpatient services. Analysis is facilitated by NYU Langone Health’s High Performance Computing (HPC) core, a centralized integrative resource for large-scale computational research and the deployment of advanced analytical workflows, including deep learning techniques and large language models. This local integration allows the registry to function as a learning health system pillar, enabling rapid impact evaluation across one of the largest health systems in the northeastern United States.

Example uses for the PMED Registry include but are not limited to: (1) conducting epidemiological research focused on risk stratification for mortality, disease progression, and disease incidence; (2) conducting pharmacoepidemiology studies for use in evaluating the risks, benefits, uptake, and discontinuation of medications in routine clinical care; (3) validating external risk prediction models locally to ensure predictions align with observed local data; (4) developing new risk prediction models and machine learning algorithms; (5) assessing and facilitating quality improvement initiatives; (6) generate eligibility data for grants or clinical trials; and (7) connecting patient data with vital statistic and United States registry data. This paper details the establishment of the PMED Registry, as well as a selection of research conducted within the registry, to highlight the multifaceted potential of and use cases for EHR-based registries for precision medicine research.

Studies published using the PMED Registry

Another example of a multi-site cohort study that utilized the PMED Registry was a 2024 investigation of risk factors for post-hospitalization venous thromboembolism. Using laboratory measurements and diagnosis codes abstracted from the registry, results showed independent associations of lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and higher urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR) with a higher risk of venous thromboembolism following hospitalization. Analysis across two health systems showed consistent results: for every 10 ml/min per 1.73 m² decrease in eGFR below 60 ml/min per 1.73 m², the risk of venous thromboembolism increased by 6% (HR 1.06 [95% CI, 1.02–1.11], P = 0.01), and each doubling of UACR was linked to a 5% higher risk (HR 1.05 [95% CI, 1.03–1.07], P < 0.001)22. This study is a first step in evaluating individual-level risk post-hospital discharge, providing the foundation for local algorithms to help estimate who might benefit from targeted monitoring or anticoagulation therapy.

The PMED Registry also contributes to large-scale and impactful meta-analyses. For example, the PMED Registry participates in the Chronic Kidney Disease Prognosis Consortium (CKD-PC), an international consortium created to advance research on kidney disease through global meta-analyses23. The PMED registry is used to generate summary-level data for comparative analyses with local, national, and international datasets. Recent projects have addressed the associations of kidney disease with different types of incident cancer and hospitalized infection24, leveraging clinical encounter data and diagnosis codes from the registry. This type of work facilitates rigorous investigation into even relatively rare forms of disease, where large sample sizes are needed to capture sufficient cases.

Registry use case: development and validation of risk scores and predictive models

The PMED Registry can also be used to advance development and clinical translation of risk scores for purposes of risk stratification. For example, prior to local implementation of the established kidney failure risk equation (KFRE)25, the PMED Registry was used as a local platform to evaluate the discrimination and calibration of the equation within NYU Langone Health. The 5-year KFRE equation had a C-statistic of 0.858, indicating very good discrimination, bolstering the evidence for use of the KFRE locally in patient care. In a 2025 study, the PMED Registry was utilized to investigate the association between the PREVENTTM risk score, which was developed to predict the risk of incident cardiovascular disease (CVD)26, and subclinical coronary atherosclerosis27, as defined by the Agatston coronary artery calcium (CAC) score. The registry allowed for the abstraction of the CAC score using pattern recognition in radiology notes describing coronary computed tomography. The results showed that the PREVENTTM risk score accurately predicted not only clinical CVD but also subclinical coronary atherosclerosis, demonstrating its robustness and reliability across different populations and providing further validity to the PREVENTTM risk score equation. With future validation, the PREVENTTM risk score might also help determine which patients should undergo coronary computed tomography. The use of real-world data underscores the potential of databases like the PMED Registry to not only enhance the precision of risk prediction models but also to ensure and expand their relevance and effectiveness in clinical practice.

Risk scores can incorporate not only laboratory results but also imaging data. One example is the Sybil28 model, a deep learning algorithm developed to accurately predict an individual’s future risk of lung cancer from low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) scans. The Sybil model demonstrated robust and consistent performance in the PMED Registry, achieving AUCs between 0.72 and 0.82 across 6 years, with Year 1 reaching a peak AUC of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.77–0.87). This example demonstrates the growing potential of AI-driven tools to enhance early detection and risk stratification for chronic diseases and the potential generalizability across health care systems. EHR registries are an excellent resource for rigorously validating and refining these models across diverse patient populations and settings, a necessary first step prior to integrating them into clinical workflows to improve patient outcomes.

Registry use case: validation of algorithms and cleaning pipelines

The massive scale of electronic health data necessitates algorithmic disease capture and cleaning pipelines for efficient use. While physician adjudication and chart review are gold standard methods for case identification and review, EHR registries can also be used to facilitate more scalable methods. For example, the PMED Registry was used to validate an algorithm for identifying ESKD using billing and procedure codes that was developed using data from another health system. Both health systems are used as the gold standard assessment registration in the USRDS29, a national registry of ESKD cases that is thought to be nearly 100% sensitive in case identification. The study found excellent algorithm specificity of 99.8% (95% confidence interval, 99.8–99.8%), and negative predictive value of 99.8% (99.5–99.8%), and acceptable sensitivity of 73.9% (72.7–75.1%) and positive predictive value of 79.9% (78.9–81.0%), suggesting an acceptable clinical algorithm30.

Cleaning pipelines are particularly useful in analyzing clinical imaging data, which can have marked variation in spatial resolution and contrast. Recently developed machine learning-based approaches leverage deep neural networks to transform clinical MRI data for optimized use in analytical pipelines (e.g., common brain segmentation techniques such as FreeSurfer)31–33. The PMED Registry has been used to test these deep learning approaches: Brain MRIs of 112 patients with and without prevalent dementia were obtained from the registry and enhanced using SynthSR34. FreeSurfer was easily deployed on the optimized images, and patients with dementia showed the expected pattern of volumetric reduction compared to age- and sex-matched controls (e.g., cortical volume: t = −2.95, p = 3.83 × 10−³; Left hippocampus: t = −2.36, p = 2.04 × 10−2 with corresponding increases in ventricular volume (e.g., right ventricle volume: t = t = 3.37, p = 1.05 × 10−3) (Fig. 1)35. Registry use case: assessment of uptake of guideline-directed management therapies

EHRs can be used to track uptake in guideline-directed management therapies as well as trends in clinical practice patterns. The laboratory and prescription data in the PMED Registry have been used to develop internal quality improvement assessments such as clinic- and provider-specific performance on metrics such as albuminuria testing in patients with diabetes, renin-angiotensin system blockade in people with CKD, and clinically recommended therapies for heart failure and other chronic diseases. One example is finerenone, a relatively new medication used in patients with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease. Prescription data from participants in the PMED Registry showed slow but steady increases in new prescriptions, punctuated by sharper increases which may correspond to guideline statements (Fig. 2). Discussion

The PMED Registry is an example of how integrated EHR data can serve as a translational engine for precision medicine and a core component of a learning health system36. As exemplified by the diverse use cases—cohort analysis, risk model validation, algorithm development, imaging harmonization, and monitoring of guideline-recommended treatment uptake—the PMED Registry illustrates how routinely collected clinical data can be transformed into actionable knowledge that feeds back into clinical care. Other institutions are also establishing frameworks for multi-institutional data sharing and integration for cross-organizational collaboration; however, we focus here on the ability to directly translate research findings into local clinical care embedded within a single health system37.

Several of the use cases described herein illustrate how local epidemiological cohort analysis may inform future quality assurance efforts. For example, the family history of CKD study demonstrates how harmonized registry data can identify high-risk subgroups that are not easily captured in routine care, while simultaneously revealing documentation gaps in pertinent medical history that could show opportunities for quality improvement. Similarly, the post-hospitalization VTE study shows how laboratory and diagnosis data already present in the EHR can be repurposed to support individualized risk stratification after discharge—an area where clinical decision support remains limited.

Local evaluation of publicly available tools such as KFRE and PREVENT™ directly informs whether and how these tools should be deployed in clinical workflows. Using the PMED Registry, we found that both models performed well, with adequate discrimination and calibration. The Sybil lung cancer example extends this paradigm to imaging-based deep learning models, showing that a local EHR registry can serve as a scalable, real-world testing resource for validating AI tools across heterogeneous clinical data. These studies are an essential step before clinical implementation, supporting and defining the clinical applicability, prior to testing predictive tools in care pathways.

The MRI harmonization work highlights an opportunity to better study disease processes at greater scale. Using machine learning pipelines, clinical MRI images, which are more variable in terms of resolution and contrast but are much more abundant and generalizable than research MRI images, can be harnessed in research, improving scientific understanding of diseases such as dementia38. The use of clinical data both aids the local translation of research findings as well as mitigates potential selection bias present in research studies, improving the depth and generalizability of research findings39.

The registry’s use in monitoring uptake of guideline-directed therapies illustrates its role as operational infrastructure for a learning health system. By tracking prescribing patterns of new therapies, such as with finerenone, and linking changes to guideline releases, the PMED Registry is an example of how EHR-based registries enable health systems to move toward active measurement of adherence to guideline recommended care. In doing so, it embeds data science directly into clinical workflows, aligning with calls in the literature40 to move from passive data collection toward continuous learning and improvement.

Taken together, these examples illustrate the distinctive contribution of the PMED Registry to the research literature and clinical landscape, and underscores the importance of EHR-based research for achieving -(1) deep longitudinal phenotyping of local patient populations, (2) integration of multimodal data types (labs, notes, imaging, procedures, etc.), and (3) an explicit focus on validation, reproducibility, and clinical translation for improved patient care and outcomes. This combination enables a form of precision medicine that is not limited to genomics or bespoke cohorts but is embedded directly in routine care and continuously refined through real-world data. Future directions for the registry aim to incorporate genetic data, unlocking deeper insights into personalized risk stratification, diagnostic accuracy, and more precise treatment strategies.

The PMED Registry, as with all EHR-based research, does have several challenges and limitations. Data quality remains a central challenge. In addition to data errors and variable reporting practices (e.g., reporting values below the lower limit of detection as a string and detectable values as numeric), there is potential for bias in data collection (e.g., less than ideal conditions) and patient selection (sicker patients receive more testing). Additionally, missing data and variability in documentation styles among healthcare providers complicate data analysis and modeling efforts when using clinical notes. Finally, as with all research involving the collection of personal health information, data privacy and security measures are paramount concerns. While these challenges exist, they can be mitigated through rigorous validation, harmonization of data standards, and the development of robust and rigorous privacy and security measures. Participant privacy, governance, and responsible data use remain foundational requirements.

Beyond these limitations, the PMED Registry demonstrates numerous strengths that underscore the value of EHR-based registries as a resource for advancing precision medicine within a single integrated infrastructure: the capture of a wide spectrum of diseases and conditions; the inclusion of persons often underrepresented in clinical research, such as the very old; and the ability to access and integrate diverse sources of data and information. The PMED Registry exemplifies how health systems can operationalize local EHR-based data to drive forward a learning health system infrastructure and drive impactful research that improves health outcomes.

Methods

NYU Langone Health is a nonprofit academic medical center and health system based in New York City, with locations throughout the New York metro area and Florida. The health system comprises 6 inpatient locations and over 300 outpatient locations. NYU Langone Health encompasses the NYU Grossman School of Medicine and the NYU Grossman Long Island School of Medicine and is one of the largest healthcare systems in the northeastern United States. NYU Langone Health utilizes the EPIC (EPIC Systems Corporation) electronic health record system for the management and handling of patient data. The Precision Medicine Registry was built from extracted EHR patient data from January 1st, 2010, to the present. The registry is routinely updated every quarter and, at times, more frequently on an ad hoc basis, allowing for longitudinal data capture and the inclusion of new participants who meet eligibility criteria.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants in the PMED Registry were selected by querying the NYU Langone Health data lake, where all raw data from the EHR is stored. Participants were included in the registry if they were aged 18 or older and had a qualifying laboratory measurement, specifically, whether serum creatinine had ever been recorded.

Data types

For eligible participants, multiple data types are abstracted. Data includes clinical encounter information from inpatient and outpatient hospital visits, physician/clinic appointments, billing records, clinical laboratory records, electrocardiogram and echocardiogram results, pathology records, and radiology records, including computed tomography (CT) scans and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Data types include structured (e.g., laboratory data) and unstructured data (e.g., notes). Data abstraction

Data collection occurs as part of routine care and is thus ongoing. To facilitate the use of retrospective data for multipurpose analysis, a data cleaning pipeline was established. Common clinical comorbidities such as diabetes and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease were identified on a project-specific basis. Diagnoses were ascertained using ICD and CPT codes, with laboratory values and medication data extracted separately; where appropriate, these data sources were combined using validated algorithms where possible41–43. Automated evaluation of laboratory value distributions and summary statistics is reviewed and compared to national data sources. In addition, data are linked to national disease registries such as the United States Renal Data System (USRDS) and the Social Security Administration Death Index (SSDI).
