Procedural Characteristics and Safety of Slender Percutaneous Coronary Intervention: Slender Is Better
Authors: Fuminobu Yoshimachi, Kyohei Yamaji, Shun Kohsaka, Yota Kawamura, Satoshi Kasai, Toshiharu Fujii, Hirofumi Nagamatsu, Kazushige Amano, Yuka Karasawa, Shinichiro Yamada, Yoshihisa Nakagawa, Yuji Ikari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesWe aimed to examine the procedural characteristics and safety profile of slender percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) using a 5 Fr or smaller sheath.BackgroundSmaller catheter size has been associated with fewer complications in PCI.MethodsOur retrospective observational study included 259,401 cases of stable elective PCI from the Japan Percutaneous Coronary Intervention Registry between 2021 and 2022. Based on sheath size, cases were categorized into the slender group (5 Fr or smaller, n = 28,300) and the non‐slender group (6 Fr or larger, n = 231,101).ResultsThe slender group was associated with more radial access (88.6% vs. 74.2%: p < 0.001), fewer PCI failures (1.7% vs. 2.8%, p < 0.001), shorter procedural times (86.1 ± 47.2 min vs. 103.0 ± 65.1 min, p < 0.001), lower contrast medium usage (104.0 ± 54.8 mL vs. 126.0 ± 69.0 mL, p < 0.001), and shorter fluoroscopy times (25.9 ± 20.0 min vs. 32.4 ± 26.9 min, p < 0.001). Additionally, the slender group experienced fewer complications, including procedure‐related myocardial infarction (odds ratio [OR] 1.25, p = 0.004), cardiac tamponade (OR 1.52, p = 0.048), heart failure or cardiogenic shock (OR 1.57, p < 0.001), stent thrombosis (OR 2.42, p = 0.02), emergency surgery (OR 3.45, p = 0.02), and bleeding complications at both the access (OR 3.16, p < 0.001) and non‐access sites (OR 1.94, p = 0.02). Although the in‐hospital mortality rate was slightly lower in the slender group, this difference was not statistically significant (OR 1.11, p = 0.39).ConclusionsSlender PCI using a 5 Fr or smaller sheath was associated with a favorable safety profile, shorter procedural times, and fewer complications compared to larger sheath sizes.

Introduction

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has become an established treatment for ischemic heart disease. The safety and efficacy of PCI have been enhanced by novel device technologies, including drug‐eluting stents (DES) and other specialized tools that have led to improved success rates, particularly with regard to complex lesions. However, PCI is still associated with certain complications, which warrant the development of effective strategies to mitigate these risks. Hemorrhagic and other complications can pose a significant threat to patient prognosis. Thus, current guidelines recommend the use of transradial access (TRA) to reduce the incidence of bleeding complications and acute kidney injury [1, 2, 3].

In recent years, small outer diameter sheaths, such as the GlideSheath Slender (Terumo Co., Tokyo, Japan), have been developed to minimize the risk of radial artery occlusion. This sheath features a thin‐walled design, offering the same internal diameter as conventional sheaths but with an outer diameter that is approximately 0.5 Fr thinner [4]. Studies have shown that slender sheaths are associated with reduced radial artery occlusion rates and shorter hemostasis times [5]. Several manufacturers now provide similar thin‐walled sheaths, collectively referred to as “slender sheaths.” Historically, if a large 7 Fr sheath is required during a procedure, transfemoral access (TFA) is often selected by the surgeon due to limitations associated with radial artery diameter in most patients. However, the advent of slender sheaths has expanded the use of TRA, even in cases where larger guiding catheters (GCs) are needed [6].

Before the widespread adoption of TRA for all PCI procedures, earlier studies found that larger diameter catheters increased the risk of surgical complications and negatively impacted patient prognosis [7]. However, there remains a lack of large‐scale data specifically investigating the safety and reduced invasiveness of slender sheaths and GCs (the “slender system”), particularly now when TRA is the preferred treatment of choice. This study aims to evaluate whether the use of slender sheaths and GCs in stable elective PCI procedures provides a safer, less invasive alternative compared to conventional larger diameter systems.

Methods

DATA Source

The Japan Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (J‐PCI) registry, a national clinical database (NCD) that was established and maintained by the Cardiovascular Intervention and Therapeutics Society, provided the data for this study. This registry aims to compile foundational statistics for PCI procedures conducted across Japan. Currently, it includes data from over 200,000 cases that are collected annually from approximately 900 institutions, which account for more than 90% of all PCI‐performing hospitals in the country [8]. Since participation in the J‐PCI registry is mandatory for all hospitals seeking accreditation for interventional cardiology or its renewal, the data is highly comprehensive.

The research protocol of the J‐PCI registry was approved by a third‐party central ethics committee (Japan Clinical Research Promotion Network, Osaka, Japan) and conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. Due to the complete anonymization of data and the observational nature of the research, the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

Study Population

A total of 479,562 cases was registered in the J‐PCI Registry from 2021 to 2022. After cases with unknown sheath sizes (n = 31,079) and sheathless methods (3837) were excluded, 444,016 cases were ultimately analyzed. Sheathless GCs were excluded not only because of their differing outer diameters but also due to the lack of evidence comparing PCI with traditional GCs and sheathless PCI. Additionally, sheathless PCI may present certain unique complications that have not been documented in detail.

Variables and Outcomes

TRA was selected when the operator deemed it necessary. In Japan, there are no specific criteria for selecting the TRA approach. However, patients with a weak palpable radial artery pulse, patients with hemodialysis shunts, and patients with vessel diameters that may be too small for the sheaths tend to be excluded. Additionally, some facilities have policies that prioritize the femoral artery approach. In any case, the choice of access site is based on the NCD data documentation, and the selection criteria remain unclear.

Each complication was defined according to the standards of the J‐PCI registry. In‐hospital mortality was defined as any death occurring during hospitalization, regardless of its relationship to the PCI procedure or the time elapsed since the procedure. Procedure‐related myocardial infarction was defined according to the Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction, which includes both Q wave and non‐Q wave infarctions and is defined as troponin T elevation to five times the upper normal limit. Cardiac tamponade was defined as the presence of new pericardial effusion that caused hemodynamic instability and required drainage. Cardiogenic shock was described as hypotension that was unresponsive to intravenous fluids (systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg or mean blood pressure < 60 mmHg) and required circulatory support or inotropic agents. Stent thrombosis, categorized as hospital‐onset, was defined according to the Academic Research Consortium criteria. Emergency surgery was defined as any unexpected surgical intervention that was required during the same hospitalization period. Bleeding complications that required transfusions were classified by site, with access site bleeding defined as hemorrhage or hematoma at the puncture site (e.g., femoral or radial arteries) during or after the procedure, and non‐access site bleeding defined as hemorrhage or hematoma not related to the puncture site. Other complications were defined as any adverse events not falling under the previous categories but deemed by the physician to be disadvantageous to the patient.

Statistics

To plan a comparative study of complications, it was necessary to exclude cases where the pathological condition could independently influence prognosis or complications. Therefore, conditions that significantly impacted survival and complications, such as ST elevation myocardial infarction (93,677 cases), non‐ST elevation myocardial infarction (32,561 cases), unstable angina pectoris (56,477 cases), preoperative cardiopulmonary arrest (1,153 cases), and cardiogenic shock (747 cases), were excluded from the J‐PCI Registry data for this analysis. This resulted in the inclusion of 259,401 cases of stable elective PCI in the study.

Based on sheath size, the cases were divided into the slender group, with sheath sizes of 3, 4, and 5 Fr (n = 28,300), and the non‐slender group, with sheath sizes of 6, 7, and 8 Fr (n = 231,101). The two groups were compared with regard to in‐hospital mortality, procedure‐related myocardial infarction, cardiac tamponade, heart failure or cardiogenic shock requiring mechanical circulatory support or inotropic agents, stent thrombosis, emergency surgery, bleeding complications requiring transfusion at the access site, bleeding complications requiring transfusion at non‐access sites, and other complications (Figure 1).

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the Student's t‐test. We constructed logistic regression models to estimate the effect of slender PCI versus non‐slender PCI. To adjust for the between‐the‐group differences in baseline characteristics and preprocedural conditions, we included the following covariates into multivariable models based on clinical relevance: age, male gender, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, smoking, chronic renal failure, PCI history, bypass history, previous heart failure, previous myocardial infarction, chronic lung disease, and peripheral vascular disease.

Discussion

During the initial era of PCI implementation, 8 Fr catheters were the standard tool of choice. Advances in interventional devices, such as low‐profile balloons and stents, have facilitated a global shift toward smaller catheters, predominantly 6 Fr, to minimize bleeding complications and reduce contrast media usage. This shift has promoted the adoption of even smaller sheaths and catheters, marking the emergence of slender PCI. In our analysis of a large‐scale nationwide registry database, slender PCI using 5 Fr or smaller sheaths accounted for 11% of elective PCI cases. The slender group demonstrated significantly lower rates for in‐hospital complications, including access site bleeding, non‐access site bleeding, procedure‐related myocardial infarction, heart failure, stent thrombosis, and emergency surgery. Although differences in in‐hospital mortality between the slender and non‐slender groups were not statistically significant in this low‐risk population, a trend toward reduced mortality was observed. These findings therefore highlight the safety of slender PCI and support its expanded use in clinical practice.

In our study, one of the major advantages of slender PCI was a lower risk of bleeding complications that required blood transfusions. To mitigate the risk of bleeding, various control measures like TRA are recommended by international clinical practice guidelines [1, 2, 3]. However, TRA is not universally applicable, and there are interhospital variations for TRA implementation, such as those pertaining to the usage of large‐sized GCs. Our findings emphasize the importance of assessing vascular diameter with ultrasound before planning the procedural strategy [9, 10]. Utilizing small‐diameter sheaths like the GlideSheath Slender can facilitate the proactive selection of TRA where feasible. Currently, preprocedural radial artery ultrasound measurements are performed at a limited number of facilities, but broader implementation of this practice may be warranted to prevent the development of clinical complications.

In this study, the non‐slender group included a higher proportion of patients with complex medical conditions, such as those undergoing dialysis. In addition, procedures in this group often involved higher rates of lesion debulking. Despite adjusting for possible confounders, the treatment of more complex lesions—including calcified lesions—may have contributed to the higher rates of PCI failure, myocardial infarction, cardiac tamponade, heart failure, and emergency bypass surgery observed in this group. Previous reports have also linked higher contrast agent use with increased risks of post‐procedural complications, which can negatively impact patient prognosis [11]. The slender approach can mitigate these risks by minimizing the usage of the contrast agent. Thus, broader implementation of slender PCI may help reduce complications associated with complex lesions and high contrast agent usage, ultimately leading to improved patient outcomes.

Previously, when 5 Fr and 6 Fr catheters were compared in TRA procedures, significant differences were found in contrast medium usage, but no detailed analyses of complications were conducted. Investigators reported a TRA rate of 79.8%, with 5 Fr and 4 Fr sheaths used in 81.9% of cases, and did not observe any major adverse cardiovascular events or bleeding complications that required transfusions. [12] By leveraging our large dataset, we were able to assess actual complication rates and adjust for known confounders, thereby providing a more comprehensive analysis. In conjunction with previous studies, our findings suggest that selecting TRA and reducing catheter size by even 1 Fr may significantly decrease complication rates. This hypothesis warrants further investigation, although it may be challenging in the current era of PCI where complication rates are already low.

The sheathless system was excluded from this study due to uncertainty about its impact on complications. However, it should be noted that the smallest diameter device currently available, the 5 Fr sheathless GC whose outer diameter is the same as that of the 3 Fr sheath, is being used for treatment. In a registry study using these devices [13], the radial rate was 90.4%, and there were zero cases of death, myocardial infarction, stroke, bleeding complications requiring transfusion, or contrast‐induced nephropathy. While further comparison of sheathless and sheathed systems is necessary, this report suggests that the use of smaller devices can minimize vascular trauma.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, since this was a retrospective observational study, we cannot eliminate biases related to case selection, lesion characteristics, and operator treatment strategies. Second, in daily clinical practice, even when treating the same patient, different facilities and operators may adopt entirely different strategies, techniques, and devices. Thus, our findings can provide objective observational data on the current situation in Japan but may have limited generalizability to other settings. Third, this study focused exclusively on elective PCI for stable angina, which has a lower risk of mortality and complications. Therefore, future studies should include ACS cases to evaluate the safety and efficacy of slender PCI in a broader patient population.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that slender PCI using 5 Fr or smaller sheaths was associated with fewer procedure‐related complications, including procedure‐related myocardial infarction, heart failure, stent thrombosis, emergency surgery, and bleeding complications. Although slender PCI using 5 Fr or smaller sheaths can be technically challenging, it is recommended for broader clinical application, given its potential benefits in reducing various complications.

Impact of Daily Practice

This study suggests that using a 5 Fr or smaller sheath for slender PCI may improve procedural safety and reduce complications. Broader adoption of this strategy, especially in stable patients, could enhance outcomes without compromising success and may encourage the use of smaller sheath sizes in daily practice.
