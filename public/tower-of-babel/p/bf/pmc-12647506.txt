Evaluation of Event-Free Survival as a Surrogate Endpoint for Overall Survival in Muscle-Invasive Bladder Cancer Following Neoadjuvant Treatment: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Cora N Sternberg, Patrick Squires, Yan Song, Aozhou Wu, Yipeng Gao, Ritesh S Kataria, Haojie Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEvent-free survival (EFS), a common endpoint in neoadjuvant/perioperative oncology trials, allows for accelerated treatment evaluation while awaiting results of overall survival (OS). However, the surrogacy of EFS for OS in muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) has yet to be established. This meta-analysis evaluated EFS as a surrogate endpoint for OS in neoadjuvant-treated MIBC.MethodsA systematic literature review identified neoadjuvant or perioperative clinical trials for MIBC reporting both EFS and OS. Treatment effect association was evaluated by assessing the association of the hazard ratio (HR) of EFS with that of OS. Survival outcome associations were evaluated by assessing the associations of survival rates and median survival times between EFS and OS, respectively. These associations were meta-analyzed using weighted linear regression. The strength of association was quantified using coefficient of determination (R2).ResultsSeven eligible trials were identified for the analysis of treatment effect association and 17 trials were included in the analyses of survival outcome associations. Strong association was observed between EFS and OS at treatment effect level (R2 = 0.80 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.38–0.99]). Consistently, significant survival outcome associations were observed between 3-year EFS and 5-year OS (R2 = 0.80 [95% CI: 0.28–0.93]) and between median EFS time and median OS time (R2 = 0.97 [95% CI: 0.45–1.00]).ConclusionsEFS is strongly associated with OS in respect to treatment effects and survival outcome measures in clinical trials for MIBC following neoadjuvant treatment. EFS can be considered as a surrogate endpoint for OS when evaluating neoadjuvant treatments for MIBC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40487-025-00399-3.Keywords: Muscle-invasive bladder cancer, Neoadjuvant treatment, Event-free survival, Overall survival, Surrogate endpoints

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Overall survival (OS) is the gold standard endpoint in oncology trials but often requires extended follow-up, particularly in early-stage diseases.Event-free survival (EFS) is commonly used as an intermediate endpoint in neoadjuvant/perioperative trials for muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC); however, its surrogacy for OS in these settings has not been fully established.This study systematically reviewed and analyzed 17 clinical trials of neoadjuvant/perioperative treatments for MIBC, evaluating the association between EFS and OS using both treatment effect measures (hazard ratios) and survival outcome measures (landmark rates and medians). What was learned from the study? Strong correlations were observed between EFS and OS across both treatment effect and survival outcome measures.Our findings suggest that EFS is a valid surrogate endpoint for OS in the neoadjuvant MIBC setting.Given its shorter follow-up requirement, using EFS as a primary endpoint in clinical trials may accelerate the regulatory evaluation of novel therapies and facilitate earlier patient access to effective treatments.

Introduction

Bladder cancer is the sixth most commonly diagnosed cancer in the USA, with over 84,800 newly diagnosed cases and 17,400 deaths estimated in 2025 [1, 2]. Among patients diagnosed with bladder cancer, approximately 20% present with a tumor invading the muscle layer of the bladder wall (stage T2–T4) without distant metastasis, a condition referred to as muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) [3–5]. With the current treatment options, approximately 50% of patients with MIBC will eventually develop distant metastasis, at which stage the 5-year overall survival (OS) is less than 10% [2, 4, 6, 7].

For patients with MIBC eligible for cisplatin-based chemotherapy, neoadjuvant treatment followed by radical cystectomy (RC) or RC plus pelvic lymph node dissection is the standard of care [8, 9]. While cisplatin-based chemotherapy has been the most used neoadjuvant regimen in MIBC for decades, the regimen has a limited benefit compared with RC only [8], associating with an absolute increase of 5% in OS and 9% benefit in disease-free survival (DFS) at 5 years compared with no neoadjuvant treatment [10]. What is more, up to 50% of patients are cisplatin-ineligible [11–13], leaving a considerable gap of unmet needs in the neoadjuvant setting. In addition, patients with adverse pathological findings in the cystectomy specimen despite prior neoadjuvant treatment are at high risk of metastatic recurrence and may benefit from subsequent adjuvant therapy [9], which has driven interest in perioperative therapy as well.

The neoadjuvant treatment landscape for MIBC has expanded beyond conventional cisplatin-based chemotherapy to include novel approaches such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, targeted therapies, and antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) [8, 14]. Clinical investigations are actively exploring the use of these novel agents either alone or in combination, in neoadjuvant and perioperative settings [15–20]. For example, the KEYNOTE-866 [18] and KEYNOTE-B15 [16] trials are assessing the perioperative use of an immune checkpoint inhibitor combined with chemotherapy and with an ADC, respectively, in cisplatin-eligible patients with MIBC. In parallel, the ENERGIZE trial is investigating a triple combination of an immune checkpoint inhibitor, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy in patients with MIBC [15].

An important treatment goal in MIBC is to prolong survival, with OS serving as the gold standard endpoint in relevant oncology trials [21]. However, measuring OS, especially for early-stage cancers such as MIBC, usually requires extended follow-up times and relatively large sample sizes. The rapid advancement of novel therapies underscores the need for evaluating efficacy on the basis of clinical endpoints that can be reached with shorter follow-up times and smaller sample sizes. Such endpoints can expedite the drug evaluation process and shorten the time interval between clinical trial initiation and approval. Indeed, intermediate clinical endpoints have been increasingly accepted by regulatory agencies for accelerated approval or conditional approval of oncology drugs if they can reasonably predict long-term benefits (i.e., act as surrogate endpoints for OS) [22, 23].

Event-free survival (EFS), a composite endpoint that typically measures time to disease progression, local or distant recurrence, or death, is recommended by the Society for Immunotherapy of Cancer & the International Bladder Cancer Group as an intermediate measure of treatment benefit [9]. It can demonstrate superiority of a new antineoplastic therapy, particularly if the magnitude of the effect is large and the therapy has an acceptable benefit–risk profile [21]. EFS has been increasingly used in lieu of OS as the primary endpoint to evaluate novel neoadjuvant and perioperative treatments in MIBC trials [15–20, 24]. Unlike OS, EFS is not affected by subsequent therapy received after treatment failure and can therefore more accurately measure the efficacy of the treatment of interest [25]. In addition, the shorter follow-up time required for EFS to establish treatment benefits compared with OS [22] may help expedite regulatory and reimbursement decision-making and enable earlier access to effective novel therapies for patients with MIBC.

To date, the establishment of EFS as a surrogate for OS in these MIBC settings has yet to undergo appropriate validation. This study aimed to validate the surrogacy of EFS for OS in MIBC following neoadjuvant treatment by assessing the associations between EFS and OS in terms of both treatment effects and survival outcomes via a meta-analytic approach.

Methods

Search Strategy and Study Selection Criteria

We conducted a systematic literature review by searching MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews for full-text articles of clinical trials or related systematic literature reviews and meta-analyses evaluating neoadjuvant/perioperative treatments for MIBC published in the English language from database inception to 12 March 2025. Additionally, we also reviewed conference abstracts and presentations published from 1 January 2017 to 12 March 2025, from the aforementioned databases as well as the Northern Light Life Sciences Conference Abstracts database and the Database of Abstracts of Reviews of Effects. The search terms included keywords related to MIBC, neoadjuvant treatments, and relevant survival outcomes (i.e., EFS, progression-free survival [PFS], recurrence/relapse-free survival [RFS], DFS, and OS). Two independent reviewers screened the retrieved articles. Any discrepancies between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion or reconciled by a third reviewer. Published systematic literature reviews and meta-analyses of clinical trials were cross-checked to identify additional trial publications if deemed eligible. The systematic literature reviews and meta-analysis studies themselves were not included in the surrogate outcome analyses. Data from the most recent publications were included when multiple eligible publications were available for the same trial.

Studies were considered eligible if (1) study types were clinical trials, systematic literature reviews, or meta-analyses; (2) study population was adult patients with MIBC (i.e., clinical stage T2-T4a, N0-N1, M0) who received any neoadjuvant treatment (e.g., chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy) prior to RC or perioperative treatments; and (3) study outcomes included both EFS and OS. In addition, to be included in the treatment effect association analyses (see the “Statistical Analysis” section), studies must be randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and reported hazard ratios (HRs) or Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves for comparisons between treatment arms for both EFS and OS. To be included in the analyses of survival outcome associations (see the “Statistical Analysis” section), studies must be RCTs or single-arm trials that reported landmark survival rates or median survival times or had corresponding KM curves for both EFS and OS.

Study Outcomes

In this study, the intermediate and long-term outcomes of interest were EFS and OS, respectively. Considering different studies may use different terms to describe endpoints similar to EFS, during the systematic literature review process, we considered a broader set of outcome terminologies related to EFS to capture survival outcomes with definitions pertaining to disease progression, local or distant recurrence, and death as the event of interest, which included EFS, PFS, DFS, and RFS (Supplementary Table S1). OS was defined as the time to death from any cause. Across included studies, the start of follow-up for EFS and OS was mostly consistent and occurred on or before the initiation of neoadjuvant treatment.

Statistical Analysis

Treatment Effect Association

The association between the treatment effects on EFS and the treatment effects on OS was evaluated. Specifically, treatment effects were quantified as the HR between the two randomized arms in an RCT, which were extracted directly from trial publications. If HR was not reported, the HR and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were estimated using a Cox proportional hazards model based on reconstructed patient-level data obtained by digitizing the corresponding published KM curves for the EFS and OS if available.

Weighted linear regression was performed using natural logarithm of HR (ln[HR]) of EFS as the independent variable and ln(HR) of OS as the dependent variable, with weights proportional to the inverse variance of ln(HR) of EFS from each trial. Coefficient of determination (R2) and its 95% CI were calculated to measure the strength of the association between EFS and OS. R2 ranges from 0 to 1 and measures how well the predicted values fit the observed data, with a value closer to 1 indicating a stronger correlation between the dependent variable and the independent variable. The 95% CI was estimated using a bootstrap method with 10,000 iterations.

The surrogate threshold effect (STE) for HR of EFS was calculated as the minimum treatment benefit on the surrogate outcome (i.e., EFS) that would be necessary to expect an OS benefit (HR < 1) with 95% confidence (i.e., calculated based on the 95% CI for the EFS-OS relationship).

Survival Outcome Associations

The analyses of survival outcome associations assessed the associations between clinically meaningful landmark EFS and OS rates or between median EFS and OS times in each treatment arm of a trial, respectively (i.e., each treatment arm in a RCT or a single-arm trial contributed to one observation in the analysis). The associations assessed were 3-year EFS rate versus 5-year OS rate, median EFS time versus median OS time, and 1-year EFS rate versus 3-year OS rate. Landmark survival rates and median survival times of EFS and OS were either extracted directly from trial publications or obtained by digitizing the corresponding published KM curves if not directly reported.

Weighted linear regression was performed using landmark rates of EFS as the independent variable and landmark rates of OS as the dependent variable, with weights proportional to the sample size of each included treatment arm. The R2 and its 95% CI were reported. The association between median survival time of EFS and that of OS was analyzed using the same analytical approach.

Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of the main results by assessing the potential impact of heterogeneity across the included trials. Specifically, subgroup analyses were conducted among the following four subsets of studies for both analyses of treatment effect association and of survival outcome associations: (1) studies using neoadjuvant therapy only, (2) studies using cisplatin-based chemotherapy alone as neoadjuvant treatment, (3) studies with only patients who had clinical stage T2-4N0M0 tumor, and (4) studies using RC as the definitive treatment for all patients.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

This systematic review and meta-analysis conformed to the PRISMA guidelines. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Results

Our systematic literature review identified 570 publications, of which 21 met the eligibility criteria [17, 26–45]. Data of 17 eligible trials were extracted from the 21 publications, as two trials had results reported in two publications each [26, 27, 34, 40] and another trial was reported in three publications [39, 41, 43]. The surrogate endpoint analyses included all 17 trials, comprising 7 RCTs and 10 single-arm trials (Fig. 1).

Treatment Effect Association

Seven eligible RCTs were included in the evaluation of treatment effect association between EFS and OS with a total sample size of 3011 patients (Table 1). The ln(HR) of EFS was significantly associated with the ln(HR) of OS, with an estimated coefficient of 0.94 (p = 0.007) and an R2 of 0.80 (95% CI: 0.38–0.99) (Fig. 2). The STE for HR of EFS was estimated to be 0.87, which represents the minimum EFS benefit (maximum HR for EFS) required to expect an OS benefit (HR < 1) with 95% confidence.

Survival Outcome Associations

Twenty-four treatment arms from 17 eligible trials were included in the evaluation of survival outcome associations between EFS and OS (Table 2). Specifically, 15 arms from 9 trials with a total sample size of 3152 were included in the 3-year EFS versus 5-year OS analysis; 5 arms from 3 trials with a total sample size of 1134 were included in the median EFS versus median OS analysis; and 24 arms from 17 trials with a total sample size of 3596 were included in the 1-year EFS versus 3-year OS analysis.

There were significant associations between EFS and OS across all three analyses of survival outcome associations (Fig. 3). Specifically, the association between 3-year EFS and 5-year OS had a coefficient of 0.87 (p < 0.001) and an R2 of 0.80 (95% CI: 0.28–0.93); the association between median EFS time and median OS time had a coefficient of 0.75 (p < 0.001) and an R2 of 0.97 (95% CI: 0.45–1.00); and the association between 1-year EFS and 3-year OS had the coefficient of 0.95 (p < 0.001) and an R2 of 0.64 (95% CI: 0.11–0.86).

Sensitivity Analyses

In sensitivity analyses evaluating the treatment effect association between EFS and OS, we observed moderate to strong correlations between HR of EFS and HR of OS, with R2 ranging from 0.61 to 0.94 in the 4 subsamples (i.e., studies using neoadjuvant therapy only, studies using cisplatin-based chemotherapy alone as neoadjuvant treatment, studies with only patients who had clinical stage T2-4N0M0 tumor, and studies using RC as the definitive treatment; Supplementary Fig. S1).

Similarly, sensitivity analyses on survival outcome associations showed moderate to strong correlations between 3-year EFS and 5-year OS, with R2 of 0.66, 0.56, 0.57, 0.66 among studies using only neoadjuvant therapy, studies using cisplatin-based chemotherapy alone, studies with only T2-4N0M0 stage patients, and studies using RC as the definitive treatment, respectively. A strong correlation was observed between median EFS time and median OS time (R2 of 1.00) in analyses restricted to patients with T2–T4N0M0 tumors and those who received RC as the definitive treatment; such analysis was not feasible for the other two subsamples owing to insufficient number of eligible studies. Although 1-year EFS was also correlated with 3-year OS in the four subsamples, the strength of the correlation was weak to moderate (R2 of 0.53, 0.35, 0.25, and 0.21, respectively) (Supplementary Table S2).

Discussion

Using data from 17 published clinical trials of neoadjuvant treatments for MIBC, our study found consistently significant associations between EFS and OS for both treatment effect measures (i.e., HR; R2 = 0.80) and survival outcome measures (e.g., 3-year EFS and 5-year OS, R2 = 0.80; median survival times, R2 = 0.97). The observed associations were largely consistent across sensitivity analyses among the subsets of studies that confined to patients received neoadjuvant therapy only, received cisplatin-based chemotherapy alone as neoadjuvant treatment, had clinical stage T2-4N0M0 tumor, or using RC as the only definitive treatment. In addition, the STE for HR of EFS suggested that observing a treatment benefit on EFS with an HR of 0.87 or better (i.e., HR ≤ 0.87) could imply an OS benefit in future trials. Our findings collectively established the strong associations between EFS and OS in MIBC following neoadjuvant treatment.

The findings of our study stemmed from a rigorous literature review process to ensure the overall comparability of the included trials, followed by a comprehensive evaluation of EFS as a surrogate endpoint for OS in MIBC following neoadjuvant treatment, utilizing standard frameworks for establishing outcome surrogacy. Specifically, the treatment effect association analysis determines whether the treatment effect observed on an intermediate outcome (i.e., EFS) within a trial could reliably predict the treatment effect on a long-term outcome (i.e., OS). Such evidence is considered the highest level in the hierarchy of the methodological framework used to validate surrogate endpoints in oncology [23]. This framework is recommended by health technology assessment and regulatory agencies such as the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence in the UK and the Institute for Quality and Efficiency in Health Care in Germany [46, 47]. Notably, Ciani and colleagues have previously suggested that an association with R2 > 0.65 can be used to identify good surrogates [22], and thus the results of our study support that the treatment effect on EFS is a reliable surrogate to predict the treatment effect on OS in MIBC following neoadjuvant treatment. In addition, the analyses of survival outcome associations further support the utility of EFS in forecasting longer-term OS outcomes in this setting. The overall consistent results in the main versus sensitivity analyses indicate that any potential heterogeneity across included trials may have limited impact on the observed associations between EFS and OS.

The results of this study may be generalizable to broad patient populations receiving neoadjuvant/perioperative treatments for MIBC. The main analysis included both trials conducted in the neoadjuvant setting only and those in the perioperative setting, where the intervention spanned both the neoadjuvant and adjuvant phases. Notably, sensitivity analyses restricted to neoadjuvant-only trials and to trials using cisplatin-based chemotherapy alone as neoadjuvant treatment yielded results largely consistent with the main analyses. Nonetheless, as the majority of included trials focused on neoadjuvant chemotherapy, future analyses incorporating a greater number of trials across both treatment settings and involving diverse therapeutic regimens are warranted to further evaluate the robustness of the surrogacy findings from this study. Additionally, the inclusion of single-center–based single-arm trials in addition to major RCTs on neoadjuvant/perioperative MIBC treatments also enriched the patient profiles to resemble those encountered in common clinical settings, which is a strength of this study.

Interpretation of the study results should consider several limitations. First, this study was conducted using aggregated trial data rather than individual patient data (IPD), which limits the ability to account for patient-level variability and potential confounding. However, surrogacy evaluations based on aggregate data are commonly conducted, in part owing to restricted access to IPD from clinical trials. Over time, this approach has become well established and widely accepted, with over 70% of surrogate-endpoint validation studies relying on published aggregate data and meta-analyses [48]. In addition, applying consistent population criteria and restricting to trials with similar treatment settings helps reduce cross-trial heterogeneity and mitigates the risk of bias in the absence of IPD. Second, to preserve sample size and minimize selection bias, we applied a broad definition of EFS that included related endpoints such as RFS, PFS, and DFS. While this approach increases generalizability, the heterogeneity in EFS definitions may have affected the strength and consistency of the observed associations between EFS and OS. In addition, the precision of the estimates could have been limited by the number of eligible studies included. For instance, variations in the strength of association observed across sensitivity analyses are likely owing to limited sample size and insufficient statistical power. Finally, owing to the relatively small number of included studies, covariate adjustment in the meta-regression was not feasible.

Conclusions

In summary, we found consistently strong associations between EFS and OS in terms of treatment effects and survival outcome measures in clinical trials, providing robust evidence that EFS is a valid surrogate endpoint for OS in MIBC following neoadjuvant treatment. The study results support that EFS may be used to assist regulatory and reimbursement decisions regarding new therapies in the neoadjuvant setting of MIBC. The findings are generally not sensitive to treatment type and may be generalizable to broad patient populations.
