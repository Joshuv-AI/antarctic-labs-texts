Evolution of distinct subclones in a CALM-AF10–driven AML murine model results in leukemias with different phenotypes
Authors: Niloofar Zandvakili, Hui Mei Lee, Rhea H. Desai, Alyona Oryshchuk, Peter J. Browett, Purvi M. Kakadia, Stefan K. Bohlander
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR:

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is an aggressive, genetically complex malignancy with a high rate of relapse, therapy resistance, and clonal evolution.1 AML exhibits a complex clonal architecture, often with multiple subclones (SCs) that change under selective pressures.2

Relapse is driven by leukemia stem cells (LSCs), which often survive chemotherapy.3,4 Their phenotype and biology vary significantly across different leukemias.5 However, the origin, identity, and characteristics of LSCs remain poorly understood.6, 7, 8

We studied a CALM::AF10 fusion gene–driven murine leukemia model. The CALM::AF10 fusion results from the rare but recurring t(10;11)(p13;q14) translocation found in AML, acute lymphoblastic leukemia, and lymphoma.9, 10, 11 The first CALM::AF10 murine bone marrow (BM) transplantation leukemia model had a predominantly myeloid phenotype with the LSCs enriched in a small myeloid marker–negative but early B-cell marker–positive subpopulation (Mac1−/B220+).12

More recently, we established a retroviral murine BM transplantation leukemia model using the CALM::AF10 minimal fusion (CA::MF)13 containing the leukemogenic domains of CALM::AF10.14 We developed 3 leukemia families: biphenotypic (L1: B220+/Mac1+), and 2 myeloid leukemias (L2 and L7; Mac1+/B220−). Consistent with previous findings, the LSC frequency (LSC-F) was >10-fold higher in L1, suggesting that CALM::AF10 LSCs tend to have high B220 expression.12,13

Here, we characterized the L7 myeloid leukemia family, which had exhibited progressively shorter latency upon serial transplantation.13 Whole-exome sequencing (WES) and analysis of retroviral insertion sites revealed that a single initially transduced cell underwent clonal evolution, resulting in distinct subclones (SCs) and in leukemias with different immunophenotypes and latencies. This evolution occurred without external forcing, suggesting that this model is a valuable tool for studying clonal evolution, a key challenge in AML.

LSC-Fs were determined using limiting dilution assays (LDAs) by transplantation of leukemia cells into sublethally irradiated C57BL/6 mice.15,16 Leukemia development and engraftment were monitored for up to 7 months (supplemental Methods; Tables 1-4).

The LSC-F of the primary L7 leukemia was 1:2339 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1:795-1:6885).13 Tertiary leukemias were established by performing an LDA on BM cells from a bulk-transplanted secondary leukemia (L7S2), for which the LSC-F was estimated at 1:485 (95% CI, 1:237-1:993). Tertiary-LDA leukemia latencies were 23 to 193 days (Figures 1A-B; supplemental Table 1). Tertiary LSC-F was estimated at 1:3175 (95% CI, 1:1073-1:9395) by performing an LDA on a tertiary-LDA leukemia, which, based on the parent LSC-F, was likely derived from a single LSC (L7S2-T20; supplemental Table 2). Single-cell BM suspensions were stained with LIVE/DEAD Blue fluorescent antibodies (Mac1, Gr1, B220, CD19, CD3, CD4, and CD8; supplemental Table 6) and analyzed on an LSRII flow cytometer and FlowJo (version 10).

Flow cytometry was performed to assess myeloid (Mac1, Gr1) and lymphoid (B220, CD19, CD3, CD4, CD8) marker expression (Figure 1C and supplemental Table 7) and showed that all L7-derived leukemias had intermediate to high Mac1 (mean ± standard deviation, 93.8% ± 9.8%) and Gr1 (88.4% ± 13.8%), and no or very low (≤10%) CD19 or T-cell marker expression. Primary and secondary leukemias showed low B220 (<7%; Figure 1C; green bar), bulk-tertiary leukemias displayed higher B220 (20% ± 4%), whereas tertiary-LDA leukemias exhibited variable B220 expression (2%-85%; Figure 1C; red bars). This heterogeneity prompted us to investigate the relationship between B220 expression and LSC-F.

LDAs were performed on B220+, B220−, and unsorted cells from 2 tertiary-LDA leukemias (supplemental Tables 3-4); L7S2-T15 (50% B220+; 27-day latency) and L7S2-T18 (2% B220+; 69-day latency), each likely derived from 1 to 5 LSCs, based on the parent LSC-F. LSCs were enriched in the B220+ fraction of L7S2-T15 and, surprisingly, the B220− fraction of L7S2-T18 (Figure 1D-E). The LSC-F of unsorted cells was comparable between leukemias (L7S2-T15, 1:20 386 [95% CI, 1:6332-1:65 633]; L7S2-T18, 1:6509 [95% CI, 1:2061-1:20 558]). Notably, the LSC-F of the B220+ fraction from L7S2-T15 (1:6509 [95% CI, 1:2062-1:20 542]) was similar to that of the B220− fraction of L7S2-T18 (1:2026 [95% CI, 1:626-1:6555]).

Interestingly, leukemias derived from L7S2-T15 B220+ cells showed variable B220 expression (15%-78.8%), whereas those from L7S2-T18 B220− cells retained low B220 expression (<5%; supplemental Table 7; Figure 1F).

Therefore, LSCs from the same parent leukemia display phenotypic heterogeneity, prompting further investigation into their underlying clonal diversity.

WES was then performed on unsorted leukemic BM DNA and matched liver DNA (supplemental Methods).13 WES of primary (L7), secondary (L7S1-S4), and tertiary bulk and LDA leukemias (L7S2-T1 to -T4, -T15, -T17, -T18, and -T19) revealed a founding clone (Ube2i D102N mutation; Figure 2B, gray) present in all L7-family leukemias, and 2 SCs, SC1 (Cdk5rap2 E1257D mutation; Figure 2B, blue) and SC2 (Ttc21a L794fs mutation, Figure 2B, red) in primary L7. In secondary leukemias, SC1 (blue) expanded and SC2 (red) modestly increased (Figure 2B).

Bulk-tertiary leukemias L7S2-T1 to -T4 and L7S2-T15 showed SC1 (blue) expansion, loss of SC2 (red), and, surprisingly, increased B220 expression (16.8%-50%). L7S2-T18 lacked SC1 or SC2 but acquired SC3 (Figure 2B, pink). L7S2-T19 and L7S2-T17 showed SC2 (red) expansion and emergence of minor SC2-1 (Figure 2B, green), whereas SC1 disappeared. L7S2-T19 developed minor SC2-1-1 (Figure 2B, yellow), illustrating ongoing evolution (supplemental Table 8).

We observed that, although LSC-F remained constant during serial transplantation, individual leukemias displayed variable B220 expression and had different latencies.

Our splinkerette PCR and WES data show that the primary L7 leukemia originated from a single retrovirally transduced cell. Previous studies suggest that CALM::AF10–driven leukemias originate from hematopoietic stem cells.18,19 Our results support this assumption because both lymphoid and myeloid phenotypes were observed. We show that the primary L7 leukemia had a founding clone and 2 independent SC1 and SC2, each characterized by different somatic mutations.

During bulk transplantations, SC1 expanded whereas SC2 declined. In the tertiary leukemias, seeded by small cell numbers, the SCs acquire additional mutations indicating continuous evolution and clonal competition. This parallels human AML, in which relapsed disease often has a different mutational pattern and clonal architecture.20, 21, 22

Different LSCs of the L7 family seeded leukemias with distinct phenotypes. Some leukemias had high B220 expression, with short latencies, whereas others had low B220 expression and longer latencies. We also found that in some of the leukemias, LSCs were enriched among B220+ cells as initially reported by Deshpande et al12, whereas in other leukemias, LSCs were present among B220− cells (Figure 2C). The LSC-Fs were similar in the B220+ and B220− populations in different leukemias. Similar correlations between clonal architecture and phenotypic heterogeneity have also been observed in patients with AML.23, 24, 25

Our results demonstrate that the genetic complexity and continuous clonal evolution of AML extend to the LSC level. Our study highlights the need to consider clonal diversity and LSC heterogeneity when developing new AML treatments.
