Posttraumatic stress disorder interventions for children aged zero to six years: a scoping review
Authors: Stella J Murphy, Emma K Malcolmson, Karen McEwan, Patrick J McGrath
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: If left untreated, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) has the potential to cause life-long risks to the health and wellbeing of young, traumatized children. Although DSM-5 diagnostic criteria recognize unique PTSD manifestations in children aged zero to six years, few well-established treatments exist.Objective: This review seeks to explore and synthesize the current literature on PTSD interventions for children aged zero to six years to inform the treatment and future research on effective, age-appropriate treatments.Method: A scoping review methodology was used to undertake this review. Following the creation of a comprehensive search strategy, a literature search utilizing the databases PubMed, CINAHL, and PsycINFO was conducted. A grey literature search was conducted in three different steps: (1) grey database search, (2) custom Google search, and (3) contacting relevant stakeholders/associations. All eligible studies were uploaded to Covidence (2024), a scoping review management service, to efficiently review and remove duplicate studies by two independent reviewers via a two-phase screening process.Results: We identified 37 original research articles that described the treatment of PTSD in children aged six years and under. Trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy (TF-CBT) was the most rigorously studied type of intervention with two published randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Child–parent psychotherapy (CPP), a skills-based form of dyadic psychotherapy, was established, with one published RCT and two large uncontrolled group studies. Most studies involved caregivers in the therapeutic process.Conclusions: It is recommended that methodologically rigorous studies further explore and compare treatment options in PTSD interventions for children aged zero to six years. No treatment recommendations are currently given for children aged zero to two years. TF-CBT is recommended to be the first-line therapy for children aged three to six years with PTSD.KEYWORDS: Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), trauma, treatment, intervention, early childhood, infant, toddler, preschool

Introduction

Trauma exposure is highly prevalent among young children, with estimates indicating that by age four, approximately 26.3% will have experienced a potentially traumatic event (Briggs-Gowan et al., 2010). In some cases, trauma exposure can lead to posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), with a recent systematic review reporting a pooled PTSD prevalence of 21.5% among trauma-exposed children under six and a half years (Woolgar et al., 2021).

PTSD was introduced into the American Psychiatric Association’s Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-III) in 1980. Research conducted since then concluded that the PTSD diagnostic criteria lacked sufficient sensitivity to consistently diagnose PTSD in young children (Scheeringa et al., 2001). Following numerous studies (Scheeringa et al., 1995; Scheeringa et al., 2003; Scheeringa et al., 2012), an alternative set of PTSD diagnostic criteria for children aged zero to six years was developed and incorporated into the DSM-5 (APA, 2013). Specifically, Criterion D and Criterion C in the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for PTSD were combined for young children, meaning one symptom of persistent avoidance of stimuli or negative alteration in cognition and mood were needed to satisfy Criterion C (APA, 2013). Overall, the new taxonomy distinguished the symptomatology of young children with PTSD from that of school-aged children, adolescents, and adults, enabling a more sensitive approach to detect PTSD in this population.

It is important to note that the rapid progression of developmental stages between birth and six years continues to make generalizing PTSD criteria to this population difficult. In toddlers aged one to three years, symptoms such as clinginess, tantrums, and startling are more common following trauma exposure (Danzi et al., 2024); however, these behaviours alone are not sufficient to meet the modified DSM-5 PTSD criteria for young children (APA, 2013). Additionally, toddlers of the same age may reach developmental milestones at different times, influenced by factors such as sex and cultural or national context, potentially impacting the suitability of interventions requiring specific cognitive and behavioural capacities (Ertem et al., 2018). For infants under nine months, some researchers believe they can exhibit conditioned fear responses after trauma, while others argue that current understanding is insufficient to link these responses to diagnosable PTSD symptoms (Blank, 2007). This scientific and developmental uncertainty challenges the validity of proposed PTSD treatments for infants and toddlers. Although a small number of studies have explored treatment in this population (Bhaskaran et al., 2016; Rachamim et al., 2015; Solter, 2007; Swimm, 2018), treatment standards remain unclear. In spite of current diagnostic challenges, the presence of a significant body of literature describing the treatment of PTSD in infants and toddlers prompted its inclusion in this review.

Despite advances in understanding and current challenges in addressing PTSD in children aged zero to six, it remains essential to treat infants, toddlers, and preschoolers who present with PTSD symptoms as early as possible to minimize developmental disruption and long-term mental health risks. The developmental changes that occur between the ages of zero to six are particularly important, with infancy and early childhood widely recognized as some of the most important developmental periods of maturation and organization of the prefrontal cortex, amygdala, and hippocampus (Likhar et al., 2022; Matte-Landry et al., 2022). Child maltreatment beginning in infancy tends to result in poor inhibitory control and working-memory performance, which has been suggested to be attributed to disruptions in normative brain structure and function due to instances of prolonged stress during the early years of life (Cowell et al., 2015). Further, Ogle et al. (2013) reported that adults who experienced their trauma in childhood exhibited more severe symptoms of PTSD and lower subjective happiness compared to those who experienced trauma in adulthood. To minimize the long-term effects of PTSD on young children’s development, it is necessary to implement effective and age-appropriate interventions.

Although there is a clear need for early and effective treatment, documented interventions for PTSD in infants, toddlers, and preschool-aged children remain limited. Le Roux and Cobham (2022) explored PTSD interventions for children under 18 years of age following exposure to a natural disaster. They found that all types of psychological intervention were associated with statistically significant reductions in symptoms of PTSD. However, their study included all youth PTSD experiences and did not address interventions tailored to young children. Stauffer et al. (2020) identified 13 psychological interventions for treating young children exposed to potentially traumatic events. However, their inclusion criteria were based solely on exposure to such events, without focusing on the presence of PTSD symptomatology. They also excluded pharmacological interventions. As a result, the current research question remained unanswered. Lastly, clinical guidelines have previously been published regarding evidence-based treatments for young children exhibiting PTSD symptoms. Scheeringa (2015) presented a clinical guide describing the use of an adapted cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) intervention aiming to meet the needs of three- to six-year-old children with PTSD.

Currently, no literature provides a comprehensive overview of interventions for PTSD in children under six; therefore, the current study aimed to summarize existing interventions and offer guidance for clinicians and future research.

Materials and methods

Eligibility criteria

Studies must have included participants under six-years-old who experienced a traumatic event and exhibited PTSD symptoms or been diagnosed with PTSD. Although the studies may have included children of other ages, the intervention(s) in young children must have been separately explored. All types of interventions were considered, including pharmacotherapy, Play Therapy, CBT, and Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR). Studies may have been conducted in any setting, including primary health facilities, community centres, and in the home. Likewise, studies from all time periods and geographical locations were considered eligible. See Figure 1 for the complete inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Search strategy

Scoping reviews are appropriate when the topic of interest has not been extensively studied and in anticipation of a diverse body of literature (Pham et al., 2014), which were the primary reasons to employ a scoping review for this topic. A scientific librarian aided in drafting the search strategy, which was further refined through team discussion. Three databases were used for the literature search: PubMed, CINAHL, and PsycINFO.

To locate all potentially relevant literature, a grey literature search was conducted in three different steps: (1) grey database search, (2) custom Google search, and (3) contacting relevant stakeholders/associations.

The grey literature database tool developed by the Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health, termed Grey Matters, was used as a guide to search numerous grey literature databases (Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health, 2022). The following categories were searched on Grey Matters: Advisories and Warnings, Background, Clinical Practice Guidelines, Clinical Trials Registries, Databases, Drug Class Reviews, Health Technology Assessment Agencies, and Internet. A preliminary search of each website was completed using the keywords ‘PTSD’, ‘trauma’, and ‘post-traumatic’ to ensure the relevancy of the website. If these keywords yielded no relevant results, meaning no literature within the database referenced trauma, the website was deemed irrelevant and was not further searched. Otherwise, 10 unique keyword combinations were used to search the website (see Figure 2). These combinations included both general and specific treatment terms in an attempt to capture all available data. Due to the variability among grey literature sources, it was decided to systematically search the first five pages of each keyword combination rather than a fixed number of individual results.

Database and grey literature searches were conducted in both May 2023 and September 2024 to ensure all recently published information was captured.

Study selection

Following the literature search, all eligible studies were uploaded to Covidence (2024), a scoping review management software, to review and remove duplicate studies via a two-phase screening process. Firstly, title and abstract screening was conducted to eliminate not meeting the inclusion criteria. All eligible literature continued to full-text screening to confirm their inclusion in the study. Two independent reviews were conducted by separate researchers (the first and second authors). When inconsistencies or disagreements arose, an independent third reviewer arbitrated the dispute (the fourth author). The extraction form for all included studies was developed with guidance from Pollock et al. (2023). Covidence generated a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow-chart that detailed the two-phase screening process of all studies (Page et al., 2021) (Figure 3).

Intervention categorization

Effective treatments for PTSD include learning skills such as breathing or cognitive restructuring and experiences such as forms of imaginal or actual exposure, with some programmes incorporating both approaches (Watkins et al., 2018). Pharmacological therapies have also been used. Given the expected heterogeneity of interventions, each study was categorized into one of the following groups: skills-based, experiences-based, a combination of experiences- and skills-based approaches, or pharmacological therapies. These categories were chosen to reflect common distinctions in clinical practice and PTSD intervention literature, allowing for meaningful comparison of outcomes across differing intervention types.

Results

Demographics

Included ages ranged from four months to six years old. Thirteen studies examined children aged two years or younger, and two included children under the age of one year. Fifteen studies reported the ethnic make-up of their participants. Of these, seven reported ≥50% Caucasian participants, six reported ≥42.2% African American or African participants, and two reported ≥38% Latinx or Latinx biracial participants. One study reported on Syrian and Afghan refugees (n = 10; Lempertz et al., 2020). All RCTs examined both males and females. Of the 14 individual case reports, 11 studied male children. The majority of studies were conducted in the United States (19). Refer to Appendix A for the demographic information of each study.

Study design

The studies varied greatly in their methodological rigour. Of the 37 extracted studies, eight were RCTs and were the only studies to include a comparator group. Ten studies were uncontrolled group studies, five studies were individually analysed case series, and 14 studies were individual case reports. Two studies contained over 100 participants. A total of 24 studies included a follow-up of a minimum of one month to monitor treatment effectiveness. Caregiver involvement was common across most studies, with all but three (Cocco & Sharpe, 1993; Lempertz et al., 2020; Lempertz et al., 2023) incorporating significant parental participation, either through their presence during sessions or by engaging in parenting skills training.

Study quality analyses were presented in Table 2. TF-CBT was the only intervention classified as well-established with two or more independent RCTs supporting its use. Coping with Accident Reactions (CARE), Child–Parent Psychotherapy (CPP), Dyadic Exposure Therapy, the Early Pathways programme, and Stepped-Care TF-CBT were all categorized as probably efficacious. EMDR was categorized as possibly efficacious.

Interventions

Eighteen unique interventions were identified. Refer to Appendix B for detailed information on each intervention.

Skills-based interventions

Eight studies were categorized as presenting interventions that were primarily skills-based. The treatments included CPP, Early Pathways, Preschool Kid’s Club, and Parent–Child Interaction Therapy. Two studies were RCTs, two were uncontrolled group studies, one was a case series, and four were individual case reports.

The goal of CPP was to restore children’s developmental progress by fostering safety and improving the caregiver–child relationship through dyadic psychotherapy sessions. Three studies, including one RCT, described the use of CPP in the treatment of children of two to five years of age. An RCT conducted by Lieberman et al. (2005) and its re-analysis by Ghosh Ippen et al. (2011) concluded that dyads who underwent CPP were significantly less likely to continue meeting PTSD diagnostic criteria post-treatment compared to those who received monthly case management and referrals to community treatment. A large uncontrolled group study (n = 199) examining CPP by Hagan et al. (2017) reported clinically significant improvements in three PTSD domains: reexperiencing, hyperarousal, and avoidance. Notably, female children exhibited greater reductions in reexperiencing (p = .018) and hyperarousal (p < .001) compared to male children. Relatively high attrition was reported, with 42.1% of families (n = 157) dropping out throughout the course of treatment. Lastly, Norlén et al. (2024) reported relative pre- to post-treatment improvements on the Young Child PTSD Checklist (p < .001) in response to CPP, however, symptoms indicating a need for clinical attention were reported for about one-third of the children at both baseline (n = 19) and post-treatment (n = 17).

The Early Pathways programme was examined in a single RCT, aiming to coach parents with clinician feedback to strengthen the child's positive behaviours and reduce challenging ones in children aged one to five years (Love & Fox, 2019). Post-treatment, 28% of the intervention group continued to exhibit symptoms, compared to 100% of the waitlist group. Treatment gains were maintained at a short-term follow-up (M = 7.5 weeks). An intervention with similar components was utilized in an individual case report of a family residing in a women’s shelter (Lehmann & Spence, 2007).

A case series and an individual case report explored the use of Preschool Kid’s Club, a multimodal model of therapy that emphasized both parenting skills and some emotion regulation principles; children aged four to six met in groups of five to seven and parents met at the same time in another room. A case series by Horn et al. (2013) found that PTSD symptoms decreased in all children (n = 4) following treatment; however, one child’s symptoms remained within the clinical range. No follow-up data were available to assess the long-term impact of the intervention. Similar outcomes were reported in a second individual case report by Howell et al. (2015). Although the participating family consistently attended treatment sessions, the authors noted significant treatment fidelity issues among other families not included in the paper, which disrupted the fluidity of treatment for the participating family.

One individual case report explored the efficacy of Parent–Child Interaction Therapy (PCIT) in a four-year-old as an alternative treatment due to the parents’ low adherence to CPP (Agazzi et al., 2023). PCIT involved observing parent–child interactions to modify negative parenting strategies and implement positive parenting behaviours. Issues regarding caregiver adherence remained, but PCIT proved effective at decreasing PTSD symptoms.

Experiences-based interventions

Ten studies were categorized as presenting interventions that were primarily experiences-based. The treatments included Dyadic Exposure Therapy, EMDR, and Eye Movement Integration (EMI). One study was an RCT, five were uncontrolled group studies, two were individually analysed case series, and two were individual case reports.

A small RCT (n = 12) conducted by Rachamim et al. (2021) reported the use of dyadic exposure therapy in children aged two to five years (M = 49.2 months). This intervention utilized developmentally appropriate toys to help children process traumatic events and addressed caregivers’ emotional responses to ensure effective trauma recovery for both the child and the parents. None of the six children met the criteria for PTSD at the three-month follow-up (Rachamim et al., 2021). A case series employing the same intervention yielded similar results post-treatment (n = 4; Rachamim et al., 2015).

Seven studies primarily utilized EMDR, none of which were RCTs or contained comparator groups. Participant age ranged from 4 months to six years. Most studies altered the EMDR protocol, either by condensing the recommended number of treatment sessions or adding dyadic components. Two studies employed a modified EMDR protocol incorporating storytelling to better tailor the intervention to children aged 4 months to four years (de Roos et al., 2025, n = 10; Potharst et al., 2025, n = 6). Both studies found EMDR Storytelling to be effective in young children displaying symptoms consistent with PTSD. Lempertz et al. (2020) implemented EMDR in a group setting with two to five participants (n = 10), and incorporated storytelling as a central component of the treatment. Six participants met PTSD diagnosis pre-treatment, compared to four at post-treatment and the three-month follow-up. Teacher reports showed a significant decrease in total PTSD symptom scores across all time periods, but parental reports did not. A non-concurrent multiple baseline study observed a modest decline in PTSD symptoms among participants, but no individual randomization tests were statistically significant (Olivier et al., 2022). The remaining three studies, comprising case series and individual case reports, all documented improvements in PTSD symptoms post-treatment (Cocco & Sharpe, 1993; Lempertz et al., 2023; Rathore, 2018). EMDR did not resolve two behavioural symptoms in one child: sleeping in their parent's bed and refusal to go to the bathroom alone at night (Cocco & Sharpe, 1993).

A single exploratory study (n = 12) used a single EMI session to treat PTSD symptoms in children aged five to six years (van der Spuy & van Breda, 2019). EMI was distinct from EMDR as it focused on integrating fragmented memories through simpler guided movements without emphasizing bilateral stimulation, whereas EMDR used bilateral stimulation to process traumatic memories by targeting memory networks and activating adaptive information-processing systems. The majority of patients (n = 11) experienced a decrease in PTSD symptoms at a two-week follow-up.

Skills- and experiences-based interventions

Seventeen studies were categorized as presenting interventions that were a combination of experiences- and skills-based. Treatments included trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy (TF-CBT), Stepped-Care TF-CBT, an Early Psychological Intervention, dyadic psychotherapy, Play Therapy, and the Integrative Attachment Trauma Protocol for Children. Five studies were RCTs, two were uncontrolled group studies, one was an individually analysed case series, and nine were individual case reports.

Five studies utilized TF-CBT with children over three years of age. Most studies adapted the standard TF-CBT protocol to better suit young children, typically modifying the intervention to involve a dyadic approach and placing greater emphasis on exposure techniques to account for their limited cognitive capacity to fully engage with the standard TF-CBT model. Scheeringa et al. (2011) and Hébert and Daignault (2015) both published RCTs examining the efficacy of TF-CBT. Scheeringa et al. (2011) reported that all PTSD symptoms remained in waitlisted participants, but only three of 54 patients who underwent TF-CBT continued to meet the PTSD diagnostic criteria post-treatment. However, the study reported high attrition that the authors attribute to Hurricane Katrina. Hébert and Daignault (2015) reported that TF-CBT treatment significantly reduced PTSD symptoms (p = .002); however, PTSD symptom severity modestly increased at a six-month follow-up, though it remained below baseline scores. Hitchcock et al. (2022) conducted an RCT that examined the efficacy of a TF-CBT-based intervention termed CBT-3M (Memories, Meanings, Management), which employed a more flexible framework and dyadic sessions to improve its efficacy in young children. Post-treatment, 11 of 12 patients no longer met PTSD criteria, and no participants met the criteria three months post-treatment. Two individual case reports utilized preschool PTSD treatment, which is heavily inspired by TF-CBT but altered to the needs of preschool-aged children (Puff & Renk, 2015; Stephenson & Renk, 2019). Both reported significant improvement in PTSD symptomatology. Lastly, Scheeringa et al. (2007) conducted a case series (n = 2) using a modified CBT protocol similar to TF-CBT to treat PTSD in two four-year-olds. It was successful in treating PTSD symptoms in both children.

Three studies explored Stepped-Care TF-CBT with children over three years of age. This treatment strategy separated TF-CBT into two distinct steps, allowing those who respond to Step One to conclude treatment and forego standard TF-CBT. Salloum et al. (2016) conducted an RCT that compared TF-CBT to Stepped-Care TF-CBT, and concluded that both interventions were effective in reducing PTSD symptoms, with SC-TF-CBT demonstrating 51.3% greater cost-effectiveness (M = $953.17 compared to M = $1957.19). An uncontrolled group study and an individual case report yielded similar results, with both showing significant improvements in children undergoing Stepped-Care TF-CBT.

Kramer and Landolt (2014) administered a short-term (2 sessions) early psychological intervention for children aged two to six years. which involved reconstruction of the traumatic event and identification and modification of accident-related appraisals. The study observed no change in PTSD symptom severity pre- to post-treatment compared to the control group, who received conventional medical treatment.

A single randomized controlled trial by Haag et al. (2020) evaluated the efficacy of CARE, a manualized two-session, trauma-focused CBT intervention adapted from Kramer and Landolt’s (2014) early psychological model, in children identified as high risk for developing PTSD within two weeks of experiencing an accidental injury. Children were aged one to six years. Although symptom severity decreased significantly more rapidly compared to treatment as usual, 45 of 59 children continued to meet the diagnostic criteria for PTSD six months post-treatment.

Play Therapy was categorized as both experiences-based, due to its use of trauma reenactment through play, and skills-based, as therapists guided children in reducing negative behaviours such as aggression. Three individual case reports explore the use of Play Therapy for children aged three to five years (Dugan et al., 2010; Gaensbauer, 1994; May, 2019). None of the studies used specific evidence-based PTSD measures to monitor symptoms, instead opting for the monitoring of specific target symptoms. All three case studies reported that the child’s PTSD symptoms improved post-treatment. One case study used an individualized phase-based approach to play therapy: phase 1 focused on a direct reworking of the traumatic experience through traumatic play, phase 2 transitioned to expansive and metaphorically based play therapy, and phase 3 directly addressed unresolved symptoms (Gaensbauer, 1994).

Keren and Tyano (2000) used dyadic psychotherapy to treat PTSD symptoms in a two-year-old. The intervention aimed to integrate the toddler’s fragmented traumatic memories into a coherent narrative while increasing the parent’s self-esteem as a protective shield for the child. The toddler’s condition steadily improved during treatment; however, symptoms consistently regressed whenever the source of trauma (the father) was reintroduced.

An individual case report described the treatment of a five-month-old infant suffering from PTSD according to the Diagnostic Classification of Mental Health and Developmental Disorders of Infancy and Early Childhood (Solter, 2007). The mother was provided with strategies, including frequent holding, protection from over-stimulation, and in-arms crying to calm the child. Following one year of treatment, the child no longer exhibited any PTSD symptoms.

Another individual case report by Swimm (2018) employed the Integrative Attachment Trauma Protocol for Children in a seventeen-month-old, which integrated EMDR with attachment-based therapies. This approach aimed to simultaneously strengthen the dyadic relationship for long-term success and address trauma exposure. The child no longer met PTSD diagnostic criteria following treatment, and the dyad exhibited an improved attachment relationship, which was sustained at the seven-month follow-up.

Pharmacological therapies

Two studies explored the use of conjoint psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy to address PTSD in cases where long-term psychological therapy was unsuccessful. Both studies were effective in reducing PTSD symptoms.

A limited open clinical trial enrolled seven participants aged three to six years to evaluate the use of a clonidine patch and psychotherapy (Harmon & Riggs, 1996). Two children experienced local irritation due to the patch, and the abrupt discontinuation of the medication in one child resulted in severe hypertension. One child was administered the medication orally because they pulled the patch off.

A case series examined the use of fluoxetine in one child aged two years and 11 months (2.5 mg daily, then 5 mg after one month) and clonazepam in another aged three years (5 mg daily) in conjunction with play therapy (Bhaskaran et al., 2016). Both children experienced a decrease in PTSD symptoms, and treatment gains were maintained at a 2-month follow-up.

Discussion

The treatment of PTSD in children aged zero to six presents unique challenges, given their limited cognitive capacities, rapid developmental changes, and broad variability in behaviour during early childhood. During this period of life, the alignment between therapeutic interventions and a child's stage of development is particularly critical. A more precise mapping of trauma responses in relation to specific developmental milestones – rather than solely by chronological age – would greatly enhance our understanding and inform more developmentally appropriate interventions. Cross sectional and longitudinal studies with more precise analyses of developmental periods are needed to ensure the generalizability of the interventions to young children. Consideration should also be given to the use of facial coding as this has been longstanding and invaluable in other areas such as pain (Grunau & Craig, 1987).

A recurring theme across nearly all treatments is the involvement of caregivers in the therapeutic process. Caregivers were often equipped with skills to better respond to and manage their child’s traumatic symptoms, were present during treatment, or served as the primary providers of treatment. The involvement of caregivers should therefore be considered by clinicians in the implementation of all PTSD treatments for young children. TF-CBT was the most frequently researched intervention and was analysed in multiple RCTs, making it the most rigorously evaluated treatment for children aged three to six years. TF-CBT protocols were successfully adapted to treat PTSD in children (e.g. Preschool PTSD Treatment) and were generally effective in reducing target symptoms. Further, Stepped-Care TF-CBT appeared to be equally effective as TF-CBT but more cost-efficient, making it a viable option in resource-limited settings (Salloum et al., 2016). In one RCT, the majority of participants (59.5%) were African American and received CBT-3M, a treatment based on TF-CBT principles, with positive outcomes (Hitchcock et al., 2022). The results indicate that clinicians should consider TF-CBT as a first-line treatment of diverse populations aged three to six years. Such interventions are not recommended for children under three years.

CPP was categorized as being probably effective in treating PTSD symptoms in children aged two- to six-years-old (Table 2). Although it tends to be longer in length than TF-CBT, it has also been shown to be effective in treating an ethnically diverse population (Appendix A), and may be a suitable second-line treatment to TF-CBT. Future research may consider conducting an additional RCT to confirm the suitability of CPP in treating PTSD symptoms in children aged two through six years.

Notably, it appears that both skills-based and experiences-based therapies are effective at reducing PTSD symptoms in young children. TF-CBT combines both approaches, while CPP places a larger focus on developing coping skills and fostering a positive dyadic relationship to improve PTSD symptoms. The efficacy of primarily experiences-based therapies is less developed in current literature, which may reflect the importance of including the development of the child’s coping skills and appropriate parenting strategies when treating PTSD in this population.

As anticipated, literature describing the treatment of trauma reactions consistent with PTSD in children under three years of age is sparse. This likely reflects the scientific discourse regarding the manifestations of PTSD in this developmentally diverse population. The majority of studies that referenced one- and two-year-olds had an average range of above two years and analysed their outcomes in combination with their older counterparts. This makes it difficult to specifically describe the efficacy of each intervention in toddlers and infants, especially as the studies did not report their current developmental stage. Of the five studies that reported results of children of whom the mean age was two years of age and under, four were case series or individual case reports, preventing their recommendation to clinicians as viable treatments in this population. Only CARE (Mage = 2.78) was suggested to be effective in toddlers following accidental injury in an RCT (Haag et al., 2020), meaning it the only treatment that can be tentatively recommended for toddlers exhibiting traumatic symptoms consistent with PTSD. Further research should be conducted regarding both the manifestations of PTSD in infants and toddlers and the most developmentally appropriate methods to treat symptoms in this population.

Additional investigation is required for multiple identified treatments. The Early Pathways programme, Crisis Intervention, and Dyadic Exposure Therapy are all analysed in a single RCT that supports their use. Although classified as likely efficacious according to the JCCAP study quality analysis (Table 2), additional rigorous studies must be undertaken prior to their recommendation for widespread use in young children. Pharmacological therapy for PTSD has not been rigorously studied in the zero-to-six-year population. If pharmacological therapy is to be considered by clinicians in this population, further systematic research must first be undertaken.

There is a lack of research on interventions that incorporate culturally sensitive treatment approaches for young children, highlighting a crucial area for future exploration. Lastly, it is recommended that researchers investigate the cost-effectiveness, efficiency, and feasibility of delivering adequate care in a wide variety of contexts including natural disasters, conflict-affected regions and with economically disadvantaged populations. Understanding these factors will ensure the allocation of resources where they are most needed and facilitate the development of interventions tailored to the specific needs of vulnerable populations.

Study limitations

Although the broad inclusion criteria of this review have allowed it to capture all available literature, it simultaneously limits its generalizability. The interventions varied greatly in their methodology, demographics, sample size, source of trauma and outcome measures. Additionally, limiting the review to English-language literature may have led to the exclusion of relevant studies published in other languages. Lastly, despite the use of rigorous methodology to maximize the comprehensiveness of the literature search, it remains possible that this review has not identified all published and grey literature sources.

Conclusions

This scoping review describes the available evidence-based treatments for children aged six years and under suffering from PTSD. It provides a comprehensive overview of many interventions, including TF-CBT, CPP, EMDR, Play Therapy, amongst others. The results indicate that additional investigation is required for most identified treatments. Currently, only TF-CBT has been identified in two or more independent randomized clinical trials, followed by CPP with one RCT and two large uncontrolled study groups supporting its use. No treatments for children under two years have sufficient evidence to allow for a recommendation. This review contributes to the field by synthesizing the current knowledge on PTSD interventions for young children. It highlights areas where further research is needed, particularly in conducting more methodologically rigorous studies, addressing demographic gaps, and expanding investigations focused on developmentally appropriate interventions in very young children. Addressing these gaps will enhance the generalizability and applicability of PTSD interventions in this crucial area of child mental health research.
