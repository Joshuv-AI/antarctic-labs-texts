Real-World Treatment Patterns in Advanced Cutaneous Melanoma in the USA
Authors: Matthew S. Block, Ashley E. Stenzel, Rene Marcotte, Katrine L. Wallace
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMelanoma has an excellent prognosis when detected at a localized stage, but when patients present with regional or distant metastases, 5-year survival decreases to approximately 76% and 35%, respectively. Although recent advances in systemic therapies have improved outcomes for patients with advanced melanoma, these treatments are often not curative, and many patients ultimately develop disease progression requiring subsequent lines of therapy.ObjectiveThis study aimed to characterize real-world treatment patterns among patients in the USA with advanced cutaneous melanoma who transitioned from first-line (1L) to second-line (2L) systemic therapy.Patients and MethodsThis retrospective, observational study used administrative claims data from a large US managed care database. Adult patients with metastatic melanoma who initiated 1L systemic therapy between January 2023 and December 2025 and subsequently received 2L therapy were included. Claims-based algorithms identified treatment regimens by line of therapy. Sankey diagrams illustrate treatment transitions overall and by BRAF V600 mutation status.ResultsOf 1772 patients who met study criteria, 680 (38.4%) were classified as presumed BRAF V600-mutated (BRAFmut) on the basis of treatment exposure, and 1092 (61.6%) were classified as presumed BRAF wild-type (BRAFwt). Immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-based regimens were the most common first-line (1L) therapies (78.5%), while BRAF + MEK inhibitors accounted for 14.4%. In second-line (2L) therapy, sequencing patterns were diverse, with no dominant 2L pathway observed. BRAF + MEK inhibitors were the most frequently used 2L therapy (27.5%). In patients with BRAFmut, targeted therapy predominated in 2L (71.8%), whereas ICI-based regimens remained dominant in patients with BRAFwt (84.9%). 1L therapy was significantly associated with 2L treatment selection (p < 0.001). Treatment distributions differed across index years for both 1L (p = 0.001) and 2L (p = 0.005) therapies. Over time, use of PD-1 monotherapy decreased, while use of nivolumab plus ipilimumab increased; use of BRAF + MEK therapy remained stable.ConclusionsAlthough treatment practices for advanced melanoma continue to evolve in response to emerging clinical evidence, substantial uncertainty remains regarding optimal treatment sequencing after 1L progression. These findings highlight the need for prospective sequencing studies and more effective therapeutic options following disease progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11523-026-01235-y.

Introduction

In the USA, more than 100,000 new cases of melanoma are diagnosed annually, and incidence has increased substantially over the past several decades, rising from 8.8 cases per 100,000 persons in 1975, to approximately 28.4 cases per 100,000 persons today [1].

Melanoma has a favorable prognosis when diagnosed at a localized stage, where the 5-year relative survival rate approaches 100% [1]. However, outcomes are substantially worse once the disease becomes advanced or metastatic. Approximately 15% of patients present with regional or distant metastases, where 5-year survival falls to approximately 76% and 35%, respectively [1].

Despite significant therapeutic advances in recent years, systemic treatments for patients with advanced melanoma are rarely curative [2, 3], and many patients eventually experience disease progression requiring multiple lines of therapy.

Treatment strategies for advanced melanoma vary depending on tumor characteristics, molecular biomarkers, patient factors, and evolving clinical guidelines. Current recommendations from major professional organizations, including the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) [4], the Society for Immunotherapy of Cancer (SITC) [5], and the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) [6], support the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) for most patients, and targeted therapies for patients with BRAF V600-mutated melanoma (BRAFmut) disease. In clinical practice, many patients receive immunotherapy in the neoadjuvant, adjuvant, or metastatic 1L setting, and a substantial proportion subsequently experience disease progression or recurrence requiring additional lines of systemic therapy [3, 7–9].

Evidence from randomized clinical trials has begun to inform optimal treatment sequencing. Combination checkpoint blockade with nivolumab plus ipilimumab has demonstrated improved survival compared with PD-1 monotherapy [10], while more recent studies have shown improved progression-free survival with the LAG-3/PD-1 combination of nivolumab and relatlimab [8]. In patients with BRAFmut disease, sequencing trials such as DREAMseq [11] and SECOMBIT [12] suggest improved outcomes when combination immunotherapy is administered prior to BRAF + MEK targeted therapy. Despite this evidence, the extent to which these emerging treatment strategies have been adopted in routine clinical practice remains unclear.

The objective of this study was to characterize real-world treatment patterns among patients with advanced melanoma who transitioned from a 1L to a 2L systemic therapy in a large administrative claims database.

Methods

This retrospective observational cohort study analyzed treatment patterns of adult patients in the USA with advanced melanoma from a large administrative claims database. Throughout this manuscript, the terms advanced and metastatic are used interchangeably to refer to patients with unresectable stage III or stage IV melanoma, consistent with the terminology used in the NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines [4]. The data source, study population inclusion criteria, definitions of lines of therapy, and study periods are summarized below. Data Source

This study utilized a large claims database, which includes enrollment information and medical and pharmacy claims for over 307 million covered lives across multiple payer types [13]. The database is geographically diverse, includes over 18,000 health plans, and is considered representative of the US commercially insured population. Medical claims in the database are collected from all types of healthcare services (e.g., inpatient hospital, outpatient hospital, emergency department, physician’s office, surgery center).

Within the claims database, diagnosis and procedure codes were used per the 10th revision, clinical modification (ICD-10-CM) [14]; current procedural terminology (CPT) [15], healthcare common procedure coding system codes; revenue codes; and site of service codes [16]. Outpatient CPT codes together with national drug codes (NDC) [17] were used to identify infused or injected systemic therapy for melanoma. Outpatient pharmacy claims and NDC codes were used to ascertain pharmacy-dispensed BRAF + MEK medications. Enrollment information, medical claims, and pharmacy claims were linked together by a unique identifier. The deidentified dataset is Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act compliant and did not require institutional review board approval or waiver of authorization. Data were obtained from a third party and are not publicly available.

Study Population

The study population was identified using a stepwise cohort selection process (Table 1). Adult patients (≥ 18 years) with a diagnosis of melanoma between 1 January 2022 and 31 December 2025, were identified from the administrative claims database. To improve diagnostic specificity, patients were required to have at least two melanoma diagnosis claims occurring at least 7 days apart but within 365 days of each other. 1L, first-line; 2L, second-line

Eligible patients were required to have received systemic therapy for melanoma after their initial diagnosis and to have at least 3 months of continuous enrollment before the first melanoma diagnosis to ensure accurate identification of 1L therapy. Patients with evidence of another primary malignancy were excluded to increase the likelihood that systemic therapies reflected treatment for melanoma.

To characterize contemporary treatment sequencing, the analysis was restricted to patients who initiated 1L systemic therapy between 1 January 2023 and 31 December 2025, had evidence of metastatic disease, and subsequently initiated second-line (2L) systemic therapy. This restriction reflects the intended study population because the objective was to characterize treatment sequencing among patients who transitioned from 1L to 2L therapy.

Because molecular testing results were unavailable in the administrative claims data, BRAF mutation status was approximated using treatment exposure. Patients who received a BRAF + MEK inhibitor at any time during the observed treatment period were classified as presumed BRAFmut, whereas those without evidence of BRAF + MEK inhibitor use were classified as presumed BRAFwt.

Ascertainment of Treatment Exposure

Treatment exposure was evaluated longitudinally for each patient beginning at the first observed systemic anticancer treatment following a metastatic melanoma diagnosis. Individual drug administrations or pharmacy dispensing events were chronologically ordered and grouped into treatment cycles and lines of therapy.

Definition of Treatment Cycles

Treatment cycles were defined on the basis of the timing of drug administrations or prescription fills. Drug administrations or fills occurring within 4 days of each other were considered part of the same administration episode to account for potential duplicate claims or billing delays. If the interval between administrations exceeded 4 days and no change in line of therapy occurred, the subsequent administration was considered a new treatment cycle. For oral therapies, each new prescription fill for a medication that the patient was already receiving was considered to initiate a new cycle.

Definition of Lines of Therapy

Lines of therapy were defined on the basis of treatment regimen composition, treatment duration, and gaps in therapy, rather than solely the individual therapeutic product, consistent with previously published claims-based oncology treatment algorithms. 1L therapy was defined as the first administration of systemic anticancer therapy after a metastatic diagnosis, and after the 3-month continuous enrollment lookback period. The start date of 1L therapy regimens was the index date.

The end of a line of therapy was defined as either: (1) the gap between drug administrations (for infused therapies) or between the end of days’ supply of the previous oral fill, and the next fill exceeded 120 days, or (2) switching within the 120-day time frame, but between clinically incompatible therapies. This gap threshold is consistent with previously published claims-based treatment algorithms in advanced melanoma and other solid tumors [18].

If one or more drug(s) was/were removed from a regimen, this did not constitute advancement to a new line of therapy as long as treatment with at least one component of the regimen continued and the gap between administrations did not exceed 120 days. Additionally, if a new drug was added within 28 days of treatment initiation or before completion of two cycles (whichever occurred later), the agents were considered part of the same line of therapy.

These rules allowed for construction of sequential treatment lines reflecting real-world treatment patterns, including regimen modifications and treatment interruptions. Definitions of the beginning and end of 2L therapy were identical as those for the end of 1L therapy. If chemotherapy was administered concurrently with an ICI within the 28-day combination window, it was classified as part of the ICI regimen. Patients with evidence of other active primary cancers were excluded from the cohort, reducing (though not completely eliminating) the likelihood that chemotherapy claims reflect nonmelanoma treatment.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient baseline characteristics and treatment patterns. Continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges, and categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages.

Treatment patterns were characterized by distribution of regimens and sequencing of lines of therapy. All analyses were done using R version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [19]. Sankey plots illustrating treatment flows from 1L to 2L were constructed using Tableau Desktop Version 2024.3 [20].

To examine factors associated with treatment patterns in the overall cohort, subset analyses were examined for BRAF V600 mutations and treatment setting. Patients with BRAF V600 mutant (BRAFmut) and BRAF V600 wild-type (BRAFwt) were examined as subgroups of the overall cohort owing to the availability of targeted therapy in the BRAFmut population and its effect on treatment patterns.

To evaluate whether second-line therapy selection was predicted by first-line treatment strategy, contingency tables were constructed comparing first-line and second-line treatment classes. The association between first-line and second-line treatment classes was assessed using Pearson chi-square tests with Monte Carlo simulation (1000 replicates) because of sparse cell numbers. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

To assess temporal changes over time in treatment patterns, contingency tables were constructed comparing therapy distributions across index years (2023–2025). Distributions over time were assessed using Pearson chi-square tests with Monte Carlo simulation (1000 replicates) to account for sparse cell counts. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05. To evaluate directional trends in individual therapies over time, Cochran–Armitage tests for trend were also performed.

Study Variables and Outcomes

Baseline Characteristics

Baseline characteristics were evaluated on, or before the 1L index date. Baseline characteristics were age, sex, insurance type, US region, and systemic therapy index year.

Utilization Variables

We calculated the prevalence of 1L and 2L systemic therapy use. Therapy prevalence was assessed using the NDC codes in each member’s pharmacy claims for BRAF + MEK oral therapies or NDC codes together with CPT codes in the medical claims for systemic anticancer therapy infusions.

Overall, 55.5% of patients were aged ≥ 65 years, although patients with BRAFmut disease were more likely to be younger than 65 years compared with those with BRAFwt disease (60.6% versus 34.5%). The cohort was predominantly male (55.0%), with similar gender distributions across BRAF subgroups. Most patients had commercial insurance (63.9%), followed by Medicare (25.1%), Medicaid/government insurance (7.0%), and other insurance types (3.1%), with only 0.9% having unknown or missing insurance information. Patients were distributed across geographic regions, with the largest proportion in the west (29.4%), followed by the central (25.7%), southeast (24.4%), and northeast (20.3%) regions. Most patients initiated 1L treatment in 2023 (53.7%), followed by 2024 (37.5%) and 2025 (8.9%), with similar distributions across BRAF subgroups.

After moving to 2L therapy, treatment patterns became more heterogeneous with decreased utilization of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy (n = 1086, 61.3%). The most common 2L therapy was targeted therapy with BRAF + MEK inhibitors (n = 488, 27.5%), followed by pembrolizumab (n = 303, 17.1%), nivolumab + ipilimumab (n = 285, 16.1%), nivolumab + relatlimab (n = 251, 14.2%), and nivolumab monotherapy (n = 234, 13.2%). Other 2L treatments included chemotherapy (n = 90, 5.1%), talimogene laherparepvec (n = 32, 1.8%), other PD-1 inhibitors (n = 13, 0.7%), and other regimens (n = 76, 4.3%) (Table 3).

Treatment patterns also differed substantially by BRAF mutation status. Patients with BRAFmut disease were more likely to receive targeted BRAF + MEK inhibitor therapy, particularly in the 2L setting, whereas treatment among patients with BRAFwt disease remained largely dominated by immune checkpoint inhibitor-based regimens.

Among the 680 patients with BRAFmut disease, 1L treatment patterns differed from those observed in the overall cohort, with greater use of targeted therapy. Immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-based regimens were the most common 1L regimen (n = 409, 60.1%), including nivolumab + ipilimumab (n = 163, 24.0%), pembrolizumab (n = 154, 22.6%), nivolumab (n = 50, 7.4%), nivolumab + relatlimab (n = 39, 5.7%), and other anti-PD-1 therapies (n = 3, 0.4%). The second most common regimen was BRAF + MEK inhibitor therapy (n = 256, 37.6%), followed by chemotherapy (n = 4, 0.6%), talimogene laherparepvec (n = 3, 0.4%), and other regimens (n = 8, 1.2%) (Table 3).

In 2L therapy, targeted therapy became the predominant treatment strategy among patients with BRAFmut disease. BRAF + MEK inhibitor therapy was used in 488 patients (71.8%), whereas ICI regimens accounted for 159 patients (23.4%), including nivolumab + ipilimumab (n = 69, 10.1%), pembrolizumab (n = 35, 5.1%), nivolumab + relatlimab (n = 28, 4.1%), nivolumab monotherapy (n = 23, 3.4%), and other PD-1 inhibitors (n = 4, 0.6%). Chemotherapy (n = 18, 2.6%), talimogene laherparepvec (n = 1, 0.1%), and other therapies (n = 14, 2.1%) were used less frequently (Table 3).

Among the 1092 patients with BRAFwt disease, immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy predominated in the 1L setting. Nearly all patients received an ICI-based regimen (n = 982, 89.9%), most commonly pembrolizumab (n = 443, 40.6%) or nivolumab + ipilimumab (n = 266, 24.4%). Additional 1L options included nivolumab monotherapy (n = 139, 12.7%) and nivolumab + relatlimab (n = 132, 12.1%), while chemotherapy (n = 65, 6.0%), talimogene laherparepvec (n = 18, 1.6%), other PD-1 inhibitors (n = 2, 0.2%), and other regimens (n = 27, 2.5%) were used less frequently (Table 3).

During 2L therapy, treatment patterns remained largely immunotherapy-based among patients with BRAFwt disease. ICI regimens accounted for 927 patients (84.9%), with relatively similar use of pembrolizumab (n = 268, 24.5%), nivolumab + relatlimab (n = 223, 20.4%), nivolumab + ipilimumab (n = 216, 19.8%), and nivolumab monotherapy (n = 211, 19.3%). Non-ICI therapies were less commonly used and included chemotherapy (n = 72, 6.6%), talimogene laherparepvec (n = 31, 2.8%), other PD-1 inhibitors (n = 9, 0.8%), and other regimens (n = 62, 5.7%) (Table 3).

Discussion

This retrospective claims-based analysis examined real-world treatment sequencing among patients with advanced melanoma who transitioned from 1L to 2L systemic therapy. Several important patterns emerged. First, immune checkpoint inhibitor-based regimens dominated first-line therapy across the overall population, whereas targeted BRAF + MEK therapy remained a common first-line strategy among patients with BRAF-mutant disease. Second, treatment selection in 2L was highly heterogeneous, with no single regimen or sequencing pathway predominating in the second-line setting. These findings highlight the complexity of treatment decision-making after first-line therapy and underscore the absence of a clearly established standard of care following 1L failure.

Importantly, treatment patterns were not static over time. Significant temporal shifts were observed in first-line therapy, characterized by decreasing use of PD-1 monotherapy and increasing use of combination immunotherapy.

In BRAFmut disease, targeted therapy remains a common 1L choice, despite recent findings from the DREAMseq and SECOMBIT trials [11, 12] demonstrating between 8 and 20% absolute overall survival advantage at 2 years for the groups that sequenced combination immunotherapy in 1L before BRAF + MEK targeted therapy. The continued use of BRAF + MEK in 1L BRAFmut melanoma may reflect the early stages of practice adoption following the 2023 publication of these trials, rather than a failure to incorporate emerging evidence, and the temporal trend of increasing combination ICI use observed over the study period is consistent with active, ongoing adoption.

Across all patients, anti-PD-1 monotherapy remained a commonly used 1L treatment, despite increasing evidence showing improved efficacy with combination checkpoint blockade in selected patients. CheckMate 067 [10] and RELATIVITY-047 [8] both demonstrated improved clinical outcomes with combination approaches relative to anti-PD-1 monotherapy. Encouragingly, monotherapy use declined over the study period while combination immunotherapy use increased, suggesting partial, but incomplete, uptake of this evidence in routine practice.

The BRAFwt cohort demonstrated treatment flows from pembrolizumab to nivolumab monotherapy and vice versa between 1L and 2L. Because these agents share the same PD-1 mechanism of action and exhibit cross-resistance [21], clinical benefit from switching between them after progression may be limited. These agents share resistance mechanisms including T-cell exhaustion, loss of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I expression, and upregulation of alternative immune checkpoints, rendering cross-PD-1 switches unlikely to yield meaningful clinical benefit. Prospective evidence supporting PD-1 to PD-1 monotherapy sequencing is lacking, and current clinical guidelines do not specifically endorse this strategy [4].

A large proportion of patients with BRAFmut appear to switch between BRAF + MEK regimens in 1L and 2L. This sequence is not specifically supported by current NCCN recommendations [4] because resistance usually develops within months, and retreatment may lead to suboptimal outcomes [22, 23]

Another notable pattern was the modest use of the nivolumab + relatlimab (LAG-3/PD-1) combination over the study period, with no statistically significant increase observed over time. This finding is interesting given its demonstrated progression-free survival benefit compared with nivolumab monotherapy in RELATIVITY-047 [8] and its designation as a Category 1 regimen in NCCN guidelines [4]. This also may reflect delayed uptake of emerging clinical evidence.

Finally, residual use of chemotherapy in the 1L setting is less aligned with contemporary therapeutic strategies. Chemotherapy (dacarbazine, temozolomide, carboplatin-based regimens) is listed in NCCN guidelines only as a subsequent-line or palliative option, explicitly not recommended as a 1L choice in patients eligible for ICI or targeted therapy [4].

Beyond the evidence–practice gaps observed in the 1L setting, the 2L melanoma landscape reveals another major clinical challenge: the lack of a clear standard of care once patients have progressed on 1L therapy [2]. Second-line therapy was significantly associated with the regimen used in the 1L setting, and no individual second-line therapy demonstrated a statistically significant temporal trend. These findings reinforce the absence of a consistent 2L treatment paradigm. Few prospective trials have defined optimal sequencing strategies after failure of modern checkpoint inhibitor combinations. The rapid expansion of effective immunotherapy and targeted therapy options for advanced melanoma has dramatically improved patient outcomes, but has also created a complex treatment landscape in which optimal sequencing strategies remain incompletely defined. The wide scatter of 2L treatment choices visible across both the BRAFmut and BRAFwt groups in this study spanned ICI combinations, targeted therapy, chemotherapy, and various salvage regimens.

For patients with BRAFwt who progress on combination ICI particularly, no consistently effective next step seems to exist [24], leaving oncologists with limited options and modest response rates. Even in patients with BRAFmut, where targeted therapy offers a meaningful cross-class switch after ICI failure, long-term outcomes in the 2L setting remain suboptimal. This represents a substantial and growing unmet need. As combination ICI regimens become increasingly dominant in the 1L, the population of patients who are ICI-exposed and ICI-refractory will only expand, making the absence of effective 2L options a more urgent clinical problem. Novel therapeutic strategies are urgently needed to address this treatment gap.

The recent approval of tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy (lifileucel) introduces a potential additional option for patients with ICI-refractory disease. Specific billing and NDC codes for lifileucel were evaluated in this analysis. Because lifileucel was approved late in the study period and is administered only at specialized centers, utilization was expected to be minimal; where captured, it would appear within the “other therapies” category. The extent to which TIL therapy uptake increases in subsequent years represents an important area for future real-world evidence studies.

Limitations

This study was descriptive by design and thus should be considered hypothesis-generating. Results should also be interpreted within the limitations inherent to claims-based studies.

Algorithms were developed to determine patient eligibility and treatment pathways on the basis of international classification of diseases-10 diagnostic and procedure, and pharmacy codes on claims. Precise disease grading and the tumor, nodes, metastasis (TNM) classification of malignant tumors staging were not available; therefore, dates of diagnosis and treatment initiation were used as proxies. Treatment exposure and sequencing were inferred from dates on claims and pharmacy codes and may be subject to coding inaccuracies or misclassification. Individuals with conflicting data were excluded, and missing data were not imputed.

BRAF mutation status was inferred algorithmically from treatment exposure because molecular testing results were not available in the administrative claims data. The use of treatment–exposure algorithms to infer BRAF mutation status is an established approach in the real-world melanoma literature; Ghate et al. [25] applied this same methodology in two large US commercial claims databases. To our knowledge, no study has formally validated this algorithmic approach against concurrent molecular testing results in claims data, and it should be understood as a pragmatic convention rather than a validated surrogate for mutation status.

Patients who received BRAF plus MEK inhibitor therapy at any point during follow-up were classified as presumed BRAFmut, whereas patients without evidence of BRAF-targeted therapy were classified as presumed BRAFwt.

Published epidemiologic data estimate BRAF V600 mutations in approximately 40–50% of cutaneous melanomas [26]; the observed rate of 38.4% is modestly below this range, consistent with some patients with true BRAFmut being classified as BRAFwt because they received ICI-only therapy. Patients with true BRAFmut misclassified into the BRAFwt group would increase the apparent proportion of the BRAFwt subgroup receiving ICI-based second-line therapy, thereby attenuating differences between the two subgroups; because this misclassification occurs primarily through patients with BRAF-mutant who never receive targeted therapy, it would be expected to bias subgroup comparisons toward the null rather than create spurious differences between groups. The primary aim of BRAF subgroup stratification in this study is descriptive, to contextualize treatment patterns within clinically meaningful groups, rather than to estimate population-level BRAF mutation prevalence.

Important clinical variables influencing treatment selection, including ECOG performance status, autoimmune disease history, symptom burden, tumor burden, LDH levels, and patient comorbidity burden were not available in the claims data. The absence of clinical covariates precludes adjustment for confounding by indication and thus limits causal inference. Treatment decisions in routine practice are influenced by patient-specific factors including disease trajectory, symptomatic burden, and comorbid conditions, which may appropriately deviate from population-level guideline recommendations. Accordingly, the observed deviations from guideline-recommended sequencing should be interpreted as descriptive signals rather than definitive evidence of suboptimal care.

Prior adjuvant or neoadjuvant systemic therapy before metastatic disease onset may not have been fully captured in treatment sequencing definitions. For patients who received adjuvant ICI or targeted therapy before developing metastatic disease, what is classified here as 1L metastatic treatment may effectively represent a subsequent line of systemic therapy, which could influence both treatment selection and response. This is a recognized limitation of claims-based sequencing studies and should be considered when interpreting 1L and 2L pattern findings.

The cohort was restricted to patients who initiated 2L therapy by study design; this reflects the intended study population, as the objective was to characterize treatment sequencing specifically among patients who transitioned to second-line therapy. Patients who did not progress to 2L were not part of the intended study population and would not have been eligible, regardless of the observation window. Published estimates suggest approximately 10–15% of patients receiving first-line immunotherapy may not survive to receive second-line treatment and are, therefore, not represented in this analysis. Findings should not be generalized to all patients with advanced melanoma initiating systemic therapy.

Findings from this US managed care claims database may not generalize to patients in other health systems, where treatment patterns, access to therapies, and prescribing norms may differ.

These data were collected from a managed care population, and results may not be universal to all patients with melanoma. This cohort does not include patients in the Veterans Administration system or those who are uninsured and includes only a small number of Medicaid/government-sponsored enrollees.

Lastly, these data were collected for administrative purposes, and not for research. As such, the dataset does not include detailed baseline demographics, such as race/ethnicity. Thus, this analysis may not be applicable to all populations.

Conclusions

This study provides a contemporary description of real-world treatment sequencing among patients with advanced cutaneous melanoma who transitioned from first-line to second-line systemic therapy in a large US administrative claims database. Although treatment practices continue to evolve in response to emerging clinical evidence, substantial uncertainty remains regarding optimal treatment sequencing after first-line progression. These findings highlight the need for prospective sequencing studies and more effective therapeutic options in the second-line setting.
