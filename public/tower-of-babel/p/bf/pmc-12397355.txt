Microstructural and mechanical characterization of foamed concrete reinforced with scrap aluminum engine residue
Authors: Abdullah Alzlfawi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFoamed concrete (FC) is a lightweight cementitious material made by adding a stable pre-formed foam to a cement paste or mortar to make a homogenized mixture of a controlled low density. The present work aims at evaluating the effect Scrap Aluminum Engine Residue (SAER) has on fresh, mechanical, thermal, and microstructural properties of FC with a water-to-cement ratio of 0.4 at targeted densities of 900 kg/m3 (FC-900) and 1100 kg/m3 (FC-1100). SAER was added at 1.5%, 2.5%, and 5.0% as a weight of cement. After 28 days curing, ASTM standard tests were conducted to analyze the fresh density, spread flow, stability, compressive strength, flexural strength, thermal conductivity along with microstructure characteristics via X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) and Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM). The addition of SAER to the wet mix enhanced fresh density and induced moderately in the early age expansion but compromised workability. The best result was obtained when 2.5% SAER was used, where the gain in compressive strength is 92.8% (FC-900) and 242.9% (FC-1100), and in the flexural strength, 68.8% and 67.8%, respectively. The thermal conductivity was reduced by up to 44% in FC-900 which implies that it is a sign of improved insulation. XRD showed lower crystallinity as the SAER content increased, but the higher density mixes showed sharper diffraction peaks. SEM observations revealed that the most uniform and dense microstructure of a mixture was the FC-900 mix with a 2.5% SAER containing minimum porosity. Such results lead to the conclusion that 2.5% SAER is the most effective dose of the component that can maximize mechanical strength and thermal insulation properties in foamed concrete.Keywords: Ordinary portland cement, Foam, Concrete, Mix design, Scrap aluminum engine residueSubject terms: Civil engineering, Engineering

Introduction

Foam concrete is a type of lightweight concrete which is produced from mixture of cement, sand and water with added foam. Foam produces pores in the concrete which reduce its self-weight. It has the density ranging from 300 kg/m³ to 1840 kg/m³1–3.For normal concrete, the density is in the range of 2240 kg/m³ to 2400 kg/m³ which is around 87–23% of unit weight compared to lightweight concrete. There are many applications of lightweight concrete in building construction which include partition, slab, and wall panel4–6. Recently, the technology of foam concrete is enhancing even more with the addition of waste material, either as cement or sand replacement or as filler in the foam concrete mixture. Metals are used as enhancements to concrete and this helps to improve some mechanical properties like its flexural strength7–9, toughness, fracture energy, impact resistance and ductility. The concrete therefore develops cracks while under tension. Thus, Studies were conducted on performance of foam concrete strengthened with waste tire steel fibers. It was found that waste tire steel fibers added as filler in foam concrete enhanced tensile strength6,10,11.However, there has been no study of Scrap Aluminum Engine Residue (SAER). Thus, the incorporation of recycled materials, such as SAER, might offer a sustainable approach to enhancing the mechanical properties of foam concrete while addressing waste management concerns. In this study, SAER is added to the foam concrete in different ratios with fixed water to cement ratio.

Recent advances in foamed and geopolymer concrete have explored diverse approaches, including pore characterization and AI-based modeling of strength12, volcanic pumice reinforced high-strength geopolymer concrete13, and the impact of silicate modulus and eggshell powder on lightweight geopolymer foam concrete performance14, and the production of machine learning-optimized artificial geopolymer aggregates using waste materials15. Other related research includes modeling, material innovations and structural performance. Examples include Voronoi modeling in reinforced concrete structure16, mesoscale concrete modeling17, and hybrid aerodynamic modeling18 as advanced types of computations. These innovations are 3D reviews of concrete printing19, and damage prediction based on deep learning20. Fracture toughness of recycles fiber mortars analysis21. Composite columns22 and seismic performance of strengthened RC columns23 are examples of experimental work, also vibration in pile-raft foundations24. Sludge stabilization study is seen in material-based investigations of subgrades25, as well as microstructural control in foam ceramics26. The International Organization of Motor Vehicle Manufacturers reported that over 90 million motor vehicles were produced globally in 2014, with production expected to grow27–29, the reuse of waste materials like SAER in concrete presents both a sustainability opportunity and a pathway for performance enhancement. The total number of vehicles on roads is estimated at approximately 1.2 billion by the same organization30. Considering that at least a third of these vehicles need to repair the head engine by lathering the head of the engine. During lathering, a SAER comes out from the lather machine, where it usually just through out to the trash because it required a lot of efforts to re-use the SAER in which it requires to process the SAER in the oven and reuse it in production aluminum again. This would be harmful to the environment and increase pollution. Therefore, this SAER might be used directly as additives of the foam concrete to enhance the flexure properties of the foam concrete without impacting the environment31,32. The application of SAER in foam concrete is a relatively unexplored field of research. Aluminum recycled and retrieved in the form of soft drink cans and engine blocks has been utilized in previous research but has focused on casting techniques rather than its use as a material as an additive to foam concrete33–37. The existing research has most notably concentrated on other sources of automotive waste in concrete such as waste tires10,38 and have shown strong enhancement in flexural strength. In this regard, the current research has analyzed the addition of SAER in the form of foam concrete mixes, specifically focusing on how it affects fresh and hardened properties.

This research aims to design and optimize foam concrete mixes to include scrap aluminum engine residue (SAER) and assess its impact on fresh (workability and density) and mechanical properties (compressive strength and flexural strength) while exploring its potential for performance enhancement and industrial waste valorization in sustainable construction.

Scrap aluminum engine residue (SAER)

Scrap Aluminum Engine Residue (SAER) was used in this research. The SAER was collected from lath shops that are located in Majmaah, Saudi Arabia. The SAER was then cleaned of any residual oil before it was dried and then sieved to prevent presence of free moisture that would affect the proportions of the mix or any properties of the foamed concrete. The test performed the drying process in a laboratory oven at 105 ± 5 °C and 24 h took the test till a constant weight was attained as is standard practice in determining moisture of an aggregate. This made sure that the material is in a totally dry form prior to sieving through the 300 μm mesh (see Fig. 2). The SAER is heterogeneous in nature as it is the byproduct of recycled aluminum engine parts, mix compositions of 20–50 wt % alumina (Al2O3), 40–55 wt % salt fluxes (NaCl/KCl),10–20 wt % of metallic Al and oxides of alloying elements (Fe, Si, Zn, Cu), reactive phases such as aluminum nitride or carbide and residual heavy metals i.e., Zn, Pb, and As. This observed expansion can be mainly attributed to reactions that involve metallic Al with water, to give hydrogen and ammonia, and effects of hygroscopic and crystallization of salts. The increase in strength may be achieved through pore-filling of aluminum hydroxides or alumina gels and hydrates or the oxide phases consisting of rigid fillers. Secondary hydrate precipitation, migration and crystallization of salts within pores, and dispersion of oxides all cause microstructural alterations, although increasing the amount of gas can also cause micro cracking.

Water

The water utilized in the concrete mixture was tap water with a pH ranging from 3.8 to 7.045. Because the tap water was not salty or briny, it was ideal to use for foam concrete. For most mixtures, the water temperature was maintained at 18 Co to 21 Co.

Mix design

There are no standard guidelines for mixing foam concrete, mix proportioning begins with the selection of the unit weight or wet density for the mixture, the quantity of cement, and the water–cement ratio (w/c). Initially, some parameters for the material, such as the foam density, also need to be determined. According to ACI 523.3R-14 (ACI, 2014)46, the air yield per volume of foam (ΦA) is typically around 0.95, the specific gravity of Type I cement is assumed to be 3.5, and the density of water is 1000 kg/m3 at a typical room temperature47,48 To perform a proper calculation, it is necessary to obtain both the design density and the water-cement ratio (w/c). The design of the foam concrete for this project was selected to be 900 kg/m3 and 1100 kg/m3. The water–cement ratio was considered to be 0.4. Table 3 shows the mix proportions of foam concrete. The dosages of SAER in the range of 1.5%, 2.5%, and 5% were selected to include the preliminary trials and previous work on cementitious materials in combination with aluminum-based residues, which concluded that the range between 1% and 5% was optimal to balance the ability to yield favorable properties without excessive expansions49,50.

As-cast wet density of foam concrete

It is common practice to ascertain the as-cast density (also known as cast wet density) at the construction location by weighing a tared container of a known volume filled with foam concrete in accordance with ASTM C796/C796M (ASTM, 2019)10. The density of the cast-wet material is determined using a cylinder that is 304 mm high and 203 mm wide. In general, foam concrete, unlike normal weight concrete, must not be compacted or vibrated, as this would alter its density. However, ASTM C796/C796M specifies that, during molding, the sides of the molds should be lightly tapped with a rubber mallet to ensure the container is completely filled. Any excess concrete on the top is then scraped off using a strike-off plate before the cast density is determined. The acceptable tolerance for the cast wet density should be the design density ± 80 cast wet density. Flowability of foam concrete

The flowability (slump) of foam concrete mixtures prepared as a part of this project were measured using flow consistency tests, which were conducted in accordance with ASTM D6103-17/D6103-17 (ASTM, 2017). In this test, the consistency of the foam concrete is evaluated after the foam is added to the cement slurry in a plastic cylinder with a diameter of 76 mm in and a height of 152 mm that was placed on top of a smooth, non-porous base measuring 250 mm × 250 mm × 5 mm, as shown in the setup in Fig. 4(a). After filling the cylinder with concrete up to its top edge (brim), a rigid metal straightedge is used to strike off any excess concrete to ensure that the top surface is even. After striking off, any spillage is removed from the base. Next, the cylinder is lifted rapidly (removed completely within 5 s) to allow the fresh mix to run freely over the smooth plate. ASTM guidance strongly suggests ensuring that the entire test (beginning with the filling of the flow cylinder and ending with its removal) be carried out continuously and lasts no more than 60 s. At the end of the test, a measuring tape is used to determine the diameter of the largest extent of resultant spread of the concrete (Fig. 4(b)). Two measurements of the spread diameter along axes that are perpendicular to one another are obtained, and the final spread is computed by taking the average of the values from the two measurements.

Assessing the stability of foam concrete

The stability of a foam concrete refers to its capacity to maintain its initial form or volume without collapsing even after it has reached its hardened state. Figure 5 (a) shows a photo of foam concrete that became unstable 24 h after field casting. There are two main factors that can influence the stability of foam concrete: external factors such as the temperature of the surrounding environment and internal factors, which include the density and temperature of the fresh concrete.

Considering that there is no universally accepted method for determining the stability of foam concrete, the test used to verify the foam stability, ASTM C940-16 (ASTM, 2016)51with some modifications, was used to evaluate the stability of the concrete mixture. In the modified method, foam concrete mixture was poured into a 150 mm x 150 mm x 150 mm cubic (as shown in Fig. 5(b). Measurements were also obtained at 60 min after placing the concrete in the model and at 120 min after concrete placement, and a final reading was taken at 24 h after the placement.

Casting and demolding of test specimens

Proper casting of foam concrete specimens is essential for obtaining accurate results. To ensure that the specimens were properly produced, the molds were sprayed with releasing oil before casting to prevent the concrete from sticking to the mold. No vibration was necessary after pouring the concrete due to the self-leveling and self-compacting properties of the concrete mixture. After pouring, the concrete surface of each specimen was leveled to provide a smooth finish, and the specimens were allowed to set for 24 h. After 24 h, the specimens were removed from the molds using the appropriate equipment and were placed in a curing chamber to complete the curing process.

Results & discussion

Fresh properties

Cast wet density

The cast wet density is influenced by the foam volume and stability, proportion of mixes and moisture content of materials and the handling and additional factors can influence it as well so additives such as SAER can influence it based on their physical properties and moisture content. Figure 8 shows the computation of ACI limitation of cast wet density in which the ACI states that the cast wet density of the foam concrete should be ± 80 kg/m3 of the design density. Mix FC-900 and FC-1100 are within the ACI limitation whereas the mixes that contains the SAER are beyond the ACI limitations due to the effect of the SAER weight. However, the mixes show good stability after a few hours of mixing.

Cast wet density of foamed concrete mixes with varying compositions.

Flowability

The spread variation with mixed density with and without the SAER is illustrated in Fig. 9. The graph illustrates the spread values for foam concrete mixes with two different densities, FC-900 and FC-1100, and varying additive contents (1.5%, 2.5%, and 5%). A clear trend emerges, showing that as the additive content increases, the spread values consistently decrease, indicating a reduction in flow ability or workability. For the FC-900 mix, the spread starts at 200 mm for the plain mix (no additives) and progressively decreases to 190 mm, 18.4 mm, and 160.2 mm as the additive content increases to 1.5%, 2.5%, and 5%, respectively. Similarly, for FC-1100, the spread begins at 160 mm for the plain mix and decreases to 150.3 mm, 140.7 mm, and 140.0 mm for the same additive levels. Across all additive levels, FC-900 consistently demonstrates higher spread values compared to FC-1100, highlighting its superior workability, which is likely attributed to its lower density. The reduction in spread values with increasing additive content is likely due to the additives making the mix stiffer by absorbing water or altering the mix’s rheological properties. Additionally, the inherently higher density of FC-1100 reduces its flow ability compared to FC-900, possibly due to high amount of cement in the mix. These findings suggest that FC-900, with its higher spread values, is more suitable for applications where ease of placement and higher workability are critical, while FC-1100, with its lower spread values, may be more appropriate for applications requiring reduced flow ability and increased stability to resist deformation or segregation. In addition, the workability loss of foam concrete mixes with SAER can be explained by the previous study of the effects of aluminum dross and these industrial wastes in cementitious mixtures. Bouchikhi et al.52, stated that an increase in water demand and less slump were caused by aluminum waste as it possesses a high surface area and is highly active in the chemical sense. It has been reported by other scholars that aluminum dross inhibits workability by consuming mix water, showing high porosity, liberating gas during alkaline reactions, which also interrupts the flow53. The analysis of foam concrete properties also proves that waste powders as fine and angular particles decrease the flowability by increasing viscosity and water demand of concrete54. These similarities confirm the current analysis in that the decreasing workability with SAER content can be attributed to the small particle size, the irregular shape, and possible reactivity within the cementitious matrix.

Spread flow test results for foamed concrete mixes with and without scrap aluminum engine residue (SAER).

Hardened properties

Stability

The mixes in this research exhibited a high level of stability, but with the addition of SAER to foamed concrete, the findings indicated that foamed concrete mixes were found to experience some early-age expansion within two hours of casting (see Fig. 10), which can be attributed to the reaction between metallic aluminum with the alkaline pore solution and aluminum nitride, which produced hydrogen and ammonia gases. The noteworthy increase in volume of SAER-incorporated foam concrete samples after two hours of casting is probably due to the chemical reaction comparable but not identical to the lightweight concrete system per producing aluminum powder. During the reaction of reactive aluminum phases with alkaline pore water, hydrogen gas is produced-which allows early-age volumetric growth and bubble formation that greatly enhance the porosity and decrease the density55. The same has been observed in foamed concretes that contain aluminum where its expansion is dependent on reactive phase concentration, dosage, and particle size56. This is just one of the mechanisms that can result in lowering the density; however, too much expansion can have an impact on dimensional stability, and more research needs to be conducted in order to quantify and regulate it.

Early-age expansion behavior of foamed concrete within the first 2 h after casting.

Compressive strength

The graph in Fig. 11 illustrates the compressive strength (in MPa) of FC-900 foam concrete mixes with varying levels of additives (1.5%, 2.5%, and 5%). The plain FC-900 mix, without any additives, has a compressive strength of 3.32 MPa, serving as the baseline value. When 1.5% additives are introduced, the compressive strength increases slightly to 3.45 MPa, showing a modest improvement in strength, but the change is not very significant. A much more pronounced increase in compressive strength occurs with the addition of 2.5% additives, where the strength rises sharply to 6.40 MPa, marking a significant peak. This suggests that 2.5% is the optimal amount of additive for achieving the highest compressive strength in FC-900, likely due to a favorable interaction between the additives and the mix, which enhances its hardening properties. However, when the additive content increases further to 5%, the compressive strength decreases to 3.30 MPa, slightly lower than both the plain mix and the 1.5% additive mix. Similar observation was seen in Fig. 12 with mix FC-1100 where the control mix, FC-1100 without any additive, achieved a compressive strength of 4.06 MPa. However, incorporating 1.5% of SAE into the mix resulted in a notable increase in compressive strength to 8.46 MPa, indicating an enhancement in the material’s structural performance. The most significant improvement was observed at 2.5%, where the compressive strength reached 13.92 MPa, more than triple the strength of the control. This suggests that the SAER material positively interacts with the foam concrete matrix at this percentage, optimizing its mechanical properties. However, at 5% waste content, the compressive strength decreased sharply to 3.93 MPa, falling below the control value. This decline may be attributed to excessive SAER material disrupting the matrix integrity, leading to weakened bonding and structural performance.

Compressive strength of FC‑900 foamed concrete with various additives after 28 days of curing.

Compressive strength of FC‑1100 foamed concrete with various additives after 28 days of curing.

These results imply that while the addition of small amounts of SAER additives can improve compressive strength, there is an optimal additive level, in this case, 2.5%, beyond which the strength begins to decrease. Therefore, it is crucial to carefully control the additive content to achieve the best performance in foam concrete mixes. The addition of SAER contributed considerably to the compressive strength at 1.5% and 2.5% both in FC-900 and FC-1100 mixes, with the greatest increase noted at 2.5% (92.8%, 242.9% respectively), whereas both FC-900 and FC-1100 experienced a strength decrease at 5% (0.60%, 3.20% decrease, respectively).

Thermal conductivity

The graph in Fig. 16 shows the relationship between thermal conductivity and varying mix percentages (0%, 1.5%, 2.5%, and 5%) for two mixes: FC-1100 and FC-900. As the SAER mix percentage increases, the thermal conductivity decreases significantly for both mixes. At 0% mix, the thermal conductivity is highest, with FC-1100 showing a value of 0.242 W/mK and 900 showing 0.183 W/mK. However, at 5% mix, the values drop drastically to 0.128 W/mK and 0.113 W/mK for 1100 and 900, respectively. This indicates that increasing the mix SAER percentage in the foam concrete mix reduces the material’s ability to conduct heat. The reason why the thermal conductive reduces when incorporation the SAER in the foam concrete mixes could be refer to the expansion that SAER incorporate after mixing as discussed in Sect. 2.1.1.1. This trend suggests that the incorporation of SAER in the foam concrete mix enhances the material’s thermal insulation properties. The decrease in thermal conductivity with SAER content (see Fig. 16) is explained by the expansion and micro-filling effects of SAER that increase closed cell porosity and refine pore distribution as shown in SEM (see Fig. 17) to form effective thermal barriers. These microstructural and material features together with naturally low thermal conductivity of aluminum residues are known to considerably improve the level of thermal insulation of foamed concrete57,58. The results are encouraging, but there is some importance in discussing the practical implications further, especially in the aspect of how they can be applied in energy-efficient building design. The decrease in thermal conductivity in SAER-modified foamed concrete might help decrease the heat transmission across the building envelopes, resulting in enhanced thermal comfort inside the building and a reduction in the need of mechanical heating, ventilation, and cooling systems and also in the energy needed to operate them.

X-Ray diffraction pattern

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of foamed concrete samples with the density of 900 kg/m3 and 1100 kg/m3, modified with the different levels of SAER (0%, 1.5%, 2.5 and 5%), are shown in Fig. 18 (a-i). The diffraction patterns show multiple different peaks that correspond to crystalline phases that are frequently generated in cementitious systems, such as mullite (Al₆Si₂O₁₃) and quartz (SiO₂). The sample with a density of 1100 kg/m³ and no SAER shows the sharpest and strongest peaks in the patterns, suggesting a highly crystalline structure. This implies that improved particle compaction and less porosity are encouraged by higher-density foamed concrete, which improves the development and growth of crystalline phases.

XRD patterns of foamed concrete (a) SAER Material (b) FC-900 (Control) (c) FC-900, 1.5% SAER (d) FC-900, 2.5% SAER (e) FC-900, 5% SAER (f) FC-1100 (Control) (g) FC-1100, 1.5% SAER (h) FC-1100, 2.5% SAER and (i) FC-1100, 5% SAER.

In contrast, samples with a lower density (900 kg/m3) have larger peaks and lower peak intensity, which indicates a less crystalline, more amorphous structure. These patterns are further influenced by the addition of SAER. Peak intensity gradually decreases and diffraction peaks broaden at 900 kg/m³ when the SAER concentration is increased from 1.5 to 5%. This suggests that increased SAER disrupts the crystalline matrix, most likely because its gel-like or polymeric properties interfere with the hydration products’ ability to arrange themselves in an ordered fashion. The weakest diffraction pattern is seen in the FC-900 sample with 5% SAER, indicating a predominance of amorphous phases and restricted crystal formation.

The crystalline structure is likewise impacted by the addition of SAER in the 1100 kg/m³ series, but the effect is not as strong. The foamed concrete retains a comparatively high level of crystallinity even at 2.5% SAER, suggesting that higher density can partially offset SAER’s crystallization-inhibiting effects. Furthermore, certain samples may exhibit the presence of CaCO3, which could be the result of carbonation during air exposure during the handling and curing process. Additionally, the intensity of the CaCO₃ peak tends to decrease with increasing density, which can be explained by decreased porosity and limited gas penetration. The crystalline–amorphous balance in foamed concrete is mostly determined by density and SAER content, with higher density and lower SAER levels causing a more crystalline microstructure, according to the XRD analysis. XRD analysis (Fig. 18) shows that at 2.5% SAER, sharp and well‑defined peaks for C‑S‑H and portlandite indicate a robust crystalline network, while a moderate increase in amorphous content from fine SAER particles improves packing density and void filling. This optimal crystalline–amorphous balance enhances load transfer, explaining the 92.8% and 242.9% compressive strength gains in FC‑900 and FC‑1100, whereas higher SAER content (5%) disrupts crystallinity and reduces strength.

Microscopic morphology

SEM microstructural analysis offers a thorough comprehension of the ways in which SAER additive concentration and bulk density affect the morphology of the produced composites. The morphological development of foamed concrete with different bulk densities (900 kg/m³ and 1100 kg/m³) and incremental additions of SAER is revealed by the SEM microstructural study shown in Fig. 17 (a–i). As an example of a synthetic additive or processed polymeric with the potential to increase interfacial bonding, the SAER material exhibits smoother and more uniform particles. With a bulk density of 900 kg/m³, the FC-900 composite exhibits a somewhat porous matrix with loosely packed particles and incomplete sintering. A steady improvement in microstructural homogeneity, marked by better particle packing, fewer interstitial spaces, and greater surface connectivity, is seen upon the addition of SAER at weight percentages of 1.5% and 2.5%. Without compromising the overall structural integrity, the 2.5% SAER content seems to be ideal for creating a continuous and well-integrated phase. The micrographs show evidence of particle agglomeration and microphase separation at 5% SAER, though, most likely as a result of oversaturation and inadequate additive dispersion in the matrix.

The higher compaction level in the FC-1100 composites (1100 kg/m3) results in a denser and more consolidated microstructure as seen in the SEM pictures. Reduced porosity and closer particle contact are the results of intrinsic densification, which is advantageous for load-bearing or thermal insulation applications. Although the advantage of adding SAER to the FC-1100 matrix at 1.5% and 2.5% plateaus at 2.5%, with small agglomerates beginning to form, SAER still improves surface smoothness and interfacial bonding between phases.

The FC-1100 samples have a more compact and closed morphology than the FC-900 samples, although this could come at the expense of less surface activity or permeability. The most advantageous microstructural configuration of all the samples is shown by FC-900 with 2.5% SAER, which balances open porosity with adequate additive dispersion and surface refinement. Depending on the intended application, this ideal combination is likely to produce better mechanical performance, increased thermal stability, or greater reactivity, especially in situations where uniform phase distribution and regulated porosity are crucial. It can be seen in SEM analysis (see Fig. 17) that at 2.5% SAER, a dense homogenous and well compacted matrix with increased interconnection between pores and uniform dispersal of particles facilitate strong packing and bonding, in addition to good strength, and on the other the 5% SAER forms agglomerations that create holes in the matrix weakening it significantly.

Conclusions

This research analyzed the outcomes of 1.5%, 2.5% and 5% addition of the Scrap Aluminum Engine Residue (SAER) into foamed concrete with desired density of 900 kg/m3 (FC-900) and 1100 kg/m3 (FC-1100), and analyzed fresh properties, mechanical performance, thermal conductivity and microstructural characteristics. All SAER-modified mixes were dimensionally stable during the initial 24 h, but some minor expansion followed the mixing. Whereas control mixes met ACI fresh density requirements, the addition of SAER addition brought about an addition of density that was above the standards. Optimum mechanical properties were observed at 2.5% SAER with compressive strength improving by 92.8% (FC‑900) and 242.9% (FC-1100) and flexural strength increasing by 68.8% and 67.8%, respectively, but 5% SAER decreased strength. The spread flow used as a parameter of workability followed the same pattern by being lower with increasing SAER content as FC‑1100 had the lowest readings of (140 mm) at 5% SAER. The thermal conductivity dropped by as much as 44% in FC-900 which is improved insulation. XRD and SEM tests revealed that 2.5% SAER in FC‑900 created a uniform and dense matrix with minimal pore structure and that increased dosages resulted in poorer crystallinity and the agglomeration of particles. The overall 2.5% SAER provides the best combination of strength, thermal performance, and microstructural integrity, and therefore is a promising additive to lightweight, thermally efficient construction application. Future studies are needed to determine the long term durability of SAER modified foamed concrete subject to cyclic freeze–thaw and wet-dry conditions, as well as chemical reagents, in combination with microstructural characterization to track pore structure evolution, phase stability, and crack development during testing. Further, evaluation of scalability, cost-benefit analyses, and life cycle environmental impact analyses are necessary to determine the technical and economic viability of large scale construction implementation.
