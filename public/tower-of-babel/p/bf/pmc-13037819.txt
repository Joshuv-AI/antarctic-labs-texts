Association of sleep, screen use with suicidal behaviour among patients with depressive disorder: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Pinki Sevda, Preethy Kathiresan, Naresh Nebhinani, Mithu Banerjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Sleep disturbances and suicidal behaviour remain common in depression. Emerging evidence suggests a role of screen use and sleep disruption. However, Indian literature is limited.Aim:To examine the association between sleep, screen use with suicidal behaviour in depression.Methods:In this cross-sectional study, 150 individuals with depressive disorder were enrolled, 75 each with and without suicidal behaviour. Hamilton Depression Rating Scale, Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, and Screen Time Questionnaire were used to measure depression severity, suicidal behaviour, sleep quality, and screen use, respectively.Results:Among 150 participants, 57.3% were female. Suicidal behaviour was associated with being unmarried, employed, longer duration of illness, earlier age of onset, greater depression severity, poor sleep quality, and higher screen time.Conclusion:Suicidal behaviour was associated with increased screen time and poor sleep quality underscoring the need for preventive strategies targeting sleep hygiene, and interventions to reduce screen time.

INTRODUCTION

The most widespread mental illness is depressive disorder, which is also a known risk factor for suicidal behaviour.[1] The World Health Organisation (WHO) predicts that by 2030, severe depression will be a significant contributor to disability.[2] In India, there was a 4.2% rise in the suicide rate from 2021 to 2022 with the current rate standing at 12.4 suicides per 100,000 population.[3]

A meta-analysis conducted by Large and colleagues showed that among patients with depression, history of intentional self-harm, hopelessness, guilt or inadequacy, low mood, suicidal thoughts, and a family history of suicide were significantly associated with in-patient suicide.[4] In addition to these, it has been shown that other variables, such as screen time and sleep, are linked to suicidal behaviours.[56] Also, with the advent of internet, there has been an increase in screen time all over the world, including India. It can also provide access to information on lethal means of self-harm. It would be useful to know whether patients with depression, who have suicidal behaviour use the screen for help-seeking or to get information on self-harm methods.[7] Sleep disturbance has also been found to be more common among patients with depression who also have suicidal behaviour.[8]

Few Indian studies have examined the link between sleep disturbances and suicidal behaviour in depression, and none have explored screen time in adult patients. Identifying these associations in the Indian context may improve the screening procedure as well as formulating management strategies to reduce the suicidal risk. Therefore, the index study was aimed to examine the association of sleep disturbances and screen use with suicidal behaviour in depressive disorder and to determine the association of demographic and clinical variables with sleep disturbances and screen time.

METHODOLOGY

After receiving approval from institutional ethics committee, this cross-sectional study was conducted from March 2022 to June 2023 at Psychiatry outpatient and inpatient setting of a multi-speciality tertiary-care hospital in north-western India. Purposive sampling was used to recruit the participants. Participants with depressive disorder were divided into two groups: with suicidal behaviour and without suicidal behaviour.

Both the groups comprised patients of either gender aged between 18 years to 60 years, diagnosed with current depressive episode according to International Classification of Diseases-11 (ICD-11)[9] (Single Episode Depressive Disorder or Recurrent Depressive Disorder) with HAM-D[10] score ≥14 (moderate to severe depression) and who provided written informed consent. Group 1 included patients having suicidal ideation/plans or who have made suicide attempt within current episode. Group 2 included patients with no suicidal ideation/plans/attempts in the current episode. Patients with bipolar affective disorders, any other co-morbid psychiatric illness (except tobacco dependence) or chronic medical illness were excluded. The sample size was calculated on the basis of study by Park et al.[11] with 75% among patients with suicidal behaviour, 60% among those without, a 95% confidence interval, and 80% study power. Total 150 of the patients, 75 in each group were recruited and assessed for depression severity, suicidality, sleep quality, and screen use. Including demographic and social profile sheet following instruments were used:

Hamilton depression rating scale (HAM-D)

It is a 21-item clinician-reporting questionnaire for evaluating the severity of depression. Scoring is based on the first 17 items only. (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.85)[10]

Columbia suicide severity rating scale (C-SSRS)

It is a clinician-administered tool measuring past and current suicidal ideation and behaviour. It assesses the dimensions of suicidality: severity, intensity, behaviour and lethality. Cronbach’s coefficient is 0.89.[12]

Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI)

It is a self-assessment tool that differentiates between “poor” and “good” sleep quality by assessing seven aspects: subjective sleep quality, the time taken to fall asleep, duration, habitual efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleeping medications, and daytime impairment over the past month. The PSQI’s overall Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.746.[13]

Screen time questionnaire

This self-report questionnaire includes screen use on an average weekday, screen use on an average weeknight, screen use on an average weekend day, and background activity.[14]

Statistical analysis

Data coding and analysis was done using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21.0 for Windows (Chicago, Illinois, USA). The normality of data was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk test. Data was non-normally distributed. Mann-Whitney-U test and Chi-square test were used. The correlations between continuous variables were determined through Spearman correlation. Binominal logistic regression and linear regression were applied. The P-value below 0.05 was deemed significant.

Demographic and clinical profile

Patients with suicidal behaviour had earlier illness onset (U = 2146, P = 0.012) and longer duration of illness (U = 2161, P = 0.01) compared to those without. Number of episodes, duration of current episode, and duration of treatment were not statistically significant. In both groups, most of the participants had a single-episode depressive disorder, and approximately half of the participants were prescribed Escitalopram. Family history of psychiatric illness was present in 12.7% with 4.7% reporting family history of suicidal behaviour. Majority (86%) of the participants were using screen daily.

Details of suicidal behaviour

Many participants reported moderate to severe suicidal ideation, occurring a few times or more. A majority of the individuals (62.7%) expressed the wish to die, 38.7% reported actively contemplating suicide with specific plan and intent, 16% had attempted suicide.

Most participants (98.7%) reported poor sleep quality. Mean HAM-D scores were higher in the suicidal behaviour group (20.36 vs. 16.24; U = 896.5, P < 0.001). This group also had poorer sleep quality (17.84; U = 2053, P = 0.004) and greater screen time (13.84 vs. 7.30; U = 1708, P < 0.001) than those without suicidal behaviour.

Association of sleep with suicidal behaviour, demographic and clinical variables

Females had poorer sleep quality compared to males (U = 2197, P = 0.03), and single individuals had poorer sleep quality compared to married individuals (U = 1726, P = 0.02). Other variables like locality, family type, religion, education, occupation, diagnosis and psychological intervention were not significantly associated with sleep quality. Depression severity was significantly associated with poor sleep quality with PSQI positively correlating with HAM-D (r = 0.35, P = <0.001), signifying that with declining sleep quality (evidenced by higher PSQI scores), there was a tendency for increased severity of depressive symptoms.

Association of screen content and screen time with suicidal behaviour, demographic and clinical profile

As shown in Table 2, searches during the current episode significantly increased for depression treatment (P ≤ 0.001), motivational quotes (P = 0.01), and yoga (P = 0.04) compared to before; other activities showed no differences. The suicidal behaviour group had significantly higher total screen time and device use (smartphones, laptops, tablets, TV devices) across weekdays and weekends, including background use. Screen time was higher in males, single individuals, urban residents, and nuclear families, and in those with family history of psychiatric illness (U = 785, P = 0.03) or suicidal behaviour (U = 259, P = 0.03). Screen time negatively correlated with age (r = −0.43), age at onset (r = −0.47), positively with income (r = 0.37), and HAM-D scores (r = 0.18; all P = 0.02).

DISCUSSION

This cross-sectional study assessed the association of various socio-demographic factors, clinical variables, sleep, and screen use with suicidal behaviour among patients with depressive disorders.

Association of suicidal behaviour with socio-demographic and clinical variables

Higher prevalence of suicidal behaviour was found in single individuals, mirroring most Indian studies, and western studies as marriage is a protective factor.[1516] Stable marriage can improve social integration, lessen loneliness, while providing people a feeling of purpose and belonging.[17] Higher prevalence of suicidal behaviour was seen in employed individuals in comparison to unemployed and housewives. Work-related stress, the demands of the workplace, including high expectations, long working hours, job insecurity, imbalance between work and personal life, isolation contribute to elevated stress levels, potentially increasing the risk of suicide. Suicidal behaviour was significantly associated with an earlier age of onset. Early-onset depression can lead to a longer illness course, causing substantial functional impairment and illness burden over time.[18]

Sleep

Individuals having depression with suicidal behaviour had poorer sleep quality compared to those without. The literature indicates a bidirectional relationship between depression and sleep disturbance. Sleep disturbances could activate or worsen an individual’s susceptibility to psychopathology.[19] In one of the Indian studies, it was found that the duration spent in the depressive phase of illness and sleep disturbance was an independent predictor of a suicidal attempt.[20] In current study, females had poorer sleep quality compared to males. Males exhibit stronger HPA axis activation under stress, potentially making them more resilient to sleep disruption.[21] Single individuals had poorer sleep quality compared to married individuals aligning with other studies.[2223]

Screen use

The present study found that individuals with suicidal behaviour use smartphone more compared to those without. A study found that excessive use of smartphones i.e. more than 5 hours a day increased suicidal behaviour in students suffering from depression.[24] Indian studies reported greater suicidal behaviour in individuals with more than 3 hours of screen use.[25] Index study suggested that males had higher screen time compared to females. Males are more likely to be able to divert themselves by playing video or computer games excessively or by using social media.[26] Individuals who were single had higher screen time compared to married individuals. Previous studies on technology use have primarily focused on younger demographics like children, adolescents, and young adults. Marital status was not assessed. In current study, individuals living in urban/town areas had higher screen time compared to those in rural areas. Urban and town areas generally boast superior access to high-speed internet and a broader array of devices compared to rural regions, facilitating more accessible and affordable technology use.[27] In previous studies, type of family was not assessed.

Index study has comprehensively explored the association of suicidal behaviour with screen use and sleep in adult patients with depression, while other studies have focused on one or few variables in children or adolescent population.

The study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causal relationships between screen use, sleep disturbances, and suicidal behaviour. Matching was not done for the demographic characteristics of both the groups. The use of self-reported measures for sleep and screen time may introduce recall and social desirability biases. As a single-centre, hospital-based study, findings may not be generalisable. Variability in clinical severity may influence the observed associations.

CONCLUSION

Index study contributes to the research related to screen time, sleep disturbances in patients with suicidal behaviour in depression. Nearly three-fifths had moderate depression, 10% reported persistent suicidal ideation, 86% used screens daily, and 98% had poor sleep. Significant associations of suicidal behaviour were increased duration of depression, increased screen time, and poor sleep quality. Significant associations of sleep disturbances were being female and single, while higher screen time was associated with being male, single, urban residence, nuclear family, and family history of psychiatric illness or suicide. Significant correlates of suicidal behaviour were screen use and sleep disturbances. Prevention strategies should focus on promoting good sleep hygiene, implementing behavioural interventions to reduce screen time.[2829] Additional research is essential to uncover the role of sleep disorders in suicidal behaviour and to better understand the relationship between screen time, depression, and suicidality, including identifying safe limits for screen use.
