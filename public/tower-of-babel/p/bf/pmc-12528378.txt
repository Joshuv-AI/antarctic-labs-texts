The effect of the use of irrigation activation methods with different file systems on the amount of apical debris extrusion
Authors: Seden Kara Ongun, Oğuz Tavşan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated apical debris extrusion caused by passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI), sonic activation (SA) and conventional irrigation (CI) in combination with different file systems. A total of 135 extracted human mandibular premolar teeth were used. The samples were divided into nine groups (n = 15) based on three main irrigation activation techniques and three subgroups of file systems. After preparing the experimental setups and completing the root canal shaping procedures with XP-endo Rise Shaper, ProTaper Ultimate, and WaveOne Gold files, irrigation activation was performed in each group. Distilled water was used as the irrigation solution. Eppendorf tubes containing distilled water and debris were incubated at 37 °C for 14 days. The tubes were then weighed three times using a precision scale, and the mean of the measurements was calculated. The mean pre-recorded empty tube weights were subtracted from the final mean weights to determine the weight of the apically extruded debris. Among the irrigation activation methods, PUI resulted in the greatest amount of debris extrusion (p = 0.003). All file systems caused apical debris extrusion; however, there was no statistically significant difference among the file systems regarding debris extrusion (p > 0.05). When the combinations of file systems with irrigation activation methods were compared, the combination of the WaveOne Gold with SA resulted in significantly less debris extrusion (p = 0.009). PUI caused significantly more apical debris extrusion compared with SA and CI. The combined use of WaveOne Gold and SA during root canal treatments may serve as an option to reduce postoperative pain and acute flare-ups. Introduction

Throughout history, various file systems made from different alloys have been developed and utilized for root canal shaping. However, the strength and superelastic properties of the NiTi alloy have enabled root canal shaping to be performed in a shorter time and with fewer files. These features have contributed to the widespread popularity of file systems manufactured from NiTi alloy in endodontics1.

Introduced to the market in 2022, the XP-endo Rise Shaper (XPRS; FKG Dentaire, La Chaux-de-Fonds, Switzerland) is a rotary file system with a triangular cross-section. Similar to its predecessor, the XP-endo Shaper, it is manufactured from MaxWire alloy and can expand from a size of 30/0.01–30/0.04 at temperatures of 35 °C and above2. The manufacturer claims that the XPRS system offers a simplified procedure using its “Glider” and “Shaper” files.

ProTaper Ultimate (PTUL; Dentsply Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland) represents the latest generation of the ProTaper series. The system consists of eight files made from three different alloys produced through distinct heat treatments (M-wire, Gold-wire and Blue-wire). All files in the series feature a rhomboidal cross-sectional geometry from the D1–D3 points and a parallelogram shape with varying acute angles from the D4–D16 points3.

WaveOne Gold (WOG; Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) is a reciprocating file system similar to its predecessor, WaveOne, but it differs in size and geometry4. The WOG features a parallelogram-shaped cross-section with two cutting edges. This file system was manufactured using Gold-Wire technology. All files within the system exhibited a variable and regressive taper design5.

Due to the complexity of the root canal system, mechanical shaping alone is insufficient to achieve effective disinfection, as current file systems cannot fully shape all root surfaces6. Therefore, mechanical preparation should always be supported with irrigation solutions and irrigation activation techniques. Conventional irrigation (CI) remains the most commonly used irrigation method among general practitioners and endodontists7. CI can be performed with two types of needles: open-ended needles, which allow the irrigant to flow directly from the tip, and closed-ended needles, which prevent direct outflow and instead deliver the irrigant through one or more side vents8. Although CI is a well-known and frequently used method, it cannot adequately reach accessory and lateral canals or irregularities within the root canal system. Because CI is unable to completely eliminate pathogenic microorganisms, various alternative irrigation activation techniques have been developed over time9,10. Ultrasonic irrigation activation has been introduced as a method to enhance cleaning efficiency by agitating the irrigant solution with an ultrasonically oscillating file11. In the literature, two types of ultrasonic irrigation have been described. The first is ultrasonic irrigation, in which irrigation and instrumentation are performed simultaneously. However, in ultrasonic irrigation, controlling dentin cutting and, consequently, the shaping of the root canal is challenging. For this reason, a second type of system (passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI)) was developed, in which only irrigation activation is performed without instrumentation12. The most commonly recommended protocol for PUI involves three periods of 20 s each, although shorter protocols, such as three periods of 10 s, are also employed13. Sonic activation (SA) is an irrigation activation method using plastic tips that oscillate horizontally and vertically at a low frequency between 1 and 6 kHz. The activation with plastic tips creates an oscillatory flow within the root canal; however, due to the very low frequency and large oscillation amplitude, neither acoustic streaming nor acoustic cavitation occurs14. The EndoActivator (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) is a sonic activation system equipped with polymer tips of different sizes, operating within a frequency range of 33–167 Hz15.

Apical debris extrusion is generally defined as the displacement of dentin particles, pulp remnants, necrotic tissue, and microorganisms beyond the apical foramen during root canal shaping and irrigation procedures. This extrusion may lead to complications such as periapical inflammation, the occurrence of postoperative pain or flare-ups, and a delay in periapical healing16. The most significant complications associated with apical debris extrusion during root canal treatment are acute flare-ups and postoperative pain and the incidence of acute flare-ups following endodontic treatments ranges between 1.4 and 16%17. Evidence from previous studies indicates that preparation techniques, file systems and irrigation protocols contribute to apical debris extrusion. The amount of extruded debris has been shown to vary depending on the irrigation protocols applied and the design characteristics of the instruments, including their size, cross-sectional geometry and the kinematics of the file system18,19. Despite the development of advanced file systems and irrigation activation methods, apical debris extrusion during root canal shaping has not been completely prevented. Given the association between apically extruded debris and various clinical complications, identifying and minimizing the contributing factors is essential to enhance patients’ postoperative outcomes and overall treatment success20,21.

Apical extrusion of debris has been extensively investigated in the endodontic literature. Several studies comparing file systems have demonstrated conflicting results: while some reported that reciprocating systems extrude more debris than rotary systems22,23, others found greater extrusion with rotary systems24,25. Additional factors, such as the presence of a glide path26 and the taper of the instruments27 have also been shown to influence the amount of apically extruded debris. Beyond instrument-related features, contemporary irrigation activation techniques have been examined regarding their potential to cause apical extrusion18,28. Notably, previous studies have indicated that PUI produces less apical extrusion compared with SA techniques such as the EndoActivator29,30. Although PUI has been widely reported to improve canal cleanliness, its high-frequency activation may increase the risk of apical extrusion. By contrast, SA systems operate at lower frequencies and generate lower streaming velocities, which might be expected to result in less extrusion31. Therefore, considering the contradictory findings of the existing evidence and previous studies, the present study aimed to evaluate the effect of combining contemporary file systems with different kinematic movements and commonly used activation techniques on apical debris extrusion. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has simultaneously compared the effect of these irrigation activation methods in combination with these different file systems on apical debris extrusion.

This study quantitatively investigated the effect of PUI, SA, and CI methods in combination with XPRS, PTUL and WOG files on apically extruded debris. In this context, the hypotheses of the study are as follows:

Null hypothesis (H0):

The use of the XPRS, PTUL rotary file system and the WOG reciprocating file system has no effect on the amount of apically extruded debris.

Alternative hypothesis (H1):

SA results in less apical debris extrusion compared with PUI.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Uşak University (Approval No: 118-118-13 ), and all procedures adhered to applicable ethical and institutional standards. All procedures performed in this study, were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations of Helsinki Declarations.

Sample selection and preparation

The sample size was calculated using the G*Power 3.1 software. Based on a medium effect size of 0.5 in the t-test family, an alpha error of 5%, a 95% confidence level, and a power of 80%, it was determined that 135 samples (15 samples per group across nine groups) would be needed, assuming an equal group size28,29,32.

This study used single-rooted human mandibular premolar teeth, which had been extracted within the past year for orthodontic or periodontal reasons and exhibited no caries, restorations, or apical developmental anomalies. The extracted teeth used in this study were obtained from patients who had undergone tooth extraction for therapeutic reasons unrelated to the study, with prior informed consent, at the Departments of Periodontology and Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Faculty of Dentistry, Uşak University. The patients voluntarily donated the extracted teeth to the faculty for research purposes. All experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Uşak University (Ethical approval number: 118-118-13). Periodontal tissue remnants on the teeth were cleaned, and radiographs were taken in the mesiodistal and buccolingual directions. Teeth with calcifications, multiple canals or resorptions were excluded. The root canal curvatures of the radiographed teeth were measured according to Schneider’s method33, and only teeth with straight canals were included.

For standardization, the length of each tooth was measured using a caliper, and the teeth were reduced to a standardized length of 17 mm by performing occlusal reduction under water cooling with a diamond fissure bur. Access cavities were prepared using a diamond round bur, and the apical patency was checked using a #15 K-file. Teeth in which the file engaged at the apical foramen were included in the study, whereas those in which the file was loose were excluded. Teeth where the file engaged at the apex were included in the study. The working length was determined by subtracting 1 mm from the length at which the file tip was visible at the apical foramen under a dental operating microscope (ZUMAX OMS 2000; Zumax Medical, Suzhou, China). The working length was fixed at 16 mm using a rubber stopper.

Experimental setup

The experimental setups were prepared based on the method developed by Myers and Montgomery34. A precision scale with an accuracy of 10−4 (RADWAG, AS 220.R2; Radom Poland) was used to measure the weight of the apically extruded debris during the experiment. For the purpose of collecting apically extruded irrigant and debris, 2 ml Eppendorf tubes were utilized in the present study35 (Fig. 1).

Experimental glass vial model used to collect and measure apically extruded debris: (a) the setup without opaque coating, (b) the setup with opaque coating applied to the glass vial.

Before constructing the experimental setups, the caps of the Eppendorf tubes were removed, and the tubes were weighed three times on the precision scale. The mean of these three measurements was recorded as the “initial average weight”. The prepared tooth samples were inserted into the caps of the Eppendorf tubes by creating a hole in each cap and securing the teeth with finger pressure. To prevent coronal leakage of debris and irrigant into the Eppendorf tubes during irrigation, the gap between the tooth and the cap was sealed using gingival barrier material (FlexDam; Ruby Dent, Istanbul, Turkey). A 27-gauge syringe needle was inserted into the caps to equalize the air pressure between the external environment and the inside of the tubes, with the needle tip remaining inside the tube. The Eppendorf tubes were placed and secured into glass vials through rubber stoppers to prevent damage during the experiment and to avoid contamination from fingerprints, grease, or debris22. These glass vials were coated with opaque paint to prevent the operator from visualizing the root apex. The entire setup was then placed inside a larger container filled with a thermostatically controlled water bath maintained at 35 °C (Memmert GmbH, Schwabach, Germany), which ensured a constant temperature throughout the experimental procedures36. This setting allowed procedures to be performed under conditions simulating the temperature of the root canal environment and facilitating the crystallographic phase transformation of the XPRS rotary file system.

Instrumentation and irrigation activation

The included teeth were divided into nine groups, consisting of three main irrigation activation groups and three subgroups based on file systems, with 15 teeth in each group, resulting in 135 samples overall. Distilled water was used as the irrigation solution for all groups. The same irrigation procedure was applied at each file change for all file systems to standardize irrigation during shaping.

In all groups, at each instrument change, and additionally after every three reciprocating motions in the reciprocating file system, the root canals were irrigated with 2 mL of distilled water, and the instruments were withdrawn and cleaned with alcohol-soaked gauze. Additionally, apical patency was checked using a #10 K- file after every file change.

Side-vented 30G EndoArt plastic irrigation needles (Inci Dental, Istanbul, Turkey) were used for irrigation. A single operator performed all root canal shaping and irrigation activation procedures. Moreover, each file was used to shape only one tooth. The shaping procedures were performed in all groups using a VDW Gold Reciproc Endomotor (VDW, Munich, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

In the XPRS group, the canals were shaped using the system’s files (XP-endo Rise Glider and XP-endo Rise Shaper) following the manufacturer’s instructions, with the endomotor set at 1000 rpm speed and 1 Ncm torque.

In the PTUL group, the root canals were shaped using the PTUL rotary file system’s files (Slider, Shaper, F1, F2, F3) following the manufacturer’s instructions, with the endomotor set at 400 rpm speed and 4 Ncm torque.

In the WOG group, the root canals were shaped using the WOG reciprocating file system’s files (Small, Primary, Medium) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with the endomotor operating in the preset “WAVEONE ALL” mode at the recommended speed and torque values.

After the shaping procedures were completed for all file groups, PUI, SA, and CI were performed sequentially according to the assigned irrigation groups.

PUI: After the root canals were irrigated with 2 mL of distilled water, the stainless-steel activation tip of the DTE S6 LED device (Guilin Woodpecker Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., Guangxi, China) was inserted into the canal up to 2 mm short of the working length. Irrigation activation was then performed with 6 mL of distilled water over 60 s, divided into three periods of 20 s each37.

SA: After the root canals were irrigated with 2 mL of distilled water, the medium tip of the EndoActivator (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) was inserted into the canal up to 2 mm short of the working length. The device was used at the highest power setting, and irrigation activation was performed with 6 mL of distilled water for over 60 s divided into three periods of 20 s each.

CI: The irrigation needle was inserted into the root canals up to 1 mm short of the working length, and irrigation was performed with 2 mL of distilled water for over 60 s. This procedure was repeated three times, and 6 mL of distilled water was used to complete the CI37.

Evaluation of debris extrusion

After completing the procedures in all experimental groups, the debris remaining on the root surfaces was rinsed with 1 mL of distilled water and added to the debris collected in the Eppendorf tubes. The tubes were then incubated at 37 °C for 14 days (Heratherm Advanced Protocol; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to allow the distilled water to evaporate.

After 14 days, the previously weighed Eppendorf tubes were weighed again three times, and the “final average weight” was recorded. The amount of extruded debris was calculated by subtracting the “initial average weight” from the final average weight of the tubes.

Statistical analysis of the data

All statistical analyses were conducted using the SPSS software package (version 29.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test together with skewness and kurtosis values. Variables that did not meet normal distribution criteria were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR).

The weights of the Eppendorf tubes were measured in triplicate both before and after the experimental procedures, and the mean values were recorded as the final measurements. The delta value was calculated by subtracting the initial measurement (empty tube) from the final measurement (tube with debris).

The distribution of apically extruded debris among more than two groups (e.g., different file systems or irrigation activation methods) was compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test (Tables 1 and 4). When statistically significant differences were identified, pairwise comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test (Tables 2 and 3). A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

When the distribution of apically extruded debris among the different file systems (XPRS, PTUL, and WOG) was evaluated, no statistically significant difference was observed between the file groups (p = 0.442) (Table 1). The distribution of apically extruded debris according to different irrigation activation methods (PUI, SA, and CI) was evaluated, and a statistically significant difference was found among the groups (p = 0.003). Pairwise comparisons revealed that the amount of apically extruded debris in the PUI group was significantly higher than in the SA and CI groups (p = 0.003). However, no significant difference was observed between the SA and CI groups in terms of the amount of apically extruded debris (p = 0.784) (Table 2).

A statistically significant difference was found when the distribution of apically extruded debris according to subgroups of the PUI and SA methods was evaluated (p = 0.049). Pairwise comparisons showed that in the PUI group, the amount of apically extruded debris was significantly higher for all subgroups shaped with the XPRS (p = 0.014), PTUL (p = 0.015), and WOG (p = 0.009) compared with the subgroup shaped with the WOG reciprocating file system in the SA group (Table 3).

No statistically significant differences were observed in the distribution of apically extruded debris among subgroups when comparing the PUI and CI methods (p = 0.104) or the SA and CI methods (p = 0.582) (Table 4).

Discussion

During the shaping and irrigation of root canals, the extrusion of debris into the periradicular tissues may lead to acute flare-ups between treatment sessions, postoperative pain, and delayed healing responses. Moreover, despite advancements in modern endodontic systems, root canal shaping and cleaning procedures still result in the extrusion of debris and irrigant through the apical foramen20. In view of this information, the present study compared the effects of combining different file systems with various irrigation activation methods on the amount of apically extruded debris and to identify the method that results in the least debris extrusion.

There are studies in the literature comparing the amount of debris extrusion among different file systems, including the XP-endo Shaper, which shares similar kinematic and alloy characteristics with the XPRS, the newer generation system38,39. In a study comparing the XP-endo Shaper with the WOG and OneCurve (Micro-Mega, Besancon, Cedex, France) single file systems, the XP-endo Shaper caused the least amount of debris extrusion among the groups40. Similarly, another study comparing seven file systems demonstrated that the XP-endo Shaper caused less debris extrusion than the WOG system41. In another study comparing five different single file systems, including the XP-endo Shaper and WOG, no significant difference was found among the groups in terms of debris extrusion42. In our study, similar to the findings of this study involving the XP-endo Shaper, no significant difference in apical debris extrusion was observed between the XPRS system and the WOG and PTUL file systems used in the experiment. The finding that the XPRS and WOG systems exhibited similar results in terms of apical debris extrusion may be attributed to the fact that both are single-file systems. Previous studies have suggested that single-file systems tend to extrude more debris apically compared with multi-file systems43,44. In the present study, however, the single-file systems XPRS and WOG demonstrated apical debris extrusion levels similar to those of the multi-file PTU system. This inconsistency with earlier studies may be explained by variations in instrument design, particularly cross-sectional geometry and taper configuration. Additionaly, the absence of a significantly lower amount of debris extrusion with the XPRS system compared with other file systems, unlike the XP-endo Shaper, may be attributed to the increased number of files used in the XPRS system, as it is used in combination with the XP-endo Rise Glider. To the best of our knowledge, no published studies in the literature explicitly investigated debris extrusion related to the XPRS rotary file system.

In this respect, we aim to use the findings of our study as a guide for future research.

A study investigating the debris extrusion associated with PTUL compared it with TruNatomy (Dentsply Sirona, Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) and VDW Rotate (VDW GmbH, Munich, Germany) file systems, and no difference was found among the groups regarding the amount of apically extruded debris45. In another study comparing PTUL with ProTaper Next (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) and R-Motion (FKG Dentaire, La Chaux-de-Fonds, Switzerland), no differences were observed among the groups46. In our study, the PTUL rotary file system was compared with the XPRS and WOG systems, and no significant difference was found among the groups in terms of apically extruded debris. The similarity in the amount of apically extruded debris between PTUL and WOG may be attributed to their cross-sectional shapes, and between PTUL and XPRS, it may be attributed to their similar apical diameters. During our literature review, we did not encounter any study comparing the PTUL system with XPRS and WOG systems regarding apical debris extrusion. Therefore, we believe that our study can serve as a guide for future research on this topic. We found no significant differences between the XPRS, PTUL, and WOG groups regarding the amount of apically extruded debris, leading to the acceptance of the null hypothesis (H₀). Although these file systems are manufactured with different alloys and kinematics, each system incorporates features explicitly developed to minimize debris extrusion. These advanced design features may explain the absence of a statistically significant difference between the file systems in our study.

Previous studies have reported conflicting results regarding the influence of reciprocating and rotary motions on apical debris extrusion. While some investigations have indicated that reciprocating files tend to cause greater debris extrusion22,47, others have reported higher extrusion with rotary systems48,49. These discrepancies may be attributed to factors such as instrument design, taper, and cross-sectional geometry. Furthermore, the rotational speed of the instruments has been proposed as a potential factor influencing debris extrusion50. Nevertheless, further studies are required to clarify the role of motion type and file speed in apical debris extrusion.

In our study, apical debris extrusion was observed in all three irrigation activation groups. This finding is consistent with previous studies11,18,29. Ada et al.29 reported that among the irrigation activation methods compared in their study (CI, manual dynamic activation, PUI, and SA) PUI resulted in the least amount of debris extrusion, while SA resulted in the most. Yusufoğlu et al.18 compared the sonic activator EDDY with PUI, photon-initiated photoacoustic streaming, conventional irrigation and reported that PUI resulted in the least amount of apical debris extrusion, followed by conventional irrigation and EDDY, respectively. Similarly, other studies have also indicated that PUI caused less debris extrusion compared with conventional irrigation51,52. Nevertheless, some investigations have reported that the use of different irrigant activation techniques did not produce significant differences in terms of apical debris extrusion53,54. Contrary to these studies, Gawdat et al.55 investigated irrigation activation using CI, PUI, and an XP-endo Finisher regarding debris extrusion. They reported that PUI caused significantly more debris extrusion than the other methods. This study shows similarities with ours regarding the experimental setup used and the irrigation methods included. Consistent with their findings, no significant difference was found in our study between the CI and SA groups; however, the PUI group resulted in significantly more debris extrusion than the other two groups, leading to the acceptance of the alternative hypothesis (H₁). The greater debris extrusion observed with PUI may be attributed to the ultrasonic activation creating small, dense, and circular fluid movement (acoustic streaming) around the ultrasonic tip. This flow may direct the irrigant apically, increasing the amount of debris extruded through the apex.

In a study comparing the use of PUI and CI in combination with two file systems operating with rotational and reciprocating motions (WaveOne and One Shape [Micro-Mega, Besancon, France]), Gummadi et al.32 reported that the WaveOne file caused more debris extrusion than the One Shape file under both irrigation methods. Additionally, CI resulted in greater debris extrusion compared with PUI, regardless of the file system used. In that study, a 30G open-ended irrigation needle was used for irrigation. Previous studies have reported that closed-ended needles result in less apical debris and irrigant extrusion than open-ended needles56,57. Unlike the findings of that study, our research showed that CI resulted in significantly less debris extrusion than PUI. This difference may be attributed to the use of side-vented closed-ended irrigation needles in our study, rather than open-ended needles. In the pairwise comparisons conducted to evaluate the effects of combining irrigation activation methods with file systems on apical debris extrusion, we found that the combination of SA with WOG resulted in significantly less debris extrusion compared to the combination of PUI with any of the three file systems. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study in the literature has compared the combination of SA with different file systems against other irrigation activation methods. Therefore, we aim for our study, which investigated the effects of combining this commonly used system with different file systems on apical debris extrusion, to guide future research in this field.

Our study used single-rooted mandibular premolars with straight canals; therefore, generalizations cannot be made for teeth with curved root canals or different canal morphologies. Although the root canal temperature was simulated, periapical resistance could not be replicated; thus, clinical conditions could not be fully imitated. Contemporary file systems with different kinematic motions were preferred in this study. Although the apical diameters of the XPRS (30/0.01) and PTUL (30/0.09) rotary file systems were the same, the WOG reciprocating file system (35/0.06) had a different apical diameter. Therefore, the lack of standardization in apical diameters can be considered a limitation. However, to minimize this deviation, canal shaping with the WOG system was completed using the file with the closest apical diameter to those of the other systems.

Conclusion

In our study, all file systems and irrigation activation methods resulted in apical debris extrusion. Among the irrigation activation methods, the PUI group exhibited the highest amount of debris extrusion (median = 0.000400 g), which was significantly higher compared with the other groups (p = 0.003). In contrast, the combination of sonic activation with WaveOne Gold produced significantly less debris extrusion (median = 0.000133 g) compared with PUI combined with the three file systems (p = 0.014, p = 0.015, p = 0.009, respectively). These findings suggest that the combined use of SA and WOG during root canal treatment may be considered a favorable option to reduce postoperative pain and acute flare-ups.
