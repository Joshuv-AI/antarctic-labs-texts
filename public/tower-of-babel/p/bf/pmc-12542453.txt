Spectrum and clinical profile of glomerular diseases in a Nepalese cohort, a tertiary care center study: focus on comparing IgA nephropathy with other primary glomerulonephritis
Authors: Kamal Prakash Saud, Dibya Singh Shah, Nishan Bhurtyal, Subodh Kumar Bidari, Sushma Jaishi, Manoj Kumar Timilsaina
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlomerular diseases show global variation in presentation, pathophysiology, and progression. IgAN (IgA nephropathy), the most common primary glomerular disease with higher prevalence in Asians, remains poorly understood regarding its clinical profile, immunopathogenesis, and treatment.ObjectivesThe aim is to study the spectrum histopathological diagnosis of kidney diseases among patients undergoing native kidney biopsy at Tribhuvan University Teaching Hospital, Institute of Medicine (TUTH, IOM) and compare clinical profile of IgA Nephropathy (IgAN) with other primary glomerular diseases i.e. MCD (Minimal Change Disease), FSGS (Focal Segmental Glomerulopathy, MN (Membranous Nephropathy) and C3 glomerulopathy.Materials and methodsThis quantitative longitudinal study was conducted at TUTH, IOM (May 2022–April 2023) on consecutively sampled patients with glomerular diseases undergoing native kidney biopsy. After informed consent, participants underwent clinical evaluation, lab tests, and imaging. Patients without consent or failed/inconclusive biopsies were excluded. Biopsies were analyzed at Dr. Lal Path Labs, India using standard protocols for light, immunofluorescent, and electron microscopy. Data was analyzed using SPSS v28 (Statistical Package for Social Science).ResultsThis study analyzed 223 native kidney biopsy patients (aged 12–81 years, mean 36.3 ± 15.9) with glomerular diseases, showing female predominance (male: female = 1:1.6) and peak incidence in the 20–40 age group (52%). Nephrotic syndrome was the leading biopsy indication (42.2%), followed by nephritic syndrome (16.1%). IgAN (25.1%) and lupus nephritis (21.1%) were most common, followed by FSGS (10.3%), MN (8.1%), and MCD (7.2%). Comparative analysis revealed IgAN presented later than other primary GNs from symptom onset (9.5 ± 6.42 vs. 4.18 ± 3.53 months, p = 0.001) and had stronger associations with hypertension (p = 0.04), nephritic syndrome (p = 0.049), microscopic hematuria (p = 0.001), and unexplained kidney impairment (p = 0.004). IgAN patients had significantly higher serum creatinine (211.36 ± 187.57 mmol/L vs. 112.41 ± 82.91 mmol/L, p = 0.001), lower hemoglobin (12.19 ± 2.07 g/dL vs. 13.26 ± 2.39 g/dL, p = 0.01), and reduced eGFR (63.8 ± 42.7 mL/min/1.73 m² vs. 83.81 ± 38.25 mL/min/1.73 m², p = 0.009) but preserved serum albumin (34.3 ± 6.85 vs. 29.79 ± 8.16 g/L, p = 0.001) compared to other primary GNs. No significant differences were found in demographic distribution, proteinuria, or serological markers between groups.ConclusionNephrotic syndrome was leading kidney biopsy indication. IgAN was the most common histological finding, followed by lupus nephritis. Primary GN was more prevalent than secondary GN. IgAN had a distinct clinical and laboratory profile. The findings emphasize establishing national kidney biopsy registry in Nepal to standardize data and track longitudinal outcomes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Along with diabetes and hypertension, glomerulonephritis is a leading cause of CKD (chronic kidney disease) worldwide [1]. Glomerulonephritis accounts for about 20% of CKD cases in most countries and, unlike major causes of chronic kidney disease such as diabetes and hypertension, they frequently affect young people, and many carry a lifelong burden of chronic kidney disease. Various studies have shown that epidemiology of glomerular diseases have been affected by age, sex, race, geographical location and even occupation of the individuals [2]. Annual incidence has been estimated as 2.5 cases per 100,000 adults for IgA nephropathy, 1.2 per 100,000 for membranous glomerulonephritis, 0.6–0.8 per 100 000 for minimal change disease and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, and 0.2 per 100,000 for membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis. However, these percentages are usually underestimates because they do not consider people with asymptomatic glomerulonephritis variants, those whose glomerulonephritis remits spontaneously, those who have advanced chronic kidney disease at first presentation for whom a kidney biopsy is no longer warranted [3]. Similarly, the incidence of secondary glomerulopathy is reported as 20%-40% for diabetes, 38% for SLE with lupus nephritis and 3–5% for cryoglobulinemic glomerulonephritis and rare in amyloid light chain amyloidosis [4].

This study aimed to determine the prevalence of histopathological diagnoses in patients undergoing native kidney biopsy and to compare the clinical profile of IgA nephropathy with other primary glomerulonephritis among Nepalese patients. Despite its significant burden on young adults, glomerulonephritis remains understudied in Nepal. This study addresses the critical lack of regional data on IgAN, a condition highly prevalent in Asian populations but lacking prospective characterization in the Nepalese context.

Materials and methods

The prospective quantitative study was conducted at TUTH from May 2022 to April 2023 after obtaining ethical clearance from TUTH’s Institutional Review Committee and written informed consent was obtained from all participants or guardians prior to enrollment, ensuring confidentiality and adherence to ethical guidelines throughout the research process. This study enrolled all the patients undergoing native kidney biopsy for suspected glomerulonephritis, excluding transplant biopsies, inconclusive samples, and non-consenting individuals.

Clinical data including demographics, symptomatology (nephrotic/nephritic syndrome, hematuria), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes), and laboratory parameters (serum creatinine, eGFR, 24-hour proteinuria) were systematically collected. Ultrasound-guided percutaneous kidney biopsies were performed following standard protocols, with samples processed at accredited pathology laboratories. Socioeconomic status was classified using the modified Kuppuswamy scale [5]. Statistical analysis employed SPSS version 28, utilizing descriptive statistics for baseline characteristics and comparative tests (t-tests, Mann-Whitney U) to evaluate differences between IgA nephropathy and other primary glomerulonephritis, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

At TUTH, 280 biopsies were performed between May 2022 and April 2023. Of these, 230 were native kidney biopsies, with 7 yielding inconclusive results. Thus, the final analysis included 223 biopsies (or patients).

Among the 223 native kidney biopsies, IgA nephropathy (IgAN) was the most common histological diagnosis (25.1%, n = 56), followed by lupus nephritis (21.1%, n = 47). Other diagnoses included FSGS (10.3%, n = 23), MN (8.1%, n = 18), MCD (7.2%, n = 16), DPGN (Diffuse Proliferative Glomerulonephritis) (5.8%, n = 13), C3 Glomerulopathy (4.9%, n = 11), hypertensive kidney disease (4.9%, n = 11), ANCA-associated GN (anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies) (3.6%, n = 8), acute interstitial nephritis (2.7%, n = 6), diabetic kidney disease (2.2%, n = 5), amyloidosis (1.8%, n = 4), cortical necrosis (0.9%, n = 2), anti-GBM disease (0.9%, n = 2), and thrombotic microangiopathy (0.4%, n = 1) [Fig. 1].

The mean age was 36.3 ± 15.9 years (range: 12–81 years), with the 20–39-year age group being most affected (48%, n = 107). Females constituted a larger proportion of the biopsied cases (male-to-female ratio: 1:1.6). However, no statistically significant sex predilection was observed across the diagnostic categories.

IgA nephropathy (IgAN) was present in 45.2% (n = 56) of the 124 patients with primary GN, whereas other primary GN were present in 54.8% (n = 68). Mean ages were 34 ± 12.24 years for IgAN and 38 ± 16.02 years for other primary GN (p = 0.241) [Fig. 3].

Most patients were from Bagmati Province and belonged to upper-lower and lower-middle socioeconomic classes in both groups. Nepal is administratively divided into seven provinces, with Bagmati Province where TUTH is located, being the most populous and urbanized. This province includes the capital city, Kathmandu, and is characterized by better healthcare accessibility compared to rural provinces. Patients from other provinces, particularly those with lower socioeconomic backgrounds and limited access to specialized nephrology services, are also referred to TUTH. There was no statistical predilection for socioeconomic status, geographic distribution and sex.

Clinical assessment revealed hypertension had statistically significant association with IgAN (p = 0.04). Symptomatically, anasarca (51.5% vs. 10.7%, p = 0.001) and dyspnea (36.8% vs. 7.1%, p = 0.001) predominated in other primary GN, while sore throat was exclusive to IgAN (8.9%, p = 0.01). The mean presentation delay was longer for IgAN (6.38 ± 6.23 months vs. 4.18 ± 3.53 months, p = 0.001), with significant differences in syndromic presentations: nephritic syndrome (p = 0.05), unexplained CKD (p = 0.004), and microscopic hematuria (p = 0.001) associated with IgAN, while nephrotic syndrome strongly correlated with other primary GN (p = 0.005) [Fig. 4].

Kidney function parameters showed marked disparities at presentation: IgAN patients presented with lower mean eGFR (63.8 ± 42.7 mL/min/1.73 m² vs. 83.81 ± 38.25 mL/min/1.73 m², p = 0.009) [Table 1], greater kidney impairment (serum creatinine 211.36 ± 187.57 mmol/L vs. 112.41 ± 82.91 mmol/L, p < 0.001) [Table 2]. Laboratory analysis revealed IgAN patients had lower hemoglobin (12.19 ± 2.07 g/dL vs. 13.26 ± 2.39 g/dL, p < 0.01) but better-preserved serum albumin (34.3 ± 6.85 g/L vs. 26.07 ± 6.12 g/L, p < 0.0001). Other primary GN showed greater proteinuria (24-hour UTP 5.07 ± 3.19 g/day vs. 3.52 ± 2.12 g/day, p < 0.0001), with nephrotic-range proteinuria significantly associated (p = 0.001) versus sub-nephrotic range in IgAN (p = 0.001). Semiquantitative proteinuria (≥ 3+) was common in both groups without significant difference (p = 0.904) [Table 3].

Discussion

In this study nephrotic syndrome (42.2%) was the primary biopsy indication, aligning with global trends [6, 7].

Single center retrospective studies from different centers of Nepal have shown varied prevalence different glomerular diseases. For example, IgA nephropathy was reported in 9.8% at TUTH (194 biopsies) [8], 9.8% at Bir Hospital (398 biopsies) [9] and 20.7% at College of Medical Sciences, Chitwan (829 biopsies) [10].

In contrast, our work represents the first prospective characterization of native kidney biopsies from Nepal’s central region (Bagmati Province), which accounts for nearly one-third of the national population. Importantly, we observed a higher prevalence of IgA nephropathy (25.1%) compared to earlier national retrospective series (9.8–20.7%). Similar pattern of variability was noted for other glomerular diseases as well suggesting either an evolving epidemiological trend, improved diagnostic yield, or differences in biopsy practices [8–10].

Unlike Brazilian data (secondary GN: 52.4%) [11], primary and secondary glomerulopathies were equally prevalent (50.67% vs. 49.33%), with IgAN (vs. FSGS in Brazil) dominating primary cases, likely due to geographic/ethnic factors.

Hypertension (50.2%), a leading comorbidity, matched global rates [11, 12], while limb swelling (49.8%) and anasarca (31.4%) mirrored local symptom patterns [11].

These comparisons highlight the novel contributions of our study. It offers more timely and reliable epidemiological data than earlier retrospective national reports being the first prospective study from a high-volume center in the most populated region of Nepal. This could help to reduce selection bias and capture a more accurate disease distribution. Furthermore, this study also provides first detailed comparative analysis of the clinical and laboratory profiles of IgA nephropathy with those of other primary glomerulonephritides within Nepal, going beyond simply reporting prevalence. This data reveals critical insights, such as the significant delay in presentation and more advanced kidney impairment at diagnosis in IgAN patients, which has direct implications for local clinical practice.

Comparing clinical profile of IgAN and other primary GN

IgAN was the most common histological finding (25.1%, n = 56) while other primary GN were 30.49% (n = 68) out of all biopsies. Most patients in both the categories were from Bagmati Province and belonged to upper lower and lower middle socioeconomic class as per Modified Kuppuswamy Scale [5].

Patients in both IgAN and other primary GN were concentrated in the age group 20–39 years. The mean age of IgAN was 34 ± 12.24 years and that of others was 38 ± 16.02 years (p = 0.192). In a retrospective study in India the mean age of IgAN patients was 39.8 ± 12.2 years [13]. The finding of mean age among primary GN is consistent with the Brazilian study as well [11].

The male to female ratio of IgAN was 1:1.4 and that of other primary GN was 1:0.9. The North Indian study of IgAN revealed male to female ratio of 1: 0.6 [13]. And the Brazilian study revealed male to female ratio of IgAN to be 0.5:1 and that among other primary GN was 1: 1.2 [11]. Similarly other Indian Studies of IgAN revealed male percentage to be ranging from 65% to 83.3% [14–17].

Hypertension had a significant association with IgAN compared to other GN (P = 0.04). Hypertension was present in 39.4% to 93% among Indian studies of IgAN [14–16]. Similarly, the Brazilian study revealed Hypertension to be prevalent in 81.1% of IgAN. This association of hypertension with IgAN compared to other primary is probably due to delayed presentation of IgAN often in advanced CKD.

In our study Anasarca and dyspnea were significantly associated with other Primary GN (P = 0.001) compared to IgAN while sore throat was significantly associated with IgAN (P = 0.04) compared to other primary GN. These findings are due to nephrotic syndrome predominance among MCD, FSGS and MN. At the same time, association of pharyngitis is common among IgAN compared to other primary GN. Syn-pharyngitis hematuria is a noted finding of IgAN though uncommon symptom [18].

The mean duration of presentation to our center of IgAN was 9.5 ± 6.42 months while that of other primary GN was 4.18 ± 3.53 months with statistically significant association (p = 0.001). This delayed presentation of IgAN is probably due to the chronic progressive nature of the disease often presenting in stages of CKD. MCD, FSGS, MN have presentations with nephrotic syndromes and troublesome volume overload with tendency to seek health care earlier.

Among various syndromic presentations Nephritic Syndrome, Microscopic hematuria and unexplained kidney impairment had statistically significant association with IgAN with P value of 0.049, 0.001 and 0.004 respectively. Similarly Nephrotic Syndrome had statistically significant association MCD, FSGS, MN and C3 glomerulopathy with P value of 0.001. North Indian study revealed that among IgAN patients RPGN was present among 40% followed by CKD (33.3%), Nephrotic Syndrome (18.3%), Nephritic Syndrome (5%) and microscopic hematuria (3.3%) while nephrotic syndrome was predominant among other primary GN consistent with our study [13].

Patients with IgAN had statistically significant (p = 0.009) lower mean GFR at presentation compared to other primary GN (63.8 ± 42.7 vs. 83.81 ± 38.25). Similarly, IgAN had more severe level of kidney impairment compared to MCD, FSGS MN and C3 glomerulopathy (211.36 ± 187.57 mmol/L vs. 112.41 ± 82.91, mmol/L). Among Indian studies the mean creatinine ranged from 149 to 247 mmol/L among IgAN patients while it was 97 to 132 mmol/L among other primary GN [13]. These findings of low GFR and more kidney impairment among IgAN is indicative of its chronic progressive inflammatory nature. At the same time kidney impairment of Other Primary GN is more likely to be AKI related to nephrotic syndrome.

In our study of lab parameters there was a statistically significant association of hemoglobin with IgAN with its tendency to be lower than that of MCD, FSGS, MN and C3 glomerulopathy patients (p < 0.01). Among north Indian studies of IgAN the mean of hemoglobin and serum albumin was 12.1 ± 1.8 gm/dl [13].

Similarly, patients with MCD, MN, FSGS and C3 glomerulopathy had statistically significant association with Serum Albumin and 24-hour UTP (P = 0.0001) whereby there was a tendency of severe urinary loss of albumin compared to that of IgAN. At the same time IgAN patients had statistical association with Sub-nephrotic proteinuria (P = 0.001) and MCD, FSGS, MN and C3 Glomerulopathy had statistical association with Nephrotic range proteinuria (P = 0.001). These findings further illustrate severe proteinuria of other primary GN. Among North Indian studies of IgAN the mean of 24-hour UTP ranged from 1.7 to 2.9 gm / day. Sub-nephrotic proteinuria was present ranging from 45% TO 81.2% of the patients [13–17]. In Brazilian study the mean proteinuria was 1.9 (range 1.1 to 3.7) gm/day [11].

There was no statistical association of semiquantitative proteinuria with disease diagnosis in our study with a P-value 0.904. Most of the patients had 3 + proteinuria among both categories which is consistent with North Indian study of glomerular diseases [19]. Similarly microscopic hematuria was seen ranging from 16% to 95.8% among other north Indian studies of IgAN [13–17].

Conclusion

This prospective study of glomerular disease from May 2022 to April 2023 among patients (n = 223) undergoing native kidney biopsy revealed that the most common indication of kidney biopsy was Nephrotic syndrome followed by Nephritic syndrome. The most common histological diagnosis was IgA Nephropathy followed by Lupus Nephritis. The most common symptom at presentation was body swelling. On comparison of clinical profile IgAN with other primary GN, IgAN showed tendency of presentation with Nephritic syndrome, unexplained kidney impairment and microscopic hematuria compared to nephrotic syndrome as major presentation of other primary GN. Higher serum creatinine, low hemoglobin, low GFR, sub nephrotic proteinuria with relatively preserved serum albumin correlated well with IgAN.

However, this study had limitations. As a single-center study, milder cases that have never reached our tertiary hospital might have been overlooked leading to underestimation of the burden. We relied on an external center for biopsy processing, a reminder of the challenges faced by institutions lacking advanced tools like electron microscopy. Most importantly, we couldn’t track long-term outcomes for these patients, leaving their journeys beyond diagnosis untold. Thus, future efforts should prioritize early screening in high-risk populations and establish a national biopsy registry to monitor long-term outcomes.
