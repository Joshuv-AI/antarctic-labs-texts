An economic and environmental evaluation of Taiwan’s manure to energy applications
Authors: Chih-Chun Kung, Shuyin Hu, Tsung-Ju Lee, Juan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryCost and benefit information is essential for Taiwan to determine economically feasible manure applications. This study adopts techno-economic analysis and life cycle assessment to evaluate manure’s energy potential under six composting, pyrolysis, and anaerobic digestion possibilities. Biogas fertilizer and biochar use in agriculture is also examined. The results show that the cost-effective manure applications depend on the recycling method, input of raw materials, and economic scale. Pyrolysis yields up to $175.74 per ton of feedstock, but composting is subject to a loss when emission prices skyrocket. Hog and cattle manure applications may break even in costs, but chicken manure could incur a loss of $25.28 under mesophilic and $19.16 under thermophilic fermentation. Manure distribution would also substantially affect large-scale biopower development due to high transportation and operation costs, and the development cost could dramatically vary by region. The 2023 US dollar is used as the basis.Subject areas: Environmental science, Applied sciences, Energy engineering

Introduction

Economic and environmental sustainability is essential to modern development, and one of the most critical issues in achieving this goal is reducing fossil fuels and emissions1,2 while keeping the energy supply stable.3 Thus, exploring reliable renewable energy sources is crucial to approaching the sustainable concept. Over the past few decades, scientists and practitioners have innovated many renewable energy technologies such as bioenergy,4 solar energy,5 wind power,6 and geothermal energy.7 Governments have applied these technologies to improve energy sustainability, economic growth, environmental protection, and climate change mitigation.8,9,10

However, in many countries and regions, renewable resources are not appropriately evaluated and utilized, understating the renewable energy potential in these areas and resulting in a less reliable renewable energy development framework. Taiwan, importing more than 96.8% of fossil energy,11 has realized that ultimately reliance on foreign energy will hinder its social development. Thus, it has promulgated environmental law to forbid the increase in fossil fuel use and has been actively looking for renewable energy possibilities to maintain its energy supply.12,13 However, as indicated in Figure 1, Taiwan’s renewable energy production is insignificant relative to the total energy supply. More promotions and policies should be implemented.

Under such considerations, the focus of Taiwan’s renewable energy development frameworks is to maximize renewable energy supply rather than mitigate climate change. This fact implies that while numerous renewable energy possibilities exist, energy-oriented technology would be preferred to offset-oriented ones. Due to the recent nuclear-free homeland policy, Taiwan will phase out its nuclear power plants, which provide approximately 28 billion kWh each year. Because the Greenhouse Emission and Management Act was in effect since 2015, renewable energy is the only possibility to replace the lost nuclear power. Since Taiwan produces significant amount of manures and most of them are not engaged in renewable energy production, the administrative authority of Taiwan is particularly interested in manure-based renewable energy possibilities.14 However, for manure-based technology to be applied on a large scale, economic feasibility is crucial because only marketable technologies can be considered sustainable. Otherwise, renewable energy relying only on government subsidies would exhaust the social capital and eventually be driven out.

Manure released by livestock and poultry is rich in carbon (C), nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), and other nutrients, and it is widely used for traditional composting methods or bioconversion technologies.15 Although such applications are beneficial, they will significantly harm the environment and human health if not used properly. If manure is directly discharged into surface water or farmland without proper treatment in many areas, it will cause severe environmental pollution problems.16 In addition, livestock and poultry manure emissions continue to grow significantly, accounting for 14.5% of global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and reaching 710 million tons of carbon dioxide (CO2) equivalent per year.17,18 Therefore, a more economical and environment--friendly approach of manure should be explored and evaluated.19

This study aims to evaluate the energy potential of livestock and poultry manure under various processing technologies and examine how such applications improve Taiwan’s energy security, environmental quality, and social sustainability. Manures from hog, cattle, and chicken are the primary sources to be evaluated in this study because they are the most significant manure resources in Taiwan (In Taiwan, more than 82% of manure comes from hog, cattle, and chicken. Based on Taiwan’s Environmental Protection Agency,20 annual production of hog manure is 8.47 million tons, 1.13 million tons from cattle, and 3.65 million tons from chicken.). Three manure-processing approaches will be examined.

To examine their values attached to renewable energy production, climate change mitigation, and agricultural sustainability, a techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life cycle assessment (LCA) will be applied. In the TEA section, we will evaluate economic components such as raw material collection, transportation efforts, manure storage and processing, facility construction and operation, and energy sales for each manure source under the six processing possibilities. The LCA section will estimate the emission consequences from energy used in the mechanical operation, material hauling and processing, and plant construction and operation. The results will be compared with other manure-to-energy studies so that the insights into this study can be explored and specified. In addition, since Taiwan has not participated in the Verified Carbon Standard (VCS) and its emission reduction cannot be traded internationally, the value of emission offset is presented at domestic prices.

This study bridges the gaps between waste treatment, renewable energy supply, and agricultural sustainability by accommodating alternative processing possibilities with different focuses. The insights into the study are helpful for several reasons. First, waste recycling and utilization are the primary concerns of many countries and regions, and thus, evaluating the energy potential of waste resources benefits energy security and is potentially helpful in shifting the energy structure. Second, by examining alternative production possibilities of manure, the study delineates approaches that can be considered economically feasible, reducing the requirement of government subsidies and improving social sustainability. With the completion of this study, several contributions are made and addressed as follows.

First, the results offer a comparable basis for alternative waste recycling and treatment approaches. Incorporating hauling distance, hauling efforts, and alternative uses of pyrolysis outputs specifies the crucial production factors and thus offers a more general picture of manure utilization rather than a simple calculation of energy potential. The results, therefore, provide valuable information to decision-makers to review and reform current policies and improve social welfare. Second, the study highlights the investment and operating risks associated with manure application. For technologies that may be currently unprofitable and unmarketable, we show that a properly designed green-financing system may alleviate the financial risks of manure-based biopower projects, even without government subsidy. The results are remarkably insightful for the large-scale manure utilization in Taiwan. Third, the results indicate that manure application benefits not only energy security but also agricultural sustainability. The use of biochar and biofertilizer can improve soil quality and sequester carbon, offering another revenue source that makes such applications more attractive.

Results

Economic analysis

For any technology to be widely adopted, it must be profitable. This section analyzes the economic and environmental benefits of each production possibility, including biomass collection and transportation, storage and pretreatment, plant construction and operation, energy sales, transportation of biochar, agricultural benefits, and carbon sequestration. The results provide a basis for profitability, which could be useful to investigate the necessity of government subsidies or social capital.

Composting

The purpose of composting is to convert organic waste into organic fertilizer. Since most composting plants operate off-site,21 the economics of composting consist of collection, transportation, plant processing, marketing, and agricultural application. Total costs consist primarily of fixed and variable costs. Fixed costs are one-time expenditures, including the discing of land, construction of facility, and purchase of equipment. In contrast, variable costs are expenditures associated with plant operations, including the purchase of raw and auxiliary materials, transportation, labor, and other management costs. Based on data from an operating off-site composting plant22 near the farm, the composting plant will process 80 tons of manure per day or 26,000 tons per year, with a daily organic fertilizer production of 35 tons per day. With a 10-year useful life, fixed costs are depreciated at a 7% borrowing rate with a 2% annual inflation rate.22 Table 1 shows the results.

In addition to product sales, organic fertilizers create value with farm-site applications. Lan et al.23 show that replacing 30% and 50% of chemical fertilizer with organic fertilizer can increase rice yield by 7.7% and 6.5%, respectively. To be conservative, we assume that organic fertilizer replaces 20% of chemical fertilizer in rice fields, with a resultant 1.5% of yield increase. Based on the crop budget data from the Taiwan Council of Agriculture,24 the value of rice production per hectare is $10,531, and we calculated the benefit of applying organic fertilizer to be $157.97/ha.

The increase in crop value is not the final because we still need to take other on-site costs and benefits into account. Based on Kung and Mu,25 the application cost of per-ton organic fertilizer is $40, and with an assumed 20% reduction in fertilizer and seed application,26,27 per-hectare cost reduction of seed and fertilizer is $65.3 and $74.3, respectively. Additionally, organic matter in composting is rapidly degraded and mineralized, and its beneficial effects are relatively short-lived.28 Heavy rainfall and runoff will lead to loss of organic fertilizer; thus, we assume that agricultural benefits will reduce by 50%, with a farming benefit of $128.78/ha. Converting the benefits to per ton of feedstock, we can get an agricultural benefit of $9.01/ton for composting one ton of hog manure, $7.73 for cattle manure, and $1.27 for chicken manure. In total, the net benefit of composting is $11.58 for hog manure, $4.4 for cattle manure, and $20.25 for chicken manure.

Anaerobic fermentation

Feedstock collection, hauling, and storage

Biomass utilization includes production, harvesting, collection, and compaction costs.29 Livestock manure has a low utilization value and is usually discarded or incinerated, so the collection cost is relatively low. Based on the Taiwan Council of Agriculture,30 Kung and McCarl,31 and Aftab et al.,32 per-ton manure collection and compaction cost is assumed to be $10. The Taiwan Institute of Agricultural Research33 reports that compaction effort is generally 10%–20% higher for chicken manure. We, thus, assume that the compaction cost for chicken manure is $12 per ton. Since manure needs to be stored somewhere before being transferred to the plant and the storage cost per cubic meter is somewhere between $6 and $20,34 we assume it to be $15 per ton. Energy sale

The biogas produced by digestion process is assumed for power generation, and we calculated the return per ton of feedstock used for power generation under mesophilic and thermophilic fermentation based on the prevailing electricity price of $0.16/kWh.11 Under the mesophilic mode, the revenues of energy sale from per ton of hog manure, cattle manure, and chicken manure are estimated to be $59.82, $41.04, and $31.54, respectively. The benefits of thermophilic fermentation are higher because it produces more biogas, and thus, the energy sales from per ton of manure under thermophilic fermentation are $60.57 for hog manure, $121.86 for cattle manure, and $38.71 for chicken manure.

Value of biogas fertilizer

Biogas fertilizers, organic residues from digestion, are rich in nutrients such as ammonium, P, and K, all of which are valuable in maintaining and improving the soil quality.36 Biogas fertilizer can replace or reduce the use of mineral fertilizers for crop production.37,38 For example, Koszel and Lorencowicz38 and Ehmann et al.39 show that land fertilizer may increase production between 17.8% and 36%, depending on the site characteristics, and Tang et al.40 also show that applying biogas fertilizer could increase rice yield by 8.9%. Thus, with a 5 ton/ha application rate of biogas fertilizer, we conservatively assume that it could save 20% in seed and chemical fertilizer and increase crop yield by 3%.

The crop budget data24 show that the per-hectare rice value is $10,531, and using the constant fertilizer cost of $40 per ton, we estimate that the benefit of biogas fertilizer application is $315.9/ha, with a $65.3 decrease in seed cost and a reduction in fertilizer cost of $74.3 per hectare. Like composting, we also consider the potential loss of the biogas fertilizer due to heavy rainfall and runoff,12 resulting in a farming return of $207.76/ha. Value of biochar

In contrast to biogas fertilizers and organic fertilizers, biochar can be used not only as a source of power generation but also as a soil amendment.46,47 Lehmann48 shows that when biochar is used as a soil amendment, it could improve the soil quality and keep carbon in a more stable form. Gaunt and Lehmann49 indicate that biochar application reduced nitrogen leaching by 60% and increased crop yields by 38%–45%, and Lehmann50 points out that biochar may improve fertilizer, seed, and irrigation efficiency by approximately 20%. With such improvements in soil quality, crop yield could be substantially improved, and the production cost can be significantly reduced. For example, Nehls51 reports that rice yields could increase by 15%–320% with proper biochar application. However, since the results will also considerably vary under different biochar application rates and the increase generally depends on the soil characteristics, we assume a commonly accepted application rate of 5 tons per hectare, with a conservative yield increase of 5%. (The Taiwan Council of Agriculture (transformed to Ministry of Agriculture on August 1, 2023) scheduled to apply 14 million tons of biochar on approximately 300,000 ha of cropland. The planned application rate is 40 tons per hectare and the announced benefit from such applications is a 5%–15% increase in crop yield.52,53,54,55 However, some scholars believe that such a large-scale application requires a more scientific experiment and evaluation. So far, this program is not employed yet and this study uses a conservative 5% yield increase for estimation.)

With the crop budget data from the Taiwan Council of Agriculture,24 we obtain per-hectare agricultural benefit with biochar application to be $526.6, subject to an application cost of $40 per ton of biochar. Similarly, we assumed a 20% reduction in fertilizer and seed application26,27 per hectare; seed and fertilizer cost reductions are $65.3 and $74.3, respectively. The irrigation cost of rice filed is $165.93 per hectare, and based on the 10% reduction in irrigation water use,56 the irrigation saving from biochar application is estimated to be $16.59.

Additionally, since stormwater and runoff can lead to loss of biochar,57 we further assumed that 10 years of application would be required to obtain permanent agronomic benefits at a discount rate of 5%.46 Thus, the return per hectare from biochar application would be $673, and we then convert this measure to the per-ton feedstock basis. Environmental costs and benefits

Composting

Feedstock production, collection, hauling, and operation

Thus, to appropriately estimate the full carbon footprint associated with manure treatment, the energy used in transportation should be taken into account. We use the data of Jin et al.60 on the energy consumption of compost over the entire life cycle process and adopt Equation 2 to calculate the CO2 emissions during the biomass transportation. Reduced input and carbon sequestration

Organic fertilizers can replace part of the chemical fertilizers. According to Jin et al.,62 the N fertilizer used per hectare for rice is 69.65 kg, the P fertilizer is 41.79 kg, and the potash fertilizer is 111.44 kg. Thus, 13.93 kg of N, 8.36 kg of P, and 22.29 kg of K would be reduced under 20% fertilizer saving. According to the United States Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA),63 the emission savings are 0.0041 MT of CO2 for hog manure, 0.0035 MT for cattle manure, and 0.0049 for chicken manure. The application of organic fertilizers on farmland also promotes carbon sequestration. Jeong et al.64 found that composting increases soil carbon storage and reduces CO2 emissions. Due to high precipitation and rapid water flow, part of the organic fertilizer may be washed away, which reduces the effect of carbon sequestration. Thus, the emission offset per ton of hog manure is 0.0359 MT of CO2, and the value is 0.0307 MT for cattle manure, and 0.0384 MT for chicken manure. Taking all these emissions and offset factors into account, the results indicate that the emissions from manure compositing are less than 10 kg per ton of biomass, with the lowest emission from hog manure.

Anaerobic plant operation

The use of fossil fuels in digestion plants generates CO2 emissions. Chen et al.35 show that approximately 300 tons of natural gas will be consumed for a 100,000 plant. According to the GREET emission factor, the emissions from per ton biomass are 0.021 tons under thermophilic digestion and 0.01 tons under mesophilic digestion, due to the fact that more energy is required to maintain the optimal temperature under the thermophilic digestion.67

Electricity generation offset

Coal is the primary energy source in Taiwan.68 In this study, we assume that the electricity generated from anaerobic fermentation will only replace coal-based electricity. Based on the GREET emission factor and electricity output, the CO2 reduction is 0.178 MT for hog manure, 0.122 MT for cattle manure, and 0.094 MT for chicken manure under the mesophilic digestion, and 0.181 MT for hog manure, 0.363 MT for cattle manure, and 0.115 for chicken manure under thermophilic digestion.

Pyrolysis plant operation

Energy used by heat reactors in pyrolysis plants would also generate CO2 emissions. Following McCarl et al.,41 a 100,000 pyrolysis plant consumes 620 tons of natural gas and 1,674 tons of diesel fuel per year. According to the greenhouse gases, regulated emissions, and energy use in transportation (GREET) emission factor, the emission from processing per ton manure under fast pyrolysis is 0.0638 MT of CO2 and the value is 0.0495 MT for slow pyrolysis and gasification. Fast pyrolysis has a higher emission ratio because it requires more energy to decompose the biomass.

Discussion

Sensitivity analysis

Profitability is the crucial index determining whether technology can be widely employed. We have examined various economic and environmental components and totalized their values. However, since market conditions constantly change and product prices are subject to variation, it is necessary to investigate further how price changes may alter profitability. Figure 2 provides a sensitivity analysis for hog manure. Figure 2A shows the variation in feedstock cost, and Figure 2B delineates the changes in construction and operation costs.

Profitability under changes

(A) Effect of feedstock cost on profits.

(B) Effect of plant cost on profits.

As illustrated in Figure 2A, an increase in feedstock costs decreases profitability. If the manure cost increases by 133%, anaerobic fermentation becomes unprofitable. In comparison, pyrolysis incurs losses when costs increase by more than 294%, and composting incurs losses when costs increase by more than 210%. The results indicate that, ceteris paribus, hog manure remains profitable as long as the feedstock cost does not double. Figure 2B shows that an increase in plant cost could significantly impact profitability. Pyrolysis incurs a loss when pyrolysis plant costs increase by 135%, anaerobic fermentation incurs a loss when anaerobic fermentation plant costs exceed 120%, and composting incurs a loss when composting plant costs exceed 70%.

Figure 3 shows the effects of changes in output prices. Specifically, this scenario examines the changes in energy price for pyrolysis and fermentation and the sales price for composted organic fertilizer. The results indicate that the output price is the most crucial factor affecting profitability. To remain profitable, fast pyrolysis requires a price above $83.45 per MWh, $105.91 for slow pyrolysis, $148.93 for gasification, $337.36 for mesophilic digestion, and $276.41 for thermophilic digestion. Composting, however, requires a selling price per ton of organic fertilizer higher than $51.68 to be profitable. Among all feedstocks, chicken manure is the most affected by changes in electricity prices under anaerobic digestion. If output price varies substantially, pyrolysis and composting may be better options than anaerobic digestion.

Change in output prices

Investigations into changes in fertilizer prices are also examined. The 2024 average potash fertilizer prices in Taiwan are $0.345 per kg, $0.277 for N fertilizer, and $0.172 for P fertilizer.77 Thus, with 69.65 kg of nitrogen fertilizer, 41.79 kg of phosphorus fertilizer, and 111.44 kg of potash fertilizer, every 1% increase in fertilizer price would increase production cost by $0.649. Since the biochar application is assumed to reduce fertilizer use by 20%, an increase in fertilizer price would have a relatively trivial effect on profitability. However, if the fertilizer prices decline dramatically, the cost savings from biochar would be lower.

Policy implications

Comparison across treatments

Economically, pyrolysis and composting are more profitable than anaerobic fermentation, and pyrolysis generates more biopower than fermentation. While Taiwan is interested in improving domestic energy production and mainly focuses on maximizing renewable energy production, it does not mean that pyrolysis should dominate anaerobic fermentation. From the social point of view, agricultural sustainability and climate change mitigation are also crucial issues.78,79,80 It is unlikely to sacrifice the environment significantly in exchange for a slight energy increase. Thus, a more thorough consideration of manure treatment is necessary.

Studies81,82,83 have shown that manure recycling and utilization provide many environmental benefits, such as reduced water pollution, odor emissions, and improved soil fertility. In addition, pyrolysis and anaerobic fermentation have great potential to reduce GHGs, with carbon reduction benefits of up to $41/ton. The administrative authority of Taiwan needs to balance the environmental benefits and biopower production by focusing on the potential of manure and taking agricultural feedback into account.

Utilization of other organic inputs

Agronomic benefits increase when biochar and biomass fertilizer output increase. Song et al.84 indicate that biochar helps retain water-soluble NO3- and K+ ions while increasing the production of carbohydrates and flavonoids, and Odlare et al.85 and Insam et al.83 show that an increase in nutrient availability, metabolic activity, and soil microbial activity can be improved with biofertilizers. The results show that when fast pyrolysis is converted to slow pyrolysis, the energy value will decrease by $23.7–$72.6 while the agronomic value will increase by $4.07–$12.92. If thermophilic digestion is replaced by mesophilic digestion, the energy value will decrease by $1–$81, and the agronomic value is expected to increase by $0.99–$12.87. While the energy value seems to outweigh the agronomic value, many environmental benefits are hard to be appropriately estimated and reflected.

A potential approach to improve the agronomic value without losing too much in energy value is to utilize more recyclable materials. For example, pyrolysis can decompose almost all organic materials such as sewer sludge, municipal solid wastes, and forest residuals.32,86,87 Expanding input categories can make the waste-to-energy development more diversified while sustaining the environmental quality.

Development of a regional emission trade scheme

One crucial insight derived from the results involves the realization of emission values. We show that the development strategy depends not only on output prices but also on offsetting emissions. However, since Taiwan is not qualified in VCS, it is difficult for firms to trade their emission offset in the international market.76 Antle and McCarl88 point out that most practices are judged as economically inferior to existing treatments because emission values are not properly realized. Thus, a local emission trade scheme development should be promoted; otherwise, the emission value attached to manure treatment cannot be realized, and the profitability of these applications will be substantially reduced.89,90

Concluding remarks

This study compares manure recycling pathways such as composting, pyrolysis, and anaerobic digestion, and examines their cost-effectiveness in achieving energy security and environmental protection in Taiwan. TEA is applied to calculate the economic factors, and LCA is used to evaluate the environmental consequences during the production life cycle. The results show that pyrolysis is most profitable in terms of economic and environmental benefits. In the case of fast pyrolysis, hog manure has a biopower potential of 9.259 million GWh, with a revenue of $1,481 million in energy sale and 5.029 million MT of CO2 reduction. The revenue from composting comes mainly from selling organic fertilizer, amounting to $161 million. While cattle and chicken manures can be profitable, losses may occur when anaerobic fermentation is applied. For example, one ton of chicken manure will result in a loss of $25.28 under mesophilic fermentation and $19.16 under thermophilic mode.

Based on the results, several key findings are revealed. First, composting and pyrolysis were profitable, while anaerobic fermentation would sometimes incur losses due to high production costs and low energy-conversion rates. However, fermentation can still be valuable to maintain agricultural systems and possibly improve food security. Second, the cost-effectiveness of manure may depend on the type of recycling methods, input raw materials, and scale of the economy. Expanding input category may balance the development paths and improve returns to scale. Third, pyrolysis and anaerobic fermentation had great potential to reduce environmental emissions, but composting is relatively inexpensive in practice that would alleviate financial burden facing the firms.

Limitations of the study

This study employs TEA and LCA to analyze manure’s energy potential and environmental benefits under different recycling and processing methods, but this approach may also have some limitations. First, the distribution of manure in Taiwan is not uniformly characterized, and this variation in distribution means that some cities cannot develop large-scale manure applications. However, under the proposed promotion policies, most cities are eager to participate with the manure-treatment programs because part of the development cost can be recovered by the promotion policies. This will incur a social loss, but the examination of the social cost is beyond the scope of this study. The concern to the social consequence may be an important topic for future research. Second, the CO2 emission from composting outweighs the emission reduction, and such a loss may be economically substantial if the emission price skyrockets. The loss may be insignificant due to Taiwan’s imperfect emission trade scheme. Still, future research could incorporate market design and other factors and apply the sectoral analysis to examine the optimal conditions of manure practice.

Composting

Composting, a traditional method of treating manure,101 is a biochemical process that uses microbial fermentation to degrade organic matter in manure, generate high temperatures to promote manure decomposition, and remove pathogenic microorganisms and weed seeds. Most of the composting bio-oxidation reactions occur under aerobic conditions.102,103 Such applications will ultimately result in the formation of compounds and humus beneficial for plant growth.

Composting is easy to operate and has low investment costs, making it a popular manure management method. Composting benefits soil and crops. From the physical aspect, the size of the particles in the compost helps to create space conducive to water retention and air exchange, thereby improving the soil structure to maintain soil physicochemical and microbial balance.104 From a chemical aspect, composting can increase the concentration of organic matter, thus enhancing the stability of soil fertility and mineralization, which eventually increases crop yield.81

In addition, microorganisms in the composting process help to degrade toxic substances and reduce the bioavailability of heavy metals.105 Zheng et al.106 found that the concentration of Cu, Zn, Cr, and As in the exchangeable state of hog manure was significantly reduced by aerobic composting, and Selvam et al.107 found a significant reduction in chloramphenicol, sulfadiazine, and ciprofloxacin during composting of hog manure. However, depending on the manure types, composting may contain high levels of heavy metals and emit substantial amounts of GHGs, limiting the popularization of composting technology.108

Pyrolysis

Pyrolysis is a thermochemical process in which biomass is heated in high-temperature reactors without oxygen.86 Depending on the heating rate and residence time, pyrolysis can be divided into three main categories: fast, slow, and gasification.109,110 During pyrolysis, the feedstock is decomposed into flammable gases, liquefied into bio-oil and water, and coked into biochar.94 Because bio-oil contains hundreds of organic compounds,111 it can be refined to produce chemicals.94,112,113 Biochar is a solid product of pyrolysis, which is generally used to generate heat and reduce energy costs. However, if biochar is used as a soil conditioner, it will improve soil fertility, reduce fertilizer and irrigation needs, increase crop yields, and store carbon.48,49,114 Biogas is a flammable gas composed primarily of hydrogen (H2), carbon monoxide (CO), methane (CH4), and carbon dioxide (CO2) that can be burned to generate electricity.115 The output ratio generally depends on the form of pyrolysis and the feedstock type.82,116 Table Outputs presents the biochar, bio-oil, and biogas yields of alternative possibilities. In general, fast pyrolysis produces more bio-oil, gasification produces more biogas, and slow pyrolysis produces more biochar.
