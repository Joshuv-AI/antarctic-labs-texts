Study design of the EDGEFIRM trial: transcatheter edge-to-edge repair versus guideline-directed medical therapy in atrial functional mitral regurgitation and HFpEF
Authors: Can Öztürk, Lukas Böhm, Enes Altintac, Johanna Vogelhuber, Marcel Weber, Sebastian Zimmer, Georg Nickenig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOver the past decade, mitral valve transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) has become an established interventional treatment option for functional mitral regurgitation. One subtype that has recently gained increasing scientific attention is atrial functional mitral regurgitation (aFMR) occurring in patients with heart failure (HF) with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). Randomized trials to date have largely excluded patients with HFpEF, leading to limited data in this cohort.Study designThe randomized evaluation of transcatheter edge-to-edge repair in patients with atrial functional mitral regurgitation and HF with preserved ejection fraction compared with GDMT (EDGEFIRM) is a prospective, open-label, randomized, multicentre trial evaluating the additive effect of M-TEER in patients with aFMR and HFpEF compared to guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) only. Eligible patients must be symptomatic under stable GDMT with significant aFMR (>grade II or grade II with hemodynamic relevance) and HFpEF and considered high risk for surgical therapy by Heart Team evaluation. Randomization will be conducted in a 2:1 manner M-TEER + GDMT vs GDMT only. The primary combined endpoint consists of cardiovascular mortality, HF hospitalization/worsening HF, improvement <10 points in KCCQ-Score after 1 year. Secondary endpoints include reduction of mitral regurgitation, device safety, functional capacity as well as the individual components of the combined endpoint.DiscussionThe EDGEFIRM trial aims to evaluate the additive effect of M-TEER compared with GDMT only in patients with symptomatic aFMR and HFpEF.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) remains a major public health burden, with an estimated prevalence of 1%–3% in the general population and a marked increase in older age groups, reaching ∼16% among individuals aged >75 years.1 The prevalence of HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is rising and has therefore gained increasing clinical relevance.1 Recent evidence suggests that mortality in HFpEF is comparable to that observed in HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF).2–4 Nevertheless, evidence-based therapies with proven prognostic benefit for patients with HFpEF remain limited. Data from the last years led to a guideline-established recommendation for sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT-2) inhibitor therapy in patients with HFpEF.5,6 Beyond guideline-directed HF medication, the treatment of comorbidities to prevent further deterioration is an important strategy, although evidence guiding specific treatment approaches remains scarce.5

Patients with HFpEF commonly exhibit comorbidities, including atrial fibrillation and arterial hypertension. Atrial congestion caused by diastolic dysfunction and atrial fibrillation may promote atrial and mitral annular remodeling, ultimately leading to atrial functional mitral regurgitation (aFMR).7 In this subtype of functional mitral regurgitation (FMR), left ventricular dimensions and structure are normal or only mildly dilated, in contrast to ventricular FMR.8

In patients with persistent severe symptomatic FMR despite guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT), current guidelines recommend evaluation by the heart team for mitral valve repair.8 Depending on patient age, comorbidities, and procedural risk, treatment options range from surgical reconstruction to a mitral valve transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER). Several studies, including the recently published 5-year follow-up of the COAPT trial, have demonstrated a prognostic benefit of M-TEER in patients with FMR overall.9 However, the simultaneously initiated MITRA-FR-trial did not show a significant benefit compared with medical therapy alone, suggesting that patient selection is critical.10

Most randomized cohorts investigating M-TEER have included either exclusively or predominantly patients with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction. Similarly, in the recently published RESHAPE-HF2-trial, the median LVEF was 32% and 31% in the interventional and the control groups, respectively.11 Consequently, data on patients with HFpEF remain very limited. Nevertheless, findings from observational studies and a small subgroup of the MATTERHORN trial suggest that the potential benefit of M-TEER may extend to broader patient populations.12–14 Overall, patients with HFpEF remain underrepresented in randomized evidence to date.

The latest guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease distinguished, for the first time, between ventricular and atrial subtypes of FMR.8 However, to the best of our knowledge, no randomized controlled data are currently available regarding the effect of M-TEER on mortality, HF hospitalization, or HF symptoms in a dedicated cohort of patients with aFMR. This evidence gap creates several uncertainties, ranging from the general recommendation for mitral valve repair to the optimal timing of intervention. Here, we describe the design and rationale of our randomized trial evaluating M-TEER compared with GDMT alone in patients with symptomatic, significant aFMR and HFpEF.

Study design

The EDGEFIRM trial is a prospective, multicentre, randomized controlled study designed to evaluate the additive effect of M-TEER compared with GDMT alone in patients with symptomatic, significant aFMR and HFpEF. The trial will be conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines and has been registered with the German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS00039479). It was approved by the local ethics committee. As an investigator-initiated trial, EDGEFIRM was conceived and designed at the Heart Center Bonn, University Hospital Bonn. The study will be conducted at ∼20 experienced, high-volume centres in Germany and is funded by Abbott.

Primary and secondary endpoints

The primary endpoint is a hierarchical composite endpoint comprising cardiovascular mortality, HF hospitalization or worsening HF, and failure to achieve a ≥10-point improvement in KCCQ overall summary score at 12 months, with components ranked according to clinical importance. The 10-point threshold was selected as a clinically meaningful change, corresponding to a moderate-to-large treatment effect.15

The hierarchical win ratio approach compares treatment–control pairs sequentially according to the pre-specified hierarchy of cardiovascular mortality, HF hospitalization or worsening HF, and KCCQ. Thus, KCCQ contributes to the primary endpoint only when patient pairs remain unresolved after comparison of the two higher-priority clinical outcomes.

Given the symptom-driven nature of HFpEF and aFMR, patient-reported health status is considered of particular clinical relevance. Accordingly, the KCCQ was incorporated into the hierarchical primary endpoint to capture clinically meaningful changes in symptoms, functional limitation, and quality of life.

Worsening HF is defined as progressive peripheral or pulmonary oedema and/or weight gain of >2 kg within 7 days, together with at least one of the following treatment escalations: an increase in oral diuretic dose by ≥50%, initiation of ambulatory intravenous diuretic therapy, or an unplanned outpatient visit specifically due to HF decompensation requiring therapeutic escalation.

Secondary endpoints include echocardiographic, clinical, functional, and laboratory outcomes. Procedural efficacy will be assessed by the proportion of patients achieving a reduction in mitral regurgitation to grade II or less at 12 months. Clinical outcomes, including all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, will be evaluated at 12 months. Patient-reported health status will be assessed by changes in the KCCQ overall summary score at 6 and 12 months compared with baseline.

Functional status will be evaluated by changes in New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class and performance in the 6-min walk test. Laboratory outcomes include changes in N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) levels during follow-up.

The primary safety endpoint includes device failure, such as single-leaflet device attachment or device embolization, infective endocarditis, mitral valve stenosis, procedure-related bleeding complications, and device-related need for urgent cardiovascular surgery.

Patient population

The study population consists of adult patients with symptomatic aFMR and HFpEF, defined by a left ventricular ejection fraction of ≥50% and diagnosed according to contemporary ESC guideline criteria, despite optimized GDMT for HF. All participants have significant aFMR (greater than grade II or grade II with haemodynamic relevance), as confirmed by echocardiographic core laboratory assessment, and remain symptomatic with at least NYHA class II symptoms despite optimized medical therapy. In addition, eligible patients must have been hospitalized for HF at least once within the preceding year or have elevated NT-proBNP levels, defined as >300 pg/ml in sinus rhythm or >900 pg/ml in sustained atrial fibrillation.

Patients with predominant ventricular tethering mechanisms or relevant left ventricular remodelling suggestive of ventricular FMR are excluded based on core laboratory assessment. GDMT will be administered according to the contemporary ESC HF guidelines and individualized according to each patient's clinical profile and comorbidities. Before randomization, all patients must receive stable, optimized GDMT for at least 4 weeks (except for diuretic adjustments). This includes SGLT2 inhibitors whenever indicated and not contraindicated. In addition, optimization of medical therapy may incorporate evidence-based therapies, such as finerenone, following the positive results of the FINEARTS-HF trial, where clinically appropriate and according to local approval and availability.16 Eligibility and optimization of medical therapy will be confirmed by the local multidisciplinary Heart Team before randomization, and GDMT will be continued throughout follow-up in both treatment arms.

The study population is expected to predominantly comprise elderly patients with a high burden of comorbidities, including arterial hypertension, atrial fibrillation, obesity, and diabetes mellitus, reflecting the typical clinical phenotype of HFpEF. Before inclusion, all patients undergo comprehensive diagnostic evaluation in accordance with ESC guidelines, as well as an assessment of surgical risk by a multidisciplinary heart team to confirm eligibility and suitability for M-TEER.8

Screening, randomization, enrolment, and follow-up

Patients will be considered eligible if all inclusion criteria are fulfilled, and no exclusion criterion are present. Before enrolment, all patients must provide written informed consent after receiving comprehensive oral and written information regarding the study procedures, potential benefits, and risks.

Flowchart of the EDGEFIRM-trial. KCCQ, Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire

Each eligible patient will undergo standardized evaluation by the local Heart Team, consisting of an HF specialist, an interventional cardiologist, an experienced echocardiographer, and a cardiac surgeon. The Heart Team will assess overall clinical suitability as well as the anatomical and procedural feasibility of M-TEER.

The echocardiographic diagnosis of aFMR and key anatomical eligibility criteria will be independently confirmed by an echocardiography core laboratory before randomization. Randomization will be performed using permuted blocks with variable block sizes within each centre and stratum. Patients will be allocated to treatment arms in a 2:1 ratio. A 2:1 randomization scheme was selected to facilitate patient recruitment and acceptance with only a limited impact on statistical efficiency.

Patients will be stratified according to the severity of mitral regurgitation to ensure balanced distribution across treatment groups. Randomization will then be performed separately within each stratum, thereby supporting comparable allocation to the M-TEER plus GDMT and GDMT-only arms.

In total, 200 participants will be randomized to the interventional group receiving M-TEER plus GDMT, and 100 participants will be randomized to the control group receiving GDMT alone. Randomization will be open-label, as blinding is not feasible due to the nature of the intervention.

Follow-up visits are planned before post-interventional hospital discharge and at 6 and 12 months after randomization.

Device and procedure

In the interventional arm, patients will undergo M-TEER, a catheter-based, minimally invasive procedure designed to reduce mitral regurgitation by approximating the anterior and posterior mitral valve leaflets. Device implantation creates a double-orifice mitral valve, thereby improving leaflet coaptation while preserving diastolic valve opening. M-TEER is an established alternative to surgical mitral valve repair or replacement in patients at elevated surgical risk and is recommended by contemporary European guidelines for selected patient populations.8 Detailed descriptions of the procedure have been published previously.18 In this trial, M-TEER will be performed using the fourth- or fifth-generation MitraClip system (Abbott Vascular, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The selection of device size and the number of implanted devices will be left to the operator’s discretion.

Statistical analysis

The primary estimand is a treatment-policy estimand including all randomized patients, irrespective of the occurrence of intercurrent events. Relevant intercurrent events include treatment discontinuation, cross-over, particularly from the control to the interventional arm, and the use of prohibited medication.

Individual outcomes will be assessed at 12 months after randomization and will comprise: (1) time to cardiovascular death, (2) time to first HF hospitalization or worsening HF, and (3) failure to achieve an improvement of at least 10 points in the KCCQ overall summary score. The population-level treatment effect will be estimated using the hierarchical win ratio method described by Pocock et al.19,20 This hierarchical approach reflects the clinical prioritization of endpoint components by assigning greater weight to fatal and HF-related events before considering patient-reported health status outcomes. The win ratio will be used to assess the potential superiority of M-TEER plus GDMT compared with GDMT alone.

An a priori sample size calculation was performed based on an expected win ratio of 1.8, a statistical power of 90%, a probability of ties of 0.2, and a two-sided significance level of 0.05, following the method proposed by Yu and Ganju.21 Under these assumptions, the required sample size is 184 patients in the interventional arm and 92 patients in the control arm, resulting in a total of 276 patients. To account for an anticipated drop-out rate of ∼8–10% within 12 months, the planned total sample size will be increased to 300 patients, with 200 patients assigned to the interventional arm and 100 patients assigned to the control arm.

The expected win ratio of 1.8 was derived from the results of the COAPT trial, which demonstrated a win ratio of ∼1.6 but using a narrower composite endpoint.22 Considering the substantial improvements in procedural experience with TEER interventions in recent years, together with technical refinements of contemporary devices, a modestly higher expected treatment effect (win ratio of 1.8) was considered reasonable.

The statistical analysis of secondary endpoints will be based on group comparisons between the interventional and control groups. Continuous outcomes will be analyzed using two-sample t-tests for independent samples or, if distributional assumptions are not met, Mann–Whitney U tests. For categorical outcomes, Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests will be applied, depending on the observed cell frequencies.

Time-to-event endpoints will be evaluated using Kaplan–Meier estimates with corresponding log-rank tests for group comparisons. As part of sensitivity analyses, multiple regression models (e.g. linear, logistic, or Cox proportional hazards models, as appropriate) will be estimated, including covariate adjustment for relevant baseline factors such as sex and age.

Subjects dropping out of the trial prior to randomization will be listed, including the reason for drop-out. Subjects dropping out of the trial after randomization will be analyzed using all available data. The technique for the replacement of missing values will be defined in the statistical analysis plan. A check of a possible treatment effect on the frequency of missing values will be done.

Cross-over from the GDMT-only group to M-TEER before completion of the 12-month primary endpoint assessment is discouraged but may exceptionally be permitted as rescue therapy after reassessment by the local Heart Team in patients with progressive HF despite optimized GDMT. Rescue cross-over may be considered in patients with (i) repetitive cardiac decompensations requiring urgent readmission for intravenous diuretic therapy less than 2 weeks after discharge and occurring more than twice within 1 month, or (ii) inability to transition to oral diuretic therapy because of acute kidney injury or electrolyte disturbances resulting in hospitalization exceeding 2 weeks. All cross-over events will be documented, including timing, clinical indication, and Heart Team decision. For the primary analysis, patients will remain analyzed according to their randomized treatment assignment under the treatment policy estimand. Additional sensitivity analyses including per-protocol and as-treated analyses will be performed to assess the potential impact of treatment cross-over.

It is noted that for the time-to-event components, incomplete observations correspond to right-censoring and therefore do not represent missing data in the statistical sense. Consequently, any missing data relevant to the primary endpoint is expected to pertain exclusively to the KCCQ component. In the context of the hierarchical win ratio analysis, missing KCCQ data impact the analysis only for those treatment–control pairs in which the comparison remains unresolved after evaluation of the two higher-priority time-to-event components.

Further analyses will be specified in the statistical analysis plan. No formal interim efficacy analysis with predefined stopping boundaries is planned. However, the study may be terminated prematurely by the coordinating investigator in case of unacceptable safety concerns, insufficient recruitment, or new evidence affecting the risk-benefit assessment.

Discussion

Prior randomized, controlled trials have confirmed M-TEER as a safe and effective therapy for patients with FMR.9–11 The differing results of the COAPT and MITRA-FR trials, however, underscore the heterogeneity of FMR and suggest that the benefit of M-TEER may vary among patient subgroups. One specific subgroup—patients with HFpEF and aFMR—was systematically excluded from all prior randomized trials comparing interventional therapy with GDMT.

Non-randomized data indicate that TEER may present a safe and technically feasible treatment option in this population.13,23,24 The recently published ESC Guidelines for the Management of Valvular Heart Disease recognize aFMR as a distinct entity for the first time and acknowledge the absence of randomized evidence for this group in their therapeutic recommendations.8

The intervention will be performed exclusively in experienced high-volume centres under strict safety standards. Continuous safety monitoring and predefined exclusion criteria are intended to reduce procedural risk. No relevant pharmacological interactions with standard HF therapy are expected.

Given the robust evidence on procedural safety, the low invasiveness of M-TEER, and the potential symptomatic and prognostic benefit in a currently undertreated subgroup, the expected clinical advantage is considered to justify the residual risk. This study is therefore ethically justified and designed to contribute essential data to close an important evidence gap in valvular heart disease while maintaining the highest standards of patient safety and scientific integrity.8–13,23,25

The primary endpoint combines objective clinical outcomes, including cardiovascular mortality and worsening HF, with patient-reported health status assessed by the KCCQ. This ensures that the trial evaluates both prognosis and clinically meaningful symptomatic benefit. Since implanted M-TEER devices are readily identifiable by echocardiography, blinding is not feasible, and the trial is therefore conducted in an open-label manner. Consequently, patient-reported outcomes may be susceptible to expectation bias. However, the hierarchical structure of the primary endpoint prioritizes objective clinical outcomes, with KCCQ assessed only if treatment pairs remain unresolved after comparison of cardiovascular mortality and HF hospitalization/worsening HF. Thus, KCCQ complements rather than outweighs the hard clinical outcomes. Its inclusion was considered essential because improvement in symptoms and health-related quality of life represents a major therapeutic goal in patients with HFpEF and atrial FMR, particularly in the predominantly elderly study population.

The EDGEFIRM trial is a prospective, multicentre, randomized, controlled study, to evaluate the additive effect of M-TEER compared with GDMT only in patients with aFMR and HFpEF. With a combined primary endpoint including hard clinical outcomes as well as self-reported health-status, the structure was specifically tailored to the clinical characteristics of our cohort. To our knowledge, EDGEFIRM is the first randomized trial designed to investigate M-TEER in aFMR and HFpEF.
