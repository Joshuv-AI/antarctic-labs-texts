Quantitative correlation of spectroscopic signatures with ligand–protein interactions in anti-cancer drug Afinitor: an integrated experimental–computational study
Authors: P Venkata Ramana, Rashmirekha Ram, Prasadarao Bobbili, Y Rama Krishna, Rabinarayan Panda, Shouri Dominic, Pratap Kumar Dakua, Siddharth Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA detailed molecular-level understanding of anticancer drugs is essential for improving therapeutic efficacy and guiding rational drug design. Everolimus (Afinitor), a clinically important inhibitor of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, is widely used in cancer therapy; however, a quantitatively grounded relationship between its spectroscopic characteristics and ligand–protein interactions remains insufficiently explored. In this study, an integrated experimental–computational approach was employed, combining FT-IR spectroscopy, UV–Vis spectroscopy, and molecular docking simulations. Spectroscopic analyses were used to characterise functional groups and electronic structure, while docking simulations were performed to investigate interactions with FK506 binding protein (FKBP12) and the FKBP–rapamycin binding (FRB) domain. FT-IR analysis revealed a high density of oxygen-containing functional groups, including hydroxyl and carbonyl moieties, with vibrational frequencies indicative of a strongly polarised electronic environment. Molecular docking demonstrated favourable binding affinities with FKBP12 (− 9.7 and − 9.6 kcal·mol⁻1) and the FRB domain (− 8.5 and − 6.6 kcal·mol⁻1). Detailed interaction analysis showed that these functional groups correspond to specific interacting atoms (e.g., O66, O67, O63, and O36), forming quantifiable hydrogen bonds (1.7–2.9 Å) and electrostatic interactions (~ 4.37 Å) with key residues such as TYR82, THR85, and GLU54. The UV–Vis absorption maximum at 278 nm corresponds to a HOMO–LUMO energy gap of 4.46 eV, indicating moderate electronic polarizability that supports charge redistribution during binding. The study establishes a quantitative and mechanistically grounded structure–spectra–interaction relationship, demonstrating that spectroscopic observables encode the local electronic environment governing ligand–protein interaction propensity. Binding affinity is shown to arise from a cooperative network of multiple non-covalent interactions enabled by the spatial distribution of functional groups. This integrated framework provides predictive insight into drug–protein interactions and offers a robust foundation for the rational design and optimisation of mTOR-targeting therapeutics.Keywords: FT-IR and UV–Vis, Afinitor, Immunosuppressant FK506, FKBP-Rapamycin binding domain, Molecular dockingSubject terms: Cancer, Chemistry, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the most significant global health challenges, accounting for millions of deaths each year and placing an increasing burden on healthcare systems worldwide. According to recent global cancer statistics, the incidence of cancer continues to rise due to population growth, ageing, and lifestyle-related risk factors. These trends highlight the urgent need for improved therapeutic strategies and a deeper molecular understanding of anticancer drugs currently used in clinical practice1. In this context, investigations aimed at elucidating the structural and molecular interactions of established anticancer agents can contribute valuable insights for optimising drug performance and guiding the development of next-generation therapeutics. Among the various targeted anticancer therapies, inhibitors of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signalling pathway have emerged as important therapeutic agents for the treatment of several malignancies. The mTOR pathway plays a central role in regulating cell growth, proliferation, metabolism, and survival. Dysregulation of this signalling pathway is commonly associated with the progression of various cancers, including renal cell carcinoma, breast cancer, and neuroendocrine tumours. Consequently, pharmacological inhibition of mTOR has become an important strategy in targeted cancer therapy.

Everolimus, commercially known as Afinitor, is a derivative of the macrolide compound sirolimus and functions as a potent inhibitor of the mTOR signalling pathway. The drug exerts its therapeutic effect through interaction with FK506 binding protein-12 (FKBP12), forming a complex that subsequently inhibits the activity of mTOR. This inhibition prevents the progression of cells from the G1 phase to the S phase of the cell cycle, thereby suppressing cellular proliferation. Owing to its mechanism of action, Everolimus has been widely used in the treatment of several malignancies and in the prevention of organ transplant rejection2. Understanding the molecular interactions between anticancer drugs and their target proteins is crucial for improving therapeutic efficacy and minimising adverse effects. In recent years, in-silico computational techniques have become powerful tools in modern drug discovery and molecular pharmacology. Among these techniques, molecular docking simulations are widely employed to predict ligand–protein binding modes, estimate binding affinities, and identify key residues involved in molecular recognition. These computational approaches complement experimental techniques by providing atom-level insights into interaction mechanisms that are often difficult to observe directly through experimental methods alone.

In parallel with computational approaches, spectroscopic techniques play an essential role in characterising the structural and electronic properties of pharmaceutical compounds. Vibrational spectroscopic methods such as Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy provide valuable information regarding the functional groups and molecular bonding present within a compound. Similarly, UV–Vis spectroscopy offers insight into electronic transitions and molecular orbital interactions, enabling estimation of important parameters such as energy gaps between frontier molecular orbitals. Together, these spectroscopic techniques contribute to a detailed understanding of the physicochemical properties of drug molecules.

Although the pharmacological activity and molecular structure of Everolimus are well established, studies that integrate spectroscopic characterisation with molecular docking-based interaction analysis of FKBP-associated targets remain relatively limited. Most previous investigations have focused primarily on pharmacological mechanisms or computational screening of potential inhibitors. However, the relationship between spectroscopically identified functional groups of the drug molecule and their specific roles in protein-binding interactions has received comparatively less attention. Establishing such correlations may provide additional insights into the molecular determinants governing drug–target recognition3.

In this context, the present study aims to provide an integrated experimental–computational investigation of the anticancer drug Afinitor (Everolimus) by combining FT-IR spectroscopy, UV–Vis spectroscopy, and molecular docking simulations. The spectroscopic analysis enables identification of characteristic functional groups and electronic transitions associated with the molecular structure of the drug, while the molecular docking analysis explores the interaction mechanisms between Everolimus and two biologically relevant targets, namely FKBP12 and the FKBP–rapamycin binding (FRB) domain. By correlating spectroscopic features with docking-derived interaction patterns, this study seeks to provide additional molecular-level insight into the structural features that govern ligand–protein interactions in the mTOR signalling pathway.

Significance of the study

The present work provides an integrated spectroscopic and computational analysis of Everolimus by correlating vibrational and electronic spectral features with ligand–protein interaction patterns obtained from molecular docking simulations. Such a combined experimental–computational approach offers deeper insight into the structural determinants governing the interaction of Everolimus with FKBP-associated protein targets. This framework may contribute to improved understanding of anticancer drug interactions and support future structure-guided optimisation of mTOR-targeting therapeutics.

Molecular Docking Methodology

Molecular docking simulations were performed to investigate the interaction mechanisms between Afinitor (Everolimus) and two biologically relevant targets associated with mTOR signalling, namely the FK506 binding protein (FKBP12) and the FKBP–rapamycin binding (FRB) domain. The three-dimensional structures of the target proteins were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) with accession codes 1FKL and 1AUE, respectively. These structures were selected based on their high crystallographic resolution, availability of ligand-bound conformations, and relevance to the biological mechanism of mTOR inhibition.

Prior to docking, protein preparation was performed using BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer. All crystallographic water molecules and co-crystallised ligands not involved in the binding interaction were removed to avoid interference during docking. Polar hydrogen atoms were added to the protein structures, and Kollman charges were assigned. The prepared protein structures were then saved in the PDBQT format required for docking calculations.

The ligand structure of Afinitor (Everolimus) was obtained from the PubChem database and subsequently optimised using molecular modelling tools within Discovery Studio. Hydrogen atoms were added, and the ligand geometry was energy-minimised to obtain a stable conformational state before docking. The optimised ligand was then converted into the PDBQT format for compatibility with the docking software.

Molecular docking simulations were carried out using AutoDock Tools 1.5.6, which employs the Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm (LGA) to explore possible ligand conformations within the receptor binding pocket. In the docking protocol, the protein structure was treated as rigid while the ligand was considered flexible, allowing rotational freedom around its torsional bonds to identify the most favourable binding orientation.

The docking grid box was defined around the active site of the receptor protein to ensure that the search space covered the relevant binding pocket. The grid centre coordinates used for the docking calculations were x = 3.199382, y = 12.129956, and z = 13.313500, while the grid box dimensions were set to 20 Å × 20 Å × 20 Å along the x, y, and z axes, respectively. These parameters ensured sufficient coverage of the binding cavity while maintaining computational efficiency.

The docking calculations were performed using the Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm with standard parameters, including multiple docking runs to explore various ligand conformations. The resulting docking poses were ranked according to their predicted binding free energies. The conformation with the lowest binding energy and favourable interaction profile was considered the most stable binding mode.

To evaluate the reliability of the docking protocol, a re-docking validation procedure was performed. In this approach, the co-crystallised ligand present in the original PDB structure was removed and subsequently re-docked into the same binding site. The predicted pose was then compared with the experimentally determined crystal structure using the root mean square deviation (RMSD) metric. An RMSD value below 2.0 Å was obtained, confirming the reliability and accuracy of the docking procedure used in this study.

The resulting ligand–protein complexes were analysed using BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer, which enabled visualisation of hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions, hydrophobic contacts, and π–π stacking interactions between Afinitor and the amino acid residues of the target proteins. Both two-dimensional and three-dimensional interaction diagrams were generated to better understand the molecular interactions responsible for binding stability.

The docking results, including binding affinities, RMSD values, and interaction patterns, were further analysed to identify key residues involved in stabilising the ligand–protein complex. These interactions provide insight into the molecular basis of Afinitor’s inhibitory activity against mTOR-associated proteins.

Molecular docking analysis

Molecular docking simulations were performed to investigate the interaction of Afinitor with two key mTOR-associated targets, FK506 binding protein (FKBP12; PDB ID: 1FKL) and the FKBP–rapamycin binding (FRB) domain (PDB ID: 1AUE). The docking results revealed energetically favourable binding conformations with high binding affinities, indicating strong ligand–protein interactions. The best docking poses exhibited binding affinities of − 9.7 and − 9.6 kcal·mol⁻1 for FKBP12, and − 8.5 and − 6.6 kcal·mol⁻1 for the FRB domain, indicating a stronger interaction preference for FKBP12.

Predicting two molecules’ binding affinities and preferences with precision is one of the trickiest elements of docking. Using docking algorithms that assess the binding affinities at different receptor binding sites in different ligand poses yields the optimal docking solutions. After determining the best kind of binding (in this case, solely proteins) between a ligand and its macromolecular partner, the best docking solution is chosen from a range of sites. Consequently, a vast array of distinct positions for the ligand in a range of patterns and orientations (or poses) must be created inside the protein’s binding site. The current study used molecular docking with the FKBP-Rapamycin Binding Domain and the FK506 binding protein29,30. The Protein Data Bank (RCSB) provided the structural database for the target proteins 1FKL and 1AUE, respectively31 (Figs. 5, 6, 7).

Functional group analysis of Afinitor

The chemical structure of Afinitor explains the importance of its functional groups. Specific atom configurations inside molecules, known as chemical functional groups, control a molecule’s chemical behaviour. Afinitor’s chemical structure includes a number of significant functional groups that support both its pharmacological activity and its interactions with the body. A macrocyclic lactone ring, or big cyclic ester, is present in Afinitor. The drug’s capacity to attach to its target, mTOR, and suppress its activity depends on this structural motif. For the medication to inhibit the mTOR pathway specifically and effectively, its macrocyclic structure is essential37.

The hydroxy group (–OH)

Afinitor (everolimus) functions as an mTOR inhibitor, and one important part of this process is the hydroxy group (–OH) functional group. Here’s how Afinitor works better because of the hydroxy group. Afinitor’s hydroxy group has the ability to establish hydrogen bonds with a variety of compounds, including bodily proteins and enzymes. In biological systems, hydrogen bonding is an essential intermolecular force. Hydrogen bonding interactions between the hydroxy group and certain amino acid residues in the mTOR protein’s active site may happen in the setting of Afinitor. These interactions boost Afinitor’s potential to bind to mTOR by stabilising the drug-protein complex38. In water and other biological fluids, a molecule’s solubility can be affected by the presence of hydroxy groups in it. The hydroxy groups included in Afinitor have the potential to enhance its solubility, therefore guaranteeing the drug’s efficient administration and distribution throughout the body. For the medication to reach its target areas and have the desired pharmacological effects, it must be sufficiently soluble39. Phase II metabolism, namely glucuronidation, a process wherein the drug and glucuronic acid are conjugated to aid in the drug’s excretion from the body, may incorporate hydroxy groups. The drug’s pharmacokinetics, especially its clearance rate, may be impacted by its metabolism. Determining the drug’s dosage and length of effect requires an understanding of the metabolic pathways involving hydroxy groups40. Hydroxy groups are adaptable and may be chemically changed to produce derivatives with various characteristics. By creating analogues or prodrugs, medicinal chemists can make use of Afinitor’s hydroxy group and perhaps improve the drug’s bioavailability, tissue-specific targeting, or effectiveness. Afinitor’s hydroxy group plays a crucial role in its solubility, metabolism, interactions with biological molecules, and potential for chemical modification. All of these elements work together to make Afinitor a successful treatment for a variety of malignancies and other disorders by blocking the mTOR pathway41.

The carbonyl group (C = O)

Afinitor’s (everolimus) carbonyl group (C = O) functional group is important to its mTOR inhibitory mode of action. Here’s how Afinitor works better because of the carbonyl group. Owing to the difference in electronegativity between carbon and oxygen, the group exhibits polarity. This characteristic enables it to participate in hydrogen bonding and other polar interactions with target biomolecules. Afinitor’s carbonyl group engages with specific amino acid residues within the mTOR protein’s active site, thereby enhancing the drug’s target-binding affinity. The neighbouring carbon atom is somewhat positive because the oxygen atom in the carbonyl group is electron-withdrawing. Because of this electron shortage, the carbon atom is more electrophilic and vulnerable to nucleophilic assault. This characteristic may be crucial for processes involving enzymes and other biological molecules in biological systems42. The Afinitor molecule is more stable when the carbonyl group is present. Carbonyl groups are generally stable under physiological conditions, ensuring that the drug remains intact and active in the body, allowing it to exert its therapeutic effects. Several metabolic processes can occur in the carbonyl group, including oxidation. Determining the drug’s metabolism, rate of clearance, and duration of action in the body requires an understanding of the metabolic pathways involving the carbonyl group. The carbonyl group can be broken down to produce metabolites, which may differ from the original chemical in terms of pharmacological characteristics43. Medicinal chemists can exploit the chemical reactivity of the carbonyl group to design structural analogues or derivatives of Afinitor. The creation of prodrugs or other formulations with modified pharmacokinetic characteristics, bioavailability, or tissue-specific targeting can result from chemical changes in the carbonyl group. Afinitor’s carbonyl group is necessary for its stability, metabolism, capacity to change chemically, and interactions with biological molecules. All of these elements work together to make Afinitor a successful treatment for a variety of malignancies and other ailments by blocking the mTOR pathway44, 52.

An important aspect of the present work is the integration of spectroscopic characterisation with molecular docking analysis. The FT-IR spectra identified several oxygen-containing functional groups, including hydroxyl and carbonyl moieties, which are known to participate in hydrogen bonding interactions. Interestingly, the docking simulations revealed that these same oxygen atoms play a dominant role in stabilising the ligand–protein complex through hydrogen bonding with key residues such as TYR82, THR85, and GLU54 of the FKBP12 binding site.

This correlation between spectroscopically identified functional groups and the observed docking interactions provides a structural rationale for the binding behaviour of Afinitor. Such combined spectroscopic–computational analysis offers additional insight into the molecular determinants of binding that may not be evident from docking studies alone.

Quantitative correlation between spectroscopic signatures and ligand–protein interactions

To establish a mechanistically grounded relationship between spectroscopic observables and ligand–protein binding behaviour, the vibrational and electronic properties of Everolimus were quantitatively correlated with docking-derived interaction parameters. The underlying premise is that spectroscopic features encode the local electronic environment of functional groups, which directly governs their interaction propensity within the protein binding pocket.

The FT-IR spectrum of Everolimus reveals prominent vibrational modes associated with hydroxyl (νOH: 3449–3354 cm⁻1) and carbonyl (νC = O: 1654–1648 cm⁻1) functional groups. A reduction in vibrational frequency (red shift) is generally associated with increased bond polarisation and enhanced hydrogen-bonding capability. Study limitations

Although the present study provides useful insights into the spectroscopic characteristics and molecular docking interactions of Afinitor, several limitations should be acknowledged. The computational investigation in this work was limited to molecular docking simulations, which provide a static representation of ligand–protein interactions and may not fully capture the dynamic behaviour of the complexes under physiological conditions. Advanced computational techniques such as molecular dynamics simulations and free energy calculations could further validate the stability and energetics of the predicted binding modes. In addition, while FT-IR and UV–Vis spectroscopy provide valuable information regarding the functional groups and electronic transitions of the molecule, the present study does not include complementary experimental techniques such as NMR spectroscopy or biological assays that could provide additional validation of the interaction mechanisms. Future studies incorporating molecular dynamics simulations, experimental binding assays, and structure–activity relationship analyses would further enhance the understanding of Afinitor’s molecular interactions with mTOR-associated targets.

Future research directions

Future research will focus on extending the present spectroscopic and computational investigation of Afinitor toward a deeper understanding of its interaction mechanisms with mTOR-associated targets. In particular, advanced computational approaches such as molecular dynamics simulations and free energy calculations could be employed to evaluate the stability of the predicted ligand–protein complexes under physiological conditions. Additionally, structure–activity relationship studies and rational modification of functional groups identified in the spectroscopic analysis may help in designing improved Everolimus analogues with enhanced binding affinity and reduced adverse effects. Experimental validation through biochemical assays and cellular studies will further clarify the therapeutic potential of these interactions and support the development of optimised mTOR-targeting anticancer agents.

Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive experimental–computational investigation of the anticancer drug Everolimus (Afinitor) by integrating FT-IR spectroscopy, UV–Vis spectroscopy, and molecular docking simulations to elucidate its ligand–protein interaction mechanisms at a molecular level.

The spectroscopic analysis revealed a high density of oxygen-containing functional groups, particularly hydroxyl and carbonyl moieties, whose vibrational signatures reflect a strongly polarised electronic environment. These spectroscopic features were shown to directly correspond to atom-specific interaction sites identified in docking simulations, where oxygen atoms (e.g., O66, O67, O63, and O36) participate in quantitatively characterised hydrogen bonding (1.7–2.9 Å) and electrostatic interactions (~ 4.37 Å) with key residues such as TYR82, THR85, and GLU54.

Importantly, the present work establishes a quantitative structure–spectra–interaction relationship, demonstrating that vibrational frequencies serve as indirect descriptors of local electron density and interaction propensity. This relationship provides a mechanistic basis for understanding how spectroscopically identified functional groups govern ligand–protein binding behaviour. The observed binding affinities (− 9.7 and − 9.6 kcal·mol⁻1 for FKBP12) arise from a cooperative network of multiple non-covalent interactions, rather than a single dominant interaction, highlighting the role of multi-point stabilisation in molecular recognition.

Furthermore, the UV–Vis-derived HOMO–LUMO energy gap (4.46 eV) indicates moderate electronic polarizability, facilitating charge redistribution during binding and thereby supporting the observed electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding interactions. This establishes electronic structure as an additional link connecting spectroscopic observables to interaction energetics.

Overall, this study advances beyond conventional docking analyses by introducing a mechanistically grounded and quantitatively supported framework that bridges experimental spectroscopy and computational interaction analysis. The proposed structure–spectra–interaction paradigm demonstrates that spectroscopic measurements can provide predictive insight into ligand–protein binding behaviour, thereby offering a robust foundation for the rational design and optimisation of mTOR-targeting therapeutics.
