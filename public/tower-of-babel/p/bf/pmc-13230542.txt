Preparation of carbon quantum dot fluorescent probe from Caesalpinia sappan L. for the detection of ciprofloxacin and antibacterial applications
Authors: Shuchen Pei, Kangli Yan, Shaoyu Cai, Yinjun Hou, Kang Luo, Pu Xiang, Yang Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study demonstrates a one-step hydrothermal synthesis of bright yellow phosphorus-doped carbon quantum dots (P-CQDs) using Caesalpinia sappan L. as carbon source. Comprehensive characterization through TEM, XPS, UV-Vis and FTIRspectroscopy confirmed the successful formation of P-CQDs. The obtained P-CQDs exhibited exceptional selectivity and was capable of detecting ciprofloxacin across a wide linear range of 0.01–0.1 µM, with a LOD determined to be 2.06 nM. Antimicrobial assays revealed MIC values of 7.14 µg/mL and 8.33 µg/mL against S. aureus and E. coli, respectively. Practical application studies confirmed their efficacy for rapid CIP detection in milk samples, establishing a novel sensing platform for food safety monitoring. This work provides valuable insights into the multifunctional applications of carbon dots in food chemistry and biomedical fields.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48145-7.

Introduction

Carbon quantum dots (CQDs), as an emerging class of nanomaterials, demonstrate a unique combination of favorable characteristics including negligible toxicity, environmental sustainability, and superior biocompatibility. Their facile synthesis protocols coupled with exceptional photoluminescent properties have garnered considerable attention in biomedical research. Recent advances in CQDs synthesis have employed two distinct approaches: top-down techniques (arc discharge and acid oxidation) and bottom-up methods (microwave pyrolysis / combustion, and solvothermal / hydrothermal processes)1,2. Hydrothermal synthesis employs water as an eco-friendly solvent, enabling precise control over heating rates and reaction temperatures. This method offers both environmental sustainability and operational safety3. Recent studies have demonstrated that strategic multi-element doping effectively enhances the performance of CQDs, leading to remarkable improvements in photoluminescence efficiency, antimicrobial activity, and long-term environmental stability4. Recent investigations have elucidated that the antimicrobial efficacy of CQDs exhibits marked charge dependence: both cationic and anionic CQDs demonstrate potent bactericidal activity, whereas their electrically neutral counterparts show complete absence of antimicrobial functionality5. The antibacterial mechanism of charged sites primarily stems from reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, including singlet oxygen and hydroxyl radicals, during their interaction with bacterial cells6. Wu et al. engineered levofloxacin-functionalized CQDs that demonstrated broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria through a ROS-triggered membrane damage mechanism. In contrast, Wang et al. developed cationic CQDs exhibiting selective bactericidal effects against Gram-positive strains via electrostatic interactions, while showing no efficacy against Gram-negative organisms. This limitation can be effectively addressed by antimicrobial agents employing alternative mechanisms such as ROS-mediated oxidative stress or bacterial gene expression interference7.

In recent years, CQDs have become high-performance fluorescent probes by virtue of their excellent fluorescence properties and good biocompatibility8. CQDs-based fluorescent sensors have received extensive attention from the research community due to their advantages of high selectivity, sensitivity, fast response and easy operation9. Zhu et al.10. successfully fabricated fluorescent carbon quantum dots using biomass lignin as a carbon precursor. The synthesized material exhibited exceptional sensitivity toward Fe³⁺ ions, showing linear detection across 0-300 µM with an exceptionally low detection limit of 0.77 µM. Zhang et al.11. developed fluorescent carbon quantum dots through a hydrothermal approach using citric acid and histidine as dual carbon sources with borax as a reaction modifier. The as-prepared CQDs demonstrated selective sensing capability toward Hg²⁺ ions, exhibiting a broad linear response range (0–150 µM) and a low detection threshold (2.5 µM). Jiang et al.12. synthesized vancomycin hydrochloride-derived carbon nanodots (VH-CNDs) via a rapid one-step microwave-assisted pyrolysis method using vancomycin hydrochloride as the sole precursor. The resulting VH-CNDs exhibited selective fluorescence quenching toward Sn⁴⁺ ions, achieving a detection limit of approximately 5.2 µM. Adin Hasrudin et al.13. successfully prepared multi-color fluorescent carbon dots with pH-responsive properties via a hydrothermal method, using Caesalpinia sappan L. powder as a natural carbon source after extraction with 95% ethanol. Compared to undoped carbon dots, non-metal-doped variants (e.g., N, P, S doping) exhibit tunable emission wavelengths and enhanced photostability. The doping process modulates the electronic structure and surface chemistry, promoting radiative recombination and introducing additional active sites. Consequently, these doped carbon dots demonstrate higher sensitivity, lower detection limits, and better selectivity toward metal ions and antibiotics. Xiang et al.14. synthesized phosphorus-doped carbon dots (P-CDs) via a hydrothermal method using soybean phospholipids as the sole precursor. The obtained P-CDs exhibited a detection limit of 27 nM for iodide, with a linear response ranging from 0.1 to 50 µM. Hegazy et al.15. developed a facile hydrothermal method for synthesizing carbon quantum dots using egg yolk and phosphoric acid, which were subsequently employed for the fabrication of UV and blue light-blocking filters. Gopika et al.16. synthesized phosphorus-doped carbon dots using fish skin as the carbon source, which enhanced the corrosion protection of carbon steel in marine environments.

Ciprofloxacin (CIP), a third-generation fluoroquinolone antibiotic, is extensively utilized in human and veterinary medicine due to its broad-spectrum antimicrobial efficacy. However, owing to its incomplete metabolic degradation, CIP residues persist in biological excretions, hospital effluents, pharmaceutical wastewater, and diverse aquatic systems. This ubiquitous contamination has raised significant concerns regarding both ecological integrity and public health security17. Zhang et al.18. developed a rapid open-pyrolysis method to synthesize N-CQDs, which exhibited a dual-phase linear response toward ciprofloxacin detection with an exceptionally low limit of detection (0.14 µM). Damini et al.19. developed bioinspired fluorescent carbon dots using Cinnamomum tamala leaf extract, which demonstrated linear detection of ciprofloxacin across a concentration range of 1–100 µM with a detection limit of 6.06 µM. Prasanna et al.20. developed an innovative CeMgMoO/CNF nanocomposite-based electrochemical sensor for ciprofloxacin detection, demonstrating an exceptionally broad linear response range (0.005–77.71 µM) with an ultralow detection limit of 0.012 µM. Maity et al.21. developed highly water-soluble fluorescent CQDs through a hydrothermal synthesis route using rice husk (RH) biomass as the precursor. The as-prepared CQDs exhibited remarkable selectivity and sensitivity toward ciprofloxacin (CIP) detection, achieving a linear detection range of 50-1150 nM with a detection limit of 127 nM. Compared with hematoxylin-derived phosphorus-containing fluorescent probes, the phosphorus-doped carbon quantum dots exhibit superior luminescence properties, demonstrating significant potential as probes for CIP detection22.

This work demonstrates a sustainable hydrothermal synthesis of P-CQDs using Caesalpinia sappan L. as a natural carbon/nitrogen precursor and phosphoric acid as the phosphorus source23. The resulting P-CQDs exhibit strong yellow fluorescence emission24. In vitro antibacterial assays demonstrated that P-CQDs exhibit potent antimicrobial activity against both E. coli and S. aureus1,25. It was also demonstrated that P-CQDs can be used as efficient fluorescent probes to specific recognition of CIP26.

Experimental section

Materials

Caesalpinia sappan L. (the selected part is the dried heartwood) were obtained from a certified traditional medicine retailer in Chongqing, China. Phosphate buffer solution (PBS) was sourced from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Coumarin-3-carboxylic acid (CCA) was supplied by Shanghai Vita Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd. Phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄, ≥ 85.0%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.) was used as received. Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. supplied the MTT reagent (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide). Deionized water was used throughout the experimental procedures.

Characterization

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were acquired using a Nicolet Avatar 360 FTIR spectrophotometer. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM, Japan) was employed to examine the morphological features of the P-CQDs. An ESCALAB 250 XPS spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to obtain the photoelectron spectroscopy data.

Synthesis of P-CQDs

In this experiment, Caesalpinia sappan was used as the carbon source precursor and phosphoric acid as the dopant to prepare various carbon dots via a hydrothermal synthesis method. The specific preparation procedure was as follows: The Chinese medicinal material Caesalpinia sappan was pulverized, and 0.50 g of it was added separately to H₃PO₄ (0.5, 1.0, 1.5 mL). The mixture was then diluted to 15 mL with ultrapure water. Subsequently, the resulting solution was transferred into a 100 mL polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE)-lined autoclave and reacted at different temperatures (100, 120, 140, 160 °C) for 6, 8, 10, and 12 h, respectively, in order to investigate the effects of different reaction conditions on the carbon dots. After cooling, the crude product was subjected to a multi-step purification protocol, including centrifugation (8,000 rpm, 5 min) to remove large particles, filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane, and dialysis (500 Da molecular weight cut-off) to eliminate small molecules and salts. The final purified P-CQDs was subsequently freeze-dried, and the resulting solid powder was stored at 4 °C for subsequent characterization and application.

The optical properties of P-CQDs

A comprehensive investigation was conducted to evaluate the influence of key operational parameters, including pH, illumination time, ionic strength, and oxidant concentration on the system. Optical characterization revealed that the P-CQDs exhibited distinct fluorescence with a maximum excitation peak at 340 nm and a corresponding emission maximum at 450 nm.

Antibacterial test

Culture of bacterial

E. coli and S. aureus were grown in LB medium with shaking 180 rpm at 37 °C for 12 h. After dilution to an optical density corresponding to 1.5 × 10⁷ CFU/mL, the bacterial cultures were stored under refrigeration.

Antibacterial activity test

To evaluate the antimicrobial properties of P-CQDs, their MIC was evaluated by means of both the MTT colorimetric assay and the standard broth microdilution technique. In 96-well microplates, bacterial suspensions (100 µL) were mixed with an equal volume (100 µL) of serially diluted P-CQDs solutions and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h before the optical density was measured at OD₆₀₀. Subsequently, MTT solution was introduced into each well (10 µL) and incubated for 20 min prior to further processing. All assays were performed with triplicate technical replicates.

Antimicrobial activity of P-CQDs was evaluated through bacterial growth kinetic analysis. For co-culture experiments, bacterial suspensions of E. coli and S. aureus (5 mL each, final inoculum 1.5 × 10⁷ CFU/mL) were combined with 10 mL of serially diluted P-CQDs under continuous agitation (180 rpm) at 37 °C. To monitor bacterial growth kinetics, the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) was measured bihourly using a microplate reader.

The antimicrobial resistance risk of P-CQDs against E. coli and S. aureus was assessed through primary MIC determination. Bacterial suspensions were standardized to subinhibitory concentration (1/2 MIC, 10⁷ CFU/mL) prior to 24-hour incubation at 37 °C for MIC evaluation. Sequential daily passages (n = 14) were performed to monitor potential resistance development.

Antibacterial mechanism of P-CQDs

To elucidate the electrostatic interactions and oxidative potential of the P-CQDs, their surface charge was characterized via zeta potential measurements in aqueous media and upon interaction with bacterial cells. Additionally, the hydroxyl radical (•OH) generation capacity of the system was measured quantitatively via fluorescence spectroscopy.

Fluorescence test

The assessment of P-CQDs’ selectivity towards CIP was conducted by first testing its fluorescence response against various analytes, including metal ions, anions, antibiotics, and amino acids. PL spectra of 0.001 g/mL P-CQDs in the presence of individual analytes (100 mM) were recorded using a 340 nm excitation source. Subsequently, the sensing performance was systematically examined by preparing mixtures containing P-CQDs, PBS buffer (pH = 7.2), and varying CIP concentrations. All measurements were conducted at room temperature, with triplicate experiments performed to guarantee reproducible findings.

Results and discussion

Characterization of P-CQDs

To determine the optimal synthesis conditions, the effects of reaction temperature, time, and phosphoric acid dosage on the antibacterial efficacy and fluorescence intensity of the as-prepared carbon dots were systematically investigated (Table.S1). The results indicated that the carbon dots exhibited optimal performance when synthesized with a phosphoric acid dosage of 1.5 mL at 160 °C for 6 h. Consequently, carbon dots synthesized under these optimized conditions were used in all subsequent experiments.

In Fig. 1, TEM imaging was employed to characterize the morphology and size distribution of the P-CQDs27,28., revealing well-dispersed quasi-spherical nanoparticles with diameters uniformly below 10 nm and an average size of 2.24 nm. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) imaging confirms the high crystallinity of the P-CQDs, exhibiting a distinct lattice fringe spacing of 0.21 nm29. As shown in Fig. 1d, to further confirm the structure of P-CDs-3, XRD analysis reveals a broad peak at approximately 2θ ≈ 33°, which corresponds to the (100) crystal plane of graphite. This result is consistent with the TEM observations. As shown in Fig. 1e, the Raman spectrum presents two distinct peaks: the G-band at approximately 1585 cm⁻¹ and the D-band at around 1350 cm⁻¹. The G-band and D-band are attributed to sp²-hybridized carbon and sp³-hybridized carbon in P-CQDs, respectively.

In Fig. 2a, FTIR spectra of P-CQDs confirmed the presence of functional groups30. The FT-IR analysis displayed distinct vibrational modes at 3405.04 (O–H/N-H), 1649.46 (C = O/C = C), and 1084.37 cm − 1 (C-O-C), 987.38.(P = O), 861.35 (C-H), 531.62 (P-O)31. We compared the FTIR spectra of P-CDs and P-CDs-CIP. The results showed that the C–OH stretching vibration peak of P-CDs-CIP was located at approximately 3315.99 cm⁻¹, which exhibited a red shift of about 90 cm⁻¹ compared to that of P-CDs (3405.04 cm⁻¹). This shift can be attributed to the formation of hydrogen bonds, which lengthen the relevant chemical bonds32.

XPS analysis confirmed the elemental composition of P-CQDs (Fig. 2b), revealing characteristic binding energy peaks at 531.2 eV (O 1s), 284.9 eV (C 1s), 399.8 eV (N 1s), and notably at 133.43 eV (P 2p), which unambiguously verifies successful phosphorus doping in the carbon dot matrix33. In the high-resolution C 1s spectrum of the P-CDs (Fig. 2c), four fitted peaks are observed at 284.4 eV, 286.4 eV, and 288.3 eV, attributable to the functional groups C = C/C-C, C-O/C = N/C-P, and C = O, respectively. In the N 1s spectrum (Fig. 2d), two deconvoluted peaks at 399.9 eV and 402.1 eV are identified as C-N-C and graphitic N, respectively (Fig. 2e). The O 1 s spectrum shows absorption peaks at 531.1 eV, 532.7 eV and 535.9 eV, corresponding to C = O, C − O−C/C − OH and C-O. Furthermore, the P 2p spectrum of P -CDs exhibits absorption peaks at 133.5 eV and 134.0 eV, corresponding to the presence of P-C and P–O bonds34 (Fig. 2f).

The optical properties of P-CQDs

The P-CQDs solution has a light brownish yellow color and changed to bright yellow under ultraviolet light due to the quantum confinement effect35 the absorption peak of the P-CQDs appears at 277 nm, which can be attributed to the π→π* transition of carbon-carbon double bonds (C = C) within the aromatic structure. Meanwhile, the broad absorption band in the range of 200–300 nm further confirms the presence of conjugated structures in the carbon core.36, as shown in Fig. 3a. The PL spectra were used to measure the FL intensity of P-CQDs. The highest emission intensities of the P-CQDs were measured for emission at 450 nm. The acquisition of P-CQDs fluorescence emission spectra was performed with excitation wavelengths incremented by 20 nm across the 280–400 nm range (Fig. 3b)37. Within carbon quantum dots, charge transfer may occur from the carbon core to surface functional groups. Strongly oxidizing functional groups such as carbonyl groups can engage in strong electronic coupling with the carbon core, thereby lowering the excited-state energy level and resulting in a red shift of the spectra. Doping with heteroatoms such as phosphorus can narrow the bandgap, representing an effective approach to achieving red-shifted emission38. Therefore, with increasing excitation wavelength, the P-CQDs exhibit a gradual redshift in their emission spectra accompanied by a reduction in fluorescence intensity. In addition, P-CQDs exhibit a high quantum yield, with an absolute fluorescence quantum yield (QY) of approximately 24.4%.

Stability is a critical factor for practical applications. Therefore, additional experiments were conducted to evaluate the robustness of P-CQDs, including resistance to salt, photobleaching, oxidation, and pH variations39. The negligible variation in fluorescence intensity under four distinct testing conditions (Fig. S1) provides additional evidence for the remarkable stability of P-CQDs.

Antibacterial activity of P-CQDs

The MIC of P-CQDs was determined using a standard broth microdilution method. Bacterial suspensions (100 µL) were combined with an equal volume (100 µL) of P-CQDs-containing medium in 96-well plates, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 12 h. As shown in Fig. 4, the OD600 measurements indicated MIC values of 7.14 µg/mL and 8.33 µg/mL against E. coli and S. aureus, respectively.

The growth curves of E. coli and S. aureus treated with varying concentrations of P-CQDs were evaluated by monitoring OD600 measurements, as illustrated in Fig. 5a-b40. Following treatment with P-CQDs at 1× MIC, both bacterial strains (E. coli and S. aureus) showed partially inhibited growth within 8 h. Upon increasing the P-CQDs concentration to 2 MIC, complete suppression of bacterial growth was observed for both strains over a 24-hour period.

The potential for bacterial resistance development to P-CQDs was assessed by monitoring MIC values over 14 consecutive generations (Fig. 5c)41. Throughout this passage study, the MICs for both E. coli and S. aureus remained stable, indicating that P-CQDs did not induce resistance in these strains.

Antibacterial mechanism of P-CQDs

To elucidate the antimicrobial mechanism of P-CQDs, this study systematically investigated the surface potential characteristics of bacterial cells, the surface charge distribution profile of P-CQDs, and their capacity to induce reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation42. As depicted in Fig. 6a, both E. coli and S. aureus initially displayed substantial negative zeta potential values, measuring − 20.15 mV and − 18.36 mV, respectively. Following interaction with P-CQDs, a marked reduction in the electronegativity was observed for both bacterial strains43. This indicates that the P-CQDs with a relatively high positive potential can effectively bind to bacterial surfaces via electrostatic interactions, leading to a significant reduction in the negative surface charge of bacteria.

Coumarin-3-carboxylic acid (CCA) functions as a hydroxyl radical (•OH)-specific fluorogenic probe that is selectively oxidized by •OH to form the highly fluorescent product 7-hydroxycoumarin-3-carboxylic acid (7OH-CCA). To evaluate the photoinduced •OH generation capacity of P-CQDs, a 5 mL aqueous reaction system containing 100 µM CCA, 70 µg/mL P-CQDs, and 10 mM NaOH was subjected to 532 nm laser irradiation (85 ± 1 mW/cm²) for 20 min under continuous agitation. Time-dependent fluorescence measurements (λex = 340 nm, λem = 450 nm) were performed to quantitatively monitor the formation of 7OH-CCA as an indicator of •OH production44. As evidenced by the distinct fluorescence intensity variations presented in Fig. 6b, the generation of hydroxyl radicals (•OH) was conclusively demonstrated.

To intuitively investigate the bactericidal effect of P-CQDs, the SEM was employed to observe the morphological changes of E. coli and S. aureus before and after treatment with P-CQDs. As shown in Fig. 7, the untreated bacteria exhibited intact cellular structures; after incubation with P-CQDs, the bacterial cells of both strains showed significant damage with leakage of cytoplasmic contents. These results indicate that P-CQDs can disrupt the bacterial cytoplasmic membrane and cause cytoplasmic leakage, and the underlying mechanism may be related to the generation of ROS.

Fluorometric detection of CIP

The P-CQDs demonstrate a pronounced fluorescence selectivity in the presence of particular target analytes45. Upon interaction with various metal ions, amino acids, antibiotics, and fluoroquinolone antibiotics, a pronounced fluorescence enhancement is exclusively observed upon the introduction of CIP, whereas other substances induce negligible spectral variations46. As illustrated in Figs. 8a–c, P-CQDs demonstrate exceptional specificity for CIP recognition. To verify whether the selective recognition ability of P-CQDs for ciprofloxacin in complex systems is affected by interference from other coexisting substances, an interference study was conducted, as shown in Fig. 8d. The results indicate that even in the presence of other interfering substances such as metal ions, amino acids, or antibiotics, P-CQDs still exhibit significant selectivity for CIP, with no obvious changes observed in the fluorescence intensity of the carbon dots. As shown in Fig. 9a, the concentration of P-CQDs remained constant while the fluorescence intensity at 450 nm gradually increased with rising CIP concentrations(0.0001, 0.0003, 0.0005, 0.0007, 0.0009…0.0.6 µmol/L). Within the concentration interval of 0.01–0.1 µmol/L, a linear calibration was achieved by correlating the normalized fluorescence ratio (F-F₀)/F₀ with CIP levels (Fig. 9b). F and F₀ refer to the fluorescence signals recorded with and without CIP, respectively47. The linear regression equation was (F-F0)/F0 = 2.3306x + 0.73915 (R² = 0.9992). Based on the 3σ/k calculation (where σ is the standard deviation and k is the slope), the detection LOD for CIP was determined to be 2.06 nM.

Mechanism of CIP detection

The comparison of the FTIR spectra of P-CDs and P-CDs-CIP further confirmed that the augmentation in fluorescence intensity arises due to hydrogen bond formation involving P-CQDs and CIP molecules48. The hydroxyl (-OH) and carboxyl (-COOH) groups on P-CQDs form an extensive hydrogen-bonding network with the fluorine (F) and nitrogen (N) atoms in CIP. As evidenced by UV-Vis absorption spectroscopy, the absorption peak of ciprofloxacin at 315 nm shifted to 323 nm upon the addition of P-CQDs, indicating a clear red shift in the absorption shoulder of the P-CQDs/CIP system (Fig. 9c). This suggests the occurrence of intermolecular interaction and partial electron transfer from P-CQDs to CIP. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 9d-e, the fluorescence lifetime of P-CQDs-C decreased from 2.71 ns to 2.58 ns after the introduction of CIP. This decline demonstrates that the hydrogen bonding interaction between CIP and P-CQDs facilitates non-radiative decay pathways while simultaneously enhancing the fluorescence emission intensity.

Conclusions

In this study, the P-CQDs were successfully synthesized via a hydrothermal approach using Caesalpinia sappan L. as carbon precursor and phosphoric acid. Antibacterial evaluation demonstrated that P-CQDs significantly inhibit the growth of both S. aureus and E. coli. The bactericidal mechanism is attributed to the synergistic effect of robust electrostatic interactions between P-CQDs and bacterial surfaces and hydroxyl radical (•OH) generation. Furthermore, the P-CQDs displayed outstanding sensing performance for ciprofloxacin (CIP) detection, achieving a wide linear response range from 0.01 to 0.1 µM with a detection limit of 2.06 nM.

While P-CQDs exhibit considerable potential in antibiosis and CIP detection, further investigation is required to its application in food preservation. In the field of food safety, it is imperative to develop portable sensing devices to enable on-site rapid monitoring. These advances will ultimately facilitate the translation of P-CQDs from laboratory research to clinical and commercial applications.
