The diagnostic value of CD101 in smear-negative pulmonary tuberculosis
Authors: Jingjing Ren, Shengyan Qu, Keyun Bian, Min Yao, Fan Yao, Feifan Xu, Rui Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnlike smear-positive tuberculosis (TB) which is more easily diagnosed due to the presence of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) in sputum, smear-negative pulmonary tuberculosis (SN-TB) poses a diagnostic challenge that can lead to delayed treatment hence new molecular diagnostic markers are urgently needed. The aim of this study was to analyze the expression of CD101 in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), T cells and natural killer (NK) cells of patients with SN-TB through a case-control study and to assess the value of CD101 alone or combined with other cytokines in the auxiliary diagnosis of SN-TB.MethodsSixty-three newly treated patients with TB and negative smear results diagnosis by clinicians were included as the SN-TB group, while 59 healthy individuals [healthy control (HC)] were included as the control group. The expression of CD101 in PBMCs of the two groups was detected via real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). The expression of CD101 on T cells and NK cells, as well as the expression of T helper (Th) 1/2/17-related cytokines was detected by flow cytometry. Spearman analysis was used to analyze the correlation between CD101 expression, cytokines and clinical indicators in patients with SN-TB. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to analyze the sensitivity and specificity of CD101 alone or in combination with other cytokines in diagnosing SN-TB.ResultsCompared with the control group, the experimental group showed high CD101 expression in PBMCs and CD4+ T cells but not in CD8+ T cells or NK cells. Th1/Th2-related cytokines interleukin-2 (IL-2), IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) were highly expressed in patients with SN-TB, but there was no significant difference in the expression of the Th17-related cytokine (IL-17A). The expression of CD101 on CD4+ T cells in patients with SN-TB was positively correlated with cytokines IL-2 and IFN-γ and the clinical indicators of ESR and CRP. CD101 on CD4+ T cells could well distinguish patients with SN-TB from HC, with a sensitivity of 71.43%, a specificity of 71.19% and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.7604. The sensitivity of CD101+CD4+ (%) combined with IL-2 was 73.25%, the specificity was 68.25% and the AUC was 0.7893. The sensitivity of CD101+CD4+ (%) combined with IFN-γ was 72.88%, the specificity was 66.67% and the AUC was 0.7678. The sensitivity of CD101+CD4+ (%) combined with IFN-γ and IL-2 was 74.58%, the specificity was 74.60% and the AUC was 0.7899.ConclusionsThe expression of CD101 on CD4+ T cells could distinguish patients with SN-TB from healthy individuals and was correlated with key TB infection cytokines, including IL-2 and IFN-γ, as well as with ESR and CRP. CD101 demonstrated good diagnostic efficacy in combination with cytokines. It might thus be an indicator for laboratory-assisted diagnosis of SN-TB.Keywords: CD101, smear-negative pulmonary tuberculosis (SN-TB), interleukin-2 (IL-2), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), molecular marker

Highlight box

Key findings

• Our study found that the detection of CD101 expression alone or combined with interleukin-2 could improve the auxiliary diagnosis of smear-negative pulmonary tuberculosis (SN-TB).

What is known and what is new?

• The role of CD101 expression in SN-TB has not been extensively investigated.

• We systematically analyzed the expression changes of CD101 in the peripheral blood mononuclear cells, T cells, and natural killer cells of patients with SN-TB and evaluated their value in diagnosing SN-TB when used alone or in combination with cytokines.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The expression of CD101 on CD4+ T cells was found to be an effective molecular marker for the diagnosis of SN-TB and may aid in diagnosis when used alone or in combination with certain cytokines.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is an infectious disease with global prevalence, with 9 million new cases and 15 million deaths being reported each year (1). Multidrug resistance in TB, particularly when accompanied by human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, poses a substantial threat to global health (2). Therefore, despite the availability of dedicated anti-TB drugs, the World Health Organization (WHO) considers the elimination of TB by 2035 to be a significant challenge. Approximately 20–50% of all TB cases are smear negative [smear-negative pulmonary tuberculosis (SN-TB)]. Due to the lack of a pathogenic evidence for Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB), patients with this disease cannot receive a rapid, effective, clinical diagnosis or timely treatment (3). Therefore, the development of novel diagnostic molecular targets or combined diagnosis is critical to improving the rate of early diagnosis and treatment of SN-TB.

CD101, a highly expressed transmembrane protein on T cells, monocytes, and dendritic cells has been shown to play an important role in several diseases, including inflammatory enteritis, diabetes, acute liver injury, asthma, acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), as well as cancer and immune diseases (4-8). Research indicates that the expression of CD101 is low in freshly isolated lymphocytes and thymocytes but significantly elevated after lymphocyte activation (9). One study found that T-cell receptor (TCR) cross-linking induced CD101 expression on human T cells, whereas anti-CD3 stimulated T-cell proliferation could be inhibited by anti-CD101 antibodies, suggesting a role for CD101 in TCR-dependent T-cell activation (10). Another study reported that CD4+CD101+ T cells could inhibit interleukin-2 (IL-2) production via treatment with a putative agonistic anti-CD101 antibody, which further supports the immunomodulatory role of CD101 (11). Recent research has also shown that a higher expression of CD101 is depleted at the end of infection and accompanied by high CD8+ T-cell dysfunction in chronic viral infection (12). Collectively, these findings suggest that CD101 is an immunomodulatory protein that may act as a suppressor of T cells in a variety of tissues and environments, including inflammatory processes, autoimmune diseases, and infectious diseases, such as TB. However, although the function of CD101 in T cells has been confirmed, few studies have specifically examined the role of CD101 in TB. We hypothesize to assess CD101 diagnostic value by examining its expression in the peripheral blood of patients with SN-TB, thus providing a theoretical foundation for the early diagnosis and treatment of SN-TB. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1299/rc).

Methods

Study participants

Outpatients or inpatients with clinically confirmed SN-TB admitted to The Sixth People’s Hospital of Nantong from January 2020 to December 2022 were included as the study cohort. The blood samples (4–5 mL heparin-anticoagulated fresh blood) of the participants were collected. As a case-control study, healthy individuals who underwent physical examination at the same period were included as the healthy control (HC) group. According to inclusion criteria, a total of 63 patients with SN-TB and 59 HC were enrolled. The inclusion criteria of SN-TB group were primarily based on the Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Tuberculosis (2) and were evaluated by clinicians. The diagnosis could be confirmed by three negative smear tests or negative culture tests and the fulfillment of three of the following criteria: (I) effective anti-TB treatment; (II) typical symptoms and chest X-ray features; (III) strongly positive findings for the purified protein derivative (PPD) test or positive findings for the TB antibody test; (IV) clinical exclusion of other nontuberculous pulmonary diseases; (V) positive findings in sputum polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and probe detection for TB; (VI) confirmed TB changes confirmed by extrapulmonary pathology; (VII) MTB detected in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF); and (VIII) MTB confirmed by pathology of bronchial or lung tissue. Participants in the HC group were identified by professional clinicians according to the following criteria: (I) no history of TB infection; (II) no abnormal imaging change; and (III) negative PPD test results. All participants were Han Chinese and none were related by blood. Participants with low immune function, hepatitis B infection, HIV infection, allergic diseases, smoking habit, alcohol habit, or other unhealthy lifestyle factors were excluded. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Sixth People’s Hospital of Nantong (No. NTLYLL2019017). All participants were aware of the study process and signed informed consent. The index test interpreters and reference standard assessors were blinded in the study.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

RT-qPCR was used to detect the expression of Cd101 in PBMCs. The steps of RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and RT-qPCR are described elsewhere (13). The primers of the study were as follows: Gapdh forward, GGTGGTCTCCTCTGACTTCAACA; Gapdh reverse, TCTCTTCCTCTTGTGCTCTTGCT; Cd101 forward, AAGGTTACCCAGTCAGCATTG; and Cd101 reverse, CTTGGTGCTAATGATCTGGACTT.

Detection of CD101 in peripheral blood T cells

Flow cytometry was used to detect the expression of CD101 in peripheral blood T cells. First, 100 µL of heparin-anticoagulated fresh whole blood was collected within 6 h after blood collection and added to a flow tube. The groups were set, and fluorescent antibodies [CD4-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), CD8-FITC, and CD101-phycoerythrin (PE); BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA] were added to each test tube and mixed. The mixture was stained for 30 min in the dark. Subsequently, 2 mL of red cell lysate was added and lysed in the dark for 20 min. Samples were centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was removed, and the cells were washed via the addition of 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Centrifugation was performed once at 1,500 rpm for 5 min, after which 200 µL of PBS was added, mixed, and tested on the FC500 flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), with experimental results being analyzed by a flow analysis software (Beckman Coulter).

Detection of T helper (Th) 1/2/17 cytokines

Cytokines were detected via flow cytometry and a Th1/Th2/17 detection kit (BD Biosciences) in accordance with the operating instructions of the manufacturer. In brief, the samples of the study participants were collected and captured microspheres of various cytokines were added to all light-blocking tubes, mixed and centrifuged at 200 g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was resuspended in serum enhancement solution, mixed thoroughly, and left at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. Subsequently, 50 µL of mixed microspheres, 50 µL of sample or standard to be tested, and 50 µL of human Th1/Th2/Th17 phycoerythrin detection reagent were added to each tube, mixed, and left at room temperature for 3 h. After a wash, the precipitate was resuspended in 300 µL of wash solution and cells were acquired on the FC500 flow cytometer. The final concentration was analyzed and calculated via flow analysis software (Beckman Coulter) according to the standard curve.

MTB etiology and acquisition of clinical laboratory indicators

The etiological diagnosis (such as GeneXpert), clinical laboratory results, biochemical and immunological indicators (such as TSPOT.TB) of all groups were obtained from the clinical test data of the patients. The testing process was conducted according to the standardized laboratory procedures.

Statistical analysis

The information of included participants in this study, CD101 expression in T and NK cells and cytokines expression were presented as the percentage (%) and the mean ± standard deviation (SD). GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA) and SPSS 17.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) were used for all data analysis. The t-test was used for statistical analysis. The sensitivity, specificity, 95% confidence interval (CI), and area under the curve (AUC) of the experimental diagnosis were calculated were analyzed via the receiver operating characteristic (ROC). Spearman correlation analysis was used to determine the correlation between the CD101 expression and associated cytokines. A two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General information of included participants

According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 63 patients with SN-TB and 59 HC were enrolled. Compared with that of HC the hemoglobin (HGB) level in patients with SN-TB was significantly lower (Table 1, P<0.05), while the levels of white blood cell count (WBC), C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and immunoglobulin E (IgE) were significantly increased (Table 1, P<0.05). Among the patients with SN-TB, 59 (93.65%) were positive for the T cell-based immunospot test for TB (TSPOT.TB). Sixty patients with SN-TB were PPD positive. The inconsistency between TSPOT.TB and PPD might have been due to the fact that PPD is inferior to TSPOT.TB. There were 52 patients with obvious shadow on computed tomography (CT) examination. The positive rate of BALF MTB culture was 4.76% in 3 patients, and the positive rate of sputum MTB culture was 3.17% in only 2 patients according GeneXpert. GeneXpert detected 10 positive cases of BALF samples, including 7 cases of very low and 3 cases of low, with a positive rate of 15.87% (Table 1). Thus, the positive rate of BALF samples was significantly higher than that of sputum samples in GeneXpert detection. There was no significant difference in other statistical indicators (Table 1, P>0.05).

Comparison of Cd101 expression in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) between the two groups

RT-qPCR was used to determine the expression of Cd101 in the PBMCs of the two groups. As shown in Figure 1A, Cd101 was highly expressed in the PBMCs of patients with SN-TB (P=0.04). In addition, we isolated PBMCs from HC and cultured the for 24 h. One group was stimulated with the standard MTB strain H37Rv [multiplicity of infection (MOI) =5], while the other group was not and was considered to be the control group. After 24 h, the expression of Cd101 in PBMCs of the two groups was detected via RT-qPCR. Compared with the unstimulated group, the expression of Cd101 in the H37Rv-stimulated group was significantly higher (P=0.03; Figure 1B). These results suggested that MTB infection could lead to the increase in Cd101 expression in PBMCs.

Comparison of CD101 expression in T cells between the two groups

PBMCs are composed of lymphocytes, monocytes, dendritic cells, and a few other cells, among which T lymphocytes play an important role in intracellular immunity against MTB infection (2). CD101 has also been shown to play an important role in T cells (9). To further determine the role of CD101 in MTB-infected T cells, the expression of CD101 on the surface of CD4 and CD8 T lymphocytes was measured by flow cytometry. As shown in Figure 2, CD101 was highly expressed on the surface of CD4+ T cells in SN-TB compared with HC (23.76%±7.34% vs. 17.21%±4.42%; P<0.001) but not on the surface of CD8+ T cells in SN-TB compared with HC (25.50%±7.77% vs. 23.30%±4.95%; P>0.05). This could indicate that CD101 was highly expressed in the peripheral blood CD4+ T cells of patients with SN-TB and might play an important role in host immunity in MTB infection.

Changes in CD101 expression in the peripheral blood of natural killer (NK) cells of the two groups

NK cells may also figure prominently in MTB infection (1). To confirm whether the CD101 expression in NK cells differed between the two groups, we analyzed the change in CD101 expression in NK cells through flow cytometry and calculated the change in CD101 expression in the CD56+ cell population. We found that there was no significant difference in CD101 expression in the CD56+ cells between the SN-TB group and the HC group (5.49%±2.08% vs. 5.33%±1.54%; P=0.32; Figure 3). These results suggested that the effect of CD101 on host immunity to MTB infection might primarily occur through CD4+ T cells rather than NK cells.

Comparison of the expression of key factors in the peripheral blood between the two groups

After MTB infection, alveolar macrophages phagocytose MTB and stimulate the T-cell immune response, prompting CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells to secrete certain cytokines (1). Th1 cytokines [such as IL-2, interferon-γ (IFN-γ), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)], Th2 cytokines (such as IL-4, IL-6, IL-10), and Th17 cytokines (IL-17A) have been demonstrated to be critical in MTB infection (1). CD101 is also involved in the regulation of IL-2-Treg/Th17 balance. In order to further confirm whether the expression of CD101 on CD4+ T cells was associated these inflammatory factors, we first detected the expression changes of these inflammatory factors in the peripheral blood of HC and patients with SN-TB using flow cytometry. According to Figure 4, compared with those in the HC group, the levels of IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, and IFN-γ in the SN-TB group were significantly higher (IL-2: 19.53±3.19 vs. 17.53±2.90 pg/mL, P<0.001; IL-4: 18.37±1.74 vs. 17.64±1.36 pg/mL, P=0.01; IL-6: 92.43±23.80 vs. 26.19±4.37 pg/mL, P<0.001; IL-10: 23.86±3.11 vs. 22.50±3.39 pg/mL, P=0.02; TNF-α: 23.67±2.26 vs. 22.42±2.57 pg/mL, P=0.005; IFN-γ: 14.76±1.65 vs. 14.03±1.57 pg/mL, P=0.01; Figure 4A-4F). There was no significant difference in serum IL-17A expression between the SN-TB group and HC group (31.66±2.69 vs. 30.60±4.25 pg/mL; P>0.05; Figure 4G).

Correlation analysis between CD101 and key cytokines

Spearman analysis was used to determine the correlation between CD101 and IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-17A in the CD4+ T cells of the SN-TB group. The expression of CD101 on the surface of CD4+ T cells was positively correlated with IL-2 (r=0.4049; P=0.001; Figure 5A) and IFN-γ (r=0.4183; P<0.001; Figure 5B), but there was no correlation with other indicators (P>0.05). Additionally, we analyzed the correlation between CD101 expression on the surface of CD4+ T cells and the clinical indicators. As shown in Figure 6A,6B, the expression of CD101 on CD4+ T cells was positively correlated with ESR (r=0.4648; P<0.001) and CRP (r=0.5017; P<0.001), but there was no correlation with the other indicators (P>0.05). These results suggested that the CD101 expression on CD4+ T cells of patients with SN-TB was positively correlated with key Th1-type cytokines, including IL-2 and IFN-γ, and with inflammatory indicators (high ESR or CRP).

Analysis of the diagnostic efficacy of CD101 on the surface of CD4+ T cells and combined cytokine detection in SN-TB

As the expression of CD101 on CD4+ T cells was higher in the SN-TB group than in the HC group, which was closely associated with certain key cytokines after MTB infection, we further analyzed the value of CD101 on CD4+ T cells alone and in combination with these cytokines in diagnosing SN-TB. Our study found that CD101+CD4+ (%) alone had a sensitivity of 71.43%, a specificity of 71.19%, an AUC of 0.7604, and a 95% CI of 0.6765–0.8444 for the diagnosis of SN-TB (Figure 7A). The sensitivity of CD101+CD4+ (%) combined with IL-2 in the diagnosis of SN-TB was 73.25%, the specificity was 68.25%, the AUC was 0.7893 and the 95% CI was 0.7116–0.8671. The sensitivity of CD101+CD4+ (%) combined with IFN-γ in the diagnosis of SN-TB was 72.88%, the specificity was 66.67%, the AUC was 0.7678 and the 95% CI was 0.6859–0.8498. The sensitivity of CD101+CD4+ (%) combined with IFN-γ and IL-2 in the diagnosis of SN-TB was 74.58%, the specificity was 74.60%, the AUC was 0.7899 and the 95% CI was 0.7112–0.8685 (Figure 7B). This indicated that CD101 on the surface of CD4+ T cells could effectively distinguish patients with SN-TB from healthy individuals, and its combination with key cytokines could improve the diagnostic sensitivity and specificity in SN-TB detection.

Discussion

Effective laboratory diagnosis and timely treatment of SN-TB remains a challenge, drawing research attention from around the globe (14). With the development of molecular biology and immunological techniques, the laboratory diagnosis of SN-TB has significantly advanced. Multicolor flow cytometry of MTB antigen-specific T cells can be used to distinguish SN-TB from other non-TB diseases (15). A team from India found that GeneXpert had better sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) in the diagnosis of SN-TB (16). It had also been found that SN-TB is characterized by fewer lung cavities, mild inflammation, a lower number of immune cells, and a higher number of B cells (17), and a novel MTB DNA detection platform of loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) and CRISPR-Cas12b detection has been developed (18). However, aside from GeneXpert technology, which has been widely recognized by WHO, technologies or molecular markers for SN-TB are generally lacking. In this study, we found that CD101 was highly expressed in the PBMCs of patients with SN-TB, especially in CD4+ T cells, but not CD8+ T cells or NK cells. We also discovered that CD101 on CD4+ T cells was associated with clinical inflammation markers ESR and CRP in patients with MTB infection, suggesting that CD101 on CD4+ T cells may serve as an auxiliary molecular target for the diagnosis of SN-TB and indicate host MTB infection status.

As an important molecule in the regulation of T cells, CD101 has been confirmed to exert its function primarily by regulating the expression of interleukin-2 receptor (IL-2R) (9). The role of IL-2 in T cells and MTB infection has also been reported, with IL-2 being found to restore the T-cell dysfunction induced by continuous antigenic stimulation in a mouse model (19). IL-2 has been also shown to effectively reverse CD8+ T-cell exhaustion in the malignant pleural effusion of lung cancer (20). During persistent viral infections such as HIV, simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV), and cytomegalovirus (CMV), recombinant IL-2 can improve T-cell survival, reverse T-cell exhaustion and maintain the number and function of virus-specific CD8+ T cells (21-23). In addition, IL-2 exerts a therapeutic effect in multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB). It was reported that IL-2 treatment of MDR-TB in combination with antibiotics results in a higher conversion rate from positive to negative sputum compared with drug treatment control alone (24). A recent study has shown that IL-2 treatment can contribute to balancing cytokine homeostasis in the bone marrow and restore dysfunctional hematopoiesis compromised by continuous antigenic stimulation (25). In our study found, that the expression of IL-2 in the peripheral blood serum of patients with SN-TB was significantly increased, and its expression was correlated with the expression of CD101+CD4+ (%). The combined detection of CD101+CD4+ (%) and IL-2 could significantly improve the sensitivity and specificity of single detection. Moreover, there was evidence of mutual regulation between CD101, MTB infection, T cells, and IL-2 expression, the mechanism of which should be further examined.

It has been reported that IFN-γ exerts a critical function in resistance to MTB infection and that CD4 T cells secrete IFN-γ to control MTB growth in infected macrophages (26). In addition, IFN-γ could also act on MTB through mechanisms such as phagosome maturation, activation of host cell programmed death, and reactive oxygen species generation (27-29). In one study, mice lacking IFN-γ signaling were one of the strains most susceptible to MTB infection (30). IFN-γ is also an important diagnostic indicator for confirming MTB infection. Kang et al. found that the combined use of a whole-blood IFN-γ release method and whole-blood count method could quickly differentiate active TB from latent MTB infection (LTBI) (31). Another study found that CD27−, CD38+, HLADR+, or Ki-67+ in IFN-γ+CD4+ T cells could distinguish LTBI from active TB. The specificity of the combination of IFN-γ and IL-6 in the diagnosis of TB was 50% (95% CI: 46.7–53.3%) (32). Other research indicates that positive results for IFN-γ and adenosine deaminase (ADA) could improve the diagnostic efficacy of TB (33). Tang et al. found that the combination of pleural fluid IFN-γ release assay, ADA and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) detection could provide a more effective diagnostic strategy for the differentiation of TB and tuberculous pleurisy (34). Chen et al. found that combined direct detection of serum IFN-γ and IGRA with lymphocyte subsets and activation indicators could provide laboratory evidence for the diagnosis and differential diagnosis of TB (35). Zhang et al. tested the IFN-γ/IL-2 fluorescent spot method and found that it could effectively distinguish active TB from inactive TB (36). In another study, researchers also found that the sensitivity, specificity and accuracy of IFN-γ, IL-2R and IL-4 in the diagnosis of MTB infection were 89.7%, 96.1% and 92.7%, respectively (37). Gene expression combinations of cytokines and chemokines may serve as effective markers for determining MTB infection status (38). Our study found that there was a positive correlation between CD101+CD4+ (%) and IFN-γ expression, but the combined detection of CD101+CD4+ (%) did not improve the sensitivity and specificity of CD101+CD4+ (%) detection, which might be due to the limitations of the single-center design and small sample size.

Conclusions

The expression of CD101 in patients with SN-TB was significantly increased in this study, suggesting that CD101 might serve as a molecular marker for the auxiliary diagnosis of SN-TB. CD101 alone or in combination with IL-2 demonstrated good diagnostic performance. Due to the limitations of the single-center design, small sample size, reference standard flaws, etc., the efficacy in the prognostic evaluation of TB needs to be scrutinized in greater depth.
