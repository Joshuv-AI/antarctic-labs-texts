Regional amyloid PET asymmetry and long‐term clinical trajectory in mild cognitive impairment
Authors: Yunjin Lee, Sungwoo Kang, Yong Sung Kim, June Sic Kim, Hee‐Jin Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONHemispheric amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) asymmetry may capture prognostic information beyond binary amyloid status in amnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI).METHODSWe retrospectively studied 158 aMCI patients with baseline 18F‐florbetaben PET and magnetic resonance imaging acquired within 12 months and a mean follow‐up of 6.89 ± 2.63 years. Participants were classified as amyloid beta (Aβ)− stable (n = 67), Aβ− progressor (n = 18), Aβ+ stable (n = 33), or Aβ+ progressor (n = 40). INTRODUCTION

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is an intermediate stage between normal aging and dementia, yet its clinical course is highly heterogeneous. 1 , 2 While some individuals progress to Alzheimer's disease (AD) dementia, others remain stable for years or revert to a cognitively normal range. 3 , 4 This heterogeneity suggests that distinct pathological mechanisms and neurodegenerative trajectories already coexist at the MCI stage, underscoring the need for biomarkers that can identify high‐risk individuals early and more precisely predict subsequent clinical progression.

In AD, the accumulation of amyloid beta (Aβ) is a key pathological hallmark and core biomarker. Amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) studies have repeatedly demonstrated that higher Aβ burden is associated with increased risk of clinical progression and faster cognitive decline in both cognitively normal and MCI populations. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 However, global Aβ burden alone may be insufficient to account for individual prognostic differences. Current clinical definitions of Aβ positivity typically rely on global cortical standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) cut‐offs or binary visual reads (e.g., brain amyloid plaque load [BAPL]), which compress a continuous and spatially heterogeneous process into a single label. As a result, subthreshold, regionally restricted, or lateralized uptake patterns may be under‐recognized, particularly among MCI individuals classified as Aβ– by global measures. Some Aβ– MCI patients still progress to dementia, while a subset of Aβ+ individuals remain clinically stable, motivating the need to consider not only the presence or global load of Aβ, but also its regional distribution when interpreting prognostic heterogeneity in MCI.

Recent studies have increasingly focused on hemispheric asymmetry in Aβ distribution. Although Aβ deposition is, on average, relatively symmetric at the group level in typical AD dementia, 9 patient‐level analyses have shown that Aβ can accumulate disproportionately in one hemisphere or within specific association cortices, and that such asymmetry may align with ipsilateral hypometabolism and lateralized cognitive symptoms. 10 , 11 Stage‐focused work further suggests that Aβ distribution tends to be more asymmetric in preclinical or early‐stage AD, with asymmetry diminishing as pathology becomes more widespread across MCI and dementia stages. 12 , 13 This pattern implies that asymmetry may index a more focal/early distribution, whereas reduced asymmetry may reflect more diffuse cortical involvement, potentially relating to prognostic heterogeneity in MCI. However, it remains unclear whether Aβ asymmetry differs by amyloid status within MCI and, critically, whether focal asymmetry can distinguish stable from progressor individuals within the same amyloid status.

In the present study, we examined an amnestic MCI (aMCI) cohort that underwent 18F‐florbetaben (FBB) amyloid PET and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Participants were classified into four subgroups according to amyloid status (Aβ− versus Aβ+) and long‐term clinical outcome (stable MCI [sMCI] vs. progression to probable AD dementia [pMCI]): Aβ− stable (Aβ− sMCI), Aβ− progressor (Aβ− pMCI), Aβ+ stable (Aβ+ sMCI), and Aβ+ progressor (Aβ+ pMCI). To capture spatially heterogeneous and potentially lateralized cortical Aβ uptake beyond global burden, we derived a cortical surface‐based SUVR asymmetry index and analyzed its magnitude (|AI|). We aimed to: (1) characterize differences in the level and spatial pattern of cortical Aβ asymmetry (|AI|) by Aβ status at the MCI stage, (2) determine whether focal regions of Aβ asymmetry distinguish sMCI from pMCI within the same Aβ status, and (3) evaluate whether Aβ asymmetry is associated with prognostic heterogeneity beyond binary amyloid grouping among patients with aMCI.

METHODS

Participants

We retrospectively identified patients with MCI who underwent baseline FBB amyloid PET, brain MRI, and neuropsychological assessment at our center between November 2013 and September 2024. Participants were included if PET and MRI were performed within 12 months of each other, and imaging data passed quality control. We excluded individuals with non‐amnestic MCI, Parkinson's disease/parkinsonism, clinically significant cerebrovascular disease (e.g., prior stroke or moderate‐to‐severe white matter hyperintensities), technical failure in image acquisition/processing, missing key laboratory/genetic data required for analysis (e.g., apolipoprotein E [APOE] genotype), or insufficient longitudinal follow‐up to determine clinical trajectory. After applying these criteria, 158 patients were included in the final analytic cohort (Figure 1).

Patients with MCI who underwent baseline FBB amyloid PET and structural MRI within 12 months were retrospectively screened (November 2013–September 2024). After applying eligibility criteria (including exclusion of non‐amnestic MCI) and imaging quality control, 158 participants with aMCI were included and followed longitudinally for a mean duration of 6.89 ± 2.63 years. Amyloid status was determined by standardized consensus visual reads (Aβ− vs. Aβ+), and clinical trajectory was adjudicated by multidisciplinary consensus as sMCI or pMCI (conversion to probable Alzheimer's disease dementia), yielding four subgroups: Aβ− sMCI (n = 67), Aβ− pMCI (n = 18), Aβ+ sMCI (n = 33), and Aβ+ pMCI (n = 40). All participants met clinical criteria for MCI at baseline according to the Albert and Petersen frameworks. 2 , 14 Only individuals with aMCI were included, defined by objective episodic memory impairment on comprehensive neuropsychological testing. 15 , 16 Global measures included the Korean Mini‐Mental State Examination (K‐MMSE), Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR), and CDR Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB). 17 , 18

Aβ status (Aβ− vs. Aβ+) was determined by standardized consensus visual reads of FBB PET; the visual reading procedure is described in section 2.2. Amyloid status was defined in accordance with the National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer's Association (NIA‐AA) research framework for AD. 19

Participants were followed longitudinally through regular outpatient visits for a mean duration of 6.89 ± 2.63 years. At each visit, clinical interviews, functional assessments, and cognitive testing (K‐MMSE, CDR/CDR‐SB, and, when available, repeat detailed neuropsychological testing) were reviewed in a multidisciplinary consensus conference comprising neurologists and neuropsychologists. sMCI was defined as no conversion to dementia during follow‐up, with maintenance of a non‐demented clinical status (global CDR ≤ 0.5) and no consensus diagnosis of dementia at any follow‐up visit. pMCI was defined as progression to probable AD dementia during follow‐up, based on NIA‐AA criteria and supported by evidence of functional decline accompanied by cognitive worsening. 20

For primary analyses, participants were stratified into four groups according to Aβ status and longitudinal outcome: Aβ− sMCI (n = 67), Aβ− pMCI (n = 18), Aβ+ sMCI (n = 33), and Aβ+ pMCI (n = 40), yielding a total sample of 158 aMCI patients.

MRI/PET acquisition and SUVR preprocessing

MRI acquisition

T1‐weighted anatomical images were acquired on a Philips Ingenia CX 3 T scanner (Philips Medical Systems; echo time/repetition time = 4.1/6.9 ms, flip angle = 8°, voxel size = 0.8 × 0.8 × 1.0 mm3). Cortical surface reconstruction and subcortical segmentation were performed using FreeSurfer v7.4.1 following established surface‐based methods. 21 , 22 White matter hyperintensities (WMHs) were segmented from fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images co‐registered to each participant's T1‐weighted MRI using the Unidentified Bright Object (UBO) Detector. Default parameters were used (k = 5, probability threshold = 0.7), and all WMH segmentations were visually inspected on the corresponding FLAIR images for quality control. WMH burden was summarized as total whole‐brain WMH volume for demographic characterization.

Amyloid PET/computed tomography acquisition

Participants underwent FBB (Neuraceq) PET/computed tomography (CT) on dedicated scanners (Biograph 6; Siemens Medical Systems). A bolus of 300 MBq FBB was administered intravenously, followed by a 90‐minute uptake period, after which PET emission data were acquired for ≈ 20 minutes. Low‐dose CT was acquired for attenuation correction. Visual amyloid classification followed the established BAPL procedure for FBB PET. 23 , 24 The PET scans were reviewed by two board‐certified nuclear medicine physicians and one board‐certified neurologist using predefined regional cortical tracer uptake (RCTU) and BAPL scoring systems. Four cortical regions—the frontal, temporal, and parietal cortices and the posterior cingulate/precuneus—were assessed in the visual analysis. The regional RCTU ratings were summarized into a three‐level BAPL score for each PET scan: BAPL 1, no Aβ load; BAPL 2, minor Aβ load; and BAPL 3, significant Aβ load. For the present analyses, BAPL 1 was classified as Aβ−, and BAPL 2 and 3 were classified as Aβ+.

SUVR preprocessing

Structural T1‐weighted MRI was first processed with FreeSurfer to generate subject‐specific anatomical segmentations and cortical surface reconstructions. Amyloid PET images were processed using FreeSurfer and FSL (v6.0.7.4). PET images were rigidly co‐registered to each participant's native T1‐weighted MRI and resampled into native T1 space with trilinear interpolation. The bilateral cerebellar cortex reference region was defined from the FreeSurfer anatomical segmentation, and voxel‐wise SUVR images were generated by normalization to mean cerebellar cortical uptake. Regional and global cortical SUVR measures were derived from the anatomical segmentation. 25 , 26

Cortical surface‐based SUVR asymmetry analysis

We quantified hemispheric asymmetry of amyloid PET signal using a cortical surface‐based approach in FreeSurfer. Co‐registered PET SUVR volumes were projected onto each participant's native cortical surface, and the resulting surface maps were resampled to the standard fsaverage surface using FreeSurfer surface‐based registration to ensure vertex‐wise correspondence across participants, with analyses restricted to the cortical ribbon. 21 , 22 A vertex‐wise asymmetry index (AI) was defined as AI (%) = (L − R)/(L + R) × 200, and analyses focused on the magnitude of asymmetry using |AI|, consistent with prior amyloid PET asymmetry studies. 10 , 12 To avoid redundancy, group analyses were performed on left‐hemisphere |AI| maps. Surface maps were smoothed with an 8 mm full width half maximum kernel on fsaverage.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics

Continuous variables were compared with one‑way analysis of variance (post hoc Tukey honestly significant difference), and categorical variables with χ2 tests (pairwise χ2 with Holm correction when applicable). Analyses were conducted in Python 3.10. Two‑tailed α = 0.05 was used for non‑imaging analyses.

Imaging statistics

Vertex‐wise modeling was performed using general linear models. The primary vertex‐wise model included group, age, sex, education years, APOE ε4 carrier status, global cortical SUVR, and clinical follow‐up duration as covariates. Multiple comparisons were controlled using Monte Carlo cluster‐wise correction with cluster‐forming thresholds of |z| > 3.3, > 3.0, and > 2.3 (two sided), with cluster‐wise p < 0.05. Primary vertex‐wise results are presented at z > 3.3, and the corresponding z > 3.0 and z > 2.3 results are provided as sensitivity analyses in Table S1 in supporting information.

To evaluate the robustness and specificity of the primary |AI| findings, we performed four additional sensitivity analyses. First, regional cortical‐thickness asymmetry indices were computed in the anatomical regions corresponding to the main vertex‐wise findings to evaluate whether thickness asymmetry could contribute to the PET |AI| signal through partial volume effects (Table S2 in supporting information). Second, |AI| was computed at the anatomical region of interest (ROI) level for the same target regions, and progression was modeled using logistic regression with the same baseline covariates plus bilateral regional SUVR, providing an ROI‐level test of incremental information beyond regional amyloid burden (Table S3 in supporting information). Third, the primary vertex‐wise model was refit with amyloid extent as an additional covariate. Amyloid extent was operationalized as the proportion of cortical regions with suprathreshold bilateral SUVR (≥ 1.30) 23 and was computed at both whole‐cortex and target‐region scales to test whether |AI| primarily reflected the spatial spread of suprathreshold amyloid signal (Table S4 in supporting information). Fourth, we assessed subject‐level influence and whole‐cohort sensitivity by excluding influential observations from the Aβ− vertex‐wise analysis and by fitting a progression‐status model in the full cohort adjusted for amyloid status (Table S5 in supporting information).

Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consent

This study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of Hanyang University Medical Center (IRB No. 2023‐06‐019; approval date: September 12, 2023), and all procedures complied with relevant national regulations, institutional guidelines, and the ethical standards of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legally authorized representatives, as applicable. Study data were used exclusively for research purposes.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we cross‐classified aMCI according to amyloid status (Aβ−/Aβ+) and long‐term clinical trajectory (stable/progressor) to evaluate whether hemispheric asymmetry (|AI|) of cortical amyloid PET SUVR reflects prognostic heterogeneity. We found that, in Aβ− MCI, the progressor group (pMCI) showed higher |AI| in the lateral orbitofrontal cortex than the stable group (sMCI), whereas in Aβ+ MCI, the progressor group showed lower |AI| in the occipital cortex, particularly in the lingual gyrus. Thus, the association between |AI| and long‐term clinical trajectory did not appear in the same direction across amyloid states; in Aβ− MCI, it was associated with a more focal and asymmetric distribution, whereas in Aβ+ MCI, it was associated with a more bilateral and homogenized distribution. These findings suggest that |AI| may not be a unidirectional prognostic marker, but rather a distribution‐based marker that reflects distinct spatial pathological patterns according to the underlying amyloid state. In addition, aMCI is regarded as an MCI subtype more closely linked to AD, and the present findings should be interpreted within this clinical context. 27

Aβ− MCI is pathologically heterogeneous even when it is clinically classified within the same category, and conceptually overlaps with suspected non‐Alzheimer pathophysiology (SNAP). 28 Accumulating evidence also suggests that some individuals classified clinically as Aβ− or subthreshold may still show MRI atrophy, fluorodeoxyglucose hypometabolism, or non‐AD biomarker changes and subsequently progress to dementia. 29 , 30 , 31 In this context, our finding that the Aβ− MCI progressor group showed higher |AI| in the lateral orbitofrontal cortex than the stable group suggests that increased orbitofrontal |AI| may reflect focal vulnerability associated with prognosis, or restricted/local distributional features not adequately captured by global or dichotomous Aβ classification, rather than a marker specific to a single underlying pathology. Reports that prefrontal/orbitofrontal regions may be involved in early co‐accumulation patterns under low‐burden conditions, 32 and observations that orbitofrontal distributional features may be associated with subsequent pathology, neurodegeneration, and cognitive decline 33 are consistent with this interpretation. In the Aβ− lateral orbitofrontal region, the vertex‐wise effect remained significant after adjustment for bilateral SUVR and after adjustment for amyloid extent, whereas the corresponding anatomical ROI effect did not (Tables S3 and S4). The Aβ− prognostic signal therefore appears spatially focal and is lost when averaged across the entire region. Surface‐based vertex‐wise analysis is needed to capture it; in low‐burden settings in which plaque‐specific signal is limited and topographically restricted, global SUVR, dichotomous amyloid classification, or anatomical ROI averaging may miss this kind of distributional signal.

By contrast, in Aβ+ MCI, the progressor group showed lower |AI| in the occipital region (lingual gyrus) than the stable group. Because |AI| quantifies the magnitude of interhemispheric difference, this reduction is more appropriately interpreted not as a favorable decrease in asymmetry, but as a distributional state in which lingual amyloid signal becomes more similar between the two hemispheres. This interpretation is supported by reports that amyloid deposition in typical AD becomes relatively widespread and bilateral with disease progression, and that early lateralization may diminish across disease stages, 10 , 12 , 13 as well as by observations that occipital involvement may be associated with more rapid clinical worsening in some cohorts. 34 , 35 , 36 The ROI‐level lingual |AI| analysis showed the same direction as the vertex‐wise result and remained associated with progression status after adjustment for bilateral lingual SUVR; the vertex‐wise lingual cluster also persisted after adjustment for amyloid extent (Tables S3 and S4).

The present findings suggest that pathological burden (load) and spatial distribution of amyloid may provide distinct yet complementary information. From a mechanistic perspective, the possibility that the spatial distribution of amyloid is linked to downstream pathology has been repeatedly proposed in previous studies. Network‐based neuroimaging studies have reported that the spatial distribution of amyloid may be related to patterns of interaction with tau pathology and to subsequent pathological spread. 37 In addition, tau PET studies have suggested that tau laterality may align with amyloid laterality, and that baseline amyloid asymmetry may be associated with the subsequent trajectory of tau asymmetry. 38 , 39 Together, these findings support an interpretive framework in which not only the total amount of amyloid accumulation, but also the cortical topography and heterogeneity of its distribution, may be linked to subsequent pathological cascades and clinical trajectory.

The present study has several strengths. First, by restricting the cohort to aMCI, we reduced one layer of additional clinical heterogeneity present across the broader MCI spectrum and were able to evaluate the prognostic significance of amyloid asymmetry within a more AD‐relevant clinical phenotype. Second, by cross‐classifying Aβ status and long‐term clinical trajectory, we analyzed both between‐status differences and within‐status prognostic contrasts within a single integrated framework. Third, the long follow‐up period of up to 13 years allowed us to define the stable group (sMCI) relatively stringently from a clinical perspective, thereby increasing the reliability of the findings. Finally, surface‐based vertex‐wise analysis allowed us to identify spatially focal asymmetry patterns at a topographic resolution beyond that captured by ROI‐level or global hemispheric measures.

Limitations

Nevertheless, the single‐center retrospective cohort design and the limited subgroup sample sizes, especially in the Aβ− group, should be considered when interpreting generalizability and statistical power. Although follow‐up duration was included as a covariate in the primary analyses, residual follow‐up–related classification bias cannot be fully excluded. Several methodological caveats should also be considered when interpreting the asymmetry signal. Amyloid PET tracers such as FBB exhibit non‐specific binding, particularly to white matter and other off‐target structures, and this background signal may differ across hemispheres or regions. Although the signed‐AI check suggested that the Aβ− lateral orbitofrontal group difference was not created solely by converting signed asymmetry values into absolute |AI| values, residual contributions of spatial normalization, non‐specific binding, or off‐target binding cannot be fully excluded, particularly in the Aβ− stratum, in which plaque‐specific signal is low. In addition, cortical thickness asymmetry analyses in the corresponding target regions did not show group differences or PET thickness alignment (Table S2). However, a separate tissue‐class partial volume correction pipeline was not implemented; residual influence of partial volume effects on |AI| therefore cannot be fully excluded. In addition, because amyloid PET was measured only once at baseline and other biological markers, including tau, were not incorporated into the analysis, the pathophysiological basis of the observed distributional signals could not be directly determined. Finally, because the cohort was restricted to aMCI, caution is warranted in directly generalizing these findings to non‐amnestic MCI, which is a more heterogeneous group in terms of both clinical trajectory and pathological background.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, we cross‐classified aMCI by amyloid status (Aβ−/Aβ+) and long‐term clinical trajectory (stable/progressor), and demonstrated how hemispheric asymmetry (|AI|) on baseline amyloid PET was associated with prognostic heterogeneity. In Aβ− MCI, the progressor group showed increased |AI| in the lateral orbitofrontal cortex compared to the stable group, suggesting that local distributional features not readily captured by global or dichotomous Aβ– classification may be associated with long‐term clinical deterioration. By contrast, in Aβ+ MCI, the progressor group showed decreased |AI| in the lingual gyrus, suggesting that reduced asymmetry in this context may be interpreted not as a favorable finding, but as a risk signal associated with a more bilateral and homogenized distributional state. Amyloid burden (load) and spatial distribution may represent complementary dimensions, and distribution‐based markers such as |AI| may help refine long‐term prognostic stratification in MCI.
