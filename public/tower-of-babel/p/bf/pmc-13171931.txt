The Evolving Role of Genomic Technologies in Multiple Myeloma: Implications for Diagnosis, Risk Stratification and Resistance
Authors: Klára Baďurová, Veronika Kapustová, Jana Kotulová, Vítězslav Brinsa, Lenka Piherová, Václav Janoušek, Michal Pohludka, Michal Richtář, Tomáš Jelínek, Roman Hájek, Zuzana Chyra, Tereza Ševčíková
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plasma cell disorders range from indolent precursors to aggressive malignancies, with multiple myeloma as the most common malignant form. Multiple myeloma biology is highly complex and driven by genomic heterogeneity. Although recent genomic advances offer increasingly precise treatment strategies, routinely used conventional diagnostic methods sometimes fail to reflect the evolution of up-to-date multiple myeloma risk stratification models, and the implementation of genomic discoveries into clinical guidelines is hindered. We reviewed the current state and emerging roles of genomic diagnostics in multiple myeloma and related conditions, emphasizing the clinical utility. We summarized clinical presentation and diagnostic criteria, mapped the mutational landscape across disease stages, and tracked the evolution of risk stratification from clinical staging to integrated genomic models. We also highlighted recent recommendations that incorporate a quantitative assessment of cytogenetic abnormalities and tumor suppressor gene mutations. Further, we compared routinely used and state-of-the-art technologies including fluorescence in situ hybridization, targeted gene panels, whole-exome and whole-genome sequencing, long-read platforms, optical genome mapping, and analyses of circulating tumor DNA, focusing on their strengths, limitations, and complementary roles in the baseline work-up, longitudinal monitoring, and detection of resistance. Last, we discussed practical barriers to implementation and proposed a framework for proactive integration of novel approaches into clinics. In summary, comprehensive genomic profiling is becoming central to modern multiple myeloma diagnostics and a key step for precise and personalized medicine that will, if integrated into routine care, enable a more precise risk assessment, earlier detection of relapse, and improved therapy selection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40291-026-00836-7.

Introduction

Monoclonal gammopathies are a group of plasma cell disorders characterized by the clonal proliferation of plasma cells that produce a monoclonal immunoglobulin (M-protein) or its fragments. These represent a biologically and clinically heterogeneous spectrum of conditions, which range from asymptomatic and benign stages, such as monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) and smoldering multiple myeloma (SMM), to malignant and life-threatening disorders, such as multiple myeloma (MM), systemic light-chain (AL) amyloidosis, and plasma cell leukemia. Among them, MM is the most common malignant form, accounting for approximately 10% of all hematologic cancers [1]. A hallmark of MM and related disorders is profound genomic heterogeneity, which is observed both across patients and within individual clonal populations [2]. This genetic complexity plays a pivotal role in disease initiation, progression, treatment resistance, and clinical outcomes. The standard diagnostic approach for plasma cell dyscrasias has historically relied on a combination of serum and urine protein electrophoresis with immunofixation, a serum-free light chain assay, a bone marrow (BM) biopsy, and imaging to detect end-organ damage. In AL amyloidosis, a tissue biopsy with Congo red staining is essential for diagnosis, whereas confirmatory typing of amyloid fibrils is commonly performed via immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence. Mass spectrometry after laser microdissection is considered the diagnostic gold standard for precise typing but is currently available only in specialized amyloidosis centers [3]. Risk stratification models, such as those based on the levels of β2-microglobulin, albumin, and lactate dehydrogenase and cytogenetics by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), have been widely adopted.

Although these conventional methods are clinically valuable, their ability to detect subclonal architecture and high-risk molecular markers is limited, and the resolution to capture the heterogeneity driving treatment resistance and progression is lacking. In light of these limitations, the proportion of circulating tumor cells in the peripheral blood has emerged as a potential prognostic marker [4, 5] but has not yet been incorporated into clinical guidelines. Moreover, the advent of molecular genetics methods, such as next-generation sequencing (NGS), has essentially transformed our understanding of the molecular landscape of MM, as it has revealed key mutations, structural variants (SVs), and clonal dynamics that drive disease progression and resistance. However, the broader integration of molecular biomarkers into routine clinical practice remains limited. Only by incorporating these evolving insights can we hope to predict individualized risks and treatment responses with the precision required for true personalized medicine. Importantly, MM is a highly heterogeneous disease, and while a few genomic lesions are included in current staging systems, most recurrent mutations are not part of established diagnostic or prognostic frameworks and are therefore not routinely evaluated. Although research in this field has advanced dramatically, particularly with the advent of NGS, these approaches are not yet uniformly implemented in routine care, and complex genomic classification systems, beyond International Staging System (ISS) and the Second Revision of the ISS (R2-ISS) [see Sect. 3.2], often lack prospective clinical validation. Thus, we provide a comprehensive overview of current and emerging genomic diagnostic tools utilized in MM and related plasma cell disorders, highlighting their clinical relevance and benefit in disease classification, treatment resistance, and risk stratification, followed by a summary of the updated 2024 International Myeloma Society/International Myeloma Working Group recommendations. These introduce a quantitative assessment of cytogenetic abnormalities (e.g., 1q21 gain, 1p32 deletion) and, for the first time, include TP53 mutation status as a key risk factor [6]. This shift reflects a growing emphasis on genomic precision medicine and the need to integrate NGS into routine diagnostics. Importantly, FISH remains the clinical gold standard for a cytogenetic risk assessment in routine practice, whereas NGS is newly required primarily to capture TP53 mutation status and other sequence-level lesions; most additional high-resolution approaches discussed (e.g., long-read sequencing, optical genome mapping [OGM], circulating tumor DNA [ctDNA]) are currently research facing or limited to specialized centers. Here, we focus primarily on MM and its clinically relevant forms from a genomic perspective, whereas references to other monoclonal gammopathies are restricted to contexts in which they yield considerable insights into mutational landscapes, methodological approaches, or risk stratification frameworks.

Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Criteria for Monoclonal Gammopathies

Monoclonal gammopathies encompass a broad spectrum of plasma cell disorders with diverse clinical manifestations and outcomes. Their diagnosis relies on a combination of clinical features, laboratory markers, imaging, and molecular techniques, which together enable accurate disease classification and risk stratification. Recent advances in diagnostics have improved early detection, monitoring, and individualized treatment approaches across the continuum from precursor conditions to advanced disease.

Newly Diagnosed Multiple Myeloma

Newly diagnosed multiple myeloma (NDMM) is generally characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation of plasma cells within the BM, leading to excessive M-protein production and progressive multi-organ damage [7]. Newly diagnosed multiple myeloma typically develops from precursor conditions such as MGUS or SMM, progressing in a stepwise manner into symptomatic disease.

Currently, the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) defines MM by ≥ 10% clonal plasma cell infiltration in the BM or biopsy-confirmed plasmacytoma, along with myeloma-defining events, including CRAB symptoms (hypercalcemia, renal failure, anemia, and bone lesions) or biomarkers indicative of high disease burden, such as ≥ 60% plasma cell infiltration in the BM, a serum involved/uninvolved free light chain ratio ≥ 100, or more than one magnetic resonance imaging-detected focal lesion. Additionally, NDMM is commonly associated with elevated levels of M-protein in serum and/or urine. Other relevant markers include β2-microglobulin and albumin, both of which are incorporated into the staging system for prognosis [7]. Recent advances in imaging techniques, such as whole-body low-dose computed tomography and positron emission tomography/computed tomography as well as in molecular diagnostic methods such as NGS, have significantly enhanced disease detection and monitoring, enabling earlier intervention and more personalized treatment approaches.

Minimal Residual Disease

Minimal residual disease (MRD) refers to the persistence of small numbers of malignant plasma cells after therapy that remain undetectable by conventional response criteria but strongly predict long-term clinical outcomes in MM [8]. Achieving MRD negativity at a sensitivity of at least 10⁻5 has been consistently associated with improved progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) [8, 9], with higher sensitivity thresholds of 10⁻⁶ providing the strongest prognostic discrimination [8, 10]. Importantly, several studies suggest that sustained MRD negativity may partially mitigate the adverse prognostic impact of high-risk cytogenetic abnormalities [8, 11–14]. Reflecting its clinical relevance, the US Food and Drug Administration recently accepted MRD negativity as an early surrogate endpoint for accelerated drug approval in MM [15, 16]. Methodologically, MRD assessment is primarily performed either by next-generation flow cytometry, which detects residual malignant plasma cells, or by NGS-based assays targeting patient-specific immunoglobulin gene rearrangements. Both approaches demonstrate high specificity and strong prognostic value [17] and are capable of detecting residual disease at sensitivities of up to 10⁻⁶ [18].

Relapsed/Refractory Multiple Myeloma

Relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM) represents a significant clinical challenge, as most patients eventually experience disease recurrence despite initial positive responses to the selected/chosen therapy [19]. Relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma is defined by disease progression following treatment, with refractory cases failing to respond or relapsing within 60 days of the last therapy [20]. In patients with a poor prognosis (high risk), relapse within 12 months after autologous stem cell transplantation is relatively common, with studies reporting an incidence that ranged between 14.5% and approximately 20% [21]. Across the disease course, most patients experience multiple relapses, reflecting the natural history of myeloma and the limited curative potential of currently available therapies. The treatment efficacy in patients with RRMM is highly dependent on the therapeutic regimen used, as well as patient- and disease-related factors. Large phase III trials, such as POLLUX and ASPIRE, have shown that modern immunotherapy- and proteasome inhibitor (PI)-based combinations can substantially prolong remission compared with older standards; however, despite these advances, virtually all patients eventually relapse [22–24]. With the advent of novel targeted agents, including bispecific antibodies and chimeric antigen receptor T cells, relapse kinetics and resistance patterns are continuing to evolve, emphasizing the need for reliable biomarkers to guide therapy selection [19, 25]. In RRMM, resistance mechanisms arise through clonal selection and microenvironmental changes, underscoring the role of ongoing clonal evolution in shaping treatment response and the potential of genomic profiling to increase personalization [22–24, 26].

Criteria for disease assessment in RRMM mirror those applied in NDMM [27]. Progressive disease is typically indicated by a ≥ 25% increase in M-protein levels, a rising free light chain ratio, or an elevated BM plasma cell burden. Advanced imaging techniques, such as positron emission tomography/computed tomography and whole-body magnetic resonance imaging, are crucial for detecting extramedullary disease and early skeletal involvement [19]. Biochemically, RRMM is associated with fluctuating levels of β2-microglobulin, lactate dehydrogenase, and albumin, which influence risk stratification [19]. Persistent anemia, renal impairment, and hypercalcemia (CRAB criteria) often signal disease progression and necessitate prompt intervention [20].

Mutational Landscape of Precursor Conditions and Newly Diagnosed Multiple Myeloma

Genomic studies have revealed that MM and its precursors, MGUS and SMM, share common initiating events such as translocations and hyperdiploidy. However, the genomic landscape of these disorders is markedly heterogeneous and shaped by diverse classes of mutations, including SVs, copy number alterations (CNAs), and point mutations.

Structural variants are early drivers of MM development. The simplest SVs represent recurrent translocations involving the immunoglobulin heavy chain (IgH) locus, a hallmark of B-cell malignancies. These translocations dysregulate oncogenes by juxtaposing them to Ig enhancers. Primary IgH translocations are reciprocal and commonly involve chromosomal partners such as 11q13 (CCND1), 6p21 (CCND3), 4p16 (FGFR3/MMSET), 16q23 (MAF), and 20q12 (MAFB). They are primarily mediated by errors in an IgH class-switch recombination, although some may arise during somatic hypermutation [28]. More complex SVs accumulate as the disease evolves and include rearrangements involving oncogenes such as MYC (8q24), which accelerate tumor progression by increasing proliferation and blocking differentiation [29–31].

The key complex mutational processes related to MM include kataegis and chromothripsis. Kataegis denotes localized hypermutation in clustered genomic regions and frequently accompanies chromothripsis, a dramatic genomic event characterized by sudden fragmentation (“shattering”) of one or more chromosomes, followed by random and error-prone reassembly. Chromothripsis results in extensive SVs, including CNAs, deletions, inversions, and complex rearrangements, all of which can drive oncogenic transformation. One or more complex SVs are detected in 63% of patients in the CoMMpass dataset [32–34]. These key processes are often driven by APOBEC-mediated cytosine deamination. The Apolipoprotein B mRNA Editing Catalytic Polypeptide-like (APOBEC) family of enzymes, which are physiologically involved in antiviral defense, aberrantly deaminate cytosine to thymine (C>T mutations), especially at TpC dinucleotides, thus contributing to a distinct mutation signature in MM.

In addition to translocations, malignant transformation involves recurrent CNAs that further reshape the genomic landscape of MM. One of the prominent CNAs is hyperdiploid MM, characterized by trisomies of odd chromosomes. This fraction is generally considered a standard-risk group and tends to have fewer structural rearrangements but frequently results in the accumulation of mutations in proto-oncogenes [35]. In contrast, smaller CNAs, such as the gain of chromosome 1q, are associated with inferior survival, likely driven by the overrepresentation of the IL6R, ILF2, MCL-1, CKS1B, and BCL9 genes [36]. Considering deleterious CNAs, the deletion of 13q has been observed in 50% of patients and is thought to contribute to genomic instability due to the deletion of RB1 and DIS3 [37]. Similarly, del(1p) is another significant alteration observed in MM and is often linked to poor survival, likely due to the loss of the cell cycle inhibitor CDKN2C at 1p32 [38, 39]. Notably, inactivation of TP53 via mutation and/or del(17p) defines a biologically aggressive subset with resistance to conventional therapy and poor survival [30]. However, other recurrent chromosomal alterations, for example, del(16q) or del(8p), are less understood, and their potential prognostic significance remains elusive [39, 40]. Moreover, recent studies suggest that some CNAs may not directly impact survival but rather influence sensitivity to modern therapies, including B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA)-targeted therapies [41].

While SVs and CNAs shape the early genomic architecture and risk profile of MM, progression and therapeutic response are further influenced by a heterogeneous landscape of recurrent point mutations. Some represent early clonal drivers, whereas others emerge in subclones and expand under selective pressure. Although no single gene is universally mutated in MM, recurrent alterations affect key signaling pathways. Studies have revealed frequent recurrent mutations in genes related to cell signaling (KRAS, NRAS, and BRAF), RNA and protein homeostasis (DIS3 and FAM46C), chromatin remodeling (ARID1A and SETD2), and cell-cycle regulation (TP53 and CDKN2C) [30, 42]. For example, the MAPK pathway alone is disrupted in approximately 40–50% of patients with NDMM [30]. Collectively, these observations indicate that MM biology is governed by the cumulative and interactive effects of genomic lesions rather than by isolated aberrations. Genome-wide integrative analyses further demonstrate that specific constellations of chromosomal alterations define biologically coherent disease states with distinct transcriptional programs and clinical behavior. Using dimensionality reduction approaches, Terragna et al. identified a subgroup of patients with newly diagnosed MM characterized by the co-occurrence of 1q gain and 13q loss, associated with adverse clinical features and divergent CCND1- versus CCND2-driven transcriptional profiles [43].

The above overview highlights the major genomic features shaping the biology and clinical heterogeneity of symptomatic MM. Importantly, MM does not arise de novo; rather, it evolves from well-defined precursor states. The progression from MGUS to SMM and ultimately to symptomatic MM is driven by both genomic and non-genomic factors. Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance typically has a low mutational burden and a simple clonal structure; nevertheless, early initiating events such as IgH translocations [e.g., t(11;14), t(4;14)] and hyperdiploidy are usually already present. Mutations in NRAS or DIS3, along with CNAs such as 1q gain, del(1p), or del(17p), occur more frequently in progressing or symptomatic cases [44]. Notably, disease evolution is often characterized by clonal stability rather than sudden acquisition of new mutations [45]. To quantify molecular risk, the recently proposed MM-like score based on whole-genome sequencing (WGS) contrasts MM-enriched alterations (e.g., RAS and TP53 mutations, 1q gain) with those more common in precursor states (e.g., MAF/MAFB translocations) [46]. A higher score reflects a more MM-like profile and predicts an increased likelihood of progression, underscoring the potential of genome sequencing to enhance risk assessment and monitoring in MM precursor conditions.

Beyond progressing to MM, MGUS and SMM may also serve as precursors to alternative clonal evolution leading to AL amyloidosis. This condition shares many early genomic lesions with MGUS but diverges biologically, as clonal plasma cells produce amyloidogenic light chains that deposit in organs and cause systemic dysfunction [47]. The hallmark lesion is represented by the t(11;14) translocation, which occurs with a higher frequency than in MM, resembling 40–60% of recorded AL cases. This aberration confers relative resistance to proteasome inhibitors such as bortezomib but predicts sensitivity to BCL-2 inhibition with venetoclax [48]. Other adverse cytogenetic features, including gain (1q21) and del(17p), are less common than in MM, but when detected, they are associated with poor prognosis, highlighting some overlap in the pathobiology of AL amyloidosis and MM.

Clonal Evolution and Resistance in Relapsed/Refractory Multiple Myeloma

Clonal evolution drives MM progression and relapse, with patterns influenced by treatment response. Patients who achieve a complete response often relapse through branching trajectories with new driver events and genomic complexity, whereas partial responders typically have stable clonal profiles [49]. Additionally, larger longitudinal studies have demonstrated that clonal shifts occur in the majority of patients. Misund et al. reported subclonal remodeling in more than 80% of cases, and others noted relapse-associated increases in RAS mutations (60%→72%), 1q21 gains (18%→35%), and TP53 alterations (9%→18%) [2, 50, 51]. The selection of new clones at relapse is coupled with changes in core biological programs as well as with alterations in the tumor microenvironment. In a xenograft mouse model treated with antitumor drugs, Shen et al. reported that only a subset of MM clones is able to adapt to the BM microenvironment and enter the bloodstream later to colonize distant niches [52].

While these general evolutionary principles apply across therapeutic contexts, specific resistance mechanisms often reflect the mode of action of individual drug classes. Resistance to proteasome inhibitors, such as bortezomib and carfilzomib, has been associated with PSMB5 mutations, which alter the proteasome structure and reduce drug binding. Although rare, these mutations may affect responses to second-generation proteasome inhibitors, and their detection could become important for guiding decisions on salvage therapy [53]. Mutations in DNA repair genes such as BRCA1/2 also drive resistance, in this case, by increasing genomic instability [54]. Targeted inhibition of BCL-2 represents an example of context-dependent resistance shaped by clonal evolution. Multiple myeloma with t(11;14)(IGH/CCND1) remains the only molecularly defined subgroup with consistent sensitivity to the BCL-2 inhibitor venetoclax; however, not all patients derive durable clinical benefit. Recent genomic analyses have demonstrated that activating mutations in the RAS pathway are strongly associated with shortened PFS in venetoclax-treated patients. In cases lacking RAS mutations, the presence of 1q gain has likewise been linked to inferior outcomes [55]. Resistance to anti-CD38 monoclonal antibodies (e.g., daratumumab) involves tumor-intrinsic mechanisms, including RPL5 loss, MYC structural variants, APOBEC mutagenesis with associated chromothripsis, and immune evasion [56]. Furthermore, relapse after BCMA-directed bispecific antibodies often results from antigen escape, involving BCMA extracellular domain mutations, which impair receptor function and reduce circulating soluble BCMA levels [57]. Finally, immune-mediated mechanisms, such as T-cell exhaustion and regulatory T-cell expansion, have been suggested to further limit the efficacy of these therapies [58]. Beyond therapy-specific mechanisms, accumulating evidence suggests that prior treatments themselves contribute to the evolving mutational landscape. In particular, alkylating agents such as melphalan used during autologous transplantation can induce characteristic mutational signatures, increasing genomic complexity and potentially shaping resistance patterns at later relapses [59]. In summary, clonal evolution is a key driver of disease progression and treatment resistance in RRMM, with dynamic shifts in subclonal architecture and emerging genetic alterations at relapse. Resistance arises through both tumor-intrinsic mechanisms and changes in the tumor microenvironment, ultimately affecting responses to various therapeutic strategies.

Key Loci with High Clinical Relevance

Despite the genomic complexity of MM, specific alterations have proven clinically relevant for both risk stratification and therapeutic resistance. While many discoveries from large-scale sequencing studies have yet to be translated into routine clinical practice, an increasing number of genetic lesions are now recognized not only as prognostic markers but also as potential predictors of treatment sensitivity or failure. The biological and clinical impact of genomic alterations in MM frequently emerges only when lesions are considered in combination. Such combinatorial genomic events are reflected already at the level of gene expression [60] and influence both prognosis in NDMM [43] where the co-occurrence of 1q gain and 13q loss defines a biologically distinct subgroup with an adverse prognosis, and resistance to targeted therapies in the relapsed setting [55, 56]. Importantly, such biologically defined genomic constellations provide a mechanistic foundation for the evolving risk stratification frameworks in MM, supporting a shift from single-marker models toward integrated, multi-lesion, and ultimately personalized genomics-driven approaches to prognosis and treatment selection. Early models, including the Durie-Salmon system and the ISS, rely on clinical parameters such as hemoglobin, calcium, and β2-microglobulin levels [76, 77] but lack sufficient prognostic precision. This limitation led to the development of R-ISS, which incorporates key cytogenetic abnormalities, such as del(17p) or t(4;14), together with elevated lactate dehydrogenase levels. Furthermore, the mSMART model distinguishes patients on the basis of FISH-defined cytogenetic risk and guides treatment accordingly, recommending more intensive regimens for high-risk patients, including autologous stem cell transplantation and PI-based maintenance [80]. Recent refinements, such as those proposed by the IMWG/International Myeloma Society, emphasize the quantitative assessment of cytogenetic abnormalities (e.g., distinguishing CNA in 1q21 or deletions in 1p32) and, for the first time, incorporate the TP53 mutation status as a key factor, thereby requiring the implementation of novel sequencing technologies in clinical practice [81]. The adoption of this uniform schema for NDMM has already been recommended, as it enables consistent risk stratification and facilitates the harmonization of clinical trials focused on high-risk MM [82]. Importantly, recent large-scale analyses challenge the use of single cytogenetic lesions as robust predictors of adverse outcomes. Although IMWG risk stratification has traditionally focused on individual high-risk chromosomal abnormalities (HRCAs) (t(4;14), t(14;16), del(17p)), extended genetic profiling, including gain(1q), has shown additional prognostic value, albeit with inconsistent effects when assessed individually. In contrast, the co-occurrence of two or more HRCAs (double- or multi-hit MM) has emerged as a more reliable marker of poor prognosis. A federated meta-analysis involving nearly 14,000 patients demonstrated that double-hit MM was consistently associated with the worst PFS and OS across transplant-eligible and transplant-ineligible NDMM as well as RRMM, establishing it as a distinct high-risk entity with a substantial unmet clinical need [83]. A major step toward personalized risk stratification is represented by the IRMMa model (2024), which applies WGS and whole-exome sequencing (WES) to define 12 molecular subgroups with distinct prognoses and therapeutic responses [39]. Although not yet standard owing to its technical demands, IRMMa reflects the growing shift toward genomics-driven risk models. In addition to these genomic systems, other approaches based on transcriptional signatures or additional biochemical markers have been proposed and comprehensively reviewed [84].

However, not all poor outcomes can be explained by baseline genetic features. The concept of functional high-risk MM has been proposed to identify patients who lack conventional high-risk cytogenetic markers yet exhibit an early relapse (within 12 months) or refractoriness to induction therapy. Analysis of the CoMMpass cohort [85] demonstrated that these functional high-risk patients, despite the absence of t(4;14), t(14;16), biallelic TP53 inactivation, or ISS stage III disease with 1q21 gain, had significantly worse survival than both standard-risk and genomic high-risk patients. Functional high-risk patients are enriched for mutations affecting the interleukin-6/Janus kinase/STAT3 pathway and present gene expression profiles associated with aberrant mitosis, impaired DNA damage response, and a characteristic mutational signature.

Current and Emerging Genomic Technologies in Myeloma Diagnosis

Given the molecular complexity and the dynamic evolution of MM, comprehensive genomic characterization is now essential in both research and clinical settings. The broad spectrum of SVs, CNAs, and recurrent point mutations, often arising in subclones under selective pressure, requires high-resolution techniques to capture the full range of genomic events. This section provides an overview of established and emerging genomic technologies, highlighting their applications, strengths, and limitations in the context of MM. An overview of these approaches is illustrated in Fig. 3. The implementation of genomic technologies in MM critically depends on the quality and composition of the starting material. Sequencing-based approaches generally require enrichment of CD138⁺ plasma cells to ensure sufficient tumor purity, typically achieved by magnetic-activated cell sorting or with fluorescence-activated cell sorting. Insufficient plasma cell purity may compromise assay sensitivity and variant detection and therefore represents a potential barrier to routine clinical implementation of sequencing-based approaches. In contrast, FISH is more flexible with respect to input material and can be performed on enriched as well as unselected BM samples [89, 90]. Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization

The FISH technique remains a cornerstone of cytogenetic diagnostics in MM, allowing direct detection of key chromosomal abnormalities within intact cell nuclei. Fluorescence in situ hybridization uses fluorescently labeled DNA probes, typically 100–200 kb in length, that hybridize to specific genomic regions. This method is particularly well suited for detecting translocations and copy number changes, and, when centromeric probes are used, ploidy status. While interpretation thresholds vary, FISH results are usually classified as positive when they are present in more than 10% and 20% of cells for structural rearrangements and copy number changes, respectively [91].

The in situ nature of FISH offers an advantage in MM, as subclonal heterogeneity and complex spatial architecture complicate genomic analysis. Fluorescence in situ hybridization can capture distinct subpopulations and untangle their specific rearrangements. Moreover, it does not require viable cells, making it robust across various sample types (e.g., paraffin-embedded tissue or touch imprints) [92]. Several limitations, however, constrain its application. First and foremost, comprehensive detection of the full spectrum of myeloma-associated abnormalities would require a large panel of probes, which is impractical in routine diagnostics. Second, the method is labor intensive and highly dependent on the quality of the sample, particularly the proportion of plasma cells in BM aspirates. The R2-ISS study highlighted this issue, reporting that a full FISH panel was available in only ≈ 30% of patients [79]. Finally, FISH cannot detect unknown or complex rearrangements, has insufficient resolution for small deletions or cryptic events, and lacks functional context. Overall, these limitations underscore the growing importance of complementary genomic approaches.

Whole-Exome and Whole-Genome Sequencing in Clinical Use

Whole-exome sequencing and WGS provide critical insights into the complex genomic landscape of MM [44, 69, 93–97]. Whole-exome sequencing targets protein-coding regions, detecting somatic single nucleotide variants (SNVs), small insertions and deletions, and CNAs that directly affect oncogenes, tumor suppressors, and genes involved in DNA repair and cell-cycle regulation [34, 98]. In contrast, WGS captures the entire genome, revealing non-coding regulatory mutations, SVs (such as translocations, inversions, and large indels), complex rearrangements, and chromothripsis events [32, 72].

Despite their power, both WES and WGS also have some essential limitations. Generally, standard short-read sequencing technologies may fail to accurately reconstruct highly complex karyotypes, especially in cancers such as MM, where tumor cells frequently display variable ploidy levels or harbor complex structural rearrangements involving multiple chromosomes [34]. Moreover, complex or cryptic translocations may be misinterpreted as focal copy number changes or may remain undetected because of mapping ambiguities in repetitive or poorly annotated regions [99].

Targeted Gene Panels

Targeted gene panels are widely used in clinical diagnostics to detect mutations in predefined sets of genes associated with specific diseases, including cancer. Compared with WES or WGS, their key advantages include high sensitivity, relatively low cost, and a fast turn-around time. These panels provide deep coverage, enabling reliable detection of SNVs, small insertions/deletions, and selected CNAs. In the context of tumor evolution, deep coverage is particularly beneficial for detecting aggressive subclonal populations [69]. Modern targeted sequencing panels used in MM are typically comprehensive in design, integrating the detection of SNVs (including TP53 mutations), CNAs, and SVs. An overview of published panels in MM is provided in Table 3. Panel performance can vary depending on the genomic locus of interest. For example, some designs offer limited resolution for regions such as MYC (e.g., 160-kb capture), and not all panels include direct cross-validation via orthogonal methods such as FISH or WGS [40]. The recently developed targeted panel aims to balance focused genomic coverage suitable for routine diagnostics with the inclusion of a broad set of clinically relevant loci [108]. The dynamic nature of MM genomics requires flexible and updatable panel content to keep pace with emerging biomarkers, drug targets, and risk classifiers. This is particularly important given the high cost of advanced therapeutics and the growing emphasis on personalized treatment strategies on the basis of molecular features. Another type of available panel is the RNA-based panel. This panel complements DNA-based panels by detecting gene fusions, aberrant splicing, and expression signatures that may not be evident at the DNA level. It is especially useful in hematologic malignancies, in which the fused genes drive oncogenesis [109]. With respect to the MM, RNA panels were used only in combination, that is, as DNA-RNA panels. These methods thus provide comprehensive molecular profiling in a single assay, identifying SNVs, CNAs, SVs, and fusion transcripts simultaneously [107].

Long-Read Sequencing

The long-read sequencing technologies provided by Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) and Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) are increasingly valuable tools for resolving complex genomes. Their ability to generate reads from ≈ 10 kb up to over 100 kb enables superior detection of SVs, phasing of mutations, and resolution of repetitive or rearranged regions, which are hallmarks of MM genomic instability. PacBio’s HiFi sequencing achieves high accuracy (> 99%), whereas ONT’s technologies have improved base calling to exceed 90% with recent updates [110, 111]. Subsequently, the DNA input requirements continue to decrease with these tools; ONT can generate data from as little as a few nanograms of DNA, and PacBio recently introduced low-input protocols to accommodate ≈ 100–500 ng, broadening the applicability from high-input to low-input clinical samples s (Table 1 of the Electronic Supplementary Material).

In MM, long-read sequencing has revealed clinically relevant findings. Research-oriented integrative approaches of PacBio’s HiFi sequencing improve the detection of somatic SVs and allow parallel epigenetic profiling via a methylation analysis [112]. Recent data from multiomics integration of long-read HiFi sequencing, short-read WGS, and Micro-C have revealed that SVs affecting compact heterochromatin, particularly centromeric and pericentromeric regions, are common in MM and can disrupt gene regulation through chromatin remodeling and methylation changes [113]. These rearrangements, which were previously undetectable with conventional genome assemblies, suggest a driving role in MM genome instability. In parallel, translational long-read strategies aimed at routine clinical application have begun to emerge. DNA sequencing using ONT with adaptive sampling technology (real-time targeted sequencing) showed, in a preliminary study, that a single flexible assay can enable detection of diverse classes of genomic alterations at clinically relevant loci in MM [114]. ONT-based transcriptomics has identified differentially expressed circular RNAs, including circZFAND6 and circLRCH3, distinguishing smoldering MM from symptomatic disease [95], while circNFATC3 has been associated with both disease progression and prognosis in MM [115].

ONT long-read sequencing provides an additional layer of information by enabling direct detection of DNA methylation from native DNA [116–118], potentially reducing reliance on conversion-based workflows. Collectively, third-generation sequencing platforms provide an integrated view of the MM genome and epigenome, supporting improved disease stratification and biomarker discovery [96].

Optical Genome Mapping

Optical genome mapping is an emerging technology that enables high-resolution analysis of ultra-long DNA molecules, typically exceeding 100 kb. It labels specific sequence motifs with fluorescent markers and linearizes DNA within nanochannel arrays, allowing direct visualization and mapping of large-scale SVs, including translocations, inversions, duplications, insertions, and deletions. These events are often difficult to resolve with traditional short-read sequencing or cytogenetic methods such as karyotyping and FISH [119].

In MM, OGM increased the prognostic yield by ≈ 30% compared with that of FISH alone [120]. Moreover, it enables the resolution of complex rearrangements such as chromoanagenesis (catastrophic genomic events resulting in extensive chromosomal rearrangements occurring in a single step, leading to complex structural alterations within affected chromosomes) and provides insights into aberrations within chromosome 1, which are poorly characterized by conventional cytogenetics [120, 121]. Furthermore, recent multicenter validation studies have demonstrated ~100% concordance of OGM with conventional cytogenetic methods. These studies have also shown that OGM identified additional pathogenic or likely pathogenic aberrations in a significant proportion of cases, supporting its potential role as a first-tier cytogenomic test in hematologic malignancies [122].

A complementary framework for clinical implementation emphasized OGM’s utility not only in a diagnostic work-up but also in risk stratification and longitudinal monitoring, particularly in plasma cell neoplasms such as MM [123]. In some cases, the integration of OGM with NGS has proven to be a powerful, clinically relevant workflow for genetic subtyping and risk stratification in MM [120, 124, 125].

Cell-Free DNA and Circulating Tumor DNA

Although not a method per se, cell-free DNA (cfDNA) and ctDNA enable the application of a wide range of sequencing and molecular techniques to monitor tumor burden, clonal evolution, and treatment resistance in real time. As a non-invasive tool, ctDNA analysis captures the complex and dynamic genomic landscape of MM, offering practical utility in both NDMM and RRMM settings. Circulating tumor DNA reflects the spatial heterogeneity of MM and allows for the detection of somatic mutations, CNAs, structural abnormalities, and immunoglobulin gene rearrangements with high concordance to BM-derived samples, especially when the tumor fraction exceeds 5% [126]. The ability to detect clinically relevant mutations in MAPK pathway genes (e.g., NRAS, KRAS, and BRAF) support its value in mutation screening and risk stratification [127]. In comparative analyses, cfDNA-based WES detected the majority of somatic SNVs and CNAs present in paired tumor biopsies, supporting its potential to approximate BM-derived genomic profiling in a minimally invasive manner [93]. Notably, cfDNA may also reveal additional alterations not detected in a single-site BM sample, likely reflecting spatial heterogeneity of MM and the sampling bias inherent to BM aspirates. For example, gains involving CDK6 (7q21.2) or FAM46C (1p12) were observed exclusively in cfDNA in one case, while most alterations were shared across sources [93]. In relation to patient survival, high levels of ctDNA/cfDNA at the time of diagnosis have been independently associated with poorer PFS and OS, even beyond standard risk factors and clinical staging systems [128, 129, 132]. In RRMM, persistence of tumor-positive cfDNA during therapy strongly predicts short PFS and may identify poor responders earlier than conventional response criteria [130]. In the context of MRD, cfDNA-based approaches remain less sensitive than BM-based methods owing to very low tumors; however, when tumor-derived alterations are detected, they provide high specificity and represent a minimally invasive option for longitudinal monitoring in patients unable to undergo repeated BM biopsies [131].

Beyond genetic alterations, cfDNA also carries epigenetic information. In the context of MM, CpG methylation changes detected in cfDNA may complement mutational profiling and provide additional layers of biological and clinical information, particularly in cases with a low tumor fraction or spatially heterogeneous disease [133]. Overall, cfDNA and ctDNA enable longitudinal, minimally invasive monitoring of clonal evolution and treatment resistance and hold growing potential for integration into routine MM diagnostics, especially in high-risk or frail patients.

Discussion and Conclusions

The genetics of MM represents an exceptionally complex and dynamic field. Genomic research in MM has advanced substantially since the early 2000s, gaining significant momentum with the advent of NGS technologies, particularly Illumina-based platforms. Today, genomic analysis is integral to MM diagnostics and prognostics, providing insights into disease biology, risk stratification, and therapeutic response [2, 69]. Despite this, the implementation of genomics-obtained knowledge and discoveries in clinical guidelines remains slow and incomplete. Several obstacles contribute to this essential gap, including the high costs associated with advanced genomic technologies, the lack of standardized protocols for integrating genomic findings into clinical decision making, and the challenges associated with interpreting complex genomic data in a clinical context [39, 134]. All these factors hinder the broader adoption of genomics-guided diagnostics in MM, despite the promise of more tailored and precise treatment strategies.

Therefore, cytogenetics, including the FISH technique, remains the gold standard in clinical practice. However, it can only partially reflect the depth of information uncovered through comprehensive genomic research. Fluorescence in situ hybridization is highly valuable for detecting hallmark MM-related lesions such as t(4;14), t(11;14), gain(1q), and del(17p), with the strength of single-cell resolution allowing detection of major subclones. However, its probe-based design and limited throughput prevent it from obtaining broader and deeper genomic information [135, 136]. According to the European Myeloma Network recommendations, NGS should be gradually incorporated as a complementary method to FISH in MM diagnostics. NGS enables the detection of a broader range of genetic alterations beyond current risk scores and should be offered at least to fit patients eligible for modern triplet or quadruplet regimens. Furthermore, NGS testing is advised to be repeated at relapse to identify emerging resistance-associated mutations and predictive biomarkers relevant for targeted therapies [113]. Therefore, novel therapies are driving the emergence of new resistance-associated variants, highlighting the role of key mutations, as demonstrated through studies of the biology of RRMM [57, 70, 98]. Aside from that, the biology of plasma cells and the intrinsic complexity of their mutational processes, such as kataegis, chromothripsis, and APOBEC activity, demand a detailed interpretation and clinical validation [32].

Targeted gene panels are used with increasing frequency, providing cost-efficient detection of recurrent mutations such as those in KRAS, NRAS, TP53, and BRAF, with sufficient depth to study subclonality [34]. A relative limitation of panels is their static design, which reduces their adaptability to the rapidly evolving mutational spectrum. Genomic profiling through a re-biopsy or ctDNA analysis has the potential to improve tailoring of treatment strategies in RRMM [126, 128, 130]. Whole-exome sequencing and WGS add further resolution by capturing SNVs, CNAs, and SVs, with WGS enabling full-genome annotation beyond coding regions ([39]; [2, 69, 98]). Although cost and DNA input requirements remain the main limiting factors, declining sequencing prices and expanding bioinformatic pipelines are making them increasingly accessible. Optical genome mapping is another emerging approach that is particularly powerful for detecting complex rearrangements such as MYC translocations and other cryptic SVs, thereby revealing recurrent alterations with potential functional and prognostic impacts beyond classical cytogenetics [99, 119]. Hence, modern technologies should be actively incorporated to deconvolute the genomic landscape into more interpretable units, thereby enabling improved insight into the functional significance of individual loci [30]. Standardizing all the above-mentioned genomic approaches, developing cost-effective solutions, and providing training for clinicians in interpreting genomic data are essential steps in ensuring that these technologies are employed routinely.

In summary, comprehensive genomic profiling is not merely an adjunct but also a cornerstone of modern MM diagnostics. The pronounced heterogeneity of myeloma demands delicate treatment strategies supported by molecular features and, owing to its nature as a hematological malignancy, necessitates systemic therapy. Integration of available genomic methods should therefore be proactive, adaptable, and forward looking, linking specific genomic biomarkers with appropriate targeted drugs, leveraging whole-genome methods to ensure robustness, allowing for future reinterpretation, and supporting the realization of personalized treatment guided by evolving molecular landscapes. Finally, experts in the field should continue to strive to be open to and actively integrate newly emerging cutting-edge techniques, as these techniques can provide a high ground in fighting complex diseases such as MM.
