Mothers Matter Most: Maternal, but Not Paternal, Age and Inbreeding Affect Nestling Telomere Length in a Wild Passerine
Authors: Jennifer Evans, Justin Eastwood, Niki Teunissen, Simon Verhulst, Alexander Mikheyev, Anne Peters
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEarly‐life telomere length can be an integrated and accessible biomarker of lifetime fitness. Telomere length can result from complex interactions between heritable genetic variation, inherited gametic variation and accumulated environmental effects acting on individuals and parents. Consequently, to understand early‐life telomere length and its fitness consequences, both offspring traits and intergenerational effects resulting from parental traits need to be studied simultaneously. This is, particularly, true for inbreeding as poor performance or impaired telomere maintenance in offspring as well as poor parental reproductive effort or changes in gametic telomeres can reduce telomere length of offspring. Here, we explore the effects of individual inbreeding, parental inbreeding and parental age in a large dataset (n = 679) of nestling telomere length in a long‐term studied wild population of purple‐crowned fairy‐wrens ( Malurus coronatus coronatus ) with multigenerational ecological and environmental data. We find no effect of nestling inbreeding on telomere length, even in adverse (hot and dry) climatic conditions. Offspring telomere length was not associated with paternal inbreeding or age (neither within‐ nor between‐fathers). However, nestling telomere length declined with maternal inbreeding and with increasing maternal age. Combined, these results do not support inherited gametic effects but rather suggest that effects of inbreeding and ageing on the mother's phenotypic state may affect pre‐ and/or post‐natal reproductive investment. Offspring from older and more inbred mothers, with shorter telomeres, will suffer reduced lifetime fitness. Moreover, inheritance of shorter telomeres could lead to population‐level changes in performance.

Introduction

Determining how early‐life environment impacts variability in lifetime fitness has been a major challenge among ecologists and conservationists alike (Eyck et al. 2019; Lindström 1999; Monaghan 2008). Early‐life telomere length is a complex trait that can function as an integrated biomarker of fitness prospects across taxa (Wilbourn et al. 2018). Telomeres are repeated sequences of non‐coding DNA that form nucleoprotein complexes capping the ends of eukaryotic chromosomes, important in maintaining chromosome integrity and protection (Aubert and Lansdorp 2008; Blackburn 1991; Shay and Wright 2019). Telomeres shorten with cell replication and, once shortening reaches a critical stage, cells no longer divide (replicative senescence) (Aubert and Lansdorp 2008; van Deursen 2014). As such, telomere length at any age can be an integrative biomarker for individual fitness prospects and health; relatively shorter telomeres have been shown to have negative associations with survival and diseases in humans (Blackburn et al. 2015; Boonekamp et al. 2013) as well as survival in model (Anchelin et al. 2013; Carneiro et al. 2016; Rudolph et al. 1999) and non‐model vertebrates (Eastwood et al. 2023; Wilbourn et al. 2018).

Early‐life telomere length is a product of complex interactions between heritable genetic variation, inherited telomere length variation from gametes, which themselves are the product of genetic and environmental effects and accumulated environmental effects post‐conception, including non‐gametic parental effects (Bauch et al. 2019). Estimates of telomere length heritability vary highly across studies and taxa but are generally moderate and independent of age (Chik et al. 2022). At conception, zygotic telomere length is determined by gametic telomeres (Blackburn 1991). After zygote formation, offspring telomere length and attrition will be affected by exposure to stressors during early development (Xiong et al. 2025), both pre‐ and post‐natal parental reproductive effort (e.g., pre‐natal effort from mothers such as egg size and incubation or post‐natal parental care such as provisioning) (Asghar et al. 2015; Criscuolo et al. 2017) and individual phenotypic and (epi‐)genetic components (Jeon et al. 2025; Pepke et al. 2022). Reaching an understanding of early‐life telomere length, therefore, requires considering how telomere length is shaped by both parental and individual traits and early‐life conditions.

Given that parental phenotype—via effects on parental care—can impact early‐life telomere length, reproductive senescence of parents may lead to parental age affecting offspring telomere length. Such effects may arise via different pathways in mothers and fathers (Eisenberg and Kuzawa 2018; Vlasova et al. 2026). As males produce gametes continuously across life, gametic telomere length can vary strongly with age, depending on the level of activity of telomerase (Eisenberg and Kuzawa 2018); in contrast, female gametes are formed in early development and may consequently be less susceptible to age‐related telomere shortening, though female gametes may be exposed to cumulative oxidative stress or females may ovulate eggs in order of production (reviewed in Eisenberg and Kuzawa 2018; and Heidinger and Young 2020). Paternal age at conception (PAC) effects on offspring telomere length have been documented in a number of species, though the directionality of results differs across species (Eisenberg and Kuzawa 2018). In several bird species, older sires produce offspring with shorter telomeres (Bauch et al. 2019; Bouwhuis et al. 2018; Criscuolo et al. 2017; Heidinger et al. 2016; Noguera et al. 2018; Sparks et al. 2022), whereas the effect of maternal age at conception (MAC) is absent or negligible (Bauch et al. 2019; Bennett et al. 2022; Bouwhuis et al. 2018; Sepp et al. 2022). However, relatively few species have been investigated thus far and a recent meta‐analysis showed that due to prevailing methodological limitations, publication bias and especially lack of within‐parent studies, variation across species in PAC and MAC effects on telomere length remains mostly ill‐understood (Vlasova et al. 2026).

Similarly, parental inbreeding may directly affect offspring telomere length or indirectly via reduced parental care. The mating of related individuals results in inbred offspring with identical‐by‐descent homozygous regions, which originate from a common ancestor (Charlesworth and Charlesworth 1987; Keller and Waller 2002). The expression of deleterious recessive alleles and loss of heterozygosity at overdominant loci within these homozygous regions can have significant negative fitness consequences, causing inbreeding depression (Charlesworth and Charlesworth 1987; Charlesworth and Willis 2009). Both pre‐ and post‐natal reproductive traits may experience performance declines associated with inbreeding (Bebbington et al. 2016; Bérénos et al. 2016; Brouwer et al. 2007; Sin et al. 2021). Consequently, inbred parents may indirectly produce offspring with shorter telomeres and/or offspring that experience more rapid attrition (Bebbington et al. 2016).

Inbreeding depression may also directly influence early‐life telomere length in inbred offspring. In inbred individuals, reduced cellular and metabolic function may result in diminished ability to prevent or repair telomere damage via several non‐mutually exclusive pathways. For example, inbred individuals may have higher intrinsic metabolic costs (Ketola and Kotiaho 2009) or suffer reduced competitive ability (Bebbington et al. 2016; Sharp 1984) and consequently have less energetic resources to produce antioxidant defences against telomere damage (Balaban et al. 2005). Consequently, inbreeding may shorten telomere length through detrimental effects on the phenotype. However, few studies have directly assessed the effect of inbreeding on telomere length or dynamics. In laboratory inbred lines of Peromyscus and Mus mice, telomere length was repeatedly longer than in outbred conspecifics and their genera (Manning et al. 2002). However, such comparisons likely reflect domestication effects (Pepke and Eisenberg 2022) rather than a direct effect of inbreeding. Studies assessing inbreeding depression in telomere traits in non‐model organisms have found mixed results, with no (Olsson et al. 2022; Sánchez‐Montes et al. 2020) or marginally negative effects (Bebbington et al. 2016; Pepke et al. 2022). In addition to variation in statistical power, conflicting findings may be a result of inbreeding depression in telomere traits confounded by condition‐dependence. Environmental condition‐dependence can result in inbreeding depression being exacerbated or only expressed under certain conditions (Cheptou and Donohue 2011; Fox and Reed 2011; Gooley et al. 2017; Keller et al. 2002; Springer et al. 2020). Given that exposure to environmental stressors can directly affect telomere length (Chatelain et al. 2020; Pepper et al. 2018; Westneat et al. 2023), adverse environmental conditions may increase the impact of inbreeding depression in telomere traits. For example, negative inbreeding effects on nestling telomere length in Seychelles warblers ( Acrocephalus sechellensis ) were only evident under poor early‐life conditions (Bebbington et al. 2016). Disentangling such complexity underlying early‐life telomere length is challenging as it requires high‐quality multigenerational ecological and environmental data and accurate estimates of telomere length, genomic inbreeding and repeated sampling of parents at multiple ages, which few studies can achieve.

To gain direct insight into the role of inbreeding in shaping individual early‐life telomere length, here we use long‐term data from a well‐studied wild population. We assess direct and intergenerational effects of inbreeding and parental age at conception on nestling telomere length of Endangered purple‐crowned fairy‐wrens ( Malurus coronatus coronatus ). Previous studies in the population have shown that early‐life telomere length and rate of telomere shortening in the first year predict lifespan (Eastwood et al. 2019; Sheldon et al. 2022). Additionally, hot and dry climatic conditions during the growth period result in shorter telomere length in nestlings (Eastwood et al. 2022). Finally, we recently found effects of inbreeding on lifetime fitness, but not early survival, in the population (Evans 2025). Consequently, this system provides a unique opportunity to address whether nestling telomere length is influenced by (a) direct effects of individual genomic inbreeding, either broadly or under environmental stress (hot and dry conditions during growth) and (b) intergenerational effects via parental inbreeding and age (within‐ and between‐parents). Owing to a large sample size, detailed ecological data and a population pedigree, we additionally control for methodological, individual, environmental and additive genetic sources of variation in telomere length.

Materials and Methods

Study Species and Population

Purple‐crowned fairy‐wrens are riparian habitat specialists occurring along tropical savannah waterways in north‐west Australia. As a result of ongoing habitat degradation associated with inappropriate fire regimes and agricultural grazing, the western subspecies (M. c. coronatus) is endangered (Skroblin et al. 2021). The long‐term study population of purple‐crowned fairy‐wrens is located in the Kimberley, Australia (17°51 S, 126°10 E) at Mornington Wildlife Sanctuary operated by the Australian Wildlife Conservancy on the traditional lands of the Bunuba and Kija people. Within a 15 km stretch of two waterways, all purple‐crowned fairy‐wrens have been individually colour‐banded since 2005 (Hall and Peters 2008; Hidalgo Aranzamendi et al. 2016), allowing us to monitor survival and social group composition. Social groups are composed of a dominant breeding pair and 0‐8 subordinate helpers of both sexes (Kingma et al. 2011), who occupy year‐round linear territories along stretches of suitable riparian habitat. Helpers provide multiple group benefits, including nest defence and provisioning young (Kingma et al. 2011; Teunissen et al. 2020a). The species shows high within‐pair fidelity, with ~10% of offspring sired by an extra‐pair male, primarily occurring when social partners are first‐order relatives as an inbreeding avoidance mechanism (Kingma et al. 2009, 2013). Breeding activity peaks during the wet season (November–April) but can occur throughout the year in response to rainfall (Hidalgo Aranzamendi et al. 2019). Nest attempts were followed year‐round 2006–2010 and during the Wet season between 2015 and 2022; from 2011, we additionally censused the entire population twice annually (for details see Hoppe et al. 2025). Ages of individuals in the population are known with a high degree of accuracy. Approximately 40% of individuals in the study area were banded as nestlings, between 4 and 10 days old (age estimated from feather development). For birds other than nestlings, mist nets were used to capture individuals for banding, morphological measurements, plumage assessments and blood sampling. Most of those individuals are captured as fledglings or juveniles, which can be aged based on plumage and behavioural characteristics (Roast et al. 2022). A small proportion are immigrants, which can be aged based on plumage characteristics (Roast et al. 2022) and known distributions of age at dispersal (Teunissen et al. 2025).

Inbreeding and Relatedness Estimation

When birds were first banded or subsequently captured, small blood samples were collected in a heparinised capillary tube and stored in one of three storage mediums (Longmire's, Queen's or ethanol). Extractions from blood samples were completed using a QIAmp DNA kit (Qiagen) which was automated with a QIAcube (Qiagen) instrument (full protocol described in Eastwood et al. 2018). Samples were sent to Diversity Arrays Technology (DArT Pty Ltd., Canberra, ACT, Australia) for reduced‐representation sequencing via DArTseq (Kilian et al. 2012). SNPs were called from allele count data provided by DArT with our own custom genotype calling scripts applying additional calling parameters (see Supporting Information). Further quality filtering was completed with the dartR R package and PLINK (Gruber et al. 2018; Peñalba et al. 2020; Purcell et al. 2007). In total, 1970 individuals were scored across 5848 autosomal loci, with a genotyping rate of 95.80%. This included most (89%) individuals banded within the core population over 2005–2022. Further information on genotype and quality filtering is available in the Supporting Information. This genomic dataset was used for both estimating individual inbreeding and pedigree reconstruction to generate an additive relatedness matrix for use in statistical modelling.

A genomic estimate of inbreeding, FGRM (also known as FIII and FUNI), was calculated with the full set of autosomal loci using GCTA methods implemented in PLINK (Purcell et al. 2007; Yang et al. 2011). This measure estimates the correlation between uniting gametes, lending weight to rare homozygous alleles and is commonly used as a metric for quantifying inbreeding and subsequent inbreeding depression analyses (Huisman et al. 2016; Kardos et al. 2015; Keller et al. 2011; Nietlisbach et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2011).

To quantify relatedness, we constructed the population pedigree using genomic data (n = 1970 individuals). Parentage assignment was completed using a subset of 344 autosomal loci in the sequoia R package (Huisman 2017). The subset of loci was selected from the full DArTseq dataset with additional filtering based on sequoia recommendations, implemented via PLINK (see Supporting Information for further details). The final pedigree was based on all available data sources, using genomic parentage assignments where available (88% cases) and otherwise previous microsatellite assignments and social parentage (combined 2%; Hidalgo Aranzamendi et al. 2016), with some unresolved parentage (10% cases) primarily associated with founders. Using the final pedigree, we also calculated an additive relatedness matrix with the pedigreemm R package (Vazquez et al. 2010), to include in animal models as specified below, accounting for trait variation that may be due to heritable components (Kruuk 2004; Wilson et al. 2010).

Measuring Telomere Length

To estimate telomere length of nestlings we used a relative qPCR method which has been validated for use in purple‐crowned fairy‐wrens (Eastwood et al. 2018). DNA was extracted using the method outlined above; however, nestling samples stored in Queen's lysis buffer were not assayed for telomere length due to poor repeatability (Eastwood et al. 2018). Before qPCR, DNA quantity and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop (ND‐1000), while integrity was assessed using agarose gel for a smear indicating degradation. Quality control thresholds were identical to those reported by Eastwood et al. (2018). We used an EpMotion 5075 to automatically prepare 25 μL qPCR reactions into 96‐well plates. Samples were randomly assigned across 64 plates. Each reaction included 12.5 μL of SYBR Green I (Roche), 300 nM of primer for each normalising control gene (glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase; GAPDH; GT2‐GAPDH‐forward 5′‐CCA TCA CAG CCA CAC AGA AG‐3′ and GT2‐GAPDH‐reverse 5′‐TTT TCC CAC AGC CTT AGC AG‐3′) primer (Atema et al. 2013) or 400 nM of each telomere primer (Tel1b 5′‐CGG TTT GTT TGG GTT TGG GTT TGG GTT TGG GTT TGG GTT‐3′ and Tel2b 5′‐GGC TTG CCT TAC CCT TAC CCT TAC CCT TAC CCT TAC CCT‐3′, Integrated DNA Technologies) (Criscuolo et al. 2009) and 10 ng of sample DNA (except the two‐fold serial dilution containing 40, 20, 10, 5 and 2.5 ng of DNA). Samples were randomly assigned to each qPCR plate and run in duplicate and repeated if cq values deviated by more than 0.5. The assay was performed on a LightCycler 480 (Roche) machine. The telomere qPCR conditions were as follows: 95°C for 15 min, followed by 35 cycles of 15 s at 95°C, 30 s at 56°C and 30 s at 72°C. The GAPDH qPCR conditions were 95°C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of 15 s at 95°C, 30 s at 60°C and 30 s at 72°C. Finally, a melt‐curve analysis ensured the correct product was amplified. Relative telomere length (rTL) was calculated for each well as described by Eastwood et al. (2018). To improve the precision of our telomere estimates, we corrected for potential sources of error using a linear mixed model approach (lme4 package; Bates et al. 2015). We used our complete telomere dataset, consisting of 6566 relative telomere estimates from 2819 samples of 1503 individuals. Random terms included sample ID and DNA ID to account for repeated measures for each capture. The following fixed effects are methodological variables that have previously been associated with telomere length: storage buffer (Longmire or ethanol) (Eastwood et al. 2018), number of days between sampling and DNA extraction (sample storage time) (Precioso et al. 2022), number of days between DNA extraction and telomere qPCR (DNA storage time) and qPCR plate well position (Eisenberg et al. 2015). The predicted telomere length values for each sample were exported for use in further analyses. All fixed terms were found to be statistically significant (p < 0.05), justifying the need to correct for these variables (Eastwood et al. 2026). Because sampling across all ages occurs during each field season and we have used a much larger dataset that includes a much broader demographic than nestlings alone, our estimates of storage time are independent and biological factors of interest, such as age, are unlikely to be confounded. We were unable to include plate ID in our model due to the imbalanced sampling design within plates, which in rare instances was biassed towards age demographic (e.g., nestling or adult) or towards recent sampling (more recent years were assayed together). Regardless, the variation between plates was accounted for directly in our calculation of the relative telomere estimate using a “gold sample” control repeated four times on each plate. In addition, qPCR plate has previously explained < 1% of the variation (Eastwood et al. 2018). rTL estimates were standardised before and after model adjustments to have a mean of zero and standard deviation of one (Verhulst 2020).

Telomere and GAPDH assay efficiencies are within acceptable ranges (100% ± 15%) and rTL inter‐assay and inter‐extraction repeatabilities were high, ≥ 0.85 and ≥ 0.77, respectively (Eastwood et al. 2018).

Statistical Analysis

Testing for Direct Inbreeding Depression in Nestling Telomere Length

We assessed the direct association between individual inbreeding and nestling rTL for 679 nestlings with requisite ecological data. To estimate inbreeding depression in nestling rTL, we fitted a Bayesian general linear animal model in brms (Bürkner 2017). We included several fixed and random effects based on their biological relevance to telomere length as established in earlier studies of this population (Eastwood et al. 2022), including most nestlings from that study (those with requisite information; n = 394) plus additional nestlings from later years (n = 285). Continuous fixed effects and nestling rTL were mean‐centred and scaled relative to standard deviation before analysis.

Fixed Effects: Genomic inbreeding, as calculated above, was included to estimate inbreeding depression. Variability in the number of helpers in this cooperatively breeding species is important for provisioning (both parental and helper) and predator defence and consequently may impact early‐life fitness (Kingma et al. 2010, 2011; Teunissen et al. 2020a). Here, we include the number of helpers as a three‐level factor (0, 1, ≥ 2) as instances of three or more helpers are relatively rare (within this dataset, approximately 10% of broods had three or more helpers; mean = 0.90, SD = 1.10, range = 0–5). We fit brood size (within this dataset, mean = 2.82, SD = 0.74, range = 1–4) as it may affect within‐brood resource competition (Boonekamp et al. 2014). Sex was also included to account for differences in telomere length associated with sex as previously seen in the population (Eastwood et al. 2022). Sex was established using a standard PCR protocol (Griffiths et al. 1998) and confirmed in birds that reached adulthood by plumage characteristics. We included nestling age at sampling to account for variation in telomere length due to time since hatching (within this dataset, mean = 6.08, SD = 0.95, range = 4–10). Natal territory quality was included as a metric describing the volume of Pandanus aquaticus vegetation within a territory (Roast et al. 2019), which may affect early‐life fitness (see Supporting Information for further description). As previous research has shown that adverse (hot and dry) climate conditions can negatively affect nestling telomere length in the population (Eastwood et al. 2022), we included average daily maximum air temperature (Tmax) over the nestling period and a two‐level factor describing water availability (low/high). Daily maximum temperature was interpolated using a regression of temperature records from two Australian Bureau of Meteorology weather stations due to incomplete temporal data availability (Mornington station number 002076 at the core site, and Fitzroy Crossing Aero station number 003093 approximately 93 km from core site: data available online at http://www.bom.gov.au/climate/data). The resulting daily maximum temperatures were averaged over the nestling period from hatching to 7 days (average age at banding) to estimate Tmax. Water availability was assigned based on a combination of the presence of rainfall in the nestling period as measured at the Mornington weather station, and hatch season (dry season: May–October; wet season: November–April); water availability was considered ‘high’ if either rainfall was present in the nestling period or nestlings hatched during the wet season, and water availability was considered ‘low’ for dry season nestlings without rainfall. To assess whether adverse (hot and dry) climatic conditions exacerbated or masked the relationship between inbreeding and nestling telomere length, we included a three‐way interaction of inbreeding, maximum temperature and water availability (Table 1A). Additionally, we also tested for possible sex‐bias and (social) environment buffering inbreeding depression by including two‐way interactions of inbreeding with sex, number of helpers and natal territory quality, respectively; these two‐way interactions were also not included in the final model as their effects were not significant (see Table S1 for details of the tested interactions).

Testing for Effects of Parental Age and Inbreeding

We tested for intergenerational associations between nestling telomere length and parental age and inbreeding in a reduced dataset where all required parental information was available. We fitted a Bayesian general linear model with the same fixed and random effects as our initial model of nestling rTL described above, except for the environment‐inbreeding interactions which were found to be non‐significant using the full dataset (Table 1). To test for parental effects on offspring telomere length, we included both parental genomic inbreeding estimates and parental age at conception (years). We included only individuals for which we had estimates of genomic inbreeding for full parent‐offspring trios and nestling rTL measurements. We excluded any individuals with founder parents where age at conception was unknown. For immigrant parents, we assumed parents were 1 year old when they first immigrated, based on age at dispersal in this population (Teunissen et al. 2025). The final dataset included 434 nestlings, with 126 unique dam‐sire pairs composed of 85 dams and 91 sires.

A relationship between parental age and nestling telomere length could also arise through survivorship bias (selective disappearance), where inherent differences between individuals surviving to reproduce at older ages drive an overall relationship (between‐individual differences) rather than senescent changes over an individual's lifetime (within‐individual differences). Repeat measures (i.e., offspring sampled from two or more broods) were available for most dams and sires within their lifetimes (on average, both sires and dams had 2.1 nests sampled). To disentangle within‐individual and between‐individual effects, we ran an additional model using a longitudinal within‐subject centring method (Roast et al. 2022; van de Pol and Wright 2009) for both maternal and PAC. In this model, we replaced each parental age at conception effect with two estimates, (1) the mean age at conception for each mother and father (μ, calculated at the nest‐level) and (2) ∆ age at conception (age at conception minus individual μ). Between‐individual effects (β B‐MAC and β B‐PAC) demonstrate how telomere length changes between mothers and fathers as a function of individual mean age at conception (survivorship bias), while the within‐individual effects (β W‐MAC and β W‐PAC) show the MAC and PAC effects that occur within individual lifetimes (senescence; see equation (2), van de Pol and Wright 2009). Mothers and fathers who only had one brood measured were included with μ = age at conception and ∆ = 0.

A relationship between parental inbreeding and offspring telomere length could also be explained by a relationship between parental and offspring inbreeding. We tested for and found no significant relationship between offspring and either parental inbreeding in a simple linear model (maternal F GRM β = 0.11 (0.07), p > 0.05; paternal F GRM β = 0.04 (0.07), p > 0.05; adjusted R 2 = 0.002).

Results

Individual Genomic Inbreeding Has no Effect on Nestling Telomere Length

We found no significant effect of inbreeding on rTL, either independently or in a (three‐way) interaction with two key climate variables, maximum temperature and water availability (Figure 1; Table 1A), though in both cases inbreeding had a very small insignificant negative effect (Table 1B: FGRM posterior mean = −0.05, 95% CI = −0.12 to 0.02). However, our results confirm previously demonstrated negative effects of average maximum temperature alleviated by water availability (Table 1B: Tmax posterior mean = −0.23, 95% CI = −0.40 to −0.06; Tmax interaction with Water availability (high) posterior mean = 0.25, 95% CI = 0.05–0.45). Nestling rTL was highly heritable (h 2 posterior mean = 0.48, 95% CI = 0.26–0.71).

Intergenerational Effects on Nestling Telomere Length

Maternal inbreeding had a significant negative effect on offspring rTL (maternal FGRM posterior mean = −0.22, 95% CI = −0.36 to −0.07; Table 2, Figure 2). Additionally, we found a significant negative effect of MAC, such that an increase in maternal age of 1 year was associated with a decrease in offspring rTL of approximately 0.07 (95% CI = −0.12 to −0.01). Calculating within‐ and between‐individual effects of age at conception separately showed no evidence for selective disappearance: the effect of maternal age at the population level was not different from the effect of within‐individual changes in age (senescence), as both βB‐MAC and βW‐MAC were themselves non‐significant and not significantly different from each other (Tables S2 and S3). We found no evidence for any paternal effects, via inbreeding or age, neither as a single term (Table 2, Figure 2C,D) nor when separated into within‐ and between‐individual effects (Tables S2 and S3).

Discussion

Early‐life telomere length is a valuable biomarker that predicts survival and lifetime fitness (Eastwood et al. 2019, 2023). However, studies investigating factors underlying complex variation in early‐life telomere length remain limited due to the requirement for high‐quality, multigenerational, ecological and environmental data and accurate estimates of telomere length, inbreeding and parental ages. Here, we assess direct and intergenerational effects of inbreeding, senescence and environmental conditions on nestling telomere length in a wild population of purple‐crowned fairy‐wrens. Using telomere measurements in a large long‐term dataset, we find no direct individual inbreeding effect on nestling telomere length, even when controlling for hot and dry climatic conditions known to negatively affect nestling telomere length (Eastwood et al. 2022). Contrary to expectations and previous avian studies (Bauch et al. 2019; Criscuolo et al. 2017; Heidinger et al. 2016; Noguera et al. 2018; Sparks et al. 2022), offspring telomere length was not associated with paternal traits, but was negatively associated with maternal age and inbreeding.

Nestling Telomere Length Is Not Affected by Inbreeding, Even Under Adverse Conditions

We find no convincing evidence to suggest that individual inbreeding affects nestling telomere length. While our analyses show a weak negative effect of inbreeding on nestling telomere length, this is non‐significant and does not appear to be exacerbated even under adverse climatic conditions known to negatively affect telomere length. This is counter to the few other studies that have found inbreeding depression in individual telomere length under stressful conditions: a study in house sparrows ( Passer domesticus ) found nestling telomere length was relatively shorter in inbred individuals with some possible environmental influence (Pepke et al. 2022) and a study in Seychelles warblers found evidence of inbreeding depression in adult telomere length and, under food‐limited conditions, juvenile telomere length (Bebbington et al. 2016). In contrast, several other studies also observed no impact of inbreeding on telomere traits in other wild vertebrates, such as sand lizards (Lacerta agilis) (Olsson et al. 2022) and natterjack toads ( Epidalea calamita ) (Sánchez‐Montes et al. 2020). However, studies quantifying both inbreeding and telomere traits are still rare and likely have some degree of publication bias (as occurs more broadly in inbreeding depression studies; Keller and Waller 2002).

Our finding of no direct inbreeding effects on nestling telomere length is unexpected in the context of prior studies of purple‐crowned fairy‐wrens. Inbreeding reduces lifespan in this species (Evans 2025). Given that nestling telomere length also predicts lifespan (Eastwood et al. 2019), we might expect that early somatic costs of inbreeding, reflected in shorter nestling telomere length, could explain reduced lifespan in inbred individuals. However, our results here suggest that such lifetime effects of inbreeding are not mediated by shorter nestling telomeres in inbred individuals, unlike two similar studies (Bebbington et al. 2016; Pepke et al. 2022). Our result appears robust, as we had a large sample size (n = 679); high‐quality estimates of genomic inbreeding and highly repeatable and well‐validated telomere length estimates (Eastwood et al. 2018) that are sensitive to environmental conditions (Eastwood et al. 2022). Additionally, we were able to control for parental, nest and additive genetic effects, known climatic (hot and dry conditions) as well as putative social (group size) and ecological (habitat quality) factors that could affect early‐life telomere length. One explanation for our negative result may be that telomere attrition during early development could provide a better metric in this context (Boonekamp et al. 2014) as inbred individuals may experience poor performance (Bebbington et al. 2016) or higher baseline metabolic costs (Ketola and Kotiaho 2009) limiting antioxidative defence or repair pathways important for telomere maintenance. While we were unable to assess telomere dynamics in the nestling stage in our population due to sampling limitations, it is possible that somatic costs of inbreeding are reflected in telomere attrition throughout early development. Future work assessing whether inbreeding depression affects telomere dynamics over the first year of life or beyond (Sheldon et al. 2022) may further investigate whether there are cumulative somatic costs of inbreeding underlying lifetime inbreeding depression. However, given our present sample size and detailed analyses, such effects, if they exist, are likely to be relatively subtle.

We find no evidence for condition‐dependent inbreeding effects on nestling telomere length. Although inbreeding depression is often only evident or exacerbated in challenging or adverse environmental conditions (Cheptou and Donohue 2011; Fox and Reed 2011; Keller et al. 2002), in purple‐crowned fairy‐wrens nestlings, inbreeding does not affect telomere length even under adverse (hot and dry; Eastwood et al. 2022) environmental conditions. The power of this analysis is somewhat reduced since only 16% (110/679) of nestlings sampled in this study experienced ‘dry’ conditions, where they hatched during the dry season with no rainfall within the nestling period and inbreeding is generally rare (Kingma et al. 2013). However, it should be noted that this sample size is sufficient to reaffirm previously identified (Eastwood et al. 2022) impacts of hot and dry conditions on telomere length. Additionally, there may have been undetected effects of the social environment of this cooperatively breeding species, as the presence of subordinate helpers provisioning young could alleviate resource deficits (Kingma et al. 2011; Teunissen et al. 2020a). Although we tested for and found no support for interactions between inbreeding and either the number of helpers or brood size on nestling telomere length, it is possible that more complex social effects (e.g., Teunissen et al. 2020b, 2021, 2023) could contribute to telomere attrition.

Intergenerational Maternal Effects on Offspring Telomere Length

We find no support for PAC impacting nestling telomere length in purple‐crowned fairy‐wrens, contrary to our predictions. As male gametes are produced continuously over a lifetime, and telomere length declines throughout life (Aubert and Lansdorp 2008), paternal inheritance of telomere length may vary accordingly, as was demonstrated in three small bird species, cross‐sectionally (Criscuolo et al. 2017; Noguera et al. 2018) as well as longitudinally (within‐father decline in offspring telomere length; Sparks et al. 2022). Additionally, our within‐father centring shows that survivorship bias—where only males with longer telomeres survive to older ages and pass on relatively longer telomeres to their offspring—does not drive the overall null effect of PAC we observed. Our results therefore suggest that age‐associated decline in sperm telomere length does not affect purple‐crowned fairy‐wrens. We are yet unable to test the underlying mechanisms, such as telomerase regulation, that could lead to such species differences (Bauch et al. 2019; Eisenberg and Kuzawa 2018). In addition to no impact of PAC, we find no relationship between paternal inbreeding and nestling telomere length. Paternal inbreeding effects on offspring telomere length have only been explicitly tested in one avian study to our knowledge (Bebbington et al. 2016) and were also found to be non‐significant, suggesting that paternal inbreeding does not have transgenerational impacts. We encourage studies of paternal inbreeding and offspring telomere length to provide further insight into transmissible intergenerational consequences of inbreeding via the paternal line.

We find substantially shorter offspring telomere length with older and inbred mothers. These results in regard to maternal age are contrary to the few studies in birds, which find no supporting evidence that MAC affects telomere length of offspring (Bauch et al. 2019; Bennett et al. 2022; Bouwhuis et al. 2018; Sepp et al. 2022; cf. Vlasova et al. 2026). Our within‐subject centring analysis suggests that this effect is not related to the selective disappearance of high‐performing mothers (survivorship bias), but instead reflects individual senescence, that is, as mothers age, they produce offspring with progressively shorter telomeres. This is not what we predicted, based on the fact female gametes are produced during early development. While there also are potential mechanisms for eggs ovulated in later life to experience telomere shortening (e.g., longer exposure to oxidative stress or preferential ovulation of eggs with fewer mitotic divisions in early life; reviewed in Heidinger and Young 2020) the balance of evidence in avian studies suggests that offspring telomere length does not vary with maternal age. In addition to the within‐mother decline in nestling telomere length with advancing maternal age, we find a similar decline in nestling telomere length with maternal inbreeding. This large effect of maternal inbreeding is similar to the only other avian study that explicitly tested this hypothesis (Bebbington et al. 2016), suggesting that maternal (but not paternal) inbreeding does have transmissible impacts on the next generation in these small songbirds. Associations between maternal age and inbreeding with offspring telomere length could be explained by direct inheritance of shorter telomeres and/or changes in female reproductive effort. This could include prenatal stages during egg development and reproductive resource allocation (Beamonte‐Barrientos et al. 2010; Kokko and Jennions 2003; Verhulst 2026), as well as post‐hatching behaviour and care, as both pre‐ and post‐natal reproductive traits may experience performance declines associated with ageing (reviewed in Lemaître and Gaillard 2017) or inbreeding (Bebbington et al. 2016; Bérénos et al. 2016; Brouwer et al. 2007; Sin et al. 2021). In our dataset, we cannot distinguish between these processes or early age‐related gametic effects. However, given the absence of detectable paternal effects, it appears parsimonious to suggest that female‐only aspects of reproductive effort (e.g., egg quality, incubation, nest building) decline with female age and inbreeding, rather than provisioning, which can be done by both parents and helpers (Teunissen et al. 2020a). Disentangling the mechanisms that contribute to older and more inbred mothers producing offspring with shorter telomere length in our population requires further investigation of senescence and inbreeding depression in maternal reproductive traits. Nestlings from inbred and older mothers are likely to suffer reduced lifetime fitness as a consequence of reduced nestling telomere length (Eastwood et al. 2019). As telomere length has substantial heritability, shorter telomeres from older and more inbred mothers may be inherited across a single or perhaps even multiple generations (Eisenberg et al. 2012; Marasco et al. 2025). In combination with effects of climate change and ongoing habitat fragmentation and degradation, inheritance of shorter telomeres could contribute to population‐level changes in performance and threaten population persistence (cf. Eastwood et al. 2022).
