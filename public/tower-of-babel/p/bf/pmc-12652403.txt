Impact of Effect Modification of Type 2 Inflammation and Sex on Asthma Exacerbation Rates
Authors: Trisha Agarwal, Ryan Peterson, Guillermo Jimenez, Zachary Taich, Sunita Sharma, Fernando Holguin, Meghan D. Althoff
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrior studies have identified predictors of asthma exacerbations; however, most lack integration of type 2 (T2) inflammatory markers.Research QuestionIn a large electronic health record database, what are predictors of asthma exacerbation rates and is there interaction by T2 inflammation, female sex, and obesity?Study Design and MethodsThis is a retrospective cohort study using electronic health record data of patients with asthma followed for at least 1 year in the UCHealth system. The primary outcome was asthma exacerbation rate, defined by the prescription of an oral corticosteroid burst. Predictors of interest included T2 high inflammation, defined as absolute eosinophil count (AEC) ≥ 300 cells/μL, BMI, and sex. Predictors of the numbers of exacerbation and prespecified interactions were identified with negative binomial models. A natural cubic spline was used to model the dose response between AEC and exacerbation rate.ResultsThe cohort included 70,939 patients with asthma; 52% had T2 high inflammation and 62% were female, with 70% of patients being overweight or obese. Individuals with T2 high inflammation had higher adjusted rates of exacerbation (adjusted incidence rate ratio, 1.13; 95% CI, 1.10-1.16). AEC predicted exacerbation frequency in a dose-dependent manner. There was significant effect modification by sex, with female participants with T2 high inflammation having increased exacerbation rates compared with male participants with T2 high inflammation.InterpretationThis study finds an increase in exacerbation rate among patients with T2 high inflammation with asthma and shows a dose-dependent response to AEC. To our knowledge, this is the first study to find effect modification by sex and T2 status, identifying a group of patients who could potentially benefit from T2-targeted biologic therapy to decrease their exacerbation rate.

Take-home PointsStudy Question: Our goal was to determine if type 2 inflammation, female sex, and obesity demonstrated effect modification on rate of asthma exacerbations seen in emergency departments of a large healthcare system.Results: We found that absolute eosinophil count predicted asthma exacerbation rate in a dose-dependent manner and effect modification was present by female sex and high type 2 inflammation.Interpretation: Female sex and high type 2 inflammation were associated with highest asthma exacerbation rates in a real-world cohort; future work should investigate mechanisms and associated factors driving exacerbations in this patient group.

Take-home Points

Study Question: Our goal was to determine if type 2 inflammation, female sex, and obesity demonstrated effect modification on rate of asthma exacerbations seen in emergency departments of a large healthcare system.

Results: We found that absolute eosinophil count predicted asthma exacerbation rate in a dose-dependent manner and effect modification was present by female sex and high type 2 inflammation.

Interpretation: Female sex and high type 2 inflammation were associated with highest asthma exacerbation rates in a real-world cohort; future work should investigate mechanisms and associated factors driving exacerbations in this patient group.

Uncontrolled asthma has a significant economic burden on health care and cost in the United States, with a projected 20-year cost burden of $963.5 billion.1, 2, 3 Asthma exacerbations contribute nearly 80% of the direct costs in the adult asthma population of the United States.3, 4, 5 There is also personal cost to exacerbation-prone asthma because these patients incur large individual economic burdens in the form of costs of provider visits, hospitalizations, and emergency department (ED) visits for these exacerbations.2 With > 11 million patients with asthma reporting ≥ 1 exacerbations per year, it is important to identify predictive factors and target interventions at the highest risk populations.6

Severe asthma and exacerbation prone asthma are widely recognized as heterogenous endotypes and clinical phenotypes.7 Exacerbation frequency, severity, and risk have been shown to vary between those with predominant type 2 (T2) or non-T2 inflammation. In addition, other factors (eg, smoking, medication nonadherence, psychosocial factors, comorbidities) are important risk factors for the exacerbation-prone phenotype.8 Asthma exacerbations are more common in female individuals than male, with female individuals being twice as likely to be hospitalized.7

This considerable heterogeneity in asthma exacerbations underscores the need to further differentiate asthma phenotypes in patients with their characteristics and demographic makeup to understand what factors contribute to exacerbations. Several studies have attempted to identify predictors of asthma exacerbations with specific subsets of populations such as the exacerbation-prone group along with a real-world study that looked at predictors of both exacerbations and poor asthma control.9, 10, 11, 12 One real-world study examined the prevalence of eosinophilic and noneosinophilic asthma with a gradient approach to biomarker data and demonstrated that the eosinophilic phenotype is larger than previously estimated and makes up most of the population at severe asthma centers.13 To our knowledge, the predictors of exacerbation in these phenotypes with biomarkers has not been explored. In this real-world study, we present a large cohort of patients with physician-diagnosed asthma in a single large regional health care system to identify predictors of asthma exacerbation in patients with T2 high and low asthma, and the extent to which demographic variables modify these associations.

Study Design and Methods

This is a retrospective cohort study of patient data collected from the Health Data Compass, a multiinstitutional enterprise health data warehouse at the University of Colorado that integrates patient clinical data from the electronic medical record for all patients who met inclusion criteria from both ambulatory and inpatient facilities in the UCHealth system. The dataset includes demographic data, laboratory results, prescription medications, and outpatient, inpatient, and ED encounter dates between January 2004 and June 2018. This study was approved by the Colorado Multiple institutional review board (No. 17-9246).

This data set is limited to UCHealth encounters and does not capture health care utilization at other health care facilities in the state; therefore, we chose to include only patients who were followed for least 1 year in an attempt to identify a cohort who largely received care in the UCHealth system. To identify patients with asthma, we limited the cohort to those with (1) at least 1 inpatient encounter with asthma as chief complaint, International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revision (ICD-9), or International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), codes for asthma as the billing diagnosis or encounter diagnosis (493.00-493.02, 493.10, 493.20, 493.81, 493.82, 493.90, J45.2-J45.5, J45.9); (2) at least 2 unique encounters with asthma in the chief complaint, billing diagnoses, or encounter diagnosis; or (3) at least 2 prescriptions of asthma medications (e-Table 1). Among all patients with asthma followed for at least 1 year, we also limited the data set to adults aged 18 to 99 years. Observation time was defined by the length between the earliest and latest asthma-related encounter. Asthma-related encounters included all ED, urgent care, inpatient, and clinic encounters in addition to prescription or laboratory encounters with asthma as the chief encounter or ICD-9 or ICD-10 billing or encounter codes, previously described.

Demographic data for each patient included age, sex, self-identified race and ethnicity, and clinical characteristics including height, weight, and smoking status. Laboratory values of interest were absolute eosinophil count (AEC) from peripheral blood and IgE. AECs were converted to 100 cells/μL, and the maximum AEC was collected for each patient. T2 high status was defined by any AEC ≥ 300 cells/μL. The primary outcome was number of asthma exacerbations, which was defined as the number of prescriptions for oral corticosteroid bursts, lasting 2 to 20 days. Data management was performed by one author (Z. T.).

Statistical Analysis

Negative binomial generalized linear models (GLMs) were used to model the relationships between our independent variables and the number of oral corticosteroids (nOCS). We present both unadjusted and adjusted incidence rate ratios (IRRs) using a series of GLMs. The latter models were adjusted for a set of preselected covariates available in the electronic health record, including sex, BMI category, smoking status, race, ethnicity, age, T2 status, and days followed in the cohort. BMI categories were normal/underweight (< 25 kg/m2), overweight (25-29.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥ 30 kg/m2).

Missing data were multiply imputed using predictive mean matching and multinomial logistic regression models via chained equations (5 complete imputed datasets were produced, and statistics resulting from the analyses previously described were aggregated across each method). We imputed only missing data pertinent to our models: this included sex (< 1% ), BMI (4% ), race (2%), ethnicity (3%), smoking status (< 1%), and AEC (12%) (e-Table 2). Age, nOCS, number of ED/inpatient visits, and days on study were included in these imputation models but were not missing for any observations. Descriptive statistics are presented preimputation.

To determine whether sex interacts with obesity and T2 status, we used GLMs with interaction terms, determining the best model based on Akaike information criterion on the complete cases, and subsequently refitting on the multiply-imputed data for inferences. As a secondary analysis, we replaced T2 status in models by each patient’s maximum AEC, using a natural cubic spline with 4 df to determine the extent to which a dose-response relationship exists between eosinophil count and the adjusted rate of oral corticosteroid (OCS) bursts. AEC was not logged before analysis because splines do not require the variable to be normally (or lognormally) distributed. This spline is visualized in a complete case analysis and inference is performed using multiparameter inference after multiple imputation. R version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and the mice package version 3.15.014 were used for analysis and multiple imputation.

There were 70,939 patients who met inclusion criteria. Of the 62,217 with at least 1 AEC, 30,179 (48.5%) were T2 low and 32,038 (51.5%) were T2 high. Baseline characteristics among T2 high male participants vs T2 high female participants are shown in e-Table 3. T2 high female participants were more likely to be obese (48%) compared with T2 high male participants (39%); however, the median BMI of the groups was similar (29.4; interquartile range [IQR], 11.1) for T2 high female participants and 28.1 (IQR, 8.5) for T2 high male participants. T2 high male participants were more likely have previously smoked (48%) compared with T2 high female participants (38%). These trends were similar when comparing characteristics of male and female T2 low participants (e-Table 4), with 42% of T2 low female participants in the obese category compared with 35% T2 low male participants. Similar comparisons were made between T2 high participants with obesity vs T2 high participants without obesity (e-Table 5) and T2 low participants with obesity vs T2 high participants without obesity (e-Table 6). In both T2 high and low groups, participants with obesity were more likely be female, with 65% in the T2 high obese group and 71% in the T2 low obese group. Those of normal or underweight BMI were more likely to have active tobacco use than the other weight categories in both T2 high and T2 low groups.

Predictors of Asthma Exacerbations

The proportion of participants with ≥ 1 exacerbations in the overall cohort was 35%, with a median number of days observed of 1,350 (IQR, 941) as described in Table 1. In the overall cohort, 8% had ≥ 3 exacerbations with a median number of ED or inpatient visits of 1.0 (IQR, 2.0). Unadjusted and adjusted IRR from the negative binomial models are reported in Table 2. Variables included in the adjusted models were T2 status, BMI category, smoking status, race, ethnicity, sex, age, and days followed in the cohort. In the adjusted models, T2 high, overweight, obese, active tobacco use, female sex, Hispanic ethnicity, and duration of follow-up were associated with an increased incidence of asthma exacerbations, whereas increasing age by decade was associated with decreased incidence exacerbations. Effect Modification by Female Sex

In the interaction model, we found a sex-specific effect of T2 high status, with female sex having a significantly more pronounced adjusted effect of T2 inflammation (adjusted IRR for T2 high among female sex, 1.17; 95% CI, 1.13-1.21; adjusted IRR for T2 high among male sex, 1.05; 95% CI, 1.00-1.11; P for effect modification = .001) (Fig 2, Table 2). There was also significant interaction between sex and BMI category (P = .015) in the adjusted models (e-Fig 1). Similar results are seen when comparing sex-stratified number of exacerbations by maximum AEC as a continuous variable (Fig 3A) and when comparing female individuals with obesity to male individuals without obesity(Fig 3B). These results taken together suggest that patients with T2 high inflammation, female sex, and obesity had more exacerbations than the other subgroups. In the total population, T2 high female patients with obesity had a mean of 0.90 (95% CI, 0.87-0.94) exacerbations compared with a mean of 0.64 (95% CI, 0.60-0.68) exacerbations among T2 high male patients with obesity in the cohort. In the T2 low group, obese female patients had a mean of 0.77 (95% CI, 0.74-0.80) exacerbations compared with 0.59 (95% CI, 0.55-0.63) exacerbations among obese male patients. Discussion

In this large retrospective cohort of a single health care system, we found differences in predictors of exacerbation by T2 status and novel effect modification by sex. T2 high female patients were overall more prone to exacerbations than T2 high male patients, an interaction that was not present in the T2 low group. These results confirm other real-world data of increased rates of exacerbations in women, but finds T2 high status as an important synergistic predictor.15 Understanding the impact of T2 inflammation on exacerbation risk and related sex-specific differences is important in understanding how to target and prevent asthma exacerbations in a high-risk phenotype. Those with T2 high and exacerbation prone asthma could benefit from highly effective biologic therapies shown to decrease exacerbations in this phenotype and should be further explored.16

Additionally, we are able to show increasing predicted number of exacerbations with a rise in AEC plateauing around 1,000 cells/μL. The accepted definition of what AEC cutoff should be used in defining high T2 inflammation varies across studies. One of the largest studies on this to date evaluated risk of exacerbation among 2,392 patients with asthma in the Kaiser Permanente system. This study found an increased risk of exacerbations among those with eosinophils > 400 cells/μL, but results were not statistically significant in lower strata of eosinophils.11 Our findings provide valuable insight into the increased risk of exacerbations even among those with low levels of eosinophilic inflammation.

Studies on T2 inflammation and peripheral eosinophila to date have been in severe asthma populations with relatively small sample sizes, and have used markers of T2 inflammation that are not readily available in most health care settings including fraction of exhaled nitric oxide and periostin.17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22 Our study uses all-comers with asthma, which reflects the full spectrum of asthma severities, has ample statistical power, and demonstrates the utility of AEC in understanding exacerbation risk, which is readily available in all clinical settings. The mechanism driving this association likely reflects untreated, ongoing T2-driven airway inflammation and promotion of mucous plugging.23,24 Data suggest that small airways dysfunction, as measured by airway oscillometry, is associated with elevated AEC and exacerbations.25

Female patients represented 60% of the total population, with a higher proportion of female individuals in the T2 low compared with T2 high group. There are well-documented asthma phenotypes that predominantly affect female individuals with T2 high asthma and obesity-associated T2 low asthma, both of which show increased risks of exacerbations,26 which is concordant with the overall cohort. Among those with T2 high inflammation, female patients had significantly higher exacerbation rates than T2 high male patients; however, this sex-specific difference was not seen in the T2 low group. To our knowledge, this finding has not been reported elsewhere. The mechanism driving this relationship is not clear; however, there are data suggesting that sex hormones influence eosinophil function. Eosinophils are known to express estrogen receptors, which has been shown to increase endothelial adhesion and promote degranulation.27, 28, 29 Conversely, testosterone has been shown to decrease endothelial adhesion of eosinophils, which has been reversed in castrated mice, suggesting that testosterone may attenuate eosinophilic inflammation.30,31 A proteomics study found that eosinophil peroxidase was higher in female mice and human bronchoalveolar lavage fluid compared with male samples.31

This study has important limitations that must be considered. First, this is a convenience sample of users of the UCHealth health care system. This may not reflect the entirety of care a patient receives, may underestimate exacerbations treated at outside facilities, and we cannot rule out possible selection bias of this sample. The UCHealth catchment area covers covers nearly 20% of all people in state with clinics and hospitals throughout the state,32 therefore representing a large proportion of the care received by patients with asthma in state. We are unable to control for ability to access asthma care, both with primary care and specialty providers, and for socioeconomic and environmental factors that are well known to influence asthma control. Additional studies with more granular information on these variables are needed to address this gap. Although this may represent important variables impacting asthma exacerbation rates, it is unlikely that variables like access to care, comorbid conditions, and neighborhood environment significantly impact whether a patient has T2-driven inflammation, which is necessary to meet the criteria for a confounding variable. In addition, we lack information regarding the age of asthma onset, which plays an important role in response to biologic therapies and the presence of nasal polyposis, and therefore were unable to explore these further in our interaction model.

We assume that a prescription for OCSs in a patient with asthma is a proxy for an exacerbation, which has been used in prior studies.11 There are likely a small proportion of patients that received OCSs for an alternative diagnosis (eg, atopic dermatitis, acute sinusitis) or to have in case of future exacerbation; however, based on our clinical expertise, we expect this number to be relatively small compared with the total number of OCS prescriptions. Finally, we use an AEC cutoff of 300 cells/μL for T2 high status; however, there is not a universally accepted AEC threshold. Commercial insurers often use an AEC ≥ 300 cells/μL to qualify for anti-IL-5 biologic therapy; however, the European Respiratory Society and American Thoracic Society severe asthma guidelines33 recommend using an AEC threshold as low as 150 cells/μL to determine appropriateness for anti-IL-5 therapy. Additionally, the T2 low phenotype has been shown to be unstable over time and measurement of T2 markers during exacerbation has demonstrated misclassification of patients into this group, promoting others to use AEC cutoffs of ≤ 200 cells/μL.34,35 Using a higher threshold for AEC therefore potentially misclassifies a small number of participants; however, our results were robust to sensitivity analyses with a lower AEC threshold of 200 cells/μL. We are also unable to use IgE and the fraction of exhaled nitric oxide in the assessment of T2 status because these data were not available in 96% of the cohort; therefore, there may be participants misclassified as T2 low due to incomplete phenotyping data available in this real-world cohort. Finally, because we performed model selection and multiple hypothesis tests, our P values should be considered nominal and exploratory rather than confirmatory.

The cohort used data from 2004 to 2018, which limits our ability to determine the impact of asthma biologic therapies on exacerbation rates in these groups. Although omalizumab was approved for asthma by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2003, the anti-IL-5/5R medications (mepolizumab, reslizumab, and benralizumab) were FDA approved for asthma between 2015 and 2017, and dupilumab and tezepelumab were FDA approved for asthma after the collection of this cohort. Prescribing patterns and effectiveness of these medications in T2 high female patients and those with obesity should be studied as the use and availability of these medications becomes more widespread.

Despite these limitations, this study provides novel insights into a previously unrecognized high-risk group for asthma exacerbations in T2 high female patients, with some models suggesting further increased risk among T2 high female patients with obesity. Future work is needed to validate these findings in prospective studies with more granular data on controller medication use, access to primary and specialty care, and neighborhood environment measures. The mechanisms driving these differences is not well understood. Although there are data suggesting hormonal differences in eosinophil function, additional work is needed to elucidate drivers of these exacerbation differences. Finally, this group represents an important phenotype to target for biologic therapy; however, there are few existing studies on understanding sex-specific and BMI-specific differences in response to these medications.

Interpretation

Overall, the findings of this real-world study demonstrate that T2 high female patients with asthma are more prone to exacerbations. We confirm findings from smaller studies that demonstrate that AEC correlates with predicted number of exacerbations in a dose-dependent manner. To our knowledge, this is the largest study to date to identify predictors of asthma exacerbations using AEC as a marker of T2 inflammation and the first to show a sex-T2 inflammation interaction. These novel insights point toward potential gaps in health care delivery because female patients with obesity and T2 inflammation had high exacerbation rates and would likely benefit from referral to subspecialty care and consideration for biologic therapy. More work is needed to better understand the drivers of this potential care gap.
