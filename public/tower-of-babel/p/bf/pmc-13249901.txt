Jorumycidine, a hexacyclic bis-tetrahydroisoquinoline alkaloid from marine symbiosis reveals new biosynthetic logic for anticancer design
Authors: Genoveffa Nuzzo, Giulia Quaini, Federica Albiani, Carmela Gallo, Simone Landi, Dalila Carbone, Gennaro Pescitelli, Daniela Castiglia, Emiliano Manzo, Giuliana d’Ippolito, Angelo Fontana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMarine opisthobranch mollusks are a valuable source of structurally diverse bioactive metabolites arising from de novo biosynthesis or dietary origins. Jorumycidine (4) is a novel bis-tetrahydroisoquinoline (bis-THIQ) alkaloid with an unprecedented hexacyclic skeleton, isolated together with jorumycin (2), renieramycin E (3), and new 21-deoxy analogues (5, 6) from the nudibranch Jorunna funebris and its dietary sponge Haliclona sp. The structure of jorumycidine, featuring a unique oxazolidine ring, was elucidated by spectroscopic, spectrometric, and chiroptical analyses. LC–MS/MS diagnostic fragmentation filtering (DFF), combined with re-annotation of the ren biosynthetic gene cluster recently identified in a Haliclona endosymbiont, supported a hybrid NRPS–PKS origin and revealed enzymatic conversion of sponge-derived renieramycins into jorumycins by the nudibranch. Jorumycidine exhibited potent nanomolar cytotoxicity (IC₅₀ = 13.8 nM) against multiple myeloma cells, outperforming its congeners. These findings expand bis-THIQ chemical diversity and demonstrate how interspecies metabolic interplay can generate bioactive scaffolds with therapeutic potential. Subject terms: Natural products, Multienzyme complexes

Introduction

Marine symbioses are rich sources of chemical diversity, often yielding bioactive metabolites with therapeutic potential. Many marine invertebrates lack biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) and instead obtain metabolites from microbial partners or dietary sources, occasionally modifying them for ecological defense and, in some cases, shaping entire benthic communities1. Opisthobranch mollusks occupy a unique niche in this chemical landscape for their capacity to not only sequester, but also enzymatically remodel or biosynthesize secondary metabolites inaccessible to single organisms, thereby expanding the diversity and functionality of natural products2–7. These molecules can serve as chemical cues to deter predation or mediate intra-species communication. In these mollusks, metabolites, whether derived from dietary source or produced de novo, are selectively accumulated in specialized mantle tissues (e.g., mantle dermal formations or mantle rim) or released into secretions, resulting in higher concentrations at the exposed surface or in the mucus compared to internal organs. This spatial distribution is commonly used in chemical ecology studies to infer sequestration and defensive function, and it underpins the stratification of metabolites observed in mantle, mucus, and whole-body extracts.

Bis-tetrahydroisoquinoline (bis-THIQ) alkaloids constitute a prominent subclass of natural products characterized by complex chemical scaffolds and significant pharmacological potential8–10. Found mainly in marine bacteria, sponges, and tunicates, the family include the anticancer drug trabectedin (1)11 and share a conserved pentacyclic core motif assembled by nonribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPSs). Anticancer and antimicrobial activity, however, depends on subtle structural differences, and the biosynthetic routes to these variations remain poorly understood. In 2000, we reported jorumycin (2) from the nudibranch Jorunna funebris, the only natural bis-THIQ alkaloid with an acetyl group at C-2212. Its similarity to the sponge metabolite renieramycin E (3) suggested a shared origin, likely through microbial symbiosis or, alternatively, transformation of dietary metabolites obtained from sponge producers such as Haliclona spp. This view has been supported by the recent identification of the ren BGC in Candidatus Endohaliclona renieramycinifaciens, an obligate symbiont housed in specialized cells of Haliclona sponges, which encodes the NRPS machinery and tailoring enzymes necessary for renieramycin biosynthesis13.

Here we report the discovery of jorumycidine (4), an unprecedented hexacyclic bis-THIQ alkaloid featuring an oxazolidine ring that represents a previously unrecognized biosynthetic product in this symbiont-sponge–nudibranch partnership. Comparative metabolomic analysis of bis-THIQs in the sponge and mollusk demonstrates that Haliclona sp. produces both jorumycidine and renieramycins, while J. funebris enzymatically converts renieramycins into jorumycins. Deep re-analysis of the ren BGC from the sponge symbiont revealed PKS modules responsible for oxazolidine formation and the assembly of jorumycidine (4). This cross-kingdom metabolic relay highlights a unique route to bis-THIQ diversification and provides a chemically stable, nanomolar-potent scaffold for anticancer drug discovery.

Results

Isolation and structure elucidation of jorumycidine and other bis-THIQ analogs

The nudibranch J. funebris and the sponge Haliclona sp. were collected from Koh Tao Island, in southern Thailand. Mucus secreted by the mollusk during collection was extracted with diethyl ether, affording 17.8 mg of organic material. This crude extract yielded a well-resolved 1H NMR spectrum (600 MHz, C6D6) consistent with the presence of bis-THIQ alkaloids (Supplementary Fig. S1). Sequential purification by silica gel chromatography using petroleum ether/diethyl ether and a methanol–chloroform gradient led to the isolation of the novel bis-THIQ alkaloid 4 (1.2 mg) together with jorumycin12 (2, 2.1 mg) and renieramycin E14 (3, 3.1 mg) and their two new 21-deoxy analogues 5 (0.2 mg) and 6 (0.3 mg).

High-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (HR-ESI-MS) spectrum of compound 4 exhibited a prominent [M + H]+ adduct at m/z 467.1825 in agreement with the molecular formula C25H26N2O7 (Supplementary Fig. S2). The 1D- and 2D-NMR spectra (Table 1) showed a well-resolved pattern of signals, including five methyl, three methylene and five methine groups. As expected for a bis-THIQ alkaloid, carbon resonances of 4 were consistent with the presence of two substituted quinone rings featured by keto (δC 186.9, 186.5, 183.6, 181.4 ppm), arylmethyl (δC 8.6 ppm) and methoxy (δC 60.5 ppm) groups. COSY and TOCSY experiments, including the diagnostic homoallylic coupling between H-1 and H2-4, were consistent with the heterocyclic motif common to jorumycin, renieramycins and saframycins nevertheless the NMR data did not reveal the presence of any substituent at C-22 as occurring in the bis-THIQ alkaloids so far reported. On the other hand, coupling of H-1 and H2-22 together with a strong long-range correlation between these latter protons (δH 4.67 and 3.46,) and C-21 (δC 90.2 ppm) suggested an additional bond bridging C-1 to C-21. The NMR resonances of the methine groups at C-3, C-4 and C-13 appeared down-field shift in comparison to the corresponding signals in 2 and 3. Taken together, these data were consistent with the presence of an additional five-membered ring extending from C-1 to C-4 through C-22, analogous to the oxazolidine ring reported in the antitumor tetrahydroisoquinoline alkaloid quinocarcin (7)15,16. Supporting this interpretation, 1H and 13C chemical shifts of 4 closely matched those of the oxazolidine ring of quinocarcin (7), providing a conclusive evidence of the hexacyclic structure of the new compound which we have named jorumycidine (4).

HR-ESI+-MS spectrum of compound 5 showed a molecular ion peak at m/z 511.2083 [M + H]+ in agreement with the molecular formula C27H30N2O8 (Supplementary Fig. S22). Detailed analysis of the NMR data (Table 1) revealed a substitution pattern closely related to that of jorumycin (2), although the reduced molecular mass indicated the lack of one oxygen atom. A diagnostic HSQC correlation between the methylene protons at 2.54 and 2.45 ppm (H2-21) and a carbon signal at 59.5 ppm (C-21) suggested the absence of the hemiaminal function and instead supported the presence of an N-bound methylene group, accounting for the mass difference observed relative to joruymycin (2). These observations were further corroborated by the analysis of 2D NMR experiments (Supplementary Fig. S19-21), leading to the assignment of compound 5 as 21-deoxy-jorumycin.

Analogously, HR-ESI+ MS and NMR data of compound 6 revealed a major peak at m/z 551.2393 [M + H]+ in agreement with the molecular formula C30H34N2O8 (Supplementary Fig. S27). The 1H NMR spectrum (Supplementary Fig. S23) displayed the characteristic resonances of an angelate ester, typical of renieramycin analogues. This assignment was further supported by a diagnostic MS fragment at m/z 83.0497, corresponding to the loss of the unsaturated organic acid. As observed for compound 5, direct correlations of geminal protons at 2.48 and 2.34 ppm with a carbon signal at 58.9 ppm suggested replacement of the C-21 hemiaminal with a methylamino group. The remaining NMR data closely matched those of renieramycin E (3), and, together with the molecular formula, unambiguously identifying compound 6 as 21-deoxy-renieramycin E (Table 1).

Species-specific distribution and metabolic intra-species relay

Jorumycidine (4) possesses a unique carbon skeleton which, although partially resembling a fragment of quinocarcin (7), shows structural features never reported before for bis-tetrahydroisoquinoline (bis-THIQ) alkaloids. To clarify its origin, we used LC–MS/MS diagnostic fragmentation filtering (DFF) to investigate the distribution of compound 4 and related bis-THIQs in Jorunna funebris and the associated blue Haliclona sponge. The DFF module within the MZmine platform enabled targeted mining of MS/MS spectra for product ions or neutral losses diagnostic for specific structural motifs17. As shown in Fig. 1, the method was adapted to distinguish renieramycin- and jorumycin-type analogues by detecting ions due to the fragment for angelic acid (m/z 83.05, C5H7O+) or due to the neutral loss of acetic acid (C2H4O2) from highest m/z peak (e.g. m/z 449.17, C25H25N2O6 [M-H2O- C2H4O2]+ for 2). Under LC–MS conditions optimized for bis-THIQ separation, analysis of the metabolites from the nudibranch mucus confirmed the presence of both families together with jorumycidine (4) that did not cluster with either consistently with the lack of C-22 substitution (Fig. 1C). By the same workflow, analysis of the sponge extract revealed renieramycin E (3) (Rt = 10.1 min; m/z = 549.22 [M–H₂O + H]⁺) as the major metabolite, together with jorumycidine (4), 21-deoxy-reniermycin E (6) and minor uncharacterized analogues, but no jorumycin-type derivatives (Supplementary Fig. S45).

Renieramycins (3 and 6) are biosynthesized de novo in the sponge Haliclona sp. and acquired by J. funebris, where they accumulate and are proposed to undergo post-acquisition biotransformation into jorumycins (2 and 5) via replacement of the angelate moiety with an acetate group, potentially mediated by an acetyl-CoA–dependent enzymatic process. Jorumycidine (4) is also accumulated by the nudibranch from its dietary sponge source.

To confirm the dietary link, three nudibranch specimens were individually extracted by acetone through gentle sonication (mantle extract) followed by whole-body grinding (total extract). After solvent partitioning, LC–MS analysis of the resulting fractions confirmed the mucus metabolome contained compounds 2-6 together with very minor related compounds showing MS features characteristic of renieramycin- or jorumycin-type (Supplementary Fig. S42). This species-specific distribution pattern is consistent with a scenario in which renieramycin-type metabolites (e.g., 3, 6) acquired from the sponge diet are further modified to jorumycin-type analogues (e.g., 2, 5), potentially via exchange of the angelate and acetate moieties (Fig. 1D). While these data do not provide direct enzymatic evidence for such a transformation, they support the possibility of a post-acquisition chemical or enzymatic modification occurring in J. funebris. Alternative explanations, including ingestion of these compounds from other sponges using substrate promiscuity within the PKS machinery leading to parallel incorporation of different acyl units, cannot be excluded.

The preferential accumulation of these metabolites in the defensive mucus suggests a mechanism of dietary toxification or functionalization, a phenomenon previously reported in other nudibranchs18–21. Notably, like renieramycin E (3), jorumycidine (4) was consistently detected in all samples, supporting its sponge origin and dietary acquisition by the nudibranch.

Reannotation of ren BGC and biosynthesis of the oxazolidine ring of jorumycidine

Tracing the origin of jorumycidine (4) in the sponge suggested its biosynthesis through a pathway shared with renieramycin E (3). To clarify this, we re-examined the ren biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC). Bioinformatic re-annotation and manual curation resolved incomplete domain assignments and identified a collinear hybrid nonribosomal peptide synthetase–polyketide synthase (NRPS–PKS) cluster fully accounting for the biosynthesis of both jorumycidine (4) and renieramycin E (3). As shown in the refined proposal of Fig. 2, the early biosynthetic steps for hydroxylation and methylation of L-phenylalanine to produce the starting precursor L-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-O-methyltyrosine (3-OH-5-Me-OMeTyr) are attributed to renA–D, which encode enzymatic functions (monooxygenase and methyltransferase - Supplementary Fig. S36) analogous to those found in the biosynthetic pathways of structurally related tetrahydroisoquinoline products including safracins (sfn), saframycins (saf and sfm), quinocarcin (qcn) and SF-1739 (cya)22–24.

Phylogenesis of the multi-modular enzyme complexes encoded in the ren BGC revealed that the full-length aminoacidic sequences of RenF and RenK clustered with distinct proteins of the biosynthesis of tetrahydroisoquinoline alkaloids, namely saframycins and quinocarcins, while RenE did not show major correlation (Fig. 3A, B). Canonical adenylation (A) domains typical of functional NRPSs25 were found only in RenE and RenF (Supplementary Figs. S35-S36), while a noncanonical condensation domain with Pictet–Spengler (PS) activity was identified solely in RenF (Supplementary Fig. S37-38)26. Consistent with its role in catalyzing the imine-based cyclization essential for tetrahydroisoquinoline core formation, this domain clustered phylogenetically with analogous specialized PS-type condensation modules conserved across the biosynthetic pathways of structurally related bis-THIQ alkaloids (Supplementary Fig. S37)22–24,26. In the absence of a PS domain in RenE, it is conceivable that the NRPS coding genes RenE and RenF jointly assemble the bis-THIQ scaffold of jorumycidine (4) and renieramycin E (3). In this model, 3-OH-5-Me-OMeTyr is first adenylated and tethered to the peptidyl carrier protein (PCP) domain of RenE, then transferred to RenF, where the PS domain catalyzes the Pictet–Spengler cyclization that drives dimerization. Downstream oxidative and methylation steps mediated by enzymes encoded by renG and renJ further tailor the bis-THIQ core scaffold, consistent with the proposed renieramycin biosynthetic pathway13.

The presence of an oxazolidine ring distinguishes the biosynthetic pathways of 4 from those of the other bis-THIQ alkaloids such as saframycins, renieramycins and ecteinascidins, where such heterocycle is absent. The molecular aspects of oxazolidine formation have been extensively investigated in the biosynthetic pathway of quinocarcin (7) and SF-1739 (8) whose enzymes are encoded by the qcn and cya gene clusters24. As shown in Fig. 3A–C, comparative genomic analysis of the qcn and ren BGCs revealed several conserved elements. Notably, renK encoded a fatty acyl-AMP ligase (FAAL)-ACP-AT cassette homologous to qcn13 that is implicated in the biosynthesis of the polyketide region of quinocarcin (7)24. A similar domain has been also described in the sfmA BGC of Streptomyces lavendulae producing saframycin A. These modules are committed to esterification of glycolic acid with FAAL-bound angelic acid, thus supporting the functional assignment of renK as a PKS responsible for loading and assembling the glycolate-based side chain of 3 and 4 (Fig. 2, pink route; Fig. 3). Based on domain organization and sequence homology, we put forward that the RenK cassette (FAAL-ACP-C-AT-PCP) corresponds to the combination of Qcn13 (FAAL-ACP) and Qcn15 (C-A-PCP – Fig. 3D). Notably, the A domain of Qcn15 shows sequence identity to canonical adenylation domains and has been proposed to selectively incorporate glycolic acid.

In the biosynthesis of quinocarcin (7), diversion of glycoacyl unit to the NRPS assembly line is performed by the proteins encoded by qcn7 and qcn9, which convert ketosugars into glycolic acid according to the two-component transketolase (TK-ase-ACP) reaction described by Tang and co-workers27. In ren BGC, this oxidative process producing the two-carbon unit is catalyzed by RenH and RenI that show sequence homology and enzymatic functions similar to Qcn7 and Qcn9 (Supplementary Fig. S37)24.

Elucidation of the hexacyclic backbone biosynthesis of jourmycidine

RenK is the only enzyme of the ren BGC possessing a FAAL-ACP-AT cassette (Fig. 3D). We suggest that the transfer of the growing polyketide moiety synthesized by RenK to the peptide core on RenF is facilitated by the AT and C domains of RenK. This refined model explicitly addresses the previously noted absence of a canonical starter domain in the original renieramycin BGC annotation13. A second Pictet–Spengler condensation, proposed to occur iteratively by RenF during late-stage biosynthesis, generates the pentacyclic fused intermediate characteristic of bis-THIQ alkaloids. Intriguingly, ren BGC belongs to the NRPS-PKS family also considering the presence of the specific amino acid motif Asp-Ser-Leu of phosphopantetheine-binding domain which is peculiar of PKS-like enzymes28. According to the established biosynthesis of quinocarcin (7)24, the oxazolidine ring in jorumycidine (4) may be formed after hydrolytic cleavage of angelic acid moiety from renieramycin E (3) by an unannotated lipase. This cleavage would generate a hydroxy-terminal intermediate that undergoes intramolecular condensation to yield the mature oxazolidine-containing scaffold. Alternatively, a direct cyclization of a hydroxymethyl intermediate released by a final product of NRPS-PKS cannot be excluded at this stage. Stereochemical elucidation of jorumycidine (4)

To substantiate the stereochemical implications of the proposed biosynthetic pathway, compound 4 was subjected to an extensive stereochemical investigation combining NMR-based spatial correlations and chiroptical analyses. NOESY experiments revealed diagnostic through-space correlations between H-21 and H-3 as well as H-11, and between H-1 and both H-3 and H-21 (Supplementary Fig. S8). These latter spatial proximities were further corroborated by selective one-dimensional NOE experiments, which showed clear signal enhancements for H-1 (δH 2.94) and H-3 (δH 2.87) upon selective irradiation of the C-21 proton (δH 4.11) (Fig. 4B). Collectively, these data unambiguously established the 21 R* relative configuration at C-21 and, by extension, defined an all-syn 1 R*,3S*,11 R*,13S*,21 R* arrangement of the central cage within the hexacyclic framework. Significantly, in agreement with the established biosynthesis of quinocarcin (7), the combined NOE-derived constraints and biosynthetic considerations were consistent with formation of the oxazolidine ring in 4 from precursor 3 via intramolecular nucleophilic cyclization, proceeding with the suggested inversion of configuration at the hemiaminal carbon.

On the basis of this well-defined relative configuration, the absolute stereochemistry of jorumycidine (4) was subsequently investigated by electronic circular dichroism (ECD) spectroscopy. As observed for other bis-tetrahydroisoquinoline alkaloids, compound 4 exhibited a characteristic ECD profile dominated by the contributions of the two p-benzoquinone chromophores (Supplementary Fig. S30). The spectrum showed a negative exciton couplet at 268 nm29, corresponding to the zero crossing between two oppositely signed Cotton effects at 255 and 280 nm (Fig. 4C). This feature arises from degenerate exciton coupling between the principal π–π* transitions of the substituted p-benzoquinone units, whose interacting transition dipole moments are approximately oriented along the O–O directions. In agreement with the absolute stereochemistry of renieramycin E (3), application of the exciton chirality method indicated a helicity of the coupled chromophores, supporting assignment of the absolute configuration of (+)-jorumycidine (4) as 1 R,3S,11 R,13S,21 R. This assignment was further reinforced by quantum-chemical ECD calculations performed using time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT)30,31, which reproduced the experimental ECD spectrum with good agreement, whereas the calculated spectrum of the hypothetical enantiomer was clearly inconsistent with the experimental data (Fig. 4C).

Absolute configuration of 21-deoxy analogs of jorumycin (5) and renieramycin E (6)

The same TD-DFT computational approach was applied to 21-deoxy-jorumycin (5), whose configuration was confirmed to be (–)-1R,3S,11R,13S, and to 21-deoxy-renieramycin E (6), with configuration (+)-1R,3S,11R,13S-(6) (Supplementary Fig. S31). Apart from the good match between calculated and experimental ECD spectra, especially in most significant low-energy region, we observe a close resemblance between ECD spectra of all compounds 2-6 (Fig. 4C and Figure. S30). Interestingly for biological activity, the presence of the oxazolidine ring in 4 does not alter much the overall molecular folding of the bis-THIQ alkaloids particularly in relation to the reciprocal orientation between the p-benzoquinone rings (Figure S35).

Comparative anticancer activity of jorumycidine (4)

The cytotoxic activity of the new bis-THIQ alkaloids 4-6 was evaluated in comparison to the known drug candidates jorumycin (2) and renieramycin E (3) against a panel of human cancer lines composed of melanoma (A2058), breast adenocarcinoma (HCC87), and multiple myeloma (JJN3) cells (Table 2). Prior to the assays, an analytical HPLC purification of each product along with MS analysis to check purity was performed in order to further ensure the quality of the results (Supplementary Fig. S49). As expected, the removal of the hemiaminal functionality in 21-deoxy analogues (5 and 6) led to a dramatic loss of activity across all cell lines. This effect was especially pronounced for 21-deoxy-renieramycin E (6), which showed minimal cytotoxicity in all tested lines, suggesting that the presence of the hemiaminal and the nature of the side chain are key determinants of bioactivity in this class of marine alkaloids. In contrast, jorumycidine (4) demonstrated remarkable potency and selectivity toward the JJN3 plasma cell leukemia related to human multiple myeloma (IC₅₀ = 13.8 nM), outperforming both jorumycin (3) and renieramycin E (5) that have inspired the development of the anticancer candidate Zalypsis®.

Discussion

The discovery of jorumycidine (4) expands the structural and biosynthetic landscape of bis-tetrahydroisoquinoline (bis-THIQ) alkaloids, a family long recognized for their biomedical potential8–10. The most well-known molecule of the family is trabectidin (1) (Yondelis®) originally derived from the tunicate Ecteinascidia turbinate and currently prepared by semi-synthesis11. Trabectedin (1) binds specifically to the minor groove of DNA at selected guanine-rich triplet sequences, leading to DNA double-strand breaks and apoptosis in tumor cells32. Additionally, the anticancer activity of 1 is also supported by immunomodulatory effects on tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and modification of the tumor microenvironment33,34. The bis-THIQ family currently comprises more than 30 bioactive natural products that have also inspired the development of Zalypsis® (PM00104) which progressed into multiple Phase II clinical trials for solid and hematologic tumors35. All these molecules maintain DNA minor groove-binding mechanism by virtue of the reactive 21-hemiaminal group whose presence is mandatory to preserve the anticancer activity36. Unlike previously reported congeners, jorumycidine (4) incorporates the 21-hemiaminal function within an oxazolidine ring fused to the canonical bis-THIQ core, yielding the first hexacyclic scaffold of its class. This structural novelty is not simply a curiosity, but it stabilizes a traditionally labile hemiaminal position, preserving the DNA alkylating property responsible for the potent cytotoxic activity while circumventing the chemical fragility that has negatively affected drug development from related scaffolds. From a chemical biology perspective, such structure–stability–activity relationships are broadly relevant for the development of THIQ-based therapeutics37.

Bioinformatic re-annotation of the Haliclona renieramycin biosynthetic gene cluster provides a mechanistic rationale for oxazolidine formation. The cluster encodes a hybrid NRPS–PKS system reminiscent of quinocarcin biosynthesis24, suggesting that glycolate-derived building blocks are funneled into the assembly line and subsequently cyclized into an oxazolidine. This pathway underscores the versatility of NRPS–PKS cassettes in creating new N,O-heterocycles and suggests that similar logic may be repurposed in other secondary metabolic contexts. In addition, our results highlight how ecological interactions create unique biosynthetic opportunities in marine environments. Use of LC–MS/MS DFF17 for mapping bis-THIQ alakloids revealed a species-specific metabolic relay, in which metazoan symbiont-derived renieramycins are enzymatically remodeled by the nudibranch into jorumycins, while jorumycidine (4) itself appears to be directly sequestered from the sponge. This interplay illustrates how animals exploit microbial or dietary metabolites not only for chemical defense but also as evolutionary platforms for scaffold innovation. More broadly, such cross-kingdom cooperation generates new molecular architectures while retaining memory of past biosynthetic innovations. Such “metabolic archives” help explain the recurrent emergence of structural variants and underscore the role of ecological interactions in shaping natural product diversity with pharmacologically privileged motifs.

In conclusion, we have isolated and fully characterized a novel THIQ compound named jorumycidine (4) showing an unprecedented structure with six cycles and a promising cytotoxic activity at nanomolar level against multiple myeloma cells. The novel carbon skeleton arises by bridging C-21 to C-1, and formation of an oxazolidine ring from a biosynthetic precursor common to renieramycin E (3) and produced by a NRPS-PKS system. Beyond the immediate novelty of a hexacyclic bis-THIQ, jorumycidine (4) exemplifies how marine chemical biology continues to uncover both new scaffolds and new biosynthetic strategies that can inspire novel drug candidates.

Methods

General Experimental Procedures

Optical rotations were measured on a Jasco P-2000 digital polarimeter. UV spectra were recorded using a Jasco V-650 spectrophotometer, and IR spectra using a Jasco FT/IR-4100 spectrometer (JASCO EUROPE S.r.l., Cremella, Italy). ECD spectra were recorded with a Jasco J-1500 spectropolarimeter (JASCO EUROPE S.r.l., Cremella, Italy) with 1-cm path cell. NMR spectra were acquired on a Bruker DRX-600 spectrometer (600 MHz for ¹H and 150 MHz for ¹³C) equipped with a triple-resonance cryoprobe (TCI). Chemical shifts (δ) are reported in ppm and referenced to residual solvent signals (C6D6: δH = 7.15 ppm; δC = 128.0 ppm). High-resolution mass spectra (HRMS) were recorded on a Q-Exactive™ Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap™ mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Milan, Italy). HPLC analyses were carried out on a Jasco system comprising a PU-2089 Plus quaternary gradient pump and a MD-2018 Plus photodiode array detector. Silica gel chromatography was performed using Merck precoated silica gel 60 F₂₅₄ plates. All reagents and solvents (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were of analytical grade and used without further purification.

Sampling and Identification

Specimens of Jorunna funebris and an associated blue sponge (Haliclona sp.) were collected by hand during SCUBA diving at 5.7 m depth on 22 June 2022 at the Aow Leuk dive site (10°4'11.65“N, 99°50'34.42“E), Koh Tao Island, Gulf of Thailand. Two voucher specimens are deposited at the Institute of Biomolecular Chemistry, National Research Council (ICB-CNR), Pozzuoli, Italy. Specimens were identified morphologically following Mehrotra and Urgell (2018).

Extraction and Purification of Compounds 2–6

A frozen specimen of J. funebris (dry weight: 0.478 g) was thawed in acetone to remove the aqueous mucus. The entire organism was then extracted with acetone via gentle sonication (mantle extract), followed by tissue homogenization (total extract). The aqueous residues from mucus, mantle, and total extracts were each partitioned with diethyl ether (3 × 20 mL), yielding 17.8, 8.0, and 31.4 mg of ether extract, respectively. The complete ether-soluble mucus extract was subjected to silica gel column chromatography using a gradient of petroleum ether (PE)/diethyl ether (EE), followed by increasing concentrations of methanol in chloroform. This process yielded jorumycidine (4, 1.2 mg, eluted with PE/EE 3:7) and a mixed fraction (2.9 mg, CHCl₃/MeOH 98:2 v/v) containing bis-THIQ congeners. The latter ones were further purified via RP-HPLC (Phenomenex PS C18, 250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) with a linear gradient from 40% to 100% acetonitrile (ACN) in water over 40 min (flow rate: 1.0 mL/min), affording: jorumycin (2) (Rt = 16.0 min, 0.3 mg), renieramycin E (3) (Rt = 24.0 min, 0.4 mg), 21-deoxy-jorumycin (5) (Rt = 20.0 min, 0.2 mg), 21-deoxy-renieramycin E (6) (Rt = 27.0 min, 0.3 mg).

Extraction and Analysis of Haliclona sp

The frozen Haliclona sp. sample (dry weight: 0.370 g) was extracted with methanol (3 × 20 mL). The crude extract was analyzed by ¹H NMR and LC-HRESI-MS/MS and compared with the mucus, mantle, and total extracts of J. funebris. LC-MS analysis was performed using a RP-HPLC column (Phenomenex Luna C18, 150 × 2.0 mm, 5 μm) with a gradient from 10% ACN (0.1% formic acid) to 100% ACN over 30 min (flow rate: 0.3 mL/min) on a Q-Exactive™ Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) coupled with a 1290 Infinity UPLC system (Agilent Technologies).

Bioinformatic Methods

Renieramycin BGC and quinocarcin BGC sequences were obtained using the Uniprot database at https://www.uniprot.org from Candidatus Endohaliclona renieramycinifaciens and Streptomyces melanovinaceus, respectively. Other BGC sequences were found using uniprot database (https://www.uniprot.org/) and NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein). Blast, alignment and interprot-scan approaches were made using Geneious software version 2025.1.2 by the use of a temporary license38. BLAST parameters were: matrix = BLOSUM62, gap cost = 11.1, max evalue = 10; adjustment = conditional compositional score matrix adjustment method. Alignment parameters were: type = global alignment with free and gaps; matrix = BLOSUM62, gap open penalty = 12, gap extension penalty = 3, refinement interaction = 2. Interprot scan analyses were based on PANTHER, PFAM and SUPERFAMILY databases.

Phylogenetic analyses were done using software MEGA version 639. Sequence alignments were obtained using the MUSCLE algorithm. The alignments were performed using Gap penalties: Gap Open =-0.01, Gap extend = 0, Hydrophobicity Multiplier = 1.2. The number of max iterations was equal to 32. The substitution model was selected by using a ProtTest approach to select the best-fit models of protein evolution using MEGA. The used test tree was a Neighbor-joining tree and the statistical method was Maximum Likelihood. Models with the lowest BIC scores (Bayesian Information Criterion) were considered as the best to describe the substitution pattern. Phylogenetic tree was constructed using the maximum likelihood method with the LG + F gamma distributed substitution model. Tree inference options were set by Nearest-Neighbor-Interchange (NNI) method. The test of phylogeny was performed using the bootstrap method with a number of bootstrap replication equal to 100.

Computational section

Input structures for (+)-(1R,3S,11R,13S,21R)-jorumycidine (4), (1R,3S,11R,13S)-21-deoxy-jorumycin (5) and (1R,3S,11R,13S)-21-deoxy-renieramycin (6) were built in Spartan’24 (v. 24.3.1, Wavefunction, Inc., Irvine, CA). A conformational search was run with Merck molecular force field (MMFF) varying all possible rotatable bonds and allowing different puckering of N atoms and endocyclic C atoms. All structures thus obtained were refined by preliminary DFT geometry optimization run at B3LYP-D3/6-31G(d) level, using default convergence criteria in Spartan’24. The optimization produced less than 10 low-energy structures for each compound with relative energies within 5 kcal/mol. Duplicate structures allied with the rotamerism of methoxy groups were disregarded. Final geometry optimizations were run with Gaussian16, Rev. C.0140, using default grids and convergence criteria, at B3LYP/6-311+G(d,p) level, including D3BJ dispersion correction and a continuum solvent model (IEF-PCM) for acetonitrile. The nature of the stationary points was verified through vibrational frequency calculations performed at the same level of theory. The calculations produced 3 minima for 4, 5 minima for 5 and 3 minima for 6 with population > 0.5% at 300 K, based on free energies. ECD calculations were run with TD-DFT at B3LYP/def2-TZVP and CAM-B3LYP/def2-TZVP levels, with IEF-PCM solvent model for acetonitrile, including 36 roots. The results obtained with the two functionals were consistent, with CAM-B3LYP better predicting the relative energies of the main ECD bands. Weighted-average ECD spectra were plotted with SpecDis v. 1.7141, using populations estimated from free energies at 300 K. The plotting parameters are listed in the figure captions.

Cell cultures and cytotoxic tests

The purity of the isolated compounds was carefully monitored by NMR and HRMS analyses, in accordance with common practice in natural products chemistry. The compounds were stored at −80 °C and, whenever possible, biological assays were carried out immediately after purification to minimize any contribution from degradation processes. Human cancer cell lines A2058 and HCC827 (ATCC) and JJN3 (DSMZ) were cultured as previously described42. For cytotoxicity assays, cells (1 × 10⁴ per well) were seeded in 96-well plates in 0.1 mL of medium. Each compound was tested in triplicate to determine IC₅₀ values. As positive controls Cisplatin and doxorubicin were all used at the concentration of 100 µM. Adherent cells were assayed using the Sulforhodamine B (SRB) assay kit (Abcam, ab235935). After 24 h treatment, cells were fixed, stained, and the optical density measured at 565 nm. Suspension cells were analyzed using the MTS assay (Abcam, ab197010). Ten µL of MTS reagent was added per well, and plates incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm.
