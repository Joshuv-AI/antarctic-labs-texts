Gastroenteropancreatic Neuroendocrine Carcinoma (GEP-NEC): An Aggressive Disease Course and Limitations for Personalized Oncology
Authors: Celine Oanæs, Herish Garresori, Dordi Lea, Marcus T. T. Roalsø, Torjan M. Haslerud, Cato Brede, Kjetil Søreide
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) is a rare, aggressive malignancy with limited treatment options and poor prognosis. We report a male patient diagnosed with a gastroenteropancreatic (GEP)-NEC with synchronous liver metastasis at the time of surgery who underwent a radical resection attempt. Despite radical-intent surgery followed by adjuvant carboplatin/etoposide, early recurrence developed with progression through multiple subsequent chemotherapy lines. During the treatment process, genetic profiling was performed twice to identify actionable genomic targets, with inclusion in the national IMPRESS study as a last resort. Comprehensive genomic profiling revealed TP53 mutation and RB1 loss but no actionable alterations. A patient-derived organoid (PDO) was successfully established from resected tumor tissue and retained key neuroendocrine and proliferative features, with partial genomic concordance to the primary tumor. Differences between the primary and subsequent PDO in variant allele frequencies suggest clonal selection during culture. Exploratory metabolomic profiling of tryptophan pathway metabolites in patient serum and PDO-culture media indicated tumor-associated metabolic alterations. We present clinical and translational efforts in difficult-to-treat NEC, illustrating both the translational challenges and the potential role of PDOs in advancing personalized treatment strategies for a cancer with very limited treatment options.

Introduction

Neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) is a rare and aggressive malignancy that usually arises in lungs and the gastrointestinal tract, the latter referred to as gastroenteropancreatic (GEP)-NEC [2]. NECs are poorly differentiated neoplasms characterized by high proliferative activity, reflected in an elevated mitotic rate and a Ki-67 proliferative index > 20%, with most cases scoring substantially higher [3]. NECs are further subdivided into small-cell NEC (SCNEC) and large-cell NEC (LCNEC) based on their morphological characteristics. Immunohistochemical expression of neuroendocrine markers such as synaptophysin and chromogranin A (CgA), together with molecular alterations including aberrant p53 expression and loss of retinoblastoma protein (RB1), helps classify NECs [4]. At diagnosis, 62% of the patients present with distant metastases [5], and survival remains limited, ranging from 38 months in localized disease to approximately 10 months for patients with metastatic disease [6]. Some studies suggest sex-related differences in incidence and overall survival in GEP-NECs, although the underlying biological basis remains unclear, and data are limited [7]. Current treatment strategies include surgical resection and adjuvant chemotherapy, with first-line therapy typically consisting of carboplatin/etoposide, or cisplatin/etoposide. However, there is currently no consensus regarding second-line treatment of NECs, and multidisciplinary discussions remains the recommended guidance for treatment decision-making [8, 9]. Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) have emerged as a promising translational model for studying tumor biology and guiding patient-specific therapy. PDOs are three-dimensional cell cultures derived directly from a patient’s tissue and can retain key histological, molecular, and functional features of the patient’s primary tumor. They have proven to be representative models for drug screening across multiple cancer types [10–13].

Alterations in tryptophan metabolism have been implicated in cancer biology, influencing tumor proliferation, immune evasion, and neurotransmission. Key metabolites, including kynurenine, serotonin, and various indole derivatives, may reflect both tumor-intrinsic metabolic activity and systemic changes in the host [14]. Assessing tryptophan metabolites in patient serum and PDO-conditioned medium can provide insight into tumor metabolism, evaluate the fidelity of PDOs in recapitulating patient tumor biology, and explore potential biomarkers for personalized therapy.

Thus, the aim of this study was to describe the clinical course and molecular characteristics of a NEC with liver metastasis and evaluate PDOs as a potential preclinical model for this rare and hard-to-treat malignancy.

Case Presentation

A man in his early 60s presented with fatigue, recent involuntary weight-loss, nausea, and jaundice. Information on ethnicity was not available in the medical records and is therefore not reported. Clinical jaundice was confirmed by laboratory tests suggesting an extrahepatic cause, including a markedly elevated bilirubin level of 182 µmol/l that subsequently increased to a peak of 326 µmol/l. An initial computed tomography (CT) scan of the thorax and abdomen did not reveal a definitive tumor as the cause of the biliary obstruction. A magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was done for further image evaluation, suggesting a possible common duct biliary stone and confirming a marked dilation of the proximal extra- and intrahepatic biliary tree ducts. The patient was referred for endoscopic clearance of the biliary tree and, in case of a non-benign stricture, a potential biopsy, and underwent endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP). ERCP was done with sphincterotomy, and the cholangiogram revealed a suspected biliary stricture. ERCP was done with sphincterotomy, and the cholangiogram revealed a suspect biliary stricture. Biopsies were obtained, and a covered metal stent was placed to relieve the biliary obstruction. Histopathology showed a small-cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) that was strongly positive for thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1) on immunohistochemistry, raising suspicion for a metastatic small-cell lung carcinoma as a potential primary origin. However, further imaging, including an FDG-PET scan and CT, did not show any thoracic or extrapancreatic lesions (Fig. 1), making a pulmonary primary less likely. The case was discussed among a multidisciplinary team, and the patient was informed about the findings, prognosis, and available treatment options. Following postoperative recovery, adjuvant chemotherapy with carboplatin and etoposide was initiated 5 weeks after surgery for six cycles, with no radiological evidence of disease after completion. Follow-up CT imaging was performed approximately 3 months post-adjuvant therapy; unfortunately, it revealed locoregional disease recurrence in the pancreatic gland and regional lymph nodes. First-line palliative therapy with carboplatin and etoposide was started, but an evaluation scan after three cycles showed further disease progression (Fig. 2A). Consequently, treatment was changed to second-line palliative therapy with capecitabine and temozolomide.

The patient was referred to a national clinical study, IMPRESS Norway [15], for comprehensive genomic profiling of the tumor with next-generation sequencing (NGS) using the TruSight Oncology 500 panel to investigate potential actionable mutations. No actionable or targetable mutations were detected. Further progression occurred 2 months after initiating second-line therapy. Following a multidisciplinary team discussion, therapy was changed to FOLFIRI, resulting in a partial response after four cycles (Fig. 2A). However, disease progression was observed after the eighth and final cycle of FOLFIRI. Fourth-line palliative chemotherapy (FOLFOX) was administered, but again further progression occurred, leading to discontinuation of treatment. The patient died 3 weeks later.

Tissue Collection and Ethical Approval

Tumor tissue was collected at the time of surgery, and blood samples (whole blood, plasma, serum) were obtained preoperatively and 4 weeks postoperatively before initiation of adjuvant chemotherapy. The study was conducted in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The patient material was collected and processed according to our research biobank protocol with informed consent and ethical approval. Informed consent was received from the patient to publish details on his case. The project was approved by the regional ethics committee, as project REK#2022/494778, and as part of biobank project REK Vest#2013/1502.

PDO Generation

The PDO was successfully generated from the NEC tissue in accordance with our existing biobanking protocol [16] and demonstrated long-term viability, with propagation beyond ten passages when established directly from the resected tumor. Following cryopreservation, thawed organoids exhibited suboptimal recovery, with limited expansion and inconsistent support for long-term propagation. Molecular and Phenotypic Characterization of Tumor and Organoids

To further assess the biological fidelity of the patient-derived NEC organoids relative to the primary tumor, molecular and phenotypic analyses were performed. This included next-generation sequencing (NGS) (Oncomine Precision Assay, Thermo Fisher Scientific), immunohistochemical staining, and metabolomic assessment. NGS analysis revealed a TP53 missense mutation with concordant allele frequency of ~ 90% in the tumor, indicating that most of the tumor cells carried this mutation (Table 1). The same TP53 mutation was also detected in the PDO, with a substantially lower allele frequency of 4.9%, suggesting that only a small population of cells in the PDO retained the mutation. A mutation in the ALK gene was also detected in both the patient's tumor and the PDO with allele frequencies of 36.2% and 46.7%, respectively, but this result was not reported to the clinicians because of its unknown clinical relevance. The extended NGS-panel, TSO500, identified additional mutations in the patient tumor, with variants of potential clinical relevance listed in Table 1. RB1 and NBN had allele frequencies of 81% and 48%, respectively. The TSO500 NGS panel also included genes commonly mutated in neuroendocrine tumors (NETs), MEN1, ATRX, and DAXX [17], with no mutations detected. Copy number variation (CNV) analysis revealed genome-wide gains and losses in the tumor. The specific CNV sites are usually not reported to clinicians because of their unknown clinical significance, but the results revealed that NTRK1 had the highest copy number gain in both the tumor tissue and the PDO, demonstrating biological concordance between the models. Discussion

NECs of the pancreatobiliary tract are rare, aggressive malignancies characterized by rapid progression, early metastatic spread, and limited therapeutic options. The present case illustrates the clinical challenges associated with advanced NEC, as well as the potential value and limitations of PDOs for modeling tumor biology and their potential clinical implications.

Despite initial surgical resection followed by adjuvant chemotherapy, the patient experienced early disease relapses, consistent with the aggressive nature of NECs. Subsequent lines of systemic therapy showed limited efficacy, with only a temporary partial response observed during FOLFIRI treatment. This underscores the necessity for improved predictive biomarkers and functional models to guide personalized treatment strategies in NEC. As often reported for surgically resected NECs, the dismal outcome in this aggressive cancer calls for better and novel systemic therapy to improve survival. In a recent, large multicenter study, median NEC survival was a median of 7.4 months [19].

Currently, there is no consensus or established standard of care for sequencing systemic therapies in advanced NEC beyond the second line [9]. While NEC and well-differentiated Grade 3 NETs differ in their biology and treatment pathways, retrospective data show that an oxaliplatin/5-FU (FOLFOX) backbone can yield meaningful anti-tumor activity in heavily pretreated neuroendocrine malignancies [20].

Molecular profiling of the patient's tumor tissue revealed common alterations associated with NEC, including a TP53 mutation, RB1 loss, and a high Ki-67 value. These findings align with previous reports describing TP53 and RB1 inactivation as hallmark events in poorly differentiated NECs, contributing to impaired cell cycle control and uncontrolled cell division [21]. The absence of NET-associated mutations further supports the NEC characterization.

Although no NEC-specific targeted therapies are established in routine clinical practice, NGS may identify rare but potentially actionable alterations, including mismatch repair deficiency, high tumor mutational burden, and oncogenic drivers such as NTRK fusion and BRAF V600E mutation [22]. Several precision oncology trials use NGS to guide treatment recommendations or targeted therapy selection across different tumor types, including rare cancers [23, 24].

The neuroendocrine biomarker CgA remained consistently low in serum, including at recurrence. Studies have shown that CgA is rarely increased in blood for poorly differentiated NECs and is more commonly elevated in well to moderately differentiated NETs. It has also been found that CgA requires sufficient tumor size to be detectable in serum [25]. Carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels in serum were significantly higher during third-line chemotherapy when a partial regression was detected radiologically. Cancer antigen 19–9 (CA 19–9) was within the normal range until it increased at radiological recurrence and remained elevated when the radiological partial regression was detected. This suggests that traditional serum biomarkers such as CgA, CEA, and CA 19–9 may not reliably reflect disease activity in poorly differentiated NEC.

Establishing PDOs could enable comparison between the in vivo tumor and an ex vivo model system. The current PDO of NEC retained key phenotypic features of the primary tumor, including neuroendocrine marker expression and high proliferative activity, supporting their relevance as a preclinical model for NEC. The PDOs revealed the presence of an ALK mutation and concordant amplification of NTRK1, alterations also observed in the patient's tumor. Although these changes currently lack therapeutic relevance, this underscores the ability of the PDO to recapitulate key aspects of the patient’s tumor biology. Notably, the TP53 mutation exhibited a markedly lower allele frequency in the PDO than the patient tumor tissue, suggesting a selection of specific subclones during organoid establishment and propagation. Such differences should be considered when interpreting PDO-based functional studies or drug-screening results.

Metabolite profiling of tryptophan metabolism revealed changes in both patient serum and PDO-conditioned medium following tumor resection. IAA showed the highest postoperative fold change in serum (1.89), with lower levels before surgery and higher levels after tumor removal. By comparing the metabolite levels in NEC to previously published healthy controls [18], IAA and IPA are markedly lower in NEC. Notably, IAA levels were lower preoperatively than in any healthy control measured with this method (n = 32) but presented normal levels after tumor resection. Both IAA and IPA have been implicated in antitumor immunity and inhibition of tumor growth in other cancers [26–28], but there is a general lack of data on tryptophan metabolite levels in NEC. The normalization of IAA after tumor resection suggests that the tumor may have altered tryptophan metabolism, creating a microenvironment that is less favorable for antitumor immune activity.

Analysis of the PDO-conditioned media revealed a 70% increase in I3A compared with media without PDOs present. In contrast, I3A decreased by 46% in patient serum postoperatively. Together, the decrease in circulating I3A after tumor removal and the increase of I3A in the presence of PDOs suggest that the tumor may actively secrete I3A. Although traditionally considered microbiota-derived, recent evidence indicates that endogenous I3A also exists [29], explaining accumulation under sterile PDO conditions.

Collectively, these findings suggest that the tumor modulates tryptophan metabolism, both directly and indirectly, to influence antitumor immune responses in NEC, though the significance of these results remains uncertain given the single-patient design. Some serum changes, such as the postoperative rise in IAA, align with the expected recovery of host metabolism, whereas the PDO-specific increase in I3A represents a novel, potentially tumor-intrinsic phenomenon that warrants further investigation.

Some important limitations should be acknowledged. The method used to quantify tryptophan metabolites was validated for serum samples but not specifically for organoid culture media. Thus, matrix-related effects may influence metabolite measurements in PDO-conditioned media, and these findings should be regarded as exploratory. In addition, the PDO culture was compromised at later time points, showing limited recovery and progressive cell loss after thawing. Immunohistochemical and metabolomic analyses were performed during this period, which may have affected cellular composition, metabolic activity, and protein expression levels. As this is a single-patient report, generalizability is inherently limited, particularly regarding the demographic and biological variability across patient populations. Moreover, clonal selection during organoid establishment and propagation could contribute to differences in variant allele frequencies and copy number profiles between the patient tumor and PDO. Finally, interpretation of several genomic alterations was constrained by current clinical reporting guidelines, highlighting the gap between biological findings and clinical actionability in rare malignancies such as NEC.

Important limitations exist in current approaches to personalized therapy and are demonstrated in the current case discussion of an NEC. Despite comprehensive molecular profiling, no actionable therapeutic targets were identified. Similarly, although a PDO was successfully established and recapitulated several phenotypic and genomic characteristics of the patient's tumor, it did not reveal clinically actionable findings or therapeutic vulnerabilities that could have informed alternative treatment strategies.

Nevertheless, the PDOs provided a platform for investigating tumor biology in an ex vivo setting and revealed both shared and divergent molecular features compared with the original tumor, highlighting the strengths and limitations of this approach. These findings support the use of PDOs as a research tool to study NEC biology and explore potential therapeutic vulnerabilities. While emerging studies suggest that PDO-based approaches may have clinical relevance in selected settings, further evidence is needed to define their role in the management of NECs.

In conclusion, this case highlights the aggressive clinical course of pancreatobiliary NEC and demonstrates the feasibility of establishing PDOs from this rare tumor type. Although the PDOs preserved key molecular features of the original tumor, challenges remain. Future research should focus on validating the reproducibility of PDO models and establishing their clinical applicability in NECs.
