Fluctuation trend of inflammatory indexes related to gestational diabetes mellitus from second trimester to third trimester of pregnancy
Authors: Mingming Xu, Linlin Tang, Yaping Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to assess the prognostic and diagnostic value of inflammatory indexes related to gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) from the second trimester to the third trimester of pregnancy.Materials and methodsIn this study, we randomly selected 65 pregnant women diagnosed with GDM at our hospital from December 2022 to June 2023 to form the GDM group (n = 65). Additionally, 65 pregnant women at the same gestational weeks without GDM were selected as the Normal group (n = 65). We collected gestational information and serum samples at 24 and 36 weeks of gestation from the participants. The levels of NLRP3, IL-1Ra, and TBP-2 were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to explore their changes during pregnancy. Further, this study analyzed the changes in the levels of NLRP3, IL-1Ra, and TBP-2 at 24 and 36 weeks of gestation in GDM patients and their correlation with gestational diabetes mellitus.ResultsThe study showed that pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), neonatal weight, gestational hypertension, and macrosomia are significantly associated with the occurrence of GDM (P < 0.05). Statistical analysis comparing the normal and GDM groups found no significant changes in the levels of NLRP3, IL-1Ra, and TBP-2 with the progression of gestation in the normal group. In contrast, in the GDM group, the levels of IL-1Ra in serum samples at 24 and 36 weeks were significantly increased (P < 0.05) while the levels of NLRP3 and TBP-2 were significantly reduced (P < 0.05). At 36 weeks, there was a positive correlation between the levels of NLRP3, IL-1Ra, and TBP-2. Compared to the normal group, the overall levels of NLRP3, IL-1Ra, and TBP-2 in the GDM group were lower (P < 0.05) and the weight of the newborns was significantly correlated with these three indicators (P < 0.05), specifically newborn weight increased with the levels of NLRP3 and TBP-2 but decreased with the increase of IL-1Ra (P < 0.05). Multifactorial logistic regression analysis further revealed that NLRP3 is an independent factor influencing GDM (P < 0.05). ROC curve analysis of the NLRP3 level at 24 weeks of gestation found that NLRP3 has a good value in predicting GDM (AUC = 0.720, 95%CI 0.630–0.809, P < 0.001) and the combined prediction of NLRP3, IL-1Ra, and TBP-2 also showed a good predictive value for GDM.ConclusionThe changes in NLRP3, IL-1Ra, and TBP-2 persisted throughout the 24 to 36 weeks of gestation, playing an important role in predicting the occurrence of GDM and the weight of the newborn.Keywords: Gestational diabetes mellitus, NLRP3, IL-1Ra, TBP-2

Background

Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) is a form of carbohydrate metabolism disorder that emerges during pregnancy, characterized by an intolerance to glucose. In China, GDM affects approximately 14.8% of pregnant women [1] and has significant implications for both the mother and the fetus, increasing the risk of various pregnancy complications such as gestational hypertension, polyhydramnios, premature rupture of membranes, infection, and preterm labor. In severe cases, GDM can lead to ketoacidosis in the mother and postpartum diabetes. Newborns may suffer from issues like respiratory distress syndrome and hypoglycemia, which in extreme cases can be fatal [2]. Although the specific molecular mechanisms of GDM are not fully understood, it is generally believed to result from a combination of chronic insulin resistance [3] and dysfunction of the pancreatic beta cells. Studies have shown that patients with GDM typically exhibit a state of low-grade inflammation [4], with elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. These factors can directly damage the insulin signaling pathway by reducing the tyrosine kinase activity of the insulin receptor, increasing the serine phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrate 1, or degrading IRS-1 through the STAT3-SOCS3 pathway, thereby inducing insulin resistance [2]. Against this backdrop, our study focuses on investigating the changes in inflammatory factors in pregnant women with GDM at 24 and 36 weeks of gestation and their impact on the pregnancy and delivery process.

Methods

Study population selection

Pregnant women at 24 and 36 weeks of gestation who received regular prenatal care and delivered at the hospital between December 2022 and June 2023 were included. They were divided into two groups: gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and normal group.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria for the GDM group were meeting diagnostic criteria for GDM, age ≥ 20 years, singleton pregnancy, and informed consent. Exclusion criteria included pre-existing diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, infection, multiple pregnancies, prenatal medication use affecting blood sugar or pressure, and a family history of diabetes.

Data collection

Clinical data including age, ethnicity, gestational age, pregnancy outcomes, mode of delivery, and newborn parameters were collected. Serum samples were obtained at 24 and 36 weeks of gestation for NLRP3, IL-1Ra, and TBP-2 level measurement using ELISA.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 25.0 software was used for statistical analysis. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were analyzed using a paired t-test.Categorical data were assessed using chi-square tests. Pearson’s tests were used for correlation analysis. Multiple linear regression and logistic regression analyses explored factors affecting newborn weight and GDM occurrence. ROC curve analysis evaluated the predictive value of biomarkers for GDM.

Changes of NLRP3, IL-1Ra, and TBP-2 during pregnancy

The data from Table 2 reveals that in the normal pregnant women group, changes in the levels of NLRP3, IL-1Ra, and TBP-2 do not have statistical significance. However, in the GDM group, these markers showed specific trends: as pregnancy progressed, the level of IL-1Ra significantly increased (P < 0.05) while the levels of NLRP3 and TBP-2 significantly decreased (P < 0.05). Further bivariate Pearson correlation tests indicated that at 24 weeks of gestation, there was no significant correlation between the changes in these three markers (P all > 0.05). By 36 weeks of gestation, however, there was a positive correlation between IL-1Ra and both NLRP3 and TBP-2 as well as between NLRP3 and TBP-2 themselves (correlation coefficients were 0.43, 0.52, and 0.53 respectively; P < 0.05) suggesting that the trends in these biomarkers are interrelated towards the late stages of pregnancy.

Changes of NLRP3, IL-1Ra, TBP-2, and fetal weight

By conducting a linear regression analysis using the levels of NLRP3, IL-1Ra, and TBP-2 measured at 24 weeks of gestation as independent variables and the newborn’s weight as the dependent variable, we found that the newborn’s weight positively correlates with the levels of NLRP3 and TBP-2, meaning that as NLRP3 and TBP-2 levels increase, the newborn’s weight correspondingly increases. Conversely, the newborn’s weight decreases as the level of IL-1Ra increases. Based on these data, we derived the following linear regression equation: Newborn weight = 3300.08 − 0.03 * IL-1Ra + 4.15 * NLRP3 + 0.05 * TBP-2. This equation, detailed in Table 3, reveals how the newborn’s weight can be predicted by considering these three biomarkers.

Logistic regression analysis of NLRP3, IL-1Ra, TBP-2, and GDM at 24 weeks of pregnancy

Using multifactorial logistic regression analysis with the levels of NLRP3, IL-1Ra, and TBP-2 at 24 weeks of gestation as independent variables and the diagnosis of GDM as the dependent variable, the analysis revealed that NLRP3 at 24 weeks is an independent risk factor for GDM with statistical significance (P < 0.05) as detailed in Table 4. Based on the Odds Ratio (OR) value, we constructed a combined predictive model equation: Log(P) = 2.049 + 0.082*NLRP3. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test yielded a result of X²=8.917, P = 0.349, indicating that the model fits well and is suitable for predicting the risk of GDM.

Combination of NLRP3, IL-1Ra, and TBP-2 to predict the ROC curve of GDM at 24 weeks of pregnancy

The results of the ROC curve analysis are as follows: For NLRP3, the analysis showed an AUC (Area Under the Curve) value of 0.720 (95% Confidence Interval: 0.630–0.809, P < 0.001) with a sensitivity of 73.1% and a specificity of 63.9%, resulting in a Youden index of 0.370. For IL-1Ra, the AUC value was 0.630 (95% CI: 0.570–0.698, P < 0.050) with a sensitivity of 43.1% and a specificity of 74.6%, yielding a Youden index of 0.177. For TBP-2, the AUC value was 0.662 (95% CI: 0.570–0.754, P < 0.001) with a sensitivity of 55.6% and a specificity of 77.8%, and a Youden index of 0.334. When considering combinations of biomarkers, the combination of NLRP3 and IL-1Ra demonstrated an AUC value of 0.704 (95% CI: 0.613–0.795, P < 0.001) with a sensitivity of 52.2% and a specificity of 86.9%, and a Youden index of 0.391. The combination of TBP-2 and IL-1Ra had an AUC value of 0.658 (95% CI: 0.564–0.753, P < 0.001) with a sensitivity of 47.8% and a specificity of 85.2%, and a Youden index of 0.330. The combination of TBP-2 and NLRP3 yielded an AUC value of 0.695 (95% CI: 0.603–0.786, P < 0.001) with a sensitivity of 68.7% and a specificity of 65.6%, and a Youden index of 0.343. Finally, when all three markers were used in combination, the AUC value was 0.696 (95% CI: 0.604–0.788, P < 0.001) with a sensitivity of 65.7% and a specificity of 73.8%, and a Youden index of 0.395. Discussion

Although there’s no foolproof way to prevent or cure gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) at the moment, the current approach focuses on reducing known risk factors [5] and maintaining strict control over blood glucose levels [6]. This study found that women with GDM had higher pre-pregnancy Body Mass Index (BMI) than those without GDM. Research indicates that among Asian women, the incidence of GDM is 13.78% for those with a pre-pregnancy BMI over 30, 10.22% for a BMI over 25, and 6.09% for a BMI over 20 [5]. Moreover, gestational hypertension is considered a significant risk factor for GDM. Previous research has shown that a key pathophysiological feature of gestational hypertension is increased oxidative stress, marked by an excess production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [7]. Recently, the role of placental oxidative stress in the pathology of GDM has gained attention, primarily due to mitochondrial fragmentation and damage caused by high blood sugar leading to an excessive oxidative stress response [8]. Thus, pregnant women with gestational hypertension are more prone to develop GDM.

This study also delved into the impact of GDM on perinatal infant weight, finding that newborns in the GDM group were significantly heavier than those in the normal group. This could be directly linked to the hyperglycemic intrauterine environment experienced by fetuses of mothers with GDM. In a high glucose state, the fetus may undergo accelerated growth, increasing the risk of macrosomia and potentially leading to premature birth [9]. This underscores the importance of GDM management not just for the mother’s health but also for the newborn’s health and development.

Furthermore, the study explored the roles and interrelationships of IL-1Ra, NLRP3, and TBP-2 in GDM. IL-1Ra, an important inflammatory response regulator, has been identified as a key early diagnostic marker for Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) along with other markers like leptin and renin [10]. Importantly, IL-1Ra plays a critical role in balancing immune responses and controlling excessive inflammation [11] by inhibiting the IL-1-induced inflammatory process. In the pathology of GDM, inflammation plays a leading role. Our research found that IL-1Ra levels in pregnant women with GDM trend upwards during pregnancy but decline after birth as glucose metabolism disorders and inflammatory states alleviate. NLRP3, a cytoplasmic complex that regulates inflammation by activating caspase-1 and promoting the conversion of pro-IL-1β to mature IL-1β[12, 13], showed a downward trend during pregnancy, possibly related to the blocking action of IL-1Ra and improvements in inflammatory responses through other mechanisms. Meanwhile, TBP-2, an important regulator of redox reactions, participates in oxidative stress responses, interacting with NLRP3 [14, 15]. TBP-2 also showed a downward trend during pregnancy. By 36 weeks of gestation, the positive correlation among IL-1Ra, NLRP3, and TBP-2 indicates their interactive roles and pathways in inflammation. This interaction could involve the participation of IL-1 in the NLRP3 activation process and a complex network where IL-1β activity is regulated by IL-1Ra [16, 17]. Therefore, while NLRP3, IL-1Ra, and TBP-2 each play distinct roles in regulating inflammation, their mutual influence highlights the role of a broader inflammatory response network in the onset and development of GDM. Especially in the late stages of pregnancy, the relationships among these markers might be influenced by physiological changes during gestation and changes in gene expression regulation, thus exhibiting a positive correlation at 36 weeks.

This study notably focused on the relationship between serum levels of NLRP3, IL-1Ra, TBP-2 at 24 weeks of gestation and newborn weight. Correlation analysis revealed a positive correlation between increased levels of NLRP3 and TBP-2 and higher newborn weight, while an increase in IL-1Ra levels was inversely related to newborn weight. Based on these findings, a regression equation was formulated: Newborn weight = 3300.08 − 0.03 * IL-1Ra + 4.15 * NLRP3 + 0.05 * TBP-2. However, the explanatory power of this model (R²=0.062) suggests it accounts for only 6.2% of the variation in weight, potentially due to the small sample size of this study. Thus, to enhance the accuracy of newborn weight prediction and the detection rate of macrosomia, future studies need to expand the sample size and further refine the linear regression analysis. This would not only allow for a more precise exploration of the relationships between these biomarkers and newborn weight but also provide stronger support for preventing and managing GDM and its related complications.

In this study, binary logistic regression analysis was utilized to investigate whether the levels of NLRP3, IL-1Ra, TBP-2 at 24 weeks could serve as risk factors for the onset of GDM. The results indicated that NLRP3 alone showed good sensitivity as a predictor of GDM but had relatively low specificity with an AUC of 0.720. When combined with IL-1Ra, the specificity improved but sensitivity decreased. By contrast, the combined prediction of TBP-2 and IL-1Ra showed the lowest sensitivity for GDM, and the combination of TBP-2 and NLRP3 was not ideal in terms of both sensitivity and specificity. When all three markers were used together to predict GDM, no significant improvement in sensitivity and specificity was observed. These findings highlight the potential value of NLRP3 in predicting GDM, though its specificity is low and needs to be used in conjunction with other markers like IL-1Ra to enhance predictive accuracy. While IL-1Ra has higher specificity, its sensitivity alone is insufficient. Therefore, to improve the detection rate of GDM, clinicians should consider the combined use of these biomarkers to select the best prediction strategy. This study further validates the value of IL-1Ra in predicting GDM and suggests the potential to enhance GDM detection rates by optimizing biomarker combinations.

Conclusion

In summary, this research demonstrates the potential of NLRP3, IL-1Ra, and TBP-2 as biomarkers for early screening of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), facilitating early identification and prevention. The article presents both strengths and limitations.

This approach aims to refine diagnostic criteria and improve intervention strategies for managing GDM, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes.
