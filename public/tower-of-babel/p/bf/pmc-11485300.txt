Development and assessment of analytic methods to improve the measurement of cognition in longitudinal studies of aging through the use of substudies with comprehensive neuropsychological testing
Authors: Emma Nichols, Richard N. Jones, Alden L. Gross, Shabina Hayat, Paola Zaninotto, Jinkook Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe Health and Retirement Study International Partner Surveys (HRS IPS) have rich longitudinal data, but the brevity of cognitive batteries is a limitation.METHODSWe used data from a substudy of the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) administering detailed cognitive assessments with the Harmonized Cognitive Assessment Protocol (ELSA‐HCAP) (N = 1273) to inform approaches for estimating cognition in ELSA (N = 11,213). We compared two novel approaches: confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)‐ and regression‐based prediction.RESULTSCompared to estimates from the full HCAP battery, estimated cognitive functioning derived using regression models or CFA had high correlations (regression: r = 0.85 [95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.83 to 0.87]; CFA: r = 0.83 [95% CI: 0.81 to 0.85]) and reasonable mean squared error (regression: 0.25 [0.22 to 0.27]; CFA: 0.29 [0.26 to 0.32]) in held‐out data. The use of additional items from waves 7 to 9 improved performance.DISCUSSIONBoth approaches are recommended for future research; the similarity in approaches may be due to the brevity of available cognitive assessments in ELSA.Highlights Estimates of cognitive functioning informed by English Longitudinal Study of Ageing‐Harmonized Cognitive Assessment Protocol (ELSA‐HCAP) data had an adequate performance.Standard errors were smaller for associations with example risks when using measures informed by ELSA‐HCAP.Performance was better when including additional cognitive measures available in waves 7 to 9.Conceptual advantages to the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) approach were not important in practice due to the brevity of the ELSA cognitive battery.

BACKGROUND

Nationally representative longitudinal studies of aging, such as the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) and the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), are a valuable resource for research on cognitive aging and dementia given the duration of longitudinal follow‐up, large sample sizes, minimal exclusion criteria, and availability of data on a broad array of potential risk and protective factors. 1 , 2 Although biennial assessments over the course of decades allow for the detailed modeling of decline in cognitive functioning over a long period, the available cognitive batteries are typically brief due to the size and scope of these studies. The lack of detailed cognitive assessments limits the precision and utility of outcome measures for longitudinal studies.

To enrich existing data, studies have administered more detailed cognitive assessments among a subsample of participants. The HRS International Partner Surveys (HRS IPS) include ELSA and many other longitudinal studies of aging modeled after the HRS in countries around the world. Many of the HRS IPS, including ELSA, have designed substudies using the Harmonized Cognitive Assessment Protocol (HCAP) to assess cognition and dementia. 3 , 4 , 5 The HCAP battery includes detailed assessments of cognition based on over 45 different cognitive test items covering cognitive domains including memory, executive functioning, language/fluency, and visuospatial functioning. Prior work has shown that the battery has adequate measurement properties across settings. 6 , 7 However, data from substudies using the HCAP battery are currently only available at either one or two time points, and the sample sizes are much smaller than the broader HRS IPS studies.

Despite limitations, the collection of data on subsamples of HRS IPS using the HCAP battery provides opportunities to leverage the richer HCAP data to inform the methods and approaches for the quantification of cognition using available measures in the HRS IPS. Most existing studies in the HRS IPS on cognitive functioning either use individual cognitive tests as outcome variables or calculate summary measures based on the sum of all available cognitive tests or the average of z‐scores from individual cognitive tests. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Use of innovative approaches to incorporate HCAP data into the estimation of cognitive functioning in the HRS IPS could lead to improvements in estimates. Furthermore, data from the HRS IPS substudies implementing the HCAP battery can be used as a reference standard to evaluate and compare the performance of both existing and novel approaches for the estimation of cognitive functioning in the HRS IPS. Such comparisons could form the basis for evidence‐based recommendations on the best methods and approaches for the quantification of cognitive functioning in these surveys.

In this study, we use data from the ELSA‐HCAP substudy to develop novel approaches to estimating summary scores of cognitive functioning in the ELSA study using either regression‐based prediction or latent variable harmonization techniques based on confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) models. We compare these approaches to each other and to two existing, commonly used alternatives. We seek to evaluate (1) comparisons between reference standard cognitive factor scores based on the full HCAP battery and estimates from both novel and existing approaches to the estimation of cognition, (2) the impact of using different approaches to the estimation of cognition on observed associations between cognition and accepted risk factors for cognitive functioning, and (3) the impact of using the different approaches on estimated trajectories of cognitive functioning.

METHODS

Samples

The English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) is a nationally representative sample of the non‐institutionalized population of adults 50 years and older. The study began in 2002 with an original sample of over 12,000 eligible individuals and their spouses; participants were interviewed every 2 years through 2019 (waves 1 to 9). 1 Refresher samples were added in waves 3, 4, 6, 7, and 9. The ELSA‐HCAP study was implemented between ELSA waves 8 and 9 and included individuals from the main ELSA study who were aged 65 years and older at the start of fieldwork and had completed an ELSA interview in person at wave 7 or 8. 5 Sampling was stratified by cognitive status to ensure that the ELSA‐HCAP sample contained sufficient numbers of individuals with low levels of cognitive functioning. All participants in ELSA and ELSA‐HCAP provided written informed consent, and study protocols were approved by the National Research Ethics Service.

In our study, we included all individuals in the ELSA‐HCAP sample (N = 1273) and all longitudinal data from participants who entered the ELSA cohort at wave 1 (N = 11,213). We excluded data from wave 6 because animal naming, an important cognitive test in the ELSA survey, was not administered in this wave. In this study, we expect that missing data due to the exclusion of wave 6 will be missing completely at random (MCAR) as missingness was based on study design. Therefore, exclusion of data from wave 6 will not induce bias. We also excluded all records from spouses under 50 years of age and those without any objective cognitive testing in the main ELSA survey, including those with proxy interviews (2202/62,773 observations).

Cognitive tests

The ELSA study included a limited number of cognitive measures across all (or almost all) waves considered in this study: 10‐word immediate and delayed word recall, orientation to time (month, day of month, year, day of week), and animal naming (all waves except wave 6). Additional cognitive measures were added in wave 7 and thereafter: counting backwards from 20, Serial 7s, object naming (scissors and cactus), and naming the monarch, prime minister, and US president. Cognitive tests available in waves 1 to 3 or tests included as ad hoc additions to other waves were not considered in this study, as these tests have limited utility for the estimation of cognitive decline longitudinally in the ELSA sample. The ELSA‐HCAP study included a much larger battery of over 45 cognitive test items assessing a range of cognitive domains including orientation, memory, language/fluency, executive functioning, and visuospatial functioning. The full list of included cognitive tests is available in Supplemental Materials 1.

Missing data in the ELSA‐HCAP study were previously imputed using an approach designed to be consistent across studies implementing the HCAP battery. 14 , 15 Data on cognitive testing were imputed using auxiliary information from demographics, socioeconomic indicators, measures of general health, prior cognitive data, and other items from cognitive assessments and informant reports using chained equations with fully conditional specification. Missing data on cognitive tests in the broader ELSA study were imputed for this study using random hot deck imputation based on information from non‐missing cognitive tests. 16

Other variables

To describe and stratify the data, we used information on self‐reported demographic characteristics, including age, gender, and educational attainment (some high school/high school equivalent/some college/college and above). Additionally, in Phase 1C described below in Section 2.5.1, we used data from a variety of other hypothesized or established risk factors for cognitive functioning, 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 including self‐reported depression symptomology (using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale‐8 questionnaire), self‐reported hearing difficulties, social isolation, 21 self‐reported doctor diagnosis of diabetes and heart attack, difficulties with seven instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs), 22 and self‐rated memory and health. We also used data on objective walking speed and olfaction. Information on risk factors was collected either at the ELSA‐HCAP visit or at the ELSA recruitment wave (wave 7 or 8) for all HCAP participants. Additional details on the administration of objective measures and the response categories used for categorical variables are available in Supplemental Materials 2.

Data from the ELSA‐Harmonized Cognitive Assessment Protocol (ELSA‐HCAP) has not been previously used to inform the construction of summary cognitive measures. Though performance was similar for a comparison summary score, standard errors for associations with example risk factors were smaller using estimates informed by ELSA‐HCAP. Estimation of a reference standard measure of cognitive functioning in ELSA‐HCAP study based on all cognitive tests

We used all available cognitive test items in the ELSA‐HCAP study to estimate latent cognitive functioning using a CFA model, which is consistent with a graded‐response model for discrete items and a factor analysis model for continuous response items. 23 , 24 These models explicitly account for measurement error and allow for cognitive test items and response options to have varying difficulties. 25 Because some of the individual cognitive test items come from the same general cognitive test (eg, immediate and delayed word recall), they may be more closely correlated with each other than with other cognitive tests. Therefore, to account for this structure and improve model fit, we modeled these “methods factors” as separate latent traits, uncorrelated with underlying latent cognitive functioning. For example, immediate and delayed recall of the Brave Man story both load on a “Brave Man” methods factor to describe stronger intercorrelations between these items due to the shared story prompt. Upon addition of these methods factors, model fit was acceptable. We used a weighted least square mean and variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimator to obtain model fit indices and a Bayesian estimator for final models. The mean of 500 plausible values was used to estimate factor scores from this model and all subsequent CFA models. CFA diagrams and additional information on the included methods factors and model fit are available in Supplemental Materials 3, 4, and 5.

Estimates of cognition from CFA models have been developed and widely used across the different substudies that implement the HCAP battery. 7 , 26 , 27 Therefore, we used factor estimates of general cognitive performance from this model as the reference standard measure of cognitive functioning and compared all alternative measures to estimates from this model.

Statistical analysis overview

We first described the characteristics of the ELSA and ELSA‐HCAP samples using means and standard deviations for continuous variables and proportions for binary and categorical variables. The main analytic approach used in this study was divided into two phases (Figure 1). In the first phase, we used the ELSA‐HCAP data to develop novel approaches to estimate cognition using only cognitive test items available in the full ELSA study, as described below. We compared the performance of estimates derived using these approaches and comparison approaches to the reference standard cognitive scores from the full ELSA‐HCAP battery. In the second phase, we estimated cognitive functioning in the full ELSA study using different approaches and compared estimated trajectories of cognitive functioning.

Phase 1A: Development of novel approaches to estimating cognition

Across all analyses, we compared four approaches to estimating cognitive functioning: two novel approaches informed by the ELSA‐HCAP sample, and two comparison approaches, defined independently of the reference standard measure in ELSA‐HCAP:

Novel Approaches: CFA: The CFA approach was based on extensions of the CFA model used to estimate our reference measure of cognitive functioning in ELSA‐HCAP. To estimate underlying latent cognition in individuals with information on the subset of cognitive test items available in the main ELSA study, we used fixed‐parameter calibration (sometimes referred to as item banking). 7 , 28 To implement this approach, we first estimated a CFA model in the cross‐sectional ELSA‐HCAP data using all cognitive test items in the ELSA‐HCAP sample and saved item parameters. For each of the novel approaches, we estimated two versions of the cognitive scores: one with cognitive test items available across all waves of ELSA and one adding the expanded battery of cognitive test items available only in waves 7 to 9. However, one advantage of the CFA model approach is that it allows for estimating comparable scores when tests change over time, 29 incorporating information from cognitive test items in a subset of waves. Therefore, for the Phase 2 analysis of longitudinal data described below in Section 2.5.2, we incorporated the additional items from waves 7 to 9 into our primary CFA model. Coefficient estimates for the regression and CFA models help describe the weighting of the different items for the two procedures and are available in Supplemental Materials 7.

Because the HCAP battery did not include three items (counting backward from 20, naming the monarch, naming the US president) included in waves 7 to 9 of the ELSA data, we supplemented the ELSA‐HCAP data with ELSA data from these three cognitive test items before estimating scores using each of the preceding approaches. We used the observations from the main ELSA recruitment wave (wave 7 or 8) for each ELSA‐HCAP participant. Because most participants were recruited following wave 8 interviews (97%), the time interval between most interviews is less than 2 years. Given anticipated rates of cognitive decline in community‐based samples, 30 we would not expect much change in cognitive functioning during this time period; we therefore treat the data as if they had been collected at a single time point.

Phase 1B: Comparison of novel approaches to estimating cognition to the reference standard measure of cognitive functioning

To directly compare the proposed approaches for the estimation of cognitive functioning to our reference standard measure in the ELSA‐HCAP data, we randomly split the ELSA‐HCAP data into training (two‐thirds of the data) and testing (one‐third of the data) subsamples. We estimated models in the training data and then applied model parameters to testing data using the proposed novel approaches. We visualized correlations between the different approaches and reference standard cognitive functioning and calculated correlations and mean squared error (MSE). We re‐estimated models and recalculated performance characteristics using 500 replications with 500 different random splits of the training and testing data to ensure results were not sensitive to a specific random split. In some replications, the Bayesian CFA models did not converge; we therefore discarded these replications (53/500 replications for models using cognitive test items available across all waves of ELSA, 58/500 replications for models using cognitive test items available in waves 7 to 9 of ELSA).

Phase 1C: Comparison of estimated associations between example risk factors and reference standard cognitive functioning as well as estimates of cognitive functioning from novel and comparison approaches

Additionally, we assessed whether the use of different measures of cognitive functioning impacted estimates of associations between cognition and potential risk factors for cognitive functioning. We compared estimates of associations using the reference standard measure of cognition in ELSA‐HCAP to estimates using each proposed measure of cognitive functioning in the 500 testing subsample replicates. To quantify associations, we used linear regression models adjusting for age and gender. We did not control for a larger covariate set, as our goal was not to focus on eliminating all potential confounding but rather to compare associations across measures. Estimated associations from models using our reference standard measure of cognitive functioning are available in Supplemental Materials 8. For each measure, we estimated the mean absolute value of bias across the 500 replications in estimated associations (βreferencestandard−βalternative) and considered whether bias was on average conservative (toward the null hypothesis of no association) or anti‐conservative (further from the null hypothesis of no association). We also computed a ratio of standard errors, comparing the standard errors from the regression model with the reference estimate of cognitive functioning to the mean of the standard errors from the 500 replicates using each alternative estimate of cognitive functioning. We summarized these metrics across 10 example risk factors: educational attainment, depressive symptomology, walking speed, olfactory performance, self‐rated memory and health, self‐reported social isolation, and self‐reported history of diabetes and heart attack.

Phase 2: Trajectories in ELSA using alternative cognitive measures

We compared the four approaches to estimating cognition – the two novel and two comparison approaches – in the main ELSA sample using the longitudinal ELSA data. While there is no reference standard measure of cognitive functioning in the full ELSA sample, we compared estimated within‐individual trajectories of each estimate of cognitive functioning across time to assess convergent validity. We used linear regression models estimated with generalized estimating equations and an exchangeable correlation structure to account for repeated observations. We adjusted for gender and baseline age using a restricted cubic spline (estimated with three degrees of freedom). To enable comparisons of the relative weighting of the individual items used in the different summary measures included in the study, we used the same model structure to estimate mean trajectories of individual items, both for items included across all waves and items added in waves 7 to 9.

We included results from two sensitivity analyses to longitudinal comparisons. First, we added predictions from the regression models made without first imputing cognitive data in ELSA. Leaving out the imputations had the effect of dropping all records with any missing data on cognitive test items. Second, because the CFA‐based approach is the only approach that utilizes data from items added in waves 7 to 9, we additionally compared results to estimates from the same CFA model without including data from the added items.

Because the focus of the analyses was to predict cognitive functioning for individuals included in the sample, analyses did not use survey weights. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.2.2, with the exception of CFA models, which were estimated using Mplus version 8. Analytic code is available at: https://github.com/enichols94/ELSA‐CogMeasurement/tree/main.

RESULTS

Due to differences in recruitment criteria, the ELSA‐HCAP sample (ages 65+) was older (mean: 75.8 years; SD: 7.0 years) than the full ELSA sample (ages 50+) (mean 69.0 years; SD: 9.7) (Table 1). The ELSA‐HCAP sample also had lower educational attainment than the full ELSA sample, with 19.0% having some college and 11.5% having a college education and above in the ELSA‐HCAP sample compared with 19.5% and 13.5% in the main ELSA study, respectively. Across the majority of cognitive tests, participants in the ELSA‐HCAP sample performed worse on average than participants in the main ELSA sample.

Correlations between estimates of cognitive functioning based on novel approaches informed by ELSA‐HCAP data, and reference standard cognitive functioning were generally strong. The measure with the lowest correlation was the word recall score (r = 0.76 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.72, 0.79), one of the commonly used comparison approaches (Figure 2). The sum score comparison measure had a higher correlation with reference standard cognitive functioning (r = 0.84; 95% CI: 0.82, 0.87). Correlations were not meaningfully different comparing the sum score to estimates from the CFA model approach (r = 0.83; 95% CI: 0.81, 0.86) or the regression‐based prediction approach (r = 0.85; 95% CI: 0.83, 0.87), but larger discrepancies existed between the models that added measures only available in waves 7 to 9 compared to the models that relied only on measures available across all waves. For example, the correlation between the CFA‐based approach using cognitive test items available across all waves and reference standard cognitive functioning was 0.83 (95% CI: 0.81, 0.85); however, this correlation was 0.88 (95% CI: 0.86, 0.90) when adding the additional cognitive test items in waves 7 to 9. Patterns in MSE were similar to those observed using the correlation as a measure of performance. There were no meaningful differences in MSE across age groups, gender, or educational attainment categories (Supplemental Materials 9).

The absolute magnitude of bias in associations between example risk factors and cognitive functioning estimated using the different approaches considered ranged between 0 and 0.41 (Figure 3). When averaging across the example risk factors included, mean bias in estimates of differences in cognitive functioning was between 0.1 and 0.2 SD units, which may be meaningful for risk factors with weak associations with cognitive functioning but would be less meaningful if associations were strong. Overall differences in mean bias across measures were small; the highest mean bias was observed for the regression‐based prediction results using measures from all waves (0.16) and the lowest mean bias was observed for the CFA model using measures from waves 7 to 9 (0.12). Across the different associations, in most cases the bias was conservative, but we did observe anti‐conservative bias in 13% to 26% of associations across the approaches considered. Examination of the ratios of standard errors showed that standard errors for associations using approaches that did not consider information from the HCAP sample were higher compared to standard errors for coefficients based on our reference standard measure of cognitive functioning (ratio for word recall: 1.16; sum score: 1.15) (Figure 3). In comparison, standard errors for models using the regression‐based prediction measures slightly underestimated the standard errors from regression models using reference standard cognition (ratios: 0.92 using cognitive items from all waves; 0.97 using cognitive items from waves 7 to 9). Standard errors for models using estimates from CFA‐based approaches were very close but slightly higher than those from models using reference standard cognitive outcome measure (ratios: 1.02 using cognitive items from all waves; 1.03 using cognitive items from waves 7 to 9).

DISCUSSION

Estimates of cognitive functioning using data from the full ELSA study had reasonable correspondence when compared to reference standard estimates in the ELSA‐HCAP data and showed convergent validity in longitudinal analyses in ELSA. Across analyses, we observed a slight benefit (higher correlations with reference standard, lower MSE, less bias in regressions with risk factors) of incorporating the additional cognitive measures available in ELSA waves 7 to 9 (serial 7s, counting backwards, naming objects, and naming leaders), illustrating the value of expanding cognitive testing when possible. Incorporating data from ELSA‐HCAP in the construction of cognitive measures did not yield meaningful improvements in observed bias in estimated scores or estimates of associations between cognitive functioning and known risk factors but did reduce the standard errors of regression coefficients, which may be important for identifying risk and protective factors with small to moderate effect sizes. For example, estimated effect sizes for significant associations in a recent systematic review on air pollution and cognition were small (range: −0.63 to −0.11). 31 Of the 12 significant associations, 2 (17%) would no longer be significant if standard errors were inflated to the same extent observed for the sum score compared to the gold standard measure in this study (by a factor of 1.15); in comparison, statistical significance would not change using the observed inflation factor for the CFA score (1.02).

Previous studies in ELSA predominantly used individual measures of cognitive functioning, including word recall or animal naming. 12 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 Other studies took different approaches to estimating overall cognitive functioning, including using sum scores, principal components, or exploratory factor analysis. 38 , 39 , 40 We observed greater correspondence between our gold‐standard factor scores and summary scores incorporating all available cognitive test items, in comparison to word recall alone. Results highlight differences when using summary scores versus individual cognitive test items but do not suggest that individual cognitive test items should not be used. Rather, results emphasize that findings using individual tests should be interpreted with respect to the more specific cognitive domain or domains assessed.

Taken together, results illustrated the potential benefits of expanding cognitive batteries available when possible. Estimates from models using cognitive test items added in waves 7 to 9 had higher correlations with reference standard cognitive factor scores, lower MSE, and less bias in estimates of associations with example risk factors. Findings are likely consequences of increases in the reliability of estimates, which are predicted by classical test theory and the Spearman–Brown Prophecy Formula to occur with the addition of items. 41 , 42 Although differences were relatively small in magnitude, of the seven items added to the battery in waves 7 to 9, six had low variability (most respondents answered correctly), meaning these items likely did not influence scores for most respondents with average to high cognitive functioning. Improvements in performance may be larger if more high‐quality items are added. However, detailed examination of findings also highlights the importance of careful examination of individual items. For example, scores on the item for “naming the prime minister” increased dramatically between waves 8 and 9, in line with the timing of the UK general election (December 2019). Given that trends in this item are likely to be swayed by contemporaneous events in politics, it may be less useful for the estimation of trajectories over time, a key goal of the ELSA study.

Estimates from regression‐ and CFA‐based approaches to using ELSA‐HCAP data to inform estimates of cognitive functioning were similar, with smaller notable differences. Although correlations and MSE indicated better correspondence between regression‐based predictions and our reference standard measure of cognitive functioning, both measures had similar levels of bias in associations with known risk factors, and standard errors of associations were more accurate when using the measure derived from CFA models. Better concordance between the reference standard estimates of cognitive functioning and the estimates from the regression‐based prediction approach may be attributable to greater flexibility in the regression model. Conversely, the CFA modeling approach is less flexible, but its functional form is theoretically driven by the measurement structure of the gold‐standard cognitive scores. These theoretical underpinnings may explain why, despite lower concordance with the gold‐standard cognitive measure, we observed that (1) bias in associations with CFA‐based estimates of associations with example risk factors was similar to bias in associations using regression‐based estimates and (2) standard errors from estimates of associations using CFA‐based estimates were closer to what was observed using the reference standard cognitive measure, whereas standard errors for regression‐based estimates were on average too small, potentially increasing risk of Type I error.

Despite slight differences in performance, the CFA‐based approach has conceptual advantages compared with the regression‐based approach. In addition to using a model structure conceptually analogous to what is used in the reference standard measure of cognitive functioning, CFA‐based estimates of cognition also have corresponding measures of uncertainty. Although not incorporated into the present analyses, uncertainty estimates can inform subsequent analyses; for example, researchers may exclude observations with low reliability. Additionally, CFA models can be used to equate cognitive test batteries with different but overlapping items to generate comparable estimates of cognition when cognitive tests change over time. 29 However, in this study, CFA models failed to produce equivalent estimates of cognitive functioning when the cognitive battery expanded in wave 7 due to the limited precision of available cognitive test items. This finding further emphasizes the importance of keeping the cognitive battery as similar as possible across waves. Although incorporating additional tests will improve precision in later waves, utility may be limited in longitudinal analyses when existing cognitive batteries are small, given challenges with estimating comparable cognitive scores over time. Future research should investigate the level of precision needed to ensure the comparability of CFA‐based estimates of cognitive functioning from studies with changing cognitive batteries. If comparability can be guaranteed, the CFA‐based approach offers important advantages.

Despite the conceptual advantages of the CFA‐based approach, the regression‐ and CFA‐based approaches performed similarly in most analyses. Ultimately, all approaches, including the sum score used for comparison, simply re‐weight cognitive test data in the main ELSA survey using different sets of weights, either chosen a priori or using models. Due to the small number of cognitive test items available in the main ELSA survey, potential differences in the relative weighting of different cognitive test items may have been limited, 43 increasing the similarity of results from different approaches. If a larger number of cognitive test items were available in the main ELSA survey, there may have been more separation in the observed performance of different approaches.

Limitations should be considered. First, longitudinal analyses focused on the main ELSA sample in Phase 2 did not have a reference standard measure for comparison. Instead, we looked at trajectories across time to evaluate convergent validity. Simulation approaches are needed to directly assess the accuracy of longitudinal trajectories against a reference standard, given that the HCAP substudies were developed because detailed cognitive assessments are typically not available in broader longitudinal studies. It is also important to note that we assumed that information from the ELSA‐HCAP sample of participants aged 65+ could be extrapolated to the full ELSA sample aged 50+, in line with previous dementia algorithms derived using study subsamples in the HRS. 44 While there may be some concerns about extrapolating to younger age groups from models developed in older populations where pathologies and co‐pathologies are more common, dementia prevalence is still low (<5%) among the younger participants (<75 years old) included. 45 Furthermore, within the ELSA‐HCAP sample we did not find heterogeneity in performance metrics across age groups, providing some evidence for age invariance. Nonetheless, to avoid extrapolation, researchers could consider limiting analyses to respondents ≥65 years.

Additionally, we did not estimate cognitive scores for wave 6 due to the exclusion of animal naming. If the exclusion of wave 6 was expected to lead to bias in future studies, imputation of animal naming based on interrelationships of cognitive items in other waves could be used, though imputation assumptions would need to be critically examined. Current analyses also excluded participants with proxy reports of cognitive functioning. Although the main ELSA survey has a relatively small number of participants without information on objective cognitive functioning, those with proxy interviews are likely those with the lowest levels of cognitive functioning, and the proportion of respondents with proxy interviews is higher in other HRS IPS. 46 , 47 Future work should focus on expanding the methods in the current analysis to include proxy respondents as well. Finally, we were only able to estimate scores for general cognitive functioning, not specific cognitive domains, due to the brevity of the available data in the main ELSA survey. This limitation highlights the importance of continuing to conduct substudies using the HCAP or other more detailed batteries, as the richer cognitive batteries allow for more detailed analyses, including the estimation of cognitive domains.

Overall, estimates from regression‐ and CFA‐based prediction approaches incorporating information from the ELSA‐HCAP substudy had adequate performance compared to reference standard estimates of cognitive functioning. Though performance was also similar to our comparison sum score measure, smaller standard errors in subsequent regression models using either regression‐ or CFA‐derived measures may be important in identifying risk factors with small to moderate effect sizes. While the CFA‐based approach has conceptual advantages over the regression‐based methods, due to the brevity of the available battery in the main ELSA study, many of these advantages were not important in practice. Given findings, we recommend studies utilize one of these approaches, but without strong preference between the two. Results also emphasized that consistency across waves is key when cognitive batteries are small and that increases in cognitive batteries are potentially valuable when possible given time and resource constraints. Design choices that prioritize these goals may further increase the utility of the available data from substudies using the HCAP battery and the precision and performance of estimates of cognitive functioning.
