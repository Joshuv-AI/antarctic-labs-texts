Viral Illness and Cardiovascular Disease

Acute viral respiratory infection is increasingly recognized as an important contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. While historically viewed primarily as pulmonary illnesses, infections such as influenza and COVID-19 have systemic inflammatory and thrombotic effects that can destabilize underlying cardiovascular disease (CVD). Emerging mechanistic, epidemiologic, and trial data now support a strong association between viral illness and adverse cardiovascular outcomes, including myocardial infarction, heart failure, stroke, and cardiovascular death.1, 2, 3 These observations have prompted increasing recognition of adult immunization as a potential CVD risk mitigation strategy in cardiovascular care and informed the development of the 2025 ACC (American College of Cardiology) Concise Clinical Guidance on Adult Immunization in Patients With Cardiovascular Disease.4

Patients with established CVD are particularly vulnerable during episodes of acute viral infection.5 Viral respiratory illnesses provoke widespread inflammatory and neurohormonal activation that can destabilize underlying cardiovascular pathology.6 Systemic immune activation can lead to release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, endothelial dysfunction, hypercoagulability, and increased sympathetic stimulation. Collectively, these processes increase myocardial oxygen demand while simultaneously promoting plaque instability and thrombosis, thereby increasing the likelihood of acute coronary syndromes. Viral infection may additionally exert direct myocardial depressive effects, contributing to worsening heart failure and hemodynamic instability.

Viral respiratory infections have also been associated with increased risk of stroke, acute kidney injury, adverse glycemic events, and functional decline, particularly among older adults and individuals with multimorbidity. This reinforces the concept that viral respiratory infection should be considered a systemic cardiometabolic stressor rather than an isolated pulmonary process.

A strong, bidirectional relationship exists with cardiovascular comorbidities and risks of acute viral infections. Patients with preexisting cardiovascular conditions experience disproportionately greater risk of infection, as well as infections that are more severe in nature and often complicate underlying cardiovascular comorbidities.

Among the strongest evidence supporting this relationship are self-controlled case series evaluating temporal associations between viral infection and myocardial infarction. In a landmark study, Kwong et al7 reported that the incidence of myocardial infarction within 7 days of laboratory-confirmed influenza infection was approximately 6-fold higher than during control periods before or after infection. Similar findings have subsequently been replicated across European and U.S. populations, and comparable associations have also been observed with COVID-19 infection.3 Collectively, these findings support a biologically plausible and temporally consistent association between acute viral infection and cardiovascular events.

Randomized clinical trials further support vaccination as a cardiovascular preventive strategy. The IAMI (Influenza Vaccination After Myocardial Infarction) trial randomized >2,500 patients hospitalized with myocardial infarction or undergoing coronary angiography to receive influenza vaccines or placebo.8 Influenza vaccination was associated with a statistically significant reduction in the composite outcome of all-cause death, myocardial infarction, or stent thrombosis at 12 months. Additional evidence has emerged among patients with heart failure. In the IVVE (Influenza Vaccine to Prevent Adverse Vascular Events) trial, influenza vaccination did not significantly reduce the primary endpoint across the full study period; however, during peak influenza circulation, vaccination was associated with reductions in cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, and nonfatal stroke.9 These findings support the concept that cardiovascular benefits from vaccination may be greatest during periods of heightened viral activity.

Despite growing evidence and updated guideline recommendations, substantial implementation gaps remain. Vaccination uptake among patients with CVD continues to be suboptimal, including among patients enrolled in cardiovascular clinical trials. Analyses from the PARADIGM-HF (Prospective Comparison of ARNI with ACEI to Determine Impact on Global Mortality and Morbidity in Heart Failure) trial found significant international variability in influenza vaccination rates, with many regions reporting rates <50%.10 Registry data additionally suggest that vaccine refusal rates may be increasing among heart failure populations.

These implementation gaps likely reflect multiple factors, including vaccine hesitancy, fragmented preventive care delivery, and inconsistent recommendations from clinicians and public health authorities. Historically, vaccination has often been considered the responsibility of primary care clinicians. However, many patients with advanced CVD interact frequently with cardiology clinicians, and a cardiologist’s recommendation is a stronger predictor of vaccination uptake.

To inform the cardiovascular community about the central role of vaccination in promoting optimal CVD care, we developed the 2025 ACC Concise Clinical Guidance on Adult Immunization in Patients With Cardiovascular Disease.4 The ACC guidance emphasizes that preventive viral immunization should now be viewed as part of routine cardiovascular care, and it reframes vaccination as a component of cardiovascular risk mitigation. The ACC guidance provides practical recommendations regarding adult immunization in patients with or at elevated risk for CVD. Annual influenza vaccination is strongly recommended for eligible patients. COVID-19 vaccination and updated booster strategies are similarly recommended according to current public health guidance. The document additionally addresses pneumococcal, respiratory syncytial virus, and herpes zoster vaccination, particularly among older adults and those with chronic medical conditions.

Beyond vaccine-specific recommendations, the ACC guidance emphasizes implementation strategies that can be integrated into routine cardiovascular practice.4 These strategies include incorporating vaccination assessment into cardiovascular encounters, improving clinician awareness of vaccine schedules, and leveraging multidisciplinary cardiovascular care teams to reinforce preventive care discussions. The document also highlights the importance of addressing vaccine hesitancy through patient-centered communication and clear discussion of cardiovascular benefits. Framing vaccination within the broader context of cardiovascular risk reduction may further improve patient engagement, particularly among individuals closely connected to their cardiovascular care teams.

In summary, acute viral respiratory infections are increasingly recognized as important triggers for cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. A growing body of evidence shows associations between infections and increased risks of myocardial infarction, heart failure exacerbation, and other adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Randomized clinical trials further suggest that vaccination may reduce cardiovascular risk in vulnerable populations. The 2025 ACC Concise Clinical Guidance on Adult Immunization in Patients With Cardiovascular Disease4 represents an important step toward integrating vaccination into routine cardiovascular preventive care and highlights the expanding role of cardiologists in promoting evidence-based adult immunization. We hope such a resource will provide an important bedrock upon which subsequent updates will allow for clear, actionable recommendations for ACC membership in promoting optimal CV preventative care.
