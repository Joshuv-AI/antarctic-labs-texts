HoloPatient-based simulation education to improve nursing students’ learning motivation and attitude: a mixed methods study
Authors: Yun Kang, Insook Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe landscape of nursing education is rapidly evolving worldwide through the integration of innovative technologies such as mixed reality (MR). While MR-based HoloPatient education shows promise for enhancing nursing education globally, particularly in settings with limited clinical placement opportunities, current research indicates positive effects on nursing students’ learning outcomes. However, no evidence exists that HoloPatient-based education improves students’ learning motivation and attitude. This study aimed to examine nursing students’ learning motivation and attitudes following HoloPatient-based simulation education, and to explore students’ perception of their HoloPatient-based simulation learning experiences.MethodsThis study used an explanatory sequential mixed methods design with a one group post-test only design followed by focus group interviews. This study was conducted from August to September 2023 at a university in South Korea. Quantitative data were collected from 100 third-year nursing students who participated in HoloPatient-based simulation education. Qualitative data were obtained through semi-structured interviews with two focus groups, conducted one week after HoloPatient-based simulation education. Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and comparative analyses. Qualitative data underwent thematic analysis following established protocols.ResultsThis study showed high level of nursing students’ learning motivation (M = 4.42, SD = 0.42) and positive attitude (M = 4.08, SD = 0.50) following HoloPatient-based simulation education. A significant positive correlation was found between learning motivation and attitude (r = .59 or higher, p < .001). Qualitative findings revealed themes of positive experience, influencing factors on HoloPatient-based simulation, and barriers to HoloPatient-based simulation.ConclusionThis exploratory study found high levels of learning motivation and positive attitudes among nursing students following HoloPatient-based simulation education. However, due to the one-group post-test design and small qualitative sample, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence. Further studies are needed to establish its effectiveness with randomized controlled trials or quasi-experimental designs.

Introduction

The landscape of nursing education is rapidly evolving worldwide through the integration of innovative technologies such as mixed reality (MR). MR combines virtual reality (VR) with augmented reality (AR), creating a blend of digital content and the real world without isolating the headset wearer from their physical environment [1, 2]. HoloPatient, an MR-based application, a commercially available holographic standardized patient by GIGXR (Los Angeles, California, US), has been used worldwide. It allows students to observe and interact with a realistic holographic standardized patient in a safe, controlled setting, displaying a wide range of symptoms and conditions [1, 3].

MR-based HoloPatient education shows positive effects on nursing students’ learning outcomes despite the self-reported data [1, 4–6]. Kang and Lee [4] in South Korea reported a significant effect of HoloPatient-based problem-based learning (PBL) on nursing students’ problem-solving abilities and critical thinking dispositions, using the MR. Frost et al. [5] in Australia found that a 2-hour HoloPatient-based simulation education improved nursing students’ clinical judgment and clinical reasoning skills. Ditzel and Collins [1] in New Zealand reported that one-hour clinical reasoning tutorial improved nursing students’ clinical reasoning skills for clinical practicum preparation, using the HoloPatient and the clinical reasoning cycle [7] as a structure.

While MR-based HoloPatient education shows positive effects on nursing students’ learning outcomes, no evidence exists that HoloPatient-based education improves students’ learning motivation and attitude. Motivation and attitude in nursing education are important psychological characteristics that influence learning outcomes [8]. Motivation is the process that inspires individuals to take action in order to achieve goals [9]. A strong motivation for learning has been related to academic achievement, increased learning efficiency, satisfaction, and reduced anxiety in nursing education [10]. Also, attitude plays crucial role in sustaining their willingness and effort to learn [11]. Thus, it needs to investigate whether HoloPatient-based simulation education improves their learning motivation and attitude.

The prevalence of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) has increased due to rapid population aging, affecting approximately 12.9% of adults over 40 and nearly one-third of those over 70 in South Korea [12]. Acute exacerbations of COPD are associated with decreased quality of life [13] and can lead to emergency department (ED) visits and hospitalisations [14]. In the United States in 2010, there were an estimated 1.5 million ED visits and 699,000 COPD hospitalisations with a mean cost of US$8.228 per hospitalisation [15, 16]. Although COPD is common in clinical settings, nursing students have limited opportunities to experience and manage acute exacerbation of COPD due to patient safety considerations and the unpredictable nature of these events. HoloPatient-based simulation provides a standardised and safe environment where students can repeatedly practice caring for patient with acute COPD exacerbation. Students in the immersive realistic situation were able to develop clinical confidence and competence, potentially enhancing their motivation to learn and fostering positive attitudes toward caring for patients with COPD. This study aimed to investigate whether HoloPatient-based simulation education improves South Korean nursing students’ learning motivation and attitudes and to explore students’ perceptions of their HoloPatient-based simulation learning experiences.

Study design

This study used a sequential explanatory mixed methods design that included a one group post-test only design was followed by focus group interviews (FGIs). FGIs in this mixed methods study were chosen to explain the quantitative results and explore nursing students’ perceptions and experiences of HoloPatient-based simulation education [17].

Participants and sample size

For the quantitative component of this study, we determined the sample size for the planned correlation analysis between motivation and attitude scores using G*Power 3.1 software. The power analysis, based on a medium effect size (r =.30) referenced from Padilha et al. [18], with a significance level of 0.05 and power of 85%, calculated a required sample size of 96. A statistical power of 0.85 was selected to optimize the balance between Type I and Type II error protection while maintaining feasibility within institutional constraints. To account for potential participant dropout, we increased the target sample size to 100.

For the quantitative component, we used convenience sampling, recruiting 100 students from among 350 third-year nursing students at a private university in South Korea. Participants (N = 100) were recruited through convenience sampling from students enrolled in the Older Patient Core Nursing Care Lab Practice Course during the second semester of 2023. Inclusion criteria specified no prior exposure to MR-based education to control for previous technology experience.

For the qualitative component, we used purposive sampling to select 10 students from the quantitative sample. These participants were divided into two focus groups (5 students per group) who participated in the quantitative component, considering the participants’ characteristics and findings from quantitative survey. We acknowledge this limited sample size may not have achieved data saturation, potentially affecting the comprehensiveness of our qualitative findings.

HoloPatient-based simulation education intervention

This study adhered to the Healthcare Simulation Standards of Best Practice and followed the Simulation Research Reporting guidelines [19]. This study followed the Simulation Research Reporting guidelines to ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting. In accordance with these guidelines, we provide detailed information about the simulation environment, facilitator training, scenario development, debriefing methods, and evaluation processes. Prior to implementing the HoloPatient-based simulation education, we created the COPD acute exacerbation care module template based on the Healthcare Simulation Standards of Best Practice of INACSL [19] and the HoloPatient case provided GIGXR (Los Angeles, California, US). This module template includes learning objectives, introduction to the situation, practice guidelines, simulation activities, self-performance evaluation, peer evaluation within the group, and self-reflection sheets. We developed the simulation activity in HoloPatient with the signs and symptoms of acute exacerbation of COPD, using the clinical reasoning cycle as a structure [1, 3]. Additionally, the facilitator trained students on how to utilize HoloPatient and HoloLens with safety guidelines. Facilitators were experienced nursing educators with at least 5 years of clinical experience in respiratory care. All facilitators completed comprehensive prior simulation-based education training, including structured debriefing workshops and HoloPatient-specific technical training in HoloPatient-based simulation-education. This training ensured standardised delivery of the simulation experience and effective debriefing practices.

Figure 1 represents the format of the HoloPatient-based simulation session. One week before the HoloPatient-based simulation session, students received preliminary learning on nursing care for older adults with acute exacerbation of COPD. During the pre-briefing phase (20 min), the facilitator explained the learning objectives and the overall procedure to the students using the HoloPatient-based simulation scenario template.

Components of the HoloPatient-based simulation sessions

The simulation running phase (40 min) was conducted as a group-based activity. In each group, one student used the HoloLens to observe the HoloPatient portraying the signs and symptoms of acute exacerbation of COPD for 10 min, while the other student observed via tablet PCs. The groups then examined the situation, identified nursing problems, created nursing goals and intervention plans, and anticipated results, using clinical reasoning cycle structure [3] in the scenario template. At the end of this phase, the facilitator summarized and organized the entire group activity.

Students wrote a self-reflection sheet to reflect on their individual performance following the HoloPatient-based simulation running phase. We developed a self-reflection sheet based on Gibb’s Reflective Cycle [20] to promote nursing students’ deeper understanding and incorporation of learning into knowledge and future practice in the care of acute exacerbation of COPD in older adults [21].

The session concluded with a 20-minute debriefing involving the entire group, utilizing the self-reflection sheet as a structured and easy-to-follow guide [2, 21]. This approach was designed to maximize learning from the simulation experience, encouraging students to critically analyse their performance and develop strategies for improvement in future clinical situations.

Data collection

This study was conducted from August to September 2023. One hundred third-year nursing students from one private university in South Korea were invited to participate in the quantitative component of the study. We provided verbal and written information about the study at information seminars. A convenience sample of 100 third-year nursing students were recruited and completed surveys after HoloPatient-based simulation education.

The FGIs in Korean were conducted approximately one week after HoloPatient-based simulation education. Semi-structured interviews with focus groups were designed to interact and generate a diverse range of ideas [22]. The semi-structured interview guide was developed based on the quantitative findings from the motivation and attitude questionnaires. Key guiding questions included experiences with HoloPatient-based simulation education and factors influencing HoloPatient-based simulation education (i.e., How did you experience the HoloPatient-based simulation education?; What aspects of the simulation influenced your motivation to learn?; How did the simulation impact your attitude towards caring for patients with COPD?; What challenges did you encounter during the simulation?).

Measurements

Learning motivation

We used an instrument based on the Instructional Materials Motivation Scale (IMMS) [23], modified by Jang et al. [24]. The IMMS was developed to measure motivation-related problems in students’ self-directed learning. It has been validated in the MR instructional simulation [25]. The 27 items of this tool included a four-dimensional measurement tool (i.e., eight items on attention, six items on relevance, eight items on confidence and five items on satisfaction). Each item was scored on a five-point Likert scale ranging from one point for ‘not at all’ to five points for ‘always.’ The learning motivation scale included seven reverse-coded items (items 3, 6, 11, 15, 19, 22, and 26). All reverse-coded items were appropriately recoded before data analysis. The total score ranged from 27 to 135 points, with higher scores indicating greater motivation to learn. The Cronbach’s α in a previous study and in this study was 0.96 [24] and 0.95, respectively.

Learning attitude

We used a tool developed by the Korea Educational Development Institute to assess students’ attitudes, habits, beliefs and motives in class [26], modified by Hwang [27]. This tool consists of 16 items based on self-concept, study attitudes and learning habits. Each item was scored on a five-point Likert scale ranging from one point for ‘not at all’ to five points for ‘always.’ The learning attitude scale included four reverse-coded items (items 2, 5, 11, and 15). All reverse-coded items were appropriately recoded before data analysis. The total score ranged from 16 to 80 points, with a higher score indicating a greater learning attitude. The Cronbach’s α in a previous study and in this study was 0.84 [28] and 0.89, respectively.

Data analysis

We coded and analysed quantitative data with Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) version 27.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York). We analysed the participants’ general characteristics using frequencies and percentages. We used mean and standard deviations to assess motivation and attitude for HoloPatient-based simulation learning. Prior to conducting parametric tests, we assessed the normality of the data distribution for both the motivation and attitude scores using the skewness and kurtosis values and Q-Q plots. The skewness and kurtosis values for both variables fell within the acceptable range of ± 1, supporting the assumption of normality. Given that the assumptions of normality were met, we used an independent samples t-test or a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare differences in motivation and attitude according to general characteristics. We used Pearson’s correlation coefficient to investigate the correlation between motivation and attitude regarding HoloPatient-based simulation learning.

We used thematic analysis to explore the data from focus groups. The process of this analysis involved coding, categorising the data to find recurring ideas, concepts and patterns, and then organising them into themes [29]. Thematic analysis was allowed to identify the main themes [30]. To improve consistency and reliability and to minimise bias, the first author conducted the initial data analysis in Korean, which was reviewed in English, discussed and refined with the co-author until both were in agreement. To improve the reliability and validity of the findings, member checking was conducted with the participants [31]. We have spent sufficient time on HoloPatient-based education and are familiar with this form of education, so the credibility can be assured.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the institutional review board at Changwon national university (IRB No. 7001066-202306-HR-036). In addition to the IRB approval, we obtained permission from the head of the department of nursing at the university where the study was conducted. Informed written consent was received from all participants prior to the start of this study. We informed participants of the purpose of this study, the procedure, and the guarantee of anonymity and confidentiality. We also informed them of their right to withdraw from this study at any time.

Results

Quantitative findings

Quantitative sample

The majority of participants were female (84%) with a mean age of 23.74 years (range: 21–51 years). 98% (n = 98) of participants were satisfied with nursing major and 92% (n = 92) achieved a grade point average above medium (3.0 or higher) in their previous semester.

Learning motivation and attitudes towards HoloPatient-based simulation learning

Following HoloPatient-based simulation education, nursing students reported high levels of learning motivation (M = 4.42, SD = 0.42, range: 3.37–4.96) and positive attitudes towards HoloPatient-based simulation learning (M = 4.08, SD = 0.50, range: 2.94–4.81) on a 5-point scale (Table 1).

Learning motivation and attitudes towards HoloPatient-based simulation learning according to nursing students’ characteristics

There were significant differences in motivation towards HoloPatient-based simulation learning by age (F = 7.11, p =.001), major satisfaction (t=−6.81, p <.001), and grade point average (GPA) (F = 5.12, p =.017). Positive experiences of HoloPatient-based simulation learning

Concentration and enhancement of COPD acute exacerbation care

The majority of participants reported that HoloPatient enabled them to concentrate on the situation the HoloPatient faced in a realistic way. One participant reflected:

Concentration is better because I could see the symptoms and signs of COPD acute exacerbation in HoloPatient directly rather than expressing it in writing. Through this, I realised that emotional support needs to be included in COPD care holistically. Also, it is important to always provide explanations before direct care to prevent older adults with COPD from receiving treatment in anxiety. (Participants 5, Female, 21)

Through this, I realised that emotional support needs to be included in COPD care holistically. Also, it is important to always provide explanations before direct care to prevent older adults with COPD from receiving treatment in anxiety. (Participants 5, Female, 21)

Combination of education and entertainment

Most participants mentioned that HoloPatient combined educational and entertaining aspects, providing visual and audibly approaches with a 360-degree view that could enhance memorization of the situation the HoloPatient faced and student engagement in real-life situations. Also, they pointed out the limitations of learning COPD care from textbook and how MR-based HoloPatient can provide a more immersive and interactive learning experience. One participant reflected:

…it was more fun. I surely remain motivated and engaged in the lessons by playing such games.’ (Participants 8, Female, 21).

…it was more fun. I surely remain motivated and engaged in the lessons by playing such games.’ (Participants 8, Female, 21).

Practical and simulated learning

Most participants reported that HoloPatient could facilitate practical and simulated learning that helped develop knowledge of COPD exacerbation care and apply them in real-life situations according to priority. One participant reflected:

The MR-based HoloPatient could give immersive and realistic experiences. It provided opportunities to use our nursing knowledge and skills in the clinical context…. It would help develop teamwork and communication skill if the healthcare team cared for HoloPatient.’ (Participants 2, Female, 21).

The MR-based HoloPatient could give immersive and realistic experiences. It provided opportunities to use our nursing knowledge and skills in the clinical context…. It would help develop teamwork and communication skill if the healthcare team cared for HoloPatient.’ (Participants 2, Female, 21).

Influencing factors on HoloPatient-based simulation education

Immersion

Most participants reported that they perceived HoloPatient-based simulation as an immersive approach that could enhance their engagement in learning. They expressed their beliefs and expectations regarding the integration of HoloPatient in simulation education. One participant stated:

It was easy to be immersed in HoloPatient. It helped me participate actively by observing the HoloPatient directly in the immersed mode, which was great to stay in my memory longer.’ (Participants 9, Female, 22)

It was easy to be immersed in HoloPatient. It helped me participate actively by observing the HoloPatient directly in the immersed mode, which was great to stay in my memory longer.’ (Participants 9, Female, 22)

Similarly, they highlighted that HoloPatient-based simulation activities were fun, which would be more engaged and motivated to learning nursing in a MR environment. One participant reflected:

‘…through HoloPatient-based simulation activity, I was able to spark my interest and then be involved motivately in each module and improve my understanding.’ (Participants 3, Male, 23).

‘…through HoloPatient-based simulation activity, I was able to spark my interest and then be involved motivately in each module and improve my understanding.’ (Participants 3, Male, 23).

Realism

Most participants reported that HoloPatient was like a real patient. It helped them provide an opportunity to care appropriately for the older adult with COPD exacerbation. One participant stated:

It helped provide realistic nursing care tailored to the situation as we could directly see what kind of the situation the HoloPatient was in like a real patient…we could empathise with the situation in a realistic way and participate actively.’ (Participants 6, Female, 21).

It helped provide realistic nursing care tailored to the situation as we could directly see what kind of the situation the HoloPatient was in like a real patient…we could empathise with the situation in a realistic way and participate actively.’ (Participants 6, Female, 21).

Barriers

Students identified two barriers that had a negative impact on their HoloPatient-based simulation learning experiences: lack of consistent Wireless Fidelity (Wi-Fi) connections and cybersickness.

Lack of consistent Wi-Fi connections

Some participants said that the lack of consistent Wi-Fi connections made it difficult to download and access to the HoloPatient simultaneously onto MR devices, so it prevented them from being immersed in the situations. One participant stated: ‘Internet connects were not always available on HoloLens, so HoloPatient application could not be opened quickly.’ (Participants 10, Female, 21).

Cyber sickness

Some participants reported that HoloLens wearers experienced symptoms of cybersickness such as nausea, headache, dizziness and difficulty concentrating. One participant stated:

‘…It was a shame that I felt dizzy and uncomfortable, while wearing the HoloLens to observe the HoloPatient in a few minutes.’ (Participants 2, Female, 21) Similarly, one participant reported: ‘I felt sick when I wore the HoloLens in a minute. I could not wear it for a while.’ (Participants 5, Female, 21).

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings

The quantitative data showed high levels of motivation (M = 4.42, SD = 0.42) and attitude (M = 4.08, SD = 0.50), which was supported by qualitative themes such as ‘Positive experiences’, and ‘Influencing factors on HoloPatient-based simulation education’. For example, the ‘immersion’ sub-theme aligns with the high motivation scores, as students reported being more engaged in the learning process. The ‘realism’ sub-theme supports the positive attitude scores, with students appreciating the authenticity of the simulated experience. One participant stated, ‘It was easy to be immersed in HoloPatient’, providing context to the high motivation scores.

Discussion

This study provides evidence on nursing students’ learning motivation and attitudes following HoloPatient-based simulation education. This study also contributes to an understandning of the factors that facilitate or hinder HoloPatient-based simulation education in improving nursing students’ learning motivation and attitudes.

This study found that nursing students exhibited moderately high levels of motivation following HoloPatient-based simulation education. The unexpectedly higher motivation among students dissatisfied with their major (n = 2) requires cautious interpretation due to the extremely small sample size. This finding may reflect novelty effects or renewed interest through hands-on learning, but remains highly speculative and requires validation in larger samples. However, as this is the first study to investigate motivation towards HoloPatient-based simulation learning, it was impossible to directly compare the finding of this study with the other studies. To examine the effectiveness of HoloPatient-based simulation on nursing students’ learning motivation, future research need to be conducted, using randomized controlled trials or quasi-experimental designs.

This finding may be attributed to the positive learning experiences of HoloPatient-based simulation education. Positive learning experiences can increase students’ motivation [32]. This study provided examples of nursing students’ positive experiences qualitatively. The majority of nursing students reported that the HoloPatient-based simulation education enabled them to concentrate on the situation the HoloPatient faced in a realistic way. Compared with traditional educational approaches, it combined educational and entertaining aspects, providing more interactive learning experiences in immersive environments. Thus, this finding suggests that the learning experiences play an important role in learning and improving motivation [32].

This study found a significant difference in attitudes towards HoloPatient-based simulation learning by gender, with male participants demonstrating more positive learning attitude towards HoloPatient-based simulation education than female participants. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously due to the highly skewed gender distribution (16 males, 84 females). The observed differences may result from the small male sample size rather than true gender effects. This gender difference may be attributed to cultural variations in coping strategies. Previous research suggests that male students tend to use problem-focused coping strategies more frequently than female student [33, 34], which might enable them to more readily adapt to and enjoy the new educational method like HoloPatient simulation. These problem-focused approaches could potentially help male students navigate the challenges of adopting novel educational technologies with less hesitation [35]. However, it is important to note that our findings regarding differences by gender should be considered alongside other variables in our study, particularly as the sample had an uneven gender distribution typical of nursing education programs. Further research is needed to more thoroughly investigate how gender influences nursing students’ perceptions and experiences of emerging educational technologies in healthcare simulation.

The positive correlation between learning motivation and attitude dimensions in this study can be attributed to the immersive and interactive nature of HoloPatient-based simulation. While COPD cases are common in clinical settings, the realistic controlled simulation environment allows students to develop clinical competence without risk to patient safety. The technology’s ability to present standardised holographic patient helps students build confidence, which may explain the high motivation scores in this study. Previous studies [1, 18] reported similar positive correlations between learning motivation and attitude in VR-based simulations for nursing students, as well as strong relationship between student engagement and learning outcomes in clinical virtual simulations. Additionally, this finding might be explained by the immersive and interactive nature of HoloPatient simulations. MR-based HoloPatient simulations can create a sense of presence and realism that enhances student engagement and motivation [5]. This immersive quality, combined with the opportunity for hands-on practice in a safe environment, may foster positive attitudes towards learning, which in turn reinforces motivation. While the qualitative sample of 10 participants provided valuable insights, it may not have fully achieved data saturation, potentially limiting the depth of emerging themes. Future studies with larger qualitative cohorts could enhance theme robustness.

This study has important limitations. First, the findings may not be generalizable due to recruitment from a single nursing department in South Korea. Also, the quantitative component included unbalanced group sizes for some variables (particularly, only 2 students reported dissatisfaction with their major), which limited meaningful statistical comparisons. The qualitative component with only 10 participants may not have achieved data saturation, potentially limiting the comprehensiveness of the thematic analysis. Second, due to limited time and resource constrains, a one group post-test only design without control groups was used, which significantly limited our ability to establish causality between HoloPatient-based simulation education and students’ learning motivation and attitudes. Without baseline measurements or comparisons, we cannot determine whether observed outcomes are attributable to the intervention or other factors. Results should be interpreted as exploratory evidence requiring confirmation through rigorous experimental designs.

While the triangulation of survey and interview data strengthens the validity of our findings, the research design cannot definitively attribute the observed high levels of motivation and attitude to the intervention itself. However, this mixed methods study used a triangulation of survey and interview data to strengthen the validity of the findings. It is considered meaningful in providing significant insights into the mechanisms underlying the effectiveness of HoloPatient-based simulation education. Thus, in order to verify the effects of the HoloPatient-based simulation education, future studies need to be conducted with randomised controlled trials or quasi-experimental designs. Also, a repeated study that involves a larger number of nursing students from different universities is needed. Finally, social desirability bias may have influenced both quantitative survey responses and qualitative interview data, as students might have provided answers they believed would please the researchers or meet perceived expectations [29]. This potential bias is particularly relevant for educational innovations where students may feel pressure to report positive experiences and outcomes.

Conclusions

This exploratory study found high levels of learning motivation and positive attitudes following HoloPatient-based simulation education. This research identified barriers, particularly technical issues and physical discomfort, which are significant concerns that need to addressed for successful implementation. These practical insights complement the quantitative findings while offering guidance for future applications. However, findings should be interpreted cautiously due to study limitations, including the one-group post-test design and small qualitative sample. Future studies using randomized controlled trials or quasi-experimental designs with larger, more diverse samples are essential to establish true effectiveness. While this study provides initial insights regarding HoloPatient-based simulation in nursing education, successful implementation requires evidence of educational effectiveness and attention to technical infrastructure and user comfort. Future studies should consider these practical aspects while exploring the potential long-term outcomes of this educational approach.
