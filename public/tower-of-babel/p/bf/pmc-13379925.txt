Giant Left Atrial Myxoma Causing Dynamic Mitral Inflow Obstruction
Authors: Harith Almimar, Osmaan Chaudhri, Khristoffer Bury-Hernandez, Musa Kiyani, Anthony M. Perez Santos, Adam Klein, Yu Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtrial myxomas are the most common primary cardiac tumors. Larger tumors (>5 cm) are associated with increased risk of obstruction and hemodynamic compromise, while very large (“giant”) myxomas are rare.Case SummaryA 34-year-old man with atrial fibrillation was found to have a large left atrial mass after presenting with tachyarrhythmia. Echocardiography demonstrated an ovoid mass prolapsing through the mitral valve, producing functional mitral stenosis (mean gradient 8 mm Hg), marked atrial dilation, and reduced ejection fraction (30%). Surgical resection revealed a near-spherical tumor measuring 8.2 × 5.5 × 5.5 cm attached to the interatrial septum. Left ventricular function improved to 45% after 7 days postop.DiscussionGiant atrial myxomas can cause severe hemodynamic compromise through mechanical obstruction, with tumor size and morphology playing key roles.Take-Home MessageObstructive physiology from atrial myxomas can be reversible, highlighting the importance of early recognition and surgical intervention.

Atrial myxomas account for approximately half of primary cardiac tumors and most commonly originate from the interatrial septum.1,2 The tumors are benign and 90% of them arise sporadically, typically in the third to sixth decade of life.1,2 Their clinical manifestations are classically divided into obstructive, embolic, and constitutional syndromes.3

Tumor size is a major determinant of presentation. Although smaller myxomas may remain clinically silent, larger tumors (>5 cm) are more likely to produce significant intracardiac obstruction.4 Very large (“giant”) atrial myxomas are rare and are associated with a higher likelihood of mitral inflow obstruction,3,4 which can cause elevated left atrial pressures, pulmonary hypertension, and secondary ventricular remodeling.3, 4, 5 Patients can present with arrhythmias from chamber dilation and symptoms of congestive heart failure due to mitral valve inflow obstruction.3,4

Tumor morphology further influences hemodynamics. Masses with smooth contours and increased bulk may intermittently occlude the mitral valve, producing a ball-valve–type physiology and amplifying obstruction.6

History of Presentation

A 34-year-old man with a history of permanent atrial fibrillation presented after being noted to be tachycardic during a routine outpatient visit. He denied chest pain, dyspnea, palpitations, or syncope.

Electrocardiography demonstrated atypical atrial flutter with an irregular rhythm.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for a large left atrial mass included atrial myxoma, thrombus, and primary cardiac malignancy.

The large size, septal attachment, and smooth morphology strongly favored atrial myxoma.7,8 Thrombi are typically irregular and laminated, whereas malignant tumors are more infiltrative and less mobile.

Intraoperatively, the tumor was described as large, smooth, and globular, attached to the interatrial septum. Gross pathologic examination demonstrated a mass measuring 8.2 × 5.5 × 5.5 cm (Figure 2). Histopathologic slides showed a hypocellular myxoid neoplasm with scattered stellate and spindle cells in abundant myxoid stroma, focal vascularity, and hemorrhagic change. Follow-Up

The postoperative course was initially notable for hemodynamic support with vasopressors, inotropes, and mechanical ventilation, followed by rapid stabilization with early extubation and weaning of vasoactive support. The patient experienced recurrent atrial fibrillation, managed with intravenous amiodarone and subsequent successful electrical cardioversion, after which he remained in normal sinus rhythm on oral therapy with resumption of beta-blockade. Guideline-directed medical therapy included low-dose lisinopril, with further optimization limited by borderline blood pressures and financial constraints; therefore, sacubitril/valsartan, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors were not initiated. Early mobilization and multidisciplinary rehabilitation facilitated steady functional recovery. Temporary pacing wires and chest tubes were removed without complication, and anticoagulation was transitioned from intravenous heparin to oral rivaroxaban.

A follow-up transesophageal echocardiogram performed 9 days postoperatively (Video 3) demonstrated the following:•Marked improvement in left ventricular systolic function (EF 45%)•Persistent but reduced left atrial dilation•Moderate functional mitral regurgitation (revised from initial concern for severe mitral regurgitation)

Discussion

This case illustrates the pathophysiologic consequences of giant atrial myxomas, particularly those exceeding 5 cm in size. Tumor size is a key determinant of clinical manifestation, with larger masses more likely to produce obstructive physiology.3,4

As atrial myxomas enlarge, they increasingly interfere with mitral inflow, functioning as space-occupying lesions within the left atrium.3, 4, 5 Tumors exceeding 5 cm are significantly more likely to•Prolapse across the mitral valve•Cause functional mitral stenosis•Elevate left atrial pressure, with consequent left atrial dilation and triggering atrial arrhythmias•Induce pulmonary hypertension•Lead to ventricular remodeling3, 4, 5

Giant myxomas, such as in this case (8.2 cm), are rare and associated with a higher burden of hemodynamic compromise.4,5

Beyond size, tumor morphology influences the degree of obstruction.6,7 Although echocardiography demonstrated an ovoid mass, intraoperative and pathological findings revealed a more globular, near-spherical structure, allowing the tumor to behave as a dynamic intracardiac “ball.” This facilitated intermittent occlusion of the mitral orifice, producing a ball-valve mechanism.6

Chronic obstruction of mitral inflow results in the following:•Sustained elevation of left atrial pressure•Pulmonary venous congestion•Increased right-sided pressures•Reduced left ventricular preload•Progressive ventricular dilation and systolic dysfunction4,8

In this patient, these processes culminated in secondary cardiomyopathy with reduced EF (30%). Importantly, the rapid improvement in EF to 45% within 7 to 10 days of resection supports a mechanical and reversible etiology of ventricular dysfunction.

Despite significant structural abnormalities, the patient remained minimally symptomatic, underscoring the ability of younger individuals to compensate for progressive hemodynamic changes.3

This case emphasizes that•Tumor size (>5 cm) strongly correlates with obstructive complications•Very large myxomas can mimic valvular heart disease•Cardiomyopathy may be secondary to mechanical obstruction and reversible•Early recognition and surgical intervention are essential to prevent irreversible remodeling

Novelty of the Submission

This case highlights a rare giant atrial myxoma (>8 cm) with globular morphology producing significant mitral inflow obstruction and rapidly reversible cardiomyopathy, emphasizing the interplay between tumor size, shape, and hemodynamic impact.

Conclusions

Giant atrial myxomas can cause severe hemodynamic compromise through mechanical obstruction of mitral inflow. Tumor size and morphology are key determinants of clinical severity. Prompt surgical resection can lead to rapid and meaningful recovery of cardiac function.Take-Home Messages•Very large myxomas are rare but carry significant hemodynamic consequences.•Giant atrial myxomas (>5 cm) are strongly associated with obstructive physiology.•Mechanical cardiomyopathy from intracardiac tumors may be rapidly reversible after resection.
