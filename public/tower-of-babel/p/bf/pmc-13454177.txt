Interpretable multitask model for clinical pathology image prediction and interpretation
Authors: Qitao Chen, Zhe Wang, Xia Lin, Yuying Shi, Botao Xu, Jie Chai, Tao Zhang, Cheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeep learning (DL)-based pathological image modelling and analysis approaches offer transformative potential for early cancer diagnostics, yet limited sample sizes and a lack of interpretability often hinder efficient clinical translation. Here, we present the interpretable Multi-Task Digital Pathology Model (iMDPath), an end-to-end, highly explainable multi-task deep learning framework that simultaneously addresses these challenges by integrating data augmentation, diagnostic prediction, and visualization of pathological image features. The iMDPath comprises three modules: Augmentation (iMDPath-Aug), Prediction (iMDPath-Pred), and Visualization (iMDPath-Vis). iMDPath-Aug incorporates a vector-quantized variational autoencoder (VQ-VAE) for enhanced data augmentation, capturing essential pathological features from limited datasets. A Swin Transformer-Based (Swin-B) predictor in the iMDPath-Pred module leverages the augmented data to achieve better performance than patch-level and foundation-model-based encoders such as InceptionV3 and Phikon across six diverse cancer pathology datasets, including gastric, breast, lung, and colorectal cancer. Finally, iMDPath-Vis, a novel visualization module combining the full gradient (FullGrad) and occlusion sensitivity analysis, provides pathologists with actionable insights by highlighting the specific tissue regions driving model predictions. Overall, iMDPath not only surpasses existing methods in diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, and generalization across these datasets, but also offers a transparent and interpretable AI solution for precision oncology, paving the way for more reliable and efficient clinical decision-making.Subject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics

Introduction

Pathological images are crucial for clinical diagnostics in cancer, providing essential information for cancer grading and progression1–3. Evaluating the cellular features based on the pathological images not only enhances the accuracy of cancer diagnosis but also helps inform therapeutic decisions, shaping personalized treatment strategies, which can significantly improve patient outcomes4,5. However, traditional manual microscopic evaluation faces challenges, including inter-observer variability and time-intensive analysis. Given these limitations, deep learning (DL)-based approaches that can effectively utilize pathological images hold significant promise to advance diagnostic accuracy and clinical workflows. Over the past decade, DL-based approaches have been increasingly developed to facilitate the analysis of pathological images, enabling rapid processing of large-scale datasets, identifying microscopic lesions, and achieving high precision in disease classification6–10. In addition, large-scale foundation models, which are pre-trained on large and diverse datasets and can be adapted to various downstream tasks, have emerged to enhance clinical decision-making based on clinical pathological images, offering valuable support to pathologists and physician scientists in optimizing therapeutic strategies11–15.

Among these developments, several landmark studies have demonstrated the versatility of different DL architectures in pathological applications. Kumar et al. 16 utilized the Visual Geometry Group (VGG) network for the Multi-Organ Nucleus Segmentation Challenge, demonstrating its ability to extract precise features from pathological images. Marme et al. 17 applied the Residual Neural Network (ResNet) to predict breast cancer sentinel lymph node status, employing skip connections to enhance training depth and accuracy. Coudray et al. 18 leveraged the Inception network for classifying and predicting mutations in non-small cell lung cancer by utilizing its multi-scale feature processing. Ghaffari et al. 19 benchmarked Vision Transformer (ViT) for whole-slide classification, utilizing its global attention mechanism to address challenges associated with limited data. Despite these studies highlighting the potential and applicability of DL models in pathological image-based diagnosis, there remains a critical need for extensive, diverse training datasets that represent the full spectrum of lesion types encountered in clinical practice for developing accurate models10,20–25. Limited training sample sizes remain a challenge for developing customizable and explainable pathological image deep learning models. In addition, the desirable DL models must be robust and generalizable to handle the variations in pathological imaging techniques and staining protocols across different institutions. The lack of interpretability hampers the ability to understand the relationship between the pathological images and disease progression26.

To address these limitations, we developed the interpretable Multi-Task Digital Pathology Model (iMDPath), an end-to-end framework designed to perform multiple tasks in pathological image modelling and analysis, including dataset augmentation, disease grade prediction, and interpretation of pathological image regions of interest. The iMDPath framework comprises three key modules: Augmentation (iMDPath-Aug), Prediction (iMDPath-Pred), and Visualization (iMDPath-Vis). The iMDPath-Aug module employs a variational autoencoder-based model for image augmentation, improving the representation of pathological features. The iMDPath-Pred module utilizes a Swin Transformer-Based (Swin-B) architecture to enhance prediction accuracy for a wide range of cancer pathological images. The iMDPath-Vis module provides interpretability by generating visualizations that highlight key regions of interest aligned with the model’s predictions. By applying iMDPath framework to six different pathological datasets, the iMDPath framework not only achieves excellent prediction performance compared to state-of-the-art pathology foundation models for patch-level image analysis, including representative models such as Phikon and UNI, but also offers instructional insights regarding the critical regions that contribute to the model’s predictions. The iMDPath exhibits great potential for scaling up the generalizability across larger and more diverse datasets to ensure its robustness and applicability in various clinical settings. The iMDPath provides a comprehensive tool for assisting pathologists in digital pathology, enhancing the clinical utility of DL models in precision oncology.

Generative model data augmentation by iMDPath-Aug

To mitigate the issues of limited data and inconsistent annotations in cancer pathological images, we optimized the generative models for data augmentation in the iMDPath-Aug module (Supplementary Table 1), with a particular focus on the vector-quantized variational autoencoder (VQ-VAE). The introduction of discrete encoding in the VQ-VAE model significantly enhanced the data compression and reconstruction process, particularly for pathological images with discrete structural features. This approach was found to outperform other models in terms of representational capacity for handling complex pathological image structures. We first assessed the impact of the VQ-VAE model on data augmentation by comparing its performance against traditional data preprocessing techniques and the variational autoencoder (VAE) on the BOT and SEED datasets. The results from training with the VQ-VAE model demonstrated notable advantages in both model convergence and classification accuracy.

The VQ-VAE model exhibited excellent convergence, as indicated by the loss curve (Figs. 2A, 3A, 4A, 5A). The loss curve exhibits stable convergence during training, with consistent trends observed between the training and validation sets. Both training and validation losses plateaued at approximately 0.01, indicating high fidelity in data reconstruction and minimal quantization error. Following data augmentation with the VQ-VAE model, the classification performance improved significantly (Table 1). The Swin-B model, enhanced by the VQ-VAE-augmented images, achieved remarkable results on the BOT dataset, with an accuracy of 0.957 and a maximum area under the curve (AUC) of 0.995. Similarly, on the SEED dataset, the Swin-B model, optimized with VQ-VAE data augmentation, achieved an accuracy of 0.980 and an AUC of 0.999. These results highlight the effectiveness of the VQ-VAE model in enhancing both classification accuracy and model stability.

To validate the performance of the VQ-VAE model on a more diverse set of gastric cancer images, we applied the VQ-VAE data augmentation to the SDGC dataset. The Swin-B model, optimized by the VQ-VAE-augmented images, achieved an accuracy of 0.795 and an AUC value of 0.825. Although the performance was slightly lower compared to the BOT (Brain of Things, http://www.datadreams.org/) and SEED (Medical and Health Track, https://www.jseedata.com) datasets, these results still underscore the potential of the VQ-VAE model in real-world clinical applications, where image quality and annotation consistency can vary. To evaluate the generalization capability of the VQ-VAE model, we applied it to the Breast Cancer Histopathological Image Classification (BreaKHis) dataset consisting of breast cancer images. The loss curves of the VQ-VAE model showed consistent behaviour, stabilizing over time and indicating the model’s robustness in preventing overfitting. The Swin-B model, optimized by the VQ-VAE-enhanced images, achieved 0.986 accuracy and an AUC value of 0.999. The significant improvement in classification performance on this dataset further demonstrates the VQ-VAE model’s excellent generalization capabilities across different cancer types.

This improvement in classification performance not only confirms the effectiveness of our VQ-VAE-based optimization but also highlights its potential to tackle broader challenges in pathological image analysis, such as dataset limitations. While foundation models provide strong pretrained representations, iMDPath-Aug directly addresses dataset limitations by expanding sample diversity and mitigating annotation inconsistencies by introducing morphological variations of annotated samples that make the model less sensitive to minor differences in image-level labels. Specifically, the VQ-VAE–based augmentation generates realistic morphological variations that are not captured through pretraining alone, thereby enhancing the robustness of downstream classifiers. Importantly, iMDPath-Aug is model-agnostic and can be integrated with foundation models. Quantitative results demonstrate significant synergy: combining iMDPath-Aug with foundation models improved accuracy by 11.1% for Phikon and 9.4% for UNI on the BOT dataset, with consistent gains of 2.0% and 1.5% respectively on the SEED dataset (Supplementary Table 2). These results suggest that augmentation and foundation-based pretraining are complementary strategies rather than mutually exclusive approaches.

The Swin-B model improves computational efficiency and enhances classification performance by leveraging a sliding window mechanism, which combines local perception with global dependencies.

During training, the Swin-B model exhibited excellent convergence trends, as indicated by the loss curve and the accuracy curve (Fig. 2A, B). In terms of classification performance, the Swin-B model, when optimized with VQ-VAE-augmented data, achieved remarkable results. On the BOT dataset, the Swin-B model achieved optimal performance with peak values of 0.957 for accuracy, 0.995 for AUC, and 0.998 for average precision (AP) (Fig. 2C, D). To further validate the model’s discriminative power, we analyzed the latent embeddings of the iMDPath-Pred model, which revealed distinct clusters for different disease categories, demonstrating the model’s ability to capture critical pathological features (Fig. 2E). The confusion matrix for this model revealed high precision and recall across all categories, and correct classifications substantially outnumbered misclassifications. This indicates strong discriminative capabilities (Fig. 2F). The model achieved 80.0% specificity for benign cases, with 100.0% sensitivity for malignant samples.

Similarly, when applied to the SEED dataset, the Swin-B model exhibited excellent convergence (Fig. 3A), with the loss curve significantly decreasing within the first 20 epochs and then gradually stabilizing. The training and validation loss curves showed consistent trends, confirming the model’s robust generalization capability. As shown in Fig. 3B, the accuracy on both training and validation sets continued to improve, achieving a stable high accuracy after several iterations, indicating superior feature extraction and classification abilities. When enhanced by the VQ-VAE model, the Swin-B model demonstrated superior performance with an accuracy of 0.980, AP of 0.998, and AUC of 0.999, surpassing the convolutional models by 1.1–2.8% in accuracy (Figs. 3C, D). The latent embeddings analysis for the SEED dataset (Fig. 3E) revealed distinct clusters for normal, tubular, and mucinous conditions. The confusion matrix revealed high classification accuracy, with significantly more correctly classified samples than misclassifications, further supporting the model’s robust performance (Fig. 3F). Multiclass accuracy exceeded 0.976 across all subtypes, with minimal misclassifications: three tubular samples misclassified as mucinous and four mucinous samples misclassified as tubular, suggesting strong overall performance but slight challenges in distinguishing these closely related categories.

To evaluate the performance of the Swin-B model in a real-world clinical setting, we tested it on the SDGC dataset. The loss curve for the Swin-B model demonstrated consistent convergence (Fig. 4A), and the accuracy curve showed a higher convergence speed and improved final accuracy, confirming the effectiveness of the model architecture and optimization techniques (Fig. 4B). Upon integration with VQ-VAE, this architecture achieved superior performance metrics, with an accuracy of 0.795, AP of 0.908, and maximum AUC of 0.825 (Fig. 4C, D). The latent embeddings analysis for the SDGC dataset (Fig. 4E) revealed distinct clusters for moderate differentiation and high-risk histology. The confusion matrix revealed fewer misclassifications, especially in key categories, validating the model’s capability to handle real-world data with less annotation consistency (Fig. 4F). The model achieved 90.4% sensitivity for high-risk histology cases, with 55.3% specificity for moderate differentiation samples. The relatively low number of misclassifications demonstrates the model’s robustness in distinguishing between the two categories.

Finally, to assess the generalization capability of the Swin-B model across various cancer types, we tested it on the BreaKHis dataset of breast cancer tissue images. The model’s loss and accuracy curves demonstrated excellent convergence and stable high accuracy (Fig. 5A, B), highlighting the effectiveness of the Swin-B architecture and the importance of data preprocessing and augmentation strategies in optimizing model performance. Notably, after incorporating VQ-VAE data augmentation, the model achieved remarkable results with maximum performance indicators: 0.986 accuracy, AUC of 0.999, and AP of 1.000 (Fig. 5C, D). The latent embeddings for the BreaKHis dataset (Fig. 5E) showed well-defined clusters, underlining the model’s ability to learn clinically meaningful representations. The confusion matrix for this dataset showed significant classification accuracy across all sample types, with a substantially higher number of correctly classified samples compared to misclassifications, indicating the model’s reliability and strong performance in complex classification tasks (Fig. 5F). The model achieved 97.2% specificity (494 cases) and 99.2% sensitivity (1065 cases) and very few benign fibroadenomas misclassified as malignant (14 cases).

The promising results obtained from initial evaluations on gastric and breast cancer datasets motivated us to conduct a more comprehensive validation study. We systematically expanded our evaluation to include colorectal and lung cancer datasets, thereby strengthening the claims of broad applicability across diverse cancer types. Remarkably, iMDPath demonstrated consistently strong performance across all cancer types, achieving 98.5% accuracy on colorectal cancer and 97.7% accuracy on lung cancer, in addition to its superior performance on gastric and breast cancers (Supplementary Table 3). These results validate the generalizability of iMDPath across diverse tissue morphologies and cellular architectures, reinforcing our hypothesis that the framework is not limited to specific cancer types but can be broadly applied in precision oncology.

While the multi-dataset evaluation demonstrated strong performance, we recognized the importance of validating our approach on completely independent data to assess real-world clinical utility. To address potential dataset size limitations and provide robust external validation, we collected an independent histopathological dataset from our institution. On the SDGC validation dataset, iMDPath achieved 77.9% accuracy with 0.832 AUC (Supplementary Table 4). The model demonstrated balanced performance across all metrics, validating its generalizability on this independent external test set for gastric cancer classification. These results, while showing some expected performance degradation compared to the training datasets due to domain shift and real-world variability, confirm the practical applicability of our approach in clinical settings.

Given the central role of the Swin-B architecture in our framework’s success, we conducted a systematic comparison to justify our architectural choice against other prominent Transformer variants, Vision Transformer-Base (ViT-B) and Multi-Axis Vision Transformer Tiny (MaxViT-T), used in computer vision and medical imaging (Supplementary Table 5). Swin-B consistently achieved superior performance, with an average accuracy of 94.7% compared to 92.4% for ViT-B and 89.8% for MaxViT-T. AUC results further highlight Swin-B’s advantage (0.969 vs. 0.959 and 0.940, respectively). The hierarchical shifted-window attention mechanism in Swin-B proved particularly effective for multi-scale pathological feature extraction, yielding substantial gains on challenging datasets such as BOT (95.7% vs. 91.5% vs. 84.6%) and BreaKHis (98.6% vs. 95.1% vs. 95.9%). These findings validate that Swin-B offers a more robust framework for pathology image analysis compared to other Transformer variants.

The recent emergence of foundation models specifically designed for pathological image analysis represents a significant advancement in the field11–15. To ensure our approach remains competitive with the current state-of-the-art, we conducted comprehensive benchmarking against two representative pathology foundation models: Phikon and UNI (Supplementary Table 6). iMDPath achieved comparable or superior performance across gastric, breast, colorectal, and lung datasets, highlighting the effectiveness of our augmentation-prediction-visualization pipeline. These results indicate that iMDPath is competitive with state-of-the-art foundation models while offering additional interpretability and task-specific adaptability.

To further characterize model reliability beyond average performance, we examined stability, statistical significance, and generalization using complementary analyses. Five-fold cross-validation demonstrated consistent performance of Swin-B across all datasets, with low variance even on more challenging cohorts such as SDGC (SD of accuracy = 0.036 in Supplementary Table 7). We then assessed statistical differences in AUC using the DeLong test, with 95% confidence intervals estimated via bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations). While differences among models were not significant on datasets with near-ceiling performance, Swin-B achieved significantly higher AUC on the clinically challenging validation SDGC-2025 cohort, which was temporally separated from the original SDGC data (e.g., vs. MaxViT-T, p < 0.001; Supplementary Table 8). Finally, to evaluate cross-domain generalization, the model trained on the COLON dataset was tested on the fully independent CRC-VAL-HE-7K cohort, achieving an AUC of 0.935 (Supplementary Table 9).

In the iMDPath-Vis module, the visualization combines classification confidence scores and class activation map (CAM)-based attention changes through weighting parameters α and β. This analysis was conducted specifically on the SEED dataset, as it is the only dataset in our study with ground-truth mask annotations for pathological regions. We systematically evaluated different combinations of α and β values (Supplementary Fig. 2). For each parameter combination, we assessed the interpretability quality on the SEED dataset with pathologist-annotated regions. These preliminary experiments revealed that the interpretability quality remained largely consistent across different parameter settings, with minimal observable differences in the visualization patterns and attention focus areas. Based on this evidence of parameter insensitivity, we adopted α = 1 and β = 1 to provide symmetric weighting between classification confidence and attention-based interpretability.

To contextualize our visualization approach within the computational pathology literature, we compared iMDPath-Vis with established methods, particularly Clustering-constrained attention multiple instance learning (CLAM). The comparative visualizations between CLAM and iMDPath-Vis on representative histological images are shown in Supplementary Fig. 3. We could observe that CLAM provides patch-level attention weights for coarse tissue localization, while iMDPath-Vis delivers fine-grained, pixel-level (within-patch) interpretability that highlights discriminative cellular regions. iMDPath-Vis employs FullGrad to achieve pixel-level within an image patch granularity versus CLAM’s patch-level aggregation, enabling identification of localized morphological patterns. Unlike learned attention maps, FullGrad leverages actual gradient flow through the network, providing more faithful model decision interpretability. Clinically, this precision better aligns with pathologist examination workflows and enables more accurate validation of model predictions in diagnostic settings.

For objective validation of the interpretability claims, we conducted quantitative assessment by comparing iMDPath-Vis heatmaps with pathologist-annotated regions of interest (Supplementary Fig. 4). Four representative cases were selected for both gastric cancer classification and gastric mucin region identification tasks. We evaluated alignment using Dice similarity coefficient (Dice) and Intersection over Union (IoU). The model achieved average Dice scores ranging from 0.263 to 0.432 and IoU values between 0.152 and 0.276. These results provide objective evidence that iMDPath-Vis highlights clinically relevant pathological regions, thereby supporting its interpretability.

To provide a comprehensive evaluation, we examined failure cases where visualizations corresponded to incorrect classifications, particularly focusing on challenging scenarios in the SEED dataset (Supplementary Fig. 5). In four misclassified cases, heatmaps focused disproportionately on mucinous areas, leading to reduced alignment with pathologist annotations. These findings highlight the challenge of accurately localizing mucin-rich regions. Future work incorporating multi-scale features and expert-in-the-loop annotations could help reduce such errors.

By visually emphasizing these critical areas, the model provides valuable insights into the underlying pathological features and decision-making processes, facilitating a better understanding of the model’s feature selection across different cancer types. Overall, the visualization approach presented in this study not only achieves remarkable accuracy in classifying pathological images of various cancers but also holds significant potential for clinical applications.

Discussion

This study presents iMDPath, a novel end-to-end framework uniquely integrating image data augmentation, disease grade prediction, and interpretability for efficient and accurate pathological image analysis. The synergistic interaction of its modules, iMDPath-Aug, iMDPath-Pred, and iMDPath-Vis, addresses critical challenges in digital pathology, paving the way for enhanced clinical utility of deep learning models. The VQ-VAE generative model within the iMDPath-Aug effectively expands and enhances the dataset sample sizes, improving disease outcome prediction by compressing and reconstructing pathological images through a discrete encoding mechanism. This significantly bolsters the generalization capability of the iMDPath framework, particularly evident in the SDGC and BreaKHis datasets. Specifically, the introduction of VQ-VAE yielded substantial improvements in prediction accuracy (5.3% increase in the SDGC dataset), highlighting the importance of generative models in pathological image data processing and their potential to enhance model performance, especially when dealing with limited and heterogeneous datasets.

Compared to state-of-the-art convolutional neural networks, including VGG16, ResNet18, and InceptionV3, the iMDPath-Pred module demonstrates superior accuracy and stability in pathological image classification tasks. The Swin-B architecture, leveraging a sliding window mechanism, integrates local perception with global dependencies, reducing computational complexity and enabling efficient processing of high-resolution pathological images. Notably, after data augmentation by the iMDPath-Aug module, iMDPath achieved remarkable performance improvements across multiple datasets. For instance, the model reached an average accuracy of 0.969 and an AUC value approaching 1.000 in the SEED and BOT datasets, showcasing its strong discriminatory power and wide applicability. Importantly, these results were consistently observed across independent test sets, further validating the robustness of the framework. These results show that iMDPath achieves better performance on different datasets, such as the average AUC improvement is 2.2%, 0.2%, 2.9%, 0.5% higher than VGG16, ResNet18, and InceptionV3 on the BOT, SEED, SDGC, and BreaKHis datasets, respectively. It is important to note that the relatively lower performance on the SDGC dataset largely stems from the unique histological complexity in distinguishing moderately differentiated adenocarcinomas from high-risk histology (poorly differentiated and signet-ring variants). These subtypes are well recognized as a diagnostic challenge in clinical pathology due to their high morphological heterogeneity, subtle cellular boundaries, and frequent overlap. Even experienced pathologists report substantial inter-observer variability in such classifications, underscoring the inherent difficulty of the task. Our model’s misclassification patterns mirror these challenges, particularly in cases where moderately differentiated adenocarcinomas shared features with poorly differentiated or signet-ring cell carcinomas. This observation suggests that iMDPath not only reflects current diagnostic realities but also highlights areas where AI systems could serve as complementary tools to support pathologists. Future work incorporating larger gastric cancer subtype-specific cohorts and expert-in-the-loop training strategies may help reduce subtype-level misclassification and enhance clinical applicability.

Enhanced by the FullGrad and occlusion sensitivity analysis, the iMDPath-Vis module provides multi-level interpretability by identifying regions of interest in the input images and quantifying the contribution of each pixel to the final prediction. This level of interpretability is crucial for clinical decision-making, providing pathologists with intuitive image interpretations and insights into the model’s feature selection process. For example, the visualization consistently highlighted tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in gastric cancer samples as key predictive features, aligning with established histopathological knowledge and suggesting potential for iMDPath to aid in identifying prognostically relevant features.

There are a couple of limitations in the study. While the iMDPath-Aug module addresses data insufficiency, the quality of the generated images may suffer from some noise, which could influence model stability, especially when the dataset sizes are very limited, and the variations of pathological images are higher. To mitigate this, we implemented rigorous quality control measures during data augmentation and employed techniques such as adversarial training to improve the realism of the generated images. Future work will focus on exploring alternative generative models and incorporating domain-specific knowledge to further enhance the quality and diversity of the augmented data. Moreover, the current study primarily focused on gastric and breast cancer datasets. Future studies should evaluate the iMDPath framework on a broader range of cancer types and imaging modalities to assess its generalizability and identify potential adaptations needed for different clinical contexts.

In conclusion, we have developed iMDPath, a novel end-to-end framework that uniquely integrates data augmentation, accurate prediction, and interpretable visualization for clinical pathological image analysis. iMDPath demonstrates exceptional performance across diverse gastric and breast cancer datasets and provides pathologists with actionable insights into the model’s decision-making process, highlighting key tissue regions. By simultaneously addressing the challenges of data scarcity and interpretability, iMDPath represents a significant step forward in the application of deep learning to precision oncology. This framework holds promise for enhancing diagnostic accuracy, streamlining pathologist workflows, and ultimately improving patient outcomes through more personalized and effective cancer treatment strategies.

Methods

Datasets

In this study, we utilized pathological datasets of gastric, breast, colorectal, and lung cancer from multiple data sources, including BOT dataset, SEED dataset, SDGC dataset, SDGC validation dataset, BreaKHis dataset, COLON dataset, and LUNG dataset. The BOT dataset was obtained from the 2017 China Big Data Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Entrepreneurship Competition, including 520 pathological images of gastric cancer classified into malignant and benign categories. The SEED dataset was collected from the Big Data Development and Application Competition, containing 743 gastric cancer images categorized into normal, tubular, and mucinous conditions.

The SDGC dataset was collected at Shandong Cancer Hospital between 2019 and 2022 and consists of 753 gastric cancer histopathology images across four categories: moderately differentiated (225), poorly differentiated (273), poorly differentiated with signet-ring cells (193), and signet-ring cells (62). Poorly differentiated and signet-ring cell carcinomas are widely recognized as aggressive subtypes, associated with higher recurrence rates, rapid progression, and worse patient prognosis compared to moderately differentiated adenocarcinomas. Clinically, pathologists often stratify these histology into prognostic groups, with moderate differentiation representing relatively favorable morphology, while poorly differentiated and signet-ring variants are considered high-risk due to their adverse outcomes and diagnostic challenges. On this basis, we grouped samples into moderate differentiation and high-risk histology (including poorly differentiated, poorly differentiated with signet-ring cells, and signet-ring cell carcinoma). To further evaluate the real-world clinical utility and robustness of our model, we performed external validation using an independent dataset collected at Shandong Cancer Hospital from patients enrolled between 2023 and 2025 (SDGC-2025). The SDGC-2025 dataset comprises 217 gastric histopathology images across two categories: moderately differentiated (68) and high-risk histology (149). The BreaKHis dataset contained 7,909 small biopsy images from 82 patients diagnosed with breast cancer, categorized into benign and malignant tissues29,30. The COLON and LUNG datasets were sourced from the publicly available LC25000 dataset from Kaggle (https://www.kaggle.com/), which contains color histopathological images specifically designed for computer-aided diagnosis research in lung and colorectal cancers. Each category in the dataset comprises 500 images, covering lung adenocarcinoma, lung squamous cell carcinoma, benign lung tissue, colon adenocarcinoma, and benign colon groups. For external validation under a compatible label scheme, we utilized the CRC-VAL-HE-7K dataset. From this dataset, 200 image patches of normal colon tissue and 200 patches of colorectal adenocarcinoma were randomly sampled for independent testing. The collection and use of the SDGC and SDGC-2025 datasets were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong Cancer Hospital and Institute (approval number: SDTHEC202503118). Given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of fully de-identified, archived histopathology slides, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee. The BOT, SEED, BreaKHis, LC25000 (COLON and LUNG), and CRC-VAL-HE-7K datasets are publicly available and fully de-identified; they were originally collected with appropriate ethical oversight in their respective primary studies.

Interpretable multi-task digital pathology model framework

The iMDPath framework comprises three core modules: an augmentation module, a prediction module, and a visualization module. The iMDPath-Aug module enhances image quality using a variational autoencoder model. The iMDPath-Pred module, powered by the Swin-B architecture, improves the prediction accuracy of pathological images. The iMDPath-Vis module generates visualizations of the predicted outcomes by highlighting key tissue regions in the pathological images, providing interpretability for the model’s predictions.

Augmentation for pathological images

The iMDPath-Aug module employs the VQ-VAE-based augmentation to address the challenge of limited pathological image data31 (Fig. 1A). This module incorporates a vector quantization mechanism to enhance the capture of critical pathological features. The iMDPath-Aug module consists of three components: an encoder, a vector quantization layer, and a decoder.

Our implementation of the iMDPath-Aug module utilizes a two-level quantization architecture with dual codebooks, each containing 512 vectors with 64-dimensional embeddings (Supplementary Table 1). The hierarchical encoder consists of a bottom encoder (stride = 4, 512 × 512 to 128 × 128) extracting morphological features and a top encoder (stride = 2, 128 × 128 to 64 × 64) capturing semantic patterns, both employing 128 base channels with 2 residual blocks (32 channels each). The dual quantization mechanism discretizes features at corresponding scales, where the bottom-level quantizer concatenates top-level decoded features with bottom-level encoded features, preserving both global pathological semantics and local cellular details.

Generalization for diagnosis prediction

Pathological image analysis poses challenges due to the variability across different clinical institutions and the noise in the data. To address these, the iMDPath-Pred module leverages the Swin-B architecture (Fig. 1B), which consists of four stages with 24 transformer blocks. The Swin-B uses shifted window attention to capture both local and global features, making it effective for identifying diagnostic patterns at multiple scales32.

The iMDPath-Pred module processes input pathological images represented as 4D tensors with dimensions (B, H, W, C), where B is the batch size, H and W are the spatial dimensions, and C = 3 represents the RGB channels. Through an initial patch partition step, these images are divided into non-overlapping 4 × 4 patches33. These patches are projected into a higher-dimensional feature space through linear embedding, generating feature maps of shape (B, H/patch size, W/patch size, C). Next, feature maps undergo progressive merging through the four stages, with feature dimensions of 128, 256, 512, and 1024, respectively. Each swin transformer block includes layer normalization (LayerNorm), window-based multi-head self-attention (W-MSA), shifted window multi-head self-attention (SW-MSA), and a multi-layer perceptron (MLP). While W-MSA captures local features within defined windows, SW-MSA employs window shifting to enable global feature extraction, enhancing the model’s capability to process diverse pathological patterns. Residual connections improve training stability and mitigate vanishing gradients, producing rich feature maps essential for diagnosis. The subsequent patch merging further captures multi-scale features by concatenating adjacent patches, reducing spatial resolution, and increasing feature dimensionality. A 1 × 1 convolution is applied to manage complexity while retaining critical diagnostic information.

The final feature map is globally pooled and passed through a softmax function for classification, effectively combining local and global information. We employed transfer learning to train the Swin-B model. Pre-trained weights from ImageNet were used to initialize the network, with the classification head fine-tuned on the dataset. The model was further optimized by unfreezing all layers and refining performance using the stochastic gradient descent (SGD) optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001. We trained the model for 100 epochs to minimize cross entropy loss, with the model achieving the lowest validation loss selected for evaluation. This approach ensured robust diagnostic performance tailored to pathological image analysis.

Comparison between iMDPath and baseline models

This study implemented several baseline models to systematically compare and evaluate the performance of the modules in the iMDPath framework. For the iMDPath-Aug module, we utilized traditional image preprocessing techniques and the VAE as baseline approaches to assess the effectiveness of the proposed VQ-VAE for data augmentation performance35. The traditional preprocessing methods, including random cropping, resizing, horizontal flipping, color jittering, and rotation, served as key reference points, enabling the evaluation of the iMDPath-Aug module’s ability to enhance image diversity and robustness. Additionally, the VAE, as a control generative model, provided a comparative framework to assess the potential benefits of integrating vector quantization in the image enhancement process. For the iMDPath-Pred module, we selected VGG16, InceptionV3, and ResNet18 as benchmark models, given their proven efficacy and widespread application in image classification tasks36. To justify our choice of Swin-B architecture, we conducted comprehensive comparisons with representative Transformer variants, including ViT-B and MaxViT-T37,38. We further benchmarked our proposed architecture against two representative pathology foundation models: Phikon and UNI39. The foundation models, trained with self-supervised learning on large-scale pathology datasets, represent the current state-of-the-art in computational pathology. These well-established deep learning architectures were chosen to provide a robust baseline, facilitating a thorough evaluation of our proposed model’s performance in classifying pathological images. For a fair comparison, all backbone networks were connected to an identical lightweight classification head, a single fully connected layer followed by softmax activation, and optimized end-to-end using cross-entropy loss.
