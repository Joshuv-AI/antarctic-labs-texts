Reflux, Reprogramming, and Progenitor Fate: Reframing the Origins of Esophageal Dysplasia
Authors: Michael Quante
Published: 2025

Gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma (GEAC) and gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) cancers are rising in incidence and remain among the most lethal gastrointestinal malignancies. In a recent issue of Cellular and Molecular Gastroenterology and Hepatology, Abuhussein et al1 extend previous work2 and now provide a mechanistic advance in our understanding of how epithelial transformation occurs at the squamocolumnar junction, revealing a reflux-responsive transcriptional axis that controls progenitor cell fate.3 Of particular interest is the study’s characterization of GPRC5B as a downstream effector of GATA activation and a surface-accessible marker of high-grade dysplasia (HGD). GPRC5B expression not only increases in HGD tissue but functionally recapitulates dysplastic phenotypes when overexpressed in Barrett’s esophagus (BE)-derived stem cells. These data extend the clinical biomarker landscape beyond traditional histologic markers like TFF3 and MUC2, offering a functional readout of progenitor cell reprogramming that may support future theranostic approaches. It reveals a critical molecular axis driving the progression from BE to HGD and EAC. The authors identify the transcriptional fulcrum between classical GATA factors (GATA4/6) and the atypical repressor TRPS1, which influences lineage fate decisions in the esophageal epithelium. Through integrative analyses—including TCGA datasets, spatial transcriptomics, and organ-on-a-chip models—they demonstrate that GATA4/6 are associated with gastroesophageal reflux disease-driven columnar metaplasia, whereas TRPS1 maintains squamous lineage identity. Central to this axis is the GATA-inducible receptor GPRC5B, which is shown to drive a proliferative, dysplastic phenotype when overexpressed in BE-derived stem cells. These findings uncover GPRC5B as a novel functional biomarker and potential therapeutic target in the dysplastic transition toward GEAC.

This work advances a growing stem cell-centric model of GEJ carcinogenesis.3 Rather than a strict sequential model (inflammation → metaplasia → dysplasia), evidence now supports a model of fate divergence from a common progenitor pool, shaped by environmental insults and mutational status. Studies from linage tracing in the mouse model4 and by Nowicki-Osuch et al using spatial and single-cell transcriptomics have already highlighted the cardia origin of BE, identifying transcriptional similarities between BE and gastric progenitor populations rather than esophageal squamous cells.5 Abuhussein et al now provide an additional functional transcriptional mechanism underpinning this transition.

This idea is further reinforced by Lian et al, who demonstrate that p53 mutations redirect squamocolumnar junction progenitors toward dysplasia rather than metaplasia, suggesting that BE and GEAC may arise in parallel, not linearly, with p53 acting as a key determinant of fate outcome.6 In the absence of TP53 mutation, inflammation promotes metaplastic differentiation; with TP53 mutation, the same stress drives direct dysplastic transformation.

These findings converge with earlier studies using a mouse model, which revealed that gastric cardia stem cells expand under reflux and inflammation to form metaplastic and dysplastic lesions.4 The regulatory role of the Farnesoid X Receptor (FXR), as recently shown by Baumeister et al,7 adds a critical link between bile acid signaling and progenitor fate: FXR is expressed in BE but lost in gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma. Its deletion promotes progenitor expansion and dysplasia, whereas pharmacologic activation with obeticholic acid reverses these effects and restores mucosal homeostasis.3

Together, these studies reveal that reflux and bile acids act not simply as irritants, but as instructive signals that rewire transcriptional programs in GEJ progenitor cells. Abuhussein’s identification of the GATA4/6–GPRC5B axis adds a precise molecular target to this framework, potentially enabling early detection of HGD through molecular imaging or biomarker-driven surveillance.

Clinically, this paradigm shift moves us away from static histological classifications toward a functional, molecularly informed understanding of GEAC risk. Although endoscopic monitoring of metaplasia remains standard, the majority of GEAC cases are diagnosed in individuals without prior BE detection. Integrating molecular markers like GPRC5B and considering stem cell-directed pathways such as FXR may shift future paradigms from passive histologic observation to active molecular interception. Rather than monitoring the presence of goblet cells or low-grade dysplasia alone, future strategies should assess transcriptional plasticity and cell state markers like GPRC5B, and target upstream regulators such as FXR and GATA factors.

In conclusion, Abuhussein et al provide compelling evidence that reflux-triggered transcriptional reprogramming of GEJ progenitor cells governs early dysplastic transformation, with GPRC5B as a central effector and potential biomarker. They offer an integrated mechanistic framework linking reflux-induced transcriptional changes, stem cell reprogramming, and dysplastic progression. Their concept of a GATA/TRPS1 fulcrum acting through GPRC5B closely mirrors and extends the work on transdifferentiation and expansion of reflux-sensitive progenitors, while providing translational extensions relevant focus on early detection and biomarker stratification. When integrated with findings on FXR signaling, p53-driven fate divergence, and gastric cardia stem cell activation, a coherent model emerges: BE and GEAC represent alternative outcomes of progenitor cell plasticity under environmental and genetic modulation. This work marks a step toward the molecular interception of gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma at its earliest stages.
