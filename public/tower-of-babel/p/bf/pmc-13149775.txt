Machine learning approach for adult age estimation using dental characteristics on panoramic radiographs
Authors: Donghwan Lee, Sehyun Oh, Seyeon Hwang, Jiae Park, Yujin Oh, Akiko Kumagai, Sang-Seob Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dental characteristics are increasingly considered valuable indicators for adult age estimation in forensic and clinical contexts, yet conventional methods struggle with complex dental data. Conventional linear regression relies on predefined equations and the number of dental characteristics aggregated across the dentition, which may lead to information loss and reduced accuracy. This study investigated machine learning methods to enhance age estimation using encoded dental characteristics. Panoramic radiographs of 2,415 individuals aged 20 to 89 years were analyzed, and dental characteristics were encoded using a modified one-hot encoding approach that preserved dual-feature information. A numerical study compared linear regression and six machine learning methods, including random forest, extreme gradient boosting, k-nearest neighbors, support vector machine, decision tree, and multi-layer perceptron. Model performance was evaluated using the root mean squared error (RMSE) and R-squared statistic on the test dataset. In the sex-aggregated dataset, linear regression achieved an RMSE of 12.04 years with an R-squared of 0.63, whereas the best-performing machine learning model achieved an RMSE of 10.88 years with an R-squared of 0.70. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis revealed that sound and missing teeth were the most influential predictors of age estimation. Given the magnitude of prediction error, the proposed models should be interpreted as adjunctive tools for dental age estimation in adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45271-0.

Introduction

Dental age estimation is important in forensic science, clinical dentistry, and anthropology, supporting human identification, pediatric treatment planning, and skeletal age estimation1–3. Among the various biological indicators used for age estimation, teeth are considered particularly advantageous because dental development follows a relatively consistent and regulated pattern4. However, in adults, the utility of age estimation based on tooth development is limited, as these developmental processes are typically completed by early adulthood in most individuals5. Accordingly, alternative indicators for adult age estimation should be considered, including regressive dental changes related to physiological aging and functional wear, as well as treatment-induced modifications6,7. Dental treatments often result in irreversible alterations, and the number and variety of such changes tend to increase over time, reflecting an individual’s cumulative dental history. Evaluating these accumulated changes may elucidate age-related patterns in dental characteristics and enhance the applicability of age estimation in adult populations.

Previous studies have applied conventional statistical methods to analyze the relationship between quantitative dental characteristics and chronological age7–11. In particular, Cheong et al.7 and Oh et al.8 employed multiple linear regression models that considered only the total number of dental characteristics. In such models, predictors are often constructed as count features from a limited subset of dental-characteristics codes, which may discard tooth-level and multi-code information. Although tooth-level modeling could preserve these details, it would require high-dimensional predictors in a conventional regression framework, increasing the risk of multicollinearity and complicating model specification and variable selection, thereby limiting scalability and reproducibility. Furthermore, conventional linear regression models assume a uniform linear relationship between all dental characteristics and chronological age, which may not be valid for features that follow non-linear or region-specific patterns.

Given these limitations, many researchers have adopted machine learning (ML) techniques as alternatives to conventional regression models. Recent studies applying ML to dental age estimation have reported performances comparable to conventional methods and may offer improved scalability for complex and large-scale datasets12–14. In contrast to conventional regression methods, which are constrained by predefined linear associations, ML models can automatically detect and utilize intricate interactions among multiple dental characteristics, thereby uncovering more complex patterns through iterative training and optimization15,16. However, recent literature suggests that machine learning models may not automatically resolve systematic estimation errors, as age-dependent residual trends have been reported to persist in some ML-based dental age estimation studies16. Thus, careful interpretation of ML outcomes is warranted.

Therefore, this study aimed to develop an ML-based model for adult age estimation using tooth-specific encoding of all nine dental-characteristics codes, including positional and dual-feature information observed on panoramic radiographs. The performance of the proposed ML model was evaluated by comparing its accuracy with that of the conventional regression models, and by examining systematic residual patterns across the age range to assess whether systematic errors were mitigated.

Materials and methods

Data collection

The inclusion criterion was panoramic radiographs of patients of both sexes, aged between 20.00 and 89.99 years, who were treated at Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital, The Catholic University of Korea. The exclusion criterion was radiographs showing only sound teeth without any observable dental characteristics.

In this study, sound teeth were defined as an anatomically intact crown with no evidence of restorative or prosthetic treatment and no observable treatment- or condition-related dental characteristics. Pathologic conditions, such as cystic lesions and periodontal disease, were not considered in this classification. In contrast, teeth with a gross crown defect, such as extensive caries, fracture, or loss of restorative material, were classified as having a defect-related dental characteristic. As this study relies on identifying specific dental characteristics, radiographs exhibiting exclusively sound teeth were excluded because they lack the feature variance requisite for learning age-related patterns.

The panoramic radiographs were acquired using two panoramic units: RAYSCAN Alpha (Ray Co., Ltd., Hwaseong-si, Korea; 71 kVp, 12 mA, 14.1 s) and PaX-i3D Green (Vatech Co., Ltd., Hwaseong-si, Korea; 71 kVp, 14 mA, 13.5 s). The panoramic images were exported in JPEG format, with a size of 3000 × 1500 pixels.

A total of 2,415 panoramic radiographs acquired between January 1, 2022 and March 31, 2023 were retrospectively collected through random sampling, and the datasets were stratified by sex and decade-based age groups with approximately evenly distributed numbers of patients (Table 1). The chronological age of each radiograph was determined by calculating the difference between the date of birth and the date of radiograph acquisition.

Evaluation of radiographic dental characteristics

The dental characteristics observed on panoramic radiographs were classified according to the criteria proposed by Cheong et al.7(Table 2). The nine dental codes were sound tooth (V), missing tooth (X), impacted tooth (I), defect (D), residual root (R), root canal treatment (T), filling (F), prosthesis (P), and dental implant (L). For each tooth observed on the panoramic radiographs, a single code was assigned if only one characteristic was identified. However, when two different characteristics were observed in the same tooth, a dual-code system was applied to represent both features simultaneously. All four third molars were excluded from the analysis because of the high variability in their presence and development.

Age estimation methods: conventional and ML methods

To develop an age estimation model, we explored both conventional regression-based methods and ML techniques. Cheong et al.7 proposed a nine-code classification system (Table 2). However, in their regression modeling they used only five selected codes (X, T, F, P, and L) as explanatory variables. Oh et al.8 similarly adopted a count-based regression approach using these five codes. In the present linear regression (LR) analysis17, we extended this approach by incorporating a complete set of nine dental characteristic codes (V, X, I, D, R, T, F, P, and L). In addition, analyses were performed under three different dental combinations, as summarized in Table 3. Following the approach of Cheong et al.7 and Oh et al.8, tooth-level dental characteristics were transformed into patient-level numerical feature vectors by counting the number of teeth corresponding to each dental characteristic. Specifically, for each subject, the frequency of each dental characteristic was calculated across all teeth, and these counts were used as explanatory variables. As an illustrative example, each subject was assumed to have 28 permanent teeth. Tooth-level dental characteristics were aggregated into patient-level explanatory variables by counting the number of teeth assigned to each dental characteristic category. For instance, a subject with 16 sound teeth (V), 2 missing teeth (X), 0 impacted teeth (I), 0 teeth with defects (D), 0 residual roots (R), 4 teeth with root canal treatment (T), 2 filled teeth (F), 8 teeth with prostheses (P), and 2 dental implants (L) was represented by the feature vector (V, X, I, D, R, T, F, P, L) = (16, 2, 0, 0, 0, 4, 2, 8, 2) (Fig. 1).

However, this count-based representation does not preserve tooth-level positional information and cannot capture situations in which multiple dental conditions coexist within a single tooth. For example, when dual codes such as PT, PL, or PX are present, the count-based aggregation ignores this co-occurrence and treats each condition independently, resulting in a loss of information at the individual tooth level.

To address this limitation, a modified one-hot encoding scheme was applied. In this encoding approach, each of the 28 dental positions was expanded into nine binary features corresponding to the dental characteristic codes. Unlike standard one-hot encoding, which restricts each tooth to a single active category, the modified scheme allows dual-feature representation by assigning a value of 1 to multiple categories when more than one condition coexists within the same tooth. For instance, if the left mandibular first molar exhibits a PT condition in Fig. 1, both the T and P features are assigned a value of 1, resulting in the tooth’s vector (V, X, I, D, R, T, F, P, L) = (0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0). This approach preserves the composite nature of dental status without information loss. To improve computational efficiency, feature columns that contained only zeros across all samples were removed.

This modified one-hot encoding was used as input for all ML models, including random forest (RF)18, extreme gradient boosting (XGB)19, k-nearest neighbors (KNN)20, support vector machine (SVM)21, decision tree (DT)22, and multi-layer perceptron (MLP)23. In addition, to enable a direct and fair comparison between conventional and ML approaches, LR models were also fitted using the modified one-hot encoded features alongside the count-based representations.

Modeling strategy: repeated nested cross-validation

To obtain an unbiased estimation of model performance and to prevent optimistic bias arising from hyperparameter tuning, a repeated nested cross-validation (CV)24 was employed for all ML models.

In the nested CV framework, the dataset is first partitioned in the outer loop, and each outer iteration designated a subset of data as a held-out test dataset. Models are trained on the remaining data and evaluated on this outer test data, ensuring that performance estimates reflect generalization to unseen data. Within each outer training set, an inner cross-validation loop is employed to optimize model hyperparameters. Hyperparameter tuning is typically conducted using grid search or similar optimization strategies, with a predefined loss function serving as the selection criterion. After identifying the optimal hyperparameters, a final model is trained on the full outer training set and evaluated on the corresponding outer test set.

Because the results of cross-validation can be sensitive to a single random data partition, the nested CV procedure can be repeated multiple times using different random splits. Repetition reduces the variance of performance estimates and provides more stable summaries of model performance across independent realizations.

In the present study, this repeated nested CV framework was implemented for all ML models. Analyses were conducted separately according to sex and dental combination settings. Specifically, a five-fold CV was used for both the outer and inner loops. Hyperparameter optimization was performed using grid search, with the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) employed as the optimization criterion. For each model, the entire nested CV procedure was repeated 20 times with different random seeds, and performance metrics were aggregated across all repetitions and folds to obtain estimates of predictive performance.

LR models do not involve hyperparameter tuning and were therefore not subjected to a nested CV procedure. Instead, LR models were evaluated using the same repeated outer CV splits as those used in the outer loops of the ML analyses, without an inner tuning loop. This approach ensured that both LR and ML models were assessed on identical held-out outer test folds, enabling a fair and consistent comparison of predictive performance. Model evaluation

The performances of both the LR and ML models were evaluated using three key metrics: RMSE, mean absolute error (MAE), and out-of-sample R-squared (R2)25,26.

The RMSE measures the standard deviation of the residuals, quantifying the average difference between the observed and predicted values in the test set. A lower RMSE indicates higher model accuracy in estimating age.

The MAE measures the average absolute difference between the observed and predicted values in the test set, providing a direct indication of the typical magnitude of prediction errors. A lower MAE indicates higher model accuracy in age estimation.

R2, the coefficient of determination, represents the proportion of variance in the observed test data that can be explained by the model. This out-of-sample R2 is computed on the outer test folds of the repeated nested cross-validation as a predictive performance metric, using the training-set mean as the reference. An R2 value closer to 1 indicates that the model accounts for a large portion of the variability in the data, suggesting a higher predictive performance.

Statistical analysis of dental characteristics across age groups

To examine whether dental characteristics varied across different age groups, Fisher’s Exact Test27 was applied to contingency tables constructed with age groups as rows and dental characteristics as columns. The null hypothesis (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{H}_{0}$$\end{document}) stated that the distribution of dental characteristics was consistent across age groups, whereas the alternative hypothesis (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{H}_{1}$$\end{document}) posited that dental characteristics differed significantly by age. Given the categorical nature of the dental characteristic variables and the presence of low expected cell frequencies, Fisher’s Exact Test was selected over Pearson’s chi-square test28. To overcome the computational burden posed by large contingency tables, a Monte Carlo simulation with 100,000 replicates (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:B=\mathrm{100,000})$$\end{document} was employed to approximate the p-value.

Observer reliability

Two experienced observers in forensic dentistry independently evaluated the dental characteristics on panoramic radiographs after pre-calibration. A total of 385 radiographs, constituting approximately 16% of the full dataset, were randomly selected for observer reliability analysis. The unit of analysis was the individual tooth, and the dental characteristics were evaluated for each position to calculate the Cohen’s kappa coefficient. Intra-observer reliability was based on the evaluation by a single observer with a two-month interval between repeated assessments.

Results

Reliability of observer assessments

The kappa coefficients for dental characteristics were 0.968 and 0.955 for intra- and inter-observer reliability, respectively, indicating almost perfect agreement (P < 0.001)29.

Discussion

This study evaluated whether tooth-specific encoding of nine radiographic dental-characteristics codes, including dual-feature information, could improve adult age estimation compared with conventional count-based LR. The best-performing configuration was Model 1, which used all sextants with XGB, yielding an RMSE of 10.88 years and an R2 of 0.70 on the sex-aggregated dataset. Notably, the improvement over linear regression was modest, and substantial prediction errors remained, particularly across the full adult age range.

Conventional dental characteristic-based age estimation and its limitations

Conventional regression-based approaches for adult dental age estimation often rely on aggregated count-based features, which can obscure tooth-level and multi-feature patterns. Using predefined mathematical equations to model the relationship between dental variables and age30, these methods often assume simplified linear relationships that may overlook non-linear interactions and variation by tooth position, which can reduce accuracy.

Consistent with these limitations, Oh et al.8 constructed regression models primarily using the total number of dental characteristics as variables and reported a test set RMSE of approximately 15.60 years with an adjusted R2 of 0.56 in their best-performing configuration. In this study, the count-based LR models achieved lower errors, with an RMSE of 12.82 years and an R2 of 0.59 in the sex-aggregated subset using the posterior-only model (Supplementary Table S2). Applying tooth-specific encoding further reduced the RMSE to 12.04 years with an R2 of 0.63 (Supplementary Table S3), although the improvement was modest and not consistent across all subsets and dentition regions. This suggested that more adaptable modeling techniques, such as ML algorithms, may be required to fully exploit the tooth-level encoded information.

Comparison of ML and conventional regression-based approaches

Accordingly, ML algorithms were evaluated alongside conventional linear regression models for adult dental age estimation. ML-based approaches showed marginally lower error than conventional regression models, with RF and XGB demonstrating the lowest RMSE among the evaluated methods. Compared with the LR models using encoded dental characteristics on the sex-aggregated dataset in Model 1, the best-performing XGB model showed a small difference in RMSE and R2. Given the prediction error observed in this study, the proposed models are not suitable for individual-level standalone decisions and should be utilized as adjunctive tools alongside other age indicators. Notably, several dental characteristics reflect treatment- and care-related history that is not determined by age alone, which may limit the achievable performance of individual-level age estimation. In non-invasive radiograph-based age estimation across broad age ranges, similar error levels have also been reported31, highlighting the intrinsic difficulty of precise individual-level age estimation in adults.

In addition, the residual analysis (Fig. 5) revealed pronounced, age-dependent error patterns across both LR and ML models, with marked overestimation in younger individuals and underestimation in older age groups, particularly toward the extremes of the age range. As shown in Fig. 5, while ML models showed only a modest reduction in residual magnitude and dispersion compared with LR, age-dependent residual trends persisted, indicating that the ML approach did not fully mitigate systematic age-related bias and that it remains a major challenge in adult dental age estimation16.

Regional contribution of dentition

Across both LR and ML approaches, model performance varied by dental sextant, with the model using only anterior teeth (Model 2) consistently showing inferior performance compared with the posterior-only (Model 3) and full dentition (Model 1) models. In the sex-aggregated subset using LR with tooth-specific encoding, RMSEs were 12.04, 15.77, and 12.04 years for Models 1, 2, and 3, respectively (Supplementary Table S3), whereas Cheong et al.7 reported 15.23, 17.29, and 14.78 years for the same sextant combinations using a count-based regression approach. These findings consistently suggest that anterior teeth provide less age-informative variation in dental characteristics than posterior teeth, which may contribute to less accurate age estimation, as also noted in previous studies7,32.

In both male and female subsets, the performance difference between the full dentition and posterior-only models was small across LR and ML models. Including anterior teeth had little impact on overall accuracy, but its effect tended to be slightly more favorable in females than in males. In females, the anterior teeth, which are more frequently visible, may be subject to more frequent dental treatments for aesthetic reasons, potentially increasing their relevance in age estimation compared with males33. These sex-specific findings may reflect sex-related differences in treatment patterns affecting anterior teeth, but the magnitude of the differences was small and should be interpreted cautiously. Future research may consider region-specific modeling strategies and whether the relative contribution of anterior and posterior teeth varies by sex and clinical context.

Interpretability of tooth specific encoding

As shown in Fig. 7, the SHAP plots for the best-performing ML models, all based on the full dentition configuration, highlight largely similar influential tooth-specific characteristics across subsets. Dental characteristics related to sound teeth (V) and missing teeth (X) were consistently among the most influential characteristics across all subsets. The present study showed that V codes were more prevalent in younger individuals, whereas X codes appeared more frequently in older individuals, suggesting that these two codes may function as opposite indicators of age and contribute substantially to the model’s performance. Importantly, the prominence of V- and X-related signals may also reflect cohort- and context-dependent factors such as preventive care, access to restorative treatment, and extraction practices, which can vary across healthcare systems and change over time. Accordingly, interpretations of SHAP values should be made cautiously, particularly when considering temporal or cross-population generalizability.

In addition, the most influential tooth-specific characteristics were derived from posterior teeth, consistent with the regional performance patterns observed in the previous section showing that posterior teeth undergo restorative treatment more frequently than anterior teeth, which may increase their potential contribution to adult age estimation7,32. The V codes of the maxillary canines were also influential across subsets, because canines typically have long roots and are relatively resistant to extraction or extensive treatment, and the persistence of sound canines may represent a stable and clinically plausible signal associated with a younger dental-characteristics pattern in this dataset.

Comparison with previous ML approaches for dental age estimation

Lee et al.13 developed an age-group classification model using various ML algorithms applied to eighteen radiomorphometric parameters extracted from dental panoramic radiographs of 471 Koreans aged 10 to 69 years. These parameters included quantitative measurements, such as tooth and pulp cavity areas, positions of anatomical landmarks, and the presence of restorations or periodontal conditions. They noted that younger and older groups could be more readily distinguished because some features at the extremes show more linear age relationships. Lee et al.13 also cautioned that their dataset was skewed toward younger individuals, with approximately 58% of samples younger than 29 years, which may have affected performance, particularly in younger age groups.

In contrast, the present study estimated chronological age using expert-coded, clinically intuitive dental characteristics observed on panoramic radiographs, without time-intensive radiomorphometric measurements. Nevertheless, the proposed model achieved performance comparable to or better than a prior regression-based model8. In addition, the present study included a more evenly distributed adult population ranging from 20 to 89 years to mitigate age-related bias and to enhance the model’s generalizability across adult age groups.

However, due to the substantial prediction errors observed in the present study, the proposed models may be most appropriate as adjunctive tools rather than standalone methods. In forensic practice, dental findings are commonly considered alongside skeletal indicators within a multi-region assessment framework34, and these sources of information should be viewed as complementary. Future studies may improve reliability by combining dental characteristics with additional morphometric features and by validating models in larger, more diverse cohorts.

Challenges and future research

Despite encouraging results, the substantial prediction errors and the lack of external validation indicate that several challenges remain. First, the selection and representation of dental characteristics require further optimization. Although this study employed a modified one-hot encoding, future research could investigate more sophisticated encoding strategies, such as embedding techniques or convolutional feature extraction from raw radiographic images, to better reflect spatial and morphological patterns in dental structures35. These approaches may address key limitations of manually assigned categorical codes by quantifying severity and extent of dental features, thereby providing a more granular representation of dental aging than traditional categorical assignments. In line with recent advances in automated dental image analysis36–38, the tooth-level labels established in this study may support image-based models that automatically detect teeth and assign the corresponding dental characteristic categories. In such image-based deep learning settings, standardized pre-processing should be implemented to reduce device-dependent artifacts and to improve reproducibility and generalizability. In addition, multimodal fusion architectures combining image-derived embeddings with structured dental-characteristics codes may improve performance and interpretability39, and explainable AI approaches remain essential for transparent forensic and clinical practice40.

Second, the generalizability of the present findings is constrained by the single-institution design and potential selection bias, including the exclusion of radiographs with only sound teeth. Future studies should incorporate broader cohorts, explicitly include radiographs with only sound teeth for comparison, and perform external validation using independent datasets from other institutions. Moreover, dental treatment patterns are influenced by various external factors, such as dietary habits, oral hygiene practices, regional differences, insurance coverage, and socioeconomic status, which were not considered in this study. Given that these factors can significantly affect the prevalence and frequency of dental restorations and treatments, future research should consider comprehensive demographic and socioeconomic data to evaluate the generalizability of the proposed model across diverse conditions. Additionally, developing region-specific models based on locally sourced datasets may be valuable, as such tailored approaches could offer more accurate age estimations for specific regions.

In conclusion, ML-based models using dental characteristics with tooth-specific encoding showed modest numerical differences in performance compared with conventional linear regression in this single-institution dataset. These findings suggest that tooth-specific encoding may retain potentially informative patterns beyond count-based features, although the overall prediction error in this study remained substantial. Therefore, the present approach should be considered a preliminary and adjunctive tool rather than a standalone method for individual-level adult age estimation.
