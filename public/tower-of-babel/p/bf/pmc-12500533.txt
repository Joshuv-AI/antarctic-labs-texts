Deep Learning for Early Detection of Cardiovascular Diseases From Medical Imaging
Authors: Sangeeta Davi, Mukesh Kumar, Zainab Muhammad Hanif, Ashvin Kumar, Muskan Kumari, F N U Ridham, Aiman Salam Shaikh, Insiya Fatima Azad, Manesh Kumar, F N U Suwasi, F N U Venjhraj, Amogh Verma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain a leading cause of mortality worldwide, making early detection vital for reducing morbidity and death rates. Echocardiography is a widely used, noninvasive imaging tool for diagnosing CVDs, but manual interpretation can be time‐consuming and subject to variability. This study aims to evaluate the performance of a deep learning model using echocardiogram videos for the early detection of CVDs.MethodsWe applied a convolutional neural network (CNN), based on the ResNet‐50 architecture, to the EchoNet‐Dynamic data set, which includes echocardiogram videos. Preprocessing involved resizing frames and applying augmentation techniques to enhance model robustness. The data set was split into training (80%) and testing (20%) subsets. The model was trained to classify patients based on the presence or absence of CVD using temporal video features.ResultsThe CNN model achieved strong performance metrics, with an overall accuracy of 92.3%, a precision of 91.5%, a recall of 92.7%, and an F1‐score of 92.1%. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC‐ROC) was 0.95, indicating excellent discriminatory ability. These results highlight the model's capability to detect CVDs accurately from dynamic echocardiographic imaging.ConclusionThis study demonstrates the potential of deep learning, particularly CNN‐based models, for automating the early detection of CVDs using echocardiogram videos. The high performance of the model suggests it could contribute to faster, more accurate, and cost‐effective diagnosis in clinical practice. Future research should focus on improving model generalizability across diverse populations and enhancing interpretability for integration into clinical workflows.Keywords: cardiovascular diseases, deep learning, echocardiography, medical imaging

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are a major global health concern, accounting for millions of deaths each year. Early diagnosis is essential for effective treatment and better patient outcomes. Echocardiography is a widely used noninvasive imaging technique that allows for the real‐time assessment of heart function. However, the interpretation of echocardiograms is complex, requiring significant expertise and time. Traditional methods of analysis often rely on manual inspection, which can lead to delays and errors. The early detection of CVDs remains essential for better patient welfare and quicker treatment access which leads to decreased mortality statistics. The process of manually reading echocardiograms for CVD diagnosis takes considerable time while also exposing pathologists to human interpretation mistakes. Medical image analysis automation receives increasing interest through deep learning (DL) advancements in artificial intelligence for improved diagnostic precision and operational effectiveness [1, 2, 3].

Medical professionals extensively use echocardiography as a noninvasive diagnostic tool to identify multiple cardiovascular abnormalities [3, 4]. Medical experts face difficulties in interpreting echocardiogram videos along with maintaining expertise in a field where patient data continues to grow rapidly. The field of medical image analysis received a major boost from convolutional neural networks (CNNs) which demonstrate excellent potential to automate medical image diagnostics including echocardiograms [5]. Such models demonstrate unique abilities to discover patterns together with abnormalities present in images which render them ideal choices for CVD detection programs.

The research focuses on employing CNNs to identify CVDs within echocardiogram video recordings at an early stage. This model aims to improve clinical outcomes by assisting healthcare professionals in the early detection of CVDs. It reduces the time spent manually reviewing echocardiogram videos by automating the classification of heart failure (HF) and left ventricular dysfunction (LVD), enabling clinicians to focus on high‐risk cases. The use of Grad‐CAM heatmaps provides visual insights into key areas of the heart, enhancing diagnostic accuracy and offering a second opinion for validation. Additionally, the model's potential extends to detecting arrhythmias and other CVDs by integrating ECG data, improving overall heart function assessments. Clinical validation through collaboration with cardiologists and pilot studies will refine the model, ensuring its accuracy, relevance, and alignment with evidence‐based practices for broader clinical adoption. A CNN‐based model was developed to determine CVD presence based on data gathered from over 100,000 frames within the EchoNet‐Dynamic data set taken from echocardiogram videos. The approach incorporates video data temporal features as a modern diagnostic solution which exceeds traditional static image‐based models. DL proves effective for automatic diagnosis of CVDs through our study's findings which bring significant benefits to clinical practice. Research creates groundwork for using AI in medical systems where it provides prompt dependable diagnostic instruments for cardiac disorders to healthcare professionals.

Literature Review

Medical image analysis using DL has been widely explored in recent years [5, 6, 7, 8]. Numerous studies have demonstrated the ability of CNNs to achieve high accuracy in detecting abnormalities from X‐rays, MRIs, and CT scans [9, 10, 11]. In the context of CVDs, previous research has shown that CNNs can effectively analyze echocardiograms for the identification of various heart conditions, such as HF, LVD, and valve diseases. Recent advancements in DL have revolutionized medical image analysis [12], with CNNs emerging as a powerful tool for diagnosing CVDs. Echocardiography, a widely used imaging technique, has been the focal point of several studies leveraging DL for CVD detection. CNNs, known for their ability to automatically extract features from images, have demonstrated remarkable success in analyzing echocardiograms, providing solutions to challenges like manual inspection errors and time constraints [4, 13, 14]. While models like VGG‐16 and Inception‐V3 focus on static image classification tasks, our video‐based approach leverages temporal features from echocardiogram videos, offering a more dynamic understanding of heart conditions. As seen in Study [7], static models like VGG‐16 may struggle to capture the temporal dynamics necessary for accurate HF and LVD classification, which is better achieved through video‐based models.

In the realm of CVD detection, multiple studies have shown that CNNs can effectively detect various heart conditions, such as HF, valve diseases, and LVD [15]. For instance [16, 17], used CNNs to classify echocardiograms and reported a significant improvement in diagnostic accuracy compared to traditional methods. Previous work, such as in Study [4], highlights the importance of multicenter validation to address biases in single‐center data sets. Our model, trained on EchoNet‐Dynamic, will benefit from future validation on diverse data sets like CAMUS to ensure generalizability across varied patient populations. Similarly, the EchoNet‐Dynamic data set [18], consisting of over 100,000 frames from echocardiogram videos, has been used in several studies to train DL models for CVD detection, demonstrating high performance in automatic heart condition classification. Despite the success of CNNs in static image analysis, video‐based approaches are less explored. Utilizing the temporal features from echocardiogram videos has the potential to enhance model accuracy by capturing dynamic aspects of heart function that are difficult to identify in static images [19]. The incorporation of temporal data allows for a more nuanced understanding of cardiovascular health, which can improve diagnostic precision. However, challenges such as data set variability, model interpretability, and generalization across diverse patient populations remain prevalent [20, 21]. Overall, while DL has proven to be a promising tool in CVD diagnosis, further work is needed to address issues related to generalization, data augmentation, and the integration of multimodal data for robust and interpretable clinical applications.

Methodology

This study uses ResNet‐50 with 3D convolutions and long short‐term memory (LSTM) layers to detect CVDs from echocardiogram videos. Data preprocessing includes resizing, normalization, and augmentation of the video frames. To differentiate between HF and LVD, the model used an LVEF cut‐off of 40%. An LVEF ≤ 40% was used to identify HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), while LVEF > 40% indicated LVD, including HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) in the absence of other symptoms. This cut‐off follows standard clinical practices for HF diagnosis. In this study, we categorized LVEF as either normal (LVEF > 50%) or abnormal (LVEF ≤ 50%). This dichotomization simplifies the distinction between HF and LVD, with abnormal LVEF encompassing both HFrEF and LVD, which share similar functional impairments but may have different underlying causes. Temporal features are extracted through 3D convolutions, while LSTM layers capture long‐range dependencies. The model is trained using categorical cross‐entropy and the Adam optimizer, with early stopping to prevent overfitting. The model's performance is evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1‐score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC‐ROC). Grad‐CAM is employed for interpretability, highlighting key regions in the echocardiogram to assist clinicians in validating model predictions. Figure 1 illustrates the architecture of the model, which processes echocardiogram video frames through various layers to classify CVDs. Echocardiogram video processing for CVD detection involves challenges like high computational costs, frame rate standardization, and data augmentation trade‐offs. The use of 3D convolutions and LSTM layers requires significant computational resources, with training taking around 20 h on a Tesla V100 GPU. Techniques like gradient checkpointing and mixed precision training helped optimize memory and speed. To ensure consistent temporal features, videos with varying frame rates were standardized to 30 fps, adding 15–20 min of preprocessing time per video. Data augmentation, which improved model robustness, increased training time by 30% and data set size by 3×, but required balancing to avoid overfitting and preserve heart structure, adding 10–15 min per epoch.

Data Collection

For this study, EchoNet‐Dynamic for analysis which contains frames obtained from different segments of more than 2000 echocardiogram videos totaling over 100,000 frames [18]. The database shows an extensive variety of cardiovascular medical issues through video recordings that monitor heart performance at different developmental levels (Figure 2).

The data set comprises 10,030 apical‐4‐chamber echocardiography videos from patients imaged at Stanford University Hospital between 2016 and 2018. Each video was anonymized, cropped, and downsampled to 112 × 112 pixels using cubic interpolation for standardization. [18].

Data Preprocessing

The preprocessing pipeline began with data cleaning to remove noise and irrelevant information, ensuring optimal model performance. Raw echocardiogram frames were standardized by resizing them to 224 × 224 pixels, a common input resolution for CNNs. This step ensured uniformity across the data set and compatibility with the CNN's feature extraction layers. To enhance data set diversity and reduce overfitting, we applied several data augmentation techniques, including rotation, flipping, and scaling. These transformations introduced variability in the training data while preserving the key features relevant to cardiovascular conditions. After preprocessing, each frame was extracted and labeled according to its corresponding cardiovascular condition. The labeled data set was then partitioned into training, validation, and test sets to support model training, hyperparameter tuning, and unbiased performance evaluation.

Model Architecture

The proposed DL model is based on a modified ResNet‐50 CNN and is effective for image‐based classification tasks. Architecture employed in this study is illustrated in Figure 3, Input echocardiographic frames are processed through convolutional layers for feature extraction and pooling layers for dimensionality reduction. Residual blocks within ResNet‐50 help maintain gradient flow, enabling efficient training of deep networks. Extracted features are fed into fully connected layers, followed by a Softmax layer that outputs class probabilities (e.g., normal, HF, LVD). The model is trained using categorical cross‐entropy loss and optimized with the Adam optimizer for robust convergence.

Temporal Feature Extraction

To leverage the dynamic aspects of echocardiogram videos, 3D convolutions are employed to process consecutive frames in a video sequence. Unlike traditional 2D convolutions, which only capture spatial information within a single frame, 3D convolutions simultaneously process both spatial and temporal features by using a sequence of frames, capturing the changes in heart motion and shape over time. This step allows the model to learn motion patterns of the heart as it moves through various phases during the echocardiogram.

Next, LSTM layers are integrated to capture long‐range dependencies within the sequence of frames. LSTM networks are designed to remember long‐term temporal dependencies, making them ideal for sequential data like echocardiogram videos, where the heart's motion and function must be understood across multiple frames. Frame sequences are processed sequentially, where each frame feeds into the network to preserve the temporal dependencies of heart activity. By combining the spatial feature extraction of 3D convolutions and the temporal memory capability of LSTM layers, the model is able to more accurately classify cardiovascular conditions by considering both individual frames and their temporal relationships.

Training and Validation

The data set was split into a training set (80%) and a testing set (20%). The model was trained on the training set for 50 epochs. Early stopping was implemented to prevent overfitting by halting training once the model's performance on the validation set stopped improving. The validation set was used during training to monitor the model's performance and make adjustments to the training process, ensuring optimal performance before final evaluation on the testing set (Figure 4). This approach helped to fine‐tune the model and avoid overfitting, ensuring better generalization to unseen data.

Experiments

The primary goal of this study was to assess the performance of the DL model in classifying CVDs based on echocardiogram video frames. The model was trained using the EchoNet‐Dynamic data set, which consists of a substantial number of labeled echocardiogram video frames, representing a variety of cardiovascular condition. The model was trained on the 80% training set of the data set using the preprocessed frames. Data augmentation techniques such as rotation, flipping, and scaling were used to increase the variability of the data, thereby improving the model's robustness and reducing overfitting.

Hyperparameter Tuning

Hyperparameters such as learning rate, batch size, and number of epochs were tuned using cross‐validation to achieve the optimal performance. The Adam optimizer was chosen due to its adaptability and efficient convergence during training. Key hyperparameters such as learning rate, batch size, and number of epochs were tuned to optimize model performance. Learning rate tested at 0.0001, 0.001, and 0.01. A rate of 0.001 achieved the best performance, with an accuracy of 92.3%, balancing fast convergence without instability. Batch size tested at 16, 32, and 64. A batch size of 32 yielded the best balance between training speed and accuracy. Smaller batches led to slower convergence, while larger batches caused overfitting. The number of epochs rested at 30, 50, and 100. Fifty epochs were optimal, providing enough learning without overfitting (Table 2).

Validation and Early Stopping

The validation set was used to monitor the performance of the model throughout the training process. Early stopping was implemented to halt training if the model's performance on the validation set stopped improving, thus preventing overfitting. The model frequently misclassified HF and LVD, especially when the ejection fraction (EF) was preserved in conditions like HFpEF. These two conditions often exhibit similar features in echocardiogram videos, making it difficult for the model to distinguish between them. Additionally, the model sometimes misclassified normal cases as HF, particularly in early‐stage or subclinical cases. Contributing factors include subtle differences in heart motion, the model's sensitivity to minor abnormalities, and class imbalance in the training data. To address these issues, solutions such as fine‐tuning the model with more targeted data, incorporating attention mechanisms to focus on critical heart regions, and integrating multimodal data like ECG signals are proposed. These approaches would help the model differentiate more accurately between conditions with overlapping symptoms and improve its overall performance.

Data Set Variability and Generalization

The proposed model achieved 92.3% accuracy on the EchoNet‐Dynamic data set, but performance can vary based on factors like video quality and patient demographics. The data set, mainly from a specific patient cohort, may not fully represent the diversity seen in clinical settings, potentially causing bias in predictions. To improve generalization, future studies should test the model on multicenter data sets, like CAMUS, which include more diverse populations.

Video quality also impacts performance. In this study, frames were resized to 224 × 224 pixels, but lower‐quality videos with reduced resolution or frame rate may lead to loss of information, decreasing accuracy. Testing with lower resolutions (e.g., 112 × 112 pixels) and frame rates (e.g., 15 fps) would help evaluate how video quality affects the model, including its robustness to compression artifacts and noise.

Results and Analysis

The performance of the DL model was evaluated on the test set, which consists of 20% of the data not used during the training phase. The evaluation was based on key classification metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1‐score, and AUC‐ROC. The results demonstrated the model's high performance in classifying CVDs from echocardiogram video frames.

The model achieved an impressive accuracy of 92.3%, indicating that it correctly classified 92.3% of the test samples. This result highlights the model's effectiveness in distinguishing between different cardiovascular conditions. The precision of the model was 91.5%, meaning that 91.5% of the instances predicted as positive (indicating the presence of a cardiovascular condition) were correctly identified. This metric emphasizes the model's ability to minimize false positives. The recall score was 92.7%, demonstrating that the model correctly identified 92.7% of all actual positive instances. A high recall is critical in medical diagnostics, as it indicates the model's effectiveness in detecting true positive cases of CVD. The F1‐score, which is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, was 92.1%. This balanced score reflects the model's overall performance in both identifying true positives and minimizing false positives and false negatives. The AUC‐ROC was 0.95, indicating excellent model discrimination ability (Figure 6). This score suggests that the model is highly capable of distinguishing between the presence or absence of cardiovascular conditions, with minimal risk of misclassification.

Model Interpretability

Model interpretability is key for clinical deployment, and while Grad‐CAM heatmaps provide valuable visual insights into model decision‐making, integrating them effectively into clinical workflows is essential. These heatmaps can be displayed alongside echocardiogram reports, helping clinicians visualize key areas involved in diagnosing conditions like HF or LVD. By integrating these heatmaps into electronic health records (EHR), clinicians can easily compare model findings with their own observations, enhancing trust in the model. To ensure clinical utility, the heatmaps should be validated with expert cardiologists through pilot studies, allowing feedback to refine their accuracy and relevance. Additionally, SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) can complement Grad‐CAM by providing a quantitative breakdown of each feature's contribution to the diagnosis. Combining Grad‐CAM and SHAP will offer a more comprehensive and interpretable framework, improving model transparency and facilitating adoption in clinical practice. Future validation will involve cardiologists comparing both methods to ensure they support informed decision‐making in CVD diagnosis.

Discussion

The evaluation shows DL through ResNet‐50 constitutes a powerful system to automate the identification of CVDs in echocardiogram video documents. A remarkable set of results emerged from using this model with 92.3% accuracy rate coupled with 91.5% precision and 0.95 AUC‐ROC score which showcased its ability to detect multiple cardiac conditions effectively. These echocardiogram video features acted as a better replacement for static images since they showed dynamic heart function changes which led to enhanced cardiovascular health understanding. Detecting hidden cardiac abnormalities becomes more effective by using this method compared to standard diagnostic practices. Our findings demonstrate how DL technology enables both accelerated medical diagnoses and decreased instances of human mistakes when reading medical visuals. By providing interpretive Grad‐CAM heatmaps the model enhances doctor's understanding of its decision process which remains crucial for clinician acceptance of the system. Additional obstacles persist in this field. The model presents varying performance levels which depend on video quality together with patient demographics so additional validation using expanded diverse data sets becomes necessary. The clinical deployment of these systems depends on following ethical practices to protect patient privacy and maintain fair model behavior. To address ethical concerns in clinical AI, we'll ensure data privacy through GDPR‐compliant anonymization and encryption. Bias will be mitigated through audits and fairness metrics, using diverse data sets like CAMUS to improve generalizability. Following Ethical AI frameworks, we'll ensure transparency and fairness, supporting clinicians with tools like Grad‐CAM and SHAP.

Clinical Implications and Future Work

The model integration into clinical processes enables instant diagnosis support which also works as a medical decision tool to boost efficiency. The system helps to support training of clinical practitioners and permits ongoing improvements to modeling algorithms. Future research needs to overcome data privacy and bias problems and consider using multiple healthcare data types to improve diagnostic accuracy across different patient populations. Future work will involve testing the model on multicenter data sets like CAMUS to assess its generalization across diverse populations and clinical settings. We will also examine how video quality, including resolution and frame rate, impacts performance, and use data augmentation to simulate low‐quality conditions. Lastly, we plan to evaluate the model's long‐term generalization with longitudinal data sets tracking heart function over time. Integrating ECG, patient demographics, and echocardiogram videos can improve CVD detection by combining electrical and motion data. Aligning ECG with echocardiograms requires techniques like temporal synchronization. Demographic data adds personalization but needs careful encoding and handling of missing values. The increased computational complexity can be managed with efficient architectures, like CNNs and LSTMs. Multimodal fusion methods, such as early or late fusion, enhance model performance by combining insights from both ECG and echocardiogram data for more accurate diagnoses.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the effectiveness of DL, specifically ResNet‐50, in automating the diagnosis of CVDs from echocardiogram videos. The implemented model demonstrated exceptional diagnostic capabilities through a performance outcome of 92.3% accuracy along with precision of 91.5% as well as 0.95 AUC‐ROC score that indicates its potential as a trustworthy diagnostic tool. Video data analysis through temporal features produces better assessment of heart function than conventional image‐based methods do. These findings demonstrate that DL technologies boost CVD detection accuracy as well as operational efficiency, thereby creating affordable and fast healthcare solutions. Follow‐up studies need to concentrate on enhancing the model's ability to recognize patients from various demographic groups across different healthcare facilities.
