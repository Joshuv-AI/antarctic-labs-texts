MHC class II genes influence the gut microbiota of wild golden snub-nosed monkeys
Authors: Ji-Bing Yan, Tian-Qi Shi, Jin-Bang Zhang, Fei Long, Paul A Garber, Bao-Guo Li, Pei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGut microbiota play a critical role in host health and nutrition and are regulated by both non-genetic factors such as season, diet, habitat, age, sex, and genetic variation. Although researchers studying mammalian feeding ecology have focused principally on non-genetic influences of gut microbiota composition, there is growing awareness of the important role that genes coding for the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) play in enabling the host immune system to distinguish between beneficial and pathogenic microbes.ResultsHere, we examined MHC genes DRB1, DRB2, DQA1 and DQB1 along with 20 neutral microsatellites to test the relationship between host genetic variation and gut microbiota in a population of wild golden snub-nosed monkeys (Rhinopithecus roxellana), a foregut fermenting colobine primate. In controlling for season, age, and sex, we found no significant association between gut microbiota alpha and beta diversity with overall MHC heterozygosity/diversity. However, gut microbiota composition (Weighted UniFrac distance) was significantly correlated with microsatellite diversity (d2). In addition, the presence/absence of MHC haplotypes was positively correlated with the relative abundance of certain core beneficial bacterial genera (i.e., Anaerofustis and Ruminococcus) and negatively correlated with the abundance of certain pathogenic bacteria (i.e., Prevotellaceae genus 1 and Phascolarctobacterium). Closer relatedness and higher MHC genetic similarity were independently associated with greater gut microbiota similarity.ConclusionsOur findings support a taxon-specific association between MHC variation and gut microbiota, whereby the presence/absence of MHC haplotypes correlate with variation in particular microbial taxa rather than with global shifts in community structure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04994-w.Keywords: Gut microbiota, SSR, MHC, Golden snub-nosed monkey

Introduction

The gut microbiota of vertebrates represents a highly complex microbial community comprising bacteria, archaea, viruses, and eukaryotic microorganisms [1]. Among these, bacteria are reported to account for more than 90% of the gut microbial community and have become the primary focus of current gut microbiota research [2]. Functionally, the gut microbiota regulates vital physiological processes of the host, including nutrient metabolism, immune responses, growth and development [3–7]. Furthermore, its homeostasis is critical for the proper functioning of the host’s disease defense mechanisms [8]. Consequently, an understanding of the genetic and nongenetic factors that have shaped host-associated microbial community structure are critical for understanding species ecology and evolution [9].

Factors regulating host gut microbial communities fall into two categories: nongenetic and genetic. Nongenetic factors include season [10, 11], diet [12, 13], habitat [14–16], age [17], sex [18], microbial transmission through social contact with conspecifics, and the presence and availability of microbes in the environment [19, 20]. In addition, host genetics play an important role in shaping gut microbiota variation [9]. First, microbial lineages have coevolved with their hosts over evolutionary time scales [21]. For example, analyses of strain-level bacterial diversity in humans (Homo sapiens), chimpanzees (Pan troglodytes), bonobos (Pan paniscus), and gorillas (Gorilla gorilla), indicate that multiple lineages of predominant bacterial taxa in the gut including Bacteroidaceae and Bifidobacteriaceae arose via co-speciation over the past 15 million years [21]. Second, the gut microbiota has been shown to be heritable [22, 23]. A study analyzing the heritability of gut microbiota in 585 wild baboons (Papio cynocephalus) revealed that 97% of both dominant and minor taxa were heritable after controlling for diet, age, matriline, social interactions (e.g., grooming) and differences in patterns of assortative mating [23]. Third, host genetic characteristics (e.g., genetic similarity, diversity, and specific genotypes) correlate with the structure of the gut microbiota [24]. For example, evidence indicates that gut microbiota similarity is substantially higher between twins than between unrelated individuals, and even higher between identical twins than between fraternal twins [22, 25]. In both mice (Mus musculus) and humans, strong associations have been identified between quantitative trait loci (QTLs) and specific gut microbial taxa [26–28]. Thus, host genetic variation represents an important contributing factor in determining gut microbiota composition.

Genome-wide genetic differentiation may indirectly shape the host gut microbiota by influencing digestive and metabolic functions [29]. Currently, neutral genetic variation (i.e., microsatellites, also known as simple sequence repeats [SSRs]) is commonly used as a proxy for genome-wide genetic variation to investigate the relationship between overall genomic variation and the gut microbiota. For example, in three-spined sticklebacks (Gasterosteus aculeatus), SSR heterozygosity was negatively correlated with gut microbiota beta diversity [30]. In Nelson’s bighorn sheep (Ovis canadensis nelsoni), populations with lower SSR expected heterozygosity, defined by examining 13 microsatellites, exhibited lower weighted distance in gut microbiota than those with higher SSR expected heterozygosity [31]. Finally, in the Phofung river frog (Amietia hymenopus), the genetic distance between hosts—calculated based on SSR—showed a positive correlation with the distance between their gut bacterial communities [32].

A major challenge to the immune system is distinguishing commensal bacteria from pathogenic bacteria. Given that host’s gut microbiota harbors both mutualistic/commensal bacteria and pathobionts, the immune system must act to prevent colonization and proliferation of harmful bacteria while promoting and maintaining beneficial taxa [33, 34]. In this regard, immunogenetic variation appears to play a crucial role in shaping hosts’ gut microbiota.

The major histocompatibility complex (MHC) is a family of highly polymorphic genes, which plays an important role in the vertebrate adaptive immunity. Its translated cell surface glycoproteins can recognize and present antigens to T lymphocytes, thus triggering an appropriate immune response [35, 36]. According to the different antigens to which MHC molecules are presented, MHC molecules can be divided into two classes: Class I are present on the surface of almost all somatic cells and are responsible for presenting endogenous antigens (e.g. viruses) to cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. Class II MHC molecules, which are located only in antigen-presenting cells, present exogenous antigens (e.g. bacteria and parasites) to helper CD4+ T cells [36]. Each MHC gene encodes protein molecules capable of binding only to antigens matching its specific antigen-binding motif [37]. Different MHC genotypes, therefore, exhibit different immune responses to different microbial taxa, and this can function to promote or reduce individual gut microbiota variation.

To date, investigations of MHC gene associations with gut microbiota in wild vertebrate populations (three-spined stickleback, G. aculeatus [38], Seychelles warbler, Acrocephalus sechellensis [9], Jamaican fruit bat, Artibeus jamaicensis [39], and reddish gray mouse lemur, Microcebus griseorufus [40]) have identified the following correlative patterns:

i)There can exist a negative correlation between MHC diversity and microbiota diversity. Each MHC molecule binds to a specific repertoire of antigenic peptides. Consequently, greater MHC diversity expands the diversity of recognizable antigenic peptides, enhancing the host’s capacity to eliminate or reduce a broader spectrum of pathogens [41].

ii)There can exist a positive correlation between MHC diversity and microbiota diversity. The immune system not only eliminates microbes but also establishes mutualistic relationships with commensal microbes [7]. Thus, if higher MHC diversity enables tolerance toward a broader range of microbes, this can lead to an increase in the number and persistence of bacteria that improve host health or digestive function.

The gut harbors a highly diverse microbial community and each microbial species can generate a vast array of peptides that theoretically are available as MHC ligands. As a result, the diversity in MHC ligands may impede the specificity of MHC-bound microbes [43].

In this study, we examine the influence of genetic factors on the gut microbiota in wild golden snub-nosed monkey (Rhinopithecus roxellana), an Endangered primate species (IUCN redlist 2025). Golden snub-nosed monkeys primarily inhabit high-elevation montane forests (1,300 – 3,400 m) across four key regions of China: Shennongjia (Hubei province), the Qinling Mountains (Shaanxi province), Gansu province, and Sichuan province [44]. Golden snub-nosed monkeys are foregut fermenters and characterized by an enlarged and sacculated stomach that maintains a pH between 5 and 7 [45]. These primates live in a multilevel or modular society (MLS) of between 50 to several hundred individuals that form several one-male multi-female units (OMUs that have 2 – 20 individuals) and at least one all-male unit (AMU) [46]. These OMUs feed, forage, rest, and travel together throughout the entire year. Previous studies of R. roxellana have revealed that ecological factors, including season, diet, and age, exert significant effects on their gut microbiota [47–49]. However, only one study has investigated the role of relatedness in shaping their gut microbiota [50]. In that study, closely related individuals (measured by a relatedness coefficient) were found to harbor more similar gut microbial communities than unrelated or less related individuals.

In order to obtain a more comprehensive understanding of the factors shaping gut microbiota composition, we employed MHC genes (immunogenetic variation) and microsatellites (genome-wide genetic variation) as molecular markers to investigate the degree to host genetic variation and non-genetic factors shape gut microbial communities, while controlling for season, age and sex. We tested the following hypotheses.

H1: Based on the immune tolerance range hypothesis, we hypothesize that individuals with higher MHC heterozygosity/diversity also maintain higher gut microbiota diversity. We predict that individuals with higher SSR heterozygosity/diversity also maintain higher gut microbiota diversity, considering parallel but potentially distinct genetic pathways through which host genetic variation may influence microbial community.

Methods

Study site and sampling

This study was carried out on a population of R. roxellana inhabiting the Guanyinshan National Nature Reserve, located on the south slope of the Qinling Mountains, Shaanxi Province, China (107°52′ ~ 108°02′E, 33°20′ ~ 33°44′N). This population has been under continuous monitoring since 2010. Individuals over two years of age can be identified based on its facial characteristics, scars, evidence of previous injuries, body size, and pelage coloration. The sex, approximate age, and birth OMU of nearly every individual is known.

Field observations and sampling were performed from December 2022 through January 2024. During the research period, 83 – 99 individuals, residing in 6 – 8 OMUs and one AMU were observed. This study subjects included 23 adult males, 31 adult females, 11 subadults, and four juveniles by the end of the study.

A total of 78 hair samples were collected using an 80 cm long pole with glue on its end that was baited with fruit. Each hair sample was stored individually in a paper envelope that contained a desiccant. In addition, 222 fecal samples were obtained from 69 individually recognized snub-nosed monkeys across four seasons (spring: April 2023, summer: July 2023, autumn: October 2023 and winter: January 2024). Within 5 min of defecation by the target animal, the samples were collected using sterile toothpicks and transferred to sterile 5 mL cryovials. Samples were then immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for preservation. During the sampling period, twelve individuals left the study population. For these individuals we were unable to obtain fecal samples for each of the four seasons.

Molecular methods

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from hair according to the Chelex protocol (Chelex 100, Bio-Rad). The microbial DNA was isolated from fecal samples using VAMNE Stool/Soil DNA Extraction Kit-BOX2 and were quantified using Qubit4.0.

Molecular analysis of microsatellite and MHC

Twenty independent microsatellites were used to measure neutral variation in the whole genome. Details of the primer information and amplification conditions are provided in Supplementary Table S2 [51]. To mitigate genotyping artifacts (e.g., false alleles, allelic dropouts), homozygotes were validated using at least seven independent reactions, and heterozygotes were identified using three independent reactions per sample. After individual identification, 69 unique individuals were identified from the 78 hair samples.

We used two microsatellite-based estimates of genetic variation for each monkey: (1) multilocus heterozygosity (MLH), which represents the proportion of heterozygous microsatellite loci in an individual relative to the total number of microsatellite loci. And (2) d2, the genetic distance between microsatellite alleles for each individual. We calculated d2 as , where and are the repeat-unit lengths of each allele at a locus [52, 53]. Additionally, a relatedness coefficient (r) was calculated using PolyRelatedness V1.11b [51].

The second exons of four MHC class II loci were amplified for these 69 unique individuals. A pair of primers was used to amplify the two loci of DRB (DRB1 and DRB2, F: 5′- TTCTCAGGAGGCCGCCCGTGTGA - 3′; R: 5′- ACCTCGCCGCTGCACTGTGAAGCTC-3′; barcode sequences were appended to the 5′ ends of both forward and reverse primers) [54]. Amplification of the DQA1 and DQB1 loci were performed using one primer pair per locus (DQA1: F: 5′-CCTTCCTGCTTGTCATCTTCACTC-3′; R: GWGAGGCAGAATGGTGRACACA-3′, DQB1: F: 5′- TCCCCGCAGAGGATTTCGTG-3′; R: 5′- GGGGCGACAACGCTCACCTC-3′) [55, 56]. All reactions were performed in a 50 µL volume containing 10 – 100 ng genomic DNA, 0.4 µM of each primer, 50 mM KCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.4), 2.5 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM of each dNTP, and one unit of ExTaq DNA polymerase (Takara, Dalian, China). Thermal cycling was conducted using a Veriti™ 96-Well Fast Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems, Singapore) under the following profile: 5 min at 95°C; 35 cycles of 30 s at 95°C, 30 s at the locus-specific annealing temperature (DQA1: 58°C; DQB1: 58.5°C; DRB: 60°C), and 30s at 72°C; followed by a final 10 min extension at 72°C [57]. Each sample was subjected to three independent PCR amplifications. Amplified products were purified using the AxyPrep™ DNA Gel Extraction Kit (AXYGEN Biosciences, USA). For DQA1 and DQB1, purified products were cloned into the pMD 18‑T Vector (Takara, China) and transformed into DH5α competent cells (Takara, China). Twelve positive clones from each transformation were selected and bidirectionally sequenced on an ABI PRISM™ 3100 Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher, China). A sequence was accepted as a genuine allele only if it was present in a minimum of two individuals or in all three replicates derived from a single individual. The DRB1 and DRB2 loci were sequenced via a high-throughput approach: purified PCR products were first quantified, normalized to a concentration of 10 ng/µL, and then pooled to construct a mixed amplicon library. Subsequent high-throughput sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, with 250 bp paired-end reads generated for analysis. Raw fastq files were processed using a previously described bioinformatic pipeline, which enabled the accurate identification of true alleles while eliminating artifacts [57, 58]. MHC-TYPER V1.0 was then used to assign DRB alleles to a specific locus [59]. Lewontin’s D’ (D’) between each pair of loci was calculated using SHEsis (http://analysis.bio-x.cn/myAnalysis.php) [60]. We defined a haplotype as a specific combination of alleles (or genetic markers) that are located on the same chromosome, tightly linked, and tend to be inherited together as an integrated unit. The linked haplotypes of DQA1, DQB1, DRB1, and DRB2 were then constructed.

We used two measures to calculate the heterozygosity and divergence of an individual’s MHC genes [57]: (1) MHC haplotype heterozygosity (HMHC), with homozygotes represented as 0 and heterozygotes represented as 1; and (2) the evolutionary amino acid distance between two haplotypes of an individual (intra-Eaadis). In addition, the parameter evolutionary amino acid distance between two individuals’ haplotypes (inter-Eaadis) was used to measure the similarity of MHC genes among individuals [57].

Microbial extraction, sequencing, and bioinformatics

The V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the universal primers 515F (5’- GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGG - 3’) and 907R (5’- CCGTCAATTCMTTTRAGTTT - 3’). Paired-end sequencing was subsequently performed on an Illumina PE 250 platform with a read length of 2 × 250 bp.

After demultiplexing, raw sequences were subjected to quality control using Trimmomatic [61]. Sequences were then analyzed using R version 4.4.2, with the DADA2 pipeline employed to filter and trim reads, denoise sequences, merge paired-end reads, remove chimeric sequences, and identify amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) [62]. Taxonomic assignment of ASVs was performed by training a naïve-Bayes classifier on the SILVA 138 16 S dataset (28,296 ASVs were detected across 222 samples) [63]. Subsequently, the final sample metadata, ASVs, and taxonomic tables were processed using the phyloseq package [64].

Eukaryotes, archaea, chloroplasts, and mitochondria were filtered based on taxonomic classification, and ASVs unclassified at the phylum level were also removed. Following filtering, a total of 18,754 ASVs were detected in 222 fecal samples. Four alpha diversity indices were calculated: Observed ASVs、Chao1、Shannon diversity index, and Simpson index. Four beta diversity indices also were calculated (after centered log ratio transformation, CLR): Bray-Curtis distance, Aitchison distance, Weighted UniFrac distance, and Unweighted UniFrac distance. Additionally, core bacterial genera were defined as taxa present in ≥ 50% of samples with minimum relative abundance ≥ 0.01%, and the abundance of all core genera were transformed using CLR.

Statistical analysis

To test whether the alpha diversity of the gut microbiota was associated with host genetic variation, generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) were constructed using the glmmTMB package [65] and the lme4 package [66] in R version 4.4.2. Alpha diversity indices were used as response variables. Explanatory variables comprised MLH, d2, the presence (1) or absence (0) of specific MHC haplotypes, and heterozygosity/diversity indices of MHC (HMHC and intra-Eaadis). Full models including all explanatory variables were first fitted. Owing to high collinearity among HMHC and intra-Eaadis, only one index was incorporated per model, and their AIC values were compared to select the model with the lowest AIC value (Table S4). Sampling season, age, and sex were also included, given their well-documented influence on gut microbiota composition [50]. Individual ID was included as a random effect.

To test whether beta diversity of the gut microbiota was correlated with genetic variation, a permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was performed using the adonis2 function in the vegan package in R version 4.4.2 [67], with 9999 permutations specified as the number of random permutations. PERMANOVA analyses were conducted within a single multivariate model for each distance matrix, in which all predictors were tested simultaneously using marginal (by = “margin”) permutation tests. In this framework, the effects of individual predictors are evaluated conditionally while accounting for the presence of the other variables in the model, rather than as independent hypothesis tests. Beta diversity indices (Bray-Curtis distance, Aitchison distance, Weighted UniFrac distance, and Unweighted UniFrac distance) were used as response variables. As with the models used in the previous prediction, MLH, d2, the presence/absence of specific MHC haplotypes, one of the heterozygosity/diversity indices of MHC (HMHC and intra-Eaadis), season, age, and sex served as predicter variables, and the individual ID served as the permutation stratum. The cor function was used to calculate the correlation coefficient matrix among explanatory variables to detect multicollinearity. The results revealed that the pairwise correlation coefficient between HMHC and intra-Eaadis was greater than 0.9, indicating strong correlations. Therefore, separate models were constructed for HMHC and intra-Eaadis for PERMANOVA analysis, and the model (intra-Eaadis) with the best fit was selected. For genetic variables that were significant in the PERMANOVA analysis, we performed additional analyses. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was first used to summarize variation in beta-diversity and then used the resulting principal coordinate scores one as response variables in linear models, while controlling for season, age and sex.

To explore the effects of genetic factors on gut microbiota at the level of core genera (rather than community-wide shifts), we fitted a generalized linear latent variable model (GLLVM) using the GLLVM package [68] in R version 4.4.2. The response variables were the relative abundances of core genera. The explanatory variables were the presence (1) or absence (0) of specific MHC haplotypes, sampling season, age, and sex. The random effect was individual ID. Diversity and heterozygosity reflect the host’s overall genetic variation. Their influence was more likely reflected in changes in the overall composition and diversity of the gut microbiota, rather than in the precise regulation of the abundance of specific core taxa. Therefore, we excluded MLH, d2, HMHC and intra-Eaadis from the model.

To examine whether genetic similarity between individuals exerts an effect on their gut microbiota compositional similarity, we established four generalized linear models (GLMs). Pairwise beta diversity metrics were used as response variables (Bray-Curtis distance, Aitchison distance, Weighted UniFrac distance, and Unweighted UniFrac distance). These are commonly used in gut microbiota research and measure different aspects of microbial community dissimilarity, including relative abundance variation, compositional structure, and phylogenetic relatedness. The explanatory variables included the relatedness coefficient (r) and the genetic similarity of MHC genes between individuals (inter-Eaadis). Additionally, sampling season and whether individuals lived in the same OMU (coded as 1 if yes, and 0 if no) were also included as fixed variables. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to assess multicollinearity among explanatory variables, and all VIF values were below three.

It is noteworthy that genetic variation in the MHC may be influenced by the genome-wide genetic background characterized by SSR markers. However, the statistical models employed in this study were designed to evaluate the independent contribution of a given variable while controlling for the effects of other variables. Therefore, the simultaneous inclusion of both MHC and SSR indices in the same model allows us to analyze the independent effect of MHC on gut microbiota composition after controlling for genome-wide variation represented by SSR, and vice versa.

Results

Gut bacterial taxonomy of golden snub-nosed monkey

A total of 18,754 ASVs were detected across 222 samples, encompassing 38 phyla, 87 classes, 209 orders, 449 families and 1,119 genera. The dominant phyla were Firmicutes (with an mean relative abundance ± standard deviation (SD) of 58.63% ± 15.61% across all samples), Proteobacteria (18.07% ± 16.14%), and Bacteroidota (15.91% ± 10.28%). The dominant bacterial families were Oscillospiraceae (20.4% ± 6.94%), Succinivibrionaceae (16.92% ± 15.72%), Lachnospiraceae (12.61% ± 5.51%), Muribaculaceae (6.17% ± 5.19%), Christensenellaceae (5.62% ± 3.14%), UCG-010 (5.16% ± 2.91%), and Rikenellaceae (5.09% ± 5.14%) The relative abundance of all other families was less than 5% (Fig. 1).

A total of 96 core genera (taxa present in ≥ 50% of samples with minimum relative abundance ≥ 0.01%) were identified, spanning 9 phyla, 13 classes, 24 orders, and 41 families (Table S3). These core genera accounted for 96.6% of the total ASV sequence reads, with the mean relative abundance of each individual core genus ranging from 0.9% to 17.6%.

MHC characteristics

We identified twelve previously reported alleles across four MHC II loci (Rhro-DQA1*01, 02, 08; Rhro-DQB1*04, 09, 17; Rhro-DRB1*03, 14, 17; Rhro-DRB2*04, 08, 16) [55, 69, 70]. Linkage disequilibrium analysis performed using the SHEsis platform revealed strong linkage disequilibrium among the four MHC class II loci (D’ > 0.931). Based on the MHC genotyping results, four MHC haplotypes (H1 ~ H4) were identified; these haplotypes were previously reported (Table S5; [71]).

Effects of host genetic variation on the diversity of the host’s gut microbiota

There was no evidence that genetic variation was significantly associated with gut microbiota alpha diversity in the golden snub-nosed monkeys (Table S6). Age and sex also had no significant effect on alpha diversity. We subsequently removed these variables to facilitate interpretation of the remaining factors. However, gut microbiota alpha diversity differed significantly among the four seasons (GLMMs: e.g., observed ASVs: pspring < 0.001, psummer = 0.017, pwinter < 0.001; Table S6), with higher values in winter and spring than in summer and autumn (observed ASVs: meanspring = 606.47 ± 226.25, meansummer = 387.60 ± 73.18, meanautumn = 352.23 ± 48.48, meanwinter = 558.02 ± 156.03; Figure S1). This suggests that seasonal changes in climate, diet, nutritional requirements, the costs of thermoregulation, social interactions (i.e. mating season, birthing season), may shape gut microbiota diversity.

A PERMANOVA analysis revealed that d2 exerted a significant effect on beta diversity (Weighted UniFrac distance: R2 = 0.010, F = 2.448, p = 0.016; Table 1 and Fig. 2; the results of other β diversity PERMANOVA analyses are shown in Table S8). However, models examining correlations between beta diversity and MLH, MHC diversity/ heterozygosity, or the presence of specific MHC haplotypes revealed no significant associations (Table 1 and Table S8). We further constructed a multiple linear model to examine the relationship between the host genetic variable d2 and the first principal coordinate axis (PCoA1) of the Weighted UniFrac distance, while controlling for potential confounding factors including season, age, and sex. The results showed a marginally significant positive association between d2 and PCoA1 (p = 0.060, see Table S9).Additionally, shifts in beta diversity were associated with differences in season, sex, and age (e.g., UniFrac distance: pseason < 0.001, psex < 0.001, page = 0.016; Table S8 and Figures S2-S5), with season exerting the strongest influence (e.g., UniFrac distance: R2season = 0.155, R2sex = 0.008, R2age = 0.006; Table S8 and Figures S2-S5).

Effect of specific MHC haplotypes on the abundance of core genera

To determine whether specific MHC haplotypes selectively recognize one or more target gut bacterial taxa in golden snub-nosed monkeys, we applied a GLLVM model to analyze the relationship between the presence or absence of specific MHC haplotypes and the relative abundance of the 96 prevalent core genera.

We found that the presence of specific MHC haplotypes was associated with changes in the relative abundance of 25 core genera (MHC - H1: 6 genera, MHC - H2: 6 genera, MHC - H3: 10 genera, MHC - H4: 3 genera; Fig. 3). The presence of MHC - H4 was associated with a significantly lower relative abundance of Sphaerochaeta and Lachnospira. Prevotellaceae genus 1 and Phascolarctobacterium decreased when the haplotypes MHC - H1 or H3 were present. In contrast, the presence of MHC - H1 or H2 were significantly correlated with an increased relative abundance of Ruminococcus and DNF00809. Anaerofustis was more abundant when MHC - H3 was present (Fig. 3).

Effect of genetic similarity between hosts on the similarity of their gut microbiota

To examine whether host genetic similarity influenced gut microbiota similarity, four generalized linear models (GLMs) were constructed. The results suggest that individuals with higher genetic similarity exhibited higher similarity of their gut microbiota. The relatedness coefficient (r) had a significantly negative effect on gut microbiota similarity measured by Bray-Curtis, UniFrac, and Aitchison distance (Table 2 and Fig. 4a). Additionally, the inter-Eaadis value had a significantly positive effect on gut microbiota similarity, as measured by the Aitchison distance (Table 2 and Fig. 4b). Although the effect of inter-Eaadis on Bray-Curtis and UniFrac distances was not statistically significant, the corresponding p-values were of marginal significance (Bray-Curtis: p = 0.051; UniFrac: p = 0.077; Table 2). We also identified that individuals living in the same OMU exhibited a more similar gut microbial composition (Table 2 and Fig. 4c).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the association between genetic variation and gut microbiota in a wild population of golden snub-nosed monkeys, using MHC genes (immune) and microsatellites (genome-wide genetic variation) as molecular markers. When accounting for the effect of season on gut microbiota diversity, no evidence was found for a correlation between genetic variation (MHC gene characteristics and microsatellite locus variation) and gut microbiota alpha diversity. In the case of beta diversity, only d2 was significantly associated with this metric (Weighted UniFrac distance: R2 = 0.010, F = 2.448, p = 0.016; Table 1). In contrast, we identified specific MHC haplotypes that were significantly associated with the relative abundances of core bacterial genera. Moreover, individuals with more similar MHC genotypes exhibited more similar gut microbiota. A similar finding was observed among individuals with a higher relatedness coefficient (closer genetic relatedness). Co-residence in the same OMUs was also associated with greater gut microbiota similarity.

This study found that the phyla Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidetes were dominant in the gut microbiota of wild golden snub-nosed monkeys, which is consistent with previous studies [47, 49]. The predominance of Firmicutes (58.63%) in the gut microbiota of golden snub-nosed monkeys was similar to that of other primates that are foregut fermenters such as R. brelichi (gray snub-nosed monkey), Trachypithecus leucocephalus (white-headed langur), and Piliocolobus gordonorum (Udzungwa red colobus) [12, 72]. The dominance of Firmicutes may represent an adaptive response to a high-fiber diet. This is because leaves, bark, and oak and pine seeds are rich in fiber– and constitute main components of R. roxellana’s diet across different seasons of the year [73]. However, the monkeys lack endogenous enzymes to degrade complex dietary fibers, such as cellulose and lignin [74]. Firmicutes can encode polysaccharide lyases that degrade complex dietary fiber into absorbable metabolites (e.g., butyrate and acetate), thus facilitating the absorption of these metabolites by the host [74].

Effects of host genetic variation on the diversity of the host’s gut microbiota

No statistical evidence was found for an association between MHC genetic variation and the alpha/beta diversity of the gut microbiota (Tables S6 and S8). The underlying reasons for this phenomenon are relative complex. First, proteins encoded by MHC genes mediate antigen-specific recognition, a process that may lead to the retention or elimination of specific bacterial taxa in the gut [9]. However, such targeted retention or elimination may be insufficient to alter the overall diversity of the gut microbiota. On the other hand, compared with captive or model animals, wild animals inhibit more complex environments and exhibit greater allelic diversity. Previous studies have reported that MHC heterozygosity exerts a significant effect on the structure and function of the gut microbiota in laboratory mice [75]. This may be attributed to the fact that model animals are typically genetically engineered to exhibit homozygous/heterozygous genotypes or differential expression at specific alleles, while captive conditions tend to simplify the composition of the gut microbiota [9]. In contrast, natural populations generally harbor greater diversity in alleles and allele combinations and are exposed to a broader range of microbial taxa. Consequently, the effects of MHC genes on the gut microbiota can be overshadowed by a range of environmental factors [76]. Moreover, in wild populations with high genetic diversity, the function of MHC II alleles may be more prominently manifested in combating dynamically changing pathogen pressures rather than in shaping the structure of relatively stable symbiotic gut microbiota communities [55].

SSR genetic diversity(d2)was significantly associated with gut microbiota composition (Table 1). The overall genetic variation of the host may regulate the composition of its gut microbiota by influencing the intestinal environment (i.e., the level of small molecule proteins or peptides). In pigs, the 2.3-kb deletion in the ABO gene (found in individuals with blood type O) inactivates the encoded N-acetylgalactosaminyltransferase. Consequently, it cannot add N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) to the highly glycosylated mucins in the intestinal mucus. This leads to reduced concentrations of GalNAc in the gut of OO genotype individuals that impair the growth of bacteria that rely on GalNAc as a carbon source [77]. In addition, the significant association of d2 with the weighted UniFrac distance — but not with the UniFrac distance — may indicate that d2 predominantly influences the relative abundance rather than the compositional presence of microbial taxa. A marginally significant positive association between d2 and PCoA1 of the weighted UniFrac distance (p = 0.060, Table S9), suggested a weak effect of d2 on community structure that is not dominated by a single ordination axis. This pattern is consistent with the PERMANOVA results, which test for overall differences in community composition rather than variation along individual ordination axes. Although d2 was significantly associated with gut microbiota β diversity as measured by weighted UniFrac distance, the corresponding effect size was small (R² = 0.01), indicating that genome-wide genetic variation explains only a minor proportion of the overall variation in microbial community composition. In contrast, seasonal factors accounted for a substantially larger share of the observed β-diversity variation, underscoring the dominant influence of non-genetic drivers. In natural populations, seasonal changes in climate, diet, nutritional requirements, the costs of thermoregulation, social interactions (i.e. mating season, birthing season), can strongly and consistently shape gut microbiota structure [76]. Relative to these ecological influences, the effects of host genetic background are therefore likely to be modest in magnitude. Nevertheless, the presence of a statistically detectable genetic signal suggests that genome-wide variation may still exert a subtle but consistent influence on microbiota composition, even if this effect is secondary to environmental factors.

Effects of specific MHC haplotypes on the abundance of core genera

The presence of specific MHC haplotypes was associated with reduced relative abundances of specific core bacterial genera, some of which are pathogens or opportunistic pathogens (Fig. 3). This result is consistent with the hypothesis that specific MHC haplotypes can selectively recognize one or more target gut bacterial taxa, present them to CD4⁺ T cells, and trigger immune responses that restrict the colonization and proliferation of these taxa in the gut [9]. For example, the relative abundance of Prevotellaceae genus 1 and Phascolarctobacterium decreased in the presence of the MHC - H1 or H3 haplotypes. Prevotellaceae, a family of common opportunistic pathogens in humans, has an increased relative abundance linked to the development and progression of inflammatory bowel diseases (e.g., ulcerative colitis) [78]. A study revealed that an increased abundance of Phascolarctobacterium is associated with a higher prevalence of diabetes and colorectal cancer in three Norwegian cohorts [79]. Thus, MHC genes can specifically inhibit the colonization and proliferation of pathogenic or opportunistic pathogenic bacterial taxa.

We also found that the presence of specific haplotypes was associated with increased relative abundances of specific beneficial bacteria. For example, MHC - H3 was associated with an increased relative abundance of Anaerofustis, whereas MHC - H1 and H2 were associated with an increased abundance of Ruminococcus. Anaerofustis is a genus of strictly anaerobic bacteria that may enhance gut barrier function and serve as an energy source by producing short-chain fatty acids (e.g., butyrate) through carbohydrate fermentation in the intestinal environment [80]. Furthermore, the genus Ruminococcus is primarily involved in the degradation of dietary fiber and resistant starches [81]. Therefore, the observed haplotype, which is associated with the enrichment of specific beneficial bacteria suggests that MHC genes may modulate the host’s immune tolerance toward commensal microbes, thereby fostering symbiotic relationships with specific gut bacteria [43].

One limitation of this study is the relatively modest number of individuals included in the individual-based genetic analyses (N = 69). This may limit the power to detect subtle effects of specific MHC haplotypes on gut microbiota diversity and core genera, particularly when multiple haplotypes are considered simultaneously. Future studies with larger sample sizes will be needed to more comprehensively assess host genetic influences on gut microbiota community.

Effects of genetic similarity between hosts on the similarity of their gut microbiota

We found that individuals with a higher relatedness coefficient exhibited a more similar gut microbiota (Table 2 or Fig. 4). A comparable result also has been reported in captive golden snub-nosed monkeys [50]. The relatedness coefficient, an index measuring genome-wide genetic similarity, may drive gut microbiota similarity by regulating immune or metabolic functions conferred by shared genetic backgrounds [29]. Previous studies have shown that greater genetic divergence between populations of Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar) was associated with greater dissimilarity in microbiota composition [82]. Similarly, increased genetic distance among individuals of the Galápagos mockingbird (Mimus parvulus) correlated with reduced gut microbiota similarity [83]. An analogous pattern has been reported in Verreaux’s sifaka (Propithecus verreauxi) [2]. Furthermore, within the one male unit of the golden snub-nosed monkeys, grooming, embracing, playing, and proximity constitute the majority of social interactions and occur principally between OMU females, especially those who are genetically closely related [84, 85]. High frequency affiliative behaviors facilitate microbial transmission, thereby further enhancing gut microbiota similarity among individuals who are already closely related. During grooming, individuals contact conspecifics’ hair and skin, often picking up salt particles, ectoparasites, or other debris and placing them into their mouth [86]. This behavior may facilitate the exchange of microbes between individuals via dermal-oral and entero-oral pathways thereby enhancing gut microbiota similarity [2]. The same principle applies to embracing and playing: skin contact and mutual biting, touching, and licking provide additional opportunities for microbial sharing [19, 87]. Similarly, greater gut microbiota similarity was found amount individuals living in the same OMU, unrelated to kinship, compared with individuals living in different OMUs of the same multilevel society (Table 2 and Fig. 4c).

Our findings also revealed that individuals with greater MHC genetic similarity exhibited more similar gut microbiota (Table 2 and Fig. 4b). This result is consistent with hypothesis three — since each MHC molecule binds a specific repertoire of antigenic peptides, and individuals with similar MHC genotypes are likely to harbor greater similarity in the composition of their gut microbiota [40]. While our study did not uncover direct evidence linking MHC variation to overall gut microbiota diversity, the established correlation between MHC genetic similarity and microbiota similarity lends plausibility to the notion that MHC variation is likely influenced by the composition of the gut microbiota. Moreover, the impact of MHC genetic similarity on microbiome similarity has been observed in other species, such as the reddish-gray mouse lemur (Microcebus griseorufus), the song sparrow (Melospiza melodia), and the Galápagos mockingbird (Mimus parvulus) [40, 42, 83].

Conclusions

In this study, we utilized MHC genes (immunogenetic variation) and microsatellites (genome-wide genetic variation) as molecular markers to investigate the effects of host genetic variation (heterozygosity, diversity, similarity and specific haplotypes) on gut microbiota while controlling for non-genetic factors such as season, age, and sex. We tested three hypotheses. The results demonstrate that antigen-specific immune responses driven by the MHC are likely to influence the establishment of a few select microbial taxa (commensals and pathogens) rather than exerting overarching control of the microbial community. However, the overall genetic variation of the host (SSR), may regulate the Weighted UniFrac distance. The results also show that both MHC similarity and r had significant partial effects on gut microbiota similarity, after accounting for their mutual influences. This demonstrates that their influences are independent. Thus H1 concerning the predicted positive association between host genetic heterozygosity/diversity and overall gut microbiota diversity, was not supported, only a weak association was detected between SSR-based heterozygosity (d²) and the Weighted UniFrac distance. H2, which tested whether the presence of specific MHC haplotypes was associated with reduced relative abundances of potentially pathogenic or opportunistic microbial taxa, was supported. Finally, H3, examining whether individuals with more similar MHC genotypes and/or higher genetic relatedness exhibit greater similarity in gut microbiota composition, was supported. Our study highlights the different roles of adaptive MHC genes and neutral microsatellites in shaping the gut microbiota of golden snub-nosed monkeys, while also demonstrating the influence of host genetic variation on the gut microbiota. We expect that similar relationships exist in other vertebrate species. Future research should consider the interactive and interdependent effects of ecological, genetic, and social factors on gut microbiota.
