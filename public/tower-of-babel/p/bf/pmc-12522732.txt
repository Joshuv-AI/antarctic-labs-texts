Influence of a pilot urban primary healthcare model on the use of medically trained healthcare providers among the low-income slum populations in Bangladesh: findings from an implementation research study
Authors: Md Golam Rabbani, Zakir Hossain, Khadija Islam Tisha, Kamrun Nahar, Zillur Rahman Sakin, Towhida Nasrin, Mohammad Wahid Ahmed, Md Zahid Hasan, Chandrasegarar Soloman, Margub Aref Jahangir, Maya Vandenent, Shehrin Shaila Mahmood
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDelivering quality primary healthcare to the urban population has been challenging in many developing countries including Bangladesh. With a fragmented and pluralistic urban health system, the country experiences major hurdles in the provision of primary healthcare to its urban dwellers. Since 2021, an urban primary healthcare model called ‘Aalo Clinic’ is being piloted to serve the low-income urban population in Bangladesh. With an aim to ensure universal health coverage for the urban population, the model delivers an essential package of health services. We aimed to assess the implementation effect of this pilot model on the utilization of healthcare from medically trained providers (MTPs) for the management of acute illness.MethodsFollowing a cross-sectional study design, an implementation research study was conducted between October 2021 and August 2023 in the Korail, Mirpur, Shyampur, Dhalpur, and Tongi-Ershadnagar slums. Cross-sectional household surveys were conducted at baseline and end-line, involving over 2000 households in each round. Descriptive analysis and Chi-squared test were performed to assess the changes in healthcare utilization from MTPs, and logistic regression models were applied to assess the effectiveness of the model on healthcare utilization from MTPs while controlling for other covariates.ResultsThe utilization of healthcare from MTPs was significantly higher in the end-line (9.81% from Aalo Clinic and 18.6% from non-Aalo Clinic MTPs) compared to the baseline (0.64% from Aalo Clinic and 17.68% from non-Aalo Clinic MTPs). Healthcare utilization from local drug stores declined from 80.56% at baseline to 67.19% at end-line. Multivariate logistic regression showed respondents were 12.43 times more likely (95% CI: 7.49–20.63) to use Aalo Clinic services at end-line, indicating increased uptake of medically trained providers following the model’s implementation.ConclusionsThe Aalo Clinic Model was effective in influencing healthcare-seeking pattern of the slum populations and enhancing the utilization of qualified care from MTPs. The study supports replicating the model within existing healthcare structures and scaling it nationwide to advance universal health coverage in urban Bangladesh, contingent on sustained government funding for its operations.Keywords: Urban primary healthcare; Aalo Clinic model; Healthcare utilization; Slum population; Medically trained provider; Drug store, Bangladesh

Background

Achieving Universal Health Coverage (UHC), a key target of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), within the stipulated time of 2030, is becoming challenging for countries, particularly for the developing world including Bangladesh [1]. The Government of Bangladesh is committed to assuring UHC for its population and has pledged to accelerate its efforts through expanding services, increasing resources, and improving efficiency [2]. While there have been numerous policy and program initiatives on the health frontier, particularly regarding primary healthcare, comprehensive and meaningful initiatives on UHC, and specifically addressing the persistent burden of out-of-pocket (OOP) health expenditures, have been a critical gap [3–5]. Although policymakers and many health sector actors have recognized the emerging priority, initiatives in this area remain largely disjointed and have not amounted to a critically compelling policy message capable of spurring broad-based national efforts. Currently, the Government of Bangladesh is implementing a few interventions focusing on primary healthcare. However, these interventions mostly target the rural population living below the poverty line and are targeted towards some specific healthcare needs, e.g. maternal and child healthcare [6, 7].

Bangladesh is facing rapid urbanization and at present around 40% of its population lives in urban areas, with an estimated annual growth rate of 3% [8] and it is worth mentioning that the rate is higher (7%) in poor informal settlements (i.e., slum areas) [9]. Such a rapid influx of people into urban areas creates continuous pressure on the urban health system [10].

Under constitutional law, urban local bodies within the Ministry of Local Government, Rural Development and Cooperatives (MoLGRDC) are tasked with delivering primary healthcare in urban areas, although not exclusively. However, these bodies lack the capacity to adequately serve the growing population [11, 12]. On the other hand, the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare is in charge of health services in the country and assumes the responsibility of secondary and tertiary care in the same settings, but the lack of coordination between these two ministries results in a significant gap in healthcare service provision [3].

This challenge is also highlighted in the recent national urban health strategy where lack of coordination between ministries, limited budget al.location, and the lack of targeting the healthcare needs of different urban communities, including the slum population have been identified as critical gaps in urban healthcare provision [3]. As a result, the urban populations, particularly the low-income slum population, have been forced to seek healthcare from alternative and more affordable sources of primary healthcare. A study conducted in Dhaka found that about 82% of the urban slum population relied on informal providers (e.g., local drug store, quack, etc.), who often lack formal training and are typically semi-skilled [11, 13]. Moreover, relying on such providers goes against the principles of quality healthcare of UHC [14–16].

To overcome the existing challenges and to ensure access to quality care for urban dwellers an urban primary healthcare model, namely the “Aalo Clinic” is being piloted in selected urban low-income areas since 2021 [17]. This model aims to strengthen the urban primary healthcare service delivery system by providing accessible and affordable essential healthcare with medically trained healthcare providers. Healthcare is provided to the urban poor through Aalo Clinics via static clinics and outreach services. The clinics operate six days a week in two shifts (8:30 a.m. − 2:30 p.m. and 3:00 p.m. − 9:00 p.m.) and provide free general practitioner consultations, essential medicines, and ten complimentary diagnostic tests, including blood sugar, hemoglobin estimation, urinalysis, and ECG, with additional diagnostic tests offered outside the clinics at a 25–40% discount. Outreach mobile vans provide counseling and referrals within the clinic catchment areas. Patient registration and care processes are managed via a digital system that facilitates real-time monitoring and electronic medical records, with referrals to higher-level facilities when necessary. Services encompass maternal, neonatal, child, and adolescent health; nutrition; non-communicable diseases; mental health; family planning; communicable diseases; and common conditions such as eye and skin care. Patients register through a mobile-linked IT system, receive an automated queue number for general practitioner consultation, and access prescribed diagnostics and medicines free of charge. More details of this model can be found elsewhere [17–19]. The current study was conducted to examine the implementation effect of the model after one year of its operation. Specifically, the study aimed to assess the effectiveness of the Aalo Clinic Model in prompting healthcare utilization from MTPs and to identify factors that influence the healthcare-seeking behavior of the patients.

Methods

Study design

This work was part of an implementation research (IR) study that continued from October 2021 and August 2023. As part of the IR study, the household survey served the purpose of assessing the effectiveness of the Aalo Clinic in increased utilization of primary health care among the target population. To assess the effectiveness of the model, two cross-sectional household surveys were conducted in five slums under three city corporations in Bangladesh. The baseline survey was conducted between January and February 2022, and the end-line survey was conducted between April and June 2023.

Study setting and population

The Aalo Clinic Model is being implemented in six selected areas adjacent to slum settings that fall under four city corporations of Dhaka, Gazipur, and Narayanganj. These areas include Korail and Mirpur under the Dhaka North City Corporation (DNCC), Shyampur, and Dhalpur under the Dhaka South City Corporation (DSCC), Tongi-Ershadnagar under the Gazipur City Corporation (GCC), and Narayangonj under the Narayangonj City Corporation (NCC). The current study was conducted in selected slum areas of two of these city corporations, Urban Health and Demographic Surveillance Systems (UHDSS) are in operation in these slum areas. The UHDSS operated by icddr, b collects routine demographic, socioeconomic, and health data of approximately 31,577 households in five slums within its coverage area since 2015. We interviewed randomly selected households from the UHDSS. The inclusion criteria were households residing within the UHDSS-covered areas in selected slums and expressing interest in participating in the study, with household members living with the selected household for at least six months prior to both surveys. Exclusion criteria included households not covered by the UHDSS and those not interested in participating in the study.

Sample size

For the household survey, the focus was to investigate the awareness level among the slum households about the Aalo Clinic model. We expected that around 30% of households would be aware of this model to utilize healthcare after its implementation. Mahmood et al., (2021) conducted a study in a similar setting and estimated that 32.2% of the sick household members in urban slums utilized healthcare from formal healthcare providers [16]. Considering this proportion, 5% error level for 95% confidence interval, and 1.2 design effect for selecting households from different slums, an estimated 387 households were required from each slum to assess the awareness and utilization of this model. Further, assuming a 10% non-response rate, an estimated 430 households were required to participate in each study area (total 430*5 = 2150 households). Finally, a total of 2000 households were interviewed in the baseline survey, whereas 2033 households were interviewed in the end-line survey. It is important to note that the sample distribution did not align with the population size of each slum. To maintain methodological consistency and ensure reliable results, an equal number of households were sampled from each slum. This uniform approach facilitated homogeneity in data collection and supported effective comparative analysis.

Sampling technique

We employed a systematic random sampling method to select the households. For each slum, we divided the total number of households by the sample size to establish an interval between households. We approached the households according to the slum-specific interval. For example, approximately 14,000 households reside in the Korail slum and 2,300 in the Shyampur slum. For Korail, we used an interval of 32 (14,000/430), and for Shyampur, an interval of 5 (2,300/430). If a selected household had multiple eligible members, we randomly chose one to participate in this study. In cases where a household had no eligible members, we proceeded to the next household until we found an eligible participant. Details about the UHDSS and its coverage are available elsewhere [15].

Data collection and variables

The baseline and end-line surveys were conducted using a pretested and identical semi-structured questionnaire. Face-to-face interviews were carried out with the adult household members aged 18 years and above. Twelve trained Field Research Assistants were recruited to conduct each survey. One Research Officer, together with the study investigators, supervised the data collectors, monitored the data collection process, and conducted regular data checks. Information on the household members’ demographic, socio-economic characteristics, illness, healthcare utilization, and providers, and knowledge about the source of PHC providers including Aalo clinic were collected. Household knowledge about the Aalo Clinic and sources of information were assessed based on respondents’ feedback. We asked respondents whether they were aware of the Aalo Clinic and its service delivery system. For those who stated ‘Yes’, we inquired about their sources of information regarding the clinic.

Generally, slum dwellers utilized healthcare from drug sellers, quacks, doctors’ chambers, community-based health facilities run by government organizations and non-government organizations (NGOs), private clinics and hospitals, government hospitals, medical colleges, and specialized healthcare institutes [15, 16]. In this study, MTPs include doctors with a minimum of an MBBS or equivalent degree who work as general practitioners, specialized doctors, or practitioners in medical colleges, government hospitals, registered non-government hospitals or clinics, and NGO-based health facilities operated by BRAC, NGO Forum for Public Health, Dhaka Ahsania Mission, Marie Stopes Bangladesh, etc. These healthcare organizations employ medically well-educated staff. In contrast, drug sellers, traditional healers, and quacks are considered non-trained providers. This classification aligns with similar categorizations used in other studies [20–22]. However, we divided the MTPs based on the Aalo Clinic and other healthcare facilities that functioned with the MTPs to assess the utilization of healthcare from MTPs of the Aalo Clinic Model.

We used asset quintiles to assess socioeconomic status because income cannot be a reliable indicator for informal workers in LMICs, where seasonal variations and income underreporting are common [23, 24]. Instead of relying on income levels, we employed asset quintiles as a measure of socioeconomic status. Household wealth was categorized into five quintiles based on various assets, such as access to utility services, improved sanitation and drinking water facilities, modern technologies, and furniture. These asset variables were used to calculate an asset score through principal component analysis. After adjusting for household size, this asset score was then used to categorize household wealth status [25]. It is noted that the wealth quintiles are relative to the study population, which resides in the slums, and is likely to belong to the poorest population segment in Bangladesh.

Statistical analysis

The characteristics of the surveyed households, household members, and their patterns of health service utilization are presented as categorical variables with frequency (n) and percentage (%). The mean and median OOP expenditure is presented in BDT (US$ 1 = BDT 110). Descriptive analysis was conducted to assess distributional changes among surveyed households between baseline and end-line periods. In addition, several statistical inferential tests, such as the Wilcoxon rank sum test for two categorical variables, the Kruskal-Wallis test for more than 2 categorical variables, and the t-test for the continuous variables were performed to assess significant changes between the waves.

Cross-tabulation and Chi-squared test were performed to assess the changes in healthcare utilization from MTP among the study population between the two waves. Further, three logistic regression models were applied in assessing the impact of the Aalo Clinic on healthcare utilization from the MTP while controlling for other covariates. In the first model, the binary dependent variable was ‘Utilization of Aalo Clinic MTP services’ (1 = used Aalo Clinic MTP services; 0 = did not use Aalo Clinic MTP services). The second model focused on ‘Utilization of non-Aalo Clinic MTP services’ (1 = used non-Aalo Clinic MTP services; 0 = did not use non-Aalo Clinic MTP services). In the third model, the dependent variable was ‘Utilization of non-MTP services’ (1 = used services from non-MTPs; 0 = did not use services from non-MTPs); independent predictor variables included age, sex, marital status, education, and occupation of the household head, household size, asset quintiles, house ownership, study area, distance to healthcare providers, survey type, illness severity, knowledge of the Aalo Clinic, and total healthcare expenditure. A p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. We analyzed the data using Stata SE, version 16.0 (StataCorp LLC).

Source of knowledge about Aalo clinic

Neighbors were the primary source of information about Aalo Clinic at both the baseline (39.93%) and end-line (41.15%). During the baseline period, Aalo Clinic’s promotional activities (e.g., signboards and displays) were the second most common source (24.31%). By the end-line, Aalo Clinic representatives (e.g., paramedics who provide services through mobile clinics) became the second most common source of information (17.14%). Healthcare utilization for acute illness

Table 2 presents the overall utilization of healthcare for the management of acute illness reported during the 30 days preceding the survey. At the end-line, about 90% of individuals who suffered from illness sought healthcare, whereas the proportion was 87% in the baseline. Healthcare utilization from public facilities and NGOs, including the Alao Clinic, significantly increased in the end-line period, while it significantly decreased from private and local pharmacies (P = 0.001). At the end-line, approximately 67% of the sick people who sought care utilized care from the local drug store or pharmacy, while the proportion was above 80% during the baseline period. In terms of healthcare expenditure, at the end-line, total expenditure significantly increased across sources of care compared to the baseline period, except for the public facilities. Specifically, during the baseline, the median cost at Aalo Clinic was estimated at BDT 180, which increased to BDT 273 at the end-line. Additionally, at baseline, the lowest cost was estimated for the pharmacy (BDT 150), whereas at the end-line, it was for the NGO facility (BDT 188).

Pattern of healthcare utilization and expenditure

Choice of healthcare providers

Figure 2 presents the distribution of healthcare service utilization from different providers by slum population between two waves. The utilization of healthcare from MTP was significantly higher in the end-line period (9.81% from Aalo Clinic and 18.6% from non-Aalo Clinic MTP) compared to the baseline period (0.64% from Aalo Clinic and 17.68% from non-Aalo Clinic MTP). However, in both periods, pharmacy (80.56% in the baseline and 67.19% in the end-line) followed by private facilities (13.17% in the baseline and 12.77% in the end-line) were identified as the main sources of healthcare among the study population.

Healthcare utilization across various providers

At baseline, a higher proportion of patients aged above 40 years (1.14%), married individuals (0.79%), those with no formal education (0.85%), household heads with secondary education or higher (1.02%), the unemployed (0.75%), irregular earners (0.71%), individuals from poorer households (1.13%), tenants (0.80%), those experiencing severe illness (0.99%), and those residing nearby (1.16%) predominantly utilized healthcare services from Aalo Clinic compared to their counterparts. In contrast, at the end-line, a higher proportion of patients aged between 0 and 5 years (11.32%), females (12.86%), widows/divorced individuals (16%), household heads with primary education (11.11%), unemployed (11.68%), currently employed household heads (10.6%), irregular earners (11.49%), members of smaller households (12.14%), those in the richest asset quintile (13.65%), homeowners (10.04%), those with mild illness (14.05%), and individuals living at a moderate distance (17.47%) predominantly utilized healthcare services from Aalo Clinic compared to their counterparts. We found significant associations between the utilization of healthcare services from non-Aalo Clinic MTPs and various factors, including marital status, distance to service providers, household head’s occupation, asset quintiles, household ownership status, slum location, survey period, and total healthcare expenditure. Married members were less likely (OR: 0.53; 95% CI: 0.32–0.88) to utilize non-Aalo Clinic MTPs compared to their unmarried counterparts. Individuals residing nearby or within a moderate distance from non-Aalo Clinic MTPs were less likely to utilize these services compared to those living at a longer distance. Members from households with heads in other occupational categories (e.g., disabled, retired, etc.) were two times more likely (OR: 2.10; 95% CI: 1.04–4.24) to utilize non-Aalo Clinic MTPs than those with currently employed household heads. Individuals from richer and richest asset quintiles were approximately two times more likely (OR: 1.88; 95% CI: 1.23–2.63 and OR: 1.66; 95% CI: 1.12–2.46, respectively) to utilize non-Aalo Clinic MTPs compared to those in the middle quintile. During the end-line period, household members were less likely (OR: 0.72; 95% CI: 0.56–0.92) to utilize non-Aalo Clinic MTPs compared to the baseline period. Moreover, household members with moderate and severe levels of illness were 2.29 and 3.68 times more likely, respectively, to utilize non-Aalo Clinic MTPs compared to those with mild illness levels.

We also found significant associations between the utilization of healthcare services from non-MTPs and various factors, encompassing sex, marital status, education of the household head, distance from service providers, asset quintiles, household ownership status, slum residency, survey period, severity of illness, knowledge about Aalo Clinic, and total healthcare expenditure. Female household members were more likely (OR: 0.65; 95% CI: 0.52–0.81) to utilize non-MTPs compared to their male counterparts. Married individuals showed a substantially higher likelihood, being approximately two times more likely (OR: 1.72; 95% CI: 1.13–2.63), to utilize non-MTPs compared to unmarried individuals. Individuals residing nearby and within a moderate distance from non-MTPs were notably more likely (OR: 22.11; 95% CI: 15.86–30.82 and OR: 5.96; 95% CI: 4.26–8.33, respectively) to utilize these services compared to those living at a longer distance. Members from richer and richest asset quintiles were less likely (OR: 0.62; 95% CI: 0.45–0.86 and OR: 0.59; 95% CI: 0.42–0.83, respectively) to utilize non-MTPs compared to those in the middle quintile. During the end-line period, household members were less likely (OR: 0.59; 95% CI: 0.47–0.73) to utilize non-MTPs compared to the baseline period. Moreover, household members with moderate and severe levels of illness were less likely (OR: 0.69; 95% CI: 0.50–0.94 and OR: 0.41; 95% CI: 0.29–0.59, respectively) to utilize non-MTPs compared to those with mild illness levels. Additionally, household members who possessed knowledge about Aalo Clinic were less likely (OR: 0.69; 95% CI: 0.51–0.95) to utilize non-MTPs compared to those without such knowledge.

Discussion

The pilot Aalo Clinic model, which provides an essential package of healthcare services free of charge, has demonstrated a significant positive impact on the utilization of primary healthcare (PHC) services among low-income slum dwellers. At the end-line assessment, these individuals were 12.43 times more likely to utilize services from MTPs through the Aalo Clinic compared to the baseline period. The establishment of this model not only improved access to and use of PHC services for the slum population but also reduced dependency on non-MTPs by approximately 13%. This is consistent with the outcomes observed in the ‘Mohalla Clinic’ initiative in Delhi, India [26], which similarly enhanced the accessibility and affordability of primary care for underserved urban populations [27, 28].

Evidence shows knowledgeable individuals can make informed decisions in choosing healthcare providers [29]. This study found that knowledge about Alao Clinic influenced the slum dwellers to more frequently utilize healthcare from Alao Clinics’ MTP compared to other sources. Zahid et al. (2023) conducted a study to identify factors that influence healthcare utilization from the MTP of a government-sponsored health protection scheme among the below-poverty-line population in Bangladesh and reported that households with knowledge about the scheme were significantly positively associated with higher healthcare utilization from the MTP of that scheme (21).

We found that females were more likely to use Aalo Clinic MTP services compared to males, while males were more likely to use non-MTP services compared to females. There may be several reasons behind this including more women and child-friendly services, occupation of patients, waiting time etc. All these may contribute to a higher number of females utilizing services from Aalo clinic compared to their male counterparts. Earlier studies reported a similar finding [30–32] including the study in Madrid, Spain where Redondo-Sendino et al. (2006) reported that women compared to men significantly utilized primary care clinics and diagnostic services [31].

This study also explored the relationship between household members’ wealth status and healthcare utilization patterns. Hence, it is also noted that the population of the study resides in the slum areas close to the Aalo Clinics, the income level of the slum population is substantially less compared to the general population. Individuals from wealthier households in the study population were more likely to use services from MTPs, be it from Aalo Clinics or other setups compared to individuals from the lower income households. Better awareness regarding quality of care, harmful practices at the non-MTP setups, ease of access, etc. could be contributing factors to this preference. Ahmed et al. (2018) conducted a study in Bangladesh, which similarly found that individuals from the wealthiest households in the study population are more likely to use healthcare services from MTPs compared to those from the poorest households [20].

This study revealed an intriguing pattern in healthcare utilization based on households’ distance and the type of healthcare provider. We found a significant association between lower distance and higher healthcare utilization from both Aalo Clinic MTP and non-MTPs which indicates that individuals tend to seek healthcare more from the providers when they are closer to their location, regardless of whether the provider is an MTP or non-MTP. Earlier studies also reported a similar finding and reported that proximity plays a critical role in healthcare-seeking behavior [33, 34]. On the other hand, the significant positive association between longer distance and higher healthcare utilization specifically from non-Aalo Clinic MTPs reveals an interesting trend. This could be indicative of several factors such as specialization/reputation, and the referral system [35]. Non-Aalo Clinic MTPs might offer specialized services or have a reputation for providing high-quality care that attracts patients from farther away or individuals may be referred to non-Aalo Clinic MTPs for certain treatments or procedures not available at closer facilities, leading them to utilize more healthcare services from distant healthcare providers. Zahid et al. (2023) also found that though healthcare, medicine, and diagnostic services are free in a government-sponsored health protection scheme, still a higher proportion of people sought care from other MTPs located within a similar or more distance [21].

Although the Aalo Clinic has demonstrated a significant impact in increasing the utilization of MTPs and reducing reliance on local drug stores for healthcare services, a considerable portion of the slum population still opts for informal providers, such as drug sellers (67.19%). This behavior may be rooted in historical patterns of healthcare seeking. Consequently, changing this behavior will require time and concerted efforts. Additionally, the prevalence of such providers in the community, coupled with lower travel time and costs associated with accessing them, could contribute to their continued use. Thus, to address this challenge effectively, it is crucial to establish and sustain awareness raising activities and satellite clinic services within the community over an extended period. These clinics can serve as platforms for educating slum dwellers about the importance of utilizing MTPs while minimizing the barriers of travel time and costs.

It is necessary to point out that for the achievement of UHC, both the affordability and the quality of healthcare are crucial. While Aalo Clinic has made strides in improving access to primary healthcare, concerns may arise regarding the affordability of its services due to associated expenditures. Despite being conveniently located for slum dwellers and offering free registration, consultation, essential medicines, and diagnostic/lab services, users of this model still incur significant healthcare expenses. This expenditure could stem from a lack of available medicines and laboratory services within Aalo Clinics, necessitating service users to procure these necessities externally, thereby leading to OOP payments. Addressing this affordability barrier requires ensuring that Aalo Clinics are adequately equipped with essential medicines and laboratory services. Close collaboration with government stakeholders responsible for urban PHC provision could be explored to address this shortfall effectively. By enhancing the readiness of Aalo Clinics and minimizing out-of-pocket expenses, equitable access to affordable healthcare services can be achieved, aligning with the goals of UHC.

While there was a positive indication of increased healthcare utilization at Aalo Clinic, the proportion of patients experiencing longer waiting times (over 20 min) rose significantly from 2.04% at baseline to 43.79% at end-line, posing a challenge for service access. This may be a consequence of higher patient flow or limited healthcare providers, as each center of the Aalo Clinic model has one male and one female general practitioner in each shift, which may be insufficient to meet demand. Evidence demonstrates that longer wait times are associated with a lower perception of care quality [36]. Improving the infrastructure and human resource capacity of the Aalo Clinic might influence tackling this challenge.

This study has several limitations. First, seasonal variations in healthcare utilization were not captured, as the baseline survey was conducted from January to February 2022 and the end-line survey from April to June 2023. These differing time frames may have influenced healthcare-seeking behaviors, potentially affecting the comparability of utilization patterns. Second, reliance on self-reported data may introduce the possibility of recall bias, which may lead to inaccurate or incomplete reporting of healthcare utilization, either through overestimation or underestimation of visits and treatments. Consequently, the validity of some findings may be affected, particularly those relying heavily on respondents’ memory of past healthcare events. Lastly, the cross-sectional study design limits the ability to draw causal inferences between identified factors and healthcare utilization outcomes.

Despite the limitations, the study’s strength lies in its significant policy implications. This is the first study that assessed the effect of this model on healthcare utilization among urban slum dwellers as well as identified the factors associated with healthcare utilization from Aalo Clinic MTP compared to other MTPs and non-MTPs. The findings of the study are expected to inspire program implementers and policymakers to design a patient-centered urban PHC model that aligns with the goals of the National Urban Health Strategies [3].

Further research is needed to understand the reasons behind complex healthcare utilization patterns, including patient preferences, healthcare provider characteristics at different centers, and the readiness of primary healthcare centers to meet the demands of target populations. This could provide valuable insights for tailoring the model to strengthen the urban PHC service delivery system and better serve diverse communities in Bangladesh. Long-term implementation research can generate valuable insights into the operation and scalability of this model. Moreover, an economic evaluation should be carried out to determine if the model is financially feasible before expanding it.

Conclusion

The Aalo Clinic model demonstrates a viable approach to strengthening the urban PHC delivery system and enabling low-income slum dwellers to access healthcare from trained healthcare providers. This model thus should be considered for scale-up in other urban areas in Bangladesh. Ensuring such healthcare for urban low-income and vulnerable populations is a formidable task in the context of countries with middle or low-income. The implementation and scale-up of this model may potentially address this challenge and expedite the achievement of UHC. However, before expanding the model further, it is essential to address the drawbacks associated with healthcare utilization.
