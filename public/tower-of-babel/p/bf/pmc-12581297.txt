The status of suicide literacy and suicide stigma: their association with demographic factors among Iranian general population
Authors: Alireza Jafari, Mahdi Moshki, Elaheh Lael-Monfared, Mahbobeh Nejatian, Fatemehzahra Naddafi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prevalence of suicide has increased in recent years, and understanding the factors that contribute to this can help prevent suicide. This study aimed to determine the status of suicide literacy and suicide stigma and their relationship with demographic factors.MethodsThis cross-sectional study aimed to determine the status of suicide literacy and suicide stigma and their relationship with demographic factors among the 960 general population in Gonabad (a city in the northeast of Iran) in 2023. Data collection instruments comprised of demographic questionnaires, the literacy of suicide scale, and the stigma of suicide scale, and data were analyzed using SPSS version 24 software.ResultsOnly 0.7% (n = 7) were able to choose the correct answer of 19 or more suicide literacy items. In addition, only 1.3% (n = 12) of the participants had chosen the incorrect answer for all suicide literacy items. There was a significant relationship between sex (p < 0.001), age group (p < 0.001), marital status (p < 0.001), education level (p = 0.003), occupation (p < 0.001), economic status (p = 0.011), sources of obtaining health information (p < 0.001), and get information related to mental illness (p < 0.001) with suicide literacy. There was a significant relationship between sex (p < 0.001), age group (p < 0.001), marital status (p = 0.001), occupation (p < 0.001), sources of obtaining health information (p = 0.032), and get information related to mental illness (p < 0.001) with suicide stigma. Based on the Pearson correlation findings, there was a negative and significant correlation between suicide literacy and suicide stigma (r= -0.277, p < 0.001). The findings of the linear regression showed that the subscales of treatment/prevention and causes/triggers had the greatest impact on predicting suicide stigma.ConclusionThese results underscore the urgent need to design health interventions to enhance suicide literacy. Also, developing evidence-based educational campaigns with the aim at improving suicide literacy particularly regarding treatment/prevention and causes/triggers could be an effective strategy for reducing suicide stigma in the general population.

Introduction

Suicide refers to harm to the self to end life. Suicide as a sad and complex phenomenon has become a serious global issue today [1, 2]. In the last 45 years, suicide rates worldwide have increased by 60% [3]. Approximately 10 million suicide attempts occur in the world annually, of which approximately one million die from suicide [4]. In Iran, suicide rates are also high, and the results of a systematic review and meta-analysis study in 2023 showed that the prevalence rates of suicide attempts and suicide deaths were 131 and 8.14 per 100,000 people, respectively. According to a systematic review and meta-analysis study and historical cohort study in Iran, completed suicide in different provinces were reviewed, and Gonabad and Bajestan (cities in northeast of Iran) had the highest prevalence (with 130.0 cases per 100,000 people) and Tehran had the lowest prevalence of completed suicide in the general population (with 0.2 cases per 100,000 people) [5, 6].

The phenomenon of suicide has a complex and multidimensional nature, and as a result, the interaction of various factors, such as personal background, family history, neurobiological factors, cultural and social factors, and stressful events occurs [7]. The costs of suicide include not only the loss of human life but also physical, mental, and emotional stress that can affect friends and family for a long time [8, 9]. On the other hand, the social, psychological, and economic impacts of suicide exceeding the global level are also significant [10].

However, suicide is a preventive phenomenon [11]. Suicide prevention is currently one of the sustainable development goals of the United Nations [12]. Therefore, suicide prevention should be prioritized at both the national and global health levels [13]. The results of the studies showed that there are several obstacles such as suicide stigma and poor suicide literacy to suicide prevention [14, 15]. Suicide literacy involves the ability to recognize early signs of suicide risk and knowledge of the risk factors and protective factors involved in suicide prevention. Individuals with greater literacy in this area are more likely to exhibit supportive behaviors for people at risk and can play an important role in reducing suicide attempt rate. Research indicates that increasing suicide literacy can help reduce social stigma and improve public attitudes toward help-seeking for individuals at risk of suicide [16].

Stigma is defined as a sign of infamy, scandal, stain, and blame [17]. Unfortunately, there was a high level of stigma on the phenomenon of suicide, and the results of numerous studies also showed that in countries with low and middle incomes, such as Iran, stigma toward suicidal people is high [18–20]. Suicide stigma can not only undermine morale, social isolation, despair, and performance disruption, but it can also be a major obstacle to seeking help from a psychological professional [17, 21]. Important aspects of stigma, such as social prejudice, fear, and shame, negative beliefs about mental illness, social isolation, and discrimination, all act as barriers to seeking help from professional psychological practitioners in people who attempt suicide or people with suicidal ideation [19]. Therefore, dealing with suicide stigma can provide a more acceptable and supportive environment for suicidal and increase the likelihood of seeking help from a psychological professional [9]. Suicide stigma is a serious barrier to help-seeking behaviors. This stigma often manifests as feelings of shame, fear of judgment, and social isolation, which ultimately prevent individuals from accessing mental health and support services [22, 23].

Poor suicide literacy can also be an obstacle to the prevention of suicide [14]. Suicide literacy is a particular type of mental health literacy that refers to various aspects of suicide, including risk factors, causes, symptoms, and prevention and treatment [24]. Increasing suicide literacy is one of the most important elements in suicide prevention strategies because the promotion of suicide literacy improves the attitudes and intentions of individuals seeking help from a professional psychological counselor [25]. The results of a study in Japan showed that high levels of help seeking from professional psychological practitioners are related to high suicide literacy and low suicide stigma [26]. A key theoretical idea in this field is that understanding suicide and increasing suicide literacy can help raise awareness of suicide-related issues in the community, and may also reduce self-stigma associated with seeking psychological help, which in turn can facilitate help-seeking behaviors [27, 28].

Finally, dealing with suicide stigma and suicide literacy by implementing and developing suicide and mental health programs can reduce stigma, increase knowledge, and prevent suicide attempts [9]. Understanding these factors can be the primary step in promoting and improving these programs. Therefore, this study was conducted to evaluate the following purposes:

Method

This cross-sectional study aimed to determine the status of suicide literacy and suicide stigma and their relationship with demographic factors in Iran in 2023.

Sample size

Considering the 95% confidence level, test power of 80%, correlation between suicide literacy and suicide stigma in similar study (r = 0.11) [9], the minimum sample size was calculated as 646 that with 20% non-response rate, the sample size increased to 808 participants. Finally, due to the availability of samples, 960 participants were selected for this study.

Sampling method

Participants were selected from health centers by proportionately stratified sampling. First, the number of centers and the population of each center were identified. Based on the population of each center, the sample size required from each center was determined. Finally, people with the entry criteria were selected and entered using a simple random-method. Residence in Gonabad city, age 18 years and older, informed consent, and desire to participate in the study were the inclusion criteria for selecting the samples.

Data collection instruments

Demographic questionnaires, the literacy of suicide scale, and the stigma scale were used for data gathering.

Demographic questionnaire: In this section, the demographic characteristics of the participants were surveyed. The Literacy of Suicide Scale (LOSS): Calear et al., designed and assessed this scale [29]. The validity and reliability of the Persian version of this scale was evaluated in the Iranian population, and the scale was approved with 25 items and 4 subscales of signs/symptoms (with 5 items), risk factors (with 7 items), causes/triggers (with 9 items), and treatment/prevention (with 4 items). Each item was measured on a three-point scale (True, I don’t know, and False). The correct answer to each question gets 1 score and the false answer or select the option of “I don’t know” in each question get the score of zero. The minimum score is zero, and the maximum score is 25; a higher score indicates a better suicide literacy status [30]. Stigma of Suicide Scale (SOSS): Batterhamet al. designed and assessed this scale [16, 31]. The short form of the scale consists of 16 question and 3 subscales of isolation/depression (with 4 items), glorification/normalization (with 4 items), and stigma (with 8 items). The items were measured on a 5-point Likert scale (strongly disagree = 1, to strongly agree = 5). [16, 31]. To calculate the mean score, sum the score of items in each subscale and then divide the obtained score by the number of questions in each subscale. Also, to calculate the total mean score of scale, sum the score of all items and then divide the obtained score by the number of all questions. The mean score in each subscale and SOSS will be between 1 and 5. Higher score indicate a higher suicide stigma status.Cronbach α coefficients of the SOSS overall, stigma subscale, isolation/depression subscale, and glorification/normalization subscale were 0.70, 0.88, 0.80, and 0.78, respectively [16, 31]. The Persian version of the SOSS consists of 15 items (one item deleted in psychometric evaluation) and three factors of Stigma (7 items), Glorification/Normalization (4 items), and Isolation/Depression (4 items). In the Persian version, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for SOSS, Stigma, Glorification/Normalization, and Isolation/Depression was reported 0.834, 0.875, 0.937, and 0.760.[32].

Statistical analysis

The relationship between demographic variables, suicide literacy, and suicide stigma was surveyed using an independent samples t-test and one-way ANOVA. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the correlation between suicide literacy and suicide stigma and their subscales. The chi-square test was used to assess the relationship between demographic variables and suicide literacy status. Linear regression was used to determine the effect of the suicide literacy subscales on suicide stigma. Data were entered in the SPSS software (version 24), and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Discussion

Suicide is a complex phenomenon in terms of its etiology, treatment, and prevention. Suicide literacy and suicide stigma can play a role in the occurrence of suicide as two of the most important factors. However, very few studies have examined the current state of suicide literacy and suicide stigma in the Iranian public population. This study was conducted to investigate the current state of suicide literacy and suicide stigma and their relationship with demographic factors in the Iranian general population. The results revealed poor suicide literacy and high suicide stigma in Iranian general population.

In this study, the mean score of suicide literacy was 10.33 ± 3.99. These results indicated that most participants answered more than half of the questions incorrectly; as a result, more than half of the participants had poor suicide literacy. The results of our study are in line with the results of studies conducted in Jordan and Bangladesh [9, 20]. The Al-Shannaq study on youth aged 16 to 24 years in Jordan found that only 22.3% of the participants were able to complete more than half of the suicide literacy questionnaire, and the results indicated that suicide literacy was very poor in the population [20]. In another study of the youth population 18–35 years in Bangladesh, the mean score of suicide literacy was less than half (this means that participants answered less than half of the questionnaire questions correctly), and suicide literacy was reported to be very poor [9]. However, the mean score of suicide literacy in studies conducted in Japan [14], Australia [16], and Germany [33] showed that the mean score of suicide literacy in the general population was more than half (this means that participants answered more than half of the questionnaire questions correctly). As a result, suicide literacy in the Iranian public population was less than in these three studies. One possible explanation for this could be that the countries of Iran, Jordan, and Bangladesh are low- and middle-income countries, and low suicide literacy is a major problem in these countries [34].

In this study, the most incorrect answer was related to the subscale of signs and symptoms of suicide and the most correct response was related to the treatment and prevention subscale. In a study among students, the incorrect answer was related to signs and symptoms [25]. Also, in the study among the public population in Germany, the most accurate response was related to the subscale of treatment and prevention, and the highest misconduct was related to the subscale of risk factors [33]. Good performance in the subscale of treatment and prevention may be attributed to the fact that the items related to this subscale are more general, understandable, and more considered in training and policy programs [35, 36].

In this study, there was a significant relationship between suicide literacy with female sex, being single, lower age (Age < 27), higher education, being a university student, and good economic status, and the score of suicide literacy was higher than other groups. In addition, participants who received health information from physicians or healthcare providers had higher suicide literacy than others. The results of a study in Australia showed that there was a relationship between age, low education level, and male sex with low knowledge about suicide [16]. In a study in Germany, suicide literacy was positively correlated with education level but negatively correlated with age [33]. In the study conducted in Bangladesh, the score of suicide literacy was low in male people, those with less education, those who divorced, and those who were widowed [9]. In a study in Japan, lower education levels and younger age were associated with increased suicide literacy, and there was no difference between the two sexes in suicide literacy [14]. In this study, individuals with good economic status had higher suicide literacy. To the best of our knowledge, only a few studies have examined the relationship between suicide literacy and financial status. Therefore, future research should investigate the relationship between this variable and suicide literacy.

In this study, the mean score of the suicide stigma was 2.91, indicating a high suicide stigma in Iran. Although suicide stigma in Iran is less than in Jordan (M = 3.24) and Bangladesh (M = 3.14). Also, it is higher than in Japan (M = 2.32) and Australia (M = 2.18) [9, 14, 16, 20]. This could be because, in Iran, social media does not provide much information about mental health; thus, suicide awareness is low and stigma is high. In countries such as Australia, the United States, and Germany, media campaigns have been developed to raise suicide awareness and reduce suicide stigma [19, 37]. On the other hand, the results of a study in Australia showed that there are approximately 60 suicide prevention networks in the country, and one of the common strategies of these networks is anti-stigma [38]. However, in Iran, the lack of national suicide prevention strategies is a major challenge toward anti-stigma prevention [39]. Another reason is cultural factors. For example, a sense of humor and joking about social issues such as patients with mental illness can lead to the development of suicide stigma. Also, most Iranians are Muslims, and suicide is forbidden in Islam. As a result, suicide in religious societies has been stigmatized. Overall, adopting a comprehensive approach, including the cooperation of authorities and organizations in developing non-governmental associations, establishing suicide prevention campaigns, the cooperation of cultural institutions and religious leaders to reduce stigma, and public education to reduce suicide stigma in Iran seems necessary [19].

In this study, the mean score of the isolation/depression subscale was 3.15. The score of this subscale is less than the studies in Bangladesh (M = 3.62), Jordan (M = 3.63), and Australia (M = 4.11). This result showed that suicidal people in Iran, unlike other societies, are less isolated and more present in the community. Also, the mean score of the glorification/normalization subscale in Iran (M = 2.18) was lower than the studies in Australia (M = 2.45), Bangladesh (M = 2.26), and Jordan (M = 2.94) [9, 16, 20]. The result also showed that in Iran, misconceptions about suicide, which can be as dangerous as stigma, are very low.

In this study, being male was associated with more suicide stigma, more attribution of suicide to isolation and depression, and more glorification and normalization of suicide. In the study conducted in Australia, being male was also associated with greater suicide stigma and less suicide attribution to depression and isolation. In this study, the age of more than 47 years was associated with higher suicide stigma, whereas in a study in Australia, a lower age was associated with suicide stigma [16]. Also, a study in Japan found no significant relationship between age and suicide stigma [14]. Given that various studies have adapted different results to the relationship between sex and age and attitude toward suicide, further research is recommended. The individuals who were divorced in this study also had higher suicide stigma and a positive attitude toward suicide glorification/normalization. The unemployed also had a higher suicidal stigma, and retired people had a more attitude toward suicide glorification/normalization. Overall, in our society, males, older people, divorced people, unemployed people, and retired people have a false attitude toward suicide, which can be a risk factor for suicide [40]. Also, being divorced or unemployed is also associated with an increased risk of suicide [41, 42]. As a result, on the one hand, these groups have more inappropriate attitudes toward suicide, and on the other hand, the risk of suicide is greater. Therefore, training and supporting these vulnerable groups over other people in the community should be a priority.

Finally, this study found a significant and negative correlation between suicide literacy and the subscales of stigma, isolation/depression, and glorification/normalization. This finding showed that promoting suicide literacy in communities can be accompanied by a more accurate attitude toward suicide. This result also matched the results of studies conducted in Jordan and Germany [20, 33].

The linear regression results also showed that the two subscales of Causes/Triggers and Treatment/Prevention had the most power to predict suicide stigma. In a similar study by Ludwig in the German population, the results showed that with increased suicide literacy, the score subscales of stigma, causes/stimuli, and treatment/prevention decreased [33]. As a result, by increasing the suicide literacy of the community in terms of causes/triggers, risk factors, signs and symptoms, treatment/prevention, inappropriate attitudes toward suicide such as stigma, isolation/depression toward suicide, and the glorification/normalization, can be reduced.

Strengths and limitations

A study strength is the large sample size. The study was also conducted on the general population, and different age groups and social classes were assessed. This study has several limitations. Questionnaires were completed through self-reporting by participants, and the data may have some errors. The cross-sectional design limits the ability of the study to establish causal relationships between the observed factors and suicide rates. In future studies, we recommend conducting qualitative research to better understand the sociocultural factors influencing suicide in northeastern Iran, exploring the impact of digital health interventions and mental health awareness programs on suicide prevention, and performing the longitudinal studies examining the effects of mental health policies in rural vs. urban settings in Iran, particularly focusing on high-risk populations.

Clinical implications

This study provides important clinical insights. Mental health professionals in rural and underserved areas, such as Gonabad, should be trained to recognize early signs of suicidal ideation, particularly in vulnerable populations . Strengthening community-based mental health services and integrating suicide prevention strategies into primary care can reduce stigma and improve access to care. Tailored intervention programs, including digital mental health tools, should be prioritized to address the unique needs of at-risk groups.

Conclusion

The results of this study indicate low suicide literacy and high suicide stigma in Iranian general population. Therefore, our findings show that information about suicide is useful for focusing on causes/triggers, risk factors, signs and symptoms, and treatment/prevention to raise awareness of suicide identification. Since suicide literacy has been negatively linked to stigma, providing educational material to the general population can help reduce suicide stigma. The results of this study highlighted the necessity of planning health education programs, designing suicide prevention strategies, and media campaigns, as well as the cooperation of cultural agents and religious leaders. The implementation of these interventions can increase suicide literacy, thereby increasing awareness and reducing stigma among individuals.
