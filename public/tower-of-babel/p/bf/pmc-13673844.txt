Analysis of the anatomical relationship between the anterior segment (S3) and adjacent segments in the left upper lobe
Authors: Xinfeng Xu, Yihan Yang, Qianrui Ren, Wei Wen, Zhibo Wang, Quan Zhu, Liang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundS3 of the left upper lobe (LUL), situated between S1 + 2 and S4 + 5, serves as a vital anatomical bridge. This study examines the anatomical relationships of S3 with surrounding segments to enhance surgical accuracy and minimize complications.MethodsA total of 120 patients with LUL lesions scheduled for surgery from October 2024 to March 2025 were included. Three-dimensional CT bronchography and angiography (3D-CTBA) allowed us to analyze S3 anatomy, its spatial relationships with S1 + 2 and S4 + 5, and lesion locations. Trans-segmental and trans-subsegmental arteries were defined.ResultsS3-related lesions accounted for 46% (61/133) of all LUL lesions. B3 branching was classified into three types, with B3a; B3b + c being the most common (86%, 103/120). An independent origin of B1 + 2c limited S3a to the central region of the LUL, while the two-branch LUL bronchus and semi-central vein were associated with S3a extension to the oblique fissure (p = 0.029, 0.001, 0.002). The predominant pattern was independent A3 (73%, 88/120), with trans-subsegmental arteries most frequently seen (31%, 22/72). Only 24% (19/79) matched the typical B3 bronchial pattern. Arterial common trunks between S3–S1 + 2 and S3–S4 + 5 occurred in 55% (66/120) and 30% (36/120), respectively; venous common trunks occurred in 70% (84/120) and 31% (37/120).ConclusionsFrequent trans-subsegmental arteries, variable S3a boundaries, and shared vascular trunks complicate surgery, making preoperative 3D reconstruction essential for precise segmentectomy planning.Keywords: Left upper lobe (LUL), Segmentectomy, Three-dimensional CT bronchography and angiography (3D-CTBA), Bronchovascular anatomy, Trans-subsegmental arteries, Anatomical variation

Background

While segmentectomy has demonstrated oncological efficacy for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) measuring ≤ 2 cm [1, 2], its advantage in preserving pulmonary function—a primary objective of sublobar resection—remains a subject of debate. Evidence indicates that this functional benefit diminishes markedly when resection involves more than two segments or five subsegments [3–5]. For example, after resection of the upper division segment, postoperative pulmonary function does not significantly differ from that following lobectomy, due to lingular segment collapse and bronchial angulation, a pathophysiology akin to middle lobe syndrome observed after right upper lobectomy [6]. Recent discussions have even focused on single segmentectomy: S3 resection provides no substantial benefit in preserving pulmonary function compared to left upper lobectomy, likely attributable to S3’s extensive anatomical interfaces with S1 + 2 and S4 + 5 [7].

Anatomically, S3 occupies a distinct position between S1 + 2 and S4 + 5, acting as a critical anatomical bridge with complex structures that present significant surgical challenges [8]. Its distribution extends widely across the costal surface and anterior mediastinal surface, and may even reach the oblique fissure [9]. Additionally, anatomical variation among B3a, B1 + 2c, and B4a results in a variable distribution of S3a along the oblique fissure, greatly complicating the identification of the intersegmental interface. Incorrect division along this interface can lead to residual lesions, injury to remaining lung tissue, atelectasis, and intersegmental bleeding [10–12].

Due to the anatomical complexity and highly variable intersegmental interfaces of segment S3, this study systematically examined the 3D anatomy of surgical patients to summarize the patterns of S3 and the characteristics of its intersegmental interfaces, aiming to optimize surgical procedures and reduce intraoperative injuries and postoperative complications.

Methods

Clinical materials

Imaging data were collected from patients scheduled for surgery due to nodules in the left upper lobe (LUL) between October 2024 and March 2025. Based on three-dimensional CT bronchography and angiography (3D-CTBA), image analysis was performed to determine nodule location, characterize S3 anatomy, and evaluate its relationship with adjacent segments.

Inclusion criteria:

Surgery limited to the LUL with comprehensive clinical and imaging data.

Clear visualization of subsegmental bronchi and their corresponding arteries and veins on 3D-CTBA.

Exclusion criteria:

Among 122 patients, two were excluded because the S3 segment of the LUL was partially replaced by B1 + 2a, B1 + 2c, or S4b, resulting in difficulties in bronchial nomenclature. For example, in cases where B3a is absent, forcing standard nomenclature would result in an anomalous common trunk of B1 + 2c and B3a. Consequently, following the principle of bronchoarterial accompaniment, the corresponding arterial nomenclature would yield an A1 + 2c+A3a pattern. Including such idiosyncratic groupings would significantly overcomplicate the classification system and reduce its clinical utility. Ultimately, 120 cases were included for anatomical analysis.

Lesion localization

Lesion localization was determined based on proximity to anatomical structures: lesions adjacent to S3-specific arteries, bronchi, or intrasegmental veins (e.g., A3c, B3c, or V3c) were assigned to S3; those near intersegmental veins (e.g., V3b) were classified as intersegmental lesions and attributed to both adjacent segments (e.g., S3 and S4).

Key anatomical definitions

Trans-subsegmental artery

Within S3a, subsegmental artery running alongside a subsegmental bronchus gives off a sub-subsegmental artery and its branches that enter an adjacent subsegment (e.g., as shown in Fig. 1a, A3a running alongside B3a gives off A3cii entering S3c, A3cii representing a trans-subsegmental artery). Lowercase Roman numerals, such as i and ii, are used as abbreviations for sub-subsegmental branches.

Schematic diagram of the S3 bronchus and artery in the left upper lobe. (a) A3a, coursing alongside B3a, gives off A3cii into subsegment S3c. A3cii represents a trans-subsegmental artery. Med.A4 + 5 denotes the mediastinal type A4 + 5. (b) A3a, accompanying B3a, gives off A1 + 2ci into segment S1 + 2. A1 + 2ci represents a trans-segmental artery

Trans-segmental artery

A subsegmental artery accompanying a subsegmental bronchus gives off a sub-subsegmental artery and its branches that cross the intersegmental interface into a neighboring segment (e.g., as shown in Fig. 1b, A3a running alongside B3a gives off A1+2ci entering S1 + 2, A1+2ci representing a trans-segmental artery).

Central vein pattern

The central vein, semi-central vein, and non-central vein types in the left upper lobe were determined by the anatomical relationship between the convergence of V1 + 2b and V1 + 2c and B3. It was defined as the central vein type if V1 + 2b and V1 + 2c converged posterior to B3, and as the semi-central vein type if they converged anterior to B3. If there was no convergence between V1 + 2b and V1 + 2c, the branches were classified as the non-central vein type [13, 14].

Mediastinal type A4 + 5 (Med.A4 + 5)

The A4 + 5 branches of the LUL originate from the mediastinal portion (pars mediastinalis) of the pulmonary artery, distinguishing them from the interlobar A4 + 5 branches that arise from the interlobar arterial trunk [15].

Results

A total of 133 lesions were identified in 120 patients, with their anatomical distribution shown in Fig. 2. S3-related lesions numbered 61 (46%, 61/133), including 32 intrasegmental and 29 intersegmental lesions (22%, 29/133). Of the 29 intersegmental lesions, nine were located between S3 and S1 + 2a (adjacent to V1 + 2a), 3 in the central region between S3 and S1 + 2 (adjacent to Vl), 9 among S3, S1 + 2, and S4a (adjacent to V1 + 2d), and 8 between S3 and S4b (adjacent to V3b). Here, Vl refers to the intersegmental vein located centrally within the upper division, between S1 + 2 and S3. Additionally, 51 lesions were within S1 + 2 and 21 within S4 + 5. The high proportion (46%) and dense distribution of S3-related lesions highlight its importance in surgical planning for LUL segmentectomy.

As detailed in Table 1, anatomical analysis of 120 cases revealed three-branch patterns of B3, with the B3a; B3b + c pattern being most prevalent (86%, 103/120), significantly exceeding the B3a + b; B3c pattern (11 cases) and the B3a; B3b; B3c pattern (6 cases). The two-branch pattern of the LUL bronchus (LUL bronchus comprising B1 + 2 + 3 and B4 + 5) was predominant (93%, 111/120), significantly more common than the three-branch pattern (LUL bronchus comprising B1 + 2, B3, and B4 + 5; 9 cases) and the independent B1 + 2c type (the upper division bronchus comprising B1 + 2a + b, B1 + 2c, B3; 24 cases). The semi-central vein type was observed in 73% (87/120) of cases, significantly surpassing the central vein type (25 cases) and non-central vein type (8 cases). B3a is deeply located centrally within the LUL and may extend to the surface of the oblique fissure. S3 extension to the oblique fissure was identified in 30 cases (25%), showing a significant association with the B3a; B3b + c pattern and semi-central vein type (P = 0.029 and P = 0.001, respectively). Conversely, when B1 + 2c originated independently from the upper division bronchus, S1 + 2c occupied the oblique fissure area, preventing B3a extension toward the fissure; no S3 extension to the oblique fissure occurred in these configurations.

The independent A3 pattern, defined as S3 being supplied by a single separate A3 artery without branches from A1 + 2 or A4 + 5 extending into S3, was observed in 88 cases (73%, 88/120). Among these, the two-branch configuration was most common (82%, 72/88), followed by three-branch (15 cases) and four-branch (1 case) patterns (Table 2). Subtype analysis of the two-branch type revealed A3a + b; A3c in 20 cases (28%, 20/72), A3a; A3b + c in 19 cases (26%, 19/72), and A3a + c; A3b in 11 cases (15%, 11/72). The trans-subsegmental artery subtype was the most frequent (31%, 22/72), making it the predominant pattern within the two-branch type.

Independent A3 Pattern and Subtypes

A3x: Trans-subsegmental artery in S3

Due to its anatomical bridging role between S1 + 2 and S4 + 5, the A3 artery often forms common trunks with the arteries of neighboring segments (Table 3). A common trunk between S3 and S1 + 2 arteries was found in 66 cases (55%, 66/120), mainly involving the independent A3 pattern with S1 + 2 arteries (47 cases, 71%, 47/66), of which 45 cases (96%, 45/47) shared a trunk with A1 + 2a; the other 19 cases (29%, 19/66) had trans-segmental arteries (A3x) originating from S1 + 2. Common trunks between S3 and S4 + 5 arteries appeared in 36 cases (30%, 36/120), most often between the independent A3 and the Med.A4 + 5 (20 cases, 56%, 20/36); the remaining 16 cases (44%, 16/36) involved trans-segmental arteries (A3x) derived from S4 + 5. In summary, the independent A3 pattern frequently shares trunks not only with S1 + 2 arteries but also often originates from the Med.A4 + 5 (Fig. 1).

Because the independent A3 pattern predominantly supplies S3 (73%, 88/120), further analysis of the relationship between B3 branching patterns and A3 patterns was conducted (Fig. 3). Among the most common bronchus patterns, B3a; B3b + c (79 cases), only 19 cases (24%, 19/79) matched the arterial pattern A3a; A3b + c. The A3x subtype, which includes trans-subsegmental arteries, was the most frequent (28 cases, 35%, 28/79). A high correlation was observed between the B3a + b; B3c pattern (6 cases) and the A3a + b; A3c pattern (5 cases, 83%, 5/6). No exact match was found between the arterial pattern and the B3a; B3b; B3c bronchus type. In summary, when S3 is perfused by the independent A3 pattern, the B3a; B3b + c bronchus type is mainly supplied by the trans-subsegmental artery subtype, while the B3a + b; B3c pattern shows strong arterio-bronchial concordance, providing an anatomical reference for understanding vessel-bronchus relationships during surgery.

In addition to arterial patterns, S3 also shows extensive common venous drainage with adjacent segments (Fig. 4). A common venous trunk between S3 and S1 + 2 was seen in 84 cases (70%, 84/120). Among these, V3c draining into V1 + 2 was most common (63 cases, 75%, 63/84), mainly ending in V1 + 2 or V1 + 2a. V3a1 [16] most often drained into V1 + 2b + c, V1 + 2a, or V1 + 2b (48 cases, 57%, 48/84). A common trunk between S3 and S4 + 5 veins occurred in 37 cases (31%, 37/120), with V3b, V3a2, and V1 + 2d draining into the lingular vein in 16 cases (43%, 16/37), and branches of V4 draining into V3b, V3a2, or V1 + 2d in 21 cases (57%, 21/37).

Discussion

Due to the rare fissural separation between the lingular and upper division segments in the LUL, S3 is located between S1 + 2 and S4 + 5 and is closely connected anatomically. Additionally, the independent origin of B1 + 2c from the upper division bronchus and the extension of S4a into the axillary region, because of hypoplastic B3a, lead to a deep central position of S3a within the lobe, making intersegmental interface dissection more challenging [15, 17].

In 1943, Brock first classified B3 into lateral and anterior branches, with the anterior branch further divided into superior and inferior subdivisions [18]. This study categorizes B3 into three types, with the B3a; B3b + c pattern being predominant (86%). Based on its course, this pattern aligns with the mainstream B3 branching described by Brock and is consistent with the findings of Boyden and Shiozawa, among others [9, 18, 19]. However, the extent of S3a competes with B1 + 2c and B4a in the axillary region. When B1 + 2c extends anteroinferiorly or B4a extends cephalad, S3a correspondingly contracts, and vice versa, resulting in considerable variability in its distribution. This study found that when B1 + 2c originates independently from the upper division bronchus, its distribution expands toward the S3a territory, thereby restricting S3a to the central region of the LUL. In contrast, in cases with a semi-central vein type or a two-branch pattern of the LUL bronchus, S3a may extend to the oblique fissure surface. During resection of S3 in the B3a; B3b + c pattern, the deep location of B3a—influenced by B1 + 2c or B4a—makes surgical exposure challenging, potentially leading to resection of only S3b + c while omitting S3a, which may result in residual lesions or inadequate margins.

Extensive common arterial trunks are observed between the S3 and the adjacent segments [9, 15, 20]. Common trunks between S3 and S1 + 2 arteries were frequent (55%), predominantly involving the independent A3 pattern sharing a trunk with A1 + 2a. Common trunks between S4 + 5 and S3 arteries account for 30% of cases, most commonly between the independent A3 and the Med.A4 + 5. Therefore, during S3 resection, care should be taken to avoid injury to the S1 + 2 artery and the Med.A4 + 5.

Anatomical studies of S3 and adjacent segments revealed statistically significant differences in the prevalence of subsegmental variations: in this series, trans-segmental arteries originating from S1 + 2 and supplying S3 were observed in 19 cases (16%, 19/120), which is higher than that reported by Makiko et al. [21]. In contrast, common trunks between the S3 artery and the Med.A4 + 5 occurred in 25 cases (21%, 25/120), which is lower than the 29% and 25% reported by Dejima et al. [20, 22]. These discrepancies may be related to two factors: the statistical prevalence of the three-branch pattern of the LUL bronchus, and the ongoing controversy in bronchial nomenclature near the B4 + 5 orifice [9, 20].

In a 1955 study on anatomical variations of the S3, Boyden reported that 33% of anterior segmental bronchi (B3) could be abnormally located—originating from B1 + 2a or B4, or independently from B1 + 2 and B4 + 5 (that is, the three-branch pattern of the LUL bronchus) [9]. In the current study, the three-branch pattern was found in only 0.7% of cases. In this variation, the distribution of S3 closely resembled that of S4b in the two-branch pattern, and the Med.A4 + 5 was often mistaken for A3, leading to misidentification of the intersegmental interface.

The B3a; B3b + c pattern, as the most common bronchus pattern of S3, makes combined subsegmental resection of S3b + c a frequently performed procedure. However, it should be noted that its matched arterial pattern, A3a; A3b + c, accounts for only 24% of cases, while the subtype containing trans-subsegmental arteries (A3x) is the most prevalent (35%). During resection of S3b + c, if A3a shares a common trunk with A3ci, intraoperative oversight of A3ci may lead to a reduced resection range and inadequate margins. Furthermore, trans-segmental arteries also exist between S3 and S1 + 2 or S4 + 5. Therefore, although S3b + c resection is a common combined subsegmentectomy, the frequent presence of both trans-subsegmental and trans-segmental arteries renders it anatomically complex despite its prevalence.

S3 exhibits extensive variations in venous drainage to S1 + 2 and S4 + 5, with incidences of 70% and 31%, respectively, consistent with previous studies [9, 15, 20, 23]. Among these, drainage from S3 to S1 + 2 veins primarily involves V3a1 and V3c. Variations in drainage between S3 and S4 + 5 are mainly caused by common trunks formed between branches of V4 and V3b, V3a2, or V1 + 2d. The intersegmental vein V3b and the multi-intersegmental vein V1 + 2d are crucial for preserving venous drainage in adjacent segments after segmentectomy. They should also be carefully preserved during resection of S1 + 2 or S4 + 5 in the LUL to prevent postoperative hemoptysis and pulmonary congestion.

Conclusions

S3 shows a high occurrence of trans-subsegmental arteries, significant variability in S3a boundaries, and extensive arterial and venous common trunks with adjacent segments, which significantly increases surgical difficulty. Due to significant anatomical variations, preoperative 3D reconstruction is essential for precise segmentectomy planning.

Limitations

First, this single-center retrospective study carries inherent selection biases, requiring validation in larger multicenter datasets. Second, the 120-case sample size might overlook extremely rare broncho-vascular variations. Finally, the profound anatomical complexity of S3 (including trans-subsegmental/trans-segmental arteries and variable interfaces) complicates 3D-CTBA interpretation, which may introduce subjectivity into segmental boundary identification.
