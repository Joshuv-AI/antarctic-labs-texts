Pyrazine-based fingerprinting for geographical origin discrimination of coffee: integrating chemometrics and machine learning
Authors: Minmin Lian, Wanyi Zheng, Qianqian Zhang, Hui Xu, Siyu Hou, Xuanrui Chen, Qihe Chen, Jicheng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To improve the accuracy of coffee origin authentication, we developed a pyrazine-targeted fingerprinting approach that synergistically integrates high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), chemometrics, and machine learning for origin discrimination. After validating the HPLC fingerprinting method, we profiled 180 batches, identifying nine shared pyrazine peaks. Based on the proposed fingerprint, chemometric analyses partitioned the 180 batches into five clusters and identified five discriminant pyrazine components. Five machine-learning models were developed; the deep neural network (DNN) performed best, achieving 91.7% accuracy on an internal hold-out validation set, 88.33% mean accuracy in 5-fold cross-validation, and 85.0% accuracy on an independent external test set from a different harvest batch (n = 20). Overall, an interpretable pyrazine fingerprint coupled with modern analytics enables efficient origin discrimination and supports authentication and supply-chain oversight.

Introduction

Coffee, a member of the Rubiaceae family, exhibits antioxidant and anti-hyperlipidemic activities (Godavarthy et al., 2020; Gowd et al., 2019; Iglesias-Aguirre et al., 2021) and is associated with reduced risks of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease (Kusumah & Gonzalez De Mejia, 2022; Seow et al., 2021). As an economically important crop and beverage worldwide (Saud & Salamatullah, 2021), coffee has a flavor quality that is highly dependent on its geographical origin (Cai et al., 2022), with region-specific growing conditions shaping distinct sensory and chemical profiles (Toci et al., 2020). Consequently, the authentication of coffee origin has long been a critical issue for quality control and brand protection. As early as the 1950s, paper chromatography was already applied to distinguish pure coffee from coffee adulterated with chicory (Martins et al., 2018), and subsequent technological advances have enabled the application of chromatographic, spectroscopic, and DNA-based methods for coffee identification and origin tracing. Representative studies are summarized in Table S1. However, many existing origin authentication methods are constrained by high costs, high-dimensional data redundancy, limited interpretability, and poor suitability for rapid routine analysis.

In recent years, the integration of fingerprint profiling with multi-component quantification has been extensively applied in quality assessment (Tistaert et al., 2011; Zhai et al., 2018). Fingerprint analysis can reveal component complexity and interrelationships (Long et al., 2023), while chemometrics enables efficient handling and interpretation of complex chemical data, resolves overlapping responses in multicomponent systems, and improves robustness against interference (Behpour et al., 2015; Heydari et al., 2019; Khoobi et al., 2013), thereby enhancing the reliability of food authentication models. For instance, recent studies (Zouari Ayadi et al., 2025) on coffee derivatives have shown that integrating comprehensive chemical profiling with chemometrics is an effective approach for revealing the complexity of the components and extracting meaningful functional information. Meanwhile, deep learning, an effective machine learning approach, has attracted extensive research attention (LeCun et al., 2015). Owing to the complexity and diversity of food constituents, deep learning has been widely applied in food classification and ingredient identification (Tseng et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2019). For instance, a study has shown (Vezzulli et al., 2021) that the physicochemical parameters of roasted coffee beans can discriminate samples from different origins. Therefore, deep learning is often employed as a predictive tool to enhance classification performance.

Against this methodological background, the selection of appropriate analytical techniques is critical for balancing chemical interpretability, analytical efficiency, and model robustness. Although comprehensive volatile analysis by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) is widely applied in coffee research, such analyses are costly and often contain substantial redundancy, limiting their suitability for rapid applications. In contrast, HPLC provides a rapid and reliable approach for quality assessment, with high sensitivity, efficiency, and accuracy (Liu et al., 2024), while reducing sample-preparation requirements and avoiding high-temperature vaporization steps. Therefore, HPLC was employed in this study as a fingerprinting platform.

However, the effectiveness of any fingerprinting strategy ultimately depends on the selection of chemically meaningful marker components. Although over 1000 volatile compounds have been identified in coffee (Zakidou et al., 2021), not all constituents contribute equally to origin differentiation. Pyrazines are widely recognized as key contributors to coffee's characteristic aroma and flavor (Angeloni et al., 2021), and have potential application value in the pharmaceutical industry (Ali et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2021). Pyrazines are a class of nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds (Wang et al., 2024) that contribute characteristic roasted, nutty, and caramel-like notes and play a central role in shaping the overall aroma profile of coffee. As representative Maillard reaction products in roasted coffee, their formation is governed by precursor composition, roasting conditions, and origin-related agricultural factors (Poisson et al., 2009). Compared with non-targeted volatile profiling, a pyrazine-focused strategy provides a chemically meaningful and mechanistically interpretable representation of coffee characteristics, while reducing interference. However, pyrazines have rarely been used as the primary chemical basis for origin authentication, as most studies rely on global volatile fingerprints or non-volatile markers. Accordingly, the present study aims to fill this gap by developing a pyrazine-centered fingerprinting approach for coffee origin authentication. Although pyrazines are commonly analyzed by gas chromatography, validated HPLC methods can provide stable and reproducible quantitative fingerprints suitable for routine origin identification.

Herein, this study proposes an approach that combines pyrazine-based HPLC fingerprinting with machine learning for coffee origin authentication. Unlike comprehensive GC–MS-based volatile profiling, this approach targets a limited set of pyrazine compounds that serve as characteristic markers relevant to origin under practical roasting variability. Chemometric and machine learning analyses were applied to relate pyrazine fingerprint patterns to origin categories, aiming to achieve rapid and accurate classification using a minimal yet chemically meaningful feature set.

Materials and methods

Materials

All standards, including Pyrazine (Pyr), 2-methylpyrazine (2-MP), 2,3-dimethylpyrazine (2,3-DMP), 2,5-dimethylpyrazine (2,5-DMP), 2,6-dimethylpyrazine (2,6-DMP), 2,3,5-trimethylpyrazine (2,3,5-TriMP), tetramethylpyrazine (TMP), 2-ethylpyrazine (2-EP), and 2-ethyl-3-methylpyrazine (EP-3-MP), with purities ≥99%, were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd. HPLC-grade acetonitrile and methanol were procured from Merck KGaA, Germany. The formic acid and anhydrous ethanol used in this study were also procured from Sinopharm Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd. Trifluoroacetic acid was procured from Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co., Ltd. All other chemicals were of analytical grade.

Coffee samples

The coffee samples were collected from five origins worldwide. Of 200 batches, 180 were used for chemometrics/model development and 20 independent batches were reserved for external validation. Specifically, 36 samples were obtained from each origin: Yunnan (China), Brazil, Ethiopia, Colombia, and Indonesia. Preparation of alkylpyrazines (APZ) standard solution and sample solutions

Standard solutions: Nine standard substances were weighed accurately and dissolved in appropriate solvents to obtain single standard solutions. All nine standard substances were soluble in water. A pyrazine mixed standard stock solution at 1000 mg/L was accurately prepared and diluted in sequential steps to yield standard working solutions at concentrations of 1, 10, 50, 100, 200, and 250 mg/L. These solutions were then refrigerated until use.

Sample extraction: Roasted coffee beans were ground using a coffee grinder at setting 5. Ground coffee (2.000 ± 0.005 g) was transferred to a 100 mL conical flask and mixed with 20.0 mL of 70% (v/v) ethanol. The mixture was sonicated for 60 min at room temperature using an ultrasonic bath (KQ-400KBD, 40 kHz). After extraction, the mixture was centrifuged at 4000 rpm at 20 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and pre-filtered through filter paper under vacuum, then 1.0 mL of extract was diluted to 4.0 mL, mixed thoroughly, and filtered through a 0.22 μm nylon membrane into an HPLC vial for analysis.

All samples were prepared by three independent extractions, and results were expressed as mean ± SD.

HPLC analysis of alkylpyrazines

HPLC analysis was performed using a Waters e2698 HPLC system (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). The Empower chromatography workstation software was used for system control and data analysis. The chromatographic column used was a SunFire C18 column (100 Å, 5 μm, 4.6 mm × 250 mm), with a column temperature of 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% trifluoroacetic acid +0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile), with a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The total run time for each sample was 30 min, and the gradient elution program was as follows: 0–13 min, 92% A/8% B; 13–14 min, linear change to 85% A/15% B; 14–22 min, linear return to 92% A/8% B; and 22–30 min, 92% A/8% B for re-equilibration. Chromatograms were recorded at 278 nm, with an injection volume of 10 μL.

Machine learning environment and training parameters

The machine-learning analysis used data from 180 coffee samples. The dataset was split into a training set (80%, n = 144) and an internal validation set (20%, n = 36). To prevent information leakage, parallel replicates from the same coffee sample were kept in the same group. The external test used 20 independent samples, which were separately collected and prepared.

Models were implemented in TensorFlow to construct a coffee origin classification model. The DNN model was trained for 400 epochs, with a learning rate set to 0.001 and a batch size set to 32. During data preprocessing, StandardScaler was fitted on the training set only and applied to validation and external test.

Model performance metrics

Accuracy

Accuracy is the ratio of the number of correctly predicted samples (including both positive and negative samples) to the total number of samples. As shown in Eq. (1), the higher the accuracy, the better the classification performance (Yang et al., 2022).(1)Accuracy=TP+TNTP+TN+FP+FNHere, True Positive (TP) and True Negative (TN) represent the numbers of correctly detected positive and negative samples, respectively, while False Positive (FP) and False Negative (FN) represent the numbers of incorrectly detected positive and negative samples, respectively.

Precision

Precision measures the proportion of correctly predicted positive samples among all samples predicted as positive. As shown in Eq. (2), higher precision indicates fewer false positives.(2)Precision=TPTP+FP

Recall

Recall is the ratio of positive samples correctly identified by the model to the total number of actual positive samples. As shown in Eq. (3), this ratio reflects the model's ability to identify positive samples.(3)Recall=TPTP+FN

F1 score

The F1 score combines precision and recall and is a comprehensive metric for evaluating the algorithm performance (Liu et al., 2021). As shown in Eq. (4), F1 increases only when both precision and recall are high.(4)F1=2·Precision·RecallPrecision+Recall

Confusion matrix and ROC curve

A confusion matrix compares the model's predicted results with the true labels (Kim et al., 2023) to show the classification results across different categories. The horizontal axis represents the predicted labels, while the vertical axis represents the true labels.

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve evaluates the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity across decision thresholds. As shown in Eq. (5) and Eq. (6), the x-axis is the false positive rate (FPR) and the y-axis is the true positive rate (TPR). The closer the curve is to the upper left corner, the better the model performance (Chen et al., 2024).(5)FPR=FPFP+TN(6)TPR=TPTP+FN

Statistical analysis

All measurements were performed in triplicate, and results were expressed as mean ± SD. Similarity analysis was performed using the Similarity Evaluation System for Chromatographic Fingerprint of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Version 2012 A). Hierarchical agglomerative cluster analysis (HAC) was performed using the Metware platform, while principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were performed using SIMCA 14.4 software. The machine learning section used Anaconda3 2024.06.1 (Python 3.12.4 64-bit) software. Origin (version 2024) was used to plot the processed data.

Results and discussion

Optimization of extraction conditions

The total content of nine pyrazine compounds (including pyrazine, tetramethylpyrazine, 2-methylpyrazine, 2,3,5-trimethylpyrazine, 2,5-dimethylpyrazine, 2,6-dimethylpyrazine, 2,3-dimethylpyrazine, 2-ethylpyrazine, and 2-ethyl-3-methylpyrazine) served as the evaluation criterion to compare three solvents: methanol, ethanol, and acetonitrile. As shown in Fig. 1A, the total pyrazine content did not differ significantly among them. Given comparable concentrations, we prioritized ethanol because it is more cost-effective, less toxic, and safer to handle. As shown in Fig. 1B, further optimization of the ethanol concentration (30%, 50%, 70%, and 90%) showed that 70% ethanol yielded the highest alkylpyrazine content, reaching a value of 9.04 ± 0.18 mg/g. Hence, 70% ethanol was identified as the optimal extraction solvent.Fig. 1Total alkylpyrazine content under different extraction conditions. The extraction conditions were further optimized using 70% ethanol as the solvent by comparing ultrasonic extraction times (15, 30, 60, and 90 min) and solid-to-liquid ratios (1:5, 1:10, 1:30, and 1:50). The experimental results are shown in Fig. 1. The total content of alkylpyrazine reached its maximum value of 12.66 ± 0.22 mg/g at an ultrasound time of 60 min. When the solid-to-liquid ratio was 1:10, the total content of alkylpyrazine also reached its maximum value of 8.26 ± 0.37 mg/g. In summary, the optimal extraction conditions were determined as follows: dissolve the coffee powder in 70% ethanol at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:10, followed by ultrasonic extraction for 60 min.

Recovery

To ensure the reliability of the method, standard addition experiments were conducted to evaluate selectivity and accuracy. A batch of coffee samples was spiked with APZ standard solution to prepare samples. The samples were tested using the method described in Section 2.3. Three parallel groups were set to obtain the average recovery, and RSD values of APZ. The results are shown in Table 3. The recovery rates ranged from 93.32% to 101.55%, the RSD values were all below 1.5%, indicating acceptable accuracy and precision for routine analysis.

We analyzed the mixed standard under the same chromatographic conditions (Fig. 2B). Nine of the chromatographic peaks were identified by comparing them with the corresponding reference standards. These peaks were pyrazine (peak 1), tetramethylpyrazine (peak 2), 2-methylpyrazine (peak 3), 2,3,5-trimethylpyrazine (peak 4), 2,3-dimethylpyrazine (peak 5), 2,6-dimethylpyrazine (peak 6), 2,5-dimethylpyrazine (peak 7), 2-ethylpyrazine (peak 8), and 2-ethyl-3-methylpyrazine (peak 9). To further support these results and address potential selectivity concerns, an analysis of the coffee samples was performed using GC–MS (Fig. 2C). The corresponding pyrazine compounds were detected in the GC–MS chromatograms, and the mass spectra of the detected peaks were matched against the NIST mass spectral library (similarity >85%). This provides additional evidence for the presence of these nine alkylpyrazines and supports the HPLC-based peak assignments.

Similarity evaluation results of fingerprint chromatograms of 180 coffee batches

Similarity values for each coffee batch were calculated using the Similarity Evaluation System for Chromatographic Fingerprint of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Version 2012 A) to assess the overall consistency among the 180 coffee batches. As shown in Fig. 2D, the similarity between each batch and the reference chromatogram ranged from 0.897 to 0.999. Similarity values for the five origins were generally concentrated at a high level, with only a small number of batches forming a low-value tail (around 0.90), indicating that the method is overall stable and shows good within-origin consistency. Although minor batch-to-batch variation may exist for a few samples, the fingerprints were largely consistent across the dataset.

Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis

To further analyze differences among coffee samples from various origins, an OPLS-DA classification model was established using SIMCA-P 14.1 software. This model used the peak areas of nine common peaks as the X matrix and origin class membership as the Y response. Prior to modeling, variables were mean-centered and Pareto-scaled. As shown in the OPLS-DA score plot (Fig. 3C), samples were separated into five origin-related groups along the predictive component, although some groups remained relatively close, consistent with partial chemical similarity in their pyrazine fingerprints. In this analysis, the model's independent variable fit index (R2X) was 0.89; the dependent variable fit index (R2Y) was 0.842; and the model predictive ability index (Q2) was 0.834. Both R2Y and Q2 exceeded 0.500, indicating good fit and predictive ability. As shown in Fig. 3C2, after 200 permutation tests, the intersection point of the Q2 regression line with the vertical axis was less than zero, suggesting no obvious overfitting and supporting model validity. Potential outliers were assessed not only by the score-plot ellipse but also by Hotelling's T2 and DModX (Q-residuals) diagnostics (Fig. S3); a few observations marginally exceeded the 95% limits, while no strong outliers were detected, and therefore no samples were excluded after checking chromatographic quality and replicate consistency.

The variable importance in projection (VIP) results of the 9 feature variables obtained through OPLS-DA are shown in Fig. 3C1. Five discriminant markers were identified using VIP > 1 as a measure of significant influence. These chemical components significantly contributed to distinguishing coffee from different regions. These variables likely reflect region-associated differences. Their influence was ranked from highest to lowest as follows: pyrazine >2-ethylpyrazine > tetramethylpyrazine >2,6-dimethylpyrazine >2,5-dimethylpyrazine.

For consistency with the model outputs, the five origins were encoded as class 0–4, corresponding to Yunnan (0), Brazil (1), Colombia (2), Ethiopia (3), and Indonesia (4).

Overall, the DT model's performance was relatively poor. Although it performed relatively well on class 4 (and partially on class 3), its classification of classes 0–2 remains suboptimal and requires further optimization.

Random forest (RF) model

As shown in Fig. 4B, the RF model achieved strong discrimination for classes 0, 2, and 4 (AUC = 0.90, 0.94, and 0.91, respectively), while classes 1 and 3 were comparatively weaker (AUC = 0.68 and 0.69). Consistent with this, the confusion matrix revealed pronounced confusion between classes 1 and 3, indicating insufficient class separability in this pair. In summary, RF performed robustly for classes 0/2/4 but its performance was limited by misclassification between classes 1 and 3.

Support vector machine (SVM) model

As shown in Fig. 4C, the SVM model performs best for class 2 (AUC = 0.94) and also shows good discrimination for classes 0 and 4 (AUC = 0.90 and 0.91). In contrast, class 1 is poorly classified (AUC = 0.68). Further analysis of the confusion matrix indicates that class 2 is generally predicted correctly, although two samples are misclassified as class 0.

In summary, the SVM model performs well for classes 0/2/4, but poorly for class 1, with persistent confusion between classes 1 and 3. This suggests that, while the model is partially effective, it is limited by overlapping feature patterns among specific classes.

K-nearest neighbor (KNN) model

Fig. 4D shows that the model had superior classification performance for class 0, with few misclassifications and an AUC value of 0.93. The model's classification performance was the weakest for class 3, with an AUC value of 0.79. Further analysis of the confusion matrix showed that the model performed well in classes 0 and 4. However, the model misclassified one sample in class 0, two in class 1, two in class 2, three in class 3, and one in class 4. Most errors were associated with class 3 being misassigned to neighboring classes (1 and 2).

Overall, the KNN model performed well across most classes but showed frequent misclassifications for class 3. This suggests KNN is generally suitable for this dataset, although limited separability of class 3 remains a key constraint.

Deep neural network model

As shown in Fig. 4E, DNN exhibited consistently high discrimination across all classes (AUC = 0.97–0.98). The confusion matrix further confirmed its strong performance, with perfect classification for class 2 and 3 and only a few errors concentrated in limited class pairs (e.g., class 0 occasionally predicted as class 2). Overall, DNN delivered the most robust and stable multiclass performance among the evaluated models.

As the number of training epochs increased from 300 to 400 (Fig. 5), the model's overall test accuracy improved from 86.1% (Fig. 5A) to 91.7% (Fig. 5C), and the learning curves indicated stable training and gradual convergence. A modest train–validation gap was observed at later epochs, but the validation loss did not rebound, suggesting only mild generalization discrepancy and overall good generalizability. The confusion matrices further showed that the performance gain mainly resulted from fewer misclassified samples, implying partial feature overlap among some classes. Cross-validation and external validation

To systematically evaluate the model's generalization ability, we performed stratified 5-fold cross-validation on the expanded dataset (n = 180). The fold-wise accuracies were 88.89%, 94.44%, 88.89%, 80.56%, and 88.89%, yielding a mean accuracy of 88.33% (Fig. 6A1), which indicates relatively stable performance across different data splits. The multi-class ROC results further show that the macro-average AUC values for each class range from 0.94 to 0.99 (Fig. 6A2), suggesting good discriminative capability. In addition, to assess out-of-sample performance, we introduced an independent external test set (n = 20; 4 samples per class) that was not used during training or model selection. The model achieved an accuracy of 85.00% on the external test. The confusion matrix indicates that overall recognition remains good on external data, while a small number of misclassifications persists, mainly between class 1 and class 3, and between class 4 and class 0 (Fig. 6B1). Consistently, the external multi-class ROC curves (one-vs-rest) in Fig. 6B2 lie well above the diagonal, with high AUC values, further supporting robust discrimination on unseen samples. Taken together, the cross-validation and external-validation results demonstrate that the proposed method maintains strong generalization under a more stringent validation framework; however, the performance is not “perfect”, suggesting that intrinsic chemical similarity may exist between certain classes and that further validation and optimization using larger, more diverse multi-source datasets are warranted.

Conclusion

This study used HPLC plus chemometric and machine-learning methods to profile pyrazine fingerprints and identify coffee origins. The fingerprint analysis method demonstrated high reliability and stability. The fingerprint similarity results among the 180 coffee samples also indicated that coffees from different regions share overall similar chemical compositions, albeit with certain differences. Based on nine shared peaks in the pyrazine fingerprint, PCA and optimized OPLS-DA revealed origin-related clustering patterns among the 180 coffee batches and identified five discriminant components. However, partial overlap among some origins suggested intrinsic chemical similarity and limited separability in low-dimensional projections. To enhance discrimination, we further evaluated multiple supervised classifiers (DT, RF, SVM, KNN, and DNN). The optimized DNN provided the best overall predictive performance, achieving 91.7% accuracy on the internal hold-out test set, 88.33% mean accuracy in stratified 5-fold cross-validation, and 85.0% accuracy on an independent external test set (n = 20). These results demonstrate strong but not perfect generalization, consistent with the observed chemical overlap between certain origins.

Overall, this integrated approach offers practical support for coffee quality standardization and origin authentication. However, although our sampling incorporated heterogeneity in processing methods, regions, and roasting degrees, these variables have not been fully controlled. Therefore, the observed differences may be influenced by both the origin and roasting factors simultaneously. Moreover, the current dataset does not yet fully represent broader seasonal, annual variability, wider global origin diversity, or complex supply-chain conditions. Future work should therefore expand to larger multi-source datasets across harvest seasons, include greater commercial heterogeneity, and integrate sensory assessment to clarify the mechanisms underlying origin-dependent pyrazine signatures and to improve reproducibility in practical settings.
