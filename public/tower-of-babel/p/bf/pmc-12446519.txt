Letter Re: ‘Development of a risk score based on clinical–pathological features to predict the presence of germline BRCA1/2 pathogenic variants in ovarian cancer patients’
Authors: S Liu, X Wu
Published: 2025

We read with great interest the article by Innella et al.,1 ‘Development of a risk score based on clinical–pathological features to predict the presence of germline BRCA1/2 pathogenic variants in ovarian cancer patients’. In an era often characterized by a research ‘arms race’ where algorithmic complexity can overshadow clinical value, the authors’ effort to develop a pragmatic, interpretable tool is a commendable step in the right direction. However, we would like to offer supplementary commentary on the methodological transition from their exploratory analysis to the final risk score.

The authors’ multistep approach is laudable, yet the simplification of their model into an additive score led to the omission of a key predictor and a loss of predictive granularity. Specifically, the decision to exclude ‘International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage’ is inconsistent with two sources of evidence: firstly, the decision tree (Figure 1), which identified FIGO stage as a powerful predictor within the subgroup of patients with a family history of ovarian cancer; and secondly, their foundational 2023 meta-analysis, which confirmed advanced FIGO stage as a statistically significant predictor across pooled data.2 By removing ‘FIGO stage’ despite this dual confirmation, the final score unfortunately discards crucial predictive information, raising concerns about its validity and applicability in broader clinical settings.

Furthermore, the method used to assign integer weights, while aiming for simplicity, masks the true magnitude of risk conferred by different predictors. For instance, ‘family history of ovarian cancer’ [relative odds ratio (OR) 5.543] and ‘low-grade serous/high-grade endometrioid histotype’ (relative OR 9.728) are both assigned a weight of 2 points, despite the latter being associated with a nearly twofold higher risk. This coarse categorization, based on subjectively chosen cut-offs for relative ORs, sacrifices the model’s fidelity to the underlying data. It treats predictors with substantially different effects as equivalent, potentially leading to mis-stratification of patient risk.

The journey from a complex dataset to a simple clinical tool requires more than avoiding a ‘black box’ model; it demands that the final tool be a robust and accurate distillation of the initial findings. A more robust approach might have been to develop a nomogram from the logistic regression model’s coefficients, or even a decision flowchart based on the superior, interaction-aware structure of the decision tree. Such tools can preserve predictive accuracy while remaining clinically manageable.

Indeed, future research could build upon the authors’ excellent exploratory work. Instead of reverting to a simplified additive model, machine learning approaches like random forests or gradient boosting machines—which are inherently designed to capture complex, non-linear interactions—could yield a more accurate and individualized risk prediction.

We thank the authors for their important work. Acknowledging and retaining the complex relationships discovered in the data will be a vital step in refining this promising tool and ensuring it is not only simple to use but also robust enough to genuinely aid clinical decision making at the bedside.
