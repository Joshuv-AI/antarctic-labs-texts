Progress and Challenges in Aqueous Batteries: Exploring Polymer Electrolytes for Extreme Temperature Resilience
Authors: Yanghyun Cho, Jae Eun Kim, Minju Song, Jongha Hwang, Eunsung Hwang, Jeonghun Kim, Woo‐Jin Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe demand for energy storage systems has been steadily increasing, and aqueous rechargeable metal‐ion batteries (ARMBs) have emerged as promising next‐generation technology due to their superior safety, environmental friendliness, and economic feasibility. Despite these advantages, the inherent limitations of aqueous electrolytes, including parasitic gas evolution arising from the narrow electrochemical stability window, dynamic pH fluctuations, temperature‐dependent ionic conductivity, and dendrite growth, remain major obstacles to practical implementation. Among these, thermal instability is a fundamental technological hurdle to commercialization. This review examines the degradation mechanisms of ARMBs under extreme temperatures, outlines polymer electrolyte design strategies for stable operation, and proposes future research directions to enable their application in extreme environments and grid‐scale systems. Overall, improving thermal stability is expected to accelerate the adoption of ARMBs.

Introduction

Over recent decades, the accelerated consumption of fossil fuels (e.g., coal, petroleum, and natural gas) has been a dominant driver of anthropogenic climate change. At the same time, the global rise in industrial activity and energy demand necessitates the rapid development of scalable, environmentally sustainable energy storage technologies to accommodate the intermittent nature of renewable energy sources.[ 1 ] In this context, electrochemical energy storage systems (EESSs) provide a distinct advantage by enabling stable, rapid, and controllable power delivery independent of generation variability. For more than 30 years, lithium‐ion batteries (LIBs) have dominated the EESS landscape, particularly in grid storage systems and electric vehicles (EVs), owing to their high energy density, excellent coulombic efficiency (CE), and negligible memory effect.[ 2 ] However, the large‐scale deployment of LIBs for stationary applications is constrained by intrinsic limitations, including the limited availability of lithium reserves and the flammability of organic electrolytes, which pose significant safety risks.[ 3 ] These challenges highlight the urgent need for next‐generation battery systems that meet the safety, cost, and durability requirements of EESSs.

Among commercially available cost‐effective technologies, lead–acid batteries are the most established, representing a substantial share of the global market. According to a 2022 report by the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL), the projected installation cost of LIBs by 2030 ranges from 230 to 690 USD per kWh, whereas lead–acid batteries remain economically competitive at ≈235 USD per kWh.[ 4 ] However, their relatively low specific energy, limited cycle life, and environmental toxicity significantly undermine their suitability for sustainable EESSs. To address these limitations, aqueous rechargeable metal‐ion batteries (ARMBs) have gained increasing attention as next‐generation candidates, primarily due to their aqueous electrolytes and operational mechanisms analogous to those of alkali‐ion systems (e.g., LIBs).[ 5 ] Metal ion aqueous battery systems (e.g., Mg2+, Zn2+, K+, Na+) have been extensively investigated, offering attractive theoretical capacities through multi‐electron redox reactions[ 6 ] while benefiting from the natural abundance of multivalent metals.[ 7 , 8 ] In addition, aqueous electrolytes possess high dielectric constants, low viscosity, and substantially higher ionic conductivity compared to organic counterparts, thereby enhancing both power density and operational stability.[ 9 ] Furthermore, the absence of flammability, toxicity, and severe environmental hazards improves resistance to thermal runaway. Nevertheless, practical deployment remains constrained by fundamental interfacial instabilities, particularly at the electrode/electrolyte interface (Figure 1 ).[ 10 , 11 , 12 ]

Schematic illustration of key degradation mechanisms in ARMBs.

Among the various challenges, thermal instability remains a major barrier to the practical deployment of ARMBs.[ 13 , 14 , 15 ] ESSs are frequently subjected to temperature fluctuations caused by climatic variations or internal heat generation. Conventional aqueous electrolytes exhibit poor thermal stability, with crystallization typically occurring at low temperatures (LT) due to intensified hydrogen bonding and restricted molecular mobility, while at high temperatures (HT), solvent volatility increases through bond dissociation. Considering potential applications of ARMBs in thermally extreme environments, such as glacial regions, aerospace systems, and military equipment, further advances in energy storage materials and system integration are essential.[ 16 , 17 ] In aerospace systems, a reliable energy supply under cryogenic conditions is crucial to maintain the continuous operation of sensors and data acquisition devices, whereas in HT conditions, such as the petroleum industry, tropical climates, and subsurface exploration, thermal durability is vital for stable battery performance.[ 18 ] Consequently, the thermal adaptability of ARMBs has emerged as a key performance parameter attracting increasing research attention. Addressing this issue requires improvements in the physicochemical stability of the electrolyte, which strongly influences energy density, safety, and lifespan. Current innovations in electrolyte formulation and electrode/electrolyte interfacial engineering are expected to enable ARMBs with reliable performance across diverse thermal conditions.

This review extends beyond previous ARMB studies, which largely focused on specific temperature conditions or isolated material systems.[ 19 , 20 ] It provides a comprehensive mechanistic analysis of electrode/electrolyte interfacial behavior under extreme thermal conditions and long‐term degradation environments. Rather than relying on simple comparisons of results, the review emphasizes major degradation pathways, including phase transitions and metal dissolution at the cathode, as well as dendrite growth, hydrogen evolution, and corrosion at the anode. Molecular‐level mechanisms, such as hydrogen‐bond regulation, ion solvation structures, and interfacial reaction kinetics, are systematically elucidated. Particular attention is given to the design of polymer electrolytes with diverse thermal adaptability strategies, with recent experimental advances explicitly linked to fundamental physicochemical principles. This integrated approach establishes key mechanisms and guidelines to enhance the thermal resilience of ARMBs, offering both academic insights and actionable directions for future research and commercialization.

Extreme Temperature Range Performance of Aqueous Batteries: Key Considerations and Challenges

Stabilizing electrolytes is critical to ensuring the reliable performance of EESSs, as they are inherently unstable under extreme environmental temperatures.[ 21 , 22 , 23 ] At LT, increased viscosity and electrolyte solidification can damage the separator, induce structural collapse, and compromise internal stability.[ 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 ] These effects also intensify concentration polarization, severely hindering ion transport during charge/discharge processes and accelerating parasitic reactions. At HT, rapid solvent evaporation causes compositional imbalances that shift pH and undermine interfacial stability.[ 28 , 29 ] Collectively, the electrolyte represents the most fundamental factor governing ARMB performance under extreme temperature conditions.[ 30 , 31 , 32 ] Without electrolyte stabilization, improvements limited to the cathode or anode cannot provide a comprehensive solution. Addressing this challenge requires an in‐depth understanding of the temperature‐dependent behavior of electrolytes, including their thermodynamic and kinetic properties, ion transport dynamics, and electrode/electrolyte interfacial stability. Sections 2.1 and 2.2 present a focused review of these mechanisms, introducing key strategies based on electrolyte design and electrode/electrolyte interactions (Figure 2 ). These insights aim to broaden the viable operating temperature range of ARMBs and enhance their practical applicability.

Comprehensive Mechanisms for Extending the Temperature Range of Aqueous Batteries

Temperature plays a critical role in modifying the physical properties of the electrolyte and the reaction environment at the electrode interface in ARMBs, thereby strongly influencing operational efficiency and stability. The operating voltage, which is directly related to energy density and overall electrochemical performance, is highly sensitive to temperature variations due to fundamental thermodynamic relationships that govern electrode potential and redox equilibrium. This dependence is described by the Nernst equation.[ 33 ] (1) E=E∘−RTnFInQ where E is the electrode potential, E° is the standard electrode potential, R is the gas constant, T is the temperature, n is the number of electrons transferred in the redox process, F is the Faraday constant, and Q is the reaction quotient representing the activity ratio of products to reactants. As temperature decreases, the thermal contribution to the potential (RT/nF) diminishes, leading to a smaller deviation from the standard potential. Consequently, the cell voltage is suppressed under operational conditions, reducing energy output at LT. Furthermore, the intrinsic temperature dependence of the standard cell voltage is captured by the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation.[ 34 ] (2)

Here, ΔrH° and ΔrS° represent the standard enthalpy and entropy changes of the redox reaction, respectively. The term T·ΔrS° increases with rising temperature and, when ΔrS° is positive, reduces the standard cell potential. This reduction at HT narrows the ESW, thereby increasing the likelihood of parasitic reactions. To systematically assess the impact of temperature, it is necessary to differentiate the specific electrochemical and physical challenges that arise under extreme thermal conditions. Temperature variations not only modify the ion transport properties of electrolytes but also trigger interfacial phenomena and decomposition pathways, ultimately influencing battery performance, lifespan, and safety.

Challenges of the Water Electrolyte at Low Temperature

A critical limitation to the LT functionality of ARMBs arises from the phase transition behavior of aqueous electrolytes.[ 39 ] Below 0 °C, partial solidification can occur, during which ice crystal nucleation excludes solvated metal ions from conductive pathways, leading to a sharp decline in ionic conductivity.[ 40 ] Even without complete crystallization, intensified hydrogen bonding enhances the rigidity of the liquid matrix and suppresses ion diffusivity, thereby reducing the mobility of metal ions. This deterioration in ion transport increases internal resistance, hinders electrochemical kinetics, and ultimately impairs charge transfer efficiency. Phase diagrams plotted along temperature–composition axes are indispensable for systematically analyzing these temperature‐dependent behaviors. Under non‐ideal cooling conditions, electrolyte systems often deviate from equilibrium thermodynamics, requiring non‐equilibrium phase diagrams that capture metastable states such as supercooled liquids and glass transitions.[ 41 , 42 ] These diagrams identify domains where solid/liquid coexistence induces heterogeneity in ion distribution and interfacial transport. For instance, localized ice crystallization may coincide with spatially confined salt precipitation, while other regions of the solution remain metastable in a supercooled state. Such spatial phase inhomogeneity generates localized polarization and instabilities related to interfacial phase separation, thereby degrading overall electrochemical performance. From an electrochemical standpoint, LT not only increases the viscosity of the electrolyte but also raises the energy barrier for the desolvation process, as described by the Stokes–Einstein equation and the dielectric effect, respectively.[ 43 , 44 ] (3) D=kT6πηr (4) q=ZiZje28πεε0kT

In these equations, D is the diffusion coefficient, Z is the viscosity of the surrounding medium, r is the solvation radius, q is the critical separation distance for ion‐pair formation, Z is the ion valency, ε0 is the vacuum permittivity, k is the Boltzmann constant, and T is the temperature. Increased viscosity reduces ion mobility, while the formation of closely associated ion pairs stabilized by solvent molecules further impedes ion transport. These limitations are particularly pronounced in aqueous electrolytes, where water phase transitions and salt precipitation occur. For instance, below ≈4 °C, hydrogen bond formation among water molecules outweighs bond cleavage, producing a semi‐solid ice–water phase mixture.[ 45 ]

The supercooling region within the phase diagram is therefore indispensable for evaluating and engineering the LT tolerance of aqueous electrolytes. Two thermophysical descriptors, the glass transition temperature‐to‐melting point ratio (Tg/Tm) and the minimum crystallization time (τ). The Tg/Tm ratio reflects kinetic resistance to crystallization, where higher values indicate improved stability of the amorphous phase under slow cooling and preservation of ion‐conducting pathways necessary for conductivity. In parallel, τ denotes the time required for nucleation and subsequent crystallization, which generally increases as temperature decreases, signifying prolonged persistence of the liquid state under metastable conditions. Both parameters are inherently temperature dependent, underscoring the critical role of thermal conditions in governing electrolyte phase stability.

Anode Degradation at Low Temperature

Under LT conditions, thermal kinetic energy is significantly diminished, increasing energy barriers along the electrochemical reaction pathway. As a result, reaction kinetics slow markedly, with the rate exhibiting an exponential dependence on temperature. Below a critical point, this thermal limitation severely degrades electrochemical performance as ion transport within the electrolyte becomes restricted, exacerbating voltage polarization and leading to premature attainment of the battery cut‐off voltage, thereby reducing the usable specific capacity. These limitations are particularly severe in systems employing metal anodes. In such cases, the diffusion behavior of metal ions is temperature dependent and governed by the Arrhenius equation. Here, D is the diffusion coefficient, D0 is the pre‐exponential factor, Ea is the activation energy, R is the gas constant, and T is the temperature. At reduced temperatures, the fraction of metal ions with sufficient energy to overcome the activation barrier decreases sharply, hindering ion transfer at the electrode interface and impairing uniform metal deposition. This limitation is especially pronounced in aqueous multivalent ion systems, where strong electrostatic interactions between metal ions and solvent molecules further restrict solid‐state diffusion pathways and substantially reduce ion mobility within the electrode structure. The resulting decrease in diffusion efficiency lowers ionic conductivity in the electrolyte and increases the activation energy required for charge transfer across the electrode/electrolyte interface. This temperature‐dependent decline in ionic conductivity can be quantitatively described by the Arrhenius equation. This reduction limits the ion current and constrains battery output under LT conditions. At further reduced temperatures, the electrolyte viscosity continuously increases, raising the desolvation energy barrier and suppressing charge carrier mobility. As a result, ion transport becomes sluggish and spatially nonuniform, leading to ion accumulation at the electrode interface. At LT, the combined effects of increased electrolyte viscosity and reduced electronic conductivity create a mismatch between charge transport and ion diffusion, thereby intensifying voltage polarization. This imbalance results in prolonged charging times, diminished specific capacity, lower CE, and an increased risk of over(dis)charging.

Cathode Degradation at Low Temperature

At LT, ion diffusion and charge transfer kinetics at the cathode of ARMBs are significantly suppressed, severely restricting electrochemical reactivity. The reduction in diffusion coefficients promotes cation accumulation at the electrode interface, increases voltage hysteresis, and leads to irreversible (dis)charge behavior. Concurrently, delayed desolvation and the thickening of the cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI) further hinder electron transfer and exacerbate polarization. These effects are particularly pronounced in systems containing cations with a large ionic radius, where sluggish interfacial dynamics cause further performance degradation. In addition, structural instability presents a critical challenge. The freezing of lattice or interlayer water can induce mechanical strain, resulting in crack formation or structural collapse. The thermodynamics of such phase transformations are governed by the following equation. (11) ΔG=ΔH−TΔS

At LT, the reduced contribution of the entropy term (TΔS) increases the likelihood of unfavorable phase transitions. Under such thermal conditions, repeated ion insertion and extraction induce mechanical degradation, including particle fracturing and pulverization, which expose pristine cathode surfaces and disrupt or delaminate the preformed CEI. This structural instability triggers a self‐reinforcing feedback loop in which CEI layers continuously reform, consuming energy and elevating interfacial resistance. Collectively, LT conditions compromise the electrochemical stability and long‐term durability of cathodes through the combined effects of diffusion delay, interfacial degradation, and mechanical collapse.

Challenges of the Water Electrolyte at High Temperature

At HT, the electrochemical behavior of aqueous electrolytes becomes increasingly complex. HT enhances ion diffusion and lower activation energy, thereby facilitating charge transfer and enabling high‐rate performance; however, these benefits are counteracted by accelerated water evaporation driven by increased vapor pressure. The resulting disproportionate rise in salt concentration can trigger precipitation and disrupt compositional uniformity, leading to interfacial instability, gas evolution, metal ion dissolution, and intensified parasitic side reactions that collectively degrade cell performance and safety. In addition, the chemical potential of electrode materials shifts according to thermodynamic principles. When the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) or lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels of the electrolyte align with the electrode Fermi level, spontaneous electron exchange may occur, causing oxidative or reductive degradation of the electrolyte.[ 50 ] This process also accelerates the growth or decomposition of the SEI or CEI, thereby increasing interfacial resistance and reducing cycling stability.[ 51 ] In summary, temperature is a primary determinant of electrolyte behavior in ARMBs, governing phase transitions, ion mobility, electrochemical kinetics, and interfacial stability. Accordingly, strict temperature regulation is essential for the design and evaluation of aqueous electrolyte systems.

Cathode Degradation at High Temperature

At HT, ion transport is enhanced, accelerating electrode reaction kinetics. However, this benefit is outweighed by critical drawbacks, including structural compromise of the cathode and heightened safety risks from excessive heat generation. Cathode materials with poor thermal stability are susceptible to structural collapse and byproduct formation, primarily through reactions with electrolyte decomposition intermediates. Elevated thermal energy also increases internal pressure and gas evolution, thereby heightening the risk of thermal runaway. Cathode degradation at HT progresses through interconnected mechanisms, including transition metal dissolution, oxygen release, interfacial decomposition, and morphological destabilization. Among these, transition metal dissolution is markedly accelerated by thermally activated reaction kinetics.[ 61 ] (22) k∝e−EaRT

The loss of redox‐active species directly reduces capacity and compromises the structural stability of the cathode. Dissolved ions can further migrate toward the anode, where they catalyze parasitic reactions. Second, oxygen release from overcharged layered cathodes is thermally activated.[ 62 ] (23) dO2dt=k·ActiveOxide·e−EaRT

This process induces irreversible structural collapse, including phase transitions from layered to spinel or amorphous phases, and initiates oxidative degradation of the electrolyte. Third, interfacial instability arises as the CEI decomposes under prolonged thermal stress. Although CEI formation is promoted at HT, the resulting layer is often porous and chemically unstable, permitting persistent side reactions between the cathode and the electrolyte. Simultaneously, metal hydroxide precipitation is enhanced by temperature‐induced pH fluctuations and hydrolysis.[ 63 ] (24) Ksp=Mn+[OH−]n∝e−ΔHsolRT

The resulting insulating layers on the cathode surface reduce both electronic and ionic conductivity, thereby hindering redox kinetics. Finally, mechanical and morphological degradation occur due to mismatched thermal expansion and internal stress accumulation. Grain boundary separation and microcrack formation permit electrolyte penetration, contributing to irreversible loss of specific capacity. In summary, the electrochemical redox behavior of electrode materials is highly temperature‐dependent, exerting a profound impact on overall battery performance.[ 35 ] Key parameters such as reaction activation barriers, ion diffusion pathways, and Rct, exhibit strong thermal sensitivity. Metal anodes offer high theoretical capacities and excellent conductivity but face severe challenges under extreme conditions, arising from interfacial dynamics with the electrolyte, repeated SEI degradation, and irregular dendritic growth. The storage mechanisms of multivalent metal ions in ARMBs are particularly intricate, rendering them highly sensitive to extreme temperatures.[ 36 , 37 , 38 ] At low temperatures, reduced ionic and electronic conductivities of the electrolyte slow cation insertion/extraction and delay charge transfer. The large solvation radius and strong electrostatic repulsion at cathode interfaces further restrict ion diffusion. In addition, many cathode materials possess intrinsically low electronic conductivity due to their wide band gaps and limited density of states, which exacerbates ion transport limitations. These combined effects lead to pronounced electrode polarization arising from ohmic, electrochemical, and concentration overpotentials. Collectively, ARMBs suffer from hindered ion mobility, restricted electron transport, structural instability, and polarization‐driven degradation under extreme temperatures, ultimately compromising their performance and durability.

The following section systematically examines the functional roles of polymer electrolytes in ARMBs, with particular emphasis on their structural and transport characteristics. These materials, defined by the synergistic integration of mechanical robustness and ionic conductivity, stabilize electrochemical interfaces and suppress morphological instabilities under extreme thermal conditions. Their capacity to inhibit dendrite growth, reinforce interfacial cohesion, and sustain ion transport at both LT and HT supports the operational durability and electrochemical consistency of the system.

Importance of Polymer Electrolyte in the Temperature Range for the Aqueous Batteries

Aqueous Polymer Electrolyte

In their self‐assembled solid and gel forms, aqueous polymer electrolytes uniquely combine robust mechanical strength with liquid‐like ionic conductivity.[ 64 , 65 , 66 ] Gel‐type polymer electrolytes provide high ion transport rates alongside structural resilience, thereby addressing the safety and performance limitations of conventional liquid electrolytes.[ 67 , 68 ] This hybrid functionality enables stable operation across extreme temperatures, mitigating failure modes such as LT freezing, HT evaporation, interfacial instability, and dendrite formation in ARMBs (Figure 3 ).[ 69 , 70 ]

Comparative performance of aqueous electrolytes and aqueous polymer electrolytes in high‐performance ARMBs.

First, water molecules confined within a polymer network experience pronounced nanoconfinement and extensive hydrogen bonding with polar functional groups, which disrupts the formation of critical ice nuclei and inhibits crystal growth.[ 71 , 72 ] Consequently, polymer gel electrolytes remain fluid and ionically conductive well below the nominal freezing point of bulk water, enabling battery operation under extreme LT conditions.[ 73 , 74 ] Second, polymer electrolytes suppress the activity of free water through strong coordination between water molecules and hydrophilic moieties (e.g., ─OH, ─COOH), effectively “locking” them within the polymer matrix.[ 75 ] This reduction in free water lowers the vapor pressure of the solution and increases the overpotentials for HER and OER, thereby extending the electrochemical stability window and suppressing parasitic gas evolution at HT.[ 76 , 77 ] Third, polymer electrolytes improve mechanical integrity and thermal resilience by forming a self‐assembled film or gel. Unlike liquid electrolytes, they eliminate leakage risks while simultaneously acting as physical barriers to dendrite growth.[ 78 ] When properly engineered, these polymer matrices exhibit both high modulus and elongation over extreme temperature ranges, accommodating volumetric fluctuations without compromising dimensional stability or structural coherence.[ 79 , 80 , 81 ] Finally, ion transport in polymer electrolytes is governed by the segmental motion of polymer chains, which facilitates ion hopping between adjacent coordination sites.[ 82 , 83 , 84 ] This transport mechanism exhibits Arrhenius‐type temperature dependence: optimizing polymer–ion interactions reduces the Ea, sustaining high ionic conductivity at LT, while enhanced polymer mobility at HT markedly increases ionic conductivity.[ 85 ] At LT, however, increased viscosity and restricted chain dynamics hinder ion mobility, underscoring the decisive role of the polymer matrix and composition in determining electrochemical performance.[ 86 , 87 ] Collectively, the features of nanoconfinement‐induced freezing suppression, reduced free water activity, mechanical and thermal stability, and optimized Arrhenius‐type ion transport render polymer electrolytes indispensable for extending the operational temperature window of ARMBs.

Non‐Aqueous Polymer Electrolyte

Unlike aqueous polymer electrolytes, which primarily rely on hydrogen‐bonding networks to suppress water activity and stabilize interfacial chemistry, nonaqueous polymer electrolytes operate under fundamentally different coordination principles. In aqueous systems, functional groups such as carbonyl, ether, sulfonate, and amide moieties compete with water molecules in the solvation sheath, partially replacing free water to reduce parasitic reactions and lower metal‐ion desolvation barriers. However, the unavoidable presence of water introduces challenges such as HER, corrosion, and ice crystallization under extreme temperatures.

In contrast, nonaqueous polymer electrolytes, including solid polymer electrolytes, quasi‐solid polymer electrolytes, and polymer‐in‐salt frameworks, provide a nearly water‐free environment where polymer functional groups act as the primary ligands for metal‐ion coordination. Ether oxygens (─C─O─C–), nitriles (─C≡N), carbonyls (C═O), sulfonates (─SO3−), amides (─CONH─), and fluorinated groups (─CF) dominate the solvation shell, directly tuning desolvation energy and transport kinetics without interference from bulk water. This distinction reduces parasitic side reactions and significantly broadens the electrochemical stability window, particularly under high‐voltage and high‐temperature conditions. Furthermore, nonaqueous systems employ dense polymer backbones, crosslinking, and hybrid inorganic fillers to enhance mechanical strength, suppress solvent evaporation, and prevent interfacial delamination during thermal fluctuations. Whereas aqueous hydrogels offer superior ionic conductivity and flexibility at subzero temperatures, they suffer from dehydration and poor dimensional stability at HT. By contrast, nonaqueous polymer electrolytes maintain thermal robustness above 100 °C, exhibit high mechanical modulus, and regulate metal‐ion redox behavior, though at the cost of reduced ionic conductivity at LT due to limited segmental mobility.

Advantages of Polymer Electrolyte on the Anode

Dendrite Suppression of Metal Ions

In ARMBs, metal anodes are highly susceptible to dendrite growth under extreme thermal conditions, where temperature fluctuations intensify metal‐ion flux heterogeneity and accelerate interfacial instability. Polymer electrolytes mitigate these effects by providing both mechanical reinforcement and ion transport pathways.[ 88 , 89 ] At HT, crosslinked polymer matrices preserve structural rigidity and high shear modulus, suppressing dendrite propagation in accordance with the Monroe–Neuman criterion by mechanically constraining protrusion growth.[ 90 , 91 , 92 ] At LT, hydrated or plasticized polymer frameworks maintain segmental mobility and continuous ion conduction, preventing localized current density spikes that typically trigger dendrite nucleation.[ 93 , 94 ] By homogenizing metal‐ion flux across the temperature spectrum, polymer electrolytes promote uniform metal deposition and significantly reduce internal short circuits. This dual capability, mechanical stabilization at HT and ionic transport retention at LT, establishes polymer electrolytes as indispensable regulators of dendrite‐free anode operation under extreme thermal conditions.[ 95 , 96 ]

SEI Formation and Hydrogen Evolution Reaction Suppression

In ARMBs, parasitic HER compromises anode stability by generating H2 gas and forming metal hydroxide passivation layers, effects that intensify under extreme thermal conditions. At LT, ice crystallization and sluggish ion transport exacerbate interfacial instability, whereas at HT, enhanced water activity and accelerated corrosion rapidly degrade the anode surface. Polymer electrolytes address these challenges through two mechanisms: (i) strong polymer–water interactions confine free water molecules, thereby reducing their reactivity at the metal interface and suppressing temperature‐driven HER kinetics, and (ii) the polymer matrix, often in conjunction with dissolved salts, decomposes or reorganizes to form a chemically robust SEI.[ 97 ] Unlike the unstable hydroxide films in conventional aqueous electrolytes, this SEI preserves both passivation and conductivity across extreme temperature ranges, effectively preventing gas evolution and corrosion.[ 98 ] Collectively, these features enable polymer electrolytes to stabilize interfacial reactions and sustain long‐term anode durability under extreme conditions.[ 99 ]

Stability of Ion Conductivity at Electrode Interface

Polymer electrolytes provide critical advantages for stabilizing anodes under extreme temperatures. At LT, they confine water molecules within polymer networks, depressing the freezing point and preventing full ice crystallization that would otherwise disrupt ion conduction. This confinement preserves a soft, gel‐like state, ensuring intimate electrode/electrolyte contact and mitigating the sharp rise in interfacial resistance associated with freezing. For example, poly(acrylic acid) (PAA) forms hydrogen bonds and van der Waals interactions with water that are nearly three times stronger than water–water bonds, disrupting the bulk hydrogen‐bonding network and thereby suppressing ice nucleation.[ 100 ] Consequently, PAA‐based hydrogels remain mechanically flexible and sustain ion transport even below 0 °C.[ 101 ] Beyond LT resilience, polymer matrices also impart structural stability at HT. Under HT conditions, conventional aqueous electrolytes undergo dehydration and interfacial delamination, whereas polymer electrolytes maintain wettability, suppress thermal contraction, and preserve conductive channels. This stability ensures reliable electrochemical performance across a broad temperature range and overcomes the loss of metal ion conductivity and interfacial failure that typically limit ARMB performance.

Advantages of Polymer Electrolyte on the Cathode

Suppression of Transition Metal Dissolution in Electrolytes

Many ARMB cathode materials are vulnerable to extreme temperatures, where thermal fluctuations accelerate transition metal dissolution, destabilize the lattice, and exacerbate capacity fading. Polymer electrolytes mitigate these effects by modulating the CEI in a thermally adaptive manner.[ 102 , 103 ] Through in situ formation or reinforcement, the polymer‐derived CEI, a thin layer of electrolyte decomposition products, acts as a protective barrier that isolates the cathode lattice from the electrolyte and suppresses thermally induced transition metal dissolution. By maintaining interfacial integrity and preserving the crystalline framework, polymer electrolytes significantly extend cycle life and enhance capacity retention under extreme thermal conditions.

CEI Stabilization and Oxygen Evolution Reaction Suppression

Beyond transition metal stabilization, the CEI also shields electrodes from parasitic reactions that intensify at elevated or fluctuating temperatures.[ 104 ] Among these, OER consumes electrolyte components and increases internal pressure, undermining both safety and electrochemical performance.[ 105 ] In aqueous polymer electrolytes, strong polymer–water interactions suppress free water activity, thereby limiting OER kinetics even when thermal fluctuations lower the oxidation barrier.[ 106 ] Simultaneously, the in situ generated polymer‐derived CEI functions as a chemically inert, ion‐selective layer that blocks solvent oxidation of the cathode surface during high‐voltage operation. At LT, hydrogen bond–driven interactions in the electrolyte inhibit ice nucleation, while the CEI preserves interfacial conductivity and mitigates oxygen‐related side reactions. In contrast, nonaqueous polymer electrolytes operate in a water‐free environment, inherently eliminating OER pathways while promoting inorganic‐rich CEI formation that retains structural integrity at HT. Collectively, these mechanisms broaden the oxidative ESW, provide robust cathode protection, and markedly improve the long‐term electrochemical durability of ARMBs.[ 107 , 108 ]

As a result, features such as dendrite suppression, stable SEI/CEI formation, and homogeneous ion flux across extreme temperatures demonstrate how polymer electrolytes mitigate mechanical, thermal, and electrochemical damage in ARMBs.[ 109 , 110 ] The key challenges to achieving stable operation under extreme conditions can be categorized as follows:

1) Physicochemical perspective: Temperature‐induced phase transitions can trigger electrolyte crystallization or evaporation, which may penetrate the separator or generate gas, ultimately leading to internal short‐circuiting. Polymer electrolytes address these issues by enhancing the mechanical flexibility and stability of aqueous electrolytes. Hydrophilic polymer matrices, such as hydrogels, regulate the local dielectric environment, suppress parasitic H+ reactions, and maintain phase stability. 2) Electrochemical perspective: Interfacial degradation reactions under extreme temperatures are tightly linked to physicochemical limitations. Freezing lowers ionic conductivity and destabilizes electrode/electrolyte contact, causing localized ion accumulation and byproduct formation. Meanwhile, solvent evaporation induces compositional imbalance, shifting electrode HOMO/LUMO levels and continuously generating byproducts through spontaneous redox reactions. Incorporating functional polymer groups (C═O, ─SO3 −, ─C≡N) regulates the hydrogen‐bonding network, thereby establishing efficient ion conduction pathways (3D polymer networks) and promoting stable SEI/CEI layers at electrode interfaces, which enhance electrochemical performance.[ 111 , 112 , 113 ] In particular, fluorinated or semi‐crystalline polymers, owing to their superior thermal stability, effectively suppress interfacial decomposition and contribute to long‐term durability. Section 3 presents practical examples where polymer electrolytes consistently improve electrode stability in extreme environments.

Strategies for Enhancing the Stability of Aqueous Batteries under Extreme Temperature

Aqueous Zinc‐ion Batteries

Aqueous zinc‐ion batteries (AZIBs) have emerged as a promising class of rechargeable energy storage systems owing to their intrinsic safety, environmental compatibility, and low material cost. Zn metal anodes provide a high theoretical capacity of 820 mAh g−1, a relatively low redox potential of −0.76 V versus the SHE, and compatibility with mild aqueous electrolytes.[ 114 ] Combined with the abundance and non‐toxicity of Zn, these attributes make AZIBs particularly attractive for grid‐scale and consumer‐level applications where safety and affordability are critical.[ 115 ] Unlike organic solvent‐based LIBs, aqueous electrolytes exhibit high ionic conductivity, non‐flammability, and simplified cell assembly. However, their relatively narrow ESW of ≈1.23 V, limited by water splitting, imposes strict constraints on electrode selection and long‐term durability. Furthermore, AZIB performance is highly temperature‐dependent, with both high and low extremes posing distinct challenges.[ 116 ] At LT, water freezing impedes ion transport and increases interfacial resistance, whereas at HT, parasitic reactions such as HER, Zn corrosion, and dendrite growth are accelerated, leading to capacity fading and potential safety hazards.[ 117 ]

To overcome these limitations, polymer electrolytes have attracted considerable attention for their ability to regulate interfacial behavior and enhance thermal resilience. They can be engineered in diverse physical forms, including hydrogels, solid‐state membranes, polymer coatings, and binder‐integrated interfaces, with tunable mechanical and electrochemical properties.[ 118 ] By confining water molecules, modulating Zn2+ solvation structures, and reinforcing electrode/electrolyte interfaces, polymer electrolytes extend the operational temperature range of AZIBs and mitigate the intrinsic thermodynamic and kinetic constraints of aqueous systems. Their role in stabilizing the highly reactive Zn anode under thermal stress has, in particular, emerged as a central focus of ongoing research (Figure 4 ).[ 14 ]

Effect of Polymer Electrolyte on Zn Anode

The Zn metal anode is highly susceptible to temperature‐induced instabilities due to its reactive surface and tendency for non‐uniform Zn deposition during (dis)charge cycling. In aqueous systems, free water further exacerbates these issues by accelerating HER, corrosion, and dendritic Zn growth, thereby compromising anode integrity and cell safety, particularly under thermal extremes.[ 119 ] To address these challenges, stabilizing the Zn/electrolyte interface, especially through tailored polymer electrolytes, has emerged as a key strategy to enable reliable Zn plating/stripping across wide temperature ranges. In this context, polymer electrolytes suppress side reactions, modulate Zn2+ solvation structures, and physically regulate Zn deposition morphology.[ 120 , 121 ] For instance, Zhang et al. developed a poly(4‐acryloylmorpholine)‐based hydrogel polymer electrolyte containing multiple zincophilic functional groups that modulate Zn2+ solvation dynamics and interfacial behavior in AZIBs. DFT and MD simulations revealed that functional moieties such as C═O, C─O─C, and C─N exhibit stronger binding affinities to Zn2+ than water, thereby promoting selective coordination that facilitates desolvation and suppresses water‐induced parasitic reactions. This tailored coordination environment lowers the Zn2+ desolvation energy barrier, as evidenced by reduced activation energy and interfacial resistance, while simultaneously directing Zn2+ flux along a gradient electrostatic potential field generated by the polymer chain, enabling uniform dendrite‐free deposition. XPS and XRD analyses further confirmed the formation of a stable SEI enriched in ZnF and polymer‐derived organic species, which collectively stabilize the Zn anode against HER and corrosion. As a result, Zn‖Zn symmetric cells demonstrated stable cycling for over 1,400 h at 0.5 mA cm−2, while V2O5‖Zn full cells delivered high capacity and long cycle life, even under high current conditions. The combined effects of coordinated desolvation, electric field homogenization, and structural reinforcement highlight the multifunctional role of polymer electrolytes, not merely as ion conductors, but as active interfacial regulators, establishing this system as a benchmark for thermally and electrochemically robust AZIBs anodes.[ 67 ] More broadly, polymer electrolytes exhibit distinct functionalities based on their physicochemical properties and can be classified into hydrogel polymer electrolytes (HGPEs) and solid‐state gel polymer electrolytes (SGPEs). HGPEs facilitate hydration‐mediated ion transport and maintain flexible interfacial contact, making them well‐suited for LT environments, whereas SGPEs provide enhanced mechanical rigidity and thermal resilience, which are advantageous for HT or high‐voltage applications.[ 122 ]

HGPEs are typically formulated from hydrophilic polymers such as polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), polyacrylamide (PAM), or polyethylene glycol (PEG), and contain substantial water content, typically 30–90 wt%. These hydrogels form hydrated polymer networks that engage in extensive hydrogen bonding with both water molecules and Zn2+, thereby reducing the activity of free water and preventing ice nucleation under LT conditions. Consequently, HGPEs maintain continuous ionic conduction and enable uniform Zn plating/stripping even in cold environments, positioning them as promising candidates for LT and flexible AZIBs.[ 123 ] Quantitatively, they exhibit ionic conductivities of 1–20 mS cm−1 at RT, with many formulations retaining values above 2 mS cm−1 at −20 °C. Their Zn2+ transference numbers generally range from 0.3 to 0.7, although recent designs incorporating zwitterionic or polyanionic components have achieved values above 0.6. Water retention is also a critical parameter, with advanced systems maintaining more than 80% water content at 60 °C. From a mechanical standpoint, HGPEs display tensile strengths of 1–4 MPa and elongation‐at‐break values exceeding 300%, supporting their integration into flexible or wearable battery architectures.[ 123 ] Thermally, these systems typically exhibit Tg between −30 and −80 °C, with certain formulations reaching as low as −117 °C. The activation energy for Zn2+ transport generally falls within 15–30 kJ mol−1, where lower values indicate enhanced ion mobility.

While HGPEs provide significant advantages at LT, conventional hydrogels still face critical limitations. At LT, excessive free water content and insufficient polymer crosslinking can cause ice formation and hinder Zn2+ transport, whereas at HT, dehydration and weak hydrogen bonding lead to mechanical instability and disruption of ion pathways.[ 124 ] To address these challenges, recent studies have reinforced the polymer network through solvent engineering, hydrogen‐bond modulation, and solvation environment tuning, strategies that collectively preserve ion‐conducting pathways and interfacial stability across extreme temperature ranges.[ 125 ]

He et al. reported a physically crosslinked PVA‐based hydrogel electrolyte (PDHE) that mitigates aggregation and dehydration issues typically induced by kosmotropic salts such as ZnSO4. Using a solvent exchange strategy, DMSO was first employed to dissolve PVA and pre‐shield hydroxyl groups through strong hydrogen‐bond acceptor interactions, thereby preventing premature interchain bonding and preserving extended polymer chain conformations, as illustrated in Figure 5a. Subsequent solvent exchange with water, a weaker hydrogen‐bond donor, spontaneously activated interchain hydrogen bonding, yielding a homogeneous and well‐hydrated physical network without chemical crosslinking. This structurally optimized matrix retained a high proportion of bound and confined water, suppressing ice nucleation down to −21.9 °C and maintaining over 80% water content at 60 °C. Notably, PDHE exhibited an ionic conductivity of 17.7 mS cm−1 at RT, comparable to liquid electrolytes (20.0 mS cm−1) and over 130 times higher than conventional freeze–thawed PVA gels. This enhancement was attributed to the uniformly hydrated network and strong Zn2+ coordination, which facilitated stable ion transport, suppressed dendrite formation and HER, and enabled extended cycling stability. Zn‖Zn symmetric cells demonstrated stable cycling for more than 1,300 h with low voltage hysteresis, while MnO2‖Zn full cells achieved 98.4% capacity retention after 100 cycles. These results underscore the effectiveness of PDHE as a high‐performance HGPE for AZIBs under extreme temperature conditions.[ 126 ]

Innovatively, mesoscale structural anisotropy can be exploited to establish continuous Zn2+ transport pathways and enhance interfacial regulation. Kang et al. reported a PVA‐based hydrogel electrolyte (PVA‐D‐SE) with directionally cast multilevel channels, fabricated via freeze casting and glycerol‐assisted solvent exchange, to improve Zn2+ transport and interfacial stability in aqueous batteries. The aligned pore architecture (Figure 5b) facilitated continuous ionic conduction, while glycerol strengthened hydrogen bonding, increasing water retention to 84.1% and depressing the freezing point to −32.7 °C. At RT, the hydrogel exhibited high ionic conductivity (5.4 mS cm−1), a Zn2+ transference number of 0.78, and a reduced Zn2+ activation energy from 28.7 to 17.3 kJ mol−1, indicating improved charge‐transport kinetics. Although LT conductivity was not explicitly reported, stable cycling for over 160,000 s at −20 °C suggested effective ion mobility under subzero conditions. Interfacial stabilization was attributed to guided Zn2+ flux along the directional channels and suppressed water activity within the hydrogen‐bonded matrix. While Zn2+ solvation chemistry remained unaltered, spatial confinement likely limited Zn2+/water interactions. This work further highlights structural reinforcement strategies, including network hierarchy, molecular crowding, and increased crystallinity, as essential for enhancing both thermal and mechanical durability.[ 127 ]

In addition, solvent polarity and ligand coordination can be co‐engineered to reshape the Zn2+ solvation environment, thereby enabling directional metal deposition and suppressing dendrite formation under thermal stress. Zhang et al. developed a PAM‐based hydrogel electrolyte (PZD) incorporating a binary solvent system of water and DMF to regulate interfacial behavior and ensure reliable performance across an extreme temperature range. DMF acts dually as a chaotropic cosolvent, disrupting bulk hydrogen bonding to lower the freezing point, and as a strong Zn2+ ligand that coordinates with both Zn2+ and the PAM matrix to modify the solvation structure. This coordination promotes Zn2+ deposition along the Zn (002) crystallographic plane, yielding compact and uniform metal layers while concurrently suppressing dendrite growth and the hydrogen evolution reaction. DFT calculations corroborated these findings by showing higher binding energies of Zn2+ to DMF and PAM compared to water. AFM analysis further confirmed suppressed dendrite growth and a stabilized electrode surface morphology (Figure 5c). The PZD‐20% hydrogel maintained an ionic conductivity of ≈7.5 mS cm−1 at RT and 3.8 mS cm−1 at –20 °C, ensuring efficient Zn2+ transport under LT conditions, although conductivity decreased at −30 °C due to resistance increases induced by partial freezing. These properties enabled 5,600 h of stable symmetric Zn cycling at RT and a capacity retention of 98 mAh g−1 after 750 cycles at −20 °C in MnO2‖Zn full cells, confirming the synergistic effect of solvent coordination and polymer confinement in stabilizing Zn2+ transport under thermal and mechanical stress.[ 128 ]

In contrast to LT strategies, Huang et al. reported a dual‐network polyanionic hydrogel electrolyte (PAM‐PAMPS‐10PD) that provides HT stability up to 100 °C in AZIBs. The incorporation of 1,5‐pentanediol (PD) as a molecular crowding agent serves dual functions: reinforcing the hydrogen‐bonding network to reduce water activity and volatility, while simultaneously coordinating with Zn2+ through its hydroxyl groups as a Lewis base ligand, thereby partially substituting water in the Zn2+ solvation sheath. This solvation restructuring lowers the desolvation energy barrier and facilitates uniform Zn plating. Concurrently, the sulfonated PAMPS matrix regulates interfacial behavior by providing electrostatic repulsion toward anions and directional coordination sites for Zn2+ transport. PAM complements this role by serving as the structural backbone of the hydrogel, enhancing mechanical integrity and water retention via hydrogen bonding with both water and PD. The flexible PAM network counterbalances the rigidity of the anion‐rich PAMPS phase, producing a dual‐network architecture that sustains dimensional and electrochemical stability at elevated temperatures. The electrolyte exhibits high ionic conductivity (87.84 mS cm−1 at 100 °C), a Zn2+ transference number of 0.692, and effective suppression of hydrogen evolution and corrosion. Zn‖Zn symmetric cells operated stably for over 500 h at 100 °C, while AC‖Zn full cells retained 47.8% of their capacity after 3,000 cycles, validating the efficiency of electrolyte in modulating solvation structure and interfacial dynamics under extreme thermal conditions (Figure 5d).[ 129 ]

To further enhance thermal resilience, Shi et al. designed a chitosan‐based hydrogel electrolyte that achieves HT Zn anode stability through coordinated regulation of solvation structure, interfacial pH, and hydrogen evolution. The amino (─NH2) and hydroxyl (─OH) groups in chitosan form coordinate bonds with Zn2+, stabilizing its solvation environment and suppressing thermally induced hydrolysis (Figure 6a). This Zn2+ chelation buffers interfacial pH fluctuations by preventing local OH‐ accumulation, while the proton‐accepting ‐NH2 groups further modulate interfacial acidity. At the same time, the formation of a dense hydrogen‐bond network immobilizes water molecules, lowering free water activity and thereby inhibiting hydrogen evolution and Zn corrosion under HT conditions. These molecular‐level interactions collectively promote uniform Zn deposition and effectively suppress dendrite formation. As a result, the electrolyte maintains high ionic conductivity (1.08 × 103 mS cm−1 at RT and 1.44 × 103 mS cm−1 at 60 °C) and electrochemical stability, delivering 98.5% CE at 60 °C and 68.7% capacity retention in PANI‖Zn full cells after 2,000 cycles. This work underscores the critical role of solvation and interfacial control in achieving thermal resilience in hydrogel‐based AZIBs.[ 130 ]

Fluorinated anions can be exploited to reconfigure hydrogen bonding in aqueous systems, enabling the development of supercooled electrolytes with enhanced mechanical resilience. For instance, Shi et al. designed a Zn(BF4)2–PAM hydrogel electrolyte that demonstrates exceptional anti‐freezing properties and mechanical robustness, allowing flexible AZIBs to operate at temperatures as low as −70 °C. The highly electronegative fluorine atoms in BF4 − form strong O─H···F hydrogen bonds with water molecules, disrupting the conventional O─H···O hydrogen‐bonding network that drives ice crystallization. This restructured hydrogen‐bond network was confirmed by FTIR (Figure 6b) and Raman spectroscopy (Figure 6c), as well as by DFT and MD simulations, which revealed significantly lower binding energies for Zn(BF4)2‐H2O compared to H2O/H2O interactions. The hydrogel exhibited a notably low Tg (─117 °C), which enhanced the segmental mobility of the polymer matrix and effectively suppressed freezing under extreme cold. At −70 °C, the electrolyte maintained high ionic conductivity (2.38 mS cm−1), enabling continuous Zn2+ transport. Integrated into flexible AZIBs, the system retained stable capacity, achieved ≈100% CE under LT conditions, and withstood repeated mechanical bending without performance degradation. These results confirm the ability of the hydrogel to inhibit ice formation, stabilize the Zn/electrolyte interface, and preserve both electrochemical and mechanical performance, making it a strong candidate for LT‐adaptive and wearable AZIB applications.[ 131 ]

Furthermore, Guan et al. introduced a ternary PAM‐based hydrogel electrolyte (PAM‐T‐S) incorporating thymine (Thy) and the zwitterionic monomer SPE to enable high‐rate performance and stability across extreme temperatures in AZIBs. These components perform dual functions: immobilizing free water through strong hydrogen bonding to lower water activity and suppress HER and coordinating with Zn2+ to partially replace H2O in the solvation sheath, thereby reducing the desolvation energy. SPE further stabilizes the solvation structure via Zn2+ SPE coordination and establishes a rapid Zn2+ transport network through its ‐SO3 − and ‐N+(CH3)3 functional groups. Together, Thy and SPE promote the formation of a hybrid solvation complex.[Zn(H2O)4(Thy)(SPE)]2+, while simultaneously facilitating the in situ growth of a stable organic/inorganic SEI comprising ZnCO3, ZnS, and ZnO, and polymeric residues. This SEI suppresses dendrite formation and promotes planar Zn deposition, as schematically illustrated in Figure 6d. The electrolyte exhibited high ionic conductivity across a wide temperature range, 6.5 mS cm−1 at −20 °C, 23.5 mS cm−1 at RT, and 31.4 mS cm−1 at 60 °C and supported stable Zn‖Zn cycling for 3,000 h at −20 °C, 3,200 h at RT, and 820 h at 60 °C. VO2‖Zn full cells retained high capacity over 2,000 cycles at LT and 5,000 cycles at HT, underscoring the robustness of this solvation‐ and interface‐regulating strategy (Figure 6e).[ 132 ]

To enhance the HT stability of Zn anodes, Wang et al. designed a hydrogen‐bonding engineered hydrogel electrolyte (HPG), composed of poly(ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate (PMEM) and N‐hydroxyethyl acrylamide (HEAA), and operated it under lean water conditions (35 wt%). The HPG matrix employs a dual‐function design that regulates both the solvation structure and interfacial reactivity at HT. Specifically, the PEG‐based PMEM chains introduce ether oxygens that partially replace water molecules in the primary Zn2+ solvation sheath, thereby reducing water activity and mitigating the HER (Figure 6f). Simultaneously, the HEAA component contributes amide groups and abundant hydrogen‐bonding sites, which immobilize residual water and enhance the mechanical stability of gel under thermal stress. This combination buffers interfacial pH fluctuations, a critical factor in suppressing Zn corrosion and the formation of side products such as Zn4SO4(OH)6∙nH2O (ZHS) (Figure 6g). Molecular dynamics simulations and operando pH measurements further revealed that the HPG gel dynamically modulates interfacial acidity by retarding local OH‐ accumulation, thereby promoting uniform Zn plating along the (002) plane. As a result, asymmetric cells exhibited dendrite‐free cycling for over 1,200 h at 1 mA cm−2 and retained stable performance at 90 °C without electrolyte decomposition or interfacial delamination (Figure 6h). This study demonstrates that molecular‐level solvation tuning combined with dynamic pH buffering, enabled by tailored hydrogel design, creates thermally adaptive interfaces for stable Zn metal anodes in quasi‐solid‐state aqueous batteries.[ 133 ]

In addition to HGPEs, SGPEs are typically composed of crosslinked polymer matrices such as poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO), polyacrylonitrile (PAN), or poly(vinylidene fluoride‐co‐hexafluoropropylene) (PVDF‐HFP), and generally contain less than 10 wt% free waters. Their dense and mechanically robust structures suppress electrolyte evaporation and parasitic reactions such as HER and Zn corrosion, particularly under HT conditions.[ 120 ] Moreover, SGPEs facilitate the formation of stable, inorganic‐rich SEIs containing ZnF2, ZnO, and polymer‐derived residues, which mitigate dendritic Zn growth and enhance interfacial compatibility. These characteristics make SGPEs promising candidates for HT and high‐voltage AZIB applications.[ 134 ] In contrast to HGPEs, typical SGPEs exhibit ionic conductivities of 10−3 to 1 mS cm−1 at RT; however, recent modifications, such as co‐polymerization with ionic monomers, have enabled conductivities exceeding 2 mS cm−1 at HT. Although their Zn2+ transference number remains modest (≈0.2–0.4), incorporation of fixed anionic groups or zwitterionic components has achieved moderate improvements.[ 118 ] Mechanically, SGPEs often display tensile strengths above 5 MPa and Young's moduli in the several‐hundred MPa range, providing excellent dimensional and structural stability. Thermally, these systems remain operational above 100 °C with negligible mass loss, while their low water content significantly reduces the risks of electrolyte drying or boiling.[ 125 ] The activation energy for Zn2+ conduction typically exceeds 35 kJ mol−1, reflecting restricted segmental mobility and limited solvation capacity. Despite these advantages, SGPEs face notable limitations under LT conditions, where restricted segmental mobility and poor solvation dynamics reduce ionic conductivity to below 10−4 mS cm−1.[ 135 ] To address these challenges, recent strategies include incorporating flexible polymer backbones, interpenetrating polymer networks (IPNs), and inorganic nanofillers such as MOFs and zincophilic ceramics, which lower ion‐transport energy barriers and improve interfacial stability. With continued material innovations, SGPEs are expected to complement HGPEs by delivering robust performance under both thermal and electrochemical stress.[ 136 ]

Unlike conventional aqueous systems that suffer from water‐induced parasitic reactions, Chen et al. developed a fully solvent‐free solid polymer electrolyte (SPE) that addresses Zn anode instability through interfacial and ionic conductivity engineering. The system integrates poly(vinylidene fluoride‐co‐hexafluoropropylene) (PVDF‐HFP) with poly(methyl acrylate)‐grafted MXene (MXene‐g‐PMA), where the PMA chains introduce abundant F─H hydrogen‐bonding sites that enable homogeneous MXene dispersion within the polymer matrix (Figure 7a). This uniform filler distribution promotes polymer chain segmental motion and facilitates Zn2+ dissociation and transport through coupled polymer–filler ion pathways, resulting in high ionic conductivity (0.269 mS cm−1 at RT and 1.07 mS cm−1 at 85 °C) despite the absence of liquid solvents. Simultaneously, the solid‐state framework suppresses dendrite growth, mitigates hydrogen evolution, and stabilizes interfacial impedance under HT conditions. Consequently, the SPE enables dendrite‐free Zn plating/stripping for over 1,000 h at RT and 200 h at 85 °C, supports long‐term full‐cell cycling (10,000 cycles at 2 C), and delivers stable full‐cell performance across extreme temperature ranges (Figure 7b), thereby demonstrating robust mechanical, thermal, and electrochemical durability.[ 137 ]

Yan et al. proposed a “polymer‐in‐salt” solid polymer electrolyte (SPE) composed of 70 wt% Zn(OTf)2 and ZnF2 dispersed within a PAN matrix (Figure 7c). The high salt content fundamentally restructures the ion‐transport environment by suppressing free water activity and establishing a salt‐dominated conduction pathway. Zn(OTf)2 weakens dipole–dipole interactions between PAN chains, while ZnF2 disrupts semicrystalline domains; together, these effects lower the Tg from 104 to ≈50 °C and reduce the Zn2+ migration barrier to 0.067 eV. Consequently, the 70% salt‐SPE achieves weaker solvation and lower migration activation energy due to the increased concentration of Zn2+ and reduced PAN polarity. Combined with reduced crystallinity and enhanced chain mobility, these modifications provide abundant ion‐transport pathways and improve ionic conductivity. The resulting amorphous yet mechanically robust polymer network supports Zn2+ conduction even at 5 °C, where the full cell delivered 43 mAh g−1 (Figure 7d). At HT, the polymer matrix remains stable without dehydration or structural collapse, while the fluoride‐rich interface promotes in situ formation of a ZnF2 based SEI, effectively suppressing dendrite growth and parasitic reactions. Although ionic conductivity was not explicitly reported, the electrolyte enabled dendrite‐free Zn plating/stripping for over 3,200 h and maintained 80% capacity retention in MnHCF‖Zn full cells after 6,000 cycles at 60 °C. These results highlight the system's thermal adaptability and interfacial stability, achieved through enhanced chain flexibility, tailored solvation structure, and chemically stable interphases.[ 139 , 140 , 141 ]

These insights establish the mechanistic foundation of modern anode‐facing polymer electrolyte strategies. By simultaneously tuning solvation structures, coordinating functional groups, and mechanical design parameters, researchers are developing materials that maintain Zn anode stability across an unprecedented range of operating conditions, thereby enabling all‐climate, next‐generation AZIB systems. Achieving this goal requires polymer electrolytes that integrate multifunctionality with dynamic responsiveness, regulating ion transport, preserving mechanical integrity, and adapting interfacial chemistry in real time under fluctuating temperatures. Emerging approaches, such as hydrogel–solid gel hybrids, tunable crosslinking chemistries, and the incorporation of functional additives including zwitterions, supramolecular binders, or ceramic nanophases, are beginning to bridge performance gaps under diverse thermal conditions.[ 139 ] Ultimately, progress toward next‐generation polymer electrolytes for all‐climate AZIBs will depend on a deeper mechanistic understanding of temperature‐sensitive solvation structures, interfacial evolution, and phase transitions.[ 142 ] In pursuit of structurally and electrochemically adaptive systems, the following anode‐focused design principles have emerged:

1) Polymers containing Zn2+ coordinating functional groups, such as carbonyl, amine, or sulfonate moieties, can partially replace water molecules in the primary solvation sheath, creating a polymer‐dominated solvation environment that lowers the desolvation energy barrier, promotes uniform Zn deposition, facilitates stable SEI formation, and enhances interfacial compatibility, particularly under HT conditions. 2) Hydrophilic polymer networks, including hydrogel‐forming polymers (e.g., PEG, PVA) and quasi‐solid matrices incorporating crowding agents (e.g., glycerol, PD), effectively immobilize free water through hydrogen bonding, thereby suppressing water activity, mitigating hydrogen evolution, preventing ice crystallization, and sustaining electrochemical performance at LT. 3) HGPEs and mechanically robust SGPEs serve as physical barriers against dendritic Zn propagation while preserving dimensional stability and enabling directional ion transport, even under repeated thermal expansion and contraction. 4) Polymer electrolytes with reduced water content, such as those based on PVDF‐HFP, PAN, or polymer‐in‐salt systems, minimize solvent evaporation and resist oxidative degradation at HT, thereby maintaining electrode–electrolyte interfacial integrity and supporting prolonged cycling stability under thermal stress.

Effect of Polymer Electrolyte on Zn Cathode

While extensive efforts in AZIBs have centered on stabilizing the Zn anode, the cathode also undergoes thermally driven degradation pathways that critically affect long‐term performance. Cathode materials such as MnO2, V2O5, and MoO3, which rely on layered or tunneled frameworks for Zn2+ insertion, are particularly vulnerable to structural distortion and irreversible phase transitions during repeated Zn2+ intercalation and deintercalation.[ 143 ] At HT, accelerated Zn2+ diffusion kinetics coupled with thermal expansion disrupt lattice spacing, promote oxygen vacancy formation and cation migration, and can even trigger crystal symmetry collapse. These instabilities often induce Jahn–Teller distortions, especially in Mn‐based systems, leading to phase transformations into electrochemically inactive products such as ZnMn2O4 spinel or amorphous MnOX.[ 119 ] In contrast, at LT, reduced ionic mobility and increased electrolyte viscosity hinder uniform Zn2+ insertion, while thermodynamic favorability of H+ co‐insertion induces proton‐coupled charge storage that destabilizes the host lattice. This process generates lattice strain, interlayer expansion, and heightened susceptibility to side reactions, including Mn2+ dissolution and parasitic HER, which collectively degrade cathode crystallinity, reduce electronic conductivity, and accelerate capacity fading with poor cycling reversibility.[ 144 ]

To address these challenges, polymer electrolytes have emerged as effective interfacial modifiers that form physically and chemically protective layers on cathode surfaces while simultaneously enhancing anode interfacial stability. Hydrogels containing functional groups such as hydroxyl, carboxyl, or amide, derived from polymers including PAM, sodium alginate (SA), or PEG, can coordinate with Zn2+ and protons, thereby buffering insertion reactions, mitigating cathode dissolution, and modulating local pH.[ 142 ] In vanadium‐ and manganese‐based systems, these polymer matrices additionally act as ion‐permeable scaffolds that trap dissolved metal ions and relieve mechanical stress during cycling. For organic cathodes such as PANI, conductive or redox‐active polymer electrolytes improve both interfacial conductivity and electrochemical reversibility.[ 145 ] The choice of polymer is generally tailored to the cathode redox chemistry and the operating temperature window. At LT, polymer electrolytes suppress water crystallization and sustain ion mobility through hydrogen bonding, whereas at HT, they function as diffusion barriers and thermomechanical buffers.[ 132 ] Through the combined effects of chemical coordination and physical confinement, polymer electrolytes enhance cathode protection and thermal adaptability, both of which are critical for reliable AZIB operation under variable climate conditions.

For example, Lee et al. reported a poly(ethylene glycol)‐grafted poly(arylene ether sulfone) (PAES‐g‐PEG) as an anti‐freezing, flexible solid polymer electrolyte (SPE) incorporating a eutectic solution of H2O and ethylene glycol. The PAES backbone provides mechanical robustness, while the PEG grafts enhance ionic conductivity via ether oxygen atoms that coordinate with Zn2+, establishing strong electrostatic interactions. This Zn2+–ether oxygen coordination, along with the flexibility of the PEG chains, facilitates ion transport through coupled ion–polymer segmental dynamics (Figure 8a). The eutectic solution, characterized by a high dielectric permittivity, lowers the freezing point and enhances cation transport, thereby increasing ionic conductivity. This behavior aligns with Debye–Hückel theory, which correlates salt dissociation strength with the dielectric constant.[ 67 ] Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements revealed multiple redox peaks between 0.2 and 1.6 V (vs. Zn/Zn2+) at both room temperature (RT) and −20 °C, indicating highly reversible, multistep Zn2+ (de)intercalation processes within the V10O24·nH2O framework (Figure 8b). Additionally, CV profiles recorded at various scan rates (0.2–1.0 mV s−1) demonstrated stable redox kinetics under LT conditions, confirming favorable Zn2+ storage dynamics facilitated by the SPE–cathode interface. A V10O24·nH2O@rGO‖Zn cell employing PAES‐g‐PEG exhibited excellent electrochemical performance, retaining 87% and 91% of its capacity at −20 °C and RT, respectively, over 300 cycles at a current density of 1.0 A g−1. Moreover, the cell demonstrated HT adaptability, maintaining 97% of its initial capacity after repeated thermal cycling between −20 and 20 °C.[ 146 ]

LT operation of PANI cathodes requires not only sustained ionic mobility but also effective proton management at the electrode/electrolyte interface. This challenge is addressed through sulfonic acid‐based internal buffering. Feng et al. reported a sulfonated hydrogel electrolyte (PAGE), containing 3 M Zn(ClO4)2, specifically designed for poly cathodes. The abundant ─SO3 − and H+ groups in the polymer matrix act as an internal proton reservoir, buffering PANI deprotonation and accelerating redox transitions between its benzenoid and quinonoid states. This proton‐enriched environment enhances pseudocapacitive behavior and enables dual‐ion storage involving both Zn2+ and ClO4 − (Figure 8c). Notably, the chaotropic nature of ClO4 − disrupts the hydrogen‐bond network in water, imparting anti‐freezing properties that support high ionic conductivity (14.2 mS cm−1) at −35 °C (Figure 8d).[ 114 ] Under these conditions, the PANI‖Zn cell maintained stable performance over 70,000 cycles at a current density of 15 A g−1 and delivered high capacities at both low temperature and room temperature (Figure 8e). These results underscore the critical role of electrolyte/cathode compatibility and demonstrate the ability of polymer‐based systems to maintain redox stability, rapid ion mobility, and long‐term cycling performance under fluctuating thermal conditions, primarily through hydrogen bond‐mediated regulation of water activity and interfacial ion transport.[ 145 ]

At the core of Chen et al.’s approach lies architectural control over both hydrogen bonding and matrix rigidity, enabling reversible cathodic reactions across extreme temperatures. They reported a multifunctional hydrogel electrolyte (CT3G30) engineered to impart exceptional thermal adaptability to aqueous Zn–MnO2 batteries, operating reliably across a extreme temperature ranges from −50 to 60 °C. The electrolyte consists of a cellulose matrix crosslinked with tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) and plasticized with glycerol, forming a mechanically robust, thermally responsive 3D polymer network (Figure 9a). Mechanistically, the silica–cellulose framework introduces a rigid, interconnected structure that facilitates long‐range ion transport while simultaneously restricting water evaporation and mitigating volume fluctuations under HT conditions. Glycerol serves a dual role: it establishes extensive hydrogen bonding with water molecules, suppressing bulk water crystallization at LT, and acts as a crowding agent, lowering the system's freezing point to −64.6 °C. These structural features synergistically enable CT3G30 to maintain electrolyte integrity and stabilize interfacial charge transfer under thermal extremes. The rGO‐supported MnO2 cathode exhibits a thermally stable intercalation mechanism, in which both H+ and Zn2+ are reversibly inserted without significant polarization shifts across varying temperatures. The flexible hydrogel matrix ensures consistent ionic accessibility to the MnO2 cathode, preserving structural stability and minimizing interfacial resistance. In situ electrochemical measurements confirm that dual‐ion storage is effectively maintained at −40 °C, attributed to the suppression of ice nucleation and preservation of a hydrated interface (Figure 9b,c). Electrochemically, the CT3G30 electrolyte exhibits high ionic conductivities, 32.3 mS cm−1 at 20 °C and 19.4 mS cm−1 at −40 °C, surpassing those of conventional aqueous electrolytes under comparable conditions. Full‐cell MnO2‖Zn batteries incorporating this electrolyte achieve specific capacities of 278 mAh g−1 at 20 °C and 262 mAh g−1 at −20 °C, with 83.4% capacity retention after 2,000 cycles at −40 °C and maintain excellent performance under mechanical bending. These results underscore the critical roles of hydrogen bond modulation, siloxane‐mediated matrix reinforcement, and hydrated interfacial preservation as key design strategies for developing polymer electrolytes suited for all‐climate AZIBs.[ 147 ]

Jin et al. developed a multifunctional anti‐freezing and self‐healing polymer electrolyte (AF‐SH–CPAM) by integrating crosslinked PAM with ethylene glycol (EG), specifically tailored for LT operable AZIBs. The key innovation lies in the electrolyte's simultaneous ability to maintain high ionic conductivity, preserve mechanical flexibility, and autonomously repair physical damage through dynamic hydrogen bonding. EG serves a dual function: it disrupts the conventional O─H···O hydrogen‐bond network in bulk water, thereby lowering the freezing point and ensuring phase stability under cold conditions; concurrently, it participates in reversible hydrogen bonding with PAM chains and water molecules, facilitating network self‐reconstruction and sustained ion transport after mechanical damage. Structurally, the polymer gel forms a dense yet stretchable matrix that resists volumetric contraction at LT, preventing interfacial delamination and maintaining intimate contact with electrode surfaces. This thermal resilience is particularly critical at the cathode interface, where a worm‐like nanostructured poly cathode benefits from strong adhesion to the AF‐SH–CPAM matrix. Such interfacial coupling minimizes resistance and enhances Zn2+ insertion/extraction reversibility by sustaining continuous ion flux under thermal contraction. Spectroscopic analyses, including XPS and in situ Raman, reveal that the gel electrolyte also regulates the local ionic environment by reversibly coordinating CF3SO3 − anions during cycling (Figure 9d), which supports stable (de)doping transitions within PANI and mitigates cathode degradation. Collectively, these features enable prolonged cycling stability over 600 cycles with minimal capacity fade at −20 °C (Figure 9e,f). This work exemplifies a comprehensive cathode‐side thermal management strategy that integrates water activity suppression, interfacial stabilization, and mechanical adaptability, key attributes of high‐performance, all‐climate AZIBs.[ 148 , 149 ]

Recent studies have demonstrated that the viscoelastic behavior and thermal response profile of polymers can be strategically exploited to engineer self‐regulating interfaces. At LT, polymers with extended hydrogen bonding networks suppress water crystallization by promoting the formation of semi‐coordinated water clusters, thereby preserving ion transport without freezing.[ 147 ] Conversely, at HT, phase‐segregated or semicrystalline polymers modulate local ion diffusivity, buffer thermal expansion, and reduce interfacial impedance by matching the mechanical modulus of the cathode. These emerging functionalities indicate that polymer electrolytes are no longer passive matrices but chemically responsive platforms capable of pristine interfacial reconfiguration. Their ability to adapt to dynamic local environments, such as pH fluctuations, redox changes, or mechanical strain, opens new pathways for enhancing cathode integrity and electrochemical reversibility, particularly under thermally demanding conditions.[ 150 ] Looking forward, the integration of site‐specific polymer design with spatiotemporal control over ion flux will be critical to fully realizing the potential of cathode materials in AZIBs operating across extreme temperature ranges. Importantly, these polymer functions are not isolated. Inter‐electrode feedback mechanisms, including pH drift and metal ion crossover, often exacerbate cathode degradation, underscoring the need for localized regulation strategies at the cathode interface. In response, four representative approaches have emerged that leverage polymer functionality to directly tailor the interfacial microenvironment and mitigate temperature‐induced cathodic failure:

1) Polymer electrolytes enriched with hydrogen bond donors and acceptors (e.g., PEG, HEMA, PVA) localize and confine water molecules at the cathode interface, mitigating freeze‐induced passivation and preserving Zn2+/H+ redox activity under LT conditions. 2) Acidic or basic functional groups such as ‐SO3, H+, ‐COO−, and –NH2 embedded within the polymer backbone dynamically regulate the local pH, thereby inhibiting proton‐induced lattice distortion and suppressing transition metal dissolution during HT cycling. 3) Chelating moieties within the polymer matrix immobilize dissolved transition metals (e.g., Mn2+, V5+), minimizing cathodic contamination and preserving crystallographic integrity under high‐rate or HT operations. 4) Polymers that polymerize or self‐assemble in situ within the cathode architecture form mechanically adaptive and chemically cohesive interfaces, which accommodate volumetric expansion, prevent structural delamination, and sustain electrochemical reversibility across extreme temperatures (Tables 1, 2, 3).

Other Aqueous batteries (Li, Na, K, Mg)

While AZIBs have received considerable attention, other charge carriers in ARMBs, such as lithium, sodium, potassium, and magnesium, offer distinct electrochemical properties and present unique challenges and opportunities for polymer electrolyte design. Comparing these systems to Zn2+ provides a valuable framework for understanding how ion‐specific characteristics influence interfacial chemistry, solvation dynamics, and temperature adaptability in polymer‐based aqueous batteries. Zn2+, a divalent ion with moderate charge density and hydration energy, readily forms labile coordination complexes with water and polymer ligands. This enables facile tuning of solvation structures and supports the formation of inorganic‐rich SEIs in hydrogel or solid polymer matrices.[ 151 ] In contrast, Mg2+, despite having a similar ionic radius (≈0.72 Å), exhibits significantly higher charge density, resulting in a tightly bound solvation shell and high desolvation energy. Consequently, polymer electrolytes for Mg‐based systems must accommodate stronger ion–polymer interactions and typically require highly polar or chelating functional groups (e.g., sulfonates, carbonyls) to facilitate efficient ion transport.[ 152 ]

Monovalent ions, Li+, Na+, and K+, exhibit lower charge densities and progressively larger ionic radii (Li+: ≈0.76 Å, Na+: ≈1.02 Å, K+: ≈1.38 Å), leading to weaker solvation and faster desolvation kinetics. Among them, Li+ maintains relatively high hydration enthalpy, necessitating dielectric‐rich polymer environments to sustain ionic conductivity and suppress HER. Na+, with its intermediate ionic size and hydration enthalpy, is compatible with flexible, moderate polar polymer matrices such as PVA or PEO, which support ion transport while mitigating dendrite formation. In contrast, K+, characterized by the lowest charge density and a highly diffuse hydration shell, requires polymer systems that provide structural confinement to prevent random diffusion and maintain interfacial uniformity.[ 7 ] Accordingly, the design of polymer electrolytes for both monovalent and multivalent aqueous systems must be precisely tailored to the intrinsic physicochemical properties of each ion. Critical parameters, including solvation strength, ionic volume, and polymer segmental dynamics, must be holistically optimized to enable stable metal plating/stripping, minimize interfacial resistance, and ensure performance across extreme temperature conditions and diverse aqueous battery chemistries.[ 153 ] In the following subsections, representative case studies are presented to illustrate how polymer electrolyte systems can be specifically engineered to accommodate the unique electrochemical behaviors of alternative ions such as Li+, Na+, Mg2+, and K+, with particular emphasis on interfacial stabilization and thermal resilience at the anode interface.

Effect of Polymer Electrolyte on Anode for Other ARMBs

Compared to Zn, which tolerates aqueous conditions due to its moderate hydration energy and labile coordination behavior, alternative aqueous cations such as Li+, Na+, K+, and Mg2+ exhibit distinct interfacial instabilities that complicate anode design, particularly under thermal extremes.[ 40 ] During practical operation, these ions are subject not only to solvation asymmetries but also to pronounced temperature‐dependent interfacial dynamics. Li+ and Na+, for example, tend to form unstable SEIs in aqueous environments, especially at LT, where desolvation slows and SEI self‐repair mechanisms become ineffective.[ 153 ] This leads to interfacial heterogeneity, voltage hysteresis, and dendritic growth, challenges that polymer electrolytes must overcome through precise control of interfacial energetics and polymer segmental mobility. To mitigate these effects, polymer systems must simultaneously suppress HER, promote directional metal deposition, and exhibit mechanical adaptability across temperature fluctuations. Incorporating nitrile (─C≡N), sulfonate (‐SO3 −), or Lewis base carbonyl and ether moieties into the polymer framework enhances cation complexation and supports SEI stabilization. Additionally, plastic crystal additives such as succinonitrile are frequently employed to lower Tg, improve ionic conductivity, and maintain mechanical flexibility under LT conditions.[ 154 ] For Li‐based systems, carbonate‐rich polymer matrices are often utilized to sustain high ionic conductivity and electrochemical stability at HT.[ 155 ]

Na‐ion systems exhibit interfacial vulnerabilities like those of LIB systems but benefit from a broader range of compatible hard carbon hosts. Strategies to improve SEI robustness at the Na interface include polymer–salt formulations that favor anion‐dominated solvation structures, thereby promoting the formation of thinner and more uniform SEIs.[ 156 ] Structural reinforcements, such as nanofiber mats or gelled polymer blends, further enhance dimensional tolerance and mechanical stability during LT cycling. Potassium anodes pose unique challenges due to the large ionic radius and diffuse hydration shells of K+, which hinder effective coordination with polymer matrices and promote isotropic diffusion. In the absence of structural confinement, K+ tends to accumulate at the interface, leading to irregular nucleation and unstable deposition. To address this, recent designs employ multilayered architectures and mechanically guided ion pathways, such as aligned pores or sandwich structures, that enforce spatial ion uniformity. The integration of polymer matrices with high transference numbers (> 0.9) and low Tg further supports reliable K+ conduction under LT conditions.[ 157 ] In contrast, Mg2+ based aqueous systems are challenged by the high charge density of ion, which leads to rigid solvation structures and sluggish ion dynamics. Conventional hydrogel matrices often fail to disrupt the primary hydration shell, resulting in interfacial passivation via Mg(OH)2 formation. To mitigate this, polymer electrolytes for Mg2+ systems must incorporate coordinating materials such as PVDF‐HFP, along with buffer regions that modulate the local pH near the interface.[ 158 ] These polymers reduce the desolvation barrier and restructure the solvation shell to favor polymer‐dominant complexes, thereby enabling reversible Mg2+ transport and improving interfacial stability.

In aqueous lithium‐ion batteries (ALIBs), Deng et al. proposed a zwitterionic oligomer‐based polymer electrolyte (OPDP) for high‐voltage operation, which provides exceptional interfacial stability and water confinement at both anode and cathode interfaces. OPDP introduces sulfonate and quaternary ammonium groups that form strong hydrogen bonds with water molecules, thereby enhancing ionic conductivity at HT (Figure 10a) while reducing water activity despite a 25.4 wt.% water content (Figure 10b). This suppression of water reactivity broadens the electrochemical stability window (ESW) to 3.26 V and mitigates parasitic reactions such as HER and OER. At the anode, Mo6S8 enables stable Li+ insertion, supported by the formation of an SEI enriched in LiF, Li2CO3, and Li2O, facilitated by the polymer's ability to coordinate Li+ and confine water (Figure 10c). Consequently, the electrolyte enables long‐term, dendrite‐free cycling across a wide temperature range, with 99.7% capacity retention after 200 cycles at 0.5 C and 89% retention after 2,000 cycles at 10 C. AIMD simulations and spectroscopic analyses confirm reconfiguration of the solvation shell into a polymer‐dominant environment, highlighting dual role as both a water‐confining medium and an interfacial stabilizer of OPDP. This work demonstrates a dual‐interface engineering strategy based on zwitterionic polymer electrolytes to overcome the intrinsic instability of ALIBs, establishing OPDP as a promising design for high‐voltage, all‐climate ALIB applications.[ 159 ]

To enhance the thermal resilience and interfacial stability of lithium metal anodes, Zhang et al. developed an SGPE based on PAN and microencapsulated hexachlorophosphazene (HCP@UF). The microcapsule design employs urea–formaldehyde (UF) resin shells to isolate the reactive HCP cores, preventing parasitic reactions with Li while providing flame retardance through thermally triggered release of Cl, P, and N radicals (Figure 10d). The PAN–HCP@UF matrix forms extensive hydrogen‐bonding networks that enhance mechanical robustness (Young's modulus ≈ 5.8 GPa) and maintain interfacial adhesion, both of which are critical for suppressing dendrite formation. The encapsulated structure also facilitates ion transport, yielding an ionic conductivity of 0.293 mS cm−1 at 30 °C and a Li+ transference number of 0.80, while lowering the activation energy for ion conduction to 28.35 kJ mol−1. In symmetric Li‖Li cells, the electrolyte enables over 4,400 h of stable cycling with minimal overpotential, and in LFP‖Li full cells, it retains 88.8% of capacity after 500 cycles. Moreover, the HCP@UF additive broadens the ESW to 5.0 V vs. Li/Li+, while thermal characterization confirms flame retardancy (limiting oxygen index, LOI: 28%) and structural integrity up to 200 °C (Figure 10e). This work underscores the importance of multifunctional SPE design, where microencapsulation, hydrogen bonding, and flame retardance are integrated to achieve safe, thermally adaptive Li‐metal batteries.[ 160 ]

In aqueous sodium‐ion batteries (ASIBs), the performance of hard carbon anodes is particularly compromised at LT due to sluggish Na+ diffusion, unstable SEI formation, and pronounced voltage hysteresis.[ 162 ] Structural engineering strategies such as ultramicropore construction, heteroatom doping, and carbon coatings have been employed to promote desolvated Na+ intercalation and enable uniform charge transfer under cold conditions. Among these approaches, GPEs provide both mechanical confinement and tailored solvent coordination environments, stabilizing the SEI and supporting dendrite‐free operation across a wide temperature range. Guo et al. developed a GPE platform based on electrospun polylactide (PLA) nanofibers incorporating NaF to achieve dual‐interface stabilization in sodium metal batteries.[ 161 ] The in situ formation of a porous, gelated structure enabled continuous Na+ conduction, while the gradual dissolution of NaF released fluoride species that modulated the Na+ solvation environment toward anion‐dominant coordination states. These modified solvation clusters promoted the spontaneous formation of a highly uniform, inorganic‐rich SEI on the sodium metal surface, effectively suppressing dendrite growth and enhancing interfacial stability. Simultaneously, the cathode/electrolyte interface benefited from a thinner CEI layer with reduced impedance and minimized structural degradation (Figure 11a). The low surface energy and flexibility of the polymer matrix ensured intimate contact at both electrode interfaces, enabling smooth ion flux. As a result, full cells employing of Na3V2(PO4)3 delivered long‐term cyclability, retaining over 92% of their initial capacity after 650 cycles at RT and demonstrating reliable performance under thermal extremes from −35 to 60 °C (Figure 11b,c). This work presents a versatile “salt‐in‐polymer” electrolyte design that offers a promising route toward thermally tolerant, stable sodium metal anode systems. Although based on non‐aqueous solvents, this study underscores the pivotal role of polymer electrolyte design in enabling stable electrochemical performance across extreme temperature windows.

In aqueous potassium‐ion batteries (APIBs), the large ionic radius and low charge density of K+, present significant challenges. To address this, Yang et al. developed a multilayered GPE (K‐ISMGPE) for high‐performance quasi‐solid‐state potassium‐ion batteries (SSPIBs), consisting of an ethoxylated trimethylolpropane triacrylate (ETPTA) layer sandwiched between PVDF‐HFP layers. The ETPTA layer, rich in carbonyl groups, exhibited weak electrostatic interactions with K+, which facilitated rapid ion migration (Figure 12a), resulting in a high transference number (0.92) and enhanced ionic conductivity (5.15 mS cm−1). Meanwhile, the outer PVDF‐HFP layers provided mechanical reinforcement (7.21 MPa) and improved interfacial contact. This in situ‐formed multilayered architecture ensured low interfacial impedance and effectively suppressed dendrite growth. The electrolyte displayed electrochemical stability up to 4.7 V and delivered strong performance in full cells using both intercalation‐type (Prussian Blue, PB1/2) and conversion‐type (PTCDA) cathodes. Specifically, K‖K‐ISMGPE‖PB1 and K‖K‐ISMGPE‖PTCDA cells retained 71.5% of their initial capacity after 800 cycles and 76.7% after 300 cycles at 0.5 A g−1, respectively. In addition, the system maintained stable ionic conductivity and a low activation energy (Ea = 0.13 eV) over a temperature range of 30 to 80 °C, demonstrating strong thermal adaptability and interfacial stability. These findings underscore a unified polymer electrolyte strategy that integrates ion mobility, mechanical robustness, and thermal resilience, offering a promising pathway for high‐capacity SSPIBs.[ 163 ]

In aqueous magnesium‐ion batteries (AMIBs), Yang et al. proposed a salt‐hydrate‐engineered system that maintains electrochemical stability at LT by tuning water activity and solvation dynamics. In divalent Mg systems, the strong electrostatic interaction between Mg2+ and solvent molecules produces compact solvation structures and extensive hydrogen bonding, which restricts ion mobility and promotes electrolyte freezing under LT conditions. By adjusting the MgCl2 concentration to 4 m, a eutectic‐like electrolyte was obtained in which Mg2+ water coordination effectively disrupted the hydrogen‐bond network, lowering the freezing point to −62 °C and widening the ESW (Figure 12b). This restructured solvation environment preserved high ionic conductivity (2.77 mS cm−1 at −50 °C) and improved Mg2+ transport by alleviating desolvation limitations. At the Mg metal anode interface, such solvation control promoted uniform Mg deposition while suppressing dendrite growth, a particularly critical issue in multivalent systems where high charge density exacerbates interfacial instabilities. Full cells employing δ‐MnO2 cathodes demonstrated stable cycling and excellent capacity retention even at −50 °C (Figure 12c). Moreover, incorporating MgCl2‐loaded polyacrylamide hydrogels yielded quasi‐solid‐state AMIBs with mechanical flexibility, interfacial robustness, and reliable performance across extreme temperature ranges.[ 164 ]

Comprehensively, these studies highlight the potential of polymer electrolytes to be precisely engineered for addressing ion‐specific challenges in alternative aqueous anode systems beyond Zn. While dendrite suppression and HER remain shared objectives, transport limitations and interfacial reactions vary significantly with the charge density, hydration enthalpy, and solvation rigidity of different ions. For instance, Mg2+ requires aggressive chelation and pH buffering to overcome its strongly bound hydration shell, whereas K+ demands structural confinement to suppress isotropic diffusion and minimize interfacial accumulation. In contrast, Li+ and Na+ systems must manage metastable SEI formation in parallel with temperature‐sensitive desolvation kinetics and pronounced interfacial reactivity.

Effect of Polymer Electrolyte on Cathode for Other ARMBs

While ion‐specific polymer design strategies have advanced stabilization of anode interfaces in aqueous Li+, Na+, K+, and Mg2+ batteries, the cathode side presents a complementary yet distinct set of challenges, particularly under temperature extremes. Unlike Zn2+‐based systems, where cathode instability typically arises from rapid proton co‐insertion and structural collapse, non‐Zn systems must contend with ion–host mismatch, transition‐metal valence instability, and kinetically limited redox reactions, all of which are exacerbated at both high and LT. At HT, Li+ and Na+ intercalation cathodes such as LiMn2O4 and Na3V2(PO4)3 suffer from increased transition‐metal dissolution, OER, and irreversible phase transitions induced by thermal expansion and lattice distortion. In K+ and Mg2+ systems, the large ionic radius of K+ and the high charge density of Mg2+ impose severe host lattice strain or hinder solid‐state diffusion, effects further intensified by the limited water coordination flexibility of these ions under non‐ambient conditions.[ 139 ] Conversely, LT operation introduces viscosity‐driven mass transport limitations, enhances parasitic H+ involvement, and promotes interfacial passivation by hydrated intermediates, collectively impairing cathode kinetics and cycling stability.[ 40 ] When rationally engineered for cathode systems, polymer electrolytes can mitigate these degradation pathways through multimodal control of solvation structure, phase compatibility, and redox interfacial stability. Functional groups embedded within the polymer matrix (e.g., sulfonates, ether linkages, and zwitterions) not only tune the local dielectric environment but also buffer transition‐metal valence states and spatially confine water molecules, thereby suppressing side reactions. In addition, topologically reinforced hydrogels and flame‐retardant polymer matrices offer thermal and mechanical protection against electrolyte decomposition and interface delamination. This section focuses on the role of polymer electrolytes in enabling cathode‐side thermal adaptability in aqueous batteries beyond Zn, with emphasis on ion‐specific redox stabilization, local solvation tuning, and interfacial reinforcement mechanisms in Li+, Na+, K+, and Mg2+ systems.

In the context of cathode‐side degradation, Deng et al. reported a zwitterionic polymer electrolyte (OPDP) that significantly stabilizes high‐voltage LiMn2O4 (LMO) cathodes in ALIBs (Figure 13a). The polymer incorporates sulfonate (‐SO3 −) and quaternary ammonium (‐N+R4) groups, which dynamically regulate the local solvation structure and redox environment at the cathode interface. These functional moieties coordinate with Li+ and water molecules to form a polymer‐dominated hydration shell that confines water, suppresses its reactivity, and thereby mitigates parasitic processes such as OER on the anode side. Crucially, zwitterionic architecture promotes the in situ formation of a dual‐layered CEI, consisting of an inner inorganic‐rich layer (e.g., LiF, MnOx) and an outer organosulfur‐rich layer, as confirmed by spectroscopic analysis (Figure 13b). This tailored CEI inhibits Mn2+ dissolution, a primary contributor to irreversible capacity loss at HT, while preserving the spinel lattice integrity by preventing acid‐driven disproportionation of Mn3+. In addition, the sulfonate groups act as local proton scavengers, buffering pH fluctuations during Zn2+/Li+ insertion–extraction cycles that would otherwise destabilize the cathode surface. As a result, full cells employing this system demonstrated exceptional long‐term cycling stability at high C rates and under extreme temperature conditions, underscoring the importance of molecular‐scale interfacial control at the cathode in complementing bulk electrolyte design to achieve both thermal resilience and electrochemical longevity in ALIBs.[ 159 ]

In addition, Jung et al. introduced a boron‐stabilized anisotropic polyvinyl alcohol hydrogel (BaP) as a novel water‐in‐polymer salt (WiPS) electrolyte for ALIBs (Figure 13c). Exploiting the Hofmeister effect, this system simultaneously leveraged Li+‐induced salting‐in and TFSI−‐induced salting‐out phenomena to concentrate lithium ions within the polymer matrix, even at low external salt concentrations. The abundant hydroxyl groups of PVA, together with boron‐induced electrostatic interaction sites, provided strong Li+ coordination, thereby confining free water molecules and effectively expanding the ESW to 3.09 V (Figure 13d). This interfacial modulation suppressed parasitic reactions such as HER and transition‐metal dissolution, minimizing cathode degradation and enabling stable cycling of LiMn2O4‖Li4Ti5O12 full cells for over 200 cycles at 0.5 C. Furthermore, directional freezing produced a vertically aligned hydrogel architecture that enhanced ionic conductivity and promoted homogeneous ion flux at both electrode interfaces, contributing to improved charge‐transfer kinetics. Although this system did not explicitly address thermal adaptability, it clearly demonstrates an interfacial stabilization strategy through solvation‐structure regulation and polymer/ion coordination, underscoring the critical role of polymer electrolyte design in enabling stable ALIB operation.[ 165 ]

While many strategies for improving cathode stability in aqueous lithium, sodium, potassium, and magnesium systems share superficial similarities with those developed for AZIBs, such as hydrogen‐bond regulation, CEI stabilization, and suppression of transition‐metal dissolution, the underlying degradation mechanisms and electrochemical requirements are fundamentally distinct. Zn cathodes typically suffer from structural collapse and disproportionation reactions driven by H+ co‐insertion or local acidification.[ 166 ] By contrast, cathodes in non‐Zn aqueous systems encounter a broader spectrum of challenges, including asymmetric redox potentials, phase transitions, and ion‐specific interfacial reactions, which are further exacerbated under thermal extremes. The higher operating voltages and broader redox windows of cathode materials such as LiMn2O4, Na3V2(PO4)3, Prussian Blue analogs, and organic compounds demand polymer electrolytes capable of functions beyond conventional pH buffering or transition‐metal coordination. Specifically, these systems require electrolytes that actively restructure the solvation shell, suppress competing side reactions such as oxygen evolution, and stabilize ion‐transport pathways across varying temperatures. Such interfacial functionalities are particularly critical for cathode stability, where redox asymmetry and structural fragility are aggravated by interfacial pH fluctuations, ion accumulation, and temperature‐induced diffusion imbalances. Therefore, future polymer electrolyte design should simultaneously account for the unique physicochemical constraints of aqueous Li+, Na+, K+, and Mg2+ systems, while addressing them through ion‐specific, customized strategies:

1) Li+ systems require polymer frameworks that stabilize high‐voltage cathodes and suppress OER at HT, while maintaining conductivity at LT. Multifunctional polymers incorporating zwitterionic units and fluorinated moieties are particularly promising, as they can regulate Li+ solvation shells, promote the self‐formation of dual‐layer CEI, and suppress metal dissolution across a wide temperature range. 2) Na+ systems face sluggish ion transport and unstable SEIs at LT. To address this, polymer electrolytes should employ flexible backbones such as PVA or PEO integrated with ion‐conductive nanostructures that dynamically regulate hydration shells. Such designs accelerate Na+ transport at LT and enable thin, uniform SEI formation at HT, thereby ensuring both structural stability and redox reversibility. 3) K+ systems are challenged by large ionic radii and diffuse hydration shells, necessitating polymer electrolytes that impose geometric confinement and directional ion‐transport pathways. Future designs should integrate aligned nanochannels or multilayered architectures into polymer matrices to suppress isotropic diffusion, prevent interfacial accumulation, and ensure uniform deposition during temperature cycling. 4) Mg2+ systems are the most demanding due to their high charge density and rigid solvation shells. Polymer electrolytes should combine strong chelating functional groups (─COO−, ─SO3 −) with local pH‐buffering functionalities to reduce desolvation barriers and stabilize reversible ion transport. In addition, flexible yet thermally robust backbones such as PVDF‐HFP should be engineered to preserve conductivity at LT while enabling stable CEI formation at HT.

Conclusion and Future Perspectives

As highlighted above, the incorporation of polymer materials enhances the thermal stability of electrolytes, making them highly practical and advantageous for the development of next‐generation ARMBs (Figure 14 ). This review has discussed fundamental design principles, emphasizing the relationship between functional groups and temperature adaptability in polymer‐based aqueous electrolytes, while also examining how electrolyte properties evolve under temperature fluctuations and how interfacial electrochemical degradation mechanisms arise at electrodes. Despite recent progress, further investigation is essential to establish a deeper understanding of the design, fabrication, and property‐tuning principles of polymer electrolytes for thermally adaptive ARMBs. Most polymer matrices are inherently static and optimized for narrow temperature windows, whereas current studies have primarily focused on empirical structure–property relationships. This underscores the need for insights into how molecular‐level design, such as the arrangement of functional groups and backbone architectures, directly and quantitatively correlates with ion mobility and interfacial stability under thermal stress. Future development should therefore be guided by multiscale design strategies that integrate molecular structures, network architectures, and mesoscale ion‐transport pathways, complemented by iterative optimization approaches enabled by machine learning. In parallel, the adoption of operando analytical tools to monitor in situ polymer/electrode interfacial dynamics during thermal cycling will be indispensable. Such integrated perspectives will accelerate the creation of next‐generation polymer electrolytes that combine chemical robustness and thermal stability with dynamic self‐regulation. For the continued advancement of ARMB technologies, polymer electrolytes must ultimately be redefined as multifunctional platforms equipped with precise interfacial control. The following section outlines several potential research directions for polymer electrolytes in ARMBs:

Schematic illustration of application types for polymer electrolytes‐based ARMBs.

1) The development of advanced qualitative and quantitative analytical techniques is indispensable for elucidating the dynamic behavior and structural interactions among polymers, electrodes, and ions under extreme temperature conditions. In situ and operando approaches are particularly valuable for probing interfacial reaction kinetics and degradation mechanisms at the molecular level. 2) Improving the performance of polymer electrolytes requires ion‐specific design strategies supported by clear performance evaluation methods. Because multivalent ions differ in size in aqueous environments, hydration energies, and surface affinities, a universal design is not feasible; instead, polymer structures must be tailored for each ion type. To demonstrate the benefits of such tailoring, it is necessary to move beyond conventional metrics such as ionic conductivity and electrochemical stability windows and employ methods that capture complex surface processes. Future research should therefore integrate broad thermodynamic and kinetic measures, including ion‐surface adhesion, charge transport resistance, and structural stability. These measures will enable a direct, stepwise correlation between tailored polymer designs and their practical electrochemical performance. 3) Polymer electrolyte design involves numerous interrelated variables, including solvent–salt combinations, ion interactions, and interfacial reactions, which render conventional experimentation highly time‐ and labor‐intensive. The challenge is further amplified under extreme temperature conditions, where achieving rapid ion transport while suppressing parasitic reactions requires managing complex kinetic processes. Machine learning–based optimization has therefore become a key tool in polymer electrolyte development,[ 167 , 168 ] enabling rapid identification of optimal conditions with minimal experimentation and expanding the design space beyond traditional limits.[ 169 , 170 ] When integrated with experimental design tools, machine learning allows efficient exploration of wide parameter spaces and facilitates autonomous optimization, making it an indispensable strategy for the systematic development of refined polymer electrolytes.[ 171 , 172 , 173 , 174 ]

In conclusion, ARMBs represent a promising next‐generation solution for grid‐scale energy storage, with polymers serving as key enablers of cost efficiency, scalability, energy density, and cycling stability. A comprehensive understanding of ion–polymer–electrolyte interactions under extreme conditions is essential, and this review provides guiding principles for molecular design and interfacial control. These insights establish the groundwork for practical implementation and the future development of high‐performance ARMB systems.
