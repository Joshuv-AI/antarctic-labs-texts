A Transient Phenolate Enables Relaxation of Z‐Tetra‐ortho‐fluoroazobenzene to the Stable State in Seconds
Authors: William Brittain, Scott Barrett, Len Mouer, Nathalie Sauceda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTetra‐ortho‐fluoroazobenzenes (TFAs) exhibit several unique properties, including a large separation in the n→π* absorption bands, which allows for efficient photoisomerization using green light, and exceptional thermal stability of their metastable Z‐isomer. These features make them attractive for light‐responsive applications, but controlling the thermal Z‐to‐E isomerization has remained a challenge. In this work, we present a hybrid molecular system that couples a TFA subunit with a spiropyran photoswitch. The TFA subunit retains its ability to undergo selective photoisomerization with green light. Z‐to‐E isomerization in the hybrid occurs approximately 1000 times faster than in a TFA model compound. This acceleration arises from a rate‐determining step involving the formation of merocyanine—the ring‐opened form of spiropyran. The transient phenolate form of merocyanine promotes rapid Z‐to‐E isomerization of the TFA, consistent with the known behavior of azobenzene phenolates. The thermodynamics of TFA isomerization are coupled with those of the spiropyran ring‐opening, allowing the isomerization process to be regulated by external stimuli that influence the spiropyran–merocyanine equilibrium. In addition to observing a 100–1000‐fold solvent‐dependent rate increase, we demonstrate a 106‐fold acceleration in TFA isomerization via either photochromism or adsorptiochromism.Keywords: Fluoroazobenzene, Merocyanine, Phenolate, Solvatochromic, Spiropyran

Introduction

The continued interest in azobenzene (AB) derivatives is evident from the growing number of publications in this area, underscoring their significance as a versatile class of organic photochromic compounds.[ 1 , 2 ] Among these, tetra‐ortho‐substituted ABs—particularly those bearing fluorine, chlorine, or methoxy groups—have attracted attention for their ability to undergo photoisomerization using only visible light.[ 3 ] Within this subclass, tetra‐ortho‐fluoroazobenzenes (TFAs) are notable for their pronounced n–π* band separation and the exceptional thermal stability of their Z‐isomers. A representative example, 1,2‐bis(2,6‐difluorophenyl)diazene (1a, Scheme 1), demonstrates a 42 nm blue shift in the Z‐isomer's n‐π* transition relative to the E‐isomer, allowing selective photoisomerization at wavelengths above 500 nm. The resulting Z‐isomer exhibits a remarkably long thermal half‐life of approximately 700 days.[ 4 ] Despite these advantageous properties, a significant limitation of TFAs is the lack of additional strategies to modulate the rate of Z‐to‐E isomerization beyond electrocatalytic isomerization.[ 5 ] Such control is critical for applications in photopharmacology[ 6 ]—where rapid deactivation is often necessary—and molecular solar thermal (MOST) energy storage systems,[ 7 ] which require on‐demand release of stored energy. The molecules reported here are intended as proof‐of‐concept and are not directly suitable for MOST or photopharmacology applications due to significant structural modifications to AB. For MOST systems, a key figure of merit is specific energy density, which is reduced by the added molecular mass. The specific energy density for compound 1d is 180 J g−1 (Figure S1) compared to 260 J g−1 for parent azobenzene, and a further decrease in energy density is expected for 2a. For the ongoing research in photopharmacology, specific binding motifs are crucial, and the hybrid structures presented here are incompatible with the current binding structure optimized for AB.

2,2′,6,6′‐Tetrafluoroazobenzene (TFA) compounds.

Among non‐heteroaromatic azobenzene derivatives, 4‐hydroxyazobenzene (azophenol) is known to undergo the fastest thermal Z‐to‐E isomerization.[ 8 , 9 ] This compound exists as a mixture of keto and enol tautomers, with isomerization rates strongly influenced by solvent environment: ns relaxation in aqueous phosphate buffer and ms in toluene. The observed solvent‐dependence of the pressure activation volume (ΔVǂ) suggests a rotational isomerization pathway.[ 10 ] However, due to its rapid thermal relaxation, azophenol cannot accumulate a significant Z‐isomer population under continuous irradiation. Inspired by this behavior, we hypothesized that TFA isomerization could be accelerated via a transient phenolate intermediate, generated through the spiropyran (SP)–merocyanine (MC) equilibrium. Hybrid molecules combining SP and AB subunits have been reported,[ 11 , 12 , 13 ] but they were not explored to accelerate Z‐to‐E isomerization. Results

Synthesis and photochemistry

To evaluate whether the transient phenolate 2b can accelerate the Z‐to‐E isomerization of a TFA subunit, we synthesized the SP–AB hybrid compound 2a (Scheme 2). The synthesis proceeded in two steps: (1) azo coupling of the diazonium salt of 2,6‐difluoroaniline with 2,4‐difluoro‐6‐hydroxybenzaldehyde to afford a tetrafluoro‐substituted AB intermediate, and (2) condensation of the resulting salicylaldehyde with 1,3,3‐trimethyl‐2‐methyleneindoline to form SP 2a.

The photophysical properties of 2a were examined by UV‐Vis spectroscopy and compared to those of the model AB 1d and a model SP compound, 1′,3′‐dihydro‐1′,3′,3′‐trimethyl‐6‐nitrospiro[2H‐1‐benzopyran‐2,2′‐[2H]indole] (nitroSP). All spectra in Figure 1 were recorded at a concentration of 8.5 × 10−5 M in toluene. The UV–Vis absorption characteristics of 2a is a composite of the model compounds. Figure 1 also shows the spectrum of 2b, which is calculated by adjusting the spectrum of the photostationary state (PSS) for 2a present. The UV‐Vis absorption profile of 2a in DMSO and methanol is similar to that in toluene (Figure S2), although higher MC content was observed in polar solvents, consistent with stabilization of the open MC form. Irradiation of 2a into toluene with 520 nm light selectively induced E‐to‐Z isomerization of the TFA subunit, evidenced by the growth of the n–π* absorption band corresponding to 2b (Figure 2). The selective isomerization of the AB unit was confirmed by 19F NMR spectroscopy following green light irradiation. In contrast, irradiation with 365 nm light resulted in photoisomerization of both AB and SP subunits as evidenced by concomitant increases in absorption at 425 nm for 2b and at 530 nm for the MC form of the SP moiety. The kinetics of E‐to‐Z photoisomerization under continuous 520 nm irradiation for 2a were monitored by UV–Vis spectroscopy in both toluene and methanol. Identical first‐order rate constants were obtained in both solvents: k₁ (λ = 520 nm) = 0.04 ± 0.01 s−1. PSS compositions were determined by 19F NMR analysis of UV–Vis samples after extended irradiation until spectral changes plateaued. The resulting Z:E ratios at PSS were: 88:12 in methanol, 86:14 in toluene, and 89:11 in DMSO.

Spiropyran‐Merocyanine Equilibrium Analysis

The temperature dependence of the SP–MC equilibrium for 2a was quantified using Van't Hoff plots in toluene, methanol, ethanol, and DMSO (Table S1, Figure S3). For comparison, the equilibrium constant (K eq) was also measured for the commercially available nitro‐substituted SP (nitroSP) under identical conditions (Table 1). Equilibrium concentrations were determined by UV–Vis and NMR spectroscopy after samples were kept in the dark for 8 h at the target temperature. Molar absorptivity values for the MC form of 2a were obtained by correlating UV–Vis absorbance data with 19F NMR integration. There is good agreement between literature averages and our measurements of K eq for nitroSP. As anticipated, K eq increased with solvent polarity due to stabilization of the zwitterionic MC form. However, the consistently higher K eq values observed for 2a compared to nitroSP across all solvents were unexpected and suggest an intrinsic stabilization of the MC form in the hybrid architecture.

Isomerization Kinetics

The thermal Z‐to‐E isomerization rate constant (k ZE) was determined using both UV–Vis spectroscopy and 19F NMR following 30 s of irradiation with 520 nm light. The use of 19F NMR provided the advantage of simultaneously monitoring the concentrations of 2a, 2b, and 2d (Figure 3). Distinct 19F chemical shifts were observed for the SP forms 2a and 2b, while only a single MC species was detected, which we assign to 2d (Table S3 lists all calibrated 19F NMR signals in four solvents). As seen in Figure 3 inset, the concentration of MC did not vary significantly during AB isomerization. Thermodynamic parameters for the thermal isomerization of 2b and the model compound 1d were extracted from kinetic data and are summarized in Table 2. These values support our hypothesis that the SP–MC manifold can modulate the stability of the TFA Z‐isomer and provides a route to externally control its reversion kinetics. This data also confirms the solvent‐dependent effect for 2a, which is absent for 1d. Eyring analysis of Z‐to‐E thermal isomerization kinetics (Table S2, Figures S4–S7) was performed to compare the entropy of activation to the calculated values reported by Hecht and Kaupp.[ 22 ] Negative ΔS ‡ values were observed for compound 1d in all solvents and for 2a in toluene and DMSO. In contrast, positive ΔS ‡ values were obtained for 2a in methanol and ethanol. Negative ΔS ‡ values indicate a type II rotation mechanism, while positive values suggest an inversion mechanism.

Merocyanine Structure

To confirm the MC structure, 13C NMR spectroscopy was used to probe the electronic nature of the MC intermediate; in particular, the chemical shift of the phenolate carbon is diagnostic of the amount of double bond character in the C─O phenolate bond. The 13C shift for the phenolate carbon in model compound 1c is 174.1 ppm in toluene. For 2a in methanol, we observed a shift of 182.6 ppm for the phenolate carbon, which is similar to the shift of 182.4 ppm reported for the MC isomer of 1′,3′,3′‐trimethyl‐6,8‐dinitrospiro[chromene‐2,2′‐indoline].[ 23 ] This downfield peak was not observed nor expected in the 13C NMR spectrum of 2a in toluene due to the low concentration of MC.(Figure S8). The downfield shift for the phenolate carbon of 2a relative to 1c shows the additional electronic stabilization present in the hybrid structure.

Model Compound Studies

The photochemistry of model compounds 1b and 1c was studied by UV–Vis. Phenol compound 1b behaved similarly to the literature results.[ 8 ] To better model the transient phenolate species, 2c, we synthesized the tetrabutylammonium (TBA) phenolate, 1c, by reaction of 1b with TBA hydroxide. The UV–Vis spectrum of 1c displayed a red‐shifted absorption compared to 1b, with a peak maximum at 415 nm (Figure S9), consistent with phenolate formation and extended conjugation. Notably, the UV–Vis spectrum of 1c remained unchanged after 30 min of irradiation at 270, 365, 385, or 520 nm in both toluene and methanol. The apparent photostability suggests that the phenolate photoisomerization kinetics do not lead to a significant Z‐isomer population in contrast to its phenol counterpart and the behavior observed for hybrid compound 2a.

Chromic Acceleration of Z‐to‐E Isomerization

We discovered that the Z‐to‐E isomerization of the TFA unit in 2b could be significantly accelerated either by UV irradiation at 385 nm or upon contact with silica gel. These chromic responses were monitored by UV–Vis spectroscopy using a toluene solution of 2b, which was generated by 520 nm irradiation of 2a. Under 385 nm irradiation (0.04 mW cm−1), the photoinduced Z‐to‐E isomerization occurred with a rate constant of 0.045 ± 0.01 s−1 in both toluene and methanol. This rate represents an increase of 106 compared to model compound 1d. To evaluate solid‐phase‐induced isomerization, we coupled a 5 mm × 20 mm glass tube packed with 60 µm silica gel to a syringe pump and a flow‐through UV–Vis cuvette for real‐time absorbance monitoring. Figure 4 displays UV–Vis spectra of 2b as a function of flow rate through the silica‐packed column. The absorbance ratio of 345 nm (π–π*) to 425 nm (n–π* ) showed a linear correlation with the E/Z ratio for quantitative data analysis. The residence time in the system was defined as the total dead volume (cuvette, tubing, and silica‐packed column) divided by the flow rate. Analysis of conversion versus residence time (inset, Figure 4) showed that 95% Z‐to‐E isomerization occurred within 120 s. First‐order kinetic analysis of the initial conversion gives a rate constant, k ZE = 0.025 s−1. These findings suggest that silica gel can act as a heterogeneous stimulus to trigger hybrid isomerization, offering a novel platform for flow‐based isomerization to the AB stable state. An additional experiment was performed using silica that had been neutralized by treating it with two column volumes of a 1 mM triethylamine solution in toluene, followed by one column volume of pure toluene. The isomerization kinetics remained largely unchanged, suggesting that adsorptive catalysis is the predominant mechanism. Ongoing studies are aimed at defining the scope and mechanistic basis of this silica‐induced process.

Discussion

The isomerization of SP to MC proceeds through a ring‐opening process that can be represented by two resonance forms: a phenolate zwitterion (arising from heterolytic cleavage of the Cspiro─O bond) and a quinoidal structure (resulting from a formal 6π electrocyclic ring‐opening).[ 24 , 25 ] Evidence for these dual pathways is drawn from Hammett correlations of racemization rates in chiral SPs[ 26 ] and the solvent‐dependent appearance of two isomerization rates—attributed to a slow (quinoidal) and a fast (zwitterionic) pathway.[ 21 , 27 ] The relative contributions of these forms are strongly influenced by solvent polarity, with polar solvents stabilizing the zwitterionic structure. Compared to the relatively subtle conformational and electronic differences between E/Z AB isomers, SP and its MC form display pronounced differences in polarity, conjugation, and reactivity.[ 25 , 28 ] The SP–MC equilibrium is modulated by a wide range of external stimuli, including temperature,[ 29 ] solvent polarity,[ 20 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 ] metal ions,[ 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 ] pH,[ 39 , 40 , 41 ] mechanical force,[ 42 ] redox potential,[ 43 , 44 ] and microenvironment polarity.[ 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 ] Of these, AB photoisomerization is primarily responsive to temperature, pH,[ 49 , 50 ] mechanical force,[ 51 ] redox potential,[ 5 , 52 ] and metal ions.[ 53 ]

A central observation in this work is the accelerated Z‐to‐E isomerization of the TFA unit when incorporated into the hybrid spiropyran framework 2a. Our results support a mechanism in which the transient phenolate form of the MC (2c) undergoes rapid isomerization of the TFA moiety. This interpretation is supported by several lines of evidence:

These findings point to a kinetic model where MC formation (k 2) is the rate‐determining step, followed by rapid Z‐to‐E isomerization (k 3), such that k 3 ≫ k 2 ≫ k₋₁ (Scheme 2). Under this framework, the effective rate of AB isomerization is governed by stimuli that influence MC concentration. The solvatochromic behavior observed for 2a reflects this sensitivity, as MC formation and stabilization are enhanced in polar solvents. Table 3 presents kinetic data comparing 2a to nitroSP, where literature values for MC decoloration (k −2) were averaged for comparison. Much of the SP literature uses the terminology of coloration/decoloration to refer to the SP‐MC equilibrium. To enable a direct comparison, we calculated k −2 for 2a from experimental K eq and k 2 values. The correspondence in activation parameters between 2a and nitroSP strongly supports our proposed mechanism of MC‐mediated control over TFA isomerization. The sole discrepancy was a lower activation energy in DMSO, the cause of which remains unclear, but may involve specific solvation effects. Scheme 2 represents a simplified mechanistic model that does not account for potential MC aggregation, which has been reported in non‐polar solvents, particularly at high concentrations or in condensed phases such as polymer films or solids.[ 25 ] However, we found no evidence of MC aggregation in our system. Specifically, the characteristic, blue‐shifted MC absorption band reported by Flannery[ 15 ] for nitroSP in hydrocarbon solvents above 0.1 M was not observed in the UV‐Vis spectra of 2a. Moreover, the Z‐to‐E isomerization rate of 2b was found to be independent of concentration over the range of 0.001–0.1 M in toluene, further indicating that aggregation is not a significant factor under our experimental conditions. Scheme 2 also depicts a single MC isomer, though up to eight geometric isomers are theoretically possible due to E/Z configurations at the α, β, and γ double bonds (see Scheme 3). Although the CCC isomer is formed initially upon SP ring‐opening, it rapidly rearranges—on the ms timescale—to the most stable TTC isomer.[ 56 ] Given that the rate of Z‐to‐E isomerization (k 3) is considerably faster than the isomerization among MC geometries, we propose that MC isomer equilibrium does not affect the observed TFA isomerization kinetics.

The unexpectedly high K eq value for 2a, relative to nitroSP, cannot be explained solely based on Hammett parameters. Swansburg and co‐workers[ 26 ] investigated the thermal racemization of 6‐substituted indolinobenzospiropyrans and found that the rate of MC formation correlated with σp. The AB substituent in 2a has a σp value of 0.39,[ 57 ] like that of CO2Ph (0.44), a group studied in their system. The MC formation rate for the 6‐CO2Ph compound was six times lower than that of the 6‐nitro analog—contrary to what would be required to rationalize the higher K eq observed for 2a. Additionally, we found[ 58 ] that an analog of 2a bearing only two fluorine atoms at the ortho positions exhibited K eq values comparable to nitroSP, supporting the idea that the tetra‐ortho substitution is crucial for enhancing MC stability.

To demonstrate that known SP–MC equilibrium stimuli can also modulate TFA isomerization in the hybrid system, we investigated UV‐light acceleration and silica treatment as proof‐of‐concept strategies in toluene solutions. These results reinforce the idea that MC concentration governs the rate of TFA Z‐to‐E isomerization. While this work focused on solution‐phase behavior, ongoing efforts aim to expand this framework to a broader set of solvents, liquid ABs,[ 59 ] and related hemiaminal structures. Compound 1d serves as an effective model for the AB subunit of 2a, as the ortho substitution adjacent to the phenolate group has minimal influence—evident from the nearly identical isomerization rates observed for m‐acetylAB and the unsubstituted AB.[ 60 ] Compared to the parent TFA compound 1d, the hybrid molecule 2a demonstrates a novel coupling of SP–MC stimuli and AB isomerization, laying the groundwork for multi‐stimuli‐responsive materials. Current work is focused on testing the generality of the silica‐induc switching concept, quantifying its limits and potential in a broader class of photoresponsive systems, and transient absorption spectroscopy of the phenolate.

Conclusion

In a hybrid molecule containing SP and TFA subunits, the rate of Z‐to‐E isomerization increases by a factor of 102–106, driven by a transient phenolate intermediate formed upon SP ring‐opening to the MC isomer. The rate‐determining step for TFA isomerization is MC formation, enabling acceleration of the return to the TFA stable state via stimuli known to modulate the SP–MC equilibrium. We have demonstrated that solvent polarity, microenvironmental polarity (silica), temperature, and UV irradiation significantly affect the TFA isomerization rate. This mechanistic approach—leveraging a transient phenolate intermediate—is a novel strategy in the field of AB dynamics and provides a systematic means of controlling Z‐to‐E isomerization in this class of photochromic systems.
