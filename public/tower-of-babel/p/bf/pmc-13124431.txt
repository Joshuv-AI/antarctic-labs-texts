Causal relationship between circulating immune cells, cytokines and different types of autoimmune liver diseases: A univariable and multivariable Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Chunli Li, Qilong Zhai, Hongyu Wu, Changjie Du, Jiajun Yuan, Jialun Peng, Yu You, Jinzheng Li, Tao Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autoimmune liver diseases (AILDs) are a group of liver diseases characterized by immune dysfunction. Recent studies have proposed a potential correlation among immune cells, cytokines, and AILDs. However, the causal nature of this relationship remains to be elucidated. Summary-level Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) statistical data for primary biliary cholangitis and primary sclerosing cholangitis were obtained from FinnGen, while autoimmune hepatitis and immune trait statistics were sourced from the GWAS Catalog. GWAS data for 41 inflammatory cytokines were acquired from the University of Bristol. The inverse-variance weighted method was the primary Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis method, with sensitivity and inverse MR analyses performed to assess result stability. Under the premise of satisfying the MR hypothesis, our analysis supported the existence of potential causal relationships between different immunophenotypes, cytokines, and AILDs. 10 immune cell phenotypes and 7 cytokines with potential causal relationships with AILDs were identified. For different types of AILDs, 4 immune phenotypes and 4 cytokines were found to be associated with primary biliary cholangitis, 4 immune phenotypes and 2 immune factors were associated with primary sclerosing cholangitis, and 3 immune phenotypes and 1 cytokine were associated with autoimmune hepatitis. However, we did not find any evidence of reverse causality between immunophenotypes, cytokines, and AILDs. MR analysis found a potential causal relationship between several immune phenotypes, cytokines, and AILDs, suggesting differences in immune responses across various types of AILDs.

1. Introduction

Autoimmune liver diseases (AILDs) are a group of immune dysfunction liver diseases caused by a special type of chronic inflammation, including autoimmune hepatitis (AIH), primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), and primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC). Different types of AILDs are characterized by different degrees of liver damage, and persistent chronic inflammation of the liver can lead to cirrhosis, which in turn increases the risk of liver cancer.[1] Epidemiological studies have shown that the incidence of different types of AILDs varies in different regions of the world, but is generally on the rise.[2] The pathogenesis of AILDs is complex and unclear, with viral infections, physicochemical factors, and genetic factors being closely related to AILDs. However, immune dysfunction is a key pathway common to many causes of AILDs.[1]

Immune cells play the role of recognizing and removing foreign substances, including neutrophils, macrophages, lymphocytes, and so on. The liver is rich in immune cells, and dynamic interactions between multiple immune cell populations maintain liver health.[3,4] Dysfunction of immune cells plays an important role in the development of AILDs.[5] Specific deletion of AMP-activated protein kinase alpha 1 in regulatory T cells (Tregs) leads to autoimmune disease.[6] Researchers have found differential expression of aryl hydrocarbon receptors in Treg and Th17 cells in the peripheral blood of AIH patients.[7] In addition, the role of antigen-presenting cells in AILDs is summarized in a review by Horst AK et al They found that different immune cells play different roles in the pathogenesis of AILDs.[8] Cytokines are a group of small-molecule proteins released by cells, playing critical roles in immune regulation. They have complex relationships with the development and progression of AILDs.[9] Compared with the control group, patients with AIH showed significantly higher levels of the cytokines interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-8 (IL-8), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) in their peripheral blood.[10] Similarly, increased levels of interleukin-23 (IL-23) and interleukin-17 (IL-17) were found in the peripheral blood of patients with PBC.[11] However, the relationship between immune cells, cytokines, and AILDs is complex, and the results of the studies on them are not the same, which may be due to the limited sample sizes and the influence of confounding factors.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a causal inference method based on genetic variation analysis. Due to the independent and random assignment principle of Mendel’s laws, MR allows causal analyses to be performed using large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) data by avoiding the confounding factors and reverse causality of traditional studies.[12,13] In this study, we performed comprehensive bidirectional two-sample MR analysis and multivariate MR analysis to determine the potential causal relationship between immune cell characteristics, cytokines and different types of AILDs.

2. Methods

In this study, we used univariable two-sample Mendelian randomization and multivariable Mendelian randomization (MVMR) techniques to explore the causal links between 731 immune cell signatures (grouped into 7 categories), 41 cytokines and AILDs (including PBC, PSC, AIH). For this study, we used summary statistics from GWAS. To ensure the precision and reliability of our findings, we rigorously adhered to the 3 fundamental assumptions of Mendelian randomization (Figure 1), which states that instrumental variables (IVs) are only associated with exposure and not with confounders and outcomes.[14]

Overall design and assumption of this Mendelian randomization between circulating immune cells, cytokines, and AILDs. Dash lines with a cross means that the association meets 2 basic assumptions of Mendelian randomization: the genetic variants are independent of confounders between exposure and outcomes, and the genetic variants only influence the outcome via exposure. 2.1. Data sources

2.1.1. GWAS data sources for AILDs

In this investigation, we utilized GWAS summary statistics for PBC and PSC, obtained from the publicly accessible FinnGen Consortium R9 release data (https://r9.risteys.finngen.fi) as of May 2023. Furthermore, the GWAS summary statistics for AIH were sourced from the GWAS Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/). The PBC GWAS summary statistics were derived from a Finnish adult population of 281,684 individuals, 557 of whom were cases and 281,127 were controls. The GWAS summary statistics for PSC incorporated data from 1715 cases and 330,903 controls. For AIH, the GWAS summary statistics were derived from an adult cohort of 651,848 individuals, comprising 906 cases and 650,942 controls.

2.1.2. Immunity-wide and inflammatory cytokines GWAS data sources

In the field of immunogenetics, a comprehensive set of GWAS summary statistics about various immune traits has been made accessible to the public via the GWAS Catalog, with identifiers ranging from GCST0001391–GCST0002121.[15] The dataset encompasses a broad spectrum of 731 immunophenotypes, which can be divided into 4 distinct categories: absolute cell counts (n = 118), median fluorescence intensities indicative of surface antigen levels (n = 389), morphological parameters (n = 32), and relative cell counts (n = 192). The median fluorescence intensities, absolute cell, and relative cell categories encompass a diverse range of immune cells, including B cells, conventional dendritic cells, T cells at mature stages, monocytes, myeloid cells, and panels of T cells, B cells, natural killer cells (TBNKs), and Tregs. In contrast, the morphological parameters category comprises panels of conventional dendritic cells and TBNKs. The initial GWAS study on these immune traits was conducted on a sample of 3757 individuals of European descent, with no cohort overlap.[15] The study employed approximately 22 million single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), which were genotyped using high-density arrays and imputed using a Sardinian sequence-based reference panel.

Data for 41 inflammatory cytokines were sourced from the University of Bristol (https://data.bris.ac.uk/data/dataset), which includes data from 3 Finnish cohort studies (N = 8293): the Cardiovascular Risk in Young Finns Study, FINRISK1997, and FINRISK2002.[16,17]

2.2. The selection of instrumental variables

In the context of MR studies, SNPs serve as IVs to represent both exposures and outcomes, thereby facilitating the exploration of potential causal relationships between them. The genome-wide significance level thresholds for exposure SNPs were set to P < 5 × 10−8 and without linkage disequilibrium (R2 ≥ 0.001 and within 10 mb). The F statistic was employed to assess the robustness of the selected IVs. IVs with F-statistics < 10 were deemed to be weak genetic instruments and were thus excluded from the study.[18] This rigorous approach ensures the validity of the MR analyses conducted in this study. Table S1–S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R731 provide detailed information about the instrumental SNPs used by AILDs in this study.

2.3. Statistical analysis

In the current investigation, we used 4 different MR methods – Inverse-variance weighted (IVW), weighted median (WM), MR-Egger, and Weighted Mode - to assess the causal influence of circulating immune cells on AILDs. Each of these methods makes unique assumptions about horizontal pleiotropy. Since the IVW method is a meta-analysis of the overall causal relationship between exposure and outcome, it is considered the primary analytical method for evaluating causality in MR analysis.[19] Cochran Q test and horizontal pleiotropy test were used to assess the heterogeneity of outcomes and horizontal pleiotropy. MR-PRESSO and the leave-one-out method were used to assess outlying SNPs and to examine the effect of individual SNPs on the overall outcome.[20] P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

The MVMR method, which can be applied irrespective of the association of multiple genetic instruments with exposure, was also used.[21] In this method, IVs can be associated with more than one risk factor, but they must satisfy the equivalent IV assumptions. Therefore, all immune cell IVs potentially causally related to AILDs were included in the application of the method to determine their independent effects on AILDs.

All computations were executed in R software (version 4.2.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the Two-Sample MR package and the MVMR package. Given the extensive number of immune cells included in this study, we applied Bonferroni and false discovery rate (FDR) corrections to the P-values to ensure the reliability of the results.[22] In this study, a causal relationship was considered to exist if P < 6.84 × 10−5 (0.05/731) or PFDR < .05. The MR estimates were presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) or β estimates with 95% CI. This rigorous approach ensures the robustness of the MR analyses conducted in this study.

3. Results

3.1. Causal effect between peripheral immune cells and PBC

After FDR adjustment (PFDR < .05), we detected protective effects of 2 immunophenotypes on PBC: HLA DR on CD14−CD16+ (monocyte panel) and HLA DR on CD14+CD16+ (monocyte panel). Specifically, by employing the IVW method, we estimated the OR of HLA DR on CD14−CD16+ influencing PBC risk to be 0.72 (95% CI = 0.61–0.86, P = 2.95 × 10−4, PFDR = .03). The results were similar when 3 additional methods were used. The OR of HLA DR on CD14+CD16+ on PBC risk was estimated to be 0.63 (95% CI = 0.53–0.76, P = 5.05 × 10−7, PFDR = 2.22 × 10−4) by using the IVW method. MR-Egger, weighted mode, and WM yielded similar results.

Additionally, we also detected the risk effects of 2 immunophenotypes on PBC: HLA DR+ CD4+ T cell (T cell panel) and Effector Memory CD8+ T cell (T cell panel). Specifically, we estimated the OR of HLA DR+ CD4+ T cell influencing PBC risk to be 2.92 (95% CI = 1.77–4.83, P = 3.01 × 10−5, PFDR = .007) by employing the IVW method. The OR of Effector Memory CD8+ T cell on PBC risk was estimated to be 1.74 (95% CI = 1.29–2.35, P = 2.77 × 10−4, PFDR = .03) by using the IVW method. The MVMR analysis showed statistically significant causal associations for genetically predicted HLA DR on CD14+CD16+ and Effector Memory CD8+ T cell on PBC. HLA DR on CD14+CD16+ had a significant negative causal effect on PBC, consistent with that observed in the forward univariable analysis (β = −0.61 ([−0.20–−1.00]; P = 3.14 × 10−3)). There was evidence of a significant positive causal effect of genetically predicted Effector Memory CD8+ T cell (β = 0.59 ([0.26–0.91]; P = 3.94 × 10−4)) and HLA DR+ CD4+ T cell on PBC (β = 1.02 ([0.52–1.53]; P = 6.57 × 10−5)). HLA DR on CD14−CD16+ was not significantly associated with PBC through MVMR analysis.

3.2. Causal effect between peripheral immune cells and PSC

Notably, our findings indicated a significant causal relationship between a higher liability of PSC and an elevated level of CD27 on switched memory B cells and CD24+ CD27+ B cells. The OR of CD27 on switched memory B cell on PSC risk was estimated to be 1.19 (95% CI = 1.09–1.31, P = 1.69 × 10−4, PFDR = .04) by using the IVW method. The OR of CD24+ CD27+ B cells on PSC risk was estimated to be 1.20 (95% CI = 1.08–1.32, P = 3.92 × 10−4, PFDR = .04) by using the IVW method. Similar results were observed by using 3 more methods.

Additionally, the study revealed that 2 immunophenotypes exhibited protective effects against the development of PSC: HLA DR on myeloid Dendritic Cell and Plasmacytoid Dendritic Cell. Specifically, by using the IVW method, the OR of HLA DR on myeloid Dendritic Cell on PSC risk was estimated to be 0.86 (95% CI = 0.79–0.93, P = 3.44 × 10−4, PFDR = .04). We obtained similar results when we used 3 additional methods. The OR of Plasmacytoid Dendritic Cell on PSC risk was estimated to be 0.75 (95% CI = 0.64–0.88, P = 3.70 × 10−4, PFDR = .04) by using the IVW method. 3.3. Causal effect between peripheral immune cells and AIH

After FDR adjustment (PFDR < .05), we detected protective effects of HLA DR+ natural killer (NK) on AIH. The OR of HLA DR+ NK on AIH risk was estimated to be 0.70 (95% CI = 0.59–0.84, P = 1.35 × 10−4, PFDR = .03) by using the IVW method. Weighted mode (OR = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.59–0.90, P = .02) and WM (OR = 0.74, 95% CI = 0.60–0.90, P = 2.60 × 10−2) yielded comparable results.

Additionally, we also detected risk effects of 2 immunophenotypes on AIH: CD28− CD8+ T cell and CD8+ % T cell. Specifically, by employing the IVW method, we estimated the OR of CD28− CD8+ T cells influencing PBC risk to be 2.35 (95% CI = 1.59–3.49, P = 2.11 × 10−5, PFDR = .01). Similar results were observed by using WM (OR = 2.34, 95% CI = 1.43–3.83, P = 6.79 × 10−4). The OR of CD8+ % T cell on AIH risk was estimated to be 2.20 (95% CI = 1.46–3.33, P = 1.80 × 10−4, PFDR = .03) by using the IVW method. Similar results were observed by using the WM method (OR = 2.15, 95% CI = 1.27–3.63, P = 4.36 × 10−3). 3.4. Causal effect between cytokines and AILDs

A causal relationship was evaluated between 41 cytokines and the 3 main types of AILDs. We discovered a potential causal relationship between 7 cytokines and AILDs. Interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels have been identified in PBC as a potential protective factor. The OR of IL-6 for PBC risk, as assessed by the IVW method, was 0.52 (95% CI = 0.28–0.97, P = .0408). Furthermore, these cytokines may also contribute to the occurrence and development of PBC, including hepatocyte growth factor levels (OR = 1.78, 95% CI = 1.08–2.92, P = .0237), Interleukin-2 (IL-2) levels (OR = 1.44, 95% CI = 1.07–1.94, P = .0171), and Interleukin-4 (IL-4) levels (OR = 1.87, 95% CI = 1.07–3.27, P = .0287). When PSC was the outcome, a potential causal relationship was observed between interferon gamma-induced protein 10 levels (OR = 0.72, 95% CI = 0.56–0.95, P = .019), TRAIL levels (OR = 0.86, 95% CI = 0.76–0.98, P = .020), and PSC. When cytokines were used as exposure for MR analysis, Stem cell growth factor beta levels (OR = 0.58, 95% CI = 0.39–0.87, P = .008) exhibited a causal relationship with AIH. Reverse MR analyses showed that PBC, PSC, and AIH do not causally affect peripheral immune cells and cytokines, suggesting that our results are not affected by reverse causality. 4. Discussion

Based on a large amount of publicly available genetic data, we explored causal associations among 731 immune cell traits, 41 cytokine traits, and different types of AILDs. To our knowledge, this study is the first Mendelian randomization analysis to systematically examine the causal relationships among diverse immune cell traits, cytokines, and AILDs. Under the premise of satisfying the Mendelian randomization hypothesis, based on IVW IV analysis, we found that immune cell phenotypes and cytokines differed in different types of AILDs.

It is now widely recognized that multiple immune processes are involved in PBC.[23] Several previous studies have demonstrated that CD4 on HLA DR+ CD4+ T cell and Effector Memory CD8+ T cell Absolute Count increase the risk of PBC, which is consistent with our findings.[24–26] In addition, we found HLA DR on CD14+ CD16+ monocytes and HLA DR on CD14−CD16+ monocytes to be protective factors for PBC by MR analysis. Previous studies have found that HLA DR expression decreases during inflammatory processes, and HLA DR on CD14+ monocytes also decreases during stressful inflammatory processes.[27,28] These findings suggest that HLA DR may play a protective role in inflammation. Zhang et al found CD25 on IgD- CD27 − B cells, CD27 on CD24 + CD27 + B cells, CD27 on IgD + CD24 + B cells, CD27 on IgD- CD38dim B cells, CD27 on unswitched memory B cells, and CD27 on switched memory B cells had a potential causal relationship with PBC, which differs from our results and may be due to differences in the data included in the population.[29] However, the results obtained by Mendelian randomization require further validation.

In PSC, we found that HLA DR on myeloid Dendritic Cell and Plasmacytoid Dendritic Cell are protective factors, while CD27 on switched memory B cell and CD24+ CD27+ B cell are risk factors. Previous studies have demonstrated that different molecular subtypes of HLA-DR correlate differently with PSC.[30] Dendritic cells (DC) are antigen-producing cells that mediate various immune and inflammatory responses and play an important role in the maintenance of immune tolerance.[31] Previous studies have confirmed the important role of T cells in the development of PSC.[32,33] In contrast, restricted antigen presentation by pDC suppresses T cell-mediated autoimmunity.[34] It has also been suggested that DC can produce type I IFN, which in turn induces KCs to produce anti-fibrotic IL-1ra and exert anti-fibrotic function.[35] However, there are fewer studies on DC and PSC, and further research is required. A switched memory B cell is a special kind of memory B cell.[36] Single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptome sequencing techniques have confirmed that a large number of B cells infiltrate into PSC livers.[37] Thapa et al found that BAFF blockade or B cells reduced the degree of hepatic fibrosis in mice.[38] The combined effect of B cells may contribute to the development of PSC, which is in agreement with our conclusion.

AIH has a complex immune environment, and immune cells are involved in the complex process of AIH pathogenesis.[39] Our study found that the risk of AIH is closely related to HLA DR+ NK, CD28−CD8+T cell, and CD8+ % T cell. Elevated HLA DR+ NK reduces the incidence of AIH, while CD28−CD8+ T cell and CD8+ % T cell are risk factors for AIH. It has been suggested that CD8+ T cells are the main infiltrating lymphoid type at the margin of active AIH.[40] Furthermore, in AIH, CD8+ T cells upregulate specific cellular mediators such as perforin and granzyme B, exacerbating AIH.[41] NK cells are an important component of innate immunity, and HLA DR+ NK cells are a distinct subtype of NK cells.[42] In our study, HLA DR+ NK was a protective factor against AIH. However, no direct evidence has suggested a potential relationship between them. Gut microbes are closely associated with the development of AIH, and it has been suggested that HLA DR+ NK can present staphylococcal enterotoxin B to T lymphocytes, which in turn clears the microbes.[43,44] Therefore, we hypothesize that HLA DR+ NK may affect the ecological niche of gut microbiota through the “liver-gut axis,” thereby influencing the development of AIH. However, further research is necessary to elucidate the specific mechanisms through which this relationship occurs.

Interleukins (ILs) are crucial cytokines that regulate the activation, proliferation, differentiation, and inflammatory responses of T and B cells.[45] They play significant roles in the pathogenesis of various liver diseases. By investigating the correlation between cytokines and AILDs, we identified 7 cytokines with potential causal relationships to AILDs. Our findings suggest potential causal relationships between IL-2, IL-4, and IL-6, members of the interleukin family, and PBC. Previous studies have demonstrated that IL-2 and IL-4 can influence the proportion of T cell subsets in PBC, thereby affecting disease progression.[46,47] Notably, Research has demonstrated that low-dose IL-2 can potentially alleviate the progression of PBC by correcting the imbalance between Th17 and Treg cells.[48] However, our findings suggest that elevated IL-2 levels may exacerbate the severity of PBC. This may be attributed to the fact that varying IL-2 levels can activate distinct immune cell types, thereby creating different influences on disease onset and progression.[49] Therefore, further research is necessary to determine the specific effects. Additionally, we found that Interferon gamma-induced protein 10 (IP-10) levels and TRAIL levels serve as protective factors for PSC. A recent study indicated that TRAIL-deficient mice exhibit enhanced hepatic inflammatory cytokine expression and cytotoxicity, leading to increased cholangiocyte apoptosis, thereby exacerbating liver damage and fibrosis.[50] This aligns with our findings. However, our results included only a subset of cytokines, offering preliminary insights into the field of AILDs. Further investigation is necessary to explore the relationship between cytokines and AILDs.

Reverse MR analysis did not show a causal relationship that AILDs can significantly affect immune cells or cytokines, which reinforces the important role of immune dysfunction due to changes in immune cell types and cytokines in the pathogenesis of AILDs. However, more studies are still needed for further validation.

Our study is the first Mendelian randomized study on immune cell phenotypes, cytokines, and AILDs, and despite our use of multiple statistical and sensitivity analyses, the present study has some limitations: firstly, the study population was predominantly of European origin. Because genetic structure differs between different populations, it limits the generalis ability of the findings to populations of other origins; secondly, AIH was not derived from the same study cohort as PBC and PSC, and we were unable to perform further stratification analyses due to the lack of personal information; thirdly, we have only included 41 types of cytokines, and other cytokines may have a potential relationship with AILDs; and finally, even after performing multiple sensitivity analyses, horizontal multidirectional anisotropy could not be fully assessed, and our conclusions still need to be further confirmed by follow-up studies.

5. Conclusion

Our study found the potential causal relationship between several immune phenotypes, cytokines, and different types of AILDs (AIH, PBC, PSC) by MR analysis. The complex link between AILDs and the body’s immune function was further confirmed. Our study provides new insights into the mechanisms of immune dysfunction and AILDs’ action. In addition, our study identified differences in the immune response of the immune system in different AILDs, which provided insights for achieving precise treatment and prevention of the disease.
