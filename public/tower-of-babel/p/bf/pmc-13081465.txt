Association between bone mineral density and coronary artery calcification: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xianghui Zeng, Dian Wang, Hao Yang, Zhenfang Liu, Xing Li, Ting Xiong, Ting Lu, Lu Dai, Feng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBone mineral density (BMD) decline and coronary artery calcification (CAC) are two age-related processes that represent major public health concerns worldwide. However, evidence regarding their association has remains uncertain despite extensive investigation.MethodsAn updated systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted following PRISMA guidelines. PubMed, EMBASE, and Scopus were searched through September 2025 for observational studies evaluating BMD (DXA, CT, or QCT) in relation to CAC (Agatston score or equivalent). Only multivariable-adjusted effect estimates were included. Fixed-effects or Random-effects models were used to pool odds ratios (ORs) or regression coefficients. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran’s Q and I². Meta-regression was performed to identify study-level modifiers, and publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots, Egger’s test, and trim-and-fill. Sensitivity analyses used a leave-one-out approach.ResultsTwenty-one studies involving 23,040 participants were included. Categorical low BMD was associated with significantly higher odds of CAC (pooled OR 2.01, 95% CI 1.43–2.83; I² = 66.9%), which remained significant after trim-and-fill correction (OR 1.59, 95% CI 1.21–2.09). Continuous BMD showed no overall association with CAC (OR 1.00, 95% CI 0.95–1.05; I² = 77.0%). Subgroup analyses showed stronger associations for femoral neck BMD (OR 2.52, 95% CI 1.53–4.15) and combined hip–spine measures (OR 3.34, 95% CI 2.04–5.45). MDCT-based CAC assessment yielded consistent associations (OR 2.38, 95% CI 1.69–3.34), whereas lumbar spine BMD and CTA-based CAC exhibited greater variability. Meta-regression identified CAC imaging modality as the only significant contributor to heterogeneity.ConclusionsOsteopenia or osteoporosis is consistently associated with a substantially higher likelihood of coronary artery calcification across diverse populations and measurement settings, whereas continuous BMD shows no clear linear association. These findings highlight decreased bone density as a potential clinical indicator of elevated vascular risk and support integrating bone health into cardiovascular risk assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05685-0.Keywords: Bone mineral density, Coronary artery calcification, Osteoporosis, Osteopenia, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Bone mineral density (BMD) decline and coronary artery calcification (CAC) are two age-related processes that represent major public health concerns worldwide. Osteoporosis, characterized by reduced BMD and impaired bone quality, is a major cause of morbidity and mortality among older adults. Recent high-quality syntheses estimate a global osteoporosis prevalence of about 19.7% (95% CI, 18.0%–21.4%) under WHO criteria, with marked regional variation, and burden analyses show LBMD-related deaths and DALYs have roughly doubled from 1990 to 2019 [1, 2]. Meanwhile, CAC—an established marker of subclinical atherosclerosis—has emerged as one of the most powerful predictors of coronary heart disease events beyond traditional risk factors [3]. Increasing evidence suggests that bone loss and vascular calcification may not be entirely independent phenomena but may share common biological pathways related to calcium-phosphate homeostasis, hormonal changes, and chronic inflammation. This concept of a “bone–vascular axis” has gained momentum in recent years, supported by imaging studies linking lower BMD to higher CAC scores and elevated cardiovascular risk [4].

Empirical studies investigating the relationship between bone mineral density (BMD) and coronary artery calcification (CAC) have yielded inconsistent findings. Several population-based and clinical studies have reported that individuals with lower BMD tend to have higher coronary calcium scores or subclinical atherosclerosis. For example, the Brasilia Study on Healthy Aging found that reduced BMD was associated with increased CAC and carotid intima-media thickness, independent of conventional cardiovascular risk factors [5]. Similar results were observed in postmenopausal women, where decreased lumbar spine and femoral neck BMD correlated with elevated CAC scores [6]. However, other studies reported no significant association after adjustment for age, body mass index, and metabolic factors [7–9]. Such inconsistencies may arise from differences in population characteristics, skeletal sites assessed, imaging modalities, and analytical models, highlighting the need for an updated meta-analysis to clarify the relationship between BMD and CAC.

Zhang et al. conducted the only dedicated systematic review and meta-analysis to date (covering studies through 2020), reporting an overall association between low BMD and CAC—with a pooled OR of approximately 2.11 for categorical comparisons—but noting that the association attenuated and in some adjusted analyses lost statistical significance [10]. Zhang et al. also documented substantial between-study heterogeneity and potential small-study/publication bias, and highlighted limited exploration of subgroup, dose–response, and measurement-specific effects. Since that review, several notable studies have been published that contribute complementary evidence [11–14]. These developments motivate an updated and expanded meta-analysis that incorporates studies published after 2021 and applies more granular subgroup and bias-robust methods.

The present study provides an updated meta-analysis to clarify the association between BMD and CAC. Following a PRISMA-guided protocol, we include only multivariable-adjusted effect estimates, distinguish categorical and continuous exposure–outcome pairings, prespecify subgroup analyses by population and perform bias-robust assessments. This research aims to integrate the most recent evidence and offer a comprehensive quantitative synthesis to determine whether lower BMD is independently associated with the presence or severity of CAC.

Materials and methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was designed and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [15]. The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251146529).

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive search of PubMed, EMBASE, and Scopus was conducted from database inception to September 13, 2025. The strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms for BMD/osteoporosis and CAC. The PubMed search string was: (“bone density“[MeSH] OR bone densit*[tiab] OR bone mineral densit*[tiab] OR bone mineral content*[tiab] OR osteopenia[tiab] OR osteoporosis[tiab]) AND (“coronary artery calcification“[tiab] OR “coronary calcium“[tiab] OR “CAC“[tiab] OR “Agatston score“[tiab]) AND (observational OR cohort OR “case control” OR “cross sectional” OR prospective OR retrospective NOT Review[pt] NOT “meta-analysis“[pt] NOT “systematic review“[pt] NOT “Case reports“[pt] NOT “Clinical Trial“[pt] NOT Editorial[pt] NOT Comment[pt] NOT Letter[pt] NOT Guideline[pt]). The search was restricted to English-language publications and yielded 85 records in PubMed on September 13, 2025. Equivalent strategies were adapted for EMBASE and Scopus, and reference lists of included articles and relevant reviews were screened to identify additional studies. Detailed strategies for all databases are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies were observational in design (cohort, case–control, or cross-sectional), enrolled adults aged ≥ 18 years from general populations or specific subgroups (e.g., postmenopausal women, high-risk individuals, or patients with chronic diseases), and evaluated BMD or osteoporosis as the primary exposure with CAC as the primary outcome. BMD had to be assessed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) or another validated method, with osteopenia/osteoporosis defined by standard T-score thresholds. The outcomes of interest were CAC presence/absence or CAC severity quantified by Agatston score or equivalent validated scales. With respect to statistical design, univariate analyses were required to demonstrate adequate baseline comparability between exposure groups, and multivariable analyses had to adjust for common confounders (at minimum age and sex when applicable) and report effect measures with 95% confidence intervals, including odds ratios (OR), relative risks (RR), hazard ratios (HR), β coefficients, or mean differences (MD), or provide sufficient raw data for their calculation. Exclusion criteria comprised non-human studies, case reports, reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, letters, guidelines, and studies in which BMD or CAC appeared only as covariates rather than primary variables. Studies lacking extractable data suitable for quantitative synthesis were also excluded.

Study selection and data extraction

All records were managed in EndNote (Clarivate, UK), with duplicates removed prior to screening. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text evaluation against the eligibility criteria. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer. Using a piloted, standardized extraction form, two reviewers independently collected study characteristics (first author, year, country, design), participant characteristics (sample size, mean age, sex distribution, ethnicity, comorbidities), details of BMD assessment (measurement site and device; T-score thresholds) and CAC assessment (imaging modality; scoring method and thresholds), and reported effect estimates with 95% confidence intervals (OR, RR, HR or β). When multiple models were reported, maximally adjusted estimates were preferentially extracted. When necessary, study authors were contacted to clarify methods or obtain missing information.

Quality assessment

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers. Cohort and case-control studies were appraised using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), and cross-sectional studies using the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) checklist. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Studies were categorized as high, moderate, or low quality according to instrument-specific thresholds, and sensitivity analyses were planned to examine the impact of excluding studies at higher risk of bias.

Statistical analysis

Pooled analyses synthesized the association between BMD and CAC using odds ratios (ORs) for dichotomous CAC outcomes or regression coefficients (β) for continuous CAC measures such as Agatston score, each with 95% confidence intervals. Separate meta-analyses or systematic review were performed for different exposure–outcome pairings, including categorical or continuous BMD in relation to categorical or continuous CAC. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q and I² statistics; when I² > 50% or p < 0.10 for Q, random-effects models were applied, otherwise fixed-effect models were used. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity across population characteristics (e.g., CKD, COPD, HIV), BMD measurement sites (lumbar spine, femoral neck, total hip), BMD assessment modalities (DXA, CT, QCT), CAC imaging methods (MDCT, EBCT, CTA), and CAC classification thresholds. Meta-regression was performed to evaluate study-level modifiers. Publication bias was examined using funnel plots and Egger’s test, with the trim-and-fill method applied when asymmetry indicated small-study effects. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using a leave-one-out approach. All analyses were performed using Stata 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA), and two-sided p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A total of 237 records were identified through database searches, and after removing duplicates and screening titles, abstracts, and full texts, 21 observational studies were included (Fig. 1). Among them, 18 were cross-sectional and 3 were prospective cohort studies, comprising 23,040 participants. Study characteristics are presented in Table 1. The investigations were conducted in North America, Europe, and Asia, involving healthy adults and individuals with chronic conditions such as CKD, COPD, HIV infection, and postmenopausal women. Participant ages ranged from 45 to 83 years, with men representing roughly half of the populations. BMD was primarily assessed using DXA or QCT, whereas CAC was measured using CT, MDCT, or EBCT. Methodological quality is summarized in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3: the four cohort studies showed high quality (NOS 7–9), while cross-sectional studies demonstrated moderate to lower risk of bias based on the AHRQ checklist. These findings indicate that the included studies provided generally reliable evidence for pooled analysis.

Association between categorical BMD and categorical CAC

The pooled analysis showed that individuals with low BMD had significantly higher odds of CAC (Fig. 2A), with a combined odds ratio of 2.01 (95% CI: 1.43–2.83; p < 0.001). Moderate heterogeneity was observed (I² = 66.87%, τ² = 0.15), and Cochran’s Q test indicated significant between-study variation (p = 0.00). Funnel-plot asymmetry and Egger’s regression test (β₁ = 1.99; p = 0.0456) suggested small-study effects. Trim-and-fill imputation added three studies and attenuated the pooled estimate to 1.59 (95% CI: 1.21–2.09), although the association remained statistically significant. Leave-one-out analysis demonstrated robust results, with pooled ORs ranging from 1.85 to 2.22 and no change in the direction or significance of the association (Fig. 2C).

Subgroup analyses (Supplementary Table 4) revealed that DXA-based studies reported a stronger association (OR 2.19, 95% CI: 1.49–3.23) compared with the single QCT study (OR 1.41, 95% CI: 1.17–1.70). Associations varied by skeletal site: femoral neck (OR 2.52, 95% CI: 1.53–4.15) and combined spine–hip measures (OR 3.34, 95% CI: 2.04–5.45) showed the strongest relationships, whereas lumbar spine alone produced a nonsignificant estimate (OR 1.44, 95% CI: 0.78–2.66). Regarding CAC assessment methods, MDCT studies demonstrated a consistent association with low heterogeneity (OR 2.38, 95% CI: 1.69–3.34), whereas CTA studies showed substantial variability. Subgroup analyses based on CAC thresholds indicated significant associations for CAC > 0 (OR 1.69, 95% CI: 1.06–2.68) and calcified plaque (OR 3.18, 95% CI: 1.88–5.39), while the CAC ≥ 100 comparison yielded a borderline estimate. Overall, despite moderate heterogeneity and evidence of small-study effects, most subgroup findings consistently supported a positive association between lower BMD and the presence of CAC.

Meta-regression analyses (Supplementary Table 5) did not identify statistically significant moderators of the association. In univariate models, none of the examined variables—including population type, BMD measurement site, BMD method, CAC assessment method, or CAC classification—significantly explained between-study heterogeneity (all p > 0.05). Multivariate models produced similar findings, with no covariates reaching statistical significance. These results indicate that the observed heterogeneity could not be attributed to any single study-level characteristic and likely reflects broader methodological or clinical variability across studies.

Association between continuous BMD and categorical CAC

The pooled analysis evaluating continuous BMD in relation to categorical CAC showed no significant overall association (Fig. 3A). The combined odds ratio was 1.00 (95% CI: 0.95–1.05; p = 0.97), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 76.98%). The funnel plot did not show obvious asymmetry (Fig. 3B), and Egger’s test was not statistically significant (β₁ = − 0.29; p = 0.106), indicating no clear small-study effects. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses yielded pooled ORs consistently close to 1.00 and remained nonsignificant (Fig. 3C).

Subgroup analyses were conducted to identify potential sources of heterogeneity in the association between continuous BMD and categorical CAC (Supplementary Table 6). Across most study populations—including healthy adults, individuals with osteopenia or osteoporosis, and patients with coronary artery disease—the pooled estimates were close to unity and not statistically significant. Statistically significant associations were observed in CKD patients (OR 0.871, 95% CI 0.762–0.996) and HIV-positive individuals (OR 1.018, 95% CI 1.005–1.032), both with low within-group heterogeneity. A single study in COPD patients reported a larger inverse association (OR 0.320, 95% CI 0.132–0.774). By study design, cross-sectional analyses showed a small but statistically significant effect (OR 1.019, 95% CI 1.006–1.032), whereas cohort studies reproduced the CKD subgroup pattern. Regarding BMD measurement methods, DXA-based studies demonstrated a minimal association (OR 1.015, 95% CI 1.002–1.029), while CT-, EBCT-, and QCT-based analyses were nonsignificant. Most skeletal sites showed no meaningful association, except for thoracic spine BMD (OR 1.137, 95% CI 1.050–1.230). Across CAC assessment methods, MDCT-based studies yielded a small yet significant association (OR 1.020, 95% CI 1.006–1.033), whereas other imaging modalities did not. Subgroups defined by CAC categories indicated modest associations for CAC > 0, cMS > 0, and CAC progression > 50/year, while other classification schemes remained nonsignificant.

Meta-regression analysesdid not identify significant sources of heterogeneity in univariate models, as study design, population type, BMD measurement method, BMD site, and CAC classification all showed p values > 0.05 (Supplementary Table 7). In the multivariate model, these variables likewise remained nonsignificant except for the CAC measurement method, which showed a modest association with effect size (β = − 0.054, p = 0.025). This suggests that differences in CAC imaging approaches may contribute to between-study variability, whereas population or BMD-related factors were less influential.

Association between categorical BMD and continuous CAC

Only one study [12] reported the association between categorical BMD and continuous CAC. In sex-specific analyses, categorical low BMD at the tibia was positively associated with CAC in both women (β = 0.03, 95% CI 0.00–0.05) and men (β = 0.18, 95% CI 0.17–0.21). In contrast, low BMD at the radius showed inverse associations with CAC in women (β = − 0.29, 95% CI − 0.31 to − 0.26) and men (β = − 0.31, 95% CI − 0.34 to − 0.29). All associations reported in this study were statistically significant (p < 0.001).

Association between continuous BMD and continuous CAC

Because the included studies used heterogeneous exposure units, BMD measurement techniques, and CAC transformations, a pooled meta-analysis was not appropriate. In two prospective cohort analyses from Campos-Obando et al., annual femoral-neck BMD change showed sex-specific estimates: β = − 0.02 (95% CI − 0.21 to 0.17) in men and β = 0.23 (95% CI 0.07 to 0.39) in women. Among patients with CAD or atherosclerosis, QCT-derived lumbar-spine BMD was associated with CAC in men (β = − 0.11, 95% CI − 0.22 to − 0.01) but not women (β = − 0.07, 95% CI − 0.20 to 0.07). In healthy adults, DXA-based assessments from Lu et al. reported small, nonsignificant β-coefficients across multiple skeletal sites, including total body (β = − 0.07, 95% CI − 0.18 to 0.03), lumbar spine (β = − 0.01, 95% CI − 0.08 to 0.07), and femoral neck (β = − 0.07, 95% CI − 0.15 to 0.01). CT-derived vertebral density analyses by Nakama et al. showed negative associations in the multi-ethnic cohort (β = − 0.185, 95% CI − 0.327 to − 0.043) and in Japanese participants (β = − 0.253, 95% CI − 0.502 to − 0.004), while other ethnic subgroups displayed β-coefficients ranging from − 0.039 to − 0.246 with wide confidence intervals. Across studies, effect estimates varied in direction and magnitude, with differences largely corresponding to variation in BMD modality, skeletal site, population subgroup, and CAC quantification method.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis, incorporating 21 observational studies with over 23,000 participants, revealed complex and measurement-dependent relationships between BMD and CAC. When BMD was categorized using clinical thresholds (osteoporosis vs. normal bone density), a statistically significant association with CAC was observed (pooled OR = 2.01, 95% CI: 1.43–2.83, p < 0.001). After adjusting for publication bias using the trim-and-fill method, this association attenuated by approximately 21% but remained statistically significant (OR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.21–2.09). In contrast, analyses treating BMD as a continuous variable did not reveal a consistent dose–response relationship with CAC, and effect estimates varied substantially across populations, measurement sites, and imaging modalities. Collectively, these findings suggest that the relationship between skeletal fragility and subclinical atherosclerosis may reflect a threshold-like phenomenon captured more effectively by categorical classifications than by linear trends. They also underscore the complexity of the bone–vascular interplay, indicating that its manifestation may depend on specific clinical and methodological contexts.

The present findings both corroborate and extend those reported by Zhang et al. in their 2022 meta-analysis [10], which included 16 studies and found a crude pooled OR of 2.11 (95% CI: 1.11–4.02) that diminished to 0.95 (95% CI: 0.86–1.05, p = 0.31) after multivariable adjustment. Our study, incorporating five additional studies published since 2021 and a larger sample size, adopted a more rigorous methodological approach by exclusively analyzing multivariable-adjusted effect estimates and systematically separating categorical from continuous exposures and outcomes. Despite these differences, both meta-analyses converge on a critical conclusion: the BMD–CAC association is highly sensitive to analytical strategy and study quality. The discrepancy between Zhang et al.‘s null multivariable-adjusted finding (OR = 0.95) and our publication bias-corrected estimate (OR = 1.59) likely reflects our categorical analysis framework, larger evidence base, and the inclusion of more recent studies with refined covariate adjustment. Importantly, both investigations demonstrate substantial attenuation of effect sizes after accounting for confounding factors, suggesting that crude associations may be driven largely by shared risk factors rather than independent relationships. Collectively, these convergent lines of evidence indicate that any observed BMD–CAC association is modest, methodologically contingent, and unlikely to represent an independent, causal relationship.

An important finding of our study is the clear discrepancy between categorical and continuous BMD metrics. When BMD was treated categorically, individuals with low BMD had nearly twice the odds of exhibiting CAC, and this association remained robust after multivariable adjustment and correction for publication bias (pooled OR ≈ 2.0). In contrast, modeling BMD as a continuous variable produced an overall null association (OR ≈ 1.00), with effect estimates varying in magnitude and direction across populations and skeletal sites. Similar discrepancies have been noted in prior research, including recent observational and Mendelian randomization analyses showing no consistent linear association between BMD and CAC despite links with other cardiovascular outcomes [2]. These findings suggest that the relationship between bone loss and coronary calcification may follow a threshold-like pattern in which CAC risk increases meaningfully only once BMD falls below a certain level—making categorical definitions more sensitive to clinically relevant risk differences. Moreover, continuous models are more vulnerable to dilution from measurement error, site-specific variability, and population heterogeneity, which may collectively bias linear estimates toward the null and obscure underlying non-linear associations.

Substantial heterogeneity observed in our pooled estimates—both for the categorical association between low BMD and CAC (I²≈67%) and for the continuous association (I²≈77%)—can be largely explained by structured differences across BMD assessment methods, skeletal sites, CAC imaging modalities, and clinical populations. First, at the level of bone measurement, our subgroup analyses demonstrated that low BMD defined at weight-bearing sites, particularly the femoral neck and combined hip/spine indices (pooled ORs 2.19–3.34), showed the most consistent associations with CAC, whereas lumbar spine BMD alone was not significantly related to CAC (OR 1.44, 95% CI 0.78–2.66). This contrast aligns with evidence that degenerative spine changes and abdominal aortic calcification can spuriously elevate lumbar DXA values and obscure inverse associations with vascular calcification, while hip BMD more reliably reflects systemic skeletal fragility [29, 30]. Second, the strongest and most stable associations were observed in studies applying ECG-gated MDCT with standard Agatston scoring (pooled OR 2.38, 95% CI 1.69–3.34), whereas studies using coronary CTA or non-standard ordinal CAC definitions yielded more variable estimates—consistent with literature establishing MDCT-based CAC scoring as the reproducible reference standard [31]. Third, heterogeneity across populations was evident when BMD was treated as a continuous predictor, with subgroup findings ranging from weakly protective in CKD (OR ≈ 0.87) to slightly positive in HIV (OR ≈ 1.02) and more strongly inverse in COPD (OR ≈ 0.32). These divergent patterns are compatible with disease-specific bone–vascular interactions: CKD–mineral and bone disorder features tightly linked impairments in bone turnover and vascular calcification [32], whereas HIV is characterized by accelerated bone loss and increased subclinical atherosclerosis, with studies showing age-dependent associations between CAC and BMD [33]. COPD cohorts also demonstrate clustering of osteoporosis, emphysema, arterial stiffness, and CAC, although the strength and direction of associations vary with disease severity and inflammation [34, 35]. Collectively, these anatomical, technical, and clinical factors coherently explain much of the residual heterogeneity in our analyses and support our meta-regression finding that CAC imaging modality was the only variable significantly influencing effect size.

Osteoporosis and CAC likely arise from shared upstream determinants rather than a direct causal pathway. Aging, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and dysregulated calcium–phosphate and hormonal balance are recognized as common drivers of both osteoporosis and vascular calcification [4, 36, 37]. These systemic factors promote osteoclast activation and bone resorption while inducing osteogenic differentiation of vascular smooth muscle cells, leading to calcium deposition within arterial walls [38]. Endocrine regulators such as fibroblast growth factor-23 (FGF23) and Klotho also influence phosphate metabolism and vascular mineralization, linking bone and vascular homeostasis at the systemic level [39, 40]. However, the bone matrix and vascular wall operate in distinct cellular and biochemical environments, and their mineral regulation differs substantially. Consequently, bone loss and vascular calcification may proceed in parallel but independently, driven by similar metabolic and inflammatory stressors rather than direct interaction. This interpretation supports the concept of multisystem aging, in which skeletal and vascular degeneration coexist as manifestations of shared biological aging processes [36, 41].

From a clinical standpoint, our findings suggest that low BMD may serve as a practical clinical alert for increased CAC risk, particularly in older adults and individuals with multiple cardiovascular risk factors. Patients with osteoporosis may therefore benefit from more attentive cardiovascular risk evaluation as part of routine care. Conversely, among those already undergoing cardiovascular assessment, considering bone health may help identify individuals with combined skeletal and vascular vulnerability. While categorical low BMD carries clear warning value, continuous BMD measurements do not provide reliable prediction of CAC burden and should not be used as quantitative surrogates. Instead, low BMD should be viewed as a risk-enhancing feature that complements, rather than replaces, established cardiovascular risk assessment tools. Beyond risk stratification, CAC burden also has practical implications for downstream management. Because higher calcification indicates more complex coronary anatomy, it frequently translates into greater procedural difficulty during percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Complex PCI is associated with increased ischemic risk and therefore often requires individualized antithrombotic strategies to balance ischemic protection with bleeding risk [42, 43].

This meta-analysis is limited by the predominance of cross-sectional studies, which prevents establishing temporal or causal relationships between low BMD and CAC. Considerable heterogeneity across BMD and CAC measurement methods, anatomical sites, and covariate adjustments may also have influenced the pooled estimates. Moreover, the lack of standardized units for continuous BMD measures precluded formal dose–response analyses. Future work should prioritize prospective cohorts to determine whether low BMD predicts CAC progression, mechanistic studies in high-risk populations, standardized imaging protocols, and evaluations of whether improving bone metabolism can modify the development or progression of coronary calcification.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis demonstrates that osteopenia or osteoporosis is consistently associated with a substantially higher likelihood of coronary artery calcification across diverse populations and measurement settings, whereas continuous BMD shows no clear linear association. These findings highlight decreased bone density as a potential clinical indicator of elevated vascular risk and support integrating bone health into cardiovascular risk assessment.
