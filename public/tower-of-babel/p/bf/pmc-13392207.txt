Identification of an Immune Cell Gene Signature for Predicting Response and Prognosis in Rectal Cancer Patients Undergoing Neoadjuvant Chemoradiotherapy
Authors: Tengfei Yan, Limin Jin, Yi Shen, Ming Cheng, Yao Xu, Yuntian Shen, Liwei Xie, Qiliang Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe objective of this research was to develop a robust classification system that accurately predicts the prognosis and therapeutic efficacy of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (NCRT) in patients with rectal cancer.MethodsWe generated an immune cell gene signature (ICGS) model employing univariate Cox regression and Lasso regression methods. Subsequently, the predictive capabilities of the ICGS model were assessed and validated for NCRT efficacy, survival outcomes, and immunotherapy benefits. Furthermore, we conducted functional annotation, investigated genomic alterations, and explored potential immune escape mechanisms.ResultsInitially, we developed three ICGS models with prognostic implications: the B cell signature, macrophage signature, and combined signature. The immune cell‐related genes were highly associated with immune function, immune response, and immunological pathways. The ROC analysis revealed that the ICGS model demonstrated a relatively strong predictive capability for the response to NCRT. Patients exhibiting high ICGS scores experienced a significantly reduced overall survival. Furthermore, multivariate Cox regression analysis verified the ICGS as an independent factor, and we constructed a predictive nomogram. The AUC value confirmed that the ICGS model was comparable to and superior to the clinical stage in predicting overall patient survival. Additionally, the low‐risk group for the macrophage signature showed a better survival advantage from immunotherapy. Finally, we systematically correlated the ICGS model with genomic alterations and uncovered potential immune escape mechanisms.ConclusionThe ICGS model can be considered a reliable tool for evaluating the efficacy of neoadjuvant therapy in rectal cancer and can further assist in guiding risk stratification and treatment decisions.Keywords: immune cell signatures, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, rectal cancer, risk stratification

Introduction

Radiotherapy holds a crucial position in the comprehensive treatment of locally advanced rectal cancer, significantly contributing to enhanced local control and anal preservation rates, as well as improved quality of life and survival [1]. In the case of locally advanced rectal cancer, the current gold standard is a combination of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (NCRT) and total mesorectal excision [2]. Nonetheless, the resistance of tumor cells to ionizing radiation often results in treatment failure, presenting a significant and pressing challenge for rectal cancer NCRT [3]. The identification of novel therapeutic targets and the enhancement of rectal cancer radiosensitivity are of immense practical value, as they can augment the efficacy of NCRT for rectal cancer, prolong patient survival, and enhance their quality of life.

To enhance tumor regression and high‐quality survival, the combination of NCRT and immunotherapy has been investigated in locally advanced rectal cancer patients [4]. Recent research indicates that ionizing radiation may have a profound immune‐stimulating impact, opening up the possibility of utilizing radiotherapy to boost anti‐tumor immunity [5]. Furthermore, numerous clinical trials based on the combination of NCRT and immunotherapy have yielded encouraging results, including a high pathological complete response (pCR) rate and an increased anal preservation rate [6, 7]. Given the promising application potential, the integration of NCRT and immunotherapy is ushering in a new era for anal preservation in rectal cancer with advanced stage [8]. Nonetheless, several challenges persist, including treatment management, efficacy prediction, biomarker exploration, survival benefit, and specific mechanisms. Additionally, there are no effective models available for accurately predicting the efficacy and prognosis of patients receiving NCRT.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a complex, ever‐changing multicellular ecosystem, comprising an array of immune cell types, cancer‐associated fibroblasts, stromal cells, cancer cells, and additional components [9]. The immune cells present within the TME have traditionally been viewed as a crucial focal point in oncology research, making significant contributions to the immunotherapy of malignancies [10]. Furthermore, several studies have corroborated the significance of the proportion of tumor‐infiltrating immune cells in determining patient prognosis [11]. Nonetheless, the specific pathogenesis mediated by immune cells remains obscure, and the regulatory mechanisms demand further exploration.

In this research, we chose NCRT‐related tumor infiltrating immune cells and established the integrative immune cell gene signatures (ICGS) model. We then conducted an extensive analysis of the ICGS model to reveal its prognostic significance, genetic variation, and potential immune escape mechanisms. The ICGS model demonstrated remarkable potential for predicting patients' prognosis in rectal cancer, offering guidance for the selection of clinical tumor immunotherapies. Materials and Methods

Data Acquisition and Processing

The gene expression datasets (GSE46862, GSE150082, GSE123390, GSE17537, GSE190826, and GSE39582) were sourced by using the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. GSE46862 comprised 69 rectal cancer patients, including 28 responders and 41 non‐responders to NCRT, whereas GSE150082 encompassed 16 responders and 23 non‐responders to NCRT [12, 13]. The training dataset was generated by merging these two datasets (GSE46862 and GSE150082) using the ComBat function from the R package “SVA” [14]. The batch effect between the two datasets was adjusted, with GSE46862 serving as the reference dataset during merging. To visually assess the effectiveness of batch‐effect correction, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed before and after ComBat adjustment for the merged GSE46862 and GSE150082 datasets. PCA plots were generated to visualize the distribution of samples according to dataset source before and after correction. Four datasets (GSE123390, GSE17537, GSE190826, GSE39582) were allocated as validation datasets, each with sample sizes of 28, 55, 117, and 585, respectively [15, 16, 17, 18]. Genetic mutations, RNA‐Seq data, and clinicopathological characteristics of 166 rectal cancer patient samples (TCGA‐READ cohort) were retrieved from the TCGA database [19]. Data from the IMvigor210 study were downloaded from the study's official website [20]. The datasets employed in this study are presented in Table 1.

NCRT‐Related Immune Cells Screening

The TIMER2.0 online database is a robust resource that offers various modules for exploring the relationships between immune infiltrates and genetic or clinical characteristics [21]. In this research, the TIMER2.0 platform was utilized for immune cell infiltration analysis based on six distinct algorithms, including CIBERSORT, Xcell, QUANTISEQ, EPIC, MCPCOUNTER, and Timer2.0. The Wilcoxon test was employed to investigate disparities in immune cells between CRT responders and non‐responders. Immune cells with significant differences in at least 2 algorithms and a consistent pattern of variations were identified as the NCRT‐associated immune infiltrating cell type.

Immune Cell Related Genes Extraction

The R package “LIMMA” was employed to screen the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between rectal cancer samples from CRT responders and non‐responders. A threshold of p‐value < 0.05 and |logFC| > 0 was utilized. Subsequently, R packages “ggplot” and “pheatmap” were employed to generate volcano maps and heat maps, respectively. A Spearman correlation test analysis was conducted to investigate the connection between the relative abundances of immune cells and DEGs. DEGs with |correlation coefficients| > 0.25 and p‐value < 0.05 were recognized as immune cell‐related genes.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

To gain deeper insights into the functions of the immune cell‐related DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were carried out [22, 23]. The R package “ClusterProfiler” was employed for functional enrichment analysis, and the R package “ggplot2” was employed to depict the enrichment outcomes in bubble plots. GO analysis comprises three levels: biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cell component (CC). A p value < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Development of ICGS

Initially, univariate Cox regression was performed to identify the immune cell‐related genes that were significantly linked to the overall survival (OS) of patients in the training cohort, using the “survival” package. Subsequently, LASSO regression analysis was executed to pinpoint the hub genes based on the findings from the univariate Cox regression analysis associated with OS, employing the R package “glmnet.” This led to the generation of ICGS. Moreover, the ICGS score model was established based on the expression levels of signature genes and their corresponding coefficients. Assessment Analysis of ICGS

To assess the predictive capability of ICGS in forecasting the response to NCRT, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed, with the area under the curve (AUC) being calculated. Among the included datasets, GSE150082, GSE123390, and GSE46862 provided explicit responder/non‐responder information and were therefore used for this analysis. Other datasets were used for prognostic validation because comparable binary NCRT response annotations were unavailable or not applicable. To further assess the prognostic potential of the ICGS model, patients from the TCGA‐READ cohort were categorized into high and low ICGS score groups based on the median ICGS score. Kaplan–Meier (K–M) curves and log‐rank tests were implemented to examine survival disparities between the two groups, utilizing the R packages “survival” and “survminer.” The time‐dependent ROC curve was employed for assessing the prediction precision regarding 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year OS with the “survivalROC” R package.

Validation Analysis of ICGS

The validation datasets GSE17537, GSE190826, and GSE39582 were employed to confirm the prognostic capability of the ICGS model. The samples from the validation datasets were categorized into high‐risk (≥ median value) groups and low‐risk (< median value) groups, using the median risk score as the cutoff value. The disparities in OS between the low‐risk and high‐risk groups were analyzed by K–M curves. Additionally, the area under the time‐dependent ROC curve was calculated to evaluate the predictive capability, utilizing the R packages “survival” and “survivalROC” once more.

Association Analysis of ICGS and Clinical Parameters

The associations between the ICGS score and various clinical characteristics, such as age, gender, and stage within the TCGA‐READ cohort, were examined using the standard Wilcoxon test and one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA). p‐value less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically meaningful.

Independent Prognostic Analysis

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to assess the independent prognostic significance of the ICGS score and other clinical variables, such as age, gender, and stage, employing the “coxph” function from the R survival package. Subsequently, a prediction nomogram was established by summing the point scores of each variable, which collectively revealed the likelihood of 1‐, 3‐, 5‐, and 10‐year OS. The “RMS” package was employed to generate the nomogram. ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive accuracy of the ICGS score, clinical stage, and nomogram for overall survival in TCGA‐READ patients. The binary endpoint for each ROC analysis was defined according to survival status, where death (status = 1) was considered the positive event and alive (status = 0) was considered the negative event. To assess the predictive capability of the nomogram, calibration curves were employed.

Genetic Variation Analysis of ICGS

In this study, the associations between the ICGS score and genome alterations were assessed. The “maftools” package was used for visualizing the somatic variant landscape [24]. GISTIC 2.0 software was employed for analyzing the GISTIC score, gene copy number amplification, and deletion data for subtypes with high or low ICGS scores [25]. Furthermore, the relationship between genetic mutations and risk score was examined. The top 20 genes with the highest mutation frequency were selected for further analysis. For assessing the survival differences among various mutation groups, KM curves were utilized.

Tumor Immune Analyses

The analysis of immune cell infiltration based on ssGSEA methods was conducted for the signature genes. In the TCGA‐READ dataset, we examined the correlation between the expression of signature genes and immune microenvironment scores, which comprised stroma score, immune score, and estimate score. Additionally, we assessed the associations between signature genes and immune‐related genes, such as immunostimulating genes, immunosuppressive genes, chemokines, chemokine receptors, and MHC genes. Various factors related to tumor immunogenicity were analyzed in different risk groups, including tumor mutation burden (TMB), cancer testis antigen (CTA), homologous recombination repair defect (HRD), immune score, stroma score, stemness score, chromosomal aneuploidy, tumor purity, lymphocyte infiltration signature score, IFN‐gamma response, and TGF‐beta response. The expression of immunomodulatory molecules, specifically co‐stimulatory and co‐repressive molecules, was compared between the high and low risk score groups. Furthermore, we examined the correlation between risk score and the expression of immune checkpoint molecules.

Exploratory Immunotherapy Response Prediction

To explore the potential association between the ICGS model and immunotherapy response, we performed an exploratory analysis using the IMvigor210 dataset, which includes 348 metastatic urothelial cancer patients treated with a PD‐L1 inhibitor. To our knowledge, publicly available rectal cancer immunotherapy cohorts with matched transcriptomic, treatment response, and survival data remain limited. Therefore, the IMvigor210 cohort, which contains transcriptomic profiles, immune checkpoint blockade response information, and survival data, was used as an exploratory proxy dataset. K–M curves were employed for evaluating the survival differences between patients with high and low ICGS scores. We performed ROC analysis to assess the predictive efficacy and calculated the AUC value. Additionally, we compared the rates of complete response (CR)/partial response (PR) and stable disease (SD)/progressive disease (PD) to immunotherapy between the high and low ICGS score groups in the IMvigor210 cohort.

Results

Identification of NCRT‐Related Immune Cells Used to Construct ICGS

First, the training dataset was created by merging the GSE46862 and GSE150082 datasets using the R package “SVA” for downstream analysis. Batch correction was performed as per manufacturer's instructions. The PCA plots demonstrate the distribution of samples by dataset source before and after batch‐effect adjustment, providing visual evidence that the batch effect was reduced after ComBat correction (Figure S1). Immune cell infiltration analysis was performed using six algorithms: CIBERSORT, Xcell, QUANTISEQ, EPIC, MCPCOUNTER, and Timer2.0. Table 2 presents the significant immune cell types with infiltration differences identified by different algorithms. B cells and macrophages were identified as immune cells related to NCRT in rectal cancer, showing significant differences in at least 2 algorithms and consistent patterns of differences. The heatmap in Figure 2A visualizes the immune cell infiltration. Afterwards, the Wilcoxon test was employed for investigating the disparities in immune cell infiltration between NCRT responders and non‐responders in rectal cancer. The results indicated that the infiltration of B cells and macrophages differed significantly between the two groups. Moreover, B cells and macrophages were significantly upregulated in NCRT responders compared to non‐responders, as shown in Figure 2B,C.

Screening NCRT‐Related Immune Cell Genes Used to Construct ICGS

Next, we identified DEGs related to the response to NCRT in rectal cancer using the R package “LIMMA.” A total of 535 DEGs were screened, with 375 upregulated in the NCRT responders group and 160 upregulated in the NCRT non‐responders group. The volcano plot in Figure 3A and the heatmap in Figure 3B visualize the DEGs associated with the NCRT response. Next, using the proportions of infiltrated cell types and the expression of DEGs, Spearman correlation tests were performed, with correlations classified as positive or negative according to correlation coefficients greater than 0.25 or less than −0.25 and a correlation test threshold of p < 0.05. Genes significantly associated with B cells (204 genes) and macrophages (193 genes) were identified as NCRT‐related immune cell genes.

To delve into the potential roles of immune cell genes associated with NCRT, we conducted functional enrichment analysis. The GO analysis revealed that the B cell genes were notably involved in pathways associated with immune response regulation, cell surface receptor signaling, and immune activation at the BP level (Figure 3C). At the CC level, these genes were associated with MHC protein complexes and MHC class II protein complexes, among other important structures (Figure 3D). Furthermore, at the MF level, the B cell genes were involved in MHC class II protein complex binding, immune receptor activity, and peptide antigen binding, among others (Figure 3E). The KEGG analysis indicated significant enrichment of B cell genes in pathways such as Th17 cell differentiation, hematopoietic cell lineage, lysosome, antigen processing and presentation, and various other pathways related to immune function and diseases (Figure 3F).

Similarly, the macrophage cell genes were found to be highly enriched in pathways related to immune response regulation, immune system process regulation, immune activation, antigen processing and presentation, and immune effector process regulation at the BP level (Figure 3G). At the CC level, these genes were associated with MHC protein complexes and MHC class II protein complexes (Figure 3H). Regarding MF, the macrophage genes were linked with cytokine binding, MHC class II protein complex binding, and peptide antigen binding, among others (Figure 3I). The KEGG analysis revealed enrichment of macrophage genes in pathways containing phagosome, lysosome, proteoglycans in cancer, and various pathways related to immune function and diseases (Figure 3J).

Construction of ICGS Based on Survival‐Related Immune Cell Genes

We conducted univariate Cox regression analysis for assessing the prognostic power of the immune cell genes in the TCGA database. Among the 204 B cell genes analyzed, 8 genes showed significant prognostic value, whereas 6 of the 193 macrophage genes displayed significant prognostic value (Figure 4A,B). Subsequently, we performed LASSO regression analysis and identified 8 B cell genes (RNF183, CHEK2, IFRD2, PPFIA2, BMP2, PACSIN2, SETD5, LITAF) and 6 macrophage genes (RNF183, GNL3, DHODH, SETD5, PARD6A, LITAF) as prognostic factors (Figure 4C,D). Furthermore, we integrated the macrophage and B cell genes in the LASSO analysis and identified a total of 8 genes (RNF183, CHEK2, PPFIA2, GNL3, BMP2, PACSIN2, SETD5, PARD6A) with significant prognostic value for the combined immune cell types (Figure 4E). Finally, based on the expression levels of these significant immune cell genes and their corresponding LASSO coefficients, we established the ICGS score models, including the B cell signature, macrophage signature, and the combined signatures. Evaluation of ICGS

Initially, we carried out a thorough examination of the individual genes in the ICGS model. The findings showed significant differences in the expression levels of B cell genes and macrophage genes between TCGA‐READ tumor tissues and their paired adjacent tissues (Figures S2 and S3). Furthermore, both B cell genes and macrophage genes exhibited high predictive power in discriminating TCGA‐READ patients from normal controls (Figures S4 and S5), indicating that each signature gene possesses excellent biomarker characteristics.

Next, we assessed the predictive value of the B cell signature in distinguishing between NCRT responders and non‐responders in rectal cancer using ROC analysis on the GSE123390, GSE150082, and GSE46862 datasets. The results demonstrated that the B cell signature displayed moderate diagnostic accuracy in predicting the response to NCRT (Figure 5A–C).

Furthermore, we assessed the prognostic capability of the ICGS model within the TCGA cohort. The TCGA‐READ patients were categorized into high (n = 77) and low (n = 79) ICGS score groups, based on the median ICGS score. K–M analysis was performed to examine the association between ICGS scores and OS in the TCGA‐READ cohort. The results revealed significant differences in survival probabilities, with higher risk scores corresponding to shorter survival times for all three signatures: B cell signature (p < 0.001, Figure 5D), macrophage signature (p < 0.001, Figure 5F), and combined signature (p < 0.001, Figure 5H). Additionally, the three ICGS score models exhibited relatively high predictive accuracy, as indicated by the AUC values obtained from time‐dependent ROC curves (Figure 5E,G,I).

Validation of ICGS in Independent Cohorts

To assess the feasibility of the ICGS model for predicting survival in independent cohorts, we evaluated its prognostic value in validation datasets. Analysis of the GSE190826 dataset demonstrated that the B cell signature had a significant prognostic impact, as evidenced by distinct K–M curves between high and low score subgroups (Figure 6A) and AUC values exceeding 0.62 at 1, 2, and 3 years (Figure 6B). Similarly, analysis of the GSE17537 dataset revealed that the macrophage signature also had a notable prognostic effect, with significant differences in K–M curves between high and low score subgroups (Figure 6C) and AUC values greater than 0.68 at 1, 2, and 3 years (Figure 6D). However, no significant prognostic efficacy of the combined ICGS model was observed in any rectal cancer dataset (data not shown). Additionally, no significant prognostic associations were found for the B cell signature in GSE17537 or the macrophage signature in GSE190826 (data not shown). To further validate the three ICGS models, we applied the GSE39582 dataset from colon cancer, which confirmed their significant prognostic value (Figure 6E–J).

Independent Prognostic Ability of ICGS

Next, we aimed to determine whether the ICGS model's prognostic ability for survival prediction in READ patients is independent of other clinicopathological parameters. To assess this, we conducted univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses on the ICGS score and various clinical parameters, including age, gender, and tumor stage, using the TCGA‐READ cohort.

For the B cell signature, the univariate Cox regression analysis revealed significant associations between age (HR = 3.81; p = 0.031), ICGS score of B cell signature (HR = 7.70; p < 0.001), and stage (HR = 3.47; p = 0.009) with overall survival in TCGA‐READ patients (Figure 8A). In the multivariate Cox regression analysis, both the ICGS score of B cell signature (HR = 6.62; p = 0.003) and stage (HR = 2.69; p = 0.042) remained as independent prognostic factors (Figure 8B). To improve the prognostic prediction of rectal cancer patients, we developed a nomogram that incorporated the ICGS score of B cell signature and clinical characteristics (Figure 8C). ROC analysis indicated that the B cell signature and the nomogram showed higher AUC values for predicting OS compared to traditional stage factors (Figure 8D). The calibration curve further demonstrated good agreement between the nomogram predictions and actual observations of survival probability (Figure 8E–G).

Correlation of Genomic Alterations With ICGS

Given the significant difference in overall survival associated with the B cell signature risk score in the ICGS model, we further explored the relationship between the risk score and genomic alterations, containing somatic copy number variations (SCNV) and mutations. First, we analyzed the GISTIC scores in the high‐risk score and low‐risk score subtypes. The results revealed that the low‐risk score group had higher GISTIC scores compared to the high‐risk score group (Figure 10A). Additionally, the low‐risk score group exhibited a higher percentage of copy number gain/loss. We then assessed the differences in genomic alteration scores (CNV ratio), genomic gain, and genomic loss between the subtypes. No significant differences were observed across different stages (Figure 10B). However, significant differences in CNV gain and loss were found between the high‐risk score and low‐risk score groups, with a higher proportion of CNV loss than CNV gain (Figure 10C). These findings suggest that heightened copy number loss could be a common genomic alteration event in patients with TCGA‐READ.

Afterwards, we examined the correlation between genetic mutations and the risk score. Using chi‐square tests, we identified eight genes (SSPO, PIK3CA, TAGAP, SZT2, ESR1, EPG5, CD163L1, and CYLC1) with noteworthy disparities in the number of mutations between the high‐risk score and low‐risk score cohorts (Figure 10D). Figure 10E displayed the top 20 genes with the highest mutation frequency in the TCGA‐READ cohort. Notably, APC, TP53, and TTN were among the 20 most commonly mutated genes and were significantly associated with the risk score. Furthermore, most mutations were more frequent in the high‐risk score group compared to the low‐risk score group. Therefore, we investigated whether these mutations could potentially serve as inherent immune evasion mechanisms for both the high‐risk and low‐risk cohorts. The findings revealed that the expression of immune checkpoints (CD274, PDCD1, and CTLA4) was notably elevated in the ROBO1, DUOX1, ACVR2A, and CHGB mutant cohorts compared to the wild‐type cohorts (Figure 10F).

Eventually, we evaluated the impact of these genes on the survival of patients with either high‐risk score or low‐risk score. Despite the significant overall p value, mutations in these four genes did not influence the survival outcomes of patients in either the high‐risk score or low‐risk score group (Figure 10G).

The ICGS Model Is Highly Involved in the Tumor Immune Microenvironment

The tumor immune microenvironment is known to be significantly influenced by the infiltrating tumor components, which play a crucial role in determining the outcome of tumor immunotherapy. To investigate this further, we performed immune cell infiltration analysis using the ssGSEA method on the signature genes. The correlation between the expression of the signature genes and the infiltration of immune cells was analyzed, as shown in Figures S7 and S8 for B cell genes and macrophage genes, respectively. The results revealed significant correlations between the expression of the signature genes and the infiltration of various immune cells.

Next, we examined the relationship between the expression of the signature genes and immune microenvironment scores, including stroma score, immune score, and estimate score, in the TCGA‐READ dataset. As depicted in Figure 11 and Figure S9, the expression of the signature genes was found to be significantly associated with the immune microenvironment scores. Furthermore, we evaluated the relationship between the signature genes and immune‐related genes, including immunostimulating genes, immunosuppressive genes, chemokines, chemokine receptors, and MHC genes. The results demonstrated significant associations between the signature genes and these immune‐related genes, as shown in Figure 12 and Figure S10. Taken together, these findings suggest that the ICGS model may play a critical role in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment.

Potential Immune Escape Mechanisms of ICGS

Several factors related to tumor immunogenicity were investigated, including TMB, CTA, HRD, immune score, stroma score, stemness score, chromosomal aneuploidy, and tumor purity (Figure S11). As depicted in Figure 13A, stromal scores were found to be higher in the high‐risk score group (p < 0.01), whereas tumor purity was higher in the low‐risk score group (p < 0.01). Following this, we examined the expression of immunomodulatory molecules between the high and low‐risk score groups using an array of co‐stimulatory and co‐repressive molecules. The findings revealed that the expression of immunomodulatory molecules was notably elevated in the high‐risk score group compared to the low‐risk score group (Figure 13B). However, the difference in molecular expression between the two groups could not be explained by somatic copy number variations (most p > 0.05).

Furthermore, we examined the correlation between the risk score and the expression of immune checkpoint molecules, revealing a significant association between the risk score and immune checkpoint molecule expression (Figure 13C). At last, we investigated the correlation between the risk score and the expression of specific immune checkpoint molecules, including PVR, TNFSF4, CD47, BTN2A1, TNFRSF4, and CEACAM1 (Figure 13D). Our findings indicated a significant positive correlation between the risk score and the expression of immune checkpoint molecules TNFSF4 and TNFRSF4 (p < 0.01). Conversely, the risk score exhibited a significant negative correlation with the expression of PVR, CD47, BTN2A1, and CEACAM1 (p < 0.01).

Exploratory Analysis of the Potential Association Between ICGS and Immunotherapy Response

To explore the potential predictive value of the ICGS model for immune checkpoint blockade treatment, we conducted an exploratory analysis using the IMvigor210 dataset. Because publicly available rectal cancer immunotherapy cohorts with matched transcriptomic profiles, treatment response annotations, and survival data are currently limited, the IMvigor210 cohort was selected as an exploratory proxy dataset. The patients in the IMvigor210 immunotherapy cohort were divided into high (n = 174) and low (n = 174) ICGS score cohorts based on the median ICGS score. Our analysis showed that the B cell signature was not an effective predictor of prognosis in immunotherapy within the IMvigor210 dataset. Additionally, no significant disparity in the risk score was observed among the various efficacy subgroups (Figure 14A). On the other hand, the macrophage signature showed some potential, as patients in the high ICGS score subgroup tended to have a shorter OS after immunotherapy in comparison to those in the low ICGS score subgroup (p = 0.034). However, the AUC value for predictive performance was not statistically significant. The distribution of immunotherapy response revealed that a higher incidence of SD or PD in the high‐risk group compared to the low‐risk group, whereas the low‐risk group exhibited a higher prevalence of CR or PR compared to the high‐risk group. However, there was no statistical difference in risk scores between patients with CR/PR and those with SD/PD (Figure 14B). In terms of the combined signatures, no notable disparity in the risk score was detected among the various efficacy subgroups (Figure 14C). It is important to note that the IMvigor210 dataset consists of bladder cancer data, and there are significant differences between bladder cancer and rectal cancer, which may heavily influence the results.

Discussion

With the increasing recognition of the immune system's role in cancer treatment, NCRT combined with immunotherapy has arisen as a prospective approach for the management of rectal cancer patients. Nonetheless, an absence of robust models exists to accurately forecast treatment efficacy and prognosis, coupled with a dearth of biomarkers to aid in patient stratification and therapeutic decisions. Additionally, the survival benefits of NCRT and immunotherapy in rectal cancer patients need further validation. It has been widely acknowledged that immune cells within the TME play a crucial role in cancer progression, prognosis, and treatment response. Therefore, it is essential to investigate the complex immune cell landscape and its functional properties in the TME. In our research, we concentrated on immune cell infiltration and investigated the disparities in immune cell ratios among various NCRT response groups. Subsequently, we explored the association between gene expression and immune cell infiltration ratios by pinpointing the immune cell categories with notable discrepancies. We subsequently identified genes significantly associated with these differential immune cell types. Furthermore, we employed a machine learning approach to identify signature genes and develop an integrative ICGS that could contribute to prognosis prediction. We also constructed a risk score system based on the ICGS to guide clinical decision‐making. According to our understanding, this is the first research to uncover immunological signatures particularly for the NCRT of rectal cancer patients.

Three ICGSs were identified, namely the B cell signature, macrophage signature, and the combined signatures. B cells, which are essential in fighting infectious diseases, are a major component of the TME [26]. Emerging evidence from preclinical and clinical studies suggests that B cells can elicit robust anti‐cancer immune responses through both humoral and cellular mechanisms [27]. In cancer therapy, B cells may contribute to tumor cell destruction by producing cancer‐specific antibodies or through antibody‐dependent cell‐mediated cytotoxicity, antibody‐dependent cell‐mediated phagocytosis, and complement‐dependent cytotoxic effects [28]. Macrophages, another crucial component of the TME, play various roles in angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, coordination of cancer cell proliferation, metastasis, immunosuppression, as well as resistance to chemotherapy and checkpoint blockade immunotherapy [29]. When appropriately activated, macrophages can engage in phagocytosis of cancer cells and exhibit tumor cytotoxicity, effectively interacting with components of the innate and adaptive immune system in a bidirectional manner [30]. In this study, we observed significant differences in B cell and macrophage infiltration between responders and non‐responders to NCRT. These findings suggest that targeted modulation of B cells and macrophages could be a potential strategy for the combined use of NCRT and immunotherapy in rectal cancer.

To gain insights into the potential mechanisms of the ICGS, we conducted functional enrichment analysis of the immune cell genes. GO analysis revealed that the B cell genes were closely associated with biological processes involved in immune response regulation, cell components related to MHC proteins, and molecular functions such as MHC protein binding, immune receptor activity, and peptide antigen binding. Additionally, KEGG enrichment analysis demonstrated that B cell genes were significantly involved in various immunological and oncogenic pathways, including Th17 cell differentiation, antigen processing and presentation, Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation, proteoglycans in cancer, intestinal immune network for IgA production, B cell receptor signaling pathway, TGF‐beta signaling pathway, and Hippo signaling pathway. These gene ontologies and pathways have been extensively studied and are known to play crucial roles in tumor immunity and immunotherapy. For instance, the B cell receptor signaling pathway is essential for the proper activation of B cells and the elicitation of immune responses [31]. The equilibrium between Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation is crucial for the immune function of the body, with Th1 cells involved in cytotoxic T cell production and specific IgG, and Th2 cells involved in suppressing cytotoxic T cell traits and participating in antibody production through specific IgE and IgA [32]. Other enriched pathways also play a central role involved in tumor microenvironment and may influence the radiation response and innate immune responses [33, 34, 35, 36]. Moreover, the enriched pathways associated with the macrophage genes were consistent with those of the B cell genes, suggesting a potential synergistic role of B cells and macrophages in NCRT and immunotherapy for rectal cancer. These findings indicate that the ICGS is significantly involved in immune function, immune response, and immunological pathways, and the identified biological processes and signaling pathways associated with immune responses variability may provide valuable guidance for personalized treatment approaches.

To delve deeper into the clinical importance of the ICGS model, we evaluated the ICGS scores in high and low‐risk cohorts and examined the disparities in survival outcomes between these cohorts. The K–M curves illustrated that patients in the high‐risk group experienced significantly inferior survival in comparison to those in the low‐risk group within the training datasets. The AUC value indicated that the ICGS model accurately predicted prognosis. These findings were further validated in independent datasets, confirming the robustness of the ICGS model. Additionally, the ICGS model exhibited good predictive power for the response to NCRT.

Next, we investigated the correlation between ICGS scores and clinical features in TCGA‐READ patients through univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. The results indicated that the ICGS score showed potential as an independent prognostic factor in TCGA‐READ patients. To enhance prognostic prediction, we developed a simplified nomogram that integrated the ICGS risk score, age, gender, and stage. The ROC analysis demonstrated that the ICGS risk score and the nomogram outperformed tumor stage, a traditional prognostic factor widely used in clinical practice. These results highlight the excellent predictive capability of the ICGS model and its potential for clinical application.

Rectal cancer is a highly heterogeneous disease, and genetic variations can influence its clinical characteristics, treatment response, and survival prognosis [37, 38]. We noticed a significant association between a low ICGS score and higher GISTIC scores in patients, as compared to those with a high ICGS score. Furthermore, the group with a low ICGS score demonstrated a greater percentage of copy number gain/loss compared to the group with a high ICGS score, characterized by a higher incidence of copy number loss. These findings suggest that copy number loss is a common genomic alteration in TCGA‐READ patients. Additionally, our findings revealed a higher occurrence of mutations in the high‐risk score group compared to the low‐risk score group. Moreover, the expression of immune checkpoints (CD274, PDCD1, and CTLA4) was significantly higher in the mutant groups of key genes compared to the wild‐type group. These findings offer valuable insights into the possible inherent mechanisms of the ICGS model in rectal cancer patients.

The tumor immune microenvironment is known to be influenced by infiltrating tumor components, which play a crucial role in determining the outcome of tumor immunotherapy. Therefore, we investigated the immune infiltration landscape of each signature gene. Our findings revealed significant correlations between the signature genes and the infiltration of various immune cells, consistent with our previous results. Furthermore, the signature genes were significantly associated with immune microenvironment scores, containing stroma score, immune score, and estimate score. Additionally, we observed significant associations between the signature genes and immune‐related genes, containing immunostimulating genes, immunosuppressive genes, chemokines, chemokine receptors, and MHC genes, all of which play important roles in anti‐tumor immunity [39, 40, 41]. These results provide further evidence that the ICGS model is closely related to the tumor immune microenvironment.

To achieve a more comprehensive understanding of the immunological aspects of the ICGS model, we conducted an exploration of potential immune evasion mechanisms across the entire cohort. Our findings indicated that the group with a high ICGS score exhibited elevated stromal scores, whereas the group with a low ICGS score demonstrated increased tumor purity. Moreover, the expression of immunomodulatory molecules was higher in the high ICGS group compared to the low ICGS group. We also observed a significant positive correlation between the ICGS score and immune checkpoint molecules TNFSF4 and TNFRSF4, whereas the ICGS score was significantly negatively correlated with immune checkpoint molecules PVR, CD47, BTN2A1, and CEACAM1. Nonetheless, we did not detect a statistically meaningful variation in TMB between the high‐risk and low‐risk groups.

To explore the potential utility of the ICGS model in predicting immunotherapy response, we conducted an exploratory analysis using the IMvigor210 dataset, whereas recognizing that its bladder cancer composition differs substantially from rectal cancer. The analysis suggested that the low‐risk group defined by the macrophage signature may be associated with improved survival after anti‐PD‐L1 therapy; however, neither the B cell signature nor the combined signature demonstrated clear prognostic value in this immunotherapy context. No significant differences in risk scores were observed across response subgroups. These observations should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating, and we acknowledge that large‐scale, prospective studies specifically designed for rectal cancer patients receiving both NCRT and immunotherapy are essential to validate the predictive relevance of the ICGS model.

The innovation of our study lies in the identification of diverse immune cells and gene signatures associated with these immune cells in predicting the efficacy of NCRT and survival outcomes, which has not been previously reported. Moreover, our study provides a novel perspective on the immunomodulation mechanisms underlying NCRT in rectal cancer patients. The methods and findings presented in our study will provide a foundation for subsequent studies and will further promote the combination of NCRT and immunotherapy in rectal cancer patients.

However, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, while we included colon cancer datasets as an auxiliary validation cohort due to their larger sample sizes, we recognize that such cross‐cancer validation, although partially justifiable, does not substitute for rectal cancer‐specific assessment. This limits the direct clinical translatability of our findings and underscores the need for independent rectal cancer cohorts to validate the ICGS model. Second, our exploratory analysis of immunotherapy response using the IMvigor210 bladder cancer dataset should be interpreted with caution; these results are hypothesis‐generating rather than conclusive, and rectal cancer‐specific validation is essential before any clinical implications can be drawn. Third, the relatively modest sample sizes in both the training and validation sets, inherent to many retrospective studies, may increase the risk of overfitting and model instability. Larger, well‐designed prospective rectal cancer cohorts are warranted to further validate and refine the ICGS model. Fourth, as this study is based solely on bioinformatics analyses, the proposed “immune escape” mechanisms and the functional roles of the identified immune cells and signature genes have not been experimentally validated through cellular or molecular assays, nor have they been confirmed using clinical tissue microarrays. Future studies incorporating functional experiments and prospective clinical samples will be essential to substantiate our findings and facilitate their translation into clinical practice.

Conclusions

In summary, we developed and validated a novel, clinically meaningful disease stratification model for rectal cancer based on immune cell signatures. The ICGS model showed good efficiency for predicting the NCRT response and the prognosis of rectal cancer. According to our understanding, this is the first study to associate immune cell signatures with classification and risk stratification in the context of NCRT for rectal cancer. The ICGS model may thus provide insights for future investigation to better understand rectal cancer biology and develop better immunotherapeutic approaches in rectal cancer. Because the present cohorts have not contained the immunotherapy data, larger independent cohorts are needed to validate our stratification model and help identify patients who would benefit from NCRT combined with immunotherapy.
