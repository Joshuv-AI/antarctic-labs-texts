Association between RAR and the prevalence and prognosis of suicidal ideation: evidence from a large population-based study
Authors: Dawei Sun, Hongyang Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies have suggested that the red blood cell distribution width to albumin ratio (RAR), a novel inflammatory biomarker, may better predict adverse outcomes in patients with various diseases. Chronic inflammation has been linked to an increased prevalence of suicidal ideation. The weight-adjusted-waist index (WWI), a recently developed anthropometric measure, provides a more accurate assessment of obesity, which is itself a risk factor for suicidal ideation. This study aimed to investigate the association between RAR and suicidal ideation, and to assess whether WWI mediates this relationship.MethodsData from 30,911 participants in the 2005–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) were analyzed. Multivariable logistic regression, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, and subgroup analyses were performed to examine the association between RAR and suicidal ideation. Mediation analysis was used to evaluate the potential mediating role of WWI. Additionally, Cox proportional hazards models were applied to assess the associations of RAR with cardiovascular and all-cause mortality among participants with suicidal ideation.ResultsIn the cross-sectional analysis, 1,172 participants (3.8%) reported suicidal ideation. Weighted multivariable logistic regression revealed an independent association between RAR and suicidal ideation (OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.19–1.62, P < 0.001). Similar results were observed for WWI. RCS analysis indicated a significant linear positive relationship between RAR and suicidal ideation. Mediation analysis showed that WWI accounted for 19.00% of the association. In the cohort analysis, there were 153 all-cause deaths (13%) and 33 cardiovascular deaths (2.8%) among participants with suicidal ideation. Weighted multivariable Cox regression demonstrated that higher RAR was associated with increased risks of all-cause mortality (HR = 2.08, 95% CI: 1.48–2.92, P < 0.001) and cardiovascular mortality (HR = 2.22, 95% CI: 1.14–4.32, P = 0.019).ConclusionRAR was positively associated with both the prevalence of suicidal ideation and mortality among individuals with suicidal ideation. Although this study provides valuable insights into the potential role of RAR in suicidal health, its cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causality. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to confirm these findings and further elucidate the underlying mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07480-1.Keywords: Suicidal ideation, Red blood cell distribution width to albumin ratio, Weight-adjusted-waist index, All-cause mortality, Cardiovascular mortality

Introduction

Red cell distribution width (RDW) is a routinely measured hematological parameter that reflects the degree of heterogeneity in erythrocyte volume. Elevated RDW, resulting from impaired erythropoiesis or abnormal red blood cell survival, has been identified as an independent risk factor for various diseases and for increased all-cause mortality [1]. Recent studies have indicated that RDW can serve as a marker of inflammation and nutritional imbalance in patients with cardiovascular disease [2], sepsis [3], and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [4]. Serum albumin, the most abundant plasma protein, plays a similar role to RDW in reflecting nutritional status and inflammatory responses [5]. Beyond its anti-inflammatory effects, albumin reduces oxidative stress and inhibits endothelial cell apoptosis [6]. The RAR has emerged as a novel inflammatory biomarker [7], demonstrating superior predictive value for adverse outcomes in diverse populations, including those with heart failure, acute coronary syndrome, stroke, and sepsis [8–11].

Suicide is a potentially fatal act initiated through a prolonged and complex psychological process, in which an individual deliberately seeks to end their own life [12]. Globally, the estimated suicide rate is 11.4 per 100,000 individuals, with approximately 804,000 suicide-related deaths annually [13]. Suicidal ideation—which encompasses suicidal thoughts, plans, and preparatory behaviors leading to self-harm [14]—is a critical predictor of suicide attempts and suicide deaths [15]. Given its strong predictive value, suicidal ideation imposes a substantial public health burden [16], underscoring the importance of enhancing preventive strategies. The etiology of suicidal ideation is multifactorial, encompassing psychiatric disorders such as major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, and substance use disorders [17, 18]; biological mechanisms, including neuroinflammation and dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis [19, 20]; psychosocial stressors, such as childhood trauma, social isolation, and low socioeconomic status [21, 22]; as well as environmental and lifestyle factors, including exposure to heavy metals and volatile organic compounds [23]. Previous research has identified significant associations between suicidal ideation and chronic conditions such as liver disease, diabetes, and asthma, with risk increasing alongside the number of comorbidities [24]. Moreover, a recent review by Italian scholars indicated that elevated levels of inflammatory markers are consistently associated with an increased risk of suicidal behaviors across diverse populations [25]. However, no prior studies have investigated the relationship between RAR—an established predictor of adverse outcomes in chronic diseases—and suicidal ideation.

Emerging evidence indicates that obesity is a risk factor for suicide [26, 27]. WWI, first proposed by Park et al. in 2018 [28], is a novel anthropometric indicator of central obesity derived by standardizing waist circumference for body weight. WWI is simple to measure, provides a more accurate assessment of central adiposity, and minimizes collinearity with body mass index (BMI) [28, 29]. Therefore, this study utilized data from the 2005–2018 NHANES to examine the association between RAR and the prevalence of suicidal ideation, with particular attention to the potential mediating role of WWI. In addition, we explored the relationship between RAR and mortality among individuals with suicidal ideation.

Methods

Study participants

Data for this study were obtained from NHANES, administered by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). NHANES is a nationally representative, cross-sectional survey that collects comprehensive health and nutritional data through interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory assessments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study protocol was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Research Ethics Review Board [30].

A total of 70,190 participants from NHANES 2005–2018 were initially screened. Individuals younger than 20 years or pregnant (n = 31,152), those with missing RAR or WWI data (n = 5,751), and those with incomplete information on suicidal ideation or mortality (n = 2,376) were excluded. The final analytic sample comprised 30,911 participants (Fig. 1).

A flow diagram of eligible participant selection in the National health and nutrition examination survey. Abbreviation:RAR Red blood cell distribution width to albumin ratio, WWI Weight-adjusted waist index

Definition of RAR

Blood samples were collected as part of the NHANES Mobile Examination Center (MEC) protocol. RDW was measured using a Beckman Coulter MAXM analyzer, which employs an automated process including cell counting, size measurement, dilution, and mixing. Serum albumin concentration was determined using the DcX800 method, a bichromatic digital endpoint colorimetric assay based on the reaction of bromocresol purple (BCP) with albumin. RAR was defined as the ratio of RDW to serum albumin concentration [31].

Assessment of WWI

WWI is a novel anthropometric indicator reflecting abdominal obesity independent of body weight. WWI was calculated as waist circumference (cm) divided by the square root of body weight (kg). Anthropometric measurements were obtained by trained technicians at the MEC, with proficiency regularly evaluated [32].

Definition of suicidal ideation

Suicidal ideation was assessed using the question: “Over the last two weeks, how often have you been bothered by thoughts that you would be better off dead or of hurting yourself in some way?” Responses of “several days,” “more than half the days,” or “nearly every day” were classified as indicative of suicidal ideation [33].

Covariables

According to the published research [23, 34], covariates included age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital/cohabitation status, education level, poverty income ratio, hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia. The specific definitions of these covariates can be found in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

All analyses incorporated appropriate sampling weights, as recommended in the NHANES analytic guidelines, to account for the complex, multistage sampling design and to ensure nationally representative estimates. Participants were categorized according to the presence or absence of suicidal ideation, and demographic and clinical characteristics were compared using the weighted chi-square test for categorical variables and the weighted t-test for continuous variables.

Three multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to examine the associations of RAR and WWI with suicidal ideation: Model 1 was unadjusted (crude model); Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, education level, marital status, poverty income ratio, and race/ethnicity; and Model 3 further adjusted for smoking, drinking, depression, anxiety, hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia. Multiple linear regression was used to assess the association between RAR and WWI. RCS models were applied to explore potential linear or nonlinear relationships between RAR and suicidal ideation. Subgroup analyses stratified by factors such as age and sex were performed to assess the robustness and heterogeneity of the associations.

Weighted multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the associations of RAR with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among participants with suicidal ideation. Mediation analysis was conducted to investigate the potential mediating role of WWI in the relationship between RAR and suicidal ideation. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In Model 3, each unit increase in RAR was associated with a 1.39-fold higher prevalence of suicidal ideation (OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.19–1.62). Compared with the lowest tertile (T1), participants in the highest tertile of RAR (T3) had a 1.34-fold higher prevalence (OR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.05–1.73), with a significant positive trend (p for trend = 0.019). For WWI, each unit increase was associated with a 1.26-fold higher prevalence (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.12–1.42), and participants in the highest tertile had a 1.50-fold higher prevalence compared with those in the lowest tertile (OR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.18–1.91, p for trend = 0.001).

Figure S1 presents the results of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. RAR, C-reactive protein (CRP), and Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) all demonstrated significant discriminatory ability in predicting suicidal ideation, with AUC values of 0.543 (95% CI, 0.526–0.560), 0.521 (95% CI, 0.503–0.538), and 0.528 (95% CI, 0.501–0.554), respectively. Among them, RAR exhibited the highest predictive value.

Mediation effect

As shown in Fig. 4, mediation analysis was conducted with RAR as the independent variable, suicidal ideation as the dependent variable, and WWI as the mediator. After adjustment for all covariates, WWI demonstrated a significant mediating effect (indirect effect = 8.67 × 10⁻⁴, p = 0.002; direct effect = 3.54 × 10⁻³, p < 0.001), accounting for 19.00% of the total effect (p = 0.002). These findings indicate that WWI partially mediates the association between RAR and suicidal ideation.

The relationship between RAR and mortality

Cox proportional hazards models (Table 4) revealed that higher RAR was significantly associated with increased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in the overall cohort (all-cause mortality: HR = 2.01, 95% CI: 1.81–2.23; cardiovascular mortality: HR = 2.71, 95% CI: 2.36–3.12), in participants with suicidal ideation (all-cause mortality: HR = 2.08, 95% CI: 1.48–2.92; cardiovascular mortality: HR = 2.22, 95% CI: 1.14–4.32), and in those without suicidal ideation (all-cause mortality: HR = 2.00, 95% CI: 1.80–2.22; cardiovascular mortality: HR = 2.74, 95% CI: 2.34–3.19). All models demonstrated significant linear trends (p for trend < 0.001).

RCS analysis (Figure S2) showed a significant linear positive association between RAR and all-cause mortality in the suicidal ideation group. Subgroup analyses yielded consistent results (Figure S3). Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the associations between RAR, WWI, and suicidal ideation, as well as their relationships with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. We found that higher RAR was positively associated with an increased prevalence of suicidal ideation, and that WWI partially mediated this association. Moreover, RAR was significantly associated with both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among individuals with suicidal ideation.

RAR, a composite biomarker calculated from red cell distribution width (RDW) and serum albumin, has emerged as a novel prognostic indicator for various inflammatory and critical illnesses [10, 35]. Increasing evidence suggests that RAR is closely linked to the prognosis of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases such as coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, stroke, and diabetes, with lower RAR levels predicting better short- and long-term survival [11, 36, 37]. RDW reflects the heterogeneity of erythrocyte size and, beyond its utility in the differential diagnosis of anemia, has been associated with systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and adverse outcomes in sepsis, cardiovascular disease, and cancer [38]. Serum albumin, an established marker of nutritional and inflammatory status, is known to decline in critically ill patients [39]. Previous studies have shown that higher RDW [40–43] and lower serum albumin concentrations [44–46] are independently associated with increased risks of chronic disease and mortality in the general population. Importantly, RAR appears to outperform RDW or albumin alone in predicting adverse outcomes and all-cause mortality [37].

Mental health has gained increasing research attention in recent years. A large Chinese cohort study reported that depression is significantly associated with both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [37], and a U.S. study similarly highlighted the elevated risk of premature death among individuals with depression [47]. Elevated inflammation and poor nutritional status may synergistically exacerbate depression severity [48], and both factors have been linked to prognosis in depressed patients [47]. According to the U.S. National Institute of Mental Health, suicidal ideation—defined as thinking about, considering, or planning suicide—is closely associated with depression. Consistent with prior reports, we found that suicidal ideation was significantly associated with both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Suicidal ideation has also been linked to chronic illnesses and functional limitations [49]. Notably, our study is the first to demonstrate a positive association between RAR and suicidal ideation (P < 0.05). Furthermore, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis from Iran reported that patients exhibiting suicidal behavior had elevated CRP levels [50]. A recent retrospective observational study from Japan indicated that the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was independently associated with suicide (OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.04–1.10) [51]. In the present study, ROC curve analysis demonstrated that the AUC of RAR exceeded those of CRP and NLR, further supporting the predictive value of RAR for suicidal ideation.

Obesity, a prevalent and costly chronic disease affecting both adults and children, has been implicated in worsening inflammatory responses when coexisting with depression. This interaction has been shown to lead to poorer prognosis and higher suicide risk in adolescents with major depressive disorder [52]. Some studies further suggest that obesity may cause insulin dysregulation, which is linked to depression [53]. Adipose tissue also secretes various adipokines that can directly influence other organs, including the brain [54]. WWI, a novel anthropometric index of central obesity, has been reported to be positively associated with suicidal ideation [33]. In agreement with these findings, our study showed that WWI indirectly increased the prevalence of suicidal ideation and acted as a mediator between RAR and suicidal ideation.

Our study has several strengths. First, to our knowledge, this is the first population-based study to simultaneously investigate the relationships of RAR and WWI with suicidal ideation, thereby providing new evidence on the potential links between inflammatory status, nutritional status, obesity patterns, and mental health. Second, we utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), which features a large sample size, broad geographic coverage, and national representativeness, ensuring the external generalizability of our findings. Third, we adjusted for a wide range of demographic, lifestyle, and clinical covariates to minimize residual confounding. Finally, our subgroup and sensitivity analyses demonstrated consistent results across different populations and analytic models, further supporting the robustness of our conclusions.

Nevertheless, this study has limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between RAR, WWI, and suicidal ideation, warranting confirmation in prospective cohort and interventional studies. Second, despite comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., psychiatric history, social support) cannot be ruled out. Third, inherent limitations of NHANES, such as reliance on self-reported variables (introducing recall or reporting bias), restriction of mental health data to specific survey cycles, and potential selection bias due to missing data, may influence the interpretation and generalizability of our results.

Conclusion

Using nationally representative NHANES data, we systematically evaluated the relationship between RAR and suicidal ideation for the first time. Higher RAR levels were significantly associated with increased prevalence of suicidal ideation and higher mortality. These findings suggest that RAR, as a composite biomarker reflecting both inflammatory and nutritional status, may hold potential value in suicide assessment and prevention strategies. However, given the cross-sectional nature of our study, causal relationships cannot be established. Future longitudinal and mechanistic studies are needed to elucidate the biological pathways underlying the RAR–suicidal ideation link and to explore its clinical implications.
