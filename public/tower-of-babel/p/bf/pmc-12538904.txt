Advanced stage melanoma during pregnancy: recommendations from a retrospective, multicentre, registry-based study
Authors: Jessica SW Borgers, Douglas B Johnson, Elisabeth Livingstone, Dirk Schadendorf, Elizabeth I Buchbinder, Lauren E Haydu, Laura Gohil, William A Robinson, Inderjit Mehmi, Omid Hamid, Michelle Ferreira, April KS Salama, Florentia Dimitriou, Matteo S Carlino, Georgina V Long, Paolo A Ascierto, Ester Simeone, Yana G Najjar, Geke AP Hospers, Jacob Choi, Sunandana Chandra, Jeffrey D Wayne, Carlos IR García, Wen Xu, Céleste Lebbé, Laetitia Da-Meda, Victoria Atkinson, Meredith Mckean, Andrea Forschner, Elliot A Asare, Antonios Daletzakis, Jolyn S Taylor, Frédéric Amant, Katy K Tsai, Zeynep Eroglu, John BAG Haanen, Isabella C Glitza de Oliva

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundDiagnosis and treatment of advanced melanoma during pregnancy represents a major challenge, with scarce data on outcome or management available. Therefore, the Melanoma Pregnancy Taskforce collected real-world data to understand the current management approaches.MethodsThis retrospective, multicenter database included patients who were either diagnosed with advanced melanoma (newly diagnosed or recurrent) during pregnancy (group 1) or conceived while on systemic treatment (group 2) between 01/01/2011 and 07/31/2024. Information regarding patient, primary tumor and advanced melanoma characteristics, intervention or treatment during pregnancy, and outcomes of mother and fetus were collected using a standardized, de-identified form.Findings72 women from seven countries were identified. In group 1 (n = 63), nine pregnancies were terminated, and two miscarried. Median gestational age of live births was 34 weeks (IQR 32–37). Nine patients initiated systemic treatment during pregnancy; 56 (85%) of 66 switched or initiated new treatment after delivery. 5-year overall survival was 53% (95% CI 39–71), with median follow-up of 27 months (IQR 1.0–4.8 years). In group 2 (n = 10), four pregnancies were terminated, one miscarried. Of the six live births, median gestational age was 33 weeks (IQR 32–NR). Seven (63%) of nine initiated new or continued treatment after delivery. 5-year overall survival was 71% (95% CI 51–100), with median follow-up of 42 months (IQR 2.5–4.5 years). One neonate born at 26 weeks died after three days and one had a fatal congenital malformation. One child with third trimester vemurafenib-exposure had cardiac malformations, and two neonates experienced toxicity after in utero immunotherapy exposure. No children developed melanoma, despite five placentas with melanoma involvement.InterpretationHere, we present the data from the largest contemporary dataset of outcomes of women diagnosed with advanced stage melanoma during pregnancy and provide recommendations for the diagnosis and management of advanced melanoma during pregnancy.FundingNot applicable.Keywords: Melanoma, Pregnancy, Treatment, Outcomes, Guidelines, Immunotherapy

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Melanoma accounts for up to 25% of malignancies identified during pregnancy. Despite this, guidance on diagnosis and treatment of advanced stage melanoma during pregnancy is largely lacking, resulting in heterogeneity in management. We searched PubMed for manuscripts published in English using the terms “pregnancy”, “melanoma”, “pregnancy-associated melanoma”, “lactation”, “fertility” and “treatment”, and checked references from relevant articles. We found that pregnant patients are often excluded from clinical trials, and studies on pregnant women with melanoma generally involve small sample sizes, limited data on advanced disease, or lack information on modern treatments or outcomes.

Added value of this study

To our knowledge, this study presents the largest contemporary dataset of real-world outcomes of women diagnosed with advanced stage melanoma during pregnancy and provides an overview of the available literature regarding diagnosis and treatment of melanoma during pregnancy, in an attempt to provide consensus guidance on how to best treat pregnant patients with advanced melanoma.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings provide insight into real-world management and outcomes of women with advanced stage melanoma during pregnancy. Our dataset, combined with existing evidence and expert opinion, could be used as initial clinical discussion point for recommendations regarding the management of these patients. Expanding our dataset and further follow-up of the patients included is warranted to develop definitive guidelines for this challenging clinical scenario.

Introduction

With an incidence of 17–31 per 100,000 women in Europe, North America and Oceania,1 melanoma represents one of the most common malignancies in women of childbearing potential, accounting for up to 25% of malignancies identified during pregnancy.2 In general, the oncological workup investigation and management for pregnant patients presenting with a suspected primary melanoma is similar to non-pregnant patients.3 However, for advanced stage patients, the lack of data and evidence-based guidelines leads to heterogeneity in management and represents a major challenge. The incidence of melanoma during pregnancy is rising, and combined with the lack of data on safety and efficacy of modern systemic therapies in this patient population, represents a pressing problem.4 Therefore, we have initiated the Melanoma Pregnancy Taskforce, consisting of melanoma specialists from various countries, with the aim to gather data on melanoma and pregnancy, and to attempt consensus guidance on how to best treat pregnant patients with advanced melanoma. Our ultimate goal is to develop evidence-based treatment recommendations for women with either newly diagnosed, or recurrent advanced stage melanoma, who require treatment or intervention during their pregnancy, or are already receiving systemic therapy (in adjuvant or metastatic setting) for stage II, III or IV melanoma at time of conception or pregnancy.

Methods

Study design and participants

In this retrospective, multicenter database, patients with advanced melanoma who either required an intervention or treatment during pregnancy (group 1), or conceived while on systemic therapy (group 2) between January 1st 2011 and July 31st 2024, were included. The Melanoma Pregnancy Taskforce invited melanoma physicians in their network, and reached out to other melanoma sites across the world.

Data collection and processing

Information regarding patient characteristics, primary tumor characteristics, advanced melanoma staging, intervention/treatment provided during pregnancy, and the outcomes of mother and fetus was collected (Supplementary File 1). After submission, forms were combined, reviewed, and centrally managed at the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center. In case of inconsistencies or missing information, additional information was requested. At time of data cut-off, data was securely sent to the Netherlands Cancer Institute for analysis.

Development of recommendations

Recommendations were developed using a combination of our data presented in this manuscript, an extensive literature search, and expert opinion. For the literature search, references were identified through searches of PubMed with the search terms “pregnancy” AND “melanoma”, “pregnancy-associated melanoma”, “cancer”, “oncology”, “diagnosis”, “surgery”, “radiotherapy”, “T-VEC”, “isolated limb perfusion”, “immunotherapy”, “immune checkpoint inhibitors”, “targeted therapy”, “case reports”, “lactation”, and “fertility”. The search was restricted to full text, peer-reviewed papers (including case reports) published in English and published between Jan 1, 1995, and Jun 31, 2025. Additional articles were identified by individual authors through searches of their own files. Inclusion criteria for the literature review were studies involving pregnant women with melanoma, or studies reporting on the diagnostics and treatments discussed in the manuscript in pregnant patients. Studies focusing on non-pregnant patients or irrelevant diagnostics or treatments were excluded. As this was not a systematic review, the initial screening process and data extraction was conducted by a single reviewer, without the use of automation tools. The final selection of papers was approved by two additional authors.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 29.0.0.0). Descriptive statistics were used to describe the two cohorts. Kaplan Meier estimates were used for survival analyses (R version 4.4.0). Maternal overall survival was defined as number of days between advanced melanoma diagnosis and date of last follow-up or date of death; for children from date of birth to last follow-up or death. Figures were created with R and BioRender.

Ethics

This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the institutional review boards at the two coordinating centers (University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center, USA—Human Subjects Assurance Number FWA00000363 and MD Anderson protocol PA13-0041; and the Netherlands Cancer Institute, The Netherlands—NKI-AVL Institutional Review Board (IRB) IRBd21-176), and at each participating center per their institutional guidelines. The need for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study using only de-identified data.

Role of the funding source

There was no funding source, and therefore funding had no role in the study design, data collection, data analyses, interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

23 institutions from seven countries (Australia, Chile, France, Germany, Italy, Netherlands, and USA) contributed seventy-two patients who met the inclusion criteria: 63 unique cases in group 1 with a total of 66 pregnancies, and 10 cases in group 2 with 11 pregnancies. One patient was included in both groups (diagnosed with advanced melanoma during her first pregnancy, second conception while receiving systemic treatment). Median age at initial melanoma diagnosis was 32 years (IQR 28–35) for group 1, and 24 years (IQR 18–28) for group 2. Information on the primary melanoma staging, location, subtype, Breslow thickness, ulceration, and mutation status can be found in Supplementary Table S1. 57 of the 63 patients (91%) in group 1 underwent treatment for their primary melanoma: 56 (89%) had surgery and three (5%) had radiotherapy. In group 2, 10 patients (100%) had received systemic treatment before pregnancy, compared to 17 patients (27%) in group 1 (Supplementary Table S1).

During pregnancy, the majority of patients were diagnosed with either stage III (n = 32/66, 49%) or stage IV (n = 33/66, 50%) melanoma (see Fig. 1 and Table 1 for substaging), while one patient was diagnosed with a stage II mucosal primary of the vagina. Biopsy was the most applied diagnostic modality during pregnancy (65%), followed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI, 30%), computed tomography scan (CT, 21%), ultrasound (21%), positron emission tomography (PET)-CT (14%), and chest X-ray (5%). At time of advanced stage melanoma diagnosis, the majority had normal LDH levels (n = 36/66, 55%), but six patients had elevated serum LDH (9%), with the rest being unknown. 40 (61%) of 66 patients had an ECOG performance score of 0, six (9%) had a score of 1, and two (3%) had a score of 2 (unknown in 18 [27%]).

In group 2, four of the eleven patients (36%) were receiving systemic treatment at time of conception for stage III melanoma and seven (64%) for stage IV. All patients had an ECOG performance status of 0 (82%) or 1 (18%), and only one patient had an elevated LDH serum level at time of advanced melanoma diagnosis (Fig. 1, Table 1).

Median age at time of pregnancy discovery was 32 years in both groups. An overview of the pregnancy years can be found in Supplementary Table S2. Four twin pregnancies occurred in group 1, one in group 2. In 25 (out of 66, 38%) of the group 1 patients the advanced melanoma diagnoses occurred during the first trimester, followed by third (n = 21, 32%), second (n = 15, 23%), and unknown (n = 5, 7.6%) trimester. Fifty-five (out of 66, 83%) of pregnancies in group 1 resulted in live birth, while nine patients (14%) terminated their pregnancy (including two twin pregnancies), and two patients (3.0%) had a miscarriage. In 4/11 cases (36%) non-live births were reported to be melanoma-related. Median gestational age for patients who had a miscarriage or termination was 12 weeks (IQR 10–22). Of the 55 live births, 53% delivered preterm (defined as gestational age < 37 weeks). The median gestational age across all live births in group 1 was 34 weeks (IQR 32–37). Deliveries were induced in 36/55 (66%) of live births; 20 (36%) delivered vaginally and 27 (49%) via C-section, including two emergency C-sections (unknown in 15%; Table 1).

Six pregnancies (55%) in group 2 resulted in live birth. One third of the live births was preterm, with a median gestational age at time of delivery of 33 weeks (IQR 32–NR). One patient (17%) was induced; four patients (67%) delivered vaginally and two (33%) via C-section. Four pregnancies (36%) were terminated, two because of melanoma, and one ended in a miscarriage after first trimester nivolumab exposure (9%) (Table 2).

In group 1, nine (of 66; 14%) patients initiated treatment during pregnancy: five (56%) with targeted treatment (dabrafenib n = 3; vemurafenib n = 2), and four (44%) with immunotherapy (anti-CTLA-4/anti-PD-1 n = 2; anti-PD-1 n = 2). Of the patients treated with targeted therapy, one patient with stage IV disease had stable disease as best response while pregnant, and one patient with unresectable stage III disease had a partial response; other responses are unknown. The two patients treated with anti-PD-1 monotherapy had a partial response and no-relapse during pregnancy; the two patients treated with combination checkpoint inhibitor therapy had progressive disease. After a median of 28 days (IQR 13–74) after delivery or termination, 56/66 (85%) patients initiated a new systemic treatment. First-line treatments after pregnancy consisted of immunotherapy in 35 (63%), targeted therapy in 16 (29%), and chemotherapy in 4 (7%) (Fig. 1, Table 2). Of note, none of the patients who were diagnosed during the third trimester initiated systemic treatment during pregnancy; most (19/21 [91%]) patients received systemic treatment after delivery, with a median time from delivery to treatment start of 35 days (IQR 12–86).

In group 2, at time of conception, nine patients (82%) were receiving immunotherapy and two (18%) targeted therapy (Fig. 1, Table 2). During pregnancy, one of the four patients with stage III disease progressed. Of the patients with stage IV disease, one was in complete remission, one partial response, and one progressed during pregnancy; the rest are unknown. None of the patients initiated new systemic treatments during pregnancy, but seven (63%) did restart their pre-pregnancy treatment or initiated a new treatment after delivery or termination at a median of 59 days (IQR 9–121): four patients restarted their previous treatment (anti-PD-1 n = 3, dabrafenib/trametinib n = 1), while three patients switched treatments (anti-CTLA-4/anti-PD-1 to BRAF/MEKi [n = 1] and single agent anti-PD-1 [n = 1], and vemurafenib to either anti-PD-1 or anti-CTLA-4 in a clinical trial setting [n = 1]).

5-year overall survival in group 1 was 53% (95% CI 39–71%), with a median follow-up of 27 months (IQR 1.0–4.8 years; Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. S1). Response evaluations were performed in 48/66 (73%) patients, most commonly using CT scan (83%). After delivery, the patient with stage II mucosal melanoma of the vagina quickly progressed to stage IV disease and was therefore included in the dataset, as she likely had already metastatic disease at time of delivery. Of the stage III patients, nine (29%) showed no relapse, six (19%) PR, and 11 (35%) disease relapse after a median of 181 days (IQR 70–509). In five cases (16%) the response was unknown. Of the stage IV patients, five (16%) had complete response as best response, 12 (38%) partial response, one (3.1%) stable disease, and ten (31%) progressive disease after a median of 70 days (IQR 54–154). In four cases, treatment response was unknown. An overview of responses can be found in Table 2. Of the patients receiving treatment during pregnancy, physicians reported ten treatment-related grade 3–4 toxicities during pregnancy and 13 grade 1–2 toxicities (this is likely underreported as the form specifically asked for grade 3–4 toxicities). The grade 3–4 toxicities included hepatitis (n = 5), fever (n = 3), skin toxicity and arthritis (n = 2), hypophysitis (n = 2), colitis, leukopenia, meningitis, myocarditis, adrenal insufficiency, tumor lysis syndrome and panniculitis (n = 1, each). Ten patients required corticosteroids, three patients were hospitalized. Other toxicity-directed treatments included infliximab, mycophenolate mofetil, tocilizumab, and hormonal replacement (Supplementary Table S3).

5-year overall survival in group 2 was 71% (95% CI 51–100%), with a median follow-up of 42 months (IQR 2.5–4.5 years). After pregnancy, two stage III patients relapsed (one local recurrence, one stage IV), and two patients remained disease-free. Of the stage IV patients, four had a complete response as best overall response, one partial response, and one progressive disease. Three patients experienced grade 3–4 toxicities: colitis, thrombocytopenia, and panniculitis/arthralgia/rash/keratosis pilaris (n = 1, each), for which corticosteroids were administered in all cases, and infliximab in one case.

At data cutoff (07/2024), 55 (97%) of 57 children in group 1 that were alive at time of birth were still alive with a median follow-up of 1.8 years (IQR 167 days–4.3 years), whereas one neonate born at 26 weeks deceased three days after birth, and one neonate has missing vital status after birth. In group 2, six of the seven (86%) children were alive, with a median follow-up of 1.7 years (294 days–4.4 years); one child deceased after seven days due to a fatal congenital heart defect, more likely associated with maternal methamphetamine, pregabalin, and hydroxychloroquine use during pregnancy, than first trimester exposure to combination immunotherapy. Moreover, four children without in utero exposure to systemic treatment were noted to have some congenital morbidity, including prematurity-related (n = 3) and congenital hypothyroidism (n = 1). One child with 3rd trimester vemurafenib exposure had an atrial septal defect, a patent ductus arteriosus, and Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome. Furthermore, two children experienced toxicity after in utero immunotherapy exposure (Table 2): one child developed hypothyroidism for which thyroid hormone supplementation was required (group 2, exposed to nivolumab), and one child exposed to pembrolizumab from week 16 until the end of the third trimester developed a severe immune-related gastro-enterocolitis, for which prolonged admission, total parental nutrition, corticosteroids and infliximab was necessary (case report by Baarslag and colleagues5). Of the 31 placentas pathologically examined, 5/31 in group 1 were positive for melanoma (16%). However, at data cut-off, none of the children had developed melanoma.

Discussion

Despite the increasing incidence of melanoma during pregnancy4 and the rapidly evolving treatment landscape, guidance on best practices is largely lacking. Pregnant patients are typically excluded from clinical trials, and previous publications reporting on the outcomes of pregnant women with melanoma typically included low numbers of patients,6, 7, 8 small percentages of patients with advanced disease (or unspecified stages),6,7,9, 10, 11 patients before the era of targeted therapy and immunotherapy,12,13 and/or provided no information on outcome.9

As with any restrospective data collection, some information considered important was incomplete, or missing altogether. This includes the intent of systemic therapy (adjuvant/palliative) for patients with stage III disease (no evidence of disease/unresectable), and the specific reasons for preterm birth or melanoma-related abortions. Moreover, while we tried to contact as many physicians as possible to collect cases, a selection bias could have been introduced based on the sites we reached out to and/or the sites that submitted cases. Furthermore, despite our efforts to include as many cases as possible, the number is still limited to perform more robust statistical analyses between the two groups or to compare our findings to what is known in non-pregnant patients with melanoma. However, our 72 unique cases of patients diagnosed with advanced melanoma during pregnancy or conceived while on systemic therapy represent, to our knowledge, the largest contemporary dataset, providing support for the development of clinical recommendations.

When patients are diagnosed with advanced stage melanoma during pregnancy, treatment plans should be made by multi-disciplinary teams of experts in the treatment of melanoma and obstetric care in oncological settings. An important decision to be made, taking into account the gestational age, maternal health risk, and local legal framework regarding termination options, is whether or not to proceed with the pregnancy. If the decision has been made to terminate the pregnancy, oncological treatments can follow the established guidelines for melanoma. However, when the decision is made to proceed with the pregnancy, the management, including diagnosis and treatment, based on data currently in the literature and on expert opinions, should be individualized, taking into consideration the extent of maternal disease, gestational age, fetal development, and the patient’s wishes, as certain standard-of-care management approaches may cause fetal harm.

In general, the serum biomarkers S100B (if part of local practice) and LDH can be used to support the suspected diagnosis, assessment of treatment response, and/or detecting recurrences as these markers remain below normal cut-off values in uncomplicated pregnancies.14,15 Sudden changes in S100B or LDH during pregnancy therefore require further investigation. However, in case of (pre)eclampsia or HELLP syndrome (hemolysis, elevated liver function tests, low platelets), these values can be elevated and therefore cannot be used for melanoma follow-up.14,15 Moreover, the use of liquid biopsy for cancer detection and monitoring is increasing; while this can be considered in pregnant patients, the technology is not mature enough to replace imaging.16

Accurate staging is of vital importance, as this determines the prognosis and decision-making regarding treatment. During pregnancy, non-ionizing imaging modalities such as ultrasound and MRI are the preferred imaging options (Fig. 2).17 Assessment of locoregional lymph nodes and the liver using ultrasound can be considered when the risk of distant metastases is low. In case of MRI, gadolinium-contrast should be avoided as it can cross the placenta with a risk of accumulating in the amniotic fluid and dissociating into free ions that can harm the fetus (pregnancy category C).18 Alternatives are diffusion-weighted imaging or non-contrast black-blood imaging sequences.17 When MRI is contraindicated or a CT scan is preferred, the cumulative fetal radiation dose should be below 0.10 Gy to avoid deterministic effects, and to minimize the risk of stochastic (i.e., childhood cancer and leukemia) effects.17 Fetal risks are dependent on gestational age and radiation dose. Growth restriction, microcephaly, and mental retardation are the most common radiation-induced adverse events, with highest risk of occurring with exposure between week 8–15, after which it decreases with gestational age. CT scans of areas that do not include the uterus, have fetal exposure below this threshold.19 Since PET-CT uses both radioisotopes and CT, the total fetal radiation dose is higher than when using CT alone.17 If (18F)-FDG PET-CT has potential therapeutic consequences, hyperhydration, the use furosemide and/or a urinary catheter is recommended to avoid tracer-accumulation in the bladder.20 In general, consultation with radiation physicists to calculate the total fetal radiation dose and to see whether adjustments can be made to lower this are important (i.e., low-dosage CT or target-organ scanning).

Excisional biopsies of any primary melanoma should be performed without delay at any time throughout the pregnancy as this allows for histopathologic analysis for staging.21 Subsequent wide local excision of primary melanomas may be indicated, and can also be performed safely in every trimester.21 Likewise, there are no contra-indications to perform sentinel node procedures using technetium 99 m; however, the use of blue dye should be avoided.22

For stage III, lymph node dissections can be performed, preferably in the second trimester as this minimizes the risk of spontaneous abortion or preterm labor associated with general anesthesia in the first/third trimester, respectively.23 Importantly, pelvic lymphadenectomy can be considered until 22 weeks of gestation, but should be postponed due to the size of the uterus at later stages of pregnancy.23 Palliative surgery is possible in most cases regardless of gestation age. Post-operatively, providing adequate pain management, intravenous hydration, and thromboprophylaxis is important until the patient is ambulant.24

If radiotherapy is indicated, this can in theory be performed as long as the fetus is not in the radiation field.25 However, as a result of internal scatter, scatter from the collimator, and leakage from the linear accelerator head, fetal exposure might be unavoidable for which careful planning is essential, taking the source of irradiation, treatment field, and proximity to the fetus into consideration. For stereotactic gamma-knife treatment with a tumor-dose of 25 Gy, fetal doses range from 0.0015 to 0.0031 Gy.26 In comparison, malformations or IQ decrease can occur above 0.1–0.2 Gy.25 Of note, specifically in the first trimester there is an increased risk of miscarriage, malformations, or developmental delays (organogenesis, gestational age 2–8 weeks) for exposures as low as 0.05 Gy.27 Furthermore, while data is limited, a recent case shows that proton therapy could significantly limit the fetal dose compared to photon therapy and may therefore be considered during pregnancy.28

Intralesional talimogene laherparepvec (T-VEC) is a genetically engineered herpes simplex virus type-1 (HSV-1) that is able to selectively replicate in and lyse tumor cells and promote anti-tumor immune responses. Wildtype HSV-1 infections have been observed in patients treated with T-VEC, and HSV-1 is known to cross the placenta.29 Viral shedding can lead to vaginal transmission during birth, which could result in serious neonatal adverse events, such as viral encephalitis, multi-organ failure, and death.29 While infections caused by T-VEC are unlikely to result in these severe complications, T-VEC should be avoided during pregnancy when possible due to unknown fetal risks.30

While none of our patients had undergone isolated limb perfusion (ILP) or infusion (ILI) and its overall use has declined in recent years, it can be considered in case of unresectable locoregional metastases of the limb, though there is limited experience during pregnancy. By isolating the affected limb using a tourniquet, high concentrations of heated melphalan and tumor necrosis factor-α (ILP) or actinomycin D (ILI) can be reached locally, while minimizing systemic toxicities as seen with traditional systemic administration. As compared to ILP, the ILI procedure is less complex, takes approximately 1 h as compared to four-six hours, does not require blood transfusion, and is possible with regional anesthesia, making this the preferred option during pregnancy.31 One case report describes a patient with soft tissue sarcoma of the left forearm, who was successfully treated with ILI in the second trimester and delivered a healthy infant.32

Systemic treatment options for melanoma share pathways necessary for successful pregnancy, including cell proliferation, vascular supply, and immune-tolerance (Table 3). However, when patients present with unresectable stage III or stage IV melanoma, systemic treatment during pregnancy can be considered in an experienced center after extensive multidisciplinary and patient/family counselling. In early stage disease settings ((neo)adjuvant indication), it is generally advisable to avoid systemic treatment or to withhold until delivery. Important to take into consideration are the gestational age, drug characteristics, transplacental transfer, and the pregnancy-associated changes in pharmacokinetics (i.e., expansion of plasma volume, enhanced renal excretion, altered liver metabolism, and slower gastric motility).24,62

Patients with an activating mutation in the BRAF oncogene, resulting in cell proliferation and metastasis via the mitogen-activated protein kinase pathway, are eligible for treatment with BRAF-inhibitors combined with a mitogen-activated protein kinase (MEK)-inhibitor, as per current guidelines.63,64 During pregnancy, the mitogen-activated protein kinase pathway plays an important role in trophoblast invasion, placenta development, vascular endothelial cell proliferation, and fetal development.34 According to the corresponding prescribing information, animal studies have shown that the MEK-inhibitors trametinib and cobimetinib, and the BRAF-inhibitor dabrafenib are teratogenic and should therefore be avoided during pregnancy (Table 3). Furthermore, animal studies also show increased post-implantation losses and fetal malformations at doses 8–178 times the clinical dose of encorafenib and binimetinib. On the other hand, four case reports describe the use of the BRAF-inhibitor vemurafenib, initiated between week 17 and 25 for the treatment of maternal metastatic melanoma (Table 4).35, 36, 37, 38 Despite the transplacental transfer leading to fetal serum drug concentrations up to 50% of the maternal concentration,35 no malformations have been reported. Preterm birth was observed in all neonates (26, 30, 34, and 36 weeks), in some combined with fetal growth restriction, and one neonate developed paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia. At last follow-up, all children were reported to be doing well. Regarding the mothers, partial response or stable disease was the best observed response during pregnancy, in three cases followed by rapid disease progression shortly after delivery. Most side effects were mild, including itching, exanthema, and gastrointestinal side-effects, but one patient developed a fatal extensive toxic epidermal necrolysis requiring intensive care support during pregnancy.36 Importantly, in our dataset one of the two fetus in utero exposed to vemurafenib (from week 26 until delivery at week 34) was born with an atrial septal defect, patent ductus arteriosus, and Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome.

Immune-checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) targeting PD-1, PD-L1, CLTA-4, and LAG-3 can enhance T-cell-mediated antitumor responses. However, during pregnancy these checkpoints also play vital roles in maternal tolerance to the semi-allogeneic fetus (‘feto-maternal tolerance’) (Table 3).40,56 Interference with these pathways during pregnancy can therefore lead to adverse pregnancy outcomes. In non-human primate studies, growth restriction, premature delivery, and increased risk of embryonal, fetal and neonatal death have been observed, without signs of malformations, neurological complications, or immunological deficiencies in surviving offspring.65,66 To date, there are a handful of case reports of patients with metastatic melanoma who either conceived while on ICI or initiated treatment during pregnancy (Table 4). In four cases placental insufficiency and/or intrauterine growth restriction was observed, for which C-sections were performed between 24 and 33 weeks.42,43,45,46 Furthermore, two patients required systemic immunosuppression to treat ICI-induced liver toxicity43,67 In case of an immune-related adverse event (irAE) during pregnancy, it is important to take into consideration that prednisone up to 20 mg per day is considered safe, while commonly required higher doses (1–2 mg/kg) are associated with gestational diabetes, and oral-facial clefts, or dysfunction of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis in the fetus.68 In addition, most second or third line immunosuppressive agents other than infliximab (category B) are classified as pregnancy category C or D.69 Importantly, most case reports describe ICI-administration in the first or second trimester, where the potential fetal impact is expected to be minimal due to absence of IgG-transporting neonatal Fc receptors on the placenta during the first 14 weeks, after which the expression increases steadily.41 The highest ICI-transplacental transfer therefore occurs during the third trimester, with fetal IgG concentrations typically exceeding maternal concentrations at term.41,70 One report describes the first case of an infant with severe immune-related gastroenterocolitis, after in utero exposure to anti-PD-1 until the late third trimester.5 This case illustrates that ICI also have the potential to induce irAEs in offspring, as has previously been hypothesized by Xu et al. where the neonate developed a possible immune-related congenital hypothyroidism.42 ICI should therefore be used with great caution during pregnancy. Of the published cases with reported outcomes, five women either progressed during pregnancy or shortly after delivery, while the other five had no evidence of disease, stable disease, or a partial response. Additionally, one patient progressed shortly after the delivery of her third child, followed by an extra-cranial complete response and intracranial partial response after discontinuation of her ICI in week eight of her fourth pregnancy.71 At last follow-up (6 weeks–2.75 years), the children were disease-free and had reached normal developmental milestones. This data is supported by a study analysing the VigiBase reports of 91 patients receiving ICI during pregnancy for various oncological indications.72 They found no significant overreporting of pregnancy, fetal, and/or newborn adverse events, however, preterm birth was significantly overreported in the group of patients receiving anti-PD-1 plus anti-CLTA-4 combination therapy. In addition, they report several cases of interest, including a case of a patient with stage III melanoma receiving adjuvant pembrolizumab developing antiphospholipid syndrome, pneumonitis, and thyroiditis resulting in a spontaneous abortion, and a fatal case of neonatal pneumonitis resulting in respiratory distress syndrome after in utero exposure to pembrolizumab, carboplatin and pemetrexed for the treatment of non-small cell lung cancer.72

Of note, as the anti-LAG-3 agent relatlimab is relatively new, there is currently no available animal or human data available, therefore its use is discouraged during pregnancy (Table 3).

In case systemic treatment is administered during the second or third trimester, it could be considered to collect and store the placenta and umbilical cord/neonatal blood for determining the infantile drug concentration, which will lead to a better understanding of the fetal exposure and can help guide the decision making in case of possible (immune-related) adverse events.5

After an advanced stage melanoma diagnosis during pregnancy, balancing optimal maternal treatment and fetal safety is important. The complexity of this situation renders early consultation of perinatologists, and because of potential fetal risks, a referral to a high volume obstetrical unit is strongly advised. Maternal cancer, malnutrition, cancer-related psychologic stress, and poor general health are risk factors for fetal growth restriction.24 As neonatal growth restriction is associated with adverse short-term outcomes and long-term morbidities, routine prenatal ultrasound to monitor fetal growth is recommended.24 If possible, at term delivery (≥37 weeks) should be pursued.23,73 However, when close monitoring, every two weeks, shows signs of fetal compromise or maternal deterioration, a temporary discontinuation in cancer treatment or a medically-induced preterm delivery may be necessary. In our cohort we observed a high rate of preterm deliveries (53% in group 1, 33% in group 2), which is much higher than in the general population (4%), but in line with other studies in patients with advanced stage cancer during pregnancy.12,74,75 Though indication for preterm delivery was not captured in our dataset, reasons for delivery may have included necessity to start systemic therapy or pathophysiologic changes in the mother due to cancer or systemic treatment leading to spontaneous preterm labor.76 In general, vaginal delivery is preferred over cesarean section, unless there are obstetric contra-indications or melanoma involvement of the cervix or vulva.23,24 Histologic examination of the placenta is important, as melanoma can metastasize to the placenta and this increases the risk of neonatal metastases. When placental metastases have been found, a thorough physical examination including the skin, an abdominal ultrasound, and blood test (with liver function) to detect neonatal melanoma are important.24,77 Of note, s100B is an unreliable tumormarker in children.78 While not observed in our cohort, eight cases of transplacental transmission of melanoma diagnosed between birth and eight months have been described, with metastases in skin, liver, lung and brain.79,80 Most cases were fatal, but one child diagnosed at three months with metastatic lesions in the left mastoid and lungs, which were ifosfamide- and adriamycin-resistant, spontaneously regressed when he was six months old, and remained disease-free at two years of age.80 Furthermore, as ICI have the ability to cause irAEs in neonates or infants after in utero exposure, regular follow-up assessments for early detection and adequate treatment of both metastases and irAEs are strongly encouraged.5,24

Breastfeeding should not be discouraged because of the melanoma itself. However, there is limited information about the use of systemic treatment during lactation, therefore it is not recommended to breastfeed while receiving active treatment. Studies have shown that while ICI concentrations in breast milk were substantially lower than serum concentrations, the active drug was detectable up to several weeks after the last infusion, indicating that excretion into breast milk can take place.47,60 Futhermore, due to the neutralizing pH of breast milk, oral monoclonal antibodies can be transported by FcRn to the systemic circulation of breastfed neonates.81 An overview of European Medicines Agency and U.S. Food and Drug Administration recommendations can be found in Supplementary Table S4.

Prior to starting systemic treatment in (neo)adjuvant or metastatic setting, fertility preservation should be discussed if there is a (future) pregnancy wish as fertility-lowering effects cannot be excluded at this point in time.82

In animal models, administration of dabrafenib, trametinib, and combimetinib led to reduced fertility and a decreased number of corpora lutea.82 While no signs of carcinogenicity or gonadotoxicity in non-human primates treated with anti-PD-1 were observed, PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA-4 have a low expression on ovarian tissue, which could impact fertility.40,83 Reassuringly, several studies, case reports, and our dataset show that multiple women were able to conceive while receiving ICI or post-ICI exposure, indicating that fertility does not seem to be severely affected on or after treatment with ICI, and appears to result in healthy progeny.68,84 However, one case in our dataset did have a miscarriage on first trimester nivolumab. Next to direct effects, ICI commonly induce immune-related endocrinopathies (i.e., hypophysitis or thyroiditis), which can affect fertility directly or indirectly. If the anterior pituitary gland is affected, ovulatory dysfunction or premature menopause can be observed as a result of impaired gonadotropin secretion.48,49 Furthermore, hypothyroidism can lead to reduced fertility and gestational complications and should therefore be monitored and adequately treated.85,86

Pregnancy should be avoided while receiving systemic treatment and until several weeks to months after treatment, depending on the half-life of the drug (Table 3 and Supplementary Table S4). Dual contraceptive methods, including a barrier method, should be used as the efficacy of hormonal contraceptives can be reduced directly via the CYP3A4-pathway (BRAF/MEK-inhibitor-specific), or indirectly via resorption reduction as a result of gastro-intestinal side effects.82

Recommendations regarding family planning after treatment completion are not standardized and should be made on an individual basis, taking into account the tumor characteristics and maternal age.87 In case of an early stage melanoma with a low recurrence risk, a future pregnancy will likely not be affected by melanoma. For patients with high risk melanomas who received adjuvant treatment, most recurrences occur within the first three years.88 Therefore, taking into account the maternal age, waiting three years after completion of adjuvant therapy could be advisable.87 Given emerging evidence that major pathological response is an excellent surrogate for relapse-free survival, this time frame may potentially be shortened in the neoadjuvant ICI setting for favourable pathological responders.89 In metastatic setting, pregnancy should be discouraged when patients are receiving active treatment. In the scenario where a patient is in long-term remission after treatment completion and has a strong pregnancy desire, it is important to discuss the risk of recurrence and the challenges of diagnosis and treatment during pregnancy.

This dataset provides insight into real-world management and outcomes of patients with advanced stage melanoma during pregnancy, while underscoring the complexity and variability in current management. While these patients represent a unique and challenging population and require a personalized, multidisciplinary approach, the results of our analyses, combined with the extensive literature search and expert opinion, could be used as clinical recommendations for their management. The Melanoma Pregnancy Taskforce plans to continue to prospectively collect cases and follow-up information from cases currently included, to build a foundation for future evidence-based guidelines.
