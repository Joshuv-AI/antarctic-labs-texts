Relationship between longer sleep and serum neurofilament light chain in american adults: evidence from the 2013–2014 US national health and nutrition examination survey
Authors: Chunyan Zhang, Yitian Yang, Huanxian Liu, Jiaqiang Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSleep disturbance is linked to neurodegenerative diseases and the related brain pathophysiology. Serum neurofilament light chain (NfL) is a reliable biomarker for neurological disorders. This study examined the association between sleep characteristics and serum NfL levels in American adults.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, data from the 2013–2014 US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey were utilized. Participants were categorized into short (≤ 6 h), normal (7–8 h), and long (≥ 9 h) sleep groups based on their self-reported sleep durations. Sleep duration, trouble sleeping, and diagnosed sleep disorders were queried, forming “sleep pattern (healthy, moderate, and poor).” The association between sleep characteristics and serum NfL levels was assessed using multivariate linear regression models. Stratification and sensitivity analyses were conducted to determine the stability of results.ResultsOverall, 1637 participants were included; among them, 48.2% were male and 51.8% were female (mean ± SD, age: 46.9 ± 15.5 years) and 38.8% reported sleeping for ≤ 6 h, 54.4% for 7–8 h, and 6.8% for ≥ 9 h. Participants with longer sleep duration, poor sleep pattern, diagnosed sleep disorders, or trouble sleeping exhibited higher serum NfL levels. A positive correlation was found between extended sleep and elevated serum NfL levels (Adjusted β = 4.82, 95%CI: 2.2, 7.44, P < 0.001), with no significant correlation observed in the short-sleep group or those with poor sleep pattern. Stratified and sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the relationship between longer sleep and elevated serum NfL levels.ConclusionsA long sleep duration is associated with higher serum NfL levels than a normal sleep duration in American adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20184-7.Keywords: Cross-sectional study, National health and nutrition examination survey, Serum neurofilament light chain, Sleep duration

Background

Poor sleep quality, nonoptimal sleep duration, and sleep disorders, including insomnia, circadian rhythm abnormalities, and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), have significant implications for physical and mental health. Optimal sleep duration is crucial because insufficient and excessive sleep duration are associated with various health issues [1]. Insufficient sleep duration is associated with a higher risk of obesity [2], hypertension [3], cardiovascular disease (CVD) [4], and type II diabetes [5], whereas prolonged sleep is correlated with a poor health-related quality of life [6], multiple sclerosis (MS) [7], CVD [8], and stroke. Sleep disturbances also increase the risk of dementia [9], and both insufficient and excessive sleep durations, along with fragmented sleep, could be risk factors for cognitive decline [10, 11]. Furthermore, sleep disturbances have been implicated in the etiology of clinical neurodegenerative diseases and various brain pathophysiological processes [12].

Serum neurofilament light chain (NfL), a protein released into the bloodstream following neuroaxonal injury, has been increasingly recognized as a biomarker of neuro-axonal injury in multiple neurological diseases [13]. Elevated levels of serum NfL have been documented in various neurological disorders [14–17], with a consistent association between higher NfL levels and more pronounced neurological damage and poorer clinical outcomes [18, 19]. This implies that NfL holds promise as a biomarker that is indicative of clinically relevant pathophysiological processes in the brain.

Numerous investigations have sought to elucidate the link between sleep and neuronal damage by assessing serum NfL levels. However, the results have been marked by inconsistency. For example, in a study involving middle-aged individuals, those with chronic insomnia had elevated NfL levels, and subsequent treatment led to a reduction in NfL levels compared with those individuals without insomnia [20]. Additionally, patients with narcolepsy exhibited heightened NfL levels in another study [21]. Serum NfL levels were significantly higher in patients with Parkinson's disease than in healthy controls. Moreover, patients with Parkinson’s disease having sleep disorders showed even higher NfL levels than those without sleep disorders, suggesting that sleep disorders may influence the serum NfL levels [22]. Conversely, certain investigations failed to establish a discernible connection between narcolepsy, subjectively impaired sleep, or experimentally acute sleep loss and NfL levels, regardless of whether they were measured in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) or plasma of patients [23–26]. Accordingly, to establish a more comprehensive understanding of such associations, there is a pressing need for large-scale population-based studies. These studies should systematically examine the association between sleep characteristics and neuronal damage, indicated based on NfL levels.

To expand our understanding of the association between sleep and neuroaxonal integrity in adults, participants from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2013–2014 cycle were recruited. The aim of this study was to examine the correlation between sleep characteristics and serum NfL levels within a representative United States (US) population.

Materials and methods

Data sources

The cross-sectional data utilized in this study were sourced from the NHANES 2013–2014 cycle, a nationally representative survey administered by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). The NHANES employs a stratified, multistage probability cluster sampling methodology to evaluate the health and nutritional status of the non-institutionalized US population [27]. Approval for the NHANES study protocol was granted by the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board, and participants provided written informed consent during the enrollment process. The Ethics Review Committee of Henan Provincial People’s Hospital deemed this study exempt as it utilized publicly available de-identified data, thereby waiving the requirement for additional informed consent.

All data in this study are accessible at the NHANES official website (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/continuousnhanes/default.aspx?BeginYear=2013). Data were analyzed between November and December 2023. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines.

Participants and study design

The cross-sectional data utilized in this study were derived from the NHANES 2013–2014 cycle because serum NfL levels were only available for adults aged 20–75 years during this period. The study population consisted of 10,175 individuals who were subjected to interviews and physical examinations. Rigorous quality control measures were implemented, including the application of exclusion criteria. Individuals with missing or incomplete sleep information or serum NfL data were excluded. Individuals with extreme serum NfL levels (> 250 pg/ml) and individuals lacking important study-relevant variables were excluded. Finally, the analysis involved 1637 participants, and Fig. 1 illustrates the enrollment process of the participants.

Flow chart of the selection process

Sleep duration and sleep pattern

Sleep duration was self-reported based on the question “How much sleep do you usually get at night on weekdays or workdays?” The participants either responded that they slept for 2–11 h or ≥ 12 h, or they refused to answer or did not know. Based on the common cutoff points used in a previous study [28], sleep duration was categorized as follows: normal, defined as 7–8 h; short, defined as ≤ 6 h; and long, defined as ≥ 9 h.

The following questionnaire items were utilized to evaluate instances of diagnosed sleep disorders and trouble sleeping, respectively: “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you have a sleep disorder?” and “Have you ever informed a medical professional about experiencing difficulty sleeping?” A “YES” response was assigned a sleep score of 0, while a “NO” response was assigned a sleep score of 1. Short or long sleep duration was considered a sleep score of 0, whereas normal sleep duration was designated as a sleep score of 1. The three sleep behaviors (self-reported sleep duration, diagnosed sleep disorder, and trouble sleeping) were combined to generate a total sleep score, ranging from 0 to 3. According to Li et al. [29], a sleep score ranging from 0 to 1 indicates a poor sleep pattern, whereas scores of 2 or 3 reflect moderate or healthy sleep pattern, respectively.

Serum NfL examination

Blood samples collected between 2013 and 2014 were processed at the Mobile Examination Center. Participants aged 20–75 years in a half-sample from NHANES 2013–2014 consented to storing their samples for future research and had stored surplus or pristine serum samples. Serum samples were tested for NfL using the fully automated Attelica immunoassay system [30], a highly sensitive immunoassay developed by Siemens Healthineers (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). The results were first published in June 2022. The assay process involves incubating the sample with acridinium-ester (AE) labeled antibodies, which bind to the NfL antigen. Subsequently, paramagnetic particles (PMP) coated with capture antibody are introduced, forming complexes of antigen bound to AE-labeled antibodies and PMP. Unbound AE-labeled antibodies are then separated and eliminated. Following this, acid and base are added to initiate chemiluminescence, and light emission is measured. Each step is executed using the fully automated Attelica immunoassay system. For further information and detailed protocols, please refer to the following link: https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Nhanes/2013-2014/SSSNFL_H.htm.

The assay procedures included quality control samples featuring low, medium, and high serum NfL concentrations. The lower and upper limits of quantitation were 3.9 and 500 pg/ml, respectively; 36 samples were below the lower detection limit, and an imputed fill value was placed in the analyte results field equal to the LLOQ divided by the √2. Individuals with extreme serum NfL levels (> 250 pg/ml) were excluded.

Covariates

In accordance with a previous study [31], we gathered sociodemographic information (age, sex, race, education level, family income, marital status, and body mass index [BMI]), lifestyle information (smoking status and alcohol consumption), medical history (diabetes, hypertension, and CVD), and laboratory data (alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase [AST], high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, total cholesterol [TC], triglyceride [TG], creatinine, and glycated hemoglobin levels, and the estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR]) related to sleep duration or serum NfL as confounding variables. In this study, self-reported race/ethnicity data were used, stratified into five categories as follows: Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and other races. BMI was computed by dividing weight (in kilograms) by height (in meters squared) and was derived from anthropometric measurements obtained during the NHANES. Marital status was defined as either married, cohabitating, or living independently. Educational attainment was segmented into three groups as follows: less than 9 years, 9–12 years, or more than 12 years of education. Family income was categorized into three brackets based on the poverty income ratio (PIR), as defined by a US government report, as follows: low (PIR ≤ 1.3), medium (PIR: 1.3–3.5), and high (PIR > 3.5). Health status data encompassed self-reported histories of hypertension and diabetes mellitus (DM). A self-reported CVD history was defined as a previous diagnosis of angina, coronary heart disease, heart failure, heart attack, or stroke. Smoking status was classified into three categories as follows: never smoker (< 100 cigarettes), former smoker (> 100 cigarettes but currently quit), and current smoker (> 100 cigarettes and currently smoking) [32]. Alcohol consumption was defined as follows: never drank (< 12 drinks in lifetime), former drinker (> 12 drinks in 1 year but did not drink last year, or did not drink last year but consumed ≥ 12 drinks in lifetime), and current drinker (> 12 drinks and currently drinking) [33]. We gathered data on coffee consumption through the initial 24-h dietary recall interview, which offers the amount of each food and beverage consumed in grams. [34]. Using the available ICD-10 codes linked to participants’ prescription medications, participants were recorded as taking sleep medications when they reported applying medications for insomnia (ICD-10 code: G47.00) or sleep disorders not otherwise specified (G47.9) [35]. Participants with neurologic disorders were identified based on ICD-10 codes associated with prescription medications. Detailed ICD codes can be found in Supplement S1 [31].

Statistical analyses

The characteristics of the study sample were presented by expressing normally distributed continuous variables as means and standard deviations, non-normally distributed continuous variables as medians and interquartile ranges, and categorical variables as proportions of the overall sample. Owing to the non-normal distribution of serum NfL levels, the natural logarithm of this variable was incorporated in the analysis. Differences between groups were assessed using one-way analyses of variance for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. The data were grouped and presented based on participants reporting normal, short, and long sleep durations.

Multivariate linear regression analysis was performed to assess associations between sleep duration or sleep pattern and serum NfL levels, and β coefficients were calculated to estimate the strengths of the relationships. The serum NfL level was used as the dependent variable, and sleep duration was used as the independent variable. In multivariate regression analysis, sleep duration was used as a continuous variable and categorized into tertiles as categorical variables. Four models of each analysis were provided to eliminate the effects of confounding variables. Model 1 served as the baseline without covariate adjustments. Model 2 introduced adjustments for demographic factors, including age, sex, race, smoking status, and alcohol consumption. Model 3 was adjusted for Model 2 and clinical variables such as BMI, diabetes, hypertension, CVD, stroke and neurological diseases, medications for sleep disorders, and coffee consumption. Building upon Model 3, Model 4 incorporated adjustments for AST, TC, TG, creatinine, and HbA1c.

In secondary analyses, we conducted stratified analyses based on sex (female vs. male), age (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 years), smoking status (never vs. former or current), alcohol consumption (never vs. former or current), BMI (< 30 vs. ≥ 30 kg/m2), history of stroke (yes vs. no), DM status (yes vs. no), and eGFR (< 60 mL/min vs. ≥ 60 mL/min). Likelihood ratio testing was employed to scrutinize interactions within these subgroups [36].

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the results. As higher serum NfL levels are observed in those with neurological disorders, we excluded those diagnosed with neurological diseases to ensure the consistency of our findings. We included individuals with serum NfL levels above the identified outlier limits. Moreover, multiple imputations based on five replicates were used for individuals with missing covariate data.

Data analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.2.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing; http://www.R-project.org) and Free Statistics software (version 1.7.1; Beijing Free Clinical Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) [36]. A significance threshold of two-tailed P-values < 0.05 was applied for all analyses. Data were analyzed from November to December 2023.

Results

Study population

In total, 10,175 participants completed the interview, among whom 8104 with missing serum NfL data were excluded. Four individuals were excluded because of a lack of sleep data, and 428 individuals were excluded owing to missing covariate data. The detailed overview of the inclusion and exclusion criteria is provided in Fig. 1.

Baseline characteristics

The sleep duration characteristics of the participants are shown in Table 1. Among the 1637 participants, 48.2% were male and 51.8% were female (mean ± SD, age: 46.9 ± 15.5 years). Medical comorbidities included hypertension (39.7%), diabetes (17.5%), CVD (8.5%), and stroke (2.6%). Among the participants, 38.8% reported sleeping for ≤ 6 h, 54.4% for 7–8 h, and 6.8% for ≥ 9 h. Compared with participants in the normal-sleep group, those in the long-sleep group were significantly more likely to live alone (45% vs. 35.5%), be non-Hispanic White (52.3% vs. 49.7%), and have a low family income (43.2% vs. 33.7%). They were also significantly more likely to be smokers (former smokers [26.1% vs. 23.0%] and current smokers [22.5% vs. 17.4%]) and to have hypertension (40.5% vs. 36.9%), diabetes (24. 3% vs. 15.8%), neurological diseases (12.6% vs. 5.5%), stroke (8.1% vs. 2%) and taking sleep medication (9.9% vs. 3.6%).

Relationship between sleep characteristics and serum NfL levels

Univariate analysis demonstrated associations between serum NfL levels and variables such as sex, age, race, smoking status, alcohol consumption, stroke, neurological diseases, CVD, hypertension, diabetes, HbA1c, AST level,, creatinine level, eGFR, TG level, sleep medication, sleep pattern, sleep duration, trouble sleeping, and diagnosed sleep disorder (Supplement S1).

In the multivariate linear regression, sleep duration was not significantly associated with the serum NfL levels regardless of all adjusted models when assessed as a continuous variable [crude model, β = 0.4, 95%CI: 0.15, 0.95, p = 0.155; Model 4, β = 0.34, 95%CI: -0.14, 0.81, p = 0.167] (Table 2), while analyzed as categorical variables, a significant correlation was observed between a long sleep duration and elevated serum NfL levels when compared with the normal sleep duration (β = 6.71, 95%CI: 3.69, 9.72, P < 0.001). After adjusting for potential confounding factors (fully adjusted model), a positive correlation persisted between a long sleep duration and higher serum NfL levels (β = 4.82, 95%CI: 2.2, 7.44, P < 0.001). However, no significant correlation was found between a short sleep duration and serum NfL levels.

Stratified analyses

In several subgroups, stratified analyses were conducted to evaluate potential modifications affecting the relationship between sleep duration and serum NfL levels. Within the subgroup analyses stratified by smoking status, current or former smokers in the short sleep duration group exhibited higher serum NfL levels than non-smokers. Across all subgroups stratified by sex, age, alcohol consumption, eGFR, diabetes, or stroke, no significant interactions were observed (Fig. 3).

Sensitivity analyses

Additionally, sensitivity analyses were performed, excluding individuals with a history of stroke or neurological diseases and including those with serum NfL levels surpassing identified outlier limits, to ensure the robustness of our results. The relationship between sleep duration and serum NfL levels remained unchanged and consistent throughout these sensitivity analyses (Supplement S2).

Discussion

In this nationwide cross-sectional study, we investigated the correlation between sleep duration and serum NfL levels within the general population of the US, utilizing data from the NHANES 2013–2014 cycle. Our findings revealed that, in comparison to a normal sleep duration, a prolonged sleep duration exhibited a significant association with elevated serum NfL levels, even after accounting for potential confounding variables. Furthermore, both stratified and sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the observed relationship between sleep duration and serum NfL levels.

Several studies have utilized NfL as a marker to investigate the association between sleep and neuronal damage. Thom et al. [37] examined the link between habitual sleep duration and neuronal damage among middle-aged and older individuals in Rotterdam. They found that spending an excessive amount of time in bed (> 9 h) was significantly correlated with higher NfL levels, whereas spending a shorter duration in bed (< 7 h) showed no significant association. Our multivariate linear regression analysis results are consistent with those reported by Thoms et al. [37]. We expanded the population to adults aged ≥ 20 years in the US. Because one type of sleep abnormality, the prevalence of a short sleep duration, was higher in the 25–45 years of age group than in the 45–65 years of age group (36.4% vs. 34.5%) [38], we believe that young people also require attention. Moreover, we adjusted for multiple covariates and conducted subgroup and sensitivity analyses to validate the robustness of our findings.

The relationship between NfL and sleep is complex. In a study of patients with COVID-19 pneumonia, those with poor sleep quality showed elevated NfL levels, vascular cell adhesion molecule 1, and (IL)-10, suggesting a potential connection between sleep quality and neuroinflammation [39]. Additionally, elevated blood levels of the NfL were observed in women in the morning after sleep loss [40]. In another study, individuals with chronic insomnia disorder exhibited elevated serum NfL levels compared with controls, with a potential decrease following treatment [20]. This increase may be attributed to decreased activity of the glymphatic system, a brain waste clearance mechanism that typically operates at peak efficiency during nighttime sleep [40]. Moreover, a correlation between lower oxygen saturation during sleep and higher serum NfL levels was observed in patients with OSA, and serum NfL levels were significantly higher in the ineffective treatment group than in the effective treatment group [41]. Conversely, a different investigation that focused on chronic insomnia failed to establish any correlation between this condition and subjective sleep quality [42]. Additionally, one study concerning type 1 narcolepsy revealed no significant difference in CSF NfL levels between patients with and without the condition, despite their known sleep fragmentation and poor sleep efficiency [26]. To further explore these associations, we conducted analyses based on various sleep characteristics. In our study, serum NfL levels were higher in participants with various sleep disturbances, which include longed sleep duration, diagnosed sleep disorder, and trouble sleeping, taking medications for sleep and poor sleep pattern, supporting the association between serum NfL and sleep disruption.

Our stratified analyses indicated that the smoking status of a patient might influence the relationship between a short sleep duration and serum NfL levels. Experimental evidence from cigarette smoke-exposed mice demonstrated neuronal damage, characterized by thinner pyramidal and granular cell layers, and reduction in neuron counts [43]. Current smokers tend to exhibit elevated serum NfL levels [44]. Furthermore, smokers have higher risks of insomnia and shorter sleep durations than non-smokers [45]. Subsequent investigations are warranted to validate and elucidate the intricate relationship among a short sleep duration, smoking habits, and serum NfL levels.

The potential mechanism underlying the association between a prolonged sleep duration and elevated serum NfL levels remains unclear. A previous study found a positive correlation between longer sleep duration and increased C-reactive protein and IL-6 levels [46]. Data collected from 260 adults (aged > 60 years) at the Mayo Clinic demonstrated an association with higher levels of CSF IL-6 and NfL [47]. These findings suggest a potential link between longer sleep duration and neuroinflammation as well as neuronal damage. Notably, patients with OSA frequently experience disruptions in non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, potentially contributing to increased daytime sleepiness [48]. Studies have indicated that NREM sleep deprivation can lead to an upsurge in CSF and plasma levels of NfL [49]. Sleep drives metabolite clearance from the brain, and the recovery function of sleep could be attributed to the enhanced clearance of potential neurotoxic waste that accumulates in the central nervous system during wakefulness [50]. One explanation of our findings is that high serum NfL levels affect the recovery function of sleep and lead to drowsiness. The complex interaction between a long sleep duration and serum NfL levels emphasizes the necessity of comprehensive research to reveal potential mechanisms and potential therapeutic interventions aimed at mitigating these effects.

In summary, the relationship between sleep and NfL appears to be bidirectional. Prolonged sleep could result in elevated NfL levels, or conversely, increased NfL levels might contribute to prolonged sleep. Moreover, both scenarios could occur simultaneously. Additionally, prolonged sleep may be pathological if triggered by an underlying disease, compensatory, or even beneficial for individuals with a naturally high propensity for sleep. In addition, napping may be one of the reasons for longer sleep duration, and a bidirectional causal relationship may exist between excessive daytime napping and Alzheimer's dementia [51]. It is important to recognize the heterogeneity of longer sleep duration and its impact on NfL correlation.

This study has both strengths and limitations. The data, drawn from NHANES, offers a robust representation of the US population, owing to its extensive reach, standardization, and reliability. However, its cross-sectional design hampers our ability to establish a causal link between sleep duration and serum NfL levels, leaving uncertainty regarding whether an abnormal sleep duration serves as a marker or risk factor for elevated serum NfL levels. To validate these findings, additional well-designed cohort studies are necessary. Moreover, relying on self-reported sleep measures introduces subjectivity and potential inaccuracies, despite being essential for obtaining representative population estimates. While validated sleep instruments offer objective indicators, self-reported inventories are preferred owing to their cost-effectiveness and practicality for large-scale studies [52]. Moreover, despite efforts to control for the covariates, residual confounding factors could persist, such as sleep-disordered breathing, napping, and insomnia, which could affect the interpretation of the results. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed to strengthen the validity of this study. Nevertheless, further research is needed to explore the mechanistic impact of an abnormal sleep duration on neuronal damage.

Conclusions

A long sleep duration is associated with higher serum NfL levels than a normal sleep duration, in American adults. Moreover, smoking status might influence the relationship between a short sleep duration and serum NfL levels. The integration of these findings into public health programs and clinical guidelines could underscore the significance of sleep hygiene and its influence on neurological health. The monitoring of serum NfL levels and sleep duration holds promise as a diagnostic or prognostic tool for neurologic disorders. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies and diverse populations to understand the biological mechanisms linking sleep duration to axonal damage.
