Symmetry-breaking dynamics of a photoionized carbon dioxide dimer
Authors: Ester Livshits, Dror M Bittner, Florian Trost, Severin Meister, Hannes Lindenblatt, Rolf Treusch, Krishnendu Gope, Thomas Pfeifer, Roi Baer, Robert Moshammer, Daniel Strasser
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhotoionization can initiate structural reorganization of molecular matter and drive formation of new chemical bonds. Here, we used time-resolved extreme ultraviolet (EUV) pump – EUV probe Coulomb explosion imaging of carbon dioxide dimer ion CO22+ dynamics, that combined with ab initio molecular dynamics simulations, revealed unexpected asymmetric structural rearrangement. We show that ionization by the pump pulse induces rearrangement from the slipped-parallel (C2h) geometry of the neutral CO2 dimer towards a T-shaped (C2v) structure on the ~100 fs timescale, although the most stable slipped-parallel (C2h) structure of the ionic dimer. Moreover, we find that excited states of the ionized CO2 dimer can exhibit formation of a CO3 moiety in the C2O4+ complex that can persist even after a suitably time-delayed second photoionization in a metastable C2O42+ dication. Our results suggest that charge asymmetry plays an important role in the ionization-induced dynamics in such dimers that are present in CO2 rich environments.Subject terms: Chemical physics, Atomic and molecular interactions with photons, Molecular dynamics, Excited states

Introduction

Bond formation by ionization of isolated molecular clusters is a potentially significant pathway for molecular synthesis in cold interstellar medium and planetary environments that are exposed to ionizing radiation1–4. Instantaneous ionization at the Franck-Condon (FC) geometry of a cold molecular cluster can provide the system with sufficient energy to overcome barriers towards structural rearrangement and formation of more complex species such as benzene rings and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAH’s)2,3. Similarly non-trivial structural rearrangements are required for recycling or upcycling of CO2, for example by forming carbonate and polycarbonate structures5,6. Considering the simplest CO2 cluster, the ground-state structure of the singly ionized CO22+ was assigned with a slipped parallel C2h symmetry, similar to the symmetry of the Van der Waals bound neutral CO22 dimer7–9. Nevertheless, a less stable T-shaped CO22+ isomer was also predicted9, suggesting the possibility of an asymmetric structural rearrangement. Furthermore, following a surprising observation of reactive collisions of a metastable CO22+ dication with neutral CO2 molecules10, a theoretical study of a potential C2O42+ intermediate has identified an asymmetric equilibrium geometry that exhibits a CO3 moiety11. It is therefore valuable to explore the ionization-induced structural rearrangement of CO2 dimers that are known to form in man-made as well as naturally-occurring CO2 rich environments12,13.

Intense fs lasers and ultrafast EUV pulses allow time-resolved probing of photoionization-induced dynamics by a time-delayed 2nd ionization of the isolated molecular or cluster system14–16. The Coulombic repulsion typically results in charge separation that encodes structural information in the kinetic energy release (KER) and momentum correlations of the fragmentation products17. Compared with intense fs laser ionization studies18, the additional complexity of implementing ultrafast EUV pulses by high-order-harmonic generation or by free electron laser facilities allows for a one-photon ionization scheme that helps simplify the theoretical modeling16,19. Here, we use an ultrafast EUV pump pulse produced at the FLASH2 free electron laser facility20, to initiate dynamics on the singly ionized CO22+ potential. The structural rearrangement of the system is time-resolved by Coulomb explosion imaging (CEI) with a time-delayed EUV probe pulse. The experimental CEI measurements, probing the non-trivial dynamics towards a T-shaped dimer structure and formation of a CO3 moiety within a stable C2O4+ are interpreted based on time-resolved pump-probe ab-initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations. Furthermore, pump-probe AIMD simulations on the 2nd excited state of the ionized dimer indicate that while instantaneous double-ionization of the neutral carbon dioxide dimer results in Coulomb explosion, delay of the second ionization can form a metastable C2O42+ dication with an asymmetric geometry that exhibits a CO3 carbonate moiety.

Results

The KER spectrum of coincident pairs of CO2+ ions is shown by the blue curve in Fig. 1. Coulomb explosion, initiated by absorbing two 39 eV photons from the ultrafast EUV pulse, releases ~3.8 eV, in agreement with earlier strong-field Coulomb explosion studies18. Furthermore, in contrast with Coulomb explosion of small alcohols that exhibits complex structural rearrangements21–24, the relatively narrow KER peak at 3.8 eV indicates the effectively instantaneous charge separation between the two monomers, followed by a predominantly Coulombic repulsion from the initial ~3.8 A distance between the two monomers8. The events with low KER, below ~0.3 eV, are attributed not to Coulomb explosion, but to random coincidence of CO2+ ions from uncorrelated parent molecules. The red curve in Fig. 1 shows the KER spectrum obtained by splitting the ultrafast EUV pulse into a pump and probe pulse, separated by a ~ 450 fs time delay. The time delay between the two photoionization steps results in appearance of both low and high KER tails, extending respectively to about 2 eV and 6 eV. Appearance of lower KER in CEI measurements is typically associated with a larger separation, e.g. due to dissociation of the dimer, while higher KER can indicate Coulomb explosion of a more compact dimer and suggests a non-trivial structural rearrangement of the singly ionized intermediate CO22+. We therefore focus on the photoionization and the dynamics that it triggers.

In the methods section, we describe the details of how we carried out the electronic structure and the molecular dynamics calculations. We first consider the electronic structure of the carbon dioxide dimer, ionized in the Franck-Condon C2h geometry (at the minimum energy of the neutral dimer). The left hand side of Fig. 2 shows the eight relevant Kohn-Sham orbitals (non-interacting Dyson orbitals), obtained by a DFT calculation within the BNL functional25–27. The electronic structure consists of two energy bands of four states each with nearly identical energies, separated by about 4.3 eV. Each band can be further divided into two sets of degenerate states according to their symmetry with respect to the σh reflection operation (symmetric Ag and Bu characters and antisymmetric Au and Bg characters).

The electronic structure of the ionized carbon dioxide dimer at the Franck-Condon geometry.

On the right-hand side, we plot the Dyson orbitals of the four lowest-energy many-body eigenstates within the SA4-XMS-CASPT2(7e6o)/aug-cc-pVDZ theory and show their excitation energies relative to the cation ground-state. Source data are provided as a Source Data file.

The right-hand side of Fig. 2 shows the Dyson orbitals of the many-body eigenstates within the SA4-XMS-CASPT2(7e, 6o)/aug-cc-pVDZ theory. Similar to the DFT/BNL electronic structure, that of CASPT2 also exhibits two energy bands, with a ~ 4 eV gap. The four-state averaging constraint results in four orbitals that break the C2h symmetry and in the localization of the hole on one of the CO2 monomers. The symmetry breaking is possible because in CASPT2 the pairs of symmetric and anti-symmetric states (Bu and Ag or Bg and Au) are degenerate. Indeed, in Fig. 2, the localized CASPT2 Dyson state looks very similar to the sum of the degenerate delocalized Dyson states in the BNL/DFT calculation. In supplementary note 5 we show that extending the CAS to enable six state-averaging does not qualitatively alter the electronic structure but reveals two of the four missing sibling states, corresponding to the difference of the delocalized states.

It is noteworthy that the DFT/BNL states do not break symmetry, even though the pairs of orbitals are nearly degenerate as well. Furthermore, the fact that CASPT2 breaks symmetry is particular to the Franck-Condon geometry: in the supplementary note 4 we show that symmetry breaking does not happen in the global minimum geometry of the ionized dimer explaining why the global minimum is dynamically inaccessible by symmetry-breaking dynamics starting in the FC geometry.

As a conclusion, whether the positively charged hole is localized or delocalized in the ionized dimer profoundly affects its Born-Oppenheimer dynamics, as shown in Fig. 3 by comparing the CASPT2 and DFT/BNL adiabatic molecular dynamics. The DFT/BNL dynamics, with the delocalized hole and a Bu symmetry, essentially preserve the slipped parallel (C2h) geometry. In contrast, the CASPT2 ground state is a linear combination of degenerate Bg and Au symmetry states with a localized hole that breaks the C2h symmetry, resulting in a T-shaped geometry that emerges when the neutral monomer rotates with respect to the ionized one. This rotation is driven by the torque developed as the charged monomer induces a dipole in the neutral one.

To resolve which of the two very different predictions of dynamics is correct, we compare the theoretical predictions with the experimental data, in which a time-delayed ionizing probe pulse induces a Coulomb explosion and the KER is measured. To demonstrate that CEI can be used for differentiating between the two dynamical scenarios, we performed ab initio molecular dynamics simulations on the dication state to estimate the KER after a time-delayed second ionization. The KER estimates for different time steps of representative DFT/BNL and CASPT2 trajectories appear in Fig. 3 in parentheses. Initiated in the same geometry, both DFT/BNL and CASPT2 trajectories predict the same KER of 3.7 eV at zero pump-probe delay, in agreement with the experimental t = 0 spectrum shown in Fig. 1. However, at longer times, they differ distinctively: DFT/BNL predicts a maximal KER of 4.4 eV already at 50 fs, when the dimer is most compact, while the slow rotation on the CASPT2 potential surface requires at least 100 fs for the KER to reach its maximal value of 4.2 eV. At that time-delay, the dimer in the DFT/BNL dynamics has already become loose, producing substantially lower KER. A typical DFT/BNL dynamics simulation is provided in supplementary movie 1.

In Fig. 3 we gave indication that the time-resolved KER measurement is a sensitive probe of the nature of the hole localization. However, in order to make a detailed comparison with experiment, we need to take into account the dynamics not only on the ground-state but also on the excited states of the ionized dimer. This is due to the fact that a very energetic photon (39 eV), well above the ionization threshold, is used for forming the cation dimer. Unfortunately, TDDFT/BNL and TDDFT/B3LYP were found unsuitable for studying dynamics on the excited states of the cation because of the incorrect description of the ground-state and the first excited state conical intersection, as discussed in the literature28. Therefore, CASPT2 is used to study the ensuing non-adiabatic molecular dynamics (NA-AIMD), as described in detail in the theoretical methods section. Going beyond the cation ground-state, we performed NA-AIMD using the fewest switches surface-hopping (FSSH) method29 on the four low-lying adiabatic potential energy surfaces of the CO22+ cation and found low surface hopping rates. In two trajectories out of 80, we observed a temporary transition from the first excited state to the ground state at about 300 and 400 fs. In both trajectories, the system hopped back to the excited state shortly afterward. We conclude that the dynamics is essentially adiabatic. In Fig. 4 we show snapshots of the geometries in four typical trajectories, all starting at the same slipped parallel (C2h) geometry of the neutral dimer but evolving on differing adiabatic states. Similar to the CASPT2 ground-state, here too, the dynamics break the C2h symmetry by rotation of the neutral monomer. As a result, the distance between the monomers diminishes, reaching a T-shaped geometry within about 150 fs after ionization. At this time delay the calculated KER following a removal of a 2nd electron increases up to 4 − 4.5 eV, reflecting the compression of the dimer. At later times, around ~300 fs, the continued rotation typically results in a larger separation between the monomers that reduces the KER to ~3 eV. At longer time delays, the system continued to oscillate around the T-shaped geometry. A typical CASPT2 dynamics simulation is provided in supplementary movie 2.

We turn the readers’ attention to the system’s behavior in the second excited state. Here, a bending vibration of the ionized monomer allows the oxygen of the neutral monomer to closely approach the carbon of the charged monomer, creating a C2O4+ structure with a moiety resembling a CO3 group. This structure appears at 150 fs and continuously forms and breaks, accompanied by redistribution of the Mulliken charges. A typical CO3 formation in an excited state simulation is shown in supplementary movie 3.

The formation of the CO3 carbonate moiety correlates with strengthening the negative charge on the central oxygen atom. It is especially noteworthy that ~45% of the trajectories on the second excited state appear to resist Coulomb explosion upon the probe ionization at time delays in the 100–500 fs range and therefore allow the formation of a metastable C2O42+. Supplementary movies 4 and 5 show typical simulations of Coulomb explosion and metastable dication formation, respectively. Unfortunately, direct experimental detection of such metastable C2O42+ products is beyond the scope of the present study because the current setup cannot distinguish it from the same charge over mass ratio of the CO2+ ion.

While both DFT/BNL and CASPT2 dynamics can account for the development of the observed high KER and low KER tails, the different dynamics can be experimentally distinguished by the pump-probe time-delays at which they appear. Figure 5 shows a direct comparison of the experimental EUV-pump EUV-probe CEI data with the ab-initio pump-probe calculation of the expected KER as a function of pump-probe delay. The top panel shows the experimental KER distribution that is measured for different time delays between the EUV pump and probe pulses. At t = 0 delay, the Coulomb explosion exhibits the ~3.8 eV peak. The yield of higher KER events appears to increase within the first 100 fs of pump-probe delay. In contrast, the appearance of lower KER events is observed at much longer ~250 fs time delays. These experimentally observed changes in the KER are in good agreement with the rotation of a neutral monomer towards a T-shaped geometry predicted in the CASPT2 simulation. The ab-initio pump-probe KER spectra shown in the bottom panel are composed by scaling the contributions of different CASPT2 trajectories with the calculated differential photoionization cross-section for each initial-geometry and ionic state and convolution with the experimental response. As the implemented pump and probe EUV pulses have similar intensities, double ionization can also occur by absorption of two photons from the same ~25 fs long pulse. Therefore, in order to allow direct comparison of theory and experiment, we include the contribution of KER simulated at t = 0 in the theoretically predicted distribution at all the time-delays.

Both tails towards high and low KER and their time-resolved onset times are in good agreement with the experimental data. To better compare the experimental and simulated onset times, the red bars and full line in Fig. 6 show respectively the normalized experimental and simulated time-resolved yield of events with KER in the 4.6−7 eV range. Both exhibit a gradual rise until reaching maximal yields at a ~ 100 fs delay. Different combinations of initial geometries and excited states result in variation in the exact time of the oscillatory rotation of the neutral monomer, therefore, the high KER yield exhibits a plateau at longer time delays. The experimental and simulated time-resolved yield of events with KER in the 1.6−2.9 eV range are compared by the blue bars and full line in Fig. 6. The appearance of these lower KER events is delayed by ~250 fs in both experiment and CASPT2 theory. In contrast, C2h conserving dynamics is expected to exhibit a distinctively different time-dependence. While a full calculation including the excited states cannot be performed using DFT/BNL potentials, ground-state simulations indicate that the C2h conserving dynamics can be expected to exhibit a distinctively faster response. High KER events would onset already at ~50 fs and low KER events at time-delays as early as 150 fs, see Fig. 3 and supplementary movie 1 for typical examples.

Discussion

While the formation of the T-shaped dimer isomer and CO2 recycling into carbonate, polycarbonate, and more complex species typically requires catalytic reagents5,30,31, ionization of cold carbon dioxide clusters may offer alternative pathways for CO2-based formation of complex molecular structures. In this combined theoretical and experimental study, CO3 carbonate moiety formation by ionizing a neutral carbon dioxide dimer presents a possible pathway for forming complex molecular species in the interstellar medium or CO2 -rich planetary environments exposed to ionizing radiation. The slipped parallel geometry of cold CO2 dimers, both in their neutral or singly ionized ground states, does not appear favorable for the formation of a CO3 moiety. Nevertheless, ionization at the FC geometry of the neutral parent breaks the symmetry between the two monomers and results in rotation of the neutral CO2, forming a T-shaped dimer geometry within ~100 fs after ionization that is experimentally identified by the increased KER in a Coulomb explosion with a time-delayed EUV probe pulse. In contrast with photoexcitation of cold CO22+ dimers that was reported earlier to result in dissociation32,33, this structural rearrangement into a stable T-shaped geometry is not limited to the ground-state of the singly ionized dimer. Ionization to the 2nd excited state of the cation leads to a particularly close approach of the oxygen atom of the neutral monomer to the carbon of the initially cationic monomer, such that a 2nd ionization at a suitable time delay is expected to form metastable C2O42+. Such a metastable asymmetric dication, featuring a CO3 carbonate moiety, was previously proposed as an asymmetric intermediate in CO22++CO2 collisions11. However, it was observed only indirectly by appearance of a C2O32+ collision product. With the advent of new merged-beam experimental setups for cation-anion and ion-neutral collisions34–38, future studies of cold dication – neutral collisions may allow to further explore formation of such metastable C2O42+ products. Furthermore, the present study points to the fact that vertical ionization of a neutral carbon dioxide dimer can be expected to result in preferential formation of the higher-lying T-shaped isomer of the singly ionized system and not the global minimum at the slipped parallel geometry. This finding may affect the interpretation of experimental studies of cationic CO2 clusters that can potentially form T-shaped or slipped parallel structures, depending on their formation mechanism. Indeed, different local minima on the electronic ground-state may form by bottom-up association of neutral monomers to a charged monomer ion, or by top-down ionization of a parent neutral cluster. It is interesting to note that although DFT/BNL and CASPT2 methods exhibit the same consistent electronic structure, the near degeneracy of the low-lying states results in remarkably different molecular dynamics of the nuclei, when calculated classically on the ab-initio potentials. This conclusion is not limited to the BNL functional, it is valid for other DFT functionals, such as B3LYP, which we found to exhibit similar dynamics. This finding should serve as an important warning to always check the DFT-based dynamics when near degeneracies appear in other molecular and cluster systems. TDDFT could perhaps allow access to the symmetry-breaking states, though such a calculation was not possible in this system. One can question the validity of NA-AIMD in systems which have degenerate adiabatic states. However, this problem arises only within the degeneracy itself: If nuclei quickly move to break symmetry, degeneracy is eradicated, and the NA-AIMD picture can subsequently be used. Still, at time zero, it is impossible to decide a priori whether one should localize the hole (as in CASPT2) or not (as in BNL/DFT). A posteriori, it turns out that only the hole-localized CASPT2 dynamics is consistent with our experimental results.

Methods

Theoretical methods

The time-resolved investigation of the CO22+ cation dynamics was modeled using the pump-probe NA-AIMD approach. The preparation of samples and the molecular dynamics calculations were done in three stages. In the 1st stage, 20 independent initial configurations were sampled at the Franck-Condon region from an AIMD trajectory of neutral CO22 at 50 K, calculated at the XMS-CASPT2/(8e,6o)/aug-cc-pVDZ level. These 20 configurations were used for initialization of the CO22+ cation simulations. Thus, 20 DFT/BNL trajectory simulations were performed on the cation ground-state, using the DFT/BNL functional under the aug-cc-pVDZ basis set. While for the CASPT2, a total of 80 NA-AIMD trajectories were simulated on the 4 low lying states of the cation at the XMS-CASPT2/(7e,6o)/aug-cc-pVDZ level. The simulated cation dynamics were in turn sampled 21 times at 25 fs time intervals to provide initial configurations for simulating the Coulomb explosion dynamics on the dication ground-state. A total of 2040 trajectories were simulated on the triplet dication ground-state, initiated from the time-resolved configurations sampled from CASPT2 and DFT/BNL cation simulations. Dication AIMD simulations were performed using the DFT/BNL functional under the aug-cc-pVDZ basis set. Coulomb explosions were simulated until reaching an asymptotic KER value, while metastable trajectories were terminated after reaching 700 fs. The DFT/BNL dynamics on the dication were validated by performing simulations also on the XMS-CASPT2/(8e,9o) level for 21 representative configurations initiated on the triplet dication ground-state, and for 80 configurations initiated on the singlet dication, see supplementary note 3 for more detail. Unfortunately, due to near-degeneracy, DFT/BNL could not simulate dynamics on the singlet dication.

Q-CHEM 5.4 was used for all the DFT/BNL calculations27. While for all the XMS-CASPT2 calculations, we used the density-fitting technique within the Brilliantly Advanced General Electronic-structure Library (BAGEL) software package39, invoking the single-state single-reference (SS-SR) contraction scheme40,41 with the vertical shift parameter set to 0.2Eh. In addition, BAGEL was interfaced with the Newton-X (v2.2) program42,43 for carrying out the surface-hopping NA-AIMD44 using a time step of 0.3 fs. We only allowed hops to the nearest energy state due to CPU time constraints in the trajectory calculations. The photoionization cross-section calculations were performed using the ezDyson software package45,46.

Experimental methods

The FL26 reaction microscope endstation at the FLASH2 FEL in Hamburg was employed for the experiments, and is described in detail in Refs. 14,20. The FEL beam was split into two identical EUV pump and EUV probe pulses. For the time-resolved measurements, the photon energy was fixed at 39 eV while the time-delay was scanned symmetrically from -500 to +500 fs in 10 fs steps, where positive and negative time delays were found to exhibit identical responses (as shown in supplementary note 6). The experimental time-response resolution was estimated by simultaneously observing the time-resolved signal from Coulomb explosions of CO2 monomers (shown in supplementary note 2) that was fitted with a σt ~ 33 ± 8 fs Gaussian, providing an upper limit estimate for the experimental time resolution. For the photon-energy scan measurement shown in supplementary note 1, the photon energy was scanned from 30 to 40 eV while the time delay was fixed at 500 fs between the two pulses. The pump and probe beams were spatially overlapped in a skimmed supersonic beam of CO2. Photoions were accelerated in a homogeneous electric field towards a time and position sensitive microchannel plate detector with a delay-line anode. Using the impact times and positions of coincident CO2+ ions, the KER was calculated for momentum-conserving ion pairs. The low KER peak in Fig. 1 shows the residual contribution from random coincidence of CO2+ ions originating from the predominant signal originating from monomer CO2 molecules. Potential coincidences from higher charge-states of the CO22 dimer, e.g. CO223+→CO2++CO++O+, were all attributed to random coincidence of ionization of separate parent molecules.
