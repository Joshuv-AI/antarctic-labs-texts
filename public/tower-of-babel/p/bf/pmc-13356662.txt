A bibliometric analysis of medication studies in congenital heart disease
Authors: Hugo Veldtman, Muhammad Abdullah Nizam, Sarah Brock, Sneha Kandi, Jan Schoones, Michael Finn, Khalid Al-Najashi, John Cleland, Alexander Opotowsky, Dominica Zentner, Tarek Alsaied, Anastasia D Egorova, Gruschen Veldtman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
I. AbstractBackgroundRandomized controlled trials represent robust foundational material for evidence-based clinical practice in cardiology; however, such trials appear to be scarce in congenital heart disease (CHD). No bibliometric analysis has formally quantified nor characterized this gap in the literature, nor examined the impact of existing medication studies in CHD patients.ObjectivesThis investigation aimed to evaluate and characterize existing medication studies in congenital heart disease.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Emcare and Academic Search Premier were searched (Jan 2000–May 2024) for CHD drug studies. Trials focusing on genetic conditions associated with cardiovascular abnormalities (e.g. Marfan syndrome) and pulmonary hypertension not associated with congenital heart disease were excluded.PROSPERO ID: CRD420251073438.ResultsA total of 160 CHD medication studies involving 59,208 participants were included, spanning a study period of 24.5 years (2000-2024). Sex was reported for 20,092 participants; 10,514 (52.3%) were women. Only 29.4% (n = 47) of trials were RCTs. Time-series breakpoint analysis showed an inflection in annual publication output in 2013 (p = 0.009), with growth before and contraction thereafter. Pulmonary-hypertension drugs accounted for 58.1% of publications. Collectively, industry-funded trials were over twice as likely to be RC type trials (56.7% vs 23.1% in non-industry funded studies).ConclusionsContemporary CHD pharmacotherapy research is limited in spectrum/demographics and appears to have been plateauing globally since 2013. Many studies are single-center, pulmonary-hypertension–focused and not placebo-controlled. There is a need to develop a broader disease evidence-base for medication interventions in CHD.Keywords: Congenital heart disease, Trial design, Pharmacotherapy/drugs, Randomized placebo-controlled trials, Clinical outcomes, Prognosis

Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common subset of congenital malformations, presenting a substantial burden of acute and chronic morbidity, and premature mortality [1]. Advances in cardiovascular therapeutics, largely structural interventions including surgical and transcatheter procedures, in addition to improved intra- and postoperative critical care have enabled most patients with CHD to survive into adulthood [2]. These widely hailed successes have, however, been accompanied by a range of late cardiovascular complications related to longer-term survival. These include heart failure, cardiac rhythm disorders, thromboembolic disease, infective endocarditis, and, increasingly, acquired cardiac and extracardiac diseases in combination with the late sequelae of the original cardiac lesion or its surgical correction [3].

In general cardiology communities, large-scale RCTs have emerged over the past several decades and formed the bedrock of guidelines and evidence-based practice. Due to lack of CHD-specific data, CHD practitioners have by necessity, extrapolated the findings of these larger general cardiovascular disease and heart failure trials into CHD clinical management. However, this approach, while pragmatic, may carries inherent risk that while proven efficacious in structurally normal hearts, may be ineffective or even harmful when applied to the anatomically and physiologically distinct circulations of congenital heart disease.

This study employs bibliometric analysis to evaluate the landscape of pharmacotherapy research in congenital heart disease, characterizing study design, scope, therapeutic focus, and temporal trends. Through quantitative assessment of research output and methodological characteristics, we aim to identify structural gaps in evidence generation not only to draw attention to the paucity of well-designed clinical trials, but also to elucidate features of existing studies that have yielded meaningful results, thereby informing the design of future, more impactful trials [4]. We hypothesize that CHD pharmacotherapy research remains limited in both volume and methodological rigor, with sparse randomized evidence and disproportionate concentration within select therapeutic classes.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This PROSPERO-registered (CRD420251073438) bibliometric analysis is a structured, quantitative evaluation of the published literature on CHD medication studies. As no human subjects were involved in this work, no ethical approval was required.

A comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Emcare and Academic Search Premier databases was conducted, from January 1st 2000 through June 21st 2024, with the guidance of an information specialist (JS) using keywords and detailed vocabulary to search for medication studies in relation to CHD. Two researchers performed the screening and a third aided in providing a consensus for disagreement of inclusion. The exact search terms can be found in appendix 1. Studies included for analysis were those with participants with CHD, that were older than ≥10 years of age at the time of the study, and with at least 10 participants. Exclusion criteria included non-CHD related pulmonary hypertension, non-CHD cardiomyopathies, inherited/genetic aortopathies, patent foramen ovale and bicuspid aortic valves [5]. We specifically excluded BAV valves as this is increasingly managed under general cardiology, and guidelines regarding its management exist in the heart valve literature. We also wanted to focus on anatomically and physiologically distinct congenital heart disease. This investigation had no external funding source and no conflicts of interest. Searches were carried out by one researcher and screenings for eligibility were conducted by two independent researchers. In cases of disagreement, consensus was achieved through referral to a third reviewer (Fig. 1).

Data analysis

Data was extracted independently by three researchers by means of whole-article screening. Duplicates were identified by means of title-keyword-based searches. Assessment of 35 study characteristics were recorded: Trial title, trial name, journal name, type of journal, year of publication, years since publication, first author, senior (last) author, trial registration ID, country of origin, journal impact factor, total number of citations since publication, citations per year (both from Web of Science database), study design, prospective vs retrospective design, placebo use, interventional drug(s), intervention drug type (e.g., heart failure, pulmonary hypertension, antiarrhythmic, or other), crossover design, blinding, type of blinding, center type (multi- or single-center), total trial duration (months), follow-up period (months), length of treatment (months), total number of subjects recruited, number of subjects receiving active drug, number of placebo/control subjects. Study quality/risk of bias assessments were not evaluated as the purpose of this bibliometric review was to document patterns of publications and impact rather than critique the individual trial quality. Citations per year were analyzed to account for the lower citation counts of newer publications. Additionally, participant characteristics were recorded as such: age (years), sex distribution, ethnicity distribution, primary disease, co-morbidities, NYHA functional class, height (cm), weight (kg) and BMI (kg/m2). Studies were defined as pediatric when the average age of participants was less than 18, while adult CHD studies were defined as an average age of 18 and above. Included study designs were categorized based on the Grimes et al., 2002 algorithm for classification of types of clinical research along with a modification to allow for the addition of single arm experimental studies as they were unrepresented in the published algorithm in the study (Fig. 2) [6]. For multicenter studies with several countries of origin, the country with where the majority of patients were enrolled, and if the study was without human subjects, majority authorship nationalities were considered.

The algorithm used to classify study designs where EAS = Experimental Single Arm, RC = Randomized Controlled, and NRC = Non-Randomized Controlled.

To account for its skewed distribution and reduce the influence of extreme values, the total number of study participants was log-transformed prior to analysis. A bidirectional stepwise selection algorithm, which minimizes the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), was employed to determine the inclusion of variables in the final model. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05. Temporal trends in participant recruitment were analyzed using LOESS (locally weighted scatterplot smoothing) regression, a non-parametric method that fits locally weighted polynomial curves to identify patterns without assuming a specific functional form. The LOESS smoothing was performed using R's default span parameter (approximately 0.75), with locally weighted least squares fitting and robust regression to minimize influence of outliers. The 95% confidence intervals were calculated using standard errors from the LOESS fit and are truncated at zero as negative recruitment values are not meaningful.

Multicollinearity among predictors in the final model was assessed using generalized variance inflation factors (GVIF), with values adjusted for degrees of freedom (GVIF1/(2·df)); a threshold of 5.0 was used to flag potentially problematic collinearity. Given that pulmonary hypertension studies (Eisenmenger syndrome and pulmonary HTN) comprised a substantial proportion of the dataset, a pre-specified sensitivity analysis was conducted by re-estimating the stepwise regression model in the non-PH CHD subgroup (n = 80) to evaluate whether findings were robust to the exclusion of PH-associated studies.

Results

The database search identified 4116 records across the six databases. Following title and abstract screening 3938 articles were excluded as irrelevant. See Fig. 1 for further details. Further deduplication and whole-article level screening revealed 160 unique records that were included for study review. This final cohort had a combined total of 59208 participants across a 24.5- year period (2000-2024) and formed the subject of this analysis.

Study design & origin

The United States included the largest number of studies (n = 36), followed by the Netherlands (n = 20), the United Kingdom (n = 16), China (n = 12), Germany (n = 12), and Italy (n = 10) (Fig. 4).

A map showing, categorically the distribution of each study's nation of origin.

Of the 160 included studies, 29.4% (n = 47) were randomized controlled trials and 70.6% (n = 113) were non-randomized designs. The most common study design was experimental single-arm (33.1%, n = 53). The total number of participants across all studies was 59,208 individuals. 90.0% (n = 144) of studies were non-crossover, whereas 10.0% (n = 16) were crossover in design. Fifty-seven studies (35.6%) were multi-center in subject recruitment. Overall, 23.8% (n = 38) of the 160 studies were placebo-controlled, and 24.4% (n = 39) were blinded. This difference was attributable to the study by Ruan et al., 2019 which was not placebo controlled but was blinded and randomized to two study arms consisting of 2 dosages of Fasudil [7]. Study duration on average was 55.0 ± 35.0 months. The mean total subjects recruited per study was 472 with a median of 43. This disparity can be attributed to an anomalous 2020 study which retrospectively analyzed over 44,000 patients. Omitting that study, the mean total recruited per study was 107.37 with a standard deviation of 82.35. Study participants averaged 34.0 ± 4.7 years old. Participant biologic sex (after participant exclusion) distributions were as follows: men (47.7%, n = 9578) and women (52.3%, n = 10514). Of 159 studies, 144 (90.6%) had adult cohorts, with pediatric studies being sparsely distributed (only 15 total, never more than 3 in a single year). The significant increase in publication output from 2005 onward was predominantly driven by adult CHD research. Only 11.3% (n = 18) of the studies reported on the ethnicity of participants. As seen in the central illustration, medication-class studied included pulmonary hypertension medication (58.1%, n = 93), heart failure medication (17.5%, n = 28), antiarrhythmics (6.8%, n = 11), and other (17.5%, n = 28). Many of the included studies focused on Eisenmenger syndrome or pulmonary hypertension (n = 79, 49.3%).

Predictors of study visibility and citation impact

A total of 160 studies were included for the bibliometric analysis (see appendix). The multivariate model identified the journal impact factor and funding source as significant predictors of a study's average annual citations. Overall, the final model accounted for 34.4% of the variance in average citations per year (Adjusted R2 = 0.344). Specifically, journal impact factor and funding source together accounted for 34.4% of the variance in citation rate (Adjusted R2 = 0.344). Industry funding had the largest standardized effect, followed by journal impact factor. A strong, positive association was observed between a journal's impact factor and citation rate; for every one-point increase in impact factor, a study was predicted to receive an additional 0.19 average citations per year (95% CI [0.14, 0.24]; p < 0.001). Compared to government-funded studies (the reference category), studies funded by industry were associated with a significantly higher number of average annual citations, showing a predicted increase of 2.99 citations per year (95% CI [1.31, 4.66]; p = 0.001). An analysis of the standardized beta coefficients revealed that a study being funded by industry had the largest relative effect on citation rates (Std. β = 0.71), followed by the effect of a one-standard-deviation increase in journal impact factor (Std. β = 0.52). The log-transformed number of study participants was not significantly associated with citation rates in the final model (p = 0.162).

Variance inflation factors for all retained predictors were low (GVIF1/(2·df) range: 1.01–1.06), indicating no evidence of multicollinearity. In a sensitivity analysis restricted to non-PH CHD studies (n = 80), bidirectional stepwise selection retained only journal impact factor as a significant predictor (β = 0.18, 95% CI [0.13, 0.24]; p < 0.001; Adjusted R2 = 0.356), consistent with the primary model. Industry funding was not retained in the CHD-only model, suggesting its predictive effect in the full sample was driven primarily by PH-focused trials, which were disproportionately industry-sponsored.

Publication frequency & participation

The analysis of publication volume over the 24-year study period identified a significant breakpoint in the year 2013 (Fig. 3a). The time series regression model demonstrated a significant positive trend in the number of publications prior to this breakpoint, with an estimated increase of approximately 0.90 publications per year (95% CI [0.46, 1.34]; p < 0.001). Following the breakpoint, the rate of publication growth showed a significant decline of 1.37 publications per year relative to the pre-2013 slope (95% CI [−2.20, −0.54]; p = 0.002), indicating that the initial period of growth was followed by a flattening of the publication trend.

Breakpoint analysis conducted of the study quantities over time and LOESS regression curve analysis of average study participation.

Furthermore, a LOESS regression curve analysis was carried out to determine trends in study subject numbers over time on average (Fig. 3b). The analysis revealed distinct phases in participant recruitment patterns over the 24-year period. An initial dormant period (2000-2004) with no recruitment was followed by modest recruitment levels (2005-2010, average ∼40 participants per trial). Subsequently, recruitment demonstrated steady growth through the mid-2010s, reaching peak average recruitment of approximately 160 participants per trial around 2018. Notable variability emerged in recent years (2021-2024), with recruitment ranging from 56 to 304 participants per trial. One exceptional year (2020) with 7574 participants per trial on average was identified as an extreme outlier and excluded from trend analysis to maintain interpretable visualization scaling. This was due to a German retrospective national registry study which included 44,097 participants [8].

Relation to guidelines & funding

With regards to contemporary guidelines, forty-two studies, representing 26.6% of the total studies, appeared in at least one of the ACHD guideline references with those of the American Heart Association (AHA) hosting the most (n = 34). Only three studies were included in both the AHA guidelines and the European Society of Cardiology/Canadian Cardiovascular Society [9]. [10] [11] These were Bradley et al., 2013, Gatzoulis et al., 2008 and Dimopoulos et al., 2010 [12], [13], [14]. Of these, the cohort study Dimopoulos et al., 2010 was deemed the most impactful as it held the highest number of citations amongst these 3 papers (n = 275), average Citations Per Year Since Publication at 18.3 and was published in one of the highest impact factor journals (Circulation).

Most studies with specified funding sources (n = 105) were supported by government grants (31.4%, n = 33), with industry funding a similar number of studies (28.6%, n = 30). Both University/Academic and Hospital/Institutional funded were the least common with a combined prevalence of 8.6% (n = 9). 21.0% (n = 22) of studies were self-funded and 10.5% (n = 11) were funded by non-profits/charities. It was observed that among industry funded studies, RCTs were the most common study design with a proportion of 56.7% (n = 17). In contrast, RCT prevalence was 23.1% (n = 30) among the remainder of non-industry funded studies.

Discussion

The central findings of this bibliometric analysis substantiate our hypothesis. The volume of pharmacotherapy research in CHD remains modest, comprising predominantly observational and non-randomized studies, with no more than eight publications per year between 2022 and 2024. Only 22% of the 160 identified studies were placebo-controlled and blinded, and a disproportionate share focused on pulmonary hypertension-related therapies. Notably, study design correlated with funding source: most industry-funded studies (56.7%) were randomized controlled trials, suggesting that external sponsorship may be a key enabler of methodological rigor in this field. The limited penetrance of this body of evidence into contemporary guideline documents is equally telling: only 42 studies (26.6%) appeared in at least one major ACHD guideline reference, with AHA guidelines citing the largest proportion (n = 34), while only three studies were shared between the AHA and the ESC/CCS guidelines. This divergence across guideline bodies is unlikely to reflect differences in evidence alone but very likely also reflects guideline authorship inherent susceptibility to systematic, if unavoidable other biases.

Evidence hierarchy, output & geography

Data derived from multiple RCTs, or meta-analyses lead to level A evidence-based recommendations, i.e., the highest level of treatment recommendation. The American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association defines level A evidence as “Data derived from multiple randomized clinical trials or meta-analyses” [15]. This has been achieved for pulmonary hypertension treatments in CHD medication studies (the pharmaceutical under evaluation in 58.1% of all studies (n = 93)). On the contrary, small, retrospective, or registry-based studies generally result in level C evidence-based recommendations only. The scarcity of high-quality medication studies for a wider array of drugs in CHD is concerning. We speculate that potential contributing factors include an initial publication focus for CHD researchers on defining late CHD outcomes and on defining specialized care centers and systems. The annual number of studies conducted peaked in 2013 (n = 15) and steadily decreased since, with only sixteen studies being conducted in the last 3 years. COVID-19 observably impacted CHD research, with a high suspension rate of 53% in 2020, resulting in a 45.5% decrease in studies from 2019 to 2020 in contrast to a 22.2% in the previous year [16]. We could not however observe this effect in the amalgamated medication studies, presumably because of their limited number overall.

The overall distribution of CHD medication studies weighs towards the global west (such as the United States and the Netherlands). Only one non-North American/non-European nation (China) had ≥10 studies attributed to it. This pattern is consistent with the wider CHD research literature i.e. including non-medication studies. Zila-Velasque et al., 2023 presented similar findings as they analyzed 8934 global CHD studies, 34% of which originated in the United States, whereas this work hosted 22.5% US studies [17]. Nabyonga-Orem et al., 2020 similarly reported that in low-income nations, only 5 people per million are involved in full-time research compared to 363 in high income western nations [18].

Demographics

Racial diversity is imperative for developing treatments that are similarly effective and safe for distinct populations. Yet over the past decade, numerous studies have documented persistent underrepresentation in clinical trials of racial and ethnic minorities in relation to the overall population [19]. In broader cardiovascular literature, in the realm of heart failure RCTs for example, <40% of trials reported race/ethnicity and <20% enrolled non-White participants [20]. The field of CHD medication research is no exception as only 11.3% (n = 18) of the 160 medication studies specified at least two ethnicities. Such limited racial demographic data all but precludes subgroup analyses that could reveal genotype-driven differences in drug exposure or in class-specific adverse effects. The 4.6% difference in sex distribution may be attributed to the fact that a higher proportion of women have pulmonary hypertension as in recent U.S. cohorts, by almost four to one [21].

Innovation and impact of COVID-19

We assert that advances in CHD medication research necessitate inclusion of a wider range of medications with trial designs focused on contributing high level evidence. Optimally, trials should be large-scale and multicenter to generate high-quality data, particularly for novel therapies such as sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors [22]. BREATHE – 5 is a prime example of an RCT with an important impact in CHD with the highest number of citations (n = 656) as well as being published in a high impact factor journal [23]. Only six randomized controlled trials have been conducted since 2020 (half of which focused on pulmonary hypertension medication), supporting the need for diversifying the spectrum of medications being studied in CHD.

Research funding gravitates toward interventions with clinically meaningful endpoints and clear regulatory pathways. Such commercially attractive targets can lead to medication overrepresentation in CHD literature. Although understandable, this pattern perpetuates critical evidence gaps for therapies addressing equally burdensome but methodologically complex lesions, leaving clinicians with little guidance beyond observational data. For instance, roughly 9% of CHD individuals have tachyarrhythmias, yet no industry funded medication trials exist focusing on antiarrhythmic therapies for CHD from 2000 to 2024 [24]. This pattern appears to reflect a wider trend in medical research. In the United States for example the NIH spent $8.1 billion on phased clinical trials of medications approved between 2010 and 2019, just 10% of what industry committed over the same period [25]. It is thus difficult to envision how a purely market driven drug research model would address the more diverse therapeutic needs in CHD.

The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted platform RCT methodology as efficient and cost effective in enabling successful cross-institutional medication collaboration and expedited IRB approval [26]. This, together with stable, collaborative networks of participating centers, can potentially help mitigate the relative scarcity of medication studies in CHD. Clinical trials in congenital heart disease face challenges largely absent from general cardiology. CHD populations are heterogeneous at multiple levels spanning different surgical eras, anatomic variation, and hemodynamic diversity even within uniform diagnostic categories and are constrained by small patient numbers. This complexity renders conventional trial designs impractical and unattractive to potential funders, particularly industry sponsors whose therapeutic portfolios are oriented toward larger, more homogeneous populations.

Meaningful progress in CHD pharmacotherapy will depend on the establishment of international multicenter research networks coupled with the adoption of innovative trial methodologies. Platform trials, for instance, offer the capacity to evaluate multiple therapies efficiently within small, heterogeneous cohorts while minimizing resource burden. Oncology provides an instructive precedent: umbrella and basket trial designs have enabled rare genetic and phenotypic subtypes to be studied rigorously without requiring standalone trials for each [27]. Similarly, the incorporation of CHD-specific arms into large cardiovascular trials would broaden the evidence base without the prohibitive cost of independent studies.

Study limitations and conclusions

A limitation of this bibliometric analysis was that the search captured only studies where all participants had CHD, as opposed to at least 50% and only peer-reviewed English-language articles were included, causing nine non-English studies to be omitted. Additionally, as the objective was descriptive, trial quality was not an exclusion criterion, and no formal Cochrane (risk of bias) or Newcastle-Ottawa assessments were performed. Consequently, highly cited studies of poor methodological rigor may be weighted equally with rigorously designed RCTs. Additionally, a single 2020 retrospective national registry contributed 44,097 participants (74% of all pooled subjects), may have distorted descriptive population statistics [8].

In conclusion, this analysis emphasizes a critical need for focused improvements in CHD medication research, specifically addressing biases toward commercially funded pulmonary hypertension studies, underrepresentation/lack of reporting regarding diverse patient populations, and scant therapy innovation. Targeted investment from the state, nonprofit organizations, academic institutions and hospitals in rigorous, multicenter randomized controlled trials across a broader spectrum of CHD medications and conditions is imperative. Such efforts will influence forthcoming clinical guidelines, ensure broader applicability of treatments, and reduce healthcare disparities, ultimately improving patient outcomes and informing equitable healthcare policies globally.
