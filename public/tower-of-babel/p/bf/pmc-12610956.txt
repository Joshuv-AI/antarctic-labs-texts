Metamorphosis of Double Doughnut Sign to Trilaminar Sign: Evolving Neuroimaging Spectrum of Acute Necrotizing Encephalopathy
Authors: Gayatri Pawar, Virupaxi Hattiholi, Mahesh Kamate
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

The double-doughnut sign (DDS) was first described by Kumar et al.[1] in 2017 in a 22-year-old primigravida with dengue infection. Cerebrospinal fluid showed lymphocytic pleocytosis with elevated proteins. Her magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain showed involvement of the bilateral thalami, midbrain, and deep cerebellar white matter. The center of the thalamic lesions showed diffusion restriction (bright signal) on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), with reversal on apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps. Susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) showed evidence of hemorrhage in the thalami. The ADC maps and SWI images, showing central hypointensity surrounded by hyperintensities in the thalami, give the characteristic double-doughnut appearance and have been described as the DDS.

Since its first description, the DDS has been reported as characteristic of dengue encephalitis in many case reports of adult patients. However, it is also noted in other neuroinfections, such as Japanese encephalitis, West Nile virus, rabies, and herpes simplex virus infections.[2] The pathogenesis of this radiological sign is hypothesized to be secondary to direct neuronal injury, resulting in vascular leakage, edema, and hemorrhage in areas of the thalamus and basal ganglia, which may be related to a brain-localized cytokine storm.[3]

Acute necrotizing encephalopathy of childhood (ANEC) is one of the infection-triggered encephalopathy syndromes. It is a fulminant type of febrile encephalopathy characterized by an altered sensorium, seizures, and focal neurological deficits. ANEC cases are typically described in children of Asian origin, with the highest prevalence between the ages of 6 and 18 months.[4] Although cases are mainly reported in the pediatric age group, it can also affect adults. The condition has multisystemic involvement, causing hepatic dysfunction, respiratory failure, diarrhea, and systemic inflammatory response syndrome, which can also lead to multiorgan failure, indicating widespread activation of the innate immune system.[5]

The initial radiological description of bilateral symmetrical involvement of the thalamus with hemorrhage and necrosis was given by Mizuguchi and colleagues.[6] Typical radiological findings are observed in the deep gray matter, reflecting edema, hemorrhage, and necrosis. There is rapid involvement of lesions, showing hypointensities on T1-weighted images and hyperintensities on T2- and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery images. Microhemorrhages are evident on susceptibility-weighted images. Similar radiological findings can be seen in Japanese encephalitis, mitochondrial disorders such as Leigh disease, and carbon monoxide poisoning, but central thalamic necrosis is typical of ANEC. Other areas of involvement of ANEC in the brain are the brainstem, cerebral and cerebellar white matter, and the putamen.

The trilaminar sign on ADC mapping is classically described for ANEC. Among the three different layers, the symmetrical central areas of hyperintensities in the bilateral thalami indicate hemorrhagic necrosis, the middle hypointense rim represents cytotoxic edema, and the outer surrounding hyperintense rim denotes vasogenic edema.[7] This imaging pattern and the reason for its appearance are almost the same as DDS, but with an additional third layer. ANEC is one of the infection-triggered encephalopathy syndromes in which an infection triggers the innate immune system. The unrestricted activation of the immune system leads to a significant release of proinflammatory mediators, resulting in a “cytokine storm” localized to the deep nuclei, brainstem, and cerebellum, similar to what is seen in dengue and other viral infections associated with DDS.

There are several case reports in which severe dengue has triggered ANEC in children.[7] The involvement of the brain in severe dengue encephalitis is described as affecting the bilateral thalamus, brainstem, cerebellum, basal ganglia, medial temporal lobes, and cortical and cerebellar white matter, similar to findings in dengue encephalitis–associated DDS.[8] This leads to the observation that both DDS and the trilaminar sign could be the same. In a study by Priyal Lnu et al.,[9] which described the MRI spectrum of dengue encephalitis cases, three out of eight cases involved the thalamus.

We saw a 4-year-old boy who presented to us with fever, altered sensorium, and seizures. His neuroimaging on day 2 of illness showed bilateral (left > right) central diffusion restriction in the thalamus on DWI, with reversal on ADC mapping, giving the appearance of doughnuts [Figure 1a and b]. SWI revealed blooming suggesting blood in the thalami [Figure 2]. Repeat neuroimaging after 6 days, due to lack of improvement, revealed a typical trilaminar sign with a central hyperintense, middle hypointense, and outer hyperintense rim [Figure 1c, d]. It also revealed that the signal changes have evolved and involved new areas such as putamen and corpus callosum [Figure 3]. There was an evolution from a DDS to a trilaminar sign in our case, suggesting that both the DDS and the trilaminar sign represent the same disease, albeit at different phases of its evolution. The appearance of the DDS or the trilaminar sign depends on the stage of the disease at which the imaging was taken. Careful analysis of our case helped us to conclude that the DDS is likely an early sign of ANEC.

The neuroimaging features of severe dengue encephalitis presenting with DDS and ANEC are overlapping. Since a few other infections can also trigger similar pathology on neuroimaging, whether we are dealing with cases of acute necrotizing encephalitis (ANE), which is infection-triggered rather than due to direct neuroinvasion per se—especially when the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cell count is normal—warrants consideration. Most cases reported with DDS in dengue encephalitis are of Asian origin, and similarly, so are the ANEC cases. The neurotropism of viruses like dengue leads to the activation of the innate immune system in the thalamus, resulting in DDS first, followed by the trilaminar sign. It is essential to recognize this distinction, as dengue encephalitis presenting with DDS is typically treated with supportive care. In contrast, in ANEC, early immunomodulation with pulse methylprednisolone and newer therapies, such as tocilizumab, can help ensure survival and reduce morbidity.[10] Although ANEC has been primarily described in children, DDS mainly represents early ANE and can occur even in adults. As this has therapeutic implications, it is essential to recognize this fact and disseminate the information so that many lives can be saved and morbidity reduced.

Therefore, we conclude that the DDS may be an early indication of infection-triggered ANE and could further evolve into the trilaminar sign. Both suggest the same disease. The presence of a doughnut sign or trilaminar sign on an MRI brain scan should suggest a diagnosis of infection-triggered encephalopathy syndrome and prompt initiation of immunomodulation with pulse methylprednisolone and tocilizumab.
