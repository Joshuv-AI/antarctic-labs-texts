Association between the Chinese visceral adiposity index and thyroid nodule risk: a retrospective analysis of cross-sectional, case-control, and cohort studies
Authors: Ting Tian, Renfang Han, Quan Zhou, Yuanli Li, Yi Wang, Nimei Zeng, Fangfei Xie, Yun Wang, Chenyi Zhong, Jingyi Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the increasing use of ultrasound, the detection rate of thyroid nodules (TNs) has risen markedly in recent years. However, the relationship between obesity and TNs remains controversial. This study aimed to investigate the association between the Chinese visceral adiposity index (CVAI), a novel obesity indicator, and the risk of TNs.MethodsA multi-design study was conducted among individuals aged 18–80 years who underwent periodic health examinations in Suzhou, China, from 2020 to 2024. The discovery stage comprised cross-sectional and case-control analyses to examine associations between CVAI and TN risk. The validation stage involved a prospective cohort study evaluating the predictive role of CVAI in incident TNs. Multivariable logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards models were applied.ResultsThe cross-sectional study included 83,757 participants, and the case-control study included 31,035 matched pairs. The median age was 40.59 and 39.28 years, respectively. Each standard deviation increase in CVAI was significantly associated with an increased risk of TN [cross-sectional: odds ratio (OR) = 1.19, 95% confidence interval (95% CI): 1.16–1.22; case-control: OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.20–1.27]. The cohort study including 18,110 participants validated the association [hazard ratio (HR) = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.07–1.18]. Restricted cubic spline analyses demonstrated a nonlinear dose-response relationship, with a steep rise in TN risk at higher CVAI levels. Subgroup analyses revealed stronger associations in females, individuals with lower body mass index (BMI), those who engaged in regular exercise, and participants without dyslipidemia.ConclusionElevated CVAI was significantly associated with an increased risk of TNs. These findings provided new insights for scientific management and prevention of TNs, highlighting the potential value of CVAI in health management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-026-02240-y.

Background

Periodic health examination (PHE) is an essential part of clinical prevention, typically involving one or more physician visits that include screening tests and counseling interventions. The aim of PHE is to avoid risk factors and diseases or to identify and treat them at an early stage [1]. Over the past two decades, with the widespread use of ultrasound examinations in PHE, an increasing number of thyroid nodules (TNs) have been detected. In China, the prevalence of TNs is strikingly high, affecting approximately 30%-50% of otherwise healthy adults [2]. Although most TNs exhibit slow and indolent progression, about 5%-15% may eventually develop into malignancy [3], a possibility that often causes considerable anxiety among examinees. Consequently, identifying the risk factors associated with TNs is essential to reducing physical and psychological distress, alleviating social and economic burdens, and aligning with the principle of prevention before treatment.

TNs are not only highly prevalent but also have complex and multifactorial risk factors. Traditional factors, such as sex, age, iodine intake, obesity, metabolic syndrome, and thyroid autoimmunity cannot fully account for the observed prevalence [4–6]. Obesity, which is increasing rapidly worldwide [7], is closed linked to metabolic syndrome, particularly when characterized by central body fat accumulation [8]. However, the association between obesity and TNs remains controversial. Many studies revealed a correlation between obesity and TNs, particularly among women [9–11], whereas others found no significant relevance [12, 13]. The aforementioned studies have focused on conventional anthropometric indicators, body mass index (BMI). Although BMI, waist circumference (WC), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), are widely used to assess visceral obesity, these indicators are incapable of precisely differentiating between muscle and fat mass, as well as between peripheral and abdominal fat. Moreover, their sensitivity to height and weight variations limits their efficacy in comprehensively assessing the body’s external morphology and internal tissue composition.

Visceral adiposity index (VAI) is a novel fat fraction calculated based on WC, BMI, and serum lipid levels, in which WC played a significant role [14, 15]. However, body fat distribution varies substantially among ethnicities [16]. Compared with Western populations, Chinese individuals are more prone to abdominal obesity, rendering the VAI an inaccurate predictor for this group. To address this limitation, Xia et al. [17] proposed the Chinese visceral adiposity index (CVAI) as a refined fat index model which integrates key parameters, including age, BMI, WC, triglycerides (TG), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), to provide a more precise assessment of visceral adiposity in Chinese population.

In this study, based on the advantages of PHE population, we calculated CVAI in accordance with the method described previously [17] and then conducted a multi-stage study to evaluate the associations between CVAI and TNs in Chinese population. We used multiple study designs including cross-sectional, matched case-control, prospective cohort and nested case-control designs to enhance the robustness of our results.

Methods

Data source and study design

We conducted a multi-stage study based on the Periodic Health Examination project from 2020 to 2024 among general populations at Healthcare Center of Affiliated Suzhou Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. Most participants undergoing physical examinations were employees of various employers in Suzhou aged 18–80 years old, who received annual or biennial check-ups. We collected data from participants who volunteered to undergo basic health examinations, thyroid scans, and face-to-face interviews. Thyroid ultrasonography was performed routinely for all participants as part of the standardized health examination protocol, rather than being based on clinical suspicion. The date when participants first underwent a thyroid ultrasound scan was set as the baseline. Participants were excluded if they (1) had TNs that were not classified or classified as “Category 0” under the Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System (TI-RADS) (N = 13,496); (2) had a history of thyroid surgery (N = 2,196); (3) had thyroid disorders such as hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, or Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (N = 246); (4) were diagnosed with cancers (N = 152); (5) were pregnant or lactating (N = 59) and (6) had missing clinical parameters data required to calculate CVAI (N = 4,076). After applying these criteria, 83,757 participants being included in the cross-sectional analysis (Discovery stage I, Fig. 1). To minimize bias and the impact of age, thereby enabling a more reasonable comparison between the TNs and the control group, we performed 1:1 ratio matching based on age and gender, yielding 31,035 matched pairs (Discovery stage II, Fig. 1). In order to validate the association of CVAI and TNs, we restricted to the participants with at least two valid thyroid ultrasound examination and a follow-up period over 180 days. 18,110 participants were included in the cohort analysis (Validation stage, Fig. 1). Given that CVAI index is composed of age and four other indicators, and age could exert a substantial influence, we further conducted a nested case-control study design with 1:1 matching by age and gender to confirm that CVAI itself was the driving factor.

This study was approved by The Affiliated Suzhou Hospital of Nanjing Medical University Ethical Committees and all study participants signed their informed consent.

Data collection

Smoking, alcohol consumption and exercise information was obtained from face-to-face interviews conducted before the physical examination. The questionnaire also included medical and medication histories. Demographic characteristics, such as age and gender, were obtained from the health records at the time of enrollment. Height and weight were measured using the Omron ultrasonic weight scale (HNH-318). During the measurement, the participant was required to stand fully upright without bending or overextending, with eyes level and the head neither lowered nor tilted back. BMI was calculated as weight divided by the square of height (kg/m2). WC was measured twice at the midpoint between the lowest edge of the chest and the highest edge of the pelvis (iliac crest) while the participants exhaled. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were measured twice using an electronic sphygmomanometer and the mean value was recorded. Blood samples were collected from participants after at least 10 h of fasting and analyzed at the Laboratory Medicine Department. Serum levels of fasting plasma glucose (FPG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), TG, total cholesterol (TC), HDL-C and low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C) for each participant were detected using the Beckman Coulter AU5800 automated biochemical analyzer (Beckman Coulter, Inc., Brea, CA, USA).

Hypertension was defined as a self-reported history of hypertension, SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg [18]. Diabetes was defined as a self-reported history of diabetes, FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or HbA1c ≥ 6.5% [19]. Hyperlipidemia was defined as a self-reported history of hyperlipidemia, TC ≥ 6.22 mmol/L, TG ≥ 2.26 mmol/L, HDL-C < 1.04 mmol/L, or LDL-C ≥ 4.14 mmol/L [20].

Thyroid nodule evaluation

Ultrasonographic examinations of multi-planar scans of the thyroid (Philips EPIQ7, The Netherlands) were conducted by two experienced sonographers. They must thoroughly study and understand the TI-RADS and must undergo operational assessment to ensure their scanning techniques comply with the research protocol requirements. Regular meetings were convened to discuss difficult cases encountered in the study, ensuring the consistency of interpretive criteria. To ensure consistency, TI-RADS classifications were independently reviewed by each other. Interobserver agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistic, showing substantial agreement (κ = 0.87). The number, location, size, shape (round, ovoid or irregular), margin (clear or blurred), echogenic feature (anechoic, hypoechoic, cystic, mixed echogenicity), and color Doppler flow signal of the thyroid nodules were documented. Nodules were subsequently classified according to the TI-RADS grading criteria. Individuals whose nodules could not be classified or were classified as “Category 0” under the TI-RADS system were excluded from the analysis (Fig. 1).

Statistical analyses

For descriptive analysis, medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) were employed for non-normal distribution variables. Categorical variables were reported using absolute frequencies and percentages. Data distribution was assessed using the Anderson-Darling test. Since all the variables deviated from normality (P < 0.001), non-parametric test Mann-Whitney U was used for group comparisons. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test.

Multivariable logistic regression models were performed to compute odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) with adjustment for age, gender, SBP, DBP, TC, LDL-C, FPG, smoking, alcohol consumption, exercise, hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidemia in the cross-sectional (Discovery stage I), matched case-control (Discovery stage II) and matched nested case-control (Validation stage) analyses. To minimize confounding, age- and sex-matched analyses were conducted using propensity score matching (PSM). Participants with thyroid nodules (TN group) and those without (control group) were matched in a 1:1 ratio using the nearest-neighbor method with a caliper width of 0.02. The matching was performed using R software (version 4.3.0) with the ‘MatchIt’ package. All participants were successfully matched, and no individuals were excluded due to matching failure. While for incident TNs, we took Cox proportional hazards regression models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs in the cohort study (Validation stage). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was also used to assess the potential nonlinear relationship between CVAI and the risk of TNs. Furthermore, we divided the participants into four categories according to the quartiles of CVAI and compared ORs or HRs for the other participants with those in the lowest quintile. Tests for linear trend across CVAI quartiles were performed using CVAI category as an ordinal variable.

In addition, subgroup analyses were conducted by age (younger: < median age; older: ≥ median age), gender (male and female), BMI (lower: < 24 kg/m2; higher: ≥ 24 kg/m2), WC (lower: <85 cm for women or < 90 cm for men; higher: ≥85 cm for women or ≥ 90 cm for men), smoking (yes and no), alcohol consumption (yes and no), exercise (yes and no), hypertension (yes and no), diabetes (yes and no) and dyslipidemia (yes and no), respectively. We also performed a subgroup analysis stratified by the TI-RADS classification to investigate the association between CVAI and thyroid nodules with different risks of malignancy in the cross-sectional and the matched case-control analyses. Testing for differences in the effects of CVAI across the groups was assessed by using the P values or I2 calculated from Cochran’s Q statistic. The interactive effects between CVAI and subgroups were tested in the models with interaction terms by the likelihood ratio test, adjusting for aforementioned confounding factors.

We also performed sensitivity analyses to examine the robustness of our results. Firstly, we excluded participants diagnosed with TNs within the first year of follow-up in the cohort study. Secondly, we excluded participants with missing covariates or diagnosed with prevalent hyperlipidemia across all three studies. A two-sided P value of < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.3).

Results

Associations of CVAI with TNs in both cross-sectional and case-control studies

First, we conducted a cross-sectional study to examine the association between CVAI and TNs (Discovery stage I). We described the characteristics of the 83,757 participants to reflect the real-world (Table 1). Among them, 52.57% (44,027/83,757) were diagnosed with TNs. The median age was 40.59 years, and 46.86% were female. The majority of individuals did not smoke (87.66%) or drink alcohol (88.57%), and over half reported regular exercise (66.76%). The prevalence of comorbidities was as follows: 22.40% had hypertension, 6.31% had diabetes, and 32.57% had dyslipidemia. The median CVAI index for all participants was 77.45 (IQR: 38.27, 111.65). We found that TN cases had a higher median CVAI index than non-TN controls (P value < 0.001). The difference in gender prevalence was statistically significant as well, with women showing a higher prevalence in cases than that in controls (53.39% vs. 39.62%, P value < 0.001). Additionally, TN cases had significantly higher values of BMI, WC, TG, HDL-C, SBP, DBP, TC, FPG, and HbA1c compared with non-TN controls (all P value < 0.001). Given the differences of age and gender distribution between TN cases and non-TN controls, we subsequently conducted a 1:1 matched case-control study. The descriptive characteristics of the case-control study (Discovery stage II) are also presented in Table 1. After matching, there was no difference in the proportions of smoking and alcohol consumption between the case and control groups (P value > 0.05). However, LDL-C levels remained significantly higher in TN cases (P value < 0.001).

Associations of CVAI and incident TNs in the cohort study

Up to date of December 31 2024, 4,546 incident TNs cases were ascertained out of 18,110 eligible participants, with an overall follow-up of 37,675 person-years. The baseline characteristics of the 18,110 participants across the quartiles of CVAI in this cohort analysis are shown in Table 2(Validation stage). Consistent with the findings from two discovery studies, density plot showed a distinct shift toward higher CVAI values in TN cases (Figure S1C), and per-sd increase in CVAI was significantly associated with an increased risk of TNs (HR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.07–1.18, Fig. 2A). When compared to the lowest CVAI quartile group, the highest CVAI quartile group had a significantly higher risk of TNs (HR = 1.47, 95% CI: 1.30–1.67, Fig. 2A). In addition, there was a significant trend of risk for TNs (P for trend = 0.028) from quartile 1 to 4 of CVAI. RCS analysis also confirmed a nonlinear association between CVAI and incident TN risk (Poverall < 0.001, Pnonlinear = 0.023, Fig. 2D).

After matching on age and gender, no significant differences were observed in baseline TC levels, smoking status, alcohol consumption, or exercise between the two groups (all P value > 0.05). Subgroup and sensitive analyses

Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate whether specific characteristics, including age, gender, BMI, WC, smoking, alcohol consumption, exercise, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, alleviated the associations between CVAI and TNs. The distributions of CVAI among subgroups in the three analyses are shown in Table S3. Across the three studies, the association between CVAI and TNs risk remained significant in most subgroups (Fig. 3), indicating the stability of the results. When stratified by gender, BMI, exercise and dyslipidemia status, the associations were significantly stronger among females, individuals with lower BMI levels, those who engaged in regular exercise, and those without dyslipidemia (P for heterogeneity < 0.05; Fig. 3) in both cross-sectional and case-control studies. In the cohort study, a similar trend was observed, although the difference did not reach statistical significance. No significant difference was observed in other subgroups (all P for heterogeneity > 0.05, Fig. 3). In the subgroup analysis stratified by the TI-RADS classification (Table S4), we observed that the effect size of CVAI varied across cases with different TI-RADS classifications (P for heterogeneity < 0.001), with the strongest effect seen in TI-RADS 4 cases in the cross-sectional (OR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.36–1.68) and the matched case-control (OR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.37–1.75) analyses.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a multi-design study based on the general populations taking PHE to evaluate the associations between CVAI, a novel indicator of visceral adiposity, and thyroid nodules (TNs). The consistent findings across all studies revealed that higher CVAI was significantly associated with an increased risk of TNs. This association was nonlinear, with a sharp rise in risk observed among individuals with higher CVAI values. Subgroup analyses revealed that the effect of CVAI was more pronounced in females, individuals with lower BMI, regular exercisers, and those without dyslipidemia.

Even if the malignant transformation rate of TNs is relatively low, the high incidence rate still imposed a considerable psychological burden on patients and their families. A meta-analysis revealed that 68.8% of all thyroid nodules undergoing surgical excision were benign [21], highlighting the potential for excess healthcare costs generated by the detection and treatment of benign and subclinical diseases. Undoubtedly, the evaluation and diagnosis of TNs were important and were under heated discussions in the management of TNs [4]. Nevertheless, the prevention of TNs remains an important but underexplored strategy, as only a limited number of studies have investigated their risk factors. Among established determinants, gender has been confirmed as a major risk factor for TNs [22]. Multiple studies, both domestic and international, have documented a striking sex disparity in TNs prevalence, with substantially higher rates among females than males [6, 23, 24]. Consistent with these findings, our study observed a significantly higher prevalence of TNs in females compared to males. In addition, abdominal obesity and adipose tissue dysfunction are closely related to thyroid diseases [25, 26], including TNs [27]. The prevalence of TNs was higher in obese males compared with lean controls [28]. A Chinese SPECT cross-sectional study identified obesity as an independent risk factor for the increased prevalence of TNs [29]. In our study, we also observed the significant difference of BMI between TN cases and non-TN controls. However, BMI is commonly used to assess peripheral obesity, but it may not accurately reflect visceral fat function and distribution in the Chinese population. Given the substantial burden of obesity-associated diseases, there is an urgent need to establish a reliable and sensitive indicator of abdominal obesity specifically tailored to the Chinese population. To address this, the CVAI was developed using data from Chinese individuals [17]. In this study, we further explored the association between CVAI and TNs.

CVAI, as a comprehensive index, includes BMI, WC, TG, and HDL-C. Previous studies have reported that CVAI outperforms BMI, WC, WHR, WHtR in diagnosing conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and carotid plaque [30–32]. As mentioned above, BMI does not provide information about fat distribution. WC, WHR, and WHtR are limited to assessing abdominal fat mass without distinguishing visceral from subcutaneous adipose tissue. In contrast, CVAI specifically reflects visceral fat accumulation [17]. In addition, compared to visceral fat content measured by CT, MRI, or DEXA scans, CVAI is more accessible and has broad clinical application prospects. Mechanistically, the accumulation of visceral fat promotes lipid synthesis due to increased levels of free fatty acids, thereby inducing metabolic syndrome through enhanced gluconeogenesis [33]. Therefore, one possible explanation for our findings is that key features of metabolic syndrome, such as obesity and insulin resistance, may lead to hyperplasia of thyroid cells, thereby facilitating the development of TNs and even thyroid cancer [34].

Interestingly, the association between CVAI and TNs appeared to be stronger in females, individuals with normal BMI, those with normal blood lipid levels, and those who engage in regular exercise. For these individuals, an elevated CVAI level increased the risk of TNs. Biologically, obesity results in the secretion of inflammatory factors that stimulate the expression of the aromatase enzyme, which converts androgens into estrogens in the adipose tissue [35]. Estrogen, in turn, exerts a certain growth-promoting effect on thyroid cells [36], which might partially explain why women, particularly those who are obese, have a higher incidence of thyroid nodules. The components of CVAI include BMI and blood lipids, so individuals with lower BMI and blood lipid levels should maintain lower CVAI levels. Similarly, physical activity is inversely correlated with visceral adiposity, meaning regular exercisers should also have lower CVAI. Therefore, for these individuals, the adverse effects associated with high CVAI levels may be amplified. This suggests that other underlying factors may also play a role, such as poor dietary habits, chronic stress, and insufficient sleep.

This study had several notable strengths. Firstly, to enhance the credibility of our conclusions, we implemented a multi-design study design that integrated cross-sectional, case-control, and prospective cohort approaches. The cross-sectional and case-control components facilitated the inclusion of a larger sample size, whereas the prospective cohort strengthened the reliability of the findings. Moreover, considering the inherent characteristic of CVAI, we also took the nested case-control design. This design integrated the strengths of methodologies and provided an interesting methodological framework for future researches. Secondly, the use of the PHE cohort ensured access to comprehensive clinical data, and repeated physical examinations allowed accurate retrieval of follow-up data. Finally, to minimize potential confounding, individuals with thyroid disorders, thyroid medication use, pregnancy, and lactation were excluded from the analysis.

However, this study also had some limitations. Firstly, compared to the general populations, individuals taking PHE tended to be more health-conscious, more willing to acquire health-related knowledge, and more likely to adopt health-promoting behaviors. In our study population, the proportions of smokers and alcohol drinkers were lower than those in the general population [37, 38], while the proportion of individuals engaging in regular physical exercise was relatively higher. Therefore, whether the conclusions of this study could be generalized to other populations remains to be further validated. Secondly, unlike the active follow-up methods used in other studies, the follow-up data in this study were collected passively from the health examination cohort, which may lead to incomplete or biased follow-up information. Thirdly, the CVAI is a calculated surrogate indicator of visceral adiposity. Although it has been validated in Chinese populations, we did not use more precise imaging methods such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to directly quantify visceral fat area, which might provide more accurate associations. Finally, while this study adjusted for several important covariates including age, gender, and key lifestyle and medical history factors, data on other potential confounders such as iodine intake, detailed dietary habits, occupational exposures, and genetic predisposition were not available. Additionally, genetic information was not available. Integrating genetic information and exploring gene-environment interaction may provide deeper insights into the determinants of TNs and represent an important direction for future studies.

Conclusions

In summary, we verified that the CVAI, a novel comprehensive index of visceral fat, was associated with an increased risk of thyroid nodules across cross-sectional, case-control and prospective cohort studies. These findings provide valuable insights for the scientific management and prevention of thyroid nodules in clinical and public health contexts.
