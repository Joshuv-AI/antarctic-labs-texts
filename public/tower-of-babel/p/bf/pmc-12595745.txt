Femoral fracture risk in transfemoral amputees with bone-anchored prosthetic limbs during activities of daily living
Authors: Ryan Tiew, Hans A. Gray, Dale L. Robinson, Karolina Siwicka, David C. Ackland, Peter V. S. Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBone-anchored prostheses (BAPs) address many of the shortcomings of socket prostheses, driving the adoption of the technology for lower limb amputees. However, higher femoral fracture rates have been observed in these patients compared to socket users. It is believed that this increased fracture rate is due to the direct transmission of loads to bone through the bone-anchored implant. While low-impact activities, such as walking, have been extensively studied using finite element (FE) models, other more demanding low-impact activities such as ambulating on stairs and inclines remain poorly understood. Most previously reported subject-specific FE models of femurs fitted with osseointegrated implants were not validated against experimental data. The current study aimed to validate an FE modelling methodology using implanted cadaveric specimens and to calculate the fracture risk of implanted femurs during various activities of daily living.MethodStrain responses of cadaveric femurs fitted with press-fit osseointegrated implants under load were recorded and compared to strain predicted by specimen-specific FE models created from CT data. Five different published relationships between Hounsfield units and Young’s Moduli were investigated to determine which relationship gave the most accurate model predictions. The validated FE models of the implanted femurs were later used to simulate in-vivo force measurements during five routine activities of daily living. Lastly, the fracture risk of the implanted femurs during these activities was quantified using factor of safety (FOS), where FOS = 1 denoted fracture was predicted at the given load.ResultsThe validated FE model predicted the experimental strain responses with a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.91 and a root mean square error (RMSE) of 81.27 με. The FOS for the activities of daily living of individual specimens ranged from 1.8 to 5.0, with walking up an incline having the lowest average FOS (1.9) across all specimens. Compared across different environments, the average FOS for level walking (4.3) and ambulating on stairs (4.0) were similar but almost double that of ambulating on an incline (2.2).ConclusionMale BAP FE models predicted the experimental bone strains and suggested that peak loads during ambulation on level ground, stairs, and inclines are unlikely to cause bone fracture.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06311-z.

Background

Today, it is estimated that there are over 36 million lower-limb amputees worldwide [1, 2], with approximately 86% of these individuals fitted with socket suspended prostheses [3]. While socket prostheses are currently considered the gold standard in prosthetic care, they have a fairly high abandonment rate of 25–27% [2, 3], leaving roughly 7.7 million people unable to use their prosthesis effectively. This high rate of abandonment is largely due to the shortcomings of socket prostheses such as tissue irritation, poor socket fit, and mobility restrictions from socket use [4–7].

For individuals who are unable to use socket prostheses, bone-anchored prostheses (BAPs) offer a promising alternative. However, these individuals must first undergo clinical screening to determine their suitability. Factors such as bone quality, residual limb length, overall health status, and risk of infection are carefully assessed before proceeding [6, 8–11]. For those deemed suitable, BAPs can address many of the limitations of traditional socket prosthesis by bypassing soft tissue and directly anchoring the prosthetic limb to the skeleton via a metal implant, also known as an osseointegrated implant [4, 6, 12]. These osseointegrated implants are surgically attached to the residual limb via a threaded [4, 13] or press-fit interface [5, 14, 15]. While the attachment interface differs between threaded and press-fit implants, they serve the same purpose of providing a stable anchor point for the external prosthetic limb [2, 6, 9, 11].

Compared to socket prostheses, BAPs have greater prosthesis stability [16, 17], improved limb control [18], and better sensory feedback through osseoperception – the ability to sense tactile stimuli through a metal implant [19]. With proper prosthetic component selection – such as the knee joint for gait modulation and foot for shock absorption [20, 21], BAP users have improved confidence [22], longer prosthesis use time, and overall improved patient outcomes compared to those using socket prostheses [22–26]. As a result, the adoption of BAPs for lower-limb amputees has been rising, with an estimated 10,000 users since its clinical introduction [27].

Although there are many advantages of using BAPs compared to socket prostheses, one of the concerns relating to BAPs is the high risk of femoral fractures. Compared to socket prosthesis users that have a fracture risk of 2–3% within the first 5 years [28], BAP users have a much higher risk of fracture at 6.3% within 4 years post-implantation according to a large retrospective study [28]. Separate studies have also reported various rates of fractures for press-fit implants (0–8%) [29–32] and for threaded implants (0–5.6%) [4, 29, 33].

To determine how these fractures occur, multiple studies [34–42] have investigated the stresses within implanted femurs under load. However, most of these studies have only focused on loads generated during level walking. No study has investigated the fracture risk of implanted femurs under more demanding but low-impact activities of daily living such as ambulating stairs and inclines. Evaluating the fracture risk of these implanted femurs during these more demanding, low-impact activities may provide valuable information in establishing a working baseline for future studies and broaden the functional capabilities of these BAP users.

To evaluate the fracture risk of these more demanding, low impact activities, finite element (FE) modelling can be used. FE models are commonly employed to study the relationship between bones and orthopedic implants [34, 43–48] because they provide an alternative to in-vivo data collection methods that can be challenging or impossible. However, current FE models of femurs fitted with osseointegrated implants often utilize material properties that are non-subject-specific [34, 49] or have not been validated with experimental data [35, 37–39, 41, 50], which raises concerns around the validity of the data produced by these FE models. Once these FE models have been validated, the fracture risk of these implanted femurs during these low-impact activities of daily living can then be quantified using factors of safety (FOS), where the FOS is calculated as a ratio of the magnitude of the fracture load predicted against the magnitude of the load applied.

Therefore, the aim of this study was two-fold: (1) to experimentally validate a methodology for creating specimen-specific FE models of femurs fitted with osseointegrated implants, and (2) to use the validated models to evaluate the fracture risk of the implanted femurs during activities of daily living, where FOS is used to quantify the fracture risk.

Methods

A total of four fresh-frozen left femurs (Male, 71.3 ± 8.38 years) were harvested from human cadavers with no reported musculoskeletal disorders. This study had ethical approval through the University of Melbourne Ethics Approval Committee (22159).

A pilot study was first conducted using specimen one. Based on initial observations, minor adjustments to the experimental setup and post-operative scanning methods were later made for specimens two to four to improve the experimental workflow. The main text describes the finalized methods applied for specimens two to four, while deviations in the methodologies specific to specimen one are detailed in the Supplementary Materials.

Specimen preparation

All specimens along with a calibration phantom (model M091, CIRS Inc, Norfolk, USA) underwent pre-operative computed tomography (CT) scans (Siemens Biograph Vision 600, Siemens, Munich, Germany) at a voxel size of 0.3 mm × 0.3 mm × 0.5 mm. The resulting CT data were segmented (Mimics software v21.0, Leuven, Belgium) to generate 3D geometric models of each femur. The size of the press-fit osseointegrated implant (Osseointegration Prosthetic Limb OPL, Osseointegration International Pty Ltd., NSW, Australia) used in this study and associated femoral resection length (Table 1) were determined using the CT scans following manufacturer recommendations and published surgical guidelines [18]. Specimens were thawed to room temperature and implanted by an orthopedic surgeon (KS) or through consultation with the orthopedic surgeon or manufacturer. After implantation, the surface of the specimens was cleaned of soft tissue and degreased to allow for strain rosette attachment following a standardized procedure [51]. Eight triaxial strain rosettes (N32-FA-1-120-11-VS3, Showa Sokki Corp, Japan; FRAB-1-11-1LJB-F, Tokyo Measuring Instruments Laboratory Co. Ltd., Japan) were attached to the bone surface at predetermined locations: the greater trochanter (anterior, medial, posterior, and lateral aspects), femoral neck (anterior and posterior aspects), and distal femoral shaft (anterior and posterior aspects) (Fig. 1a). Experimental setup and data collection

The implanted femur was clamped rigidly at the distal end of the implant in a custom fixture with a rectangular aluminum base plate (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Material S1 – Figure S1). The coordinate system of the femur was defined as follows: The origin was at the center of the distal end of the implant (Fig. 1); the anterior–posterior (AP) axis aligned with the anterior notch on the distal implant; the superior-inferior (SI) axis aligned with the longitudinal axis of the distal implant; and the medial–lateral (ML) axis was perpendicular to the SI and AP axes. The custom fixture ensured the femur axes and the edges of the aluminum base plate were parallel to each other (Fig. 1bi). The implanted femur-fixture assembly was then mounted on a uniaxial test machine ensuring the edges of the aluminum base plate were parallel to the base of the test machine (Instron 8800, Instron, Massachusetts, USA).

Each specimen was subjected to 12 different target loads (Table 2). These target loads comprised six loading conditions, with two loading magnitudes each. One of the loading conditions was purely vertical while the other five represented loading conditions consistent with peak loads that occur during common activities of daily living: level walking, stair ascent, stair descent, incline ascent, and incline descent. The directions and magnitudes of loading during these activities were derived from measurements made by Frossard et al. [52] who attached a load transducer at the distal end of a bone-anchored implant of a 96 kg transfemoral amputee. The load magnitudes were first scaled to an assumed donor bodyweight (BW) of 75 kg for all specimens. Then, to reduce the risk of bone fracture during testing, the scaled loads were further reduced to 50% (LOAD50) and 75% (LOAD75). The purely vertical loads were 50% and 75% of body weight (75 kg). Since the test machine could apply only vertical loads, we designed five custom wedges that enabled the application of loading in the required directions (Fig. 1, Supplementary Material S1 – Figure S1, Supplementary Material S2 – Figure S2). The wedges were attached to the actuator, rotated about the actuator axis, and the center of the femoral head was aligned to the actuator’s loading axis before loading was finally applied onto the femoral head. Each bone was first tested in the six directions with LOAD50 and then with LOAD75. Petroleum jelly was applied on the wedges to lubricate the contact surfaces and allow for sliding during loading to ensure contact loads were nearly perpendicular to the wedge surface. The loading protocol started off by gradually increasing the load on the femoral head to a 100 N vertical pre-load over a 10 s period to settle the specimen into the fixture [34]. The pre-load was held for 10 s and then gradually increased over another 10 s period to one of the 12 target loads which was then held for 15 s. Strain rosette data was recorded at a frequency of 25 kHz for the total duration of femoral loading. First, data was recorded for the four strain rosettes near the trochanteric region. The bone was then left unloaded for 10 min to allow for bone strain relaxation [34]. The experiment was then repeated while collecting data for the remaining four strain rosettes (femoral neck rosettes and distal femoral rosettes). Maximum and minimum surface principal strains during peak load, averaged over the last eight seconds of peak loading, were calculated.

Finite element modelling

3D geometric models of the fully intact femurs were first created using the pre-operative CT images. Post-operative models of the implanted femurs were then created using a 3D scanner (Artec Space Spider, Artec 3D, Luxembourg) (Supplementary Material S3) and registered to the pre-operative models using a commercial software package (Geomagic Wrap 2021, 3D Systems, North Carolina, USA). Virtual resection of the intact femur models and implant positioning were performed and locations of the strain rosettes on the bone were registered.

Both femur and implant models were meshed with first-order tetrahedral elements (Hypermesh, v2021.2, Altair Engineering, MI, USA), which resulted in an average of 1,010,347 elements per implanted femur model and an average element size of 1.32 mm across all four FE models (Table 1). Finally, reaction forces measured by the six-axis load cell were applied to a set of nodes on the femoral head ensuring the resultant force passed through the femoral head center (Fig. 2). A total of 240 FE simulations (12 loading conditions × 4 femur models × 5 density-elasticity relationships) were performed.

Finite element vs experimental strain comparison

From the FE simulations, strain tensors at the locations of each of the strain rosettes were extracted and used to calculate the principal strains on the bone surface for each specimen and each loading condition. The maximum and minimum principal strain predicted by the FE analysis and the corresponding experimental measurements were fitted to a linear regression model. For each specimen, five linear regression models were generated – one for each density-elasticity relationship. The coefficient of determination (R2), slope (m), and intercept (c) of each regression model, and the root mean square error (RMSE) of the regression models were calculated. Bland–Altman plots were also created for each regression model to assess agreement between FE and experimental measurements [59].

To assess the net accuracy of each density-elasticity relationship, the regression analyses were repeated using the FE predicted and experimentally measured strains pooled across all four specimens. The regression analysis metrics (R2, m, c, RMSE) were calculated and corresponding Bland–Altman plots were generated.

Fracture risk evaluation

To evaluate the fracture risk of the implanted femurs during activities of daily living, we calculated the FOS of implanted femurs at peak loads occurring during level walking, stair ascent, stair descent, incline ascent and incline descent. The methods used for calculating the FOS have previously been published [47] and are described here in brief.

FE models of implanted femurs were created using the methodology described in the experimental validation section. Femoral elements were assigned heterogenous material properties using the density-elasticity relationship that yielded the lowest RMSE from the validation study. Peak forces and moments acting at the knee during the five activities of daily living were estimated (Table 3) from published data [52] and applied at the knee center in the FE model (Fig. 3). To ensure no moments were transmitted through the femoral head, distributed moments were applied to the surface nodes of the trochanteric region. Discussion

The objectives of this study were to validate a FE methodology of femurs fitted with osseointegrated implants and evaluate the fracture risk of the implanted femurs during various activities of daily living. Results from this study revealed that the FE models using Eq. (3) best predicted the experimentally measured strain responses of the implanted femurs under load with the highest R2 (0.91) and lowest RMSE (81.27 με). Results also revealed that the FOS of these implanted femurs during the five activities of daily living investigated ranged from 1.8 to 5.0, with incline ascent recording the lowest average FOS (1.9), thereby posing the highest fracture risk.

Initially, FE models using Eq. (1) best predicted the experimentally measured strain responses of the implanted femurs when all four specimen data were pooled together with the highest R2 (0.84) and lowest RMSE (134.19 με) values out of the five density-elasticity relationships investigated. However, analysis of individual specimen data showed that specimen four behaved inconsistently compared to the other specimens, with regression slopes for specimen four being approximately twice as low as the regression slopes for specimens one to three for the same density-elasticity relationships (Supplementary Material S4 – Figure S3 to Figure S6, Table S1 to Table S4). Further investigation revealed that specimen four recorded notably larger strain values (−1600 to 1,000 με) (Supplementary Material S4 – Figure S6) in the loading experiments compared to the other three specimens (−1000 to 600 με) (Supplementary Material S4 – Figure S3 to Figure S5) under the same loading conditions. These large strain values were also inconsistent with the experimentally measured strain responses reported in a similar study of comparable load magnitude [34] (−600 to 500 με). Initial visual inspection of specimen four revealed that the specimen appeared discolored (Supplementary Material S5 – Figure S7), but no visible deformities on the surface of the specimen nor physical abnormalities in the CT images were observed, hence, the initial inclusion of the specimen in the study. However, subsequent investigations revealed that the donor was diagnosed with end-stage renal failure and had been undergoing dialysis before death, both conditions which may have affected the quality of the bone [61–63]. Additionally, the FE model of specimen four showed a noticeably lower percentage of high-density finite elements compared to specimens one to three (Supplementary Material S5 – Figure S8). Taken together, the inconsistency in strain measurements, visible discoloration, and past medical history suggests that specimen four likely had compromised bone quality and was, therefore, excluded from further analysis. Exclusion of specimen four ultimately resulted in Eq. (3) as the best performing density-elasticity relationship. The improved R2 of 0.91 (from 0.84) and reduced RMSE of 81.27 με (from 134.19 με) were also more comparable to other validation studies of intact [54, 64–67] and implanted femurs [34, 68].

Fracture risk evaluation in this study suggests that transfemoral amputees fitted with osseointegrated implants can safely perform level walking, stair ascent, stair descent, incline ascent, and incline descent with a comfortable FOS of 1.8–5.0 (Fig. 6). Although ambulating on stairs is a more demanding activity compared to level walking, there was no discernible difference in the average FOS (4.0 for stairs vs 4.3 for level walking), indicating similar levels of fracture risk. Ambulating on inclines, however, had a much lower average FOS (2.2) – approximately 45% lower – than level walking, suggesting higher fracture risk. This could be due to the higher anterior forces generated during incline ambulation compared to level walking and stair ambulation (Table 3). In our previous study [47], we found that the implanted femur was predicted to fracture at forces approximately six times lower in the AP and ML axes compared to forces in the SI axis. This finding suggests that activities that have high AP and ML forces will bear a higher fracture risk than those with low AP and ML forces. Nonetheless, the FOS of these activities were still within a comfortable safety margin and can be safely performed. However, the same cannot be said for high impact activities. Jogging and running both generate about three to five times BW on the hip [45, 69], while jumping can reach up to seven times BW [70]. While these high impact activities were not explicitly investigated, loads of three to seven times BW – according to our model – would be sufficient to cause fracture in the implanted femurs. Hence, high impact activities are advised against for these BAP users.

Direct comparison of results in the current study to the literature is difficult as this is the first study, to the authors’ knowledge, that evaluated the fracture risk of transfemoral amputees with bone-anchored prosthetic limbs during activities of daily living using a fracture criterion. However, comparison of peak femoral stresses in this study to literature could provide some confidence in the results. In a previous study [37], peak femoral stress of a transfemoral amputee fitted with a BAP during level treadmill walking was found to be around 40 MPa. Peak femoral stresses during level walking in this study ranged from 22 to 38 MPa (Supplementary Material S6 – Figure S9). While the peak femoral stresses in this study were lower than the stresses reported in [37], they remain in comparable range. The use of personalized muscle force vectors, differences in bone material properties, and implant used likely account for these differences.

One of the limitations of this study is that only bone specimens from older male (71.3 ± 8.38 years) donors were investigated. While only data for male specimens were available, the results in the validation section of the study are expected to remain valid. Given that the bone material properties in the FE models were derived from HU values in the CT scans, the FE models should be able to capture demographic variations between male and female bone specimens through the changes in HU. However, since females generally have lower bone mineral densities than males [71, 72], the absolute FOS values in the fracture risk evaluation section of this study should be interpreted with caution in clinical contexts, where additional analyses of safety margins should be considered for female patients. Future work should include samples from a broader demographic range to ensure the generalizability of the results. An incidental observation during the study was that all female specimens, along with some male specimens, fractured during implantation and could not be carried forward for testing. The reasons for this outcome remain uncertain, though implant oversizing may have contributed. While not the primary focus of this study, this observation highlights an area that could be explored further in future work.

Another limitation of this study is the use of a single implant type (press-fit) with a fixed length and corresponding femoral resection length. While this provided consistency across all simulations and experiments, it inherently limited the generalizability of the findings. Different implant designs and lengths may alter the mechanical behavior of the bone-implant construct and therefore influence fracture risk. As such, the results presented here should be interpreted as specific to the implant configuration used in this study. Future work could expand on this by exploring a wider range of implant geometries and resection lengths to improve the applicability of the findings to a broader population.

Lastly, the FE models used a tie constraint to model the interaction between the femur and implant which rigidly bonded these components instead of a contact formulation [34, 40, 41, 73] that allow for micromotions in both the experimental validation and fracture risk analysis sections of the study. In reality, micromotions exist at the bone-implant interface and this can influence local stress distributions and subsequently fracture risk. However, while a tie constraint is limited in representing the boundary conditions at the bone-implant interface, it is consistent with baseline or initial validation studies with press-fit implants [46, 74, 75]. Additionally, the use of a tie constraint was crucial to reduce model complexity, and the computational time needed to run all 240 FE validation simulations. In the fracture risk analysis, the tie constraint represented a fully osseointegrated condition, consistent with long term clinical scenarios. Given that this study was intended to establish an initial benchmark for assessing fracture risk during low-impact daily activities in BAP users, this modelling approach was also considered a reasonable first step. Nonetheless, the choice of interface formulation is a critical factor that could influence stress predictions, and its impact should be explored in future work.

In conclusion, this study presented a validated FE methodology for femurs fitted with osseointegrated implants. The models also predicted that the implanted male bones are likely to transmit peak loads occurring in routine low-impact activities of daily living with a low risk of fracture. However, caution should be taken to avoid high impact activities such as running and jumping as these activities could exceed the load bearing capacity of the implanted femur. Future studies should focus on expanding the demographic range to further refine the outcomes of this study and exploring more femur-implant combinations.
