Exam reform: an opportunity for the redistribution of academic power
Authors: Gregory J. Crowther, Benjamin L. Wiggins
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStudents in STEM know well the stress, challenge, and effort that accompany college exams. As a widely recognizable feature of the STEM classroom experience, high-stakes assessments serve as crucial cultural gateways in shaping both preparation and motivation for careers. In this essay, we identify and discuss issues of power around STEM exams to further the understanding of exam practices that can unjustly hold students back. Through theory and practical examples, we consider the numerous ways in which power manifests both on and off the pages of the exams themselves, as well as ways in which power is consolidated away from students through logistical norms and tradeoffs. Centering the “rules of the culture of power” as delineated by Dr. Lisa Delpit, we reflect on exam practices that prioritize faculty voice and faculty convenience above student learning and student identity. We share some of what we have learned from our students as part of a call to improve STEM education by relinquishing some of our exam-related power over students, redistributing it to students so that they have more power to shape their own education.

PERSPECTIVE

Within the broad realm of equity, inclusion, access, and justice in biology education, one area where the progress has been slow and the challenges formidable is that of assessment design. Although discussions of justice-oriented teaching do not usually center around assessment, the impacts of assessments on students’ identities, mindsets, learning, and future career prospects are profound.

To better understand how assessment affects equity, inclusion, access, and justice, we access frameworks that go beyond assessment reliability and interpretability and speak to students’ experiences as learners of biology. Various overlapping frameworks have been developed for understanding and addressing inequitable access to opportunities in STEM education (1). These frameworks have different emphases, for example, systemic barriers that reduce marginalized students’ persistence in STEM (2, 3) and the assets of marginalized students and their communities (4, 5). Our goal in this essay is to combine what we have seen within and across college STEM classrooms with a framework specifically designed to guide a new understanding and positive change.

Along with the above-mentioned lenses on educational equity and justice, we have especially benefited from another (overlapping) lens: the lens of academic power, especially as elaborated by education scholar Lisa Delpit. In a seminal 1988 essay (6) subsequently reprinted in multiple books (7–9), Delpit proposed that what she called “the culture of power” could be understood in terms of five fundamental rules (Table 1). In considering this lens of power, we have felt especially challenged and inspired by Delpit’s fifth and final rule: “Those with power are frequently least aware of—or least willing to acknowledge—its existence.”

This type of acknowledgment is, we think, an especially salient form of acknowledging privilege. As able-bodied white cis-gender male college professors from college-educated families, we acknowledge our privileged position along multiple axes. But what if we go a step further and explicitly acknowledge our power? Admitting our power is tantamount to admitting that we have significant leverage over our students, which leads to further admissions that despite our best intentions, we harm students at times (17–19) and that we have a moral responsibility to try to do better in the future. Even a lack of control over teaching loads, class sizes, and STEM major progress requirements does not entirely excuse us from mitigating power imbalances within our courses. Thus, for the two of us, considering power per se elevates an acknowledgment of privilege to a more urgent self-call-to-action.

THE POWER OF EXAMS

All learning, even in “objective” STEM fields like biology, occurs in social and cultural contexts that strongly impact valuations of different kinds of knowledge and judgments of conduct as appropriate or not. This reality is nicely encapsulated in, for example, Bell et al.’s concept of cultural learning pathways (20) and Bang and Medin’s discussion of community-based science education (21), but is also evident in older work such as that of Delpit (6). Here, we prioritize the framework of Delpit’s rules in part because its explicit focus on power resonates strongly with us, perhaps because Delpit derived her rules from classroom situations that feel familiar to us, despite her original pedagogical context (primary school reading) being different from ours (undergraduate biology). Beyond these rules’ personal appeal to us, they have proven timely and relevant for a wide range of educational environments, as implied by the original paper’s accumulation of over 7,000 citations. In addition, we note that Delpit’s rule #4 is essentially the complement of others’ observation that secrecy can be a vehicle for consolidating power (22) and thus for maintaining discriminatory practices (23, 24).

As explained by Delpit’s first rule (Table 1), there are many forms of academic power. For the remainder of this essay, we will focus on academic exams (and the people who create and grade them) as structures that consolidate power in the hands of instructors, both via their direct impact on grades and via upstream anticipation of and downstream effects of these grades.

By a common definition of power as “the capacity to change outcomes,” exams are hugely impactful on students’ lives. Exam scores go a long way both toward determining pathways that are open to students (especially in an era of institutional pre-acceptance and rapid decisions about majors), and toward convincing students whether or not they should identify as scientists (25). Another useful definition of power is “asymmetric control over valued resources in social relations” (22). By this definition, grade assigners and exam creators certainly have more control than their students over the valued resource of access to future education and careers via grades. Finally, the SAGE Handbook of Power (26) helpfully distinguishes between power to and power over. In this vein, traditional exams manifest instructors’ power to create value in particular actions/documents as well as their power over students by being a primary determiner of grades.

Understandably, high-stakes exams’ enormous power over students makes some (perhaps many) STEM instructors uncomfortable. This discomfort has provided fuel for overlapping movements often referred to as “alternative grading” or “ungrading” (27–30). Members of these movements generally aim to make exams fairer, reduce their emphasis, and/or replace them with other forms of evaluation, for example, project-based assessment (30).

Realistically, many instructors lack the power to stop giving exams, and departmental and/or institutional expectations may further constrain them to give exams that appear similar to those given by predecessors and colleagues. For many instructors we have worked with, this perceived lack of power is logistical; large teaching loads constrain instructors to prior methods. Since instructors have significant but not unlimited power to structure their assessments, we continue below with additional real-world examples of the numerous, sometimes-subtle ways in which exam-style assessments may reflect and perpetuate power imbalances.

EXAMPLES OF EXAM PRACTICES THAT ILLUSTRATE POWER ISSUES

While exams are indisputably powerful forces, students may experience this power in vastly different ways, depending on how their exam experiences are structured. To highlight some of the ways in which structural features of assessment can leverage power, we offer the examples below. Within our roles as instructors, support staff, evaluators, and professional developers, we have observed the key features of these scenarios in recent classrooms and present here a fictionalized-but-realistic collage of those features as fodder for discussion.

Imagine that three students in different learning environments—Students X, Y, and Z—will on Friday take a 100-point exam that includes the following questions:

Q1 (4 points): Why would a patient with Type 2 diabetes mellitus be unlikely to respond well to injections of insulin? (Circle the single correct answer.)

A1: Because Type 2 diabetics already produce enough insulin, the addition of more insulin does not restore glucose receptor function [This answer is correct.]

A2: Because Type 2 diabetics already produce enough insulin, they can only be cured with treatments that target the liver.

A3: Because Type 2 diabetics do not produce enough insulin, the addition of more insulin does not restore glucose receptor function.

A4: Because Type 2 diabetics do not produce enough insulin, they can only be cured with treatments that target the liver.

Q2 (8 points): Draw a diagram that shows your understanding of the hormonal control of glucose receptor concentration in a well-functioning human body. Label, with short phrases or sentences, the key aspects of the system.

Student X

On the Monday before the Friday exam, Student X learns about Type 1 and 2 diabetes mellitus in an active teaching style typified by in-class discussion and audience-response devices. They are told that on Friday they will have an exam that accounts for 25% of their final grade, and are reminded that they need 75% of class points to continue with courses in their major. Student X has heard their instructor’s side comment during lecture indicating a belief that exam scores are key predictors of success in graduate school. X and their peers are encouraged to take detailed notes in class to share with colleagues for co-studying. Very soon after the exam, Student X receives their question-by-question points and is able to ask questions in office hours if they did not understand the grading.

Student Y

On Monday, Student Y learns the same concepts about diabetes mellitus in an active teaching style typified by guided worksheets and jigsaw exercises. Student Y has also read in the syllabus that students may request regrades of exam questions in writing, but with a limit of one question per exam. On Tuesday night, they are given online access to a study guide with an outline of topics covered, approximate point values per topic for the exam, brief explanations of core concepts, and learning objectives that include coverage of these concepts. Student Y hears repeated verbal encouragement to study creatively and try new studying techniques like peer teaching. A day or two after the exam, the instructor posts anonymized examples of diagrams, with examples of high-, middle-, and low-scoring explanations.

Student Z

On Monday, Student Z learns the same concepts in preparation for the same Friday exam. This class is taught as a traditional lecture for the first 80% of the class session, and then for the rest of class time students work in groups on a data interpretation task to reinforce these concepts. After class, students have access to examples of exam questions and key answers about Type 1 and 2 diabetes mellitus, with the understanding that the Friday exam will use a very similar style and format to these examples but with different details. Students are reminded that peer teaching is an effective way to practice gapless explanations and identify gaps in understanding, and the instructor shares an anecdote about learning from a peer during a particularly difficult undergraduate course. Student Z has come to believe that their instructor sees exams as part of a learning process in which post-assessment reflection is praised and students are encouraged to explain their reasoning. In keeping with this belief, the instructor spends several minutes of next Monday’s class time discussing particular well- or poorly-answered questions on the exam and listens to verbal feedback from students before finalizing the grading rubric and returning grades several days later.

Each aspect of these three students’ experiences is taken directly from our own observations of college science classrooms, and none is clearly superior in all aspects. These multifaceted scenarios are simplifications of reality, especially in their lack of information on the cultural identities or backgrounds of the participants. If you read these examples and came away frustrated at the potential complexity of the experiences, then you can appreciate the web of conflicting motivations that students parse in each of their courses. From the perspective of a researcher analyzing the text of the assessment, all three students might be assumed to have had very similar assessment experiences, but that assumption would likely be wrong! To truly understand student challenges and opportunities, we need to consider sociocultural issues beyond the assessing instrument itself. Below we discuss the widely different potential impacts of power on these students and the ways in which their instructors structured each experience.

Instructors reading to this point may be dismayed that even these everyday, relatable examples of assessment seem rife with problematic power issues. Since we do not intend our assessments to be so fraught, we might ask how these power issues arose despite our good intentions. Delpit’s rule #3 gives us an answer: “The rules of the culture of power are a reflection of the rules of the culture of those who have power.” As professionals who largely succeeded in systems that historically rewarded individual learning, written academic English proficiency, and simplified personal relationships, we instructors tend to write exams that reflect those norms whether or not we believe they are best for science and for scientists-in-training. For example, Students X, Y, and Z are all being directed to address Q1 and Q2 instead of engaging in the authentic scientific process of generating their own questions. Asking questions, while widely regarded as a necessary career skill in STEM, is presented in these examples as a practice reserved only for the powerful. This likely reflects the education histories of the three instructors, but may not reflect the skills that those instructors most want to foster in their students.

Are there opportunities for students to succeed within these power structures? Delpit’s guidance (via rule #4) seems clear: “If you are not already a participant in the culture of power, being told explicitly the rules of that culture makes acquiring power easier.” The ways in which we make assessment information, rules, and norms transparent for students are key to their ability to succeed in multiple cultural contexts. Anecdotally, we frequently see exam-givers assume that their own perceived norms will inform their students how the complex interactions of exams should occur. Imagine if Student Y were confused when encountering Q2 above. Should they guess at a possible correct answer, or express their uncertainty in their diagram? Should they write short notes to the grader in a personal voice, or keep to an essay style? Should they assume that the evaluator has a key, or is learning the material from them as they would speak to a peer? Should they define keywords, or let them stand on their own? We have frequently conversed with instructors who work with the same populations of students but give different answers to each of these questions, each believing their answers to be norms that should have been obvious to students from prerequisite coursework.

Throughout these examples and across STEM, secrecy is a norm often accepted as logistically necessary for testing (34), with many instructors opposed to “teaching to the test” (35) and/or unwilling to answer the perennial student question of “Will this be on the test?” (36). This secrecy, while defensible in certain contexts, makes it harder to create positive, trusting relationships between instructors and students. Perhaps even more worrisome is the fact that secrecy also masks the codes of power that students, especially those crossing cultures into academic institutions, most need to acquire power.

This brings us to Delpit’s fifth rule, which may be the most difficult for instructors to internalize within the constraints of a complex, time-consuming profession. Delpit reminds us, “Those with power are frequently least aware of—or least willing to acknowledge—its existence.” Did Student X’s instructor intend for their multiple-choice exam to use secrecy to consolidate power over student studying? Did Student Y’s professor push students into peer teaching in the hope that students with social anxiety would suffer? Was Student Z’s studying experience designed to be oppressive? No, of course not—and yet powerful, oppressive structures can and do emerge without any nefarious intent. It is our responsibility to perceive, acknowledge, and when possible dismantle them.

Dismantling power structures means accepting logistical tradeoffs. Student X’s institution may need to decrease class sizes or hire more TAs to enable the grading of more free-response answers. Student Y’s instructor may need to spend more hours generating examples of unrelated diagram questions with keys for reference on the general scientific skill of drawing diagrams. Similarly, Student Z’s instructor might be persuaded to grade in more time-consuming ways that accept multiple ways of building diagrams based on a variety of mental models. Since these tradeoffs entail allocations of additional money and time, there will be pressure on faculty and institutions to be “efficient” in their use of resources. We caution, however, that a perceived need for efficiency is not in and of itself an adequate reason for unnecessary power-hoarding.

GENERAL SUGGESTIONS FOR REDISTRIBUTING EXAM POWER

While instructors have significant power to build better assessments, and a moral responsibility to do so, the structural improvements that are most appropriate for dismantling consolidated power will be different in every setting. With that said, we see at least three general, broadly useful mechanisms for redistributing power.

First, we can aim to decrease assessments’ secrecy, since secrecy is antithetical to empowerment in that it collects power in the existing structure and reduces agency for others (22). The previous section explained how Students X, Y, and Z could be informed of the rules of their exam culture, thus decreasing secrecy.

Second, we can aim to increase assessments’ authenticity, that is, their coverage of knowledge and skills likely to increase students’ power in their pursuit of desired careers. Exam questions that ask students to take on career-relevant tasks (like editing AI-generated descriptions of concepts or writing abstracts of detailed findings) give students a window into potential future careers they can identify with.

To further illustrate how broad power-redistribution goals relate to instructors’ specific assessment design choices, we offer Table 2. Since instructors’ time and resources are limited, the power-consolidating options listed are not necessarily “wrong,” nor are the power-redistributing options necessarily “right,” for a particular instructional context. Nevertheless, careful planning can enable concerted changes in which several power-consolidating options are simultaneously converted to power-redistributing ones. In our own work, we have created exam frameworks known respectively as Public Exams (14) and Test Question Templates (15), which share a common strategy of pre-releasing selected exam materials to students well in advance of the exam day. These pre-released materials offer extensive advance guidance on the scope and format of exam questions (issue A of Table 2) while preserving just enough uncertainty to prevent students from formulating and pre-memorizing their exam answers in advance. In addressing issue A well before the test, we also effectively address concerns such as issue B (connections to learning activities, which can be seen via comparison to the pre-released exam materials), issue C (cultural assumptions, which surface in the pre-released exam materials), and issue D (time available to review lecture materials prior to assessment, which is extensive in our pre-release models). Progress on these fronts may yield synergistic benefits for students. For example, when we provide ample time and space to analyze data with classmates before exams (issues A and D), and ample time and space for meaningful post-exam follow-up (issues F–H), we find that our students suffer less from exam-related anxiety, which disproportionately challenges students from backgrounds historically served poorly by academia (39, 40). And while it is difficult to imagine exams that are entirely free of cultural assumptions, stating those assumptions up front (issue C) and encouraging students to explain content in their own words (issue E) should be less hostile to students’ identities than the alternatives would be. We offer these suggestions in hopes of motivating others to find new ways to give back power over students and redistribute power to understand and power to make change back to them.

LIMITATIONS OF EXAM-CENTRIC REFORMS

This essay is limited in part by its focus on the power of individual instructors to adopt more equitable exam practices within their own classrooms, without necessarily perturbing the larger systems in which those classrooms reside. Is it naive, or even irresponsible, to concern ourselves first with helping students navigate unjust systems, rather than with reforming the systems themselves?

This important issue is taken up by Colina Neri et al. (44) in a recent reconsideration of Delpit’s essay (6), which they quote as follows (p. 292):

I prefer to be honest with my students. Tell them that their language and cultural style is unique and wonderful but that there is a political power game that is also being played, and if they want to be in on that game there are certain games that they too must play.

Colina Neri et al. then comment (p. 1516):

We do not take the “if” in [Delpit’s] statement in an off-handed way. As a matter of justice, it is vital that situated communities, with collective agency, determine if—and if so, how—they might “want to be in on that game.” (This is all the more so in the agency-invigorating context of Black Lives Matter mobilizations that challenge domination by White and upper-class norms). Yet we also do not make light of how agency among nondominant groups, and among teachers working with them in schools, encounters systemic devices of sorting-and-selecting across multiple institutions—not just schools—where access and normative “success” are crucial for life-chance surviving, let alone thriving. This is “the power game” for which Delpit calls educators to take responsibility to redistribute access to its codes.

Colina Neri et al. thus speak of a “focal tension” in which a focus on teaching the current codes of power runs the risk of being overly assimilationist, i.e., of legitimizing and perpetuating existing power inequities. We aim to remain cognizant of this risk so that, in trying to mitigate local (within-classroom) inequities, we keep one eye on more global (across- and beyond-classroom) inequities. In addition, we call on the supervisors of STEM instructors to regard changes to assessment power structures as impactful innovations that, like research publications and active-learning innovations, deserve to be recognized and rewarded.

CONCLUSION

For the immediate future, the vital instructional goal of supporting all biology students is likely to remain in tension with academic biology’s roots in White Eurocentric values and practices, which include high-stakes exams. Instructors have power over students, and power to create structures that bring students into greater control. We cannot run from that power. Even instructional faculty who rightly feel that they lack agency to impact larger structures (45, 46) should remember that they have great power relative to our students, and thus a responsibility to hold that power with care. Instructors who try to improve the equity of their classrooms must attend to power relationships around assessment or risk alienating students from diverse ways of knowing, regardless of the quality of their other direct, student-facing methods. We can minimize exams’ harm and maximize their usefulness to student persistence with practices that explicitly connect exams to goals, clarify formats and assumptions, and help students prepare accordingly. While details of exam practices might be dismissed as logistical minutiae rather than engines of justice, lurking within those details are valuable opportunities to redistribute power to students.
