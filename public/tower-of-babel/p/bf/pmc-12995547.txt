Comparative study of dental age estimation methods against known chronological age in Nigerian local horses
Authors: Olumide Odunayo AKINNIYI, Dorcas Oluwadara AFOLABI, Philip Wayuta MSHELIA, Tolulope Ademola OLAKOJO, Iskiil Oladehinde OYENEKAN, Osereime ADAH, Dideolu OSUNKOYA, Sunday Charles OLAOGUN, Olalekan Taiwo JEREMIAH, Temidayo Olutayo OMOBOWALE
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dental age estimation in horses is very important in the context of quality healthcare, breeding programs, and welfare management. However, information on the most suitable dental age estimation methods for Nigerian local horses is not available in the literature. We evaluated and compared dental age estimates based on six dental age estimation methods with known chronological ages of Nigerian local horses to establish the most accurate techniques for this population. A cross-sectional study of 180 Nigerian local horses was conducted in Ibadan, Nigeria. Six dental age estimation methods (eruption patterns, grinding surface, cups, dental stars, angle of incidence, and Galvayne’s groove) were applied by three blinded veterinarians to independent groups of 30 horses each. Data analyses included t-tests, Pearson correlation coefficients, and Bland-Altman plots. None of the estimated ages based on the estimation methods showed significant differences from the chronological age (P>0.05). The angle of incidence method showed perfect alignment with chronological age (11.9 ± 5.4 years) and the highest correlation (r=0.988, P<0.001). The eruption pattern method showed the smallest variation in estimates (range of agreement: 2.3 years), while Galvayne’s groove exhibited the largest variation (range of agreement: 9.9 years). Strong correlations were also observed for the grinding surface shape and dental stars assessments (both r=0.955, P<0.001). The angle of incidence method showed the highest correlation with chronological age for Nigerian local horses (between 5 and 30 years, r=0.988), though it provides age estimates in broad categories rather than precise years. The eruption pattern method demonstrated the highest precision for younger horses (between 3 and 5 years), with the smallest range of agreement (2.3 years). Veterinarians and horse owners in Nigeria should prioritize these methods for reliable age determination, while exercising caution when using Galvayne’s groove method due to its higher variability.

Dental age estimation in horses is an ancient technique in equine veterinary medicine and management and has important implications for health care, breeding programs, and horse welfare [10]. There are different methods used in estimating the age of a horse. They include the following: eruption of temporary and permanent incisors, changes in the grinding surface of the incisors, angle of incidence between the upper and lower incisors, the development of Galvayne’s groove on the upper corner incisors, and identifying dental features such as the 7-year and 11-year hooks [2, 3]. Each of these methods gives useful indicators that, when combined, can offer a more accurate estimate of the age of a horse. These age estimation methods are useful to formulate diets, predict diseases, and make decisions about how to care for a horse over the course of a lifetime [4]. Accurate determination of age improves the wellbeing of horses, as it helps veterinarians and even horse owners to provide age-appropriate care and make decisions about a horse’s abilities and needs.

However, it is crucial to emphasize that dental age estimation methods, while valuable, should be considered supplementary tools rather than replacements for proper age documentation. Accurate record-keeping through birth certificates, microchip registration, veterinary records, and detailed management notebooks remains the gold standard for age determination. Dental estimation methods are most appropriately used when documented age information is unavailable or when verification of existing records is necessary.

While the morphology of cheek teeth can also provide indicators of age through patterns of eruption and wear, this study focuses specifically on incisor-based methods which are more readily accessible during routine examination and commonly used in field conditions. It is important to note that routine dental care practices, particularly floating (the dental procedure of filing sharp enamel points), can affect the appearance of grinding surfaces and potentially influence age estimation accuracy. Additionally, dental diseases such as equine odontoclastic tooth resorption and hypercementosis (EOTRH) can significantly alter dental morphology independent of age.

Although early research dating back to the 1990s by Richardson et al. [17] and Muylle et al. [11, 12] shed some light on the complexities of dental age estimation methods, more recent studies have continued to investigate this area, illustrating both the challenge and the need for breed-specific approaches. Paşca et al. [15] highlighted the need for non-biased methods to estimate horse age. They used Romanian horses to demonstrate how the change in the shape of the dental plate was a helpful marker, with specific shapes associated with different ages. They also referred to the dental star appearing at specific ages on certain teeth. However, it was observed that the Galvayne’s groove had high variability and low reliability, suggesting the need for extra caution with certain age estimation methods. Recent research has also shed new light on breed-specific differences in dental age estimation. Khazaeel et al. [6] performed a study on pure Arabian horses in Khuzestan, Iran. For different incisors, they found strong correlations between the actual age and clinical crown length. Importantly, they showed that there was a very strong correlation between estimated dental age and actual age in their study population, meaning that breed-specific approaches can provide results that are accurate. Łuszczyński et al. [9] estimated dental age in Icelandic horses to be equivalent to the real age in only about a third of cases. Their findings demonstrated that the ages of younger horses (0–2 years) could be estimated more accurately from the eruption and growth of deciduous incisors.

Although several studies have been carried out on dental age estimation, there still remains a dearth of data on dental age estimation methods specifically for Nigerian local horse breeds. These breeds, having evolved under unique environmental, nutritional, and genetic conditions, may exhibit distinctive dental characteristics that could significantly affect age estimation accuracy. This knowledge gap impacts not only horse management techniques but also breeding strategies and veterinary practices within Nigeria and other parts of the world. The lack of a comparative analysis between dental age estimation methods and established chronological ages for Nigerian local horses leads to doubts regarding the reliability and precision of existing age estimation methods for these breeds.

Hence, we aimed to evaluate and compare age estimates based on dental age estimation methods with the known chronological ages of Nigerian local horses. Establishing the most precise age estimation methods for Nigerian local horses will improve equine use, care, breeding, and commerce in the region.

Materials and Methods

Study area

The study was conducted in Ibadan, Oyo State, Nigeria, a region known for its significant population of horses (Fig. 1Fig. 1.Map of Nigeria showing Ibadan (adopted from Olanrewaju et al. [14]).). Geographically, Ibadan lies between latitudes 7°3′N and 9°12′N and longitudes 2°47′E and 4°23′E and is characterized by a tropical climate with distinct rainy (April–October) and dry (November–March) seasons. The choice of this location was based on the availability of well-documented horse populations, including privately owned stables and equestrian centres [1].

Map of Nigeria showing Ibadan (adopted from Olanrewaju et al. [14]).

Study animals and sample size

This study focused on two indigenous Nigerian horse breeds: the Sulebawa and Arewa. All horses were maintained under typical Nigerian management conditions. Most of the horses were kept in intensive systems, while some were kept in semi-intensive systems. Diets consisted primarily of grass hay and pasture grazing, which were supplemented with concentrates.

A total of 180 horses (80 Sulebawa and 100 Arewa) were selected to ensure 30 unique animals per dental age estimation method, with no overlap of individuals between methods. The study population consisted of 40 stallions and 140 mares. Horses were recruited from private stables, research institutes, and equestrian centres across Oyo State, Nigeria.

Each of the six dental age estimation techniques was applied to a distinct group of 30 horses within their biologically appropriate age ranges: eruption patterns, 3–5 years; changes in grinding surface shape, 5–30 years; changes in the cups on grinding surfaces, 6–11 years; changes in the stars on grinding surfaces, 8–30 years; angle of incidence, 5–30 years; and Galvayne’s groove, 10–30 years (30 horses each).

Study design and sampling method

This study employed a cross-sectional design that compared age estimates based on dental age estimation methods with the known chronological ages of the selected horses. A purposive sampling method was used to select Nigerian local horses with known ages from Oyo State. Within each age-appropriate range for the six dental aging methods, 30 horses were randomly selected. The sampling framework was designed such that each of the six dental age estimation methods was applied to a separate group of 30 horses.

Data collection

Ethical considerations

The study adhered to ethical standards outlined by the Animal Care and Use Research Ethics Committee (ACUREC) of the University of Ibadan (approval number: NHREC/UIACUREC/05/12/2022A). All oral examinations were conducted using gentle handling techniques following standard veterinary practice. No invasive or painful procedures were performed. Horses were examined under routine clinical conditions with their owners’ informed consent.

Chronological age

Chronological age was determined using verified documentary evidence of age (birth certificates, veterinary records, or purchase agreements). Chronological age was rounded to the nearest whole year: horses 6 months or more into their current year of age were rounded up to the next year, while those less than 6 months into their current year were rounded down to their current year. This age served as the reference standard against which the dental age estimation methods were compared.

Age estimation using dentition

To ensure objectivity and minimize bias in dental age estimation, a strict blinded assessment protocol was implemented. Three equine veterinarians, each with a minimum of five years’ clinical experience in equine dentistry, independently examined all horses. All oral examinations were performed without sedation using gentle restraint techniques. Crucially, examiners were kept unaware of each horse’s documented chronological age throughout the evaluation process, and no communication regarding age estimates was permitted between examiners during the assessment phase. Each veterinarian performed a complete oral examination following standardized protocols:

Eruption patterns: Horses with their permanent central incisors erupted were regarded as 3 years old, those with their permanent intermediate incisors erupted were regarded as 4 years old, and those with their permanent corner incisors erupted were regarded as 5 years old [10, 16].

Changes in grinding surface shape: Horses with oval-shaped grinding surfaces from side to side on all lower incisors were regarded as 5–9 years old. Horses with round grinding surfaces on the lower incisors were regarded as around 10–14 years old. Horses with triangular-shaped grinding surfaces on all lower incisors were considered 15–19 years old. When the grinding surfaces became distinctly oval from front to back, the horses were estimated to be 20 years old or older [4].

Changes in the cups on grinding surfaces: Horses with cups disappearing from the lower central incisors were regarded as 6 years old. As the horse aged, the cups continued to disappear in a specific sequence. Those with cups disappearing from the lower intermediate incisors were considered 7 years old, while horses with cups disappearing from the lower corner incisors were estimated to be 8 years old. The process then continued to the upper incisors. When cups disappeared from the upper central incisors, horses were regarded as 9 years old. Disappearance of cups from the upper intermediate incisors indicated 10 years of age. Finally, horses with cups disappearing from the upper corner incisors were considered 11 years old. This progressive disappearance of cups provided a reliable indicator of age up to 11 years [4, 16].

Changes in the stars on grinding surfaces: Horses with dental stars first appearing as a faint line near the labial edge on the lower central incisors are generally regarded as 8 years old. As the horse ages, the stars continue to develop in a specific sequence. By 9 years old, dental stars become more visible on the lower central incisors and begin to appear on the lower intermediate incisors. Horses with visible stars on all lower incisors are typically considered 10 to 12 years old. When dental stars become more pronounced and begin to move towards the centre of the tooth surface on the lower incisors, horses are estimated to be around 15 years old. As the process continues, horses with round dental stars, positioned closer to the centre but not yet fully central on all lower incisors, are considered approximately 18 years old. Finally, horses with prominent, round dental stars positioned centrally on all lower incisors are estimated to be 20 years old or older [5].

Angle of incidence: The angle formed between the upper and lower incisors when the horse’s mouth is closed was measured using a protractor. Horses with an almost vertical angle of incidence (approximately 180 degrees) were considered to be 5–7 years old. Those with an angle of 160–170 degrees were estimated to be 10–12 years old, while horses showing an angle of 140–150 degrees were deemed 15–18 years old. Horses with an angle as acute as 90–100 degrees were regarded as 20 years or older [8].

Galvayne’s groove: Horses with the groove just appearing on the upper corner incisor were regarded as 10 years old, those with the groove extending halfway down the tooth were considered 15 years old, and those with the groove reaching the bottom of the tooth were deemed 20 years old. Horses showing signs of the groove disappearing from the top were estimated to be 25 years old, while those with no visible groove were considered to be 30 years or older [10].

Following data collection, inter-rater reliability was statistically evaluated using a two-way mixed-effects intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with absolute agreement. This analysis quantified the consistency of age estimates across all three examiners. In our study, the ICC values ranged from 0.82 to 0.91 across the different dental age estimation methods, indicating excellent inter-examiner agreement [7]. These high ICC values demonstrated that all three veterinarians were applying the dental age estimation criteria consistently and reliably.

Given the strong agreement between examiners (with no cases exceeding the ± 2-year discrepancy threshold), the final estimated age for each horse and each dental method was determined by calculating the arithmetic mean of the three independent estimates, which were rounded up to one significant figure in years.

Data analysis

The accuracy of each dental age estimation method was assessed by comparing the estimated dental ages with the known chronological ages of the horses. An independent t-test was performed to compare the estimated dental ages with the chronological ages. A Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to assess the relationship between chronological age and age estimated using dental methods. The strength and direction of the correlation were interpreted as follows: coefficients between 0.90 and 1.00 (−0.90 to −1.00) indicated very strong positive (or negative) correlation, 0.70 to 0.90 (−0.70 to −0.90) strong positive (or negative) correlation, 0.50 to 0.70 (−0.50 to −0.70) moderate positive (or negative) correlation, 0.30 to 0.50 (−0.30 to −0.50) weak positive (or negative) correlation, and 0.00 to 0.30 (−0.00 to −0.30) negligible correlation. Also, a Bland-Altman plot was used to assess the agreement between the estimated dental ages and chronological ages. P≤0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 25.0).

Discussion

Dental age estimation methods in Nigerian local horses provide reliable age estimates with varying precision. The present study revealed that the angle of incidence method of dental age estimation in horses had the highest correlation with chronological age among the six dental age estimation methods tested, while the eruption pattern method offered the greatest precision for young horses despite showing no overall significant correlation. Therefore, the angle of incidence method showed exceptional accuracy, reflecting predictable changes in tooth angulation with age. The strong correlation of the angle of incidence method in our study (r=0.988) corresponds with the finding of Richardson et al. [17] that the incisor profile angle correlated well with true age, although our correlation coefficient was considerably higher. However, it is important to note that the foundational work of Richardson et al. was published 30 years ago, and equine dentistry has evolved significantly over the past two decades, particularly regarding dental care practices and breeding management. Modern horses may exhibit different dental characteristics compared with those studied in the 1990s, which could affect the universal applicability of these earlier findings. This difference regarding our higher correlation coefficient might be attributed to population-specific factors, methodological variations, or changes in contemporary horse management practices that affect dental development and wear patterns differently than in previous decades. The strong correlations for the grinding surface shape and dental stars methods suggest consistent morphological development patterns. Nigerian local horses follow dental development patterns consistent with established equine age estimation principles, which was particularly evident in younger horses, where the eruption pattern method showed remarkable precision (average difference of 0.1 years) despite lacking correlation across all age groups.

However, it is important to acknowledge that the accuracy of dental age estimation methods can be significantly influenced by various factors not examined in this study. Modern equine dentistry literature emphasizes that dental appearance and wear patterns are substantially affected by dental care practices, dietary habits, and pathological conditions such as EOTRH. These factors can alter the morphological features used in age estimation, potentially affecting the reliability of these methods. The horses in our study were from managed facilities with documented ages, but variations in dental care, feeding practices, and oral health status among individual animals may have contributed to the variability observed in some methods, particularly Galvayne’s groove. Future studies should systematically document and account for these variables when validating dental age estimation methods.

The wide limits of agreement for Galvayne’s groove method (9.9 years range) indicate greater individual variation, emphasising the importance of using multiple dental indicators for age estimation, especially in older horses. Our findings regarding the variable reliability of Galvayne’s groove align with Muylle et al. [13], who reported that “Galvayne’s groove and the presence of a hook on the upper corner incisors are poor indicators of age in horse teeth”. Their study found Galvayne’s groove in horses of various ages (9+ years in Standardbreds, 11+ years in Belgian draft horses and Arabians, and 13+ years in mini-Shetland ponies), but its length could not be reliably related to specific ages. This supports our observation that despite showing correlation with age, Galvayne’s groove demonstrated the largest range of agreement (9.9 years) and thus the least precision. The high level of precision observed in the eruption pattern method for younger horses in our study aligns with the findings of Richardson et al. [17], who identified clear age ranges for both temporary and permanent incisor eruptions. However, they also highlighted significant individual differences, especially in the eruption timing of permanent corner incisors, which can occur anywhere between 3 years and 5 months to as late as 5 years and 1 month. This variability may account for why our eruption pattern method exhibited strong precision in certain age cases but lacked consistent correlation across different age groups. Martin et al. [10] introduced a systematic framework suggesting that age estimates for horses under five should be highly accurate (within the same year), accurate within 1 year for horses between 5 and 10 years, and acceptable within a 2-year range for horses aged 10 to 15. Our results generally support this graduated expectation of precision, as our methods displayed narrower limits of agreement in younger horses and broader variations in older ones.

Khazaeel et al. [6] identified a very strong correlation between estimated dental age and actual age in Arabian mares (r=0.992), which closely reflects our observations related to the angle of incidence method. Their equation could account for 98.5% of the age variation, indicating remarkable consistency in dental age estimation indicators among different populations when suitable methods are employed. Łuszczyński et al. [9] found that age estimation errors were least prevalent in young Icelandic horses (0–2 years) at only 2.1%, compared with higher error rates in older groups. This supports our finding that the eruption pattern method had excellent precision in younger horses. Their observation that age was more commonly underestimated than overestimated differs from our varied results across different methods, suggesting population-specific disparities in dental development rates. Muylle et al. [11] noted significant variations in dental development timelines between mini-Shetland ponies and donkeys, underscoring the need for breed-specific considerations in dental age estimation. Our research focused on Nigerian local horses provides valuable data for this particular population, which may differ from previously studied equine groups.

The strong correlations noted between most dental age estimation methods and chronological age reinforce the biological principle that dental growth and wear follow predictable patterns that can be reliably measured. The varying levels of precision observed across methods support the idea that different dental characteristics evolve at different rates and exhibit varying degrees of individual differences throughout horses’ lifespans. The high accuracy of the angle of incidence method suggests that this should be prioritised when estimating age in Nigerian local horses. The excellent precision of the eruption pattern method in younger horses makes it particularly valuable for determining age during the first five years of life, whilst the grinding surface shape and dental stars methods offer reliable alternatives for middle-aged and older horses. By establishing the reliability of different dental age estimation methods in this specific population, this study provides a scientific basis for age verification in sales, competitions, and regulatory compliance involving Nigerian local horses.

For equine veterinarians and practitioners working with Nigerian local horses, these findings have direct clinical applications. When examining horses without documentation, practitioners should prioritize the angle of incidence method while acknowledging its categorical nature (5–7-year ranges). In young horses aged 3–5 years, the eruption pattern method provides the most reliable and precise estimates, making it valuable for determining appropriate vaccination schedules, deworming protocols, and training readiness. For middle-aged and older horses, combining multiple methods—particularly the grinding surface shape and dental stars—improves estimate reliability. Practitioners should exercise caution when relying solely on Galvayne’s groove due to its high variability (± 5 years), using it only as a supplementary indicator in horses over 10 years. These age estimates are crucial for age-appropriate dental prophylaxis timing, nutritional management adjustments, geriatric care planning, and making informed decisions about a horse’s working capacity and retirement needs. However, it must be emphasized that dental age estimation methods should complement, not replace, proper animal identification systems. Permanent identification methods, such as microchipping, breed registry documentation, official passports, and veterinary records, remain the gold standard for age verification. Dental age estimation should be considered a supplementary tool for situations where documentation is unavailable, questionable, or for newly acquired horses of unknown history.

The sample size in this study may not have represented the full diversity of Nigerian local horse populations across different regions and management systems. Changes in nutrition, dental care practices, and environmental factors could have affected the development of the teeth and wear patterns in ways not captured in this study. Importantly, this study did not systematically document the dental care history, dietary management, or presence of dental pathologies such as EOTRH for individual horses, which are known to significantly influence dental morphology and could affect age estimation accuracy. The age distribution of the sample may have also had an influence on the correlation results, particularly for methods that are applicable only to specific age ranges. The eruption pattern method, for example, is inherently limited to young horses, and this might explain its lack of overall correlation but excellent precision.

In conclusion, the present study provides important information regarding the reliability of six dental age estimation methods in Nigerian local horses. All methods showed no statistically significant differences from chronological age, but their precision varied considerably. The angle of incidence method demonstrated the highest correlation with chronological age, followed by the grinding surface shape and dental stars methods. However, it is important to note that the angle of incidence method provides age estimates in broad categorical ranges (5–7 years, 10–12 years, 15–18 years, 20+ years) rather than precise year-by-year estimates. The eruption pattern method showed excellent precision in younger horses (bias of only 0.1 years) despite lacking overall correlation across age groups, making it the most reliable method for horses under five years of age. Based on the findings of this study, we recommend that equine practitioners working with Nigerian local horses prioritise the angle of incidence method for general age estimation within broad age categories (5–7 years, 10–12 years, 15–18 years, and 20+ years), recognizing its limitation as a rough estimation tool rather than a method for precise age determination. This method should be supplemented with the eruption pattern method for horses under five years of age, where more precise age estimates are needed, and the grinding surface shape and dental stars methods for middle-aged and older horses. Although, Galvayne’s groove still correlated with age, it should be used with caution due to its wider variation in precision. Future research should expand the sample size and geographical scope so as to validate and refine these findings, potentially including more diverse horse populations and accounting for additional environmental and genetic variables.
