Air Cholangiography versus Contrast Cholangiography in Patients with Hilar Biliary Obstruction: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Zahid Ijaz Tarar, Umer Farooq, Mustafa Gandhi, Abdallah El Alayli, Ahtshamullah Chaudhry, Faisal Kamal, Baltej Singh, Yezaz A. Ghouri, Nirav Thosani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimsTo minimize the risk of post-procedure complications, air cholangiography has been proposed as an alternative to contrast cholangiography in patients with hilar biliary obstruction undergoing endoscopic retrograde cholangiography (ERCP). We compared the rate of post-ERCP complication between these two groups.MethodsWe systematically reviewed all studies that compared the safety, success, and complication rate of air versus contrast cholangiography in malignant hilar obstruction patients.ResultsA total of six studies consisting of 450 patients were included in the analysis. The overall rate of post-ERCP complication was less in the air cholangiography group (relative risk [RR]: 0.43, 95% CI: 0.30–0.61, I2: 1.97%) compared to the contrast cholangiography group. Compared to contrast cholangiography, the pooled risk of post-ERCP cholangitis was significantly lower in the air cholangiography group (RR: 0.25, 95% CI: 0.14–0.44, I2: 0%). There was no difference in risk of post-ERCP pancreatitis (RR: 0.79, 95% CI: 0.32–1.94, I2: 0%), bleeding (RR: 1.17, 95% CI: 0.30–4.54), and 30-day mortality (RR: 0.59, 95% CI: 0.23–1.50, I2: 0%) between the two groups. The pooled odds of clinical (odds ratio [OR]: 1.14, 95% CI: 0.39–3.34, I2: 0%) and technical success (OR: 2.91, 95% CI: 0.52–16.35, I2: 0%) were similar between both groups.ConclusionIn comparison to contrast cholangiography, air cholangiography had a better safety profile and a lower rate of post-ERCP complications.

Introduction

Biliary obstruction is associated with significant morbidity and mortality and can be of either malignant or nonmalignant etiology. Nonmalignant causes included Mirizzi syndrome, autoimmune conditions like primary sclerosing cholangitis or primary biliary cholangitis, parasitic infections, and AIDS-related cholangiopathy. Nonmalignant hilar biliary strictures are commonly postoperative from liver resection, transplantation, and cholecystectomy. Malignant hilar obstructions are caused by tumors from the bile ducts, gallbladder, liver, or metastatic lesions [1]. Cholangiocarcinoma arises from the epithelial cells of the intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts. Cholangiocarcinoma involving the hepatic duct bifurcation (hilar cholangiocarcinoma’s) are called Klatskin tumors and are a type of extrahepatic bile duct cancer. Each year, about 15,000 individuals in the USA are newly diagnosed with liver and biliary tract carcinoma, with an increasing incidence in recent years [2]. Klatskin tumors account for roughly 10% of these cases. Cholangiocarcinoma is a type of cancer diagnosed in about 2% of all cancer cases, with an overall incidence of 1.2 per 100,000 people. This cancer is more common among individuals over 65, and its incidence increases nearly tenfold in those above 80. Men and women are affected by this type of cancer at similar rates [2].

Even though surgical resection is the gold standard, due to the complex anatomy and delayed diagnosis, most hilar malignant lesions cannot be curatively resected at presentation, with the 5-year survival being less than 10% [3]. Palliative chemotherapy and biliary drainage with stenting are the main alternatives known to prolong survival and improve the quality of life in these patients [4]. Biliary drainage resolves the bothersome symptoms of jaundice, abdominal discomfort, pruritus, and anorexia [4]. There is a similar benefit to the drainage of nonmalignant hilar obstructions.

Percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), or endoscopic ultrasound-guided drainage can be used in such cases. Studies have shown ERCP is superior to percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage for biliary drainage, especially in patients with perihilar tumors [5, 6]. It is crucial to map the biliary system by injecting contrast through the standard biliary catheter or tri-tome after selective biliary cannulation, as this helps identify any strictures and plan for appropriate drainage, including selecting the correct length, diameter, and type of biliary stent [7]. Additionally, understanding the biliary anatomy before surgery helps distinguish operable from non-operable cases, particularly in Klatskin tumors. Cholangitis leading to septicemia is a devastating complication of ERCP, which is treated with early aggressive management comprising intravenous hydration, potent antibiotics, and biliary decompression [8]. Some studies have shown that cholangitis is uncommon in patients with malignant hilar obstruction before biliary drainage [9–11]. Theoretically, this could be due to corrosive injury to the biliary epithelium from the contrast in the poorly drained segments or from the injection of contaminated contrast in immunocompromised individuals. Therefore, to decrease the risk of cholangitis, the best approach would be to avoid injecting any dye during cholangiography.

Although the air was used as a contrast medium for cholangiography years ago, there have been recent studies and reports where the air has been used as a contrast medium for cholangiography in patients with an allergy to iodine-dye contrast or to reduce post-ERCP cholangitis in patients with complex hilar strictures. We performed a meta-analysis of studies comparing air cholangiography to iodine-dye contrast cholangiography in patients with hilar biliary obstruction (malignant and nonmalignant). Our study primarily aimed to compare cholangitis incidence in these patient groups.

Materials and Methods

Data Search and Screening

We conducted our analysis following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) statement. We thoroughly searched major databases, including PubMed/Medline, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, Google Scholar, reference citation analysis, and Cochrane, from inception to May 30, 2023. We have provided detailed search strategy in online supplementary File 1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000547420). The search items words were (Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography or ERCP or Endoscopic cholangiography or Air cholangiography or contrast-enhanced and/or contrast cholangiography) AND (Hilar biliary obstruction or bile duct obstruction or malignant biliary obstruction) And (Cholangitis or post-ERCP cholangitis or pancreatitis or 30-day mortality or mortality or bleeding or perforation or clinical and/or technical success). We manually searched the included studies’ bibliography to find any additional studies. PRISMA flowchart illustrates the screening results (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flowchart for study selection.

Study Selection and Eligibility Criteria

Study selection was performed by two researchers (Z.I.T. and M.G.). They independently screened the titles, abstracts, and full manuscripts to select the eligible studies. Any conflict was resolved through detailed discussion between them. The inclusion criteria were as follows: PICO – P: patient above age 18 receiving cholangiography for benign or malignant biliary obstruction; I: patient in which cholangiography was performed using air media; C: compared to the patient group receiving contrast cholangiography; O: risk of post-ERCP complications including cholangitis, pancreatitis, bleeding, and 30-day mortality between these two groups.

We included retrospective, randomized controlled or prospective studies. We excluded case reports, case series, review articles, and conference abstracts. We only included the studies in which air cholangiography was compared with contrast. We excluded the studies in which CO2 was used to obtain cholangiogram. The reason for excluding CO2 studies was to decrease the risk of bias and limit heterogeneity.

Data Extraction

Data were extracted by two researchers (Z.I.T. and M.G.). The information about study design, country of study, study cohort characteristics, patient demographics, type of procedure, stent type, stent occlusion and reinsertion, rate of adverse events, clinical and technical success, and procedure time was obtained. Once data were extracted, two reviewers (U.F. and Y.A.G.) independently reviewed the data sheet, and a final data sheet was prepared after a discussion between the four reviewers.

Outcomes

Our outcome of interest was the overall rate of post-ERCP complications in both air and contrast cholangiography group and comparative analysis of air vs. contrast cholangiography groups. We also analyzed the rate of post-ERCP cholangitis, pancreatitis, bleeding, 30-day mortality, technical and clinical success between the two groups.

Data Synthesis and Statistical Analysis

We used the open meta-analyst software to run the analysis [12]. The random-effect model was used to calculate the pooled event rate, relative risk (RR), and odds ratio (OR) with a corresponding 95% CI. Cochrane chi-square and I2 statistics were used to assess the variance and heterogeneity.

Quality and Grading Assessment

We relied on Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) criteria for assessing the quality of included studies [13]. We scored comparative studies on twelve items of MINORS criteria, and each item is scored from 0–2 (0 if not reported; 1 when reported but inadequate; and 2 when reported and adequate). Thus, a maximum ideal score of 24 could be obtained for comparative studies. Additionally, we used the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool designed explicitly for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) when assessing the risk of bias [14]. Studies assessed using this tool are evaluated based on randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, and incomplete and/or selective reporting of results.

We used the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) approach for the assessment of the certainty in the reported outcomes [15]. RCTs are considered of high certainty by default and were later downgraded in case of risk of bias, imprecision, indirectness, inconsistency, and publication bias. While observational studies are considered low quality, it can be downgraded for the same reasons or upgraded if there is a large magnitude of effect, cofounding factors, or a dose-response gradient. We summarized all the certainty of the evidence assessments in summary of findings tables using the GRADEpro online tool [16].

Results

Study Selection and Exclusion

We shortlisted 249 articles on the initial search, of which 180 were excluded due to duplication. After reviewing titles and abstracts, another 56 articles were excluded. Thirteen papers were considered relevant for our analysis, so we reviewed these articles in detail and finally included six studies [17–22] in our meta-analysis. Among the selected studies, 2 were RCTs [19, 21] and 4 were retrospective cohort [17, 18, 20, 22]. A total of 452 patients were included out of which 206 underwent air cholangiography, whereas 246 got contrast. The mean age of the air cholangiography group was 60.7 ± 13.6 and contrast cholangiography group was 60.3 ± 13. Twenty patients had nonmalignant hilar strictures, and the remaining 430 patients received ERCP for malignant hilar obstruction. The ERCP was the modality to achieve cannulation of bile duct in all studies and after that air or contrast was injected. The dose of air and dye was different in studies as Elshimi et al. [21] used 20–100 mL of air and 10–20 cc of diluted urographin. Sud et al. [19] used 10–15 mL of air or contrast. Table 1 describes the baseline characteristics of study population.

Overall Adverse Events

Six studies [17–22] provided the details of adverse events in the air and contrast cholangiography group. Thirty adverse events were reported in 206 patients who underwent air cholangiography compared to 94 in 246 patients in the contrast cholangiography group. The pooled adverse event rate in air cholangiography and contrast cholangiography group was 16.1% (95% CI: 7.8–30.2%) and 41% (95% CI: 24.3–59.9%), respectively (Table 2). Patients in the air cholangiography group have a significantly lower risk of post-ERCP complications (RR: 0.43, 95% CI: 0.30–0.61, I2: 1.97%) than contrast cholangiography (Fig. 2a).

Post-ERCP Cholangitis

Six studies [17–22] reported cases of post-ERCP cholangitis. ERCP with air cholangiography technique demonstrated cholangitis in 12 of 206 patients, whereas 65 out of 246 patients in the contrast cholangiography group developed cholangitis post-procedure. The pooled event rate of post-ERCP cholangitis in air cholangiography was 6.9% (95% CI: 3.5–12.9%) and 27.3% (95% CI: 19.3–37.1%) in contrast cholangiography group (Table 2). The pooled risk of post-ERCP cholangitis was significantly lower in the air cholangiography patient group (RR: 0.25, 95% CI: 0.14–0.44, I2: 0%) (Fig. 2b).

Post-ERCP Pancreatitis

Five studies [17, 19–22] reported rates of post-ERCP pancreatitis. Nine out of 141 patients in which air cholangiography was used developed pancreatitis, whereas, in the contrast cholangiography group, 14 of 155 patients had post-procedure pancreatitis. The pooled event rate of pancreatitis in air cholangiography and contrast cholangiography was 7.6% (95% CI: 4–13.9%) and 8% (95% CI: 3.2–18.5%), respectively (Table 2). There was no difference in pooled risk of post-ERCP pancreatitis in both groups (RR: 0.79, 95% CI: 0.32–1.94, I2: 0%) (Fig. 2c).

Thirty-Day Mortality

Five of the 206 patients who underwent air cholangiography died within 30 days of the procedure and 12 out of 246 patients after contrast cholangiography. The pooled rate of 30-day mortality in air cholangiography population was 4.4% (95% CI: 1.9–9.9%), whereas pooled rate in contrast cholangiography was 6% (95% CI: 2.8–12.4%) (Table 2). There was no difference in the pooled risk of 30-day mortality in both groups (RR: 0.59, 95% CI: 0.23–1.50, I2: 0%) (Fig. 2d).

Post-ERCP Bleeding

Four studies [17, 19, 21, 22] provided information on rates of post-ERCP bleeding. In the air cholangiography group, 3 out of 118 patients developed bleeding post-procedure compared to 3 out of 131 in the contrast cholangiography group. The pooled event rate of post-ERCP bleeding in air and cholangiography patients was 3.9% (95% CI: 1.5–9.9%) and 3.4% (95% CI: 1.3–8.8%), respectively (Table 2). The pooled risk of post-ERCP bleeding was similar in both groups (RR 1.17, 95% CI: 0.30–4.54, I2 0%) (Fig. 2e).

Clinical Success

Four studies [19–22] documented the clinical success rates in air and contrast cholangiography groups. 96 of 103 patients in the air cholangiography group achieved clinical success and 94 of 103 patients in the contrast group. The pooled rate of clinical success in air cholangiography patient group was 93% (95% CI 73%–98.5%), while contrast cholangiography group had a clinical success rate of 91% (95% CI: 72.6%–97.5%) (Table 2). No statistically significant difference in the pooled odds of clinical success was demonstrated between both groups (OR 1.14, 95% CI: 0.39–3.34, I2 0%) (Fig. 3a).

Technical Success

Four studies [19–22] examined the rates of technical success. Technical success was achieved in 103 out of 106 patients in the air cholangiography group, whereas 102 out of 110 patients in contrast cholangiography had technical success. The pooled technical success rate in air cholangiography group was 95.2% (95% CI: 84.1–98.7%) and pooled success rate in contrast cholangiography was 91.1% (95% CI: 83.5–95.4%) (Table 2). No difference in pooled odds of technical success between the groups was seen (OR 2.91, 95% CI: 0.52–16.35, I2 0%) (Fig. 3b).

Risk of Bias Assessment

The quality assessment of studies is summarized in online supplementary Table 2. Based on MINORS criteria for non-randomized studies, the quality of studies was classified as poor (score ≤5), fair (score 6–10), or high quality (≥11), as described previously [23]. As such, all the studies were rated as high quality. Based on Cochrane’s RoB tool, all included RCTs were found to have high or unclear RoB in at least one domain. The study by Sud et al. [19] demonstrated successful randomization and allocation concealment; however, blinding posed an issue for this trial. The study by Elshimi et al. [21] exhibited either unclear or elevated risk of bias across most domains of Cochrane’s RoB tool.

Certainty of Evidence

The certainty in the evidence was found to range from very low to low for the reported outcomes. The certainty in the evidence was downgraded due to the following factors: the presence of risk of bias in the included randomized trials, and the presence of imprecision in the reported analysis due to the low number of patients, leading to a wide confidence interval. The full GRADE assessment can be found in online supplementary Table 3.

Discussion

In our systematic review and meta-analysis, we have collected data from six studies that reported the efficacy and safety of air cholangiography versus contrast cholangiography in patients with hilar biliary obstruction undergoing endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP). We found a pooled event rate of 41% of overall adverse events in contrast cholangiography group, compared to 16.1% in air cholangiography group. The patients underwent air cholangiography had a 57% less risk of developing post-ERCP complications compared to contrast cholangiography. On detailed analysis, we found that pooled event rate of post-ERCP cholangitis in air cholangiography was 6.9%, whereas the pooled event rate of 27.3% was noted in contrast cholangiography group. The air cholangiography group had a significant lower risk (RR 0.25, 95% CI: 0.14–0.44) of post-ERCP cholangitis. The pooled rate of post-ERCP pancreatitis, bleeding, and 30-day mortality in the air versus contrast cholangiography was as follows: 7.6% versus. 8%, 3.9% versus. 3.4%, and 4.4% versus. 6%, respectively. We did not observe any statistical difference in the risk of pancreatitis, bleeding, and 30-day mortality between both groups. We analyzed the technical and clinical success rate in both group and found that technical success rate was 91.1% in contrast cholangiography compared to 95.2% in air cholangiography. The pooled percentage of clinical success in contrast cholangiography was 92.8% in comparison to 93% in air cholangiography group. The odds of clinical and technical success were similar in both groups.

We conducted a thorough literature review to include all trials which compared air cholangiography to iodine contrast cholangiography. Two reviewers independently reviewed the included studies in detail and determined the eligibility. A detailed data extraction sheet was compiled by two researchers, which was reviewed by the senior author before analysis was run. In this analysis, we have focused only on the comparison of post-ERCP complications in air cholangiography group compared to contrast, excluding CO2 cases which provide more focused and unbiased assessment of complication rate between these groups. We included patients with malignant and nonmalignant hilar obstruction to generalize the result to a larger population. We used a random-effect model to obtain a more conservative estimate of pooled event rates and risk difference. In addition to analyzing the pooled rate and risk difference of overall adverse events between air and contrast cholangiography groups, we also performed a subgroup analysis and calculated the pooled events rates risk difference of post-ERCP cholangitis, pancreatitis, bleeding, and 30-day mortality in both groups.

There are also some limitations of our meta-analysis. All the studies were conducted on patients with malignant hilar obstructions only except Elshimi et al. [21], which also included nonmalignant hilar strictures causing biliary obstruction. We included both observational and RCTs and this difference in methodology of the studies raise the issue of study validity. There is a risk of measured and unmeasured bias in the observational study. There was no stratified data provided on the rate of complication based on type of stents.

Overall outcomes of our analysis reinforce the available literature on this topic. A meta-analysis performed by Tang et al. [24] investigating the efficacy and safety of interventions for preoperative biliary drainage in patients with hilar malignancies found that ERCP-related cholangitis is the most common complication occurring in 36–65% of cases. Giri et al. [25] performed a meta-analysis on this topic previously but compared combined air and CO2 group with contrast cholangiography and reported similar results. The main difference between our analysis and Giri et al. [25] is that we excluded the studies in which CO2 was used as contrast media to prevent the bias and heterogeneity. The data on the current topic are scarce, and our meta-analysis is the first to provide the pooled event rate and risk difference of post-ERCP complications between contrast and air cholangiography. Another study performed by Singh et al. [26] reported that air cholangiography is safe and effective for unilateral stent placement in hilar obstruction patients but in the comparison group was of contrast-free cholangiography. Sud et al. [27] also conducted a study on 17 patients with malignant hilar obstruction using air cholangiography and reported no cases of cholangitis or mortality within 30 days of the procedure, but no comparison group was recruited in this study.

Pisello et al. [18] describe that the primary reason for cholangitis is the inability to drain entirely or blockage of the bile duct, often caused by the injection of iodine contrast. Inadequate drainage of the contrast agent from the poorly drained segments leads to prolonged exposure and chemical injury from the contrast agent, leading to cholangitis [20, 21]. Additionally, injection of contaminated contrast agents in immunocompromised individuals can introduce pathogens, resulting in infection and inflammation of the biliary ducts [17]. Even though air insufflation of the bile ducts during ERCP can lead to air embolism, air cholangiography has not been directly linked to this rare but deadly complication [28]. To the best of our knowledge, there were no reported cases of air embolism in the studies used in our meta-analysis.

The hydrostatic law states a direct relationship between pressure and density within a system. Specifically, as density increases or decreases, the pressure within the system will increase or decrease proportionally. As the density of air (1,205 kg/m3) is much lesser than the density of contrast (>1,000 kg/m3), the pressure exerted by the air on the biliary system is a fraction compared to that of iodine contrast (for equal volumes). When the pressure inside the biliary system increases from contrast injection, it can disrupt the hepatocyte tight junctions and Kupffer cell dysfunction, reducing toxin clearance and bile flow [29]. This reduces the risk of gut bacteria migrating into the bile duct or bloodstream to a significant extent [22].

Ozden et al. [30] suggested that the risk of post-procedural cholangitis can be significantly minimized by ensuring proper biliary drainage and timely removal of biliary stents. At the same time, the use of prophylactic antibiotics was controversial. To reduce the risk of cholangitis, other studies have suggested measures such as using minimal contrast to avoid filling of undrained segments, administering broad-spectrum antibiotics, ensuring proper hydration, inserting metal stents, opting for less invasive procedures, and minimizing the duration of the operation [31, 32].

In conclusion, air cholangiography is a feasible option in patients with hilar obstruction with a reduced risk of complications. In our analysis, the maximum follow-up period was 1 month which proves the safety and efficacy of air cholangiogram over a short period, but the possibility of late development of adverse events cannot be ruled out. Further prospective randomized control trials with an extended follow-up period are warranted.
