Favorable outcome in a neonate with methamphetamine, tobacco, and alcohol exposure with Hypoxic Ischemic Encephalopathy managed using therapeutic hypothermia
Authors: Hazel S. Baconga, Carissa Paz C. Dioquino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This report details the management of a neonate with moderate Hypoxic Ischemic Encephalopathy (HIE) and prenatal polysubstance exposure. Born at 36 weeks gestation following maternal methamphetamine, tobacco, and alcohol use, the infant suffered a placental abruption. Therapeutic cooling was initiated at three hours of life for 72 h. While urine toxicology confirmed methamphetamine exposure, Neonatal Abstinence Syndrome (NAS) scores remained below the pharmacological threshold. Despite limitations in advanced neurophysiological monitoring common in resource-limited settings, the infant’s recovery was successfully managed through diligent clinical monitoring, supportive care, and early multidisciplinary neurodevelopmental follow-up. Notably, an average Battelle Developmental Inventory score administered at 5 months of age showed promise, but long-term monitoring is needed given the patient's high-risk prenatal history.

Background

A hidden epidemic of polysubstance use, particularly in pregnancy, represents a significant and often concealed public health crisis. The issue is especially concerning in regions like the Philippines, where a lack of comprehensive data may mask the true scope of the problem. While studies from the United States estimate methamphetamine use in pregnancy could be as high as 4.8% [1], the absence of published data from the Philippines creates a critical knowledge gap. This is compounded by local studies that report significant rates of nicotine exposure during pregnancy around 5.4% [2] and high levels of alcohol consumption, with over 75% of respondents in a Leyte study reporting drinking during their first trimester [3].

Exposure to multiple substances in utero is particularly damaging during critical periods of fetal development. Based on the IDEAL study, in utero methamphetamine exposure is associated with neurodevelopmental problems, increased prematurity, and smaller-for-gestational-age infants [4]. Likewise, prenatal nicotine exposure has been linked to a greater risk for neurodevelopmental, respiratory, and cardiovascular problems in childhood [5], while ethanol exposure can lead to the constellation of defects known as Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorders.

When a neonate is born after exposure to substances of abuse, they are at high risk for Neonatal Abstinence Syndrome (NAS), a collection of withdrawal symptoms that includes central nervous system irritability, gastrointestinal dysfunction, and respiratory distress [6]. Although most commonly associated with opioid withdrawal, NAS can also be precipitated by maternal use of methamphetamines, nicotine, and alcohol [7]. Managing NAS is a complex task that requires both supportive care and, when needed, medication. For non-opioid exposures, however, standardized treatment guidelines remain elusive.

In addition to NAS, maternal illicit drug use is a known risk factor for other serious neonatal conditions, such as hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE), which is a brain injury caused by a lack of oxygen to the brain [8]. The convergence of polysubstance exposure and HIE presents a formidable clinical challenge, with the potential for additive or synergistic brain injury. Managing these cases in developing nations is further complicated by limited access to advanced diagnostic tools like continuous amplitude-integrated EEG (aEEG) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), necessitating a heavy reliance on serial clinical assessment, available bedside imaging, and comprehensive developmental monitoring post-discharge. This report details the complex clinical course of a neonate who presented with this exact combination of factors, highlighting the resulting diagnostic and management complexities.

Case presentation

The infant’s birth was complicated by his 20-year-old mother's severe methamphetamine, tobacco, and alcohol use disorders. The mother, a primigravida, reported chronic methamphetamine insufflation 6 years prior to birth, which continued throughout her pregnancy at a rate of 2–3 times daily, with her last use just four hours before delivery. This extensive substance use was compounded by a 12 pack-year smoking history and daily alcohol consumption (3–4 standard drinks), all occurring in the context of inconsistent prenatal care, with only two reported check-ups.

The delivery, necessitated by an emergency Cesarean section at 36 weeks for non-reassuring fetal status, premature rupture of membranes, and placental abruption, immediately revealed the severity of the infant's condition. He presented with profound birth asphyxia, evidenced by APGAR scores of 1, 4, and 7 at one, five, and ten minutes, respectively. The baby was limp, apneic, and cyanotic with a heart rate of approximately 100 beats per minute. Despite immediate resuscitation efforts, including continuous stimulation and positive pressure ventilation (PPV), he required endotracheal intubation at one minute of life. Post-intubation, he remained lethargic with poor muscle tone, flexed limbs, slow respirations, and a compromised sucking reflex. An arterial blood gas taken at 20 min of life confirmed severe uncompensated metabolic acidosis (pH 7.19, bicarbonate 14.6 mmol/L, pO2 209 mmHg, base deficit −12.9), solidifying the diagnosis of moderate (Stage II) HIE.

Initial clinical impressions centered on moderate (Stage II) HIE, while ruling out early-onset neonatal sepsis and considering NAS secondary to methamphetamine exposure. Neurologically, the infant exhibited lethargy, poor tone, occasional tremors, and mydriasis. He required mechanical ventilation for apnea and cyanosis. His abdomen was soft with normoactive bowel sounds, but he showed poor feeding tolerance with minimal coffee ground output from his orogastric tube.

Diagnostic investigations provided crucial insights. Both maternal and infant urine drug screens were positive for methamphetamine on admission and for the following two days, confirming in utero exposure. While meconium or umbilical cord tissue testing would provide a more comprehensive window of exposure, these were unavailable, necessitating a reliance on urine screens and clinical history. Subsequent blood gas analyses showed persistent metabolic acidosis that later evolved. Other significant laboratory findings included coagulopathy (prolonged PT and PTT), elevated liver enzymes (AST and ALT), and impaired renal function (creatinine 87 µmol/L), alongside hypocalcemia. Hyperbilirubinemia with jaundice was attributed to ABO incompatibility, supported by a positive Direct Coombs test, and prematurity. NAS monitoring via Finnegan scores remained low (maximum 4) and generally trended downwards, leading to a later recommendation to discontinue scoring due to a lack of significant withdrawal symptoms.

Alongside resuscitation and mechanical ventilation, therapeutic hypothermia was started at three hours of life, maintaining a core body temperature of 33–35 °C for 72 h as a neuroprotective strategy for HIE. Owing to constraints in the hospital's capacity, continuous aEEG monitoring and brain MRI were unavailable. Recovery was instead monitored through serial neurological examinations using Sarnat staging and close observation for clinical seizure activity. No clinical seizures were noted. A bedside cranial ultrasound performed on day 3 showed no evidence of intracranial hemorrhage nor structural abnormalities. Infection prophylaxis with Ampicillin and Amikacin was administered. He received Erythropoietin (EPO) and Vitamin K for coagulopathy. Neurological management included standby Midazolam for potential seizures and environmental modifications for minimal stimulation. Nutritional support progressed cautiously from NPO to incremental orogastric feedings and was successfully transitioned to cup feeding with pasteurized donor human milk, as the mother was advised against breastfeeding due to her positive drug screen.

Significant social services involvement became paramount due to the mother's ongoing substance abuse. Discharge to maternal care was deemed unsafe, prompting extensive collaboration with the Child Protection Unit (CPU), and the infant was placed in a children’s home. His clinical course showed marked improvement: the moderate HIE resolved, the ABO incompatibility was managed, and sepsis was ruled out. Crucially, post-stabilization monitoring was conducted in coordination with developmental pediatrics and rehabilitation services. During the multidisciplinary assessment, the Battelle Developmental Inventory (BDI) was utilized to evaluate the infant's developmental trajectory. The examination revealed that his adaptive (self-care skills), social-emotional (interaction & self-concept), communication (understanding & using language), motor (gross & fine physical skills), and cognitive (learning & problem-solving) domains were all at par with average levels for his age, which is highly promising. Despite this progress, long-term vigilance remains necessary given the potential for delayed neurodevelopmental sequelae from prenatal MAP, alcohol, and nicotine exposure. Patient was eventually discharged to a children’s home.

Discussion

This case illustrates the successful management of moderate HIE via therapeutic hypothermia despite the confounding factor of polysubstance exposure and the lack of advanced neuroimaging. The infant’s immediate presentation with severe neurological depression and metabolic acidosis necessitated therapeutic hypothermia, which is the standard neuroprotective intervention for moderate-to-severe HIE [8]. While MRI is the gold standard for predicting neurodevelopmental outcomes in HIE, its unavailability in many global settings remains a significant hurdle. In this case, the transition from Stage II HIE symptoms (lethargy, poor tone) to a normal clinical exam, coupled with favorable early developmental screening, served as the primary indicator of recovery.

Therapeutic cooling's foundational effect is the reduction of the cerebral metabolic rate, which decreases by approximately 5–7% for every 1 °C drop in brain temperature [9]. This suppression of metabolic demand is critical, as it conserves adenosine triphosphate (ATP) by limiting the brain's reliance on injurious anaerobic glycolysis [10], thereby mitigating the downstream cascade of lactate accumulation and intracellular acidosis [11].

Maternal methamphetamine use was a primary complicating factor that likely contributed to the placental abruption and may have exacerbated the excitotoxic brain injury. Prenatal MAP exposure is associated with deficits in cortical and subcortical brain structures [4], [12]. Although overt neurobehavioral abnormalities may resolve within the first month of life, the underlying neurological and hepatotoxic risks remain [4]. Methamphetamine may cause neurotoxicity through both direct action and indirect hypoxic-ischemic injury. Direct neurotoxicity results from a flood of monoamine neurotransmitters that creates profound excitotoxicity in the developing brain [13]. This triggers a cascade of cellular damage, including severe oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA damage, and apoptosis [14]. These injury pathways show significant overlap with the secondary injury phase of HIE. The indirect effect stems from MAP-induced vasoconstriction of uterine and placental blood vessels, which can lead to placental insufficiency and an increased risk of abruption [12].

The pathophysiology of NAS stems from the fetus's neuroadaptation to chronic drug exposure. The abrupt cessation of this drug supply at birth leads to a state of neurochemical imbalance and hyperexcitability that manifests as withdrawal [6]. The clinical presentation of NAS is highly variable and its symptoms are non-specific, often mimicking other serious conditions like sepsis, hypoglycemia, or HIE [6]. The clinical picture was further obscured by prenatal exposure to alcohol and nicotine, both established confounders in assessing neurodevelopmental outcomes [4], [6], [7], [12]. Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorders (FASD) are known to impair growth and cognition, while nicotine adversely affects physical and neurobehavioral development [7], [15]. Animal models show that combined alcohol and methamphetamine exposure negatively impacts treatment outcomes, underscoring the difficulty in isolating the effects of each substance [16].

Management of this patient’s potential NAS highlights the evolution of clinical practice. While the Finnegan Neonatal Abstinence Scoring System (FNASS) is a traditional tool, its subjectivity is a known limitation [6], [17]. Emerging function-based protocols like the Eat, Sleep, Console (ESC) method are proving effective for infants with Neonatal Opioid Withdrawal Syndrome (NOWS) [18]. Although this case involved stimulant-related NAS, the principles of ESC emphasizing functional recovery while minimizing pharmacology remain clinically relevant. In resource-limited settings, the clinical priority remains the timely initiation of cooling and the prevention of "medical collisions" between HIE management and NAS monitoring. The potential application of therapeutic hypothermia for neonatal encephalopathy associated with MAP exposure is biologically plausible but clinically unproven. It could theoretically confer neuroprotection by mitigating the HIE-like component of MAP-induced injury and interrupting the final common pathways of cell death shared by both conditions.

Finally, the socio-familial context was a critical determinant of the patient's disposition. The extensive coordination between medical staff, social services, and the CPU underscores the principle that decisions about child removal must hinge on a holistic assessment of safety and support systems, not on prenatal substance use alone [19].
