Distribution of gut microbiota and prognostic value in elderly patients with coronary atherosclerosis: a preliminary culture-based study
Authors: Hengchao Du, Xiaobo Xu, Qunyan Lei, Chengsi Qian, Zhaofeng Wang, Xinyan Yu, Qunfang Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to characterize the distribution of specific cultivable gut microbiota in elderly patients with coronary artery disease (CAD) and evaluate their prognostic significance.MethodsA cohort of 200 elderly CAD patients, categorized into mild, moderate, and severe groups according to Gensini scores, together with 50 healthy elderly controls, were enrolled. Levels of specific gut microbial taxa (Bifidobacterium, Enterobacteriaceae, Lactobacillus, Enterococcus, Bacteroides) were quantified using standard anaerobic culture on selective agar media. Serum lipid profiles (TG, TC, HDL-C, LDL-C) were assessed. Correlations among microbial composition, disease severity, and lipid metabolic parameters were analyzed. Patients were followed for 6 months and stratified according to the occurrence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) to assess the association between gut microbiota and clinical outcomes. Multivariable logistic regression was used to adjust for potential confounders.ResultsCompared with controls, CAD patients exhibited reduced abundances of Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Bacteroides, along with elevated levels of Enterobacteriaceae and Enterococcus. The magnitude of these changes correlated with CAD severity. These microbial alterations were significantly associated with dyslipidemia and the degree of coronary atherosclerosis. Patients with unfavorable outcomes demonstrated more pronounced alterations in these bacterial groups. After adjusting for potential confounders, the combined microbiota score remained independently associated with poor prognosis (adjusted odds ratio [OR] = 3.484, 95% CI: 2.196–5.527, P < 0.001).ConclusionElderly CAD patients display distinct alterations in the abundance of selected cultivable gut bacteria, which correlates with both disease severity and lipid metabolic status. Assessment of these bacterial groups may hold potential value for prognostic prediction in this population, but these findings require validation in larger, independent cohorts using more comprehensive sequencing-based methods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05715-x.Keywords: Gut microbiota, Coronary artery disease, Elderly, Triglycerides, Total cholesterol, MACE

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) secondary to atherosclerosis is a prevalent cardiovascular condition among the elderly population. Its progression is closely linked to various metabolic mechanisms and can lead to severe adverse cardiovascular events such as myocardial infarction [1]. In recent years, the gut microbiota has been recognized as a critical factor in regulating host metabolism and immunity and is believed to play a key role in the development of atherosclerosis [2]. However, research on the compositional characteristics of the gut microbiota in elderly patients with CAD and its relationship with disease severity and lipid metabolism remains limited, particularly regarding its prognostic value [3]. Therefore, systematically analyzing the distribution characteristics of the gut microbiota in elderly CAD patients and clarifying its association with disease progression and prognosis holds significant clinical importance for improving early warning and intervention strategies for CAD.

Current studies have preliminarily revealed a close association between gut microbiota dysbiosis and various metabolic diseases, such as diabetes and hypertension [4]. This dysbiosis may contribute to the occurrence and progression of CAD through mechanisms such as influencing short-chain fatty acid metabolism and activating inflammatory pathways [5]. Some evidence suggests that an imbalance in the ratio of beneficial commensals (e.g., Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus) to potentially pathogenic bacteria (e.g., Enterobacteriaceae, Enterococcus) in the gut may be related to dyslipidemia and the atherosclerotic process [6]. Nevertheless, existing research primarily focuses on middle-aged populations or early disease stages [7]. A systematic understanding of the gut microbiota characteristics in elderly CAD patients—a high-risk group—and its relationship with disease severity and prognosis has not been established [8].

This study enrolled elderly patients with CAD and stratified them into mild, moderate, and severe groups based on their Gensini scores. Using classical microbiological culture methods, it analyzes differences in the distribution of specific, cultivable gut bacteria (Bifidobacterium, Enterobacteriaceae, Lactobacillus, Enterococcus, Bacteroides) and explores their correlations with lipid metabolism indicators. Through a 6-month follow-up, the study further investigates the association of these bacterial groups with MACE. The aim is to provide preliminary data on microbiota-based approaches for clinical prognosis assessment.

Data and methods

Study population

This study selected 200 elderly patients with CAD admitted to Zhejiang Rongjun Hospital from July 2019 to June 2023 as the observation group. Inclusion criteria were: (1) Confirmed diagnosis of CAD via coronary angiography, demonstrating at least one epicardial coronary artery stenosis of ≥ 50%; (2) No recent use of medications affecting gut microbiota (e.g., antibiotics, probiotics) within the past 3 months; (3) No history of cardiovascular disease; (4) No other conditions known to cause significant gut microbiota alterations; (5) No autoimmune systemic diseases. Exclusion criteria: (1) Presence of organic lesions in the digestive tract; (2) Recent history of active bleeding or major surgery; (3) History of gastrointestinal surgery; (4) Acute cerebrovascular diseases; (5) Presence of important organ diseases such as liver and kidney; (6) Complicated with malignant tumors; (7) Coagulation dysfunction; (8) Accompanied by mental illness.

Additionally, 50 healthy individuals of the same age group who underwent physical examination at our hospital during the same period were selected as the control group (excluding severe cardiovascular, hepatic, or renal diseases). The baseline data of the two groups were comparable (P > 0.05), as shown in Table 1. This study was approved by the hospital’s ethics committee and complied with the requirements of the Declaration of Helsinki. Study registration: This study does not involve a clinical trial requiring prospective registration in a public registry. Therefore, trial registration is not applicable.

Methods

Gut microbiota detection

Upon enrollment, a fresh stool sample was collected from each participant using sterile, screw-top containers. A 1-gram aliquot was precisely weighed using a calibrated analytical balance and homogenized in sterile saline to a final volume of 5 mL. Subsequently, a 0.5 mL aliquot of the homogenate was serially diluted 10-fold in saline. Aliquots (50 µL) from appropriate dilutions were plated onto selective agar media: Bifidobacterium on BL agar, Enterobacteriaceae on MacConkey agar, Lactobacillus on Lactobacillus Selective agar, Enterococcus on Enterococcus agar, and Bacteroides on BDS Medium agar. Following anaerobic incubation under standard conditions, colonies were enumerated and identified using the VITEK MS IVD system (bioMérieux, Lyon, France). Microbial abundance was quantified and expressed as the logarithm of colony-forming units per gram of wet feces (log₁₀ CFU/g).

Lipid metabolism indicator detection

Following enrollment, 4 mL of fasting venous blood was drawn from each participant. Serum was subsequently separated by centrifugation at 2500 × g for 10 min under controlled temperature. Levels of total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and triglycerides (TG) in serum were quantified using standardized enzymatic colorimetric assays.

CAD severity assessment

The Gensini score was selected because it is a well-validated, widely used scoring system that quantifies coronary atherosclerosis severity based on angiography, accounting for both the degree of luminal narrowing and stenosis location. Scores were assessed on the day of admission. According to CAD severity, patients were divided into mild CAD (Gensini scores ≤ 24, n = 43), moderate CAD (Gensini scores 25–49, n = 71), and severe CAD (Gensini scores ≥ 50, n = 86).

Follow-up and prognosis

Elderly CAD patients were followed up for 6 months after discharge via telephone, WeChat, and outpatient visits. They were divided into a good prognosis group and a poor prognosis group based on the occurrence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), defined as a composite of: cardiovascular death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, and unstable angina requiring hospitalization. Events were adjudicated by two independent cardiologists who reviewed medical records and follow-up documentation.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 27.0). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %), with intergroup differences assessed using the χ² test. For continuous variables, homogeneity of variance was verified using Bartlett’s test, and normality was examined with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data conforming to both assumptions were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s). Comparisons across multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) test. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction where applicable. Comparisons between two independent groups were made with the independent samples *t*-test. Correlations between variables were analyzed using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation coefficient, as appropriate based on the data distribution. The association of gut microbiota with patient prognosis was evaluated using multivariable logistic regression, adjusting for potential confounders including age, sex, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, and statin use. A combined microbiota risk score was generated using regression coefficients from the multivariable model. A two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the study population. No significant differences in age, sex, or body mass index (BMI) were observed between the control and CAD groups. Among CAD patients, those with poor prognosis exhibited a higher prevalence of hypertension and diabetes and were more likely to receive statin and antiplatelet therapy than those with good prognosis.

Distribution of gut microbiota in the two groups

Compared with the control group, CAD patients exhibited significantly lower abundances of Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Bacteroides and higher abundances of Enterobacteriaceae and Enterococcus across all severity subgroups (P < 0.05). Within the observation group, comparisons showed: mild CAD > moderate CAD > severe CAD for Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Bacteroides; and mild CAD < moderate CAD < severe CAD for Enterobacteriaceae and Enterococcus (P < 0.05). Multivariable analysis of gut microbiota for prognosis prediction

Multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed that the combined gut microbiota score was independently associated with MACE (adjusted OR = 3.484, 95% CI: 2.196–5.527, P < 0.001), indicating a strong association even after adjusting for age, sex, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, and statin use.The relatively high OR value may reflect the strong cumulative effect of dysbiosis on cardiovascular risk in this elderly population, although the possibility of overestimation due to limited sample size cannot be excluded. The area under the ROC curve for the combined score was 0.925 in unadjusted analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1), but this is likely overfitted and requires external validation.

Discussion

CAD is a prevalent arterial disease, and its progression can lead to myocardial ischemia, hypoxia, or necrosis, potentially culminating in myocardial infarction [9–11]. Clinically, CAD is considered to be influenced by various factors including hypertension and hyperlipidemia [12, 13]. The gut microbiota participates in physiological processes including digestion and nutrient absorption and influences intestinal microecology, host immunity, and metabolic homeostasis. Changes in gut microbiota have thus gained significant clinical attention in recent years.

Emerging evidence indicates that in metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes, gut bacteria modulate intestinal endocrine cell function, thereby regulating systemic immunity and glucolipid metabolism and influencing disease pathogenesis. Gut microbiota can regulate the intestinal barrier, participate in vitamin synthesis, and modulate immune system activity. Abnormal distribution of gut microbiota is associated with pathogen invasion and the occurrence of various diseases [14]. Our findings demonstrate that, compared with healthy controls, CAD patients had significantly lower abundances of Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Bacteroides and higher abundances of Enterobacteriaceae and Enterococcus. This reflects a state of gut microbiota dysbiosis in elderly CAD patients. Furthermore, the degree of dysbiosis varied significantly with CAD severity, with beneficial commensals decreasing and potentially pathogenic bacteria increasing progressively from mild to severe CAD. Enterococcus and Enterobacteriaceae are frequently regarded as opportunistic pathobionts. An expansion of these bacteria may compromise intestinal barrier integrity, promote pathogen overgrowth, stimulate endotoxin secretion, and trigger systemic inflammation through circulating inflammatory cytokines [15, 16]. In contrast, Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Bacteroides are generally regarded as beneficial commensals. Studies indicate that these bacteria play important roles in the prevention and treatment of metabolic diseases such as fatty liver disease and diabetes, and in regulating glucose-lipid metabolism [17, 18]. A report by Li XS et al. [19] also showed significant correlations between gut microbiota and glucose/lipid metabolism in coronary heart disease patients. These beneficial commensals can directly interact with the intestinal epithelium, inhibit pathogen proliferation, and help maintain intestinal ecological homeostasis. Additionally, research indicates that the gut microbiota metabolite trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) can promote CAD formation and plaque growth by inducing pro-inflammatory cytokines, upregulating plasma cholesterol and macrophage scavenger receptors, thereby participating in CAD pathogenesis, which may be one mechanism affecting the prognosis of elderly CAD patients [20, 21].

The present study revealed that Enterobacteriaceae abundance was positively correlated with LDL-C levels and CAD severity, and inversely correlated with HDL-C levels. Enterococcus was positively correlated with CAD severity and negatively correlated with HDL-C. Conversely, Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Bacteroides were positively correlated with HDL-C and negatively correlated with LDL-C, TG, TC, and CAD severity. Moreover, gut microbiota dysbiosis was more pronounced in the poor prognosis group compared to the good prognosis group. These results indicate that alterations in gut microbiota distribution are associated with blood lipid metabolism levels and disease progression in elderly CAD patients. The underlying mechanisms may involve dysbiosis-driven increases in metabolites such as TMAO, coprostanol, and bile acids, which alter lipid trafficking and subsequently modulate host lipid metabolism [22, 23]. A reduction in beneficial commensals may exacerbate metabolic disturbances, increase metabolic burden, and promote CAD progression.

This study investigated alterations in selected cultivable gut bacteria and their association with disease severity, lipid metabolism, and short-term prognosis in elderly patients with CAD. Our findings demonstrate that elderly CAD patients exhibit significant changes in the abundance of specific bacterial groups, characterized by decreases in Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Bacteroides and increases in Enterobacteriaceae and Enterococcus. These changes correlated with Gensini scores and lipid parameters, and were more pronounced in patients who experienced MACE during follow-up.

Alterations in gut microbiota composition may promote coronary atherosclerosis (CAD) through impaired intestinal barrier function and increased systemic endotoxin exposure. An overgrowth of opportunistic pathogens can downregulate the expression of key intestinal tight junction proteins, such as ZO-1 and occludin. This compromise in barrier integrity elevates intestinal permeability, allowing microbial products like lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to translocate into the circulation. Upon entry, LPS activates the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway, triggering a state of chronic, low-grade inflammation and contributing to vascular endothelial dysfunction—key drivers of atherosclerotic plaque formation [24]. In the present study, the significant correlations observed between Enterobacteriaceae/ Enterococcus abundance and adverse lipid profiles may be partially explained by this endotoxin-induced inflammatory CADcade, which is known to disrupt normal lipid metabolism.

Moreover, metabolites derived from the gut microbiota may directly modulate the progression of CAD. A prominent example involves dietary nutrients such as choline and carnitine. These compounds are metabolized by specific gut bacteria (e.g., Bacteroides) into trimethylamine (TMA), which is subsequently oxidized in the liver to trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO). Elevated circulating TMAO levels are implicated in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis through multiple mechanisms, including activation of macrophage scavenger receptors, inhibition of reverse cholesterol transport, and promotion of foam cell formation [25]. Concurrently, a decrease in beneficial commensals may result in reduced production of beneficial short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as butyrate and propionate. SCFAs exert pleiotropic effects, including anti-inflammatory actions and maintenance of intestinal epithelial barrier integrity. Furthermore, they modulate systemic lipid metabolism and vascular inflammation via signaling through G protein-coupled receptors (e.g., GPR41, GPR43). Consequently, a deficiency in SCFAs may attenuate these inherent cardiovascular protective mechanisms [26].

Furthermore, crosstalk between the gut microbiota and the host immune system plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of CAD. Dysbiosis can disrupt immune homeostasis both locally within the intestinal mucosa and systemically. For instance, it can skew the differentiation and balance of T-cell subsets, particularly by modulating the equilibrium between pro-inflammatory T helper 17 (Th17) cells and anti-inflammatory regulatory T (Treg) cells. This imbalance directly influences systemic inflammatory tone and may affect atherosclerotic plaque stability. Additionally, specific microbial metabolites are capable of activating intracellular signaling complexes such as the NLRP3 inflammasome. This activation triggers the maturation and release of potent pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), thereby amplifying vascular inflammation and contributing to disease progression [27, 28].

Finally, the considerable predictive value of combined gut microbiota detection for CAD prognosis likely integrates the aforementioned pathways: microbiota dysbiosis collectively drives disease progression and adverse outcomes by affecting metabolite profiles, activating immune-inflammatory networks, and exacerbating endothelial injury and lipid deposition.

Limitations and future perspectives

This study has several important limitations that must be acknowledged:

Culture-based methodology

The most significant limitation is our use of traditional culture-based methods, which target only a small, pre-selected fraction of the gut microbiota. This approach fails to capture the immense diversity of the gut microbiome, including uncultivable bacteria, archaea, fungi, and viruses. Therefore, our findings are strictly confined to the five bacterial groups we measured and cannot be generalized to the entire gut ecosystem. Future studies should employ high-throughput sequencing technologies (e.g., 16 S rRNA or metagenomic sequencing) to provide a comprehensive view of the gut microbiota.

Incomplete control for confounding

While we adjusted for several key confounders, data on inflammatory markers (e.g., hs-CRP), frailty indices, dietary habits, and detailed medication adherence were not consistently available. The associations we report may be influenced by these unmeasured factors. Additionally, many medications (especially statins and PPIs) are known to modulate the gut microbiota, and we could not fully account for their effects.

Prognostic model limitations

Our prognostic analysis, while improved with multivariable adjustment, was not internally validated (e.g., via bootstrapping) and lacks external validation. The high AUC of 0.925 observed in the unadjusted analysis likely reflects overfitting and should be interpreted with considerable caution. The true predictive performance of these microbiota markers remains to be determined in independent cohorts.

Single-center design and limited follow-up

As a single-center retrospective study with a relatively short 6-month follow-up, our findings may not be generalizable and did not allow for robust time-to-event analyses.

Age range

Our inclusion of patients aged 60–64, while increasing sample size, deviates from the standard elderly definition of ≥ 65 years. Age-related changes in microbiota composition may have influenced our results, although we attempted to address this by including age as a covariate.

Single timepoint assessment

Gut microbiota was assessed at baseline only, which does not capture temporal variability or changes over time.

Future research should utilize animal models with fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), integrated multi-omics analyses, and prospective multicenter trials with longer follow-up to validate our findings, establish causality, and elucidate specific molecular mechanisms. Comprehensive metagenomic and metabolomic approaches will help identify novel therapeutic targets.

Conclusion

In conclusion, elderly patients with CAD exhibit characteristic alterations in the abundance of selected cultivable gut bacteria, characterized by reduced levels of Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Bacteroides and elevated levels of Enterobacteriaceae and Enterococcus. These microbial alterations correlate with disease severity and lipid metabolic status. The combined assessment of these bacterial taxa demonstrates an independent association with short-term prognosis; however, these findings are preliminary and require validation in larger, independent cohorts using more comprehensive sequencing-based approaches.
