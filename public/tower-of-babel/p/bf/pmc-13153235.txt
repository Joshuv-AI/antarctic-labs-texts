Functional protein design and enhancement with ontology reinforcement iteration
Authors: Bing He, Chenchen Qin, Yu Zhao, Long-Kai Huang, Zihan Wu, Fang Wang, Fandi Wu, Fan Yang, Jianhua Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe discrepancy between computational modeling and experimental performance remains a major challenge in protein engineering. We present ORI (Ontology Reinforcement Iteration), a scalable framework integrating ontology-conditioned decoding with reinforcement learning from experimental feedback (RLWF). ORI leverages structured ontologies as semantic prompts to impose multi-level constraints, enabling controllable and interpretable protein generation. A closed-loop iterative workflow—comprising generation, experimental measurement, and model updating—enables continuous optimization under real-world objectives. We demonstrate ORI’s practical applicability through diverse tasks, including enzymatic activity optimization, thermal stability enhancement, and multifunctional protein engineering. Using this framework, we engineer variants with substantial improvements over natural baselines, such as a lysozyme with 100-fold higher activity, a chitinase stable at 85 °C, and dual-function enzymes exhibiting both lysozyme and chitinase activities. These results establish ORI as a robust technical platform for efficient, multi-objective protein engineering in real-world experimental settings.Subject terms: Machine learning, Protein design, Enzymes, Computational platforms and environments, Bioinformatics

Introduction

Proteins are central to nearly all biological processes, including catalysis, signaling, transport, and structural organization1. The ability to engineer proteins with targeted functional, structural, and physicochemical properties is therefore a foundational challenge in molecular biology, biotechnology, and bioengineering2. Progress in this area has broad implications, ranging from basic biological research to applications in therapeutics, diagnostics, and industrial biocatalysis. However, achieving reliable functional protein engineering remains difficult due to the complex, nonlinear relationships among protein sequence, structure, and function.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence have substantially expanded the scope of computational protein engineering. Structure prediction models have enabled accurate inference of protein folds from sequence alone3–5, while generative models have demonstrated the ability to produce protein sequences conditioned on structural or functional constraints6. Contemporary approaches now span diverse technical paradigms. Diffusion-based models such as DPLM7 and EvoDiff8 operate directly in sequence space to capture evolutionary constraints, whereas DiMA9 and MeMDLM10 leverage latent or discrete diffusion to improve controllability, enabling applications including membrane protein design. Score-based models such as ProteinSGM11 provide an alternative generative formulation, while large-scale protein language models—including ProGen312 and ESM-C13—demonstrate the benefits of scale and the integration of structural information. Additional frameworks, such as FusOn-pLM14, PepTune15, and AMP-Diffusion16, further illustrate the growing applicability of generative models to peptide and protein engineering tasks. Parallel developments in discrete flow matching methods—such as Dirichlet, Fisher, and Gumbel Softmax flow matching17—have enabled guided and conditional generation in discrete sequence spaces. Collectively, these approaches exemplify the prevailing paradigm of guided discrete sampling in generative biology.

Related progress has also been achieved in RNA generative modeling. Systems such as Rinalmo18 and EvoFlow-RNA19 achieve state-of-the-art performance in RNA sequence design and structure prediction, underscoring the generality of generative frameworks across biomolecular modalities. Together, these advances reflect a broader trend: generative models are increasingly expected not only to capture sequence–structure relationships, but also to support functional optimization under experimentally relevant constraints.

Despite these advances, substantial challenges remain in translating computational designs into experimentally functional proteins. Many existing methods perform well when strong structural priors or templates are available, such as in regular folds or well-characterized binding motifs. However, they often struggle in settings where functional differences arise from subtle sequence variations among similar structures, or where experimental objectives—such as enzymatic activity, stability, or expression—are sparse, noisy, or non-differentiable. As a result, protein engineering workflows often rely on resource-intensive trial-and-error experimentation, resulting in low success rates and limited scalability.

To incorporate functional optimization signals more directly, several strategies have been explored. Guided sampling approaches, including diffusion and flow matching, offer stability and sample efficiency when the objective is differentiable or can be approximated. However, these methods are limited when the target objective is sparse, noisy, or non-differentiable—such as enzyme activity, expression levels, thermostability, or other experimental phenotypes. Reinforcement learning (RL) combined with experimental assays has emerged as an alternative: pioneering studies from Baker’s group and others have shown the feasibility of iterative design guided by experimental feedback, and follow-up frameworks (e.g., EVOLVEpro) have adopted similar principles in active learning or small-sample settings20,21. While RL is attractive for directly optimizing non-differentiable objectives, existing approaches often suffer from instability and low sample efficiency in high-dimensional sequence spaces, and most prior implementations remain constrained to specific scaffolds or narrowly defined targets.

Building on these insights, we introduce ORI (Ontology Reinforcement Iteration), a general-purpose and scalable computational framework for functional protein engineering. ORI unifies ontology-conditioned decoding with reinforcement learning from experimental feedback (RLWF) in a closed-loop system. Structured ontologies serve as semantic prompts to encode functional, structural, and property-level constraints, enabling controllable, interpretable protein generation. Reinforcement learning allows direct optimization of sparse or non-differentiable experimental objectives, while an iterative workflow—comprising sequence generation, experimental measurement, reward assignment, ontology refinement, and model updating—enables the co-evolution of both the generative model and its functional representations. By integrating guided discrete sampling with closed-loop experimental optimization, ORI addresses key limitations of prior methods and supports robust, multi-objective protein engineering.

In this study, we evaluate ORI as a technical platform through multiple protein engineering tasks, including the optimization of enzymatic activity, thermal stability, and multifunctionality. Using this framework, we generated and experimentally validated hundreds of protein variants across diverse enzyme families. These results demonstrate that ORI enables efficient exploration and optimization of protein functional landscapes under real-world experimental constraints, highlighting its potential as a general framework for functional protein engineering.

Results

The framework of ORI

ORI is a closed-loop computational framework for functional protein engineering, consisting of three tightly integrated components (Fig. 1). First, ORI includes a protein generation agent (PGA), a self-evolving language model that controls sequence generation under user-specified functional and property constraints. Second, a function-aware protein generation model (PGM) produces candidate protein sequences by following structured ontology guidance provided by the agent. Third, a unified sequence model (USM) evaluates generated sequences based on predicted quality, structural consistency, and functional properties prior to experimental testing. ORI further incorporates reinforcement learning from experimental feedback, enabling iterative refinement of model parameters based on wet-lab assay results. Through this closed-loop optimization process, the framework continuously improves its ability to navigate protein functional landscapes and to support efficient, high-quality protein engineering across diverse experimental objectives.

The ORI computational framework for protein engineering.

ORI integrates three components that operate in a closed loop: the protein design agent (PDA), the protein generation model (PGM), and the unified sequence models (USMs). PDA translates user-defined functional and property-level requirements into structured ontology prompts, which condition the generation process in PGM. PGM is a function-aware large language model (3 billion parameters) trained on an ontology-annotated representative protein dataset comprising approximately 60 billion tokens, enabling controllable protein sequence generation under semantic constraints. The generated sequences are evaluated by USMs, which assess structural confidence, functional relevance, and other properties to support quality control and candidate selection for wet-lab assays. Experimental results are incorporated through reinforcement learning with experimental feedback, allowing iterative refinement of the generative process. Together, these components form a scalable framework that couples computational generation with experimental validation.

Inspired by Gorilla22 and Toolformer23, we developed a self-evolving PDA using a large language model (LLM) with 2 billion (2B) parameters, enabling it to effectively leverage both internal and external tools to generate high-quality proteins with desired biological functions (Methods). Firstly, PDA takes the user’s natural-language description of protein function and transforms it into structured ontology prompts to generate proteins using PGM. These ontology prompts are tag terms that reflect species, structures, functions, and other essential features guiding the protein creation using the PGM. Following this, USM models predict the properties of the generated proteins, including thermostability, solubility, and functionality. Then, ORI filters out high-quality proteins that may match the user’s requirements. Users can select proteins from high-quality ones for further wet lab validation. In ORI, the process of filtering proteins based on predicted characteristics is called the quality control pipeline. After the wet-lab assays, ORI incorporates feedback from these experiments to update itself via reinforcement learning, thereby improving protein design.

Beyond PDA, another essential component of ORI is the function-aware PGM with 3 billion (3B) parameters. PGM is an autoregressive large language model, which adopts a decoder-only architecture similar to that of LLaMA serial models24. The primary obstacle in developing PGM is enabling the model to learn the associations between protein sequences, structures, and biological functions. This is a complex task, as over three billion years of evolution have produced a rich resource of functional proteins with highly diverse and complex protein sequences and structures. To tackle this issue, we built a unified protein ontology system that integrates protein structures, biological functions, and other characteristics using information from UniProt25, SCOPe26, and published literature27,28, etc. We used this protein ontology system to create a well-annotated representative protein dataset. Over 166 million representative protein sequences, identified following the UniRef90 rule29, are annotated with ontology terms encompassing species, structures, functions, thermostability, solubility, etc. We used this well-annotated dataset to train the protein generation model. Thereafter, we constructed a large protein-generation model with 3B parameters and extensive training. This thorough training of the model aimed to decipher the generalized rules that connect protein sequences, structures, and biological functions. With this, the PGM is now prepared to generate proteins with the guidance of PDA.

ORI employs the USM model to predict the characteristics of proteins generated by the PGM, evaluating whether the resulting protein satisfies the user’s requirements (Fig. 2a). We designed USM, a unified biological language model, to address the limitations of current models in handling both single-sequence (SS) and multiple-sequence alignment (MSA) data, and to provide a unified representation of various biomolecular forms, including DNA, RNA, and protein. USM model is featured by specially designed Transformer blocks, which include Column Attention, Fast Row Attention, and SwiGLU Activation (Supplementary Fig. 1). This design allows the model to efficiently extract sparse features from multiple sequence alignment (MSA) data, leading to improved computational efficiency and parameter scalability. Furthermore, the USM model employs a dynamic masking and mixed-sequence pre-training method, which enhances the model’s ability to learn from MSA data and improves its performance on various downstream tasks. Compared to previous models, USM is superior in computational efficiency (Supplementary Table 1) and accuracy (Supplementary Table 2). USM with 100 million parameters (USM 100 M) enables 2,939 Giga Floating-point Operations Per Second (GFLOPS) in model training, over 100 times that of ESM230 with the same parameters (Supplementary Table 1). Moreover, USM 100 M achieved comparable or better performance than ESM2 with 15 billion parameters (ESM 15B) in predicting protein characteristics using benchmark datasets, such as fluorescence31, stability32, antibiotic resistance33 and metal ion binding34 (Supplementary Table 2). In this study, the USM model is pre-trained using UniRef90 protein sequences in the unified MSA form. Thereafter, we fine-tuned the USM model to predict protein characteristics, such as thermostability, solubility, signal peptide types, etc. Using USM-predicted protein features, users can select candidate proteins for further investigation.

Wet lab experiments are vital for advancing protein generative models as they provide valuable biological insights into the quality of the generated proteins. Researchers analyze these experimental outcomes to develop heuristic methods that refine or optimize the models, thereby enhancing their performance. However, this approach heavily relies on the subjective expertise of individual researchers, leading to potential variability and limited reproducibility. Taking inspiration from the success of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), such as iterative Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), in improving language models35,36, we propose an automated framework called Reinforcement Learning from Wet-lab Feedback (RLWF), for iterative optimization of PDA and PGM models in ORI. RLWF leverages feedback from wet-lab experiments, such as successful and unsuccessful protein expression, to iteratively optimize these models. To address the common issue of length bias in RLHF, where the optimized models tend to generate excessively long sequences, we propose the R-DPO loss function in RLWF. This loss function incorporates a length regularizer, ensuring that the optimized model generates protein sequences of appropriate lengths. RLWF systematically increases the likelihood of producing high-quality protein designs that possess the desired functionality and characteristics, streamlining the model improvement process and reducing reliance on subjective judgment.

Engineering proteins with enzyme-like activity

To evaluate the capability of ORI in functional protein engineering, we apply the framework to proteins with enzyme-like activity. Enzymes represent a broad and well-characterized class of functional proteins, making them a suitable testbed for assessing the effectiveness and robustness of the ORI pipeline. Using ontology prompts provided by the protein design agent (PDA), the protein generation model (PGM) generates 10,000 candidate protein sequences. These candidates are subsequently evaluated using ORI’s quality control pipeline, which integrates functional domain identification, sequence and structural similarity analysis relative to known enzymes, and property prediction using the unified sequence model (USM) (Fig. 2a).

Here, sequence and structural identities quantify the similarity between generated proteins and their closest natural counterparts that perform the same function. The generated proteins exhibit high overall similarity to known enzymes, with a median sequence identity of 0.96 (Fig. 2b), reflecting the effective training and calibration of the PGM. To focus the analysis on engineered variants rather than exact reproductions, sequences identical to known proteins are excluded from downstream evaluation.

Notably, analysis of the remaining candidates reveals a weak correlation between sequence identity and structural identity (Pearson’s r = 0.072; Fig. 2c). Proteins with relatively low sequence identity (approximately 0.5) can nonetheless maintain high structural similarity to known enzymes (TM-score > 0.8; Fig. 2d). Conversely, among proteins with high sequence similarity ( > 0.8), two distinct structural regimes emerge (Fig. 2e): one corresponding to highly conserved structures (TM-score ≈ 0.9), and another exhibiting substantially lower structural similarity (TM-score ≈ 0.4). These observations indicate that neither sequence similarity nor structural similarity alone is sufficient to assess the quality of engineered proteins. Accordingly, ORI’s quality control pipeline jointly incorporates both sequence- and structure-based metrics to identify reliable candidates for experimental validation.

In addition to structural evaluation, ORI employs USM models to perform in silico functional assessment. Functional domains serve as key indicators of enzymatic activity. In the raw generation output, 45.89% of candidates contain the desired functional domain. After filtering through the USM-based quality control pipeline, this proportion increases to 98.91% (Fig. 2f), demonstrating the effectiveness of the USM models in functional feature prediction.

We further benchmark ORI and its core generative module, PGM, against recent state-of-the-art protein generation methods, including ProteinSGM11, ProGen312, and EVO237, representing structure-based, sequence-based, and DNA-based generative paradigms, respectively. For each method, 1,000 proteins are generated and evaluated using structural confidence and functional relevance metrics. ORI achieves a substantially higher proportion of proteins with strong structural confidence, as measured by pLDDT thresholds of 70 and 90 (Supplementary Fig. 2), as well as a higher fraction of proteins containing the target functional domains (Supplementary Fig. 3). Comparison between ORI and PGM further highlights the benefit of integrating PDA-guided generation with USM-based evaluation, underscoring the advantage of ORI’s unified framework for functional protein engineering.

Successful protein expression remains a critical step in translating computational designs into experimentally testable candidates38. From the proteins passing quality control, we randomly select 100 candidates with predicted lysozyme activity for experimental validation (Methods). Lysozyme (EC 3.2.1.17) catalyzes the hydrolysis of β-(1–4) glycosidic linkages in bacterial cell walls. The selected proteins are expressed in eukaryotic HEK293 cells, prokaryotic E. coli, and a cell-free expression system. Overall expression success rates are comparable to those reported for natural lysozymes in prior studies6,38. Expression levels vary across systems, with 16.19% of proteins exceeding 0.1 mg/ml in HEK293 cells (Fig. 2g), compared to 40% and 50% in E. coli and the cell-free system, respectively. In addition to higher expression yields, the cell-free system provides a host-independent and highly reproducible environment that facilitates rapid, parallel evaluation of engineered proteins. Proteins exhibiting high expression in cellular systems consistently achieve high expression in the cell-free system, making it well-suited for standardized assessment within ORI’s closed-loop experimental workflow. Accordingly, the cell-free system is used as the default expression platform in subsequent experiments.

Finally, we assess lysozyme activity for 10 randomly selected proteins expressed using the cell-free system (Methods). These proteins display sequence identities ranging from 0.53 to 0.82 relative to their closest natural homologs (Supplementary Table 3). Six proteins exhibit detectable lysozyme activity at pH values above 6, and two variants, TX-L2 and TX-L6, show higher activity than natural hen lysozyme (Fig. 2H). At pH 7.5, TX-L6 reaches a median activity of 1,598 U/mg, approximately 2.7-fold higher than the natural enzyme under the same conditions. The protein sequence of TX-L6 is shown in Supplementary Table 4.

Closed-Loop Protein Engineering with Experimental Feedback

As the ORI training data do not include protein expression levels or enzymatic activity measurements, proteins generated by the initial ORI model exhibit expression and activity profiles comparable to those of natural proteins in wet-lab assays. This behavior is consistent with expectations for generative models trained primarily on sequence and structural information. We therefore investigated whether incorporating experimental feedback could systematically improve design outcomes within the ORI framework.

To this end, we collected protein expression and enzyme activity data from the initial wet-lab assays and used reinforcement learning from wet-lab feedback (RLWF) to update the ORI system, with optimization primarily focused on the protein generation module (PGM) (Fig. 3a, Methods). Using the optimized model, we generated an additional set of 10,000 proteins under the same functional specifications as before.

In silico evaluation revealed a clear shift toward improved structural confidence in proteins generated by the optimized model. Specifically, the median pLDDT score increased from approximately 75 in the original model to around 85 after RLWF optimization (Fig. 3b). As pLDDT is commonly used as a proxy for structural stability and expression robustness39, this shift suggests that experimental feedback enabled the model to preferentially generate sequences with improved structural integrity, which is expected to facilitate downstream expression and handling.

To assess whether these in silico improvements translated into experimental gains, we conducted wet-lab expression assays on 80 randomly selected lysozyme-like proteins, including 20 designs from the original model and 60 from the optimized model. Proteins were grouped by functional domain family—glucosaminidase, transglycosylase, and phage lysozyme—to control for family-dependent expression differences. SDS–PAGE analysis of proteins expressed in the cell-free system revealed consistently higher expression levels for designs generated by the optimized model across all three families (Fig. 3c–e). Median expression levels increased from 0.061 to 0.168 mg/ml for glucosaminidase, from 0.099 to 0.170 mg/ml for transglycosylase, and from 0.109 to 0.207 mg/ml for phage lysozyme (Fig. 3f). These results indicate that RLWF effectively improved expression-related properties across diverse enzyme families.

We next examined whether RLWF also enhanced enzymatic function. Twenty proteins with robust expression in the cell-free system were randomly selected for lysozyme activity assays, and their activities were compared against TX-L6, a high-performing design from the original model. Thirteen of the twenty proteins exhibited higher lysozyme activity than TX-L6, with the top-performing design, TX-RL15, showing a 66-fold increase relative to TX-L6 (Fig. 3g). Given that TX-L6 itself exhibits higher activity than hen egg-white lysozyme, TX-RL15 corresponds to an overall improvement exceeding two orders of magnitude relative to the natural baseline.

To place these results in context, we further benchmarked ORI-generated lysozymes against T4 lysozyme (T4L), a well-characterized enzyme with high catalytic efficiency40. While T4L consistently outperformed hen lysozyme across pH conditions (Supplementary Fig. 4), a substantial fraction of ORI-generated lysozymes exceeded T4L activity under the same assay conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5). Together, these results demonstrate that experimental feedback can be effectively integrated into ORI to iteratively bias the generative model toward sequences with improved expression and enzymatic performance.

Engineering thermostable enzyme-like proteins

Thermostability is a critical determinant of enzyme performance in industrial and biomaterial applications, where elevated temperatures can enhance reaction rates but also accelerate protein denaturation. Chitinase (EC 3.2.1.14), an enzyme widely used in chitin degradation, typically loses activity at temperatures near or below 60 °C, which limits its practical deployment41.

We investigated whether ORI could be used to design enzymes with tunable thermostability while retaining catalytic function. Ontology terms related to thermostability are included in the ORI training corpus (Fig. 4a), allowing the model to associate sequence features with thermal resilience. Using PDA, we generated ontology-conditioned prompts and produced thousands of chitinase-like proteins. Thermostability was assessed using a USM model trained to predict melting temperature, which achieves a Pearson correlation of 0.818 between predicted and experimental values on an independent test set (Fig. 4b).

When no thermostability constraint was applied, the predicted melting temperature distribution of ORI-generated proteins closely matched that of natural chitinases, with a median of 48.3 °C (Fig. 4c). In contrast, conditioning generation on increasing target melting temperatures resulted in systematic upward shifts in predicted thermostability (Fig. 4d), demonstrating controllable modulation of this property within the ORI framework.

We benchmarked ORI against ProteinSGM11, ProGen312, and EVO237 in generating thermostable proteins, and against ProteinMPNN39 and Rosetta42 in thermostability optimization. ORI consistently produced proteins with higher predicted melting temperatures while requiring substantially lower computational cost (Supplementary Figs. 6, 7), highlighting an advantageous trade-off between performance and efficiency.

For experimental validation, we selected 20 chitinase-like proteins with predicted melting temperatures above 60 °C. Eighteen were successfully expressed in the cell-free system, and nine exhibited measurable chitinase activity. All nine retained full activity after 3 min at 60 °C, and five retained over 90% activity after one hour. Further assays showed that three proteins maintained high activity at 70 °C, and one design, TX-SC2, retained full activity after prolonged incubation at 85 °C, with partial activity even at 95 °C (Fig. 4e, f).

Comparisons with selected naturally thermostable chitinases, including OfChth, SmChiB, and SmChiC from Ostrinia furnacalis and Serratia marcescens43–45, show that, under the same experimental conditions, ORI-designed chitinases retain substantial enzymatic activity at 85 °C, whereas the tested natural enzymes exhibit little or no detectable activity (Supplementary Fig. 8). These results demonstrate that ORI can extend the operational temperature range of functional chitinases while preserving catalytic activity.

Engineering proteins with multi-enzyme activity

We next evaluated whether ORI could be applied to the design of multifunctional enzymes. Lysozyme and chitinase share related catalytic mechanisms and structural motifs but typically exhibit limited cross-reactivity. Natural dual-function enzymes, such as Hevamine A, generally display reduced activity relative to specialized monofunctional enzymes, making simultaneous optimization of multiple activities a challenging design problem46.

During initial PGM training, both monofunctional and multifunctional enzymes were included (Fig. 5a). RLWF was subsequently applied using experimental activity data from lysozyme and chitinase assays, enabling the model to associate sequence features with quantitative functional outcomes. Using PDA-conditioned prompts, we generated 10,000 candidate proteins with potential dual activity. Designs supported by three independent USM models with high confidence scores were retained, yielding 426 candidates for further analysis.

From these, 100 proteins were randomly selected for experimental validation. Sixty-five were successfully expressed in the cell-free system, of which 25 exhibited dual-enzyme activity (Fig. 5b). Sequence analysis revealed that while overall sequence identity to natural enzymes was moderate, conservation was higher within catalytic regions, suggesting preservation of essential functional motifs alongside broader sequence diversification (Fig. 5c).

Dual-activity proteins were further compared to Hevamine A. Functional-region sequence identities were substantially lower than those of monofunctional designs, indicating that ORI did not simply recapitulate known dual-function architectures but instead explored alternative combinations of catalytic features (Fig. 5d).

Proteins were ranked using a weighted score integrating both activities. The top-ranked design, TX-ME, adopts a TIM-barrel fold common to both enzyme families (Fig. 5e). In comparative assays, TX-ME outperformed both monofunctional enzymes (T4 lysozyme and GH18 chitinase) and the natural dual-function enzyme Hevamine A in both activities (Fig. 5f), demonstrating that ORI can balance and enhance multiple functional objectives within a single protein scaffold.

Discussion

Recent advances in structure-based artificial intelligence have substantially expanded the capabilities of computational protein engineering, enabling the generation of protein sequences under increasingly complex structural and functional constraints. Despite this progress, a central bottleneck remains: many existing approaches depend heavily on human expertise to specify appropriate structural priors or design templates for a desired function. This reliance limits scalability and makes it difficult to optimize experimentally relevant properties that are sparse, noisy, or not easily captured by differentiable objectives.

In this work, we introduce ORI, a closed-loop computational framework that addresses these limitations by integrating ontology-guided generation with reinforcement learning from experimental feedback. Rather than treating protein design as a one-shot generation problem, ORI formulates protein engineering as an iterative optimization process. Its key technical advance lies in the tight coupling of three components—the protein design agent (PDA), function-aware protein generation model (PGM), and unified sequence model (USM)—within a feedback-driven loop. Wet-lab measurements are directly incorporated into the learning process, allowing the framework to self-refine and progressively align generated sequences with experimentally validated functional objectives. This design shifts the role of human expertise from specifying exact structural solutions to defining higher-level functional and property constraints through ontologies.

A practical consideration in ORI is the choice of model scale. We adopt a default parameter size of 3 billion for the protein generation models, which is larger than many prior generative approaches, such as RFdiffusion47 and ProGen6, yet substantially smaller than representative models, such as ESM348. This choice reflects a balance between representational capacity, computational efficiency, and experimental throughput. Our empirical evaluation indicates that models at this scale effectively capture sequence–structure–function relationships while remaining tractable for iterative training with experimental feedback. Importantly, ORI does not rely on a single large model; instead, it leverages the complementary roles of PDA, PGM, and USM to achieve robust closed-loop optimization, demonstrating that architectural coordination can be as critical as parameter scaling in functional protein engineering.

The experimental results presented in this study demonstrate the practical applicability of ORI across multiple protein engineering tasks, including the optimization of enzymatic activity, thermal stability, and multifunctionality. The successful experimental validation of hundreds of protein variants highlights the framework’s ability to efficiently explore and refine functional protein landscapes under real-world constraints. Improvements in expression, catalytic activity, and thermostability observed in lysozyme and chitinase variants illustrate how closed-loop feedback enables systematic performance gains without reliance on predefined structural templates. These results underscore the potential of ORI as a general engineering platform rather than a task-specific design tool.

A notable demonstration of ORI’s flexibility is its ability to support the engineering of proteins with multiple enzymatic activities, as exemplified by the TX-ME protein, which exhibits both lysozyme and chitinase activities. While the detailed molecular mechanisms underlying this multifunctionality remain to be investigated, this result suggests that ORI can navigate complex functional spaces in which multiple objectives must be satisfied simultaneously. From a framework perspective, this capability highlights the advantage of combining ontology-guided constraints with experimental feedback to coordinate multiple functional requirements within a single protein sequence.

Unlike classical fragment assembly or template-based approaches, ORI employs a generative language model that learns continuous representations of the sequence–structure–function landscape. Ontology-conditioned decoding guides generation toward functionally relevant regions of sequence space while preserving structural plausibility, without explicitly reusing structural templates or fragments. This design reduces template bias and mitigates information leakage, enabling broader generalization across protein families. As such, ORI represents a data-driven and extensible alternative to traditional protein engineering pipelines.

Sequence diversity is an important consideration for generative protein models, and many existing approaches emphasize broad exploration of sequence space. ORI is instead designed for goal-directed protein engineering, where generation is guided by explicit functional objectives and iteratively refined through experimental feedback. Under this setting, functional constraints naturally focus the search on a functionally enriched subset of sequence space rather than maximizing global diversity. Accordingly, our evaluation prioritizes functional performance and optimization efficiency, which more directly reflect ORI’s intended use in practical protein engineering.

Despite its demonstrated capabilities, ORI has several limitations. Domain-specific systems—such as PepTune15 for peptide optimization, UniPTM49 and PTM-Mamba50 for post-translational modification–aware modeling, and DPAC51 for DNA-binding protein engineering—incorporate specialized inductive biases that are not yet explicitly modeled within ORI. In addition, protein classes characterized by high conformational flexibility, extensive post-translational modifications, or intrinsic disorder, including therapeutic peptides, antibodies, and viral glycoproteins, remain challenging. Extending ORI to accommodate these complexities and systematically benchmarking it against specialized frameworks represent important directions for future work.

In summary, ORI advances functional protein engineering by reframing protein design as a closed-loop, experimentally grounded optimization problem. By integrating ontology-guided generation with reinforcement learning from wet-lab feedback, ORI provides a scalable computational framework that bridges computational modeling and experimental validation. This approach lays the foundation for more robust, efficient, and generalizable protein engineering workflows, with broad implications for biotechnology, medicine, and industrial applications.

Methods

Preparing ontology datasets for model training

Representative protein sequences

The UniRef90 and UniRef50 datasets were collected from the UniProt database29. The UniRef90 dataset, containing 166 million representative protein sequences, was created by clustering UniRef100 sequences that are 11 or more residues long, ensuring that each cluster contains sequences with at least 90% identity and 80% overlap with the longest sequence in the cluster. The UniRef100 sequences consolidate identical sequences and sub-fragments of 11 or more residues from any organism into a single entry, displaying a representative protein sequence along with the accession numbers of all merged entries and links to the corresponding UniProtKB and UniParc records. UniRef50, containing 59 million representative protein sequences, is formed by clustering UniRef90 seed sequences that share at least 50% sequence identity and 80% overlap with the longest sequence in the cluster. We further annotated these datasets and used them for model training.

Protein Species and Function Annotation

The UniProtKB dataset was downloaded from the UniProt database29. UniProt Knowledgebase (UniProtKB) is a comprehensive repository of functional information on proteins, encompassing amino acid sequences, protein names or descriptions, taxonomic data, widely accepted biological ontologies, classifications, cross-references, and clear indications of annotation quality, including evidence attribution for both experimental and computational data. The UniProtKB dataset includes functional information for approximately 250 million proteins. For our purposes, we extracted taxonomic information, cellular component data, molecular function details, and biological process information from the UniProtKB dataset to annotate representative protein sequences in UniRef90 and UniRef50. The taxonomic lineage information was preserved from the superkingdom level down to the species level. Overall, 107,891 unique taxonomic terms were retained in the annotated UniRef90 and UniRef50 datasets. The functional annotation of proteins includes Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Enzyme Commission (EC) numbers. The annotated UniRef90 and UniRef50 datasets contain 30,391 unique GO function terms and 9217 EC function terms.

Protein structure annotation

The protein structures were collected from PDB52, AlphaFold Protein Structure Database53, and ESM Metagenomic Atlas30. The PDB is an archive of experimentally determined 3D structural data for large biological molecules, such as proteins, DNA, and RNA, which is crucial for research and education in fundamental biology, health, energy, and biotechnology. AlphaFold Protein Structure Database and ESM Metagenomic Atlas archive of protein structure predicted by AlphaFold3 and ESMfold30, respectively. For annotating the UniRef90 and UniRef50 datasets, we used experimentally validated protein structures and high-quality predicted protein structures with pTM > 0.7 and pLDDT > 0.7. These protein structures were then classified into SCOPe Class, Fold, and Superfamily classification for further annotation. The Structural Classification of Proteins—extended (SCOPe) is a database that builds on SCOPe, a manually curated hierarchy of domains from most proteins with known structures, organized according to structural and evolutionary relationships. The classification includes Classes based primarily on secondary structure content and organization, Folds grouping structurally similar superfamilies, and Superfamilies that connect protein families with common functional and structural features inferred from a common evolutionary ancestor.

Protein characteristics annotation

The protein characteristics data were collected from literature, including the thermal stability of 48,000 proteins across 13 species ranging from archaea to humans and covering melting temperatures of 30–90 °C27, 8926 proteins of high thermal stability from Archaea species living in an extreme high-temperature environment54, 58 689 soluble and 70 954 insoluble proteins28, 25693 signal peptides that control protein secretion and translocation in all living organisms55, and protein subcellular localizations collected from SwissProt56 and Human protein atlas57. These data were used to train USM models in the quality control pipeline.

Tokenizer construction

We constructed a byte-pair encoding (BPE)58 tokenizer tailored for protein design, with a vocabulary size of 49,152. To support flexibility in encoding heterogeneous biological inputs, we reserved 200 token IDs as special tokens, which can be dynamically replaced at runtime to represent ontology terms, annotations, or experimental constraints. The reserved token set is drawn from a comprehensive replacement resource pool encompassing digits, Latin and Greek alphabets, punctuation (! “ # $ % & ‘ () * +, -. /:; < = >? @ [\] ^ _ ‘ {|} ~), whitespace characters (space \t \n \r \x0b \x0c), custom tokens ( < CLS > , <PAD > , <MASK > , <EOD > , <:>), and additional domain-specific symbols (£, ¥, «, », ±, ·). This design ensures broad coverage for encoding biological prompt phrases and precise parsing of specialized terminology, such as protein functional annotations and enzyme classification numbers (e.g., EC:1.1.1.181).

During tokenization, each protein sequence was segmented into BPE subword tokens, optimized to capture both short functional motifs (e.g., catalytic triads, signal peptides) and long repetitive patterns (e.g., coiled-coils, tandem repeats). Ontology inputs—including functional annotations, EC numbers, structural constraints, and protein family names—were tokenized separately using the special token pool and subsequently concatenated with the protein tokens to form unified input sequences. In total, this process produced over 60 billion tokens from the integrated protein sequence and annotation datasets, which were used to train the PGM model.

The protein design agent (PDA)

The Protein Design Agent (PDA) serves as the orchestration layer that translates user requirements into ontology prompts and function calls to the Protein Generation Model (PGM). In our protein design agent (PDA), we use a module similar to Gorilla22 and Toolformer23, to convert users’ task requirements to function calls of our models.

Supervised fine-tuning with LoRA

To achieve this goal, we perform low-rank parameter adaption (LoRA)59 on the open-sourced large language model Gemma2-2B60. The model is fine-tuned for 1 epoch with a single pass of all data. The batch size is 32, and the learning rate is 1e-4. The rank for LoRA is 16.

To obtain the training data, we use GPT4 to generate input-output pairs for each function and characteristic, providing it with the input requirements and functionality descriptions for each. The inputs are the user’s requirements and the outputs are the corresponding function and characteristic ontology prompts and model function calls. We also provide high-quality examples generated by human experts as examples to GPT4 for the generation. Finally, we collected 5000 pairs of data generated by GPT4 and used GPT4 to score these generated data based on the scope of requirements and the correctness of ontology prompts and function calls. We further validated the correctness of the data with human experts. We used the top 2000 scoring data pairs for training. In addition, to detect unsupported requirements, we extracted 200 input prompts from the Stanford Alpaca dataset and designated the corresponding outputs as “unsupported”.

The protein generation model (PGM)

To train the protein generation model (PGM), we adopt a prompt-based encoding schema. The sequence begins with ontology tokens that provide conditioning context, introduced at the first position of the sequence. The ontology context typically contains 5–30 tokens, depending on the granularity of the annotation (e.g., “enzyme class EC:1.1.1.181” or “IgG1 antibody heavy chain”). In contrast, protein sequences are substantially longer, ranging from 100 to 1000 tokens after BPE segmentation. The input structure follows the format:

<CLS> Ontology_Tokens <:> Protein_Tokens <EOD>

This design ensures that ontology context acts as a prefix, guiding generation while preserving autoregressive protein modeling. All tokens (protein and ontology) are mapped into a shared embedding space via a learnable embedding layer. Positional encodings are applied to preserve order. Ontology tokens, although distinct in semantics, share the embedding matrix with protein tokens to enable the transfer of contextual information. This joint representation allows the PGM model to attend simultaneously to biological prompts and sequence context during self-attention, enabling it to learn the statistical patterns and relationships among protein sequences, structures and biological functions.

PGM is an autoregressive large language model that shares similarities with the decoder-only architecture used in LLaMA serial models. PGM consists of embedding layers, multiple decoder blocks, and multilayer perceptron (MLP) layers. The embedding layer maps each token to a continuous vector representation, capturing its unique properties. The decoder blocks capture the sequential dependencies between tokens, allowing the model to learn the context and relationships between functions and protein sequences. The decoder block follows the standard transformer architecture62, applies RMSNorm for pre-normalization63 and multi-head attentions, uses the SwiGLU activation function64, and rotary positional embeddings65. The MLP layers consist of a fully connected layer and a normalization layer, which are responsible for generating the final output tokens that represent the generated protein sequences. The 3B PGM model consists of 24 decoder blocks and 24 multi-head attentions, with embedding dimensions of 3072. For evaluation purposes, we also trained PGM models with parameter sizes of 1 billion (1B), 3 billion (3B), and 7 billion (7B). The annotated UniRef90 dataset and UniRef50 were utilized to train these models, respectively. After evaluation, we used the 3B PGM model as the default protein generation model in ORI.

The unified sequence model (USM)

We proposed unified sequence models (USM) to make fast and accurate predictions of protein characteristics. The architecture of the USM is similar to the conventional Encoder Only Transformer model, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. The model takes a multiple sequence alignment (MSA) Token MT ∈ NM×L as input, where N represents the set of non-negative integers, M is the number of sequences (1 for single sequence input), and L is the sequence length. The output is denoted as O ∈ RB×L×D, where R represents the set of real numbers, B is the batch size, and D is the dimension of the embedding. The first layer of the model is the MSA Embedding layer, which embeds the discrete MSA Tokens. The core of the USM model consists of multiple USM Transformer Blocks, which extract features from the MSA. The final output is generated by the language model (LM) head layer. The LM Head layer consists of RMSNorm and Linear layers, which transform the MSA features into language probability outputs. Compared to the MSA Transformer model66, the USM Transformer Block design is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1. It consists of three parts: Column Attention, Fast Row Attention, and SwiGLU Activation. Assuming the MSA features h ∈ RB × row × col × D, where row represents the number of rows in the MSA and col represents the number of columns. The Row Attention is similar to the conventional Multihead Attention and has a computational complexity of O(hdLM2). The Column Attention can be simplified as a transposed form of Row Attention, with a computational complexity of O(hdML2), where h is the number of attention heads, d is the head dimension, M is the number of sequences in the MSA, and L is the sequence length.

Unlike the MSA Transformer Block, the USM Block starts with Column Attention. The USM Block consists of three major parts: Column Attention, Fast Row Attention, and SwiGLU Activation. The Column Attention consists of Transpose and Multi-head Attention layers. We proposed a method called Fast Row Attention (FRA) based on the assumption of redundancy along the Column direction in MSA data, which leads to sparsity in the MSA features h along the same direction. To achieve this, we apply Average Pooling along the Column direction, reducing the MSA features h to h_row ∈ RB×row. This operation speeds up the subsequent Multi-Attention by a factor of col, and the computational complexity of Fast Row Attention is O(hdL2). By leveraging Fast Row Attention, the USM model demonstrates significantly improved speed and the ability to handle larger parameter sizes. SwiGLU Activation is a type of MLP layer that consists of Linear and SiLU activation functions. Additionally, we enhance the model’s ability to extrapolate long sequences by combining Multihead Attention with Rotary Position Embedding (ROPE).

When there is only one sequence in the MSA input, the USM can be considered as a single sequence (SS) model, and the Column Attention degenerates into an MLP. When degenerating into a single sequence model, the USM is similar to the ESM model, highlighting its versatility. The USM model can be used as both an MSA model and an SS model.

The USM model is trained using the Masked Token prediction approach, similar to the conventional BERT model training67. The existing Masked Token method typically uses a fixed masking ratio, such as 0.15, during model training. However, due to the large number of MSA sequences, the tokens along the Column direction are often similar. This results in lower training efficiency with smaller masking ratios. To address this, we propose a dynamic Mask Token method that combines two masking schemes:

Column random masking

This operation randomly masks entire columns. Since learning from column masking is more challenging, we use a smaller proportion, set to 5% in our approach.

Global random masking

This operation applies dynamic masking with varying proportions. It randomly masks individual tokens. The masking ratio decreases gradually during training, starting from 0.3 and decreasing to 0.15.

Iterative optimization with wet lab feedback

Wet-lab experiments are crucial for developing protein-generative models, as they provide valuable biological insights into the functions of the generated proteins. These insights are essential for validating the accuracy and effectiveness of the models. However, it is often observed that only a subset of these proteins exhibit the desired functionalities and activities, while the majority do not show significant activity. To improve the performance of these models, researchers often analyze the outcomes of wet-lab experiments and devise heuristic methods to refine or retrain them. However, this approach relies heavily on individual researchers' subjective expertise, which can introduce variability and limit reproducibility.

To address this challenge, we propose a framework called Reinforcement Learning from Wet-lab Feedback (RLWF). This framework utilizes feedback from wet lab experiments and selectively incorporates generated data to optimize model preference. Doing so systematically increases the likelihood of generating protein sequences with the targeted properties. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has been effectively employed to optimize the output of Large Language Models (LLMs), aligning the generated responses with user preferences68. In this approach, a dataset D containing preference pairs (x, yw, yl)—comprising a prompt x, a preferred response yw, and a less preferred response yl—is utilized. Iterative enhancements of RLHF, such as Iterative DPO35,36,70, have been developed to iteratively refine the model. This method involves generating a set of new preference pairs using the current model, evaluating them via a reward model or human judgment, and optimizing the model on this preference dataset using DPO in each iteration. Our framework adopts this iterative approach. Initially, a series of protein sequences S0 is generated using the current model, prompted by x. The first iteration uses a pre-trained model. Subsequent virtual screening identifies a subset of promising candidates, S1, for wet-lab testing, with the remainder labeled as S2. Post-testing, sequences that exhibit desired functionality are used as preferred responses Sw1. The sequences in S2 with low reference model loss and low pLDDT scores are used as unpreferred responses Sl2.

This selection is based on the premise that the model should generate more functionally expressive sequences, while avoiding those preferred by the current model yet less likely to exhibit desired functionality. This method involves generating a set of new preference pairs using the current model, evaluating them via a reward model or human judgment, and optimizing the model on this preference dataset using DPO in each iteration.

Our framework adopts this iterative approach to refine the model for a given prompt x, for example, “<Glucosaminidase><temperature90><:>”. In the first iteration, both the reference model and the current model are initialized with the pre-trained model. The reference model is fixed.

At the start of each iteration, the current model is used to generate protein sequences by repeatedly feeding it the prompt x. This process is repeated 10,000 times to obtain a dataset, S₀, comprising 10,000 sequences. Virtual screening is then performed to identify a subset of promising candidates, S₁ (typically ~100 sequences), for wet-lab validation. The remaining 9,900 sequences form the set S₂.

For the sequences in S₂, we estimate their perplexity using the reference model and select the top K sequences with the highest perplexity to form the set S2pplx. We also evaluate their predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) scores and select the K sequences with the lowest scores to form the set S2pLDDT. The intersection of S2pplx and S2pLDDT is used as the set of unpreferred responses, Sl. The value of K is chosen such that the size of Sl is greater than 100; in practice, K is set to 500. The preferred responses set, Sw, is defined as S₁.

We then construct the training dataset D using all possible pairs of preferred (yw ∈ Sw) and unpreferred (yl ∈ Sl) responses. This selection is based on the premise that the model should generate more functionally expressive sequences, while avoiding those preferred by the current model yet less likely to exhibit desired functionality.

The model is fine-tuned using the R-DPO loss for one epoch over the dataset D. The updated model is then used as the current model in the next iteration to generate new test sequences, following the same process to construct the preference dataset and optimize the model. The reference model in each iteration is a fixed copy of the model updated in the previous iteration. The model loss of the generated sequences is estimated by the auto-regressive loss of the reference model.

Benchmark with baseline methods

To comprehensively evaluate ORI, we benchmarked it against a wide range of recent state-of-the-art models, spanning conditioned protein generation and thermostability optimization.

ProteinSGM

ProteinSGM is a score-based diffusion model that produces native-like protein structures11. We used the crystal structure of hen egg-white lysozyme (PDB ID: 132 L) and T4 lysozyme (PDB ID: 216 L) as a template, where specific regions were masked to provide partial structural information as input. ProteinSGM was then used to generate 1000 conditional 6D coordinates from the ProteinSGM model, including Cβ–Cβ distances, ω and θ dihedral angles, and ϕ planar angles. These coarse-grained 6D representations were then converted into full-atom protein backbones using Rosetta MinMover42, which performed constrained backbone minimization to ensure physically realistic geometries.

ProGen3

ProGen3 is an autoregressive protein language model designed for sequence generation12. We provided the sequences of hen egg-white lysozyme (PDB ID: 132 L) and T4 lysozyme (PDB ID: 216 L) as conditioning contexts. Using these templates, we employed the ProGen3-3B model to generate amino acid sequences in both N-to-C (forward) and C-to-N (reverse) directions, conditioned on the functional and sequence constraints. A total of 1000 protein sequences were sampled, ensuring coverage of diverse sequence space.

Evo2

Evo 2 is a large-scale biological foundation model trained on 9.3 trillion DNA bases that learns genome-wide biological features directly from DNA sequence, enabling accurate prediction of functional variants and controllable genome-scale sequence generation37. For Evo2-based conditional protein generation, we first designed functional prompts according to the model’s requirements, encoding structural and biochemical features of target proteins. Specifically, we constructed text-based prompts describing hen egg-white lysozyme (PDB ID: 132 L) and T4 lysozyme (PDB ID: 216 L), including their structural class, enzymatic activity, and functional attributes. Conditioned on these prompts, Evo2 was used to generate 1000 DNA sequences for each protein, ensuring that the outputs satisfied the specified biochemical and functional constraints. The generated DNA sequences were subsequently translated into protein sequences, and incomplete or invalid coding regions were filtered out before downstream evaluation.

ProteinMPNN

ProteinMPNN is a graph-based generative model for protein sequence design conditioned on backbone structures39. For thermostability optimization, we first predicted the backbone structure of each target protein using AlphaFold23, which served as the input scaffold. ProteinMPNN was then applied to redesign the amino acid sequences on this backbone. For each target, we generated 1000 redesigned variants and selected the top 100 candidates based on the predicted stability score. The redesigned sequences were further filtered by predicted folding quality (pLDDT > 70) to ensure structural plausibility.

Rosetta

Rosetta is a widely used computational platform for macromolecular modeling, design, and analysis, integrating physics-based energy functions with knowledge-based statistical potentials42. For Rosetta-based thermostability optimization, we used the Rosetta ddG and Rosetta FastDesign protocols to perform sequence redesign to improve folding stability. Specifically, starting from the experimentally determined or AlphaFold2-predicted protein structure, iterative rounds of side-chain repacking and backbone minimization were performed, guided by the Rosetta energy function. Each design trajectory generated multiple variants, and 1000 candidate sequences were obtained for each protein. The designed sequences were ranked according to Rosetta’s energy scores, with the top 100 lowest-energy conformations selected as putative thermostable variants.

Evaluating the similarity to natural proteins

To evaluate the similarity of PGM-generated proteins to natural ones, we employed several methods. Firstly, we used MMseqs273 to determine the sequence identity between the generated proteins and natural proteins. Additionally, we predicted the structure of the generated proteins using AlphaFold23, ESMFold30 and USMFold, while assessed the structural identity to natural proteins using Foldseek74. The final structural identity of the generated proteins was determined by calculating the median structural identity among the structures predicted by the three methods. For each generated protein, we compared it to a collection of natural proteins obtained from the UniProt database29. The highest value obtained from these comparisons was considered as the final sequence and structure identity of the generated protein. This comprehensive evaluation allowed us to assess the similarity of the generated proteins to their natural counterparts accurately.

The reaction was performed in a total volume of 100 μL in a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube. The mixture was incubated at 30 °C for 3 h on an orbital shaker at a constant speed of 800 rpm. Following incubation, the crude reaction mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove insoluble debris and pellets. The CFPS supernatant was collected and the target His-tagged proteins were purified using HisPur Ni-NTA Magnetic Beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 88831). Between purification steps, samples underwent buffer exchange into Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS, pH 7.4) using Amicon Ultra-0.5 Centrifugal Filter Units (3 kDa MWCO, Millipore, Cat# UFC5003) to remove imidazole or other salts. The final purified protein was concentrated and exchanged into a storage buffer (PBS with 10% glycerol). The purity and concentration of the samples at each stage, as well as the final product, were assessed by SDS-PAGE and UV absorbance at OD280 using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# ND-2000). Uncropped SDS–PAGE scans corresponding to Fig. 3c–e are provided in the Source Data.

Lysozyme enzyme activity assessment

After protein purification, a small amount of the purified protein is taken and diluted for the assessment of its enzymatic activity. The enzymatic activity is measured using a lysozyme assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#E22013), with hen lysozyme (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#L4919) used as a positive control. The lysozyme activity assay is conducted using Micrococcus lysodeikticus cell walls that are labeled to the extent that their fluorescence is quenched. Lysozyme action can alleviate this quenching effect, resulting in a significant increase in fluorescence that is directly proportional to the lysozyme activity. To perform the assay, 50 µL of a working suspension containing 50 µg/mL of DQ lysozyme substrate is added to each well of a microplate containing the diluted proteins. The reaction mixtures are then incubated for at least 30 min at 37 °C, while being protected from light. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a Varioskan LUX Multimode Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# VLBL00D0) equipped with standard fluorescein filters (Excitation/Emission: 494/518 nm). Enzymatic activity is quantified in Units (U), which is defined as the amount of enzyme required to produce a change in the absorbance at 450 nm of 0.001 units per minute, using a suspension of Micrococcus lysodeikticus as the substrate.

The lysozyme antibacterial enzymatic activity is measured using the Nuclease Activity QC System (SinoBiological, Cat#NE001) by Sino Biological, Inc. Lysozyme digestion of bacteria releases bacterial nucleases. The measurement of nuclease activity in the supernatant allows for the evaluation of bacterial disruption, which is indicative of the lysozyme’s antibacterial enzymatic activity. To perform the assay, Escherichia coli in the logarithmic growth phase is collected by centrifugation. The bacteria are then washed five times with PBS buffer until no nuclease activity is detectable in the supernatant. The bacteria are resuspended and diluted in a resuspension buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 150 mM NaCl). Next, 100 μL of the bacterial suspension, 40 μL of the enzyme to be tested, and 4 μL of the nuclease fluorescent substrate are mixed together. The mixture is incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by the addition of a termination solution to stop the reaction. The resulting fluorescence (Ex/Em: 480/520 nm) was measured to quantify nuclease activity, reflecting the degree of bacterial lysis.

Chitinase enzyme activity assessment

For the evaluation of chitinase-like activity, a small portion of the purified protein was diluted in assay buffer (200 mM sodium phosphate, pH 8.0). The reaction was initiated by mixing 20 µL of the diluted protein with 80 µL of the substrate solution, containing a final concentration of 125 µM 4-Methylumbelliferyl-beta-D-$N,N’,N”-triacetylchitotrioside [Mu-(GlcNAc)3] (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# M5639).The reaction mixtures were incubated in black 96-well microplates (Greiner Bio-One, Cat# 655076) at specified temperatures (e.g., 37 °C or 85 °C) for 3–6 h in a humidified incubator to prevent evaporation. The fluorescence signal of the released product, 4-methylumbelliferone (4-MU), was measured using a Varioskan LUX Multimode Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# VLBL00D0) at an excitation wavelength of 372 nm and an emission wavelength of 445 nm. To quantify the enzymatic activity, a standard curve of 4-methylumbelliferone (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# M1381) was prepared under identical buffer and temperature conditions. One unit of activity (Uc) was defined as the amount of enzyme required to release 1 nmol of 4-methylumbelliferone per minute under the assay conditions.

Naming rules of ORI-engineered proteins

Functional proteins that have been validated through wet lab assays are assigned names consisting of a prefix followed by a number. The prefix consists of two parts separated by a hyphen (“-“). The first part, “TX,” represents Tencent and Unknown, respectively. The second part consists of abbreviations indicating the type of protein. “C” represents Chitinase, “L” represents Lysozyme, “S” represents thermostable proteins, “R” represents proteins refined by RLWF models, and “ME” represents proteins with multi-enzyme function. The number following the prefix is used to differentiate proteins within the same category.
