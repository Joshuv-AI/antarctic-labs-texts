Maternal monocyte-derived IFN-α1 triggers early fetal demise
Authors: Dandan Wang, Wu Li, Kai Jiang, Yue Xu, Ruhua Deng, Xingbo Yang, Huici Jiang, Jiayi Liang, Ke Wei, Jinlong Qin, Yaozu Xiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly fetal demise (EFD), defined as the absence of cardiac activity in a visible fetus, represents a severe form of pregnancy loss with incompletely understood mechanisms. While genetic factors constitute the primary etiology, emerging evidence implicates maternal immune dysregulation in EFD pathogenesis, though the specific cellular and molecular pathways remain undefined.Materials and methodsWe performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from a 20-subject cohort comprising EFD patients, healthy pregnant women, and non-pregnant controls. Parallel murine studies involved GFP-labeled monocyte transfer into E8.5 pregnant mice, with subsequent embryonic viability assessment. Human villous tissues from EFD and control pregnancies were analyzed alongside functional characterization using primary neonatal rat cardiomyocytes exposed to IFN-α1.ResultsScRNA-seq revealed significant CD14+ monocyte accumulation in EFD patients versus controls. In murine models, adoptively transferred maternal monocytes breached the placental barrier, inducing 25.7% embryonic lethality. EFD villous tissues exhibited CD14+ monocyte infiltration with selective IFN-α1 elevation (absent in IFN-α2). Cardiomyocyte functional assays demonstrated IFN-α1-mediated contractile impairment through significant reduction in beating frequency.ConclusionThese findings establish maternal CD14+ monocytes as key mediators of EFD via IFN-α1-dependent cardiac dysfunction, revealing a targetable immune axis for pregnancy preservation. The study provides the first direct evidence linking monocyte trafficking with embryonic viability, offering new therapeutic paradigms for miscarriage prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07230-7.Keywords: Early fetal demise, CD14+ monocytes, IFN-α1

Introduction

The human heart undergoes its initial morphogenesis since the end of the 2nd gestational week, preceding the development of most other organ systems [1]. This early developmental timeline of the human heart reflects the critical requirement for circulatory function to sustain embryo viability. Early fetal demise (EFD), defined as the cessation of embryonic development in the first trimester (< 12 weeks of gestation) with absent cardiac activity, represents one of the most common complications of early pregnancy [2]. Epidemiological studies indicate that approximately 10% of clinically recognized pregnancies end in EFD [3]. Although chromosomal abnormalities such as aneuploidy remain the leading cause of first-trimester pregnancy loss, emerging evidence suggests that maternal factors, including thrombophilia, endocrine dysfunction, and inflammatory responses, also play critical roles [4, 5]. Thus, investigating the underlying mechanisms of EFD, particularly the impact of maternal microenvironmental disturbances on embryonic survival, is essential for improving pregnancy outcomes.

Accumulating evidence reveals that embryonic-derived CCR2− macrophages, a tissue-resident population, which originates from yolk sac, are essential for embryonic cardiac development and repair, while blood monocyte-derived CCR2+ infiltrating macrophages promote inflammation and heart damage [6, 7]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines, TNF-α, which can be released from several immune cells including monocytes, has been reported to disrupt cardiac morphogenesis and functional maturation [8], suggesting that abnormal maternal immune response might contribute to embryonic cardiac arrest. Previous study suggested that type I IFNs is a possible mediator of fetal demise in the context of viral infections [9], but the cellular source of IFNs and underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Moreover, whether and how maternal monocytes affect the development of embryonic heart, further leading to EFD, remains unknown.

To address these problems, we generated a 20-sample human peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset from pregnant and non-pregnant women, among whom two of the pregnant women were diagnosed with EFD. According to the cellular composition changes in different conditions and further experimental validation, we identified the abnormal increase of maternal CD14+ monocytes cells in EFD patients and highlight interferon secreted by maternal CD14+ monocytes could play an essential role in EFD.

Materials and methods

Human studies

We collected peripheral blood from pregnant (n = 9) women in 5–10 weeks of gestation and non-pregnant women (n = 11) with a history of EFD in the Shanghai Fourth People’s Hospital between December 2023 and March 2025. Among the 9 pregnant women, 2 were diagnosed with EFD after blood collection. The ultrasound diagnosis of EFD is based on the absence of either an embryo within a gestational sac or cardiac activity in a visible embryo in 5–10 weeks of gestation. Detailed information of the patients was listed in Table S1.

All patients provided written informed consent for their participation in the study, and the study protocol was approved by the ethical committees of Shanghai Fourth People’s Hospital (No: 2022-042-001).

ScRNA-seq library preparation and sequencing

Peripheral blood was extracted and diluted with Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS). The diluted blood was layered over an equal volume of Ficoll solution (Solarbio, P4350), and centrifugated at 2000 rpm on a horizontal rotor at 20 ℃ for 20 min. The PBMC layer (intermediate white membrane cells) were then washed with PBS, centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min, and re-suspended in PBS. After a second wash, the cells were re-suspended in 1 mL RPMI 1640 medium (Conring, 10-040-CVR) supplemented with 0.04% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA). Single-cell suspension concentration and cell viability were assessed via Luna-FL cell counter (Logos Biosystems, Korea).

Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3ʹ Reagent Kits v3.1 (10×Genomics, PN-1000268) was used for library preparation according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The constructed libraries were sequenced on the Illumina Nova 6000 PE150 platform. The sequencing was provided by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

ScRNA-seq data processing, annotation, and pathway analysis

The FASTQ files were processed and aligned to GRCh38 human reference genome using Cell Ranger software (v5.0.1) from 10x Genomics. Pre-processing of the data was performed via Seurat (v4.0.1) [10]. Cells with less than 500 genes and 1000 UMIs, more than 10% of mitochondrial counts, and doublets identified by DoubletFinder (v2.0.4) [11] were filtered for downstream processing, including normalization, integration using reciprocal PCA (RPCA) [12], dimensional reduction and clustering, according to the Seurat standard workflow. Differential expression genes (DEGs) between clusters, with a log2-transformed fold change (FC) of at least 0.5 and adjusted p value less than 0.05, were identified using Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Cells were annotated by marker gene expression including ten main cell types, B cells (CD79A), CD4 + T cells (CD3D, CD4), CD8 + T cells (CD3D, CD8B), natural killer cells (NK) (NKG7), dendritic cells (DC) (CST3, FCER1A), monocytes (CST3), neutrophils (CST3, FCGR3B), platelets (CST3, PF4), plasma cells (IGHA1), hematopoietic stem cells (HSC) (CD34) and proliferating cells (MKI67).

AUCell (v1.16.0) [13] was performed for pathway analysis with the human Hallmark Gene Set in the Molecular Signatures Database (MsigDB) [14]. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) [15] was performed to compare gene set enrichment between conditions.

Animal studies

The mice participated in this experiment were eight-week-old female C57BL/6J, purchased from Sibeifu Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and were kept in the SPF environment of the animal room of Tongji University (TJ-HB-LAC-2024-16). All the mice were kept in a constant temperature of 21–25 °C and under a 12-hour light and 12-hour dark cycle. After one week of adaptive feeding, the female mice were mated at around 8:00 pm, and the ratio of female to male was 2:1. The female mice were examined at 7:00 am the next day, and if there was a vaginal plug achieved, it was regarded as pregnancy mice and recorded as embryonic day 0.5 (E0.5).

Experimental mice were divided into three groups. Normal pregnancy was set as control, while the abortion group was intraperitoneally injected with 0.5 mg/kg lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (MCE, HY-D1056) solution in E8.5. The next group was intraperitoneally injected in E8.5 and E11.5 at the dose of 2 × 106 monocytes. At the same time that LPS or cell injection treatment was performed in the group, the untreated groups were injected in same volume of normal PBS at the same time. Four mice were included in each group.

Euthanasia was performed using 5% isoflurane anesthesia followed by cervical dislocation. All pregnant mice were put to death in E15.5, and we collected their peripheral blood, spleens, uteroplacental complexes and took photographs of spleens, uteruses without fetuses peeled, and fetuses. We calculated the fetus absorption rates of each mouse: fetus absorption rate = number of fetuses absorbed/ (number of fetuses absorbed + number of fetuses surviving) × 100%.

Ultrasound biomicroscopy

Pregnant mice were anesthetized via inhalation of isoflurane (induction: 3–5%, maintenance: 1.5-2.0%) and placed on a heating pad. Ultrasound imaging was performed using a high-frequency Silicon Wave 60 system (Kolo Medical Technology (Suzhou) Co., Ltd.) equipped with an L38-22K3 transducer (center frequency: 30 MHz). Following abdominal hair removal, pre-warmed ultrasound gel was applied. The uterus was scanned in B-mode by slowly translating, rotating, and tilting the probe along the longitudinal axis of the uterine horn. The fluid-filled bladder was first identified as a pelvic landmark. Both uterine horns were then traced, revealing multiple embryos appearing as oval, hypoechoic structures containing hyperechoic skeletal shadows and hypoechoic soft tissue. Upon clear visualization of a target embryo, the system was switched to Color Doppler mode. The velocity scale (2–6 kHz) and gain were optimized to enhance sensitivity for low-velocity flow. The region exhibiting the brightest color signal (typically red), indicative of the highest flow velocity within either the embryonic cardiac region or umbilical vessels, was identified. A pulsed-wave Doppler (PW) sample gate was precisely positioned at this site, with the angle correction line aligned parallel to the vessel direction (angle < 30) Blood flow spectra were recorded. Embryonic viability was confirmed by the observation of characteristic, regular, periodic pulsatile flow waveforms.

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) of GFP+ monocytes

Monocyte isolation was performed using 8-to-10-week-old female GFP mice (Jackson Laboratory). Following euthanasia by CO₂ asphyxiation and cervical dislocation confirmation, femurs and tibiae were aseptically dissected and marrow was flushed with ice-cold PBS (Gibco, 10010023) supplemented with 0.5% BSA (Sigma, A9418) using a 25-gauge needle (BD PrecisionGlide). The cell suspension was sequentially filtered through 100 μm and 40 μm nylon meshes (Corning, 352360/431750), followed by erythrocyte lysis with Red Blood Cell Lysis Buffer (RBC) lysing buffer (BioLegend, 420301) for exactly 2 min at 25 °C with gentle agitation. Cells were washed twice with sorting buffer, then resuspended at 1 × 106 cells/mL in the same buffer.

For immunophenotyping, cells were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies at optimized concentrations: anti-CD45-BV605 (Biolegend, 103140), anti-CD11b-APC (Biolegend, 101218), anti-Ly6G-PE/cy7 (Biolegend, 127618), anti-Ly6C-precp/cy5.5 (Biolegend, 128012), anti-B220-Alexa Fluor 594 (Biolegend, 103254) and anti-CD19-FITC (Biolegend, 152404) for 30 min at 4 °C protected from light. After staining, cells were washed and resuspended in sorting buffer containing 1 µg/mL DAPI (Sigma, D9542) for live/dead discrimination.

Cell sorting was performed on a BD FACS Aria III (BD Biosciences) equipped with a 100 μm nozzle at 20 psi, maintaining 4 °C throughout the procedure. The instrument was calibrated daily using Precision Count Beads™ (Biolegend, 424902). Monocytes were gated as CD45+CD11b+Ly6C+ singlets (FSC-A/FSC-H) with DAPI exclusion, achieving > 95% purity as confirmed by post-sort reanalysis. Sorted cells were collected in PBS with 10% FBS and processed immediately for downstream applications. Typically, pooling cells from 5 mice yielded 1.2–3.0 × 10⁶ monocytes per experiment (mean yield: 3.5 ± 0.6 × 10⁵ cells/mouse).

Flow cytometry

Peripheral blood, bone marrow (BM), and spleen samples were collected from mice in all three experimental groups. Peripheral blood was drawn into tubes containing 5% sodium citrate as an anticoagulant. BM cells were isolated by flushing tibiae with PBS using a 26-gauge needle, followed by gentle resuspension. Spleen were mechanically dissociated through a 70-µm nylon mesh filter to generate single-cell suspensions. Erythrocyte lysis was performed using RBC lysing buffer (BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Following lysis, cells from all three sources were stained with the following antibody cocktail for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark: CD45-BV605, CD11b-APC, Ly6G-PE-Cy7, Ly6C-PerCP-Cy5.5, B220-AF540, and CD19-FITC. Neutrophils were identified as CD45+CD11b+Ly6G+, monocytes as CD45+CD11b+Ly6C+, and B cells as CD45+B220+CD19+ populations [16]. Flow cytometry was performed on a CytoFLEX instrument (Beckman Coulter), with a minimum of 50,000 events recorded per sample. Data analysis was conducted using CytExpert software (version 2.4; Beckman Coulter), applying appropriate fluorescence minus one (FMO) controls for gating strategy validation.

Western blotting

Protein extraction from human villous tissues (No: 2022-042-001) was performed using RIPA Lysis Buffer (Cell Signaling Technology, 9806 S) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Protein concentrations were determined in triplicate using the Bradford assay (Thermo Scientific, YC373312) with bovine serum albumin as standard. Equal amounts of protein (20 µg per lane) were resolved on 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels under reducing conditions and electrophoretically transferred to 0.22 μm PVDF membranes (Immobilon-P, Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) using a semi-dry transfer system (Bio-Rad) at 25 V for 30 min. Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) BSA in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoblotting was performed using the following primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer with overnight incubation at 4 °C: rabbit polyclonal anti-IFNα1 (1:1000, ABclonal, A1294), rabbit polyclonal anti-IFNα2 (1:1000, ABclonal, A11153), mouse monoclonal anti-CD14 (1:1000, ABclonal, A0964), rabbit monoclonal anti-β-Actin (1:10,000, ABclonal, AC026), and rabbit monoclonal anti-HSP90 (1:10,000, ABclonal, A0237). After three 10-min washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, ABclonal, AS014) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34096) and imaged with a ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad). Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) with background subtraction. Protein expression levels were normalized to β-Actin and HSP90 as internal controls. All experiments included technical triplicates and were repeated with biological replicates from three independent tissue collections.

Immunofluorescence staining

Tissue samples (placenta and spleen) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Sigma-Aldrich, 158127) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at 4 °C for 24 h, followed by cryoprotection in 30% sucrose solution (w/v, Sigma-Aldrich, S0389) at 4 °C for an additional 24 h. Tissues were then embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT; Sakura Finetek, 4583) and stored at -80 °C until sectioning. Serial 5-µm cryosections were obtained using a CM3050S cryostat (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany), with all placental sections collected from the central region (defined as within 500 μm of the umbilical cord insertion site) to ensure anatomical consistency.

For GFP detection, sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, T8787) in PBS for 15 min at room temperature, followed by blocking with 5% normal donkey serum (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 017-000-121) in PBS for 1 h. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in blocking solution overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. Negative controls were processed in parallel using species-matched non-specific IgG (1 µg/mL; Jackson ImmunoResearch, 015-000-003) instead of primary antibodies. After three 5-min washes with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 (PBST), sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500; Invitrogen, A-31573, A-21206) for 1 h at room temperature protected from light. Nuclear counterstaining was performed with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, D9542) for 5 min.

Slides were mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen, P36930) and imaged using a DMi8 fluorescence microscope equipped with a DFC9000 GT camera (Leica Microsystems). Image acquisition parameters (exposure time, gain, and offset) were kept constant across all samples within each experiment. Quantitative analysis was performed by two independent investigators blinded to experimental conditions using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) with cell counting conducted in at least three non-overlapping fields per section (20 × objective). The number of biological replicates (n) is indicated in respective figure legends, representing independent litters with multiple placental sections analyzed per animal.

Isolation and culture of neonatal rat ventricular myocytes (NRVMs)

NRVMs were isolated from 1-to-3-day-old Sprague-Dawley rat pups (Shanghai SLAC Laboratory) as previously described with modifications [17]. Neonatal rats were fully immersed in alcohol, and their chest cavities were opened with scissors to excise the hearts, which were then placed in pre-cooled PBS solution. After neonatal rat hearts were collected, the atria and other components were removed with forceps, retaining only the ventricular tissue. Ventricles were rinsed in ice-cold PBS (Gibco, 10010023), minced into 1-mm³ fragments, and subjected to sequential enzymatic digestion: (1) overnight cold digestion in 0.25% trypsin (without EDTA) at 4 °C for 10–12 h, followed by (2) six sequential 15-min digestions at 37 °C with 0.1% collagenase type II in DMEM (Gibco, 11965092) containing 1% fatty acid-free BSA (Sigma, A8806) under gentle agitation (80 rpm). After each digestion cycle, supernatants were collected in DMEM/F-12 (Gibco, 11330032) supplemented with 20% FBS (Gibco, A5669701) to inhibit enzymatic activity.

Pooled cell suspensions were filtered through 100-µm nylon mesh (Corning, 352360) and centrifuged at 200 g for 5 min at 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended in plating medium (DMEM/F-12 with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin [Gibco, 15140122], and 0.1 mM 5-bromo-2’-deoxyuridine [Brdu; Sigma, B5002]). Cells were pre-plated for 90 min in 10-cm dishes (Corning, 430167) at 37 °C/5% CO₂ to enrich cardiomyocytes by differential adhesion. Non-adherent NRVMs were collected and seeded at 1 × 10⁶ cells/well in 48-well plates (Corning, 3513). Cells for experiments were distributed across three 48-well plates, with 24 wells used per plate. After 24 h, medium was replaced with maintenance medium (plating medium with 0.1 mM Brdu), which was changed every 48 h. Cell viability was assessed by trypan blue exclusion (> 95% viable cells), and cardiomyocyte purity was confirmed by α-actinin immunofluorescence (> 90% positive cells; Millipore, 04-934).

Pharmacological stimulation of NRVMs

Neonatal rat ventricular cardiomyocytes (NRVMs) were cultured in complete DMEM/F-12 medium (10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin) containing 0.1 mM 5-Brdu to suppress fibroblast proliferation. After 36 h when stable beating was established (60–80 beats/min), baseline contractility was recorded. Baseline cardiomyocyte beating activity was recorded 1–4 h after medium exchange. Contraction dynamics of NRVMs were captured using a high-speed digital CMOS camera (Point Grey: GS3-U3-51S5M-C) mounted on a Leica Dmi1 inverted microscope. Videos were acquired via FlyCapture2 Camera Selection 2.13.3.61 software, with continuous 15-second recordings at a frame rate > 60 fps. Following baseline recording, cells were washed twice with pre-warmed (37 °C) PBS, and drug-containing medium was added for 1-hour stimulation in a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator. Post-treatment beating activity was recorded under identical conditions. Drug concentrations: IFN-α1 (MCE, HY-P74864) (100, 300 pg/mL and 1, 10, 50 ng/mL) and IFN-γ (MCE, HY-P7071) (25, 50 ng/mL). Only videos of morphologically intact and contractile NRVMs were included for subsequent analysis. All experiments were conducted at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Assessment of cardiomyocyte beating activity

To quantify cardiomyocyte contractile dynamics, we analyzed myocardial cell contractions using ContractionWave (an open-source software for large-scale contraction analysis of cardiomyocytes), with a focus on measuring contraction velocity parameters [18]. Contractile activity was recorded using a high-speed CMOS camera mounted on a Leica Dmi1 inverted microscope. Videos were acquired at 61.54 fps for 10 s using FlyCapture2 software (v2.13.3.61) and analyzed with ContractionWave. Only morphologically intact and functionally contracting NRVMs were included.

Statistical analysis

All data were showed as the mean ± SD Student’s 2-tailed t tests and were employed for comparing the difference between two groups. For comparison of more than two groups, one-way ANOVA with Tukey test was used. Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 8.0 software.

Results

A comprehensive scRNA-seq atlas of PBMC from EFD patients, pregnant and non-pregnant women

To uncover the complete cellular composition and transcriptome changes of the maternal peripheral blood, we collected peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) from pregnant women (n = 9) in 5–10 weeks of gestation and non-pregnant women (n = 11) for scRNA-Seq (Table S1). All these individuals have a history of EFD, and 2 of the 9 pregnant women were diagnosed with EFD by ultrasonography. In total, we captured 204,674 cells after stringent quality control filtering, with per-cell medians of 4,978 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs), 2,118 unique genes, and 2.90% of mitochondrial counts (Fig. S1A-C).

After normalization, integration using reciprocal PCA (RPCA) [12] and unsupervised clustering analysis, we established a single-cell transcriptional atlas in 20 samples across early-fetal demise, pregnancy and non-pregnancy (Fig. 1A-B and S2A-B). The former six cell types were further subdivided into subpopulations: Naïve B cells, Memory B cells, Naïve CD4+ T cells, CD4+ central memory T (TCM) cells, Naïve CD8+ T cells, CD8+ effector memory T (TEM) cells, CD8+ terminally differentiated effector memory T (TEMRA) cells, Mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells, CD8+ precursor exhausted T (Tpex) cells, γδ T cells, CD56dimCD16+ NK cells, CD56brightCD16- NK cells, conventional DC (cDC), plasmacytoid DC (pDC), CD14+ monocytes and CD16+ monocytes (Fig. 1A-B and S3).

CD14+ monocyte subset is associated with the presence of early fetal demise

Contrary to pregnant and non-pregnant women, we found that the proportion of CD14+ monocytes in PBMC from EFD patients was higher (Figs. 1C and 2A), suggesting that the abnormality of CD14+ monocytes is associated with the prevalence of EFD. Thus, we performed AUCell [13] for pathway analysis with the human Hallmark Gene Set in the Molecular Signatures Database (MsigDB) [14] to investigate the transcriptional variation across cell subpopulations, and observed the activation of inflammatory response signature in CD14+ monocytes, which indicates the excessive inflammatory response of CD14+ monocytes (Fig. 2B and C).

Abundant placental blood flow in early gestation suggests potential maternal immune cell trafficking

To examine the potential impact of maternal monocytes on embryonic development, we established a pregnancy model using C57BL/6J mice. Ultrasonographic analysis revealed stage-specific hemodynamic patterns during gestation (Fig. 3). At E11.5, robust placental blood flow was observed alongside normal embryonic development, whereas cardiac blood flow remained undetectable (Fig. 3A), suggesting predominant reliance on maternal circulation during early organogenesis. By E15.5, distinct fetal heartbeat and vigorous cardiac blood flow were observed (Fig. 3B), whereas placental perfusion signals became undetectable, indicating complete transition to fetal circulatory dominance during mid-gestation.

Placental and fetal cardiac blood flow were detectable in normally developing embryos at E11.5 and E15.5. (A) Representative B-mode ultrasound images of E11.5 fetuses. Lower panels depict placental hemodynamics in representative embryos at specified time points. (B) Intrauterine views of embryonic hearts at E15.5. Lower panel 1 shows dynamic cardiac contractions at sequential developmental stages. Lower panel 2 demonstrates fetal cardiac blood flow patterns at corresponding time points

These findings demonstrated the physiological transition from placental-dependent to cardiac-driven circulation during murine pregnancy. The established hemodynamic progression (Fig. 3), consistent with standard developmental timelines [19, 20], provides critical baseline data for investigating maternal immune modulation of embryogenesis. Notably, the observed placental-to-cardiac transition mirrors human fetal development during equivalent gestational stages, highlighting the translational potential of this murine model system.

Maternal monocytes translocate into embryos during early but not late gestation

Hemodynamic analysis revealed stage-specific patterns of fetal blood supply, showing predominant placental circulation at E11 that transitioned to cardiac-driven flow by E15.5. To investigate whether maternal peripheral monocytes migrate into embryos, we intravenously administered GFP + monocytes into E8.5 pregnant recipients (Fig. 4A). Six hours post-injection, we detected limited but significant GFP+ monocyte migration into embryos (Fig. 4B), which at this stage exhibit undifferentiated placental-fetal vascular connections. Quantitative analysis revealed substantially fewer embryonic GFP+ cells compared to the control group, suggesting alternative cellular trafficking. Consistent with known splenic sequestration of circulating immune cells [21], flow cytometry identified >85% of injected GFP+ monocytes in maternal spleens (Fig. 4C), demonstrating predominant splenic sequestration.

In contrast, when GFP+ monocytes (1 × 106 cells) were intravenously administered to E13.5 pregnant mice (Fig. 4D), subsequent analysis at E15.5 revealed outcomes distinct from those in E8.5 pregnancies—specifically, no GFP⁺ monocytes were detected within embryos. At this later gestational stage, where placental and fetal tissues are clearly distinguishable (Fig. 4A right panel), fluorescent imaging demonstrated minimal GFP+ monocyte presence in either compartment compared to PBS controls (Fig. 4E and F). Quantitative assessment showed a reduction in detectable GFP+ cells relative to the E8.5, with residual signal primarily localized to maternal decidual tissue rather than embryonic structures. This striking temporal restriction of cellular trafficking coincides with the developmental maturation of placental barrier functions, including complete formation of the trophoblast layer and establishment of tight junctions between endothelial cells. The near-absence of GFP+ cells in fetal tissues at E15.5 suggests stringent mid-gestational barriers to maternal monocyte infusion, markedly differing from the trans-placental permeability characteristic of early organogenesis.

These findings collectively demonstrate that maternal peripheral monocytes can migrate into embryos during early gestation, though the majority become sequestered in secondary lymphoid organs. The transient embryonic monocyte infiltration observed at E8.5 suggests a developmentally restricted window for maternal-fetal cellular exchange, likely corresponding to the primitive placental vasculogenesis period in humans.

Elevated maternal monocytes in early gestation induce embryonic arrest

To investigate the developmental consequences of maternal monocyte trafficking into embryos, we established three experimental groups (Fig. 5A). At E15.5, differential maternal phenotypes were observed among the treatment groups. The PBS-treated group maintained the normal pregnancy characteristics with typical abdominal distension, LPS-injected mice showed complete absence of pregnancy features, while monocyte-infused animals exhibited intermediate abdominal expansion consistent with embryonic loss (Fig. 5A). Longitudinal ultrasound monitoring revealed three distinct phenotypic outcomes (Fig. 5B). In the PBS group, embryos exhibited normal developmental progression throughout gestation, with expected cardiac activity and growth parameters at all examined timepoints (E8.5-E15.5). In contrast, LPS administration induced complete embryonic resorption within 72 h, resulting in uterine involution to a non-pregnant state by E15.5 (100% absorption rate). The monocyte-infused group displayed an intermediate phenotype, characterized by partial embryonic arrest with surviving embryos developing normally, as confirmed by both ultrasound and direct morphological examination during tissue collection. Minimally invasive monitoring at E8.5 and direct uterine examination at E15.5 confirmed the ultrasound findings (Fig. 5C). Representative images of dissected uteri, fetuses, placentas, and resorption sites (Fig. 5D) demonstrated: (i) normal gestational structures in PBS controls, (ii) complete embryonic resorption in LPS-treated group, and (iii) coexisting viable fetuses and resorption sites in monocyte-infused mice. Quantification revealed complete embryonic resorption in LPS-treated mice (100%) versus partial resorption in monocyte-infused mice (25.7%, p = 0.0027 vs. PBS controls) (Fig. 5E; Table 1). Maternal weight followed this pattern: LPS group showed significant reduction (p = 0.0049) while monocyte group exhibited intermediate values (Fig. 5F). Although fetal number was reduced in the monocyte-treated group, both placental and fetal weights showed no significant difference compared to the control, indicating compensatory nutrient partitioning to the remaining viable embryos. (Fig. 5G and H). To evaluate potential systemic effects, we compared splenic weights across all groups and found no significant differences, indicating that the observed embryonic effects were not secondary to systemic maternal immune activation (Fig. 5I and J). The selective 25.7% embryonic loss pattern - distinct from the universal resorption induced by LPS - suggests monocyte-mediated disruption of specific developmental pathways rather than generalized toxicity.

Embryonic arrest induced by monocyte transfer is independent of systemic inflammation

To investigate the basis of monocyte-mediated embryonic arrest, we first hypothesized that maternal systemic inflammation—analogous to the LPS-positive control—might underlie developmental disruption. Flow cytometric analysis of peripheral blood revealed no significant differences in neutrophils (CD45+CD11b+Ly6G+), monocytes (CD45+CD11b+Ly6C+), or B cells (CD45+B220+CD19+) across groups (Fig. 6A-B), suggesting no systemic immune activation at E15.5. Similarly, bone marrow analysis demonstrated comparable hematopoietic profiles (Fig. 6C-D), indicating that neither LPS nor monocyte administration altered myeloid or lymphoid differentiation. In contrast, splenic immunophenotyping showed that LPS—but not monocyte infusion—modestly activated neutrophils (1.8-fold increase) and monocytes (2.1-fold increase) without affecting B cells (Fig. 6E-F). Critically, the monocyte group exhibited no splenic or systemic immune perturbations relative to PBS controls. These findings collectively suggest that embryonic arrest results from direct interactions between trafficked monocytes and embryonic tissues, rather than secondary maternal immune activation.

Monocyte-derived IFN-α1 directly suppresses fetal cardiac contractility

To elucidate how maternal monocytes impair embryonic development, we performed gene set enrichment analysis comparing maternal CD14+ monocytes from EFD patients versus control, revealing significant upregulation of interferon signaling pathways (Fig. 7A-B). This transcriptional signature suggested potential involvement of interferon dysregulation in pregnancy loss. Subsequent validation in clinical specimens demonstrated elevated CD14 expression (confirming monocyte infiltration) and specifically increased IFN-α1 (but not IFN-α2) protein levels in villous tissues from EFD patients (n = 6 per group), consistent with the transcriptomic findings (Fig. 8A-D). To further investigate how IFN-α1 contributes to embryonic arrest in mice, we performed functional characterization using NRCM. Given the existence of distinct IFN subtypes released by monocytes, we compared the effects of IFN-α1 with those of IFN-γ. Our results demonstrated that, unlike IFN-γ which showed no observable effects on cardiomyocytes, IFN-α1 at 1 ng/mL significantly suppressed the contractile amplitude of neonatal rat ventricular myocytes (Fig. 8E and Fig. S4), suggesting that IFN-α1 directly impairs cardiomyocyte function by decreasing contractile capacity. These findings established a coherent pathogenic cascade: (1) maternal monocyte infiltration into embryonic tissue, (2) preferential secretion of IFN-α1 over IFN-α2, and (3) released IFN-α1 directly impairing fetal cardiac function by suppressing fetal cardiac contractility, ultimately resulting in selective embryonic arrest. The cardiac toxicity specifically mediated by IFN-α1 accounts for the observed partial (25.7%) resorption phenotype, reflecting localized impact of embryonically infiltrated monocytes through paracrine inflammatory factor secretion on fetal development.

Discussion

Several studies have demonstrated dysregulation of the embryonic immune microenvironment in EFD, characterized by an imbalance between pro- and anti-inflammatory signals [22–24]. However, the specific maternal contributions to these embryonic immune abnormalities have remained poorly characterized. Our study establishes a novel mechanism of pregnancy loss mediated by maternal monocyte trafficking and interferon signaling. The stage-specific vulnerability of embryos to monocyte infiltration (peaking at E8.5-E11.5) coincides with critical windows of placental vascular development and cardiac morphogenesis. This temporal restriction suggests that the establishment of the placental barrier progressively limits maternal-fetal cellular exchange, with monocyte penetration becoming negligible by E15.5. Importantly, our study provides the first direct evidence that maternal immune cells can reprogram the local embryonic microenvironment through targeted cytokine release, thereby establishing a mechanistic link between systemic maternal immunity and selective fetal developmental arrest.

EFD, defined as spontaneous abortion occurring before 12 weeks of gestation, accounts for approximately 80% of all pregnancy losses and represents a common adverse pregnancy outcome [25–27]. Epidemiological data indicate that nearly one million clinically recognized pregnancies in the United States alone end in first-trimester miscarriage annually, with the majority occurring during the embryonic period [28]. Embryonic developmental arrest, a specific form of EFD characterized by failed fetal development or intrauterine demise during early gestation, contributes significantly to these cases [29, 30]. Furthermore, preclinical pregnancy failures - those occurring prior to detectable menstrual delay - are estimated to affect 30–50% of all conceptions, though many remain undetected without sensitive biochemical monitoring [5]. Our study provides novel mechanistic insights by demonstrating that maternal immune cells, particularly monocytes, can be infused across the placental barrier during early gestation and subsequently reprogram the embryonic microenvironment through localized cytokine release. From a translational perspective, these findings suggest several clinical implications. First, the IFN-α1 signature in CD14+ monocytes could serve as a predictive biomarker for women at high risk of recurrent pregnancy loss. Second, targeted blockade of IFN-α1 signaling (e.g., through monoclonal antibodies or IFNAR antagonists) might prevent monocyte-mediated embryonic arrest without compromising essential immune functions. However, therapeutic development must consider the developmental stage-dependency of this mechanism, as our data indicate intervention would only be required during the narrow vulnerability window from E8.5 to E11.5.

Successful pregnancy requires carefully coordinated communication between the maternal and fetal systems. Immune cells and cytokine signaling pathways serve as critical mediators of this communication, actively promoting healthy gestation. However, certain maternal infections and inflammatory conditions can disrupt this delicate balance, potentially leading to severe complications with detrimental effects on fetal development [31]. Our study demonstrates that maternal monocytes can infiltrate embryonic tissues and impair development through the release of cytokines, representing one plausible mechanism for monocyte-mediated embryonic arrest. However, this constitutes only one facet of a broader biological phenomenon, as monocytes exhibit context-dependent functional plasticity [32]. Prior research has established that upon tissue entry, monocytes often differentiate into functionally specialized macrophages, which may exert distinct developmental effects [33]. Notably, a recent study revealed that neutrophil-driven placental inflammation compromises barrier integrity, facilitating maternal monocyte migration that disrupts fetal cardiac development through macrophage population alterations and structural remodeling, with persistent consequences extending into adulthood [8]. This mechanistic cascade illustrates how gestational inflammation programs lifelong cardiovascular dysfunction through placenta-embryo crosstalk, offering novel therapeutic targets for the developmental origins of cardiac disease. Together, these findings underscore the multifactorial nature of monocyte-related embryotoxicity, wherein both direct and indirect (e.g., macrophage-mediated remodeling) mechanisms may operate in parallel or sequentially, depending on the inflammatory milieu and developmental stage. Future studies should delineate the relative contributions of these pathways across gestation.

IFN-α1 was identified as the specific effector molecule underlying monocyte-mediated embryotoxicity, providing crucial mechanistic insights. During embryonic development, IFN family members play distinct roles: IFN-γ primarily regulates immune responses (e.g., Th1 differentiation), while IFN-α maintains antiviral defense and placental immune tolerance (e.g., maternal-fetal interface homeostasis) [34–36]. These differential effects are mediated through specific receptor systems (IFNGR vs. IFNAR) that activate unique signaling pathways to shape the embryonic immune microenvironment [37, 38]. The selective action of IFN-α1 may originate from its distinct receptor binding affinity and downstream signaling kinetics, as IFNAR1/2 exhibit isoform-specific binding properties that preferentially amplify IFN-α1’s biological effects [39, 40]. Existing research indicates that immune cell-derived IFN establishes a self-amplifying feedback loop between cardiac fibroblasts and macrophages through the IFN-IFNAR-pSTAT1 signaling axis, which actively suppresses cardiac reprogramming [41]. In vitro studies demonstrate that IFN exposure induces cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) accumulation in cardiomyocytes, triggering an initial increase in contraction frequency followed by progressive deterioration of beating function [42, 43]. Our human tissue analyses further support this mechanism, demonstrating elevated IFN-α1 levels in EFD villous tissues while excluding systemic inflammation through comprehensive immunophenotyping.

Several important questions remain for future investigation. The precise regulatory mechanisms whereby monocyte-derived IFN-α1 governs embryonic cardiac development require clarification—particularly whether its effects are mediated through direct cardiomyocyte responses or indirectly via cardiac fibroblasts. Additionally, the factors enabling monocyte trafficking specifically during early gestation require elucidation, with placental chemokine gradients and vascular permeability regulators being prime candidates. Finally, human studies should validate whether the IFN-α1-mediated mechanism operates similarly in clinical pregnancy loss, particularly in unexplained EFD cases. These investigations could ultimately lead to mechanism-based therapies for this devastating condition.
