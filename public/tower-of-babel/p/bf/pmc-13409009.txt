Active Surveillance Outcomes After Template-Guided Mapping Biopsy Among Patients With Prostate Cancer
Authors: Mutlay Sayan, Grgur Mirić, Robert Galbreath, Ryan Fiano, Kent Wallner, Shalini Moningi, Jonathan Leeman, Alicia Smart, Martin King, Anthony D’Amico, Peter Orio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cohort study assesses long-term oncologic and quality-of-life outcomes after transperineal template-guided mapping biopsy in patients with prostate cancer.

Introduction

Active surveillance (AS) is the preferred management strategy for patients with low-risk prostate cancer, but its safety depends on accurate baseline risk assessment. Conventional transrectal biopsy may undersample clinically significant disease, particularly in anterior and apical regions,1,2 whereas multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may miss some clinically significant cancers3,4 and is not universally used.5 Transperineal template-guided mapping biopsy (TTMB) provides systematic whole-gland sampling6,7,8 and may improve confidence in selecting patients for surveillance. We evaluated long-term oncologic and quality-of-life (QoL) outcomes after baseline TTMB.

Methods

This prospective, single-institution, longitudinal cohort study included patients with clinically localized prostate cancer enrolled in an AS program between April 2005 and January 2020. Eligible patients had clinical stage cT1c to T2a disease and Gleason Grade Group (GG) 1 or selected GG2 prostate cancer. Patients underwent baseline TTMB before enrollment. The study was approved by the Bethany Ethical Research and Animal Care Committee, and participants provided written informed consent. This study followed the STROBE reporting guideline.

Follow-up included prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing every 4 months and annual digital rectal examination (DRE). Repeat biopsy was not performed routinely and was recommended only for a PSA doubling time of less than 3 years, change in DRE, or patient request. The primary end point was freedom from therapeutic intervention (interval from AS enrollment to definitive treatment initiation). Secondary end points included overall survival and longitudinal QoL measures using the International Index of Erectile Function, International Prostate Symptom Score, Rectal Function Assessment Scale, and Center for Epidemiologic Studies–Depression Scale.

Baseline characteristics were summarized descriptively. Self-reported race was assessed because of known racial differences in prostate cancer outcomes. The cumulative incidence of therapeutic intervention was estimated using Fine-Gray competing-risks methods. Associations between baseline factors and therapeutic intervention were assessed using Fine-Gray regression. A 2-sided P ≤ .05 was statistically significant.

Results

Among patients who underwent TTMB for possible AS enrollment, 374 (45.0%) were included (median [IQR] age, 66.0 [44.0-81.0] years; 8 [2.1%] Black and 366 [97.9%] White). The median PSA (IQR) level was 5.2 (1.5-22.2) ng/mL (to convert to micrograms per liter, multiply by 1), and 357 patients (95.5%) had GG1 disease (Table). The median (IQR) number of biopsy cores was 71.0 (29.0-115.0), with 3.0 (1.0-28.0) positive cores.

At a median (IQR) follow-up of 6.5 (3.8-9.7) years, 18 patients underwent definitive treatment after a median (IQR) interval of 5.9 (3.8-9.0) years. The 8- and 12-year landmark cumulative incidences of therapeutic intervention were 5.2% (n = 13) and 8.7% (n = 17), respectively (Figure). Among the 18 patients who underwent treatment, 15 (83.3%) had baseline GG1 and 3 (16.7%) had baseline GG2 disease, 10 (55.6%) were upgraded to GG3 and 5 (27.8%) to GG4 on repeat biopsy, and 3 (16.7%) underwent treatment for nononcologic reasons.

No distant metastases or prostate cancer–related deaths were observed. Fifty patients (13.4%) died during follow-up. The leading causes of death were cardiovascular disease and other malignant tumors. In an exploratory, multivariable Fine-Gray analysis, PSA density greater than 0.15 ng/mL/cm3 remained associated with therapeutic intervention (subdistribution hazard ratio, 2.93; 95% CI, 1.09-7.87; P = .03).

QoL measures remained generally stable over time. Urinary and bowel function showed no meaningful decline, and depressive symptoms did not increase. Sexual function declined gradually, consistent with aging.

Discussion

In this study, AS after baseline TTMB was associated with a low rate of therapeutic intervention, no distant metastases or prostate cancer–related deaths, and stable urinary, bowel, and psychological QoL outcomes in a selected TTMB-staged cohort. Because treatment initiation may reflect surveillance intensity, clinician judgment, and patient preference, therapeutic intervention should not be interpreted as synonymous with oncologic progression. These findings describe favorable long-term outcomes of AS after comprehensive baseline staging.

The 12-year therapeutic intervention rate of 8.7% was lower than rates reported in many contemporary AS cohorts.9,10 However, direct comparisons are limited by differences in patient selection and surveillance practices. These findings suggest that more complete baseline sampling may help identify patients less likely to require delayed treatment, potentially reducing uncertainty during surveillance. PSA density greater than 0.15 ng/mL/cm3 remained associated with later treatment, supporting its role in risk stratification even after extensive baseline biopsy.

This study has some limitations. It was conducted at a single institution, the predominantly White cohort may limit generalizability, and ot lacked a comparator arm. MRI and genomic classifiers were not routinely incorporated, reflecting practice patterns during the study period. Repeat biopsy was not mandated at fixed intervals, downstream biopsy and MRI use were not systematically captured, TTMB-related morbidity and tolerability were not systematically captured, and QoL outcomes were analyzed descriptively because assessment timing and questionnaire completion varied. The few patients with GG2 disease precluded conclusions for this subgroup. Future studies should evaluate how this approach of AS after baseline TTMB can be integrated with MRI and genomic classifiers to personalize surveillance intensity.
