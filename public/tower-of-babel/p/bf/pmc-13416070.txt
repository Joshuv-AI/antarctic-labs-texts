Uncertainty-aware instance-wise feature selection with adaptive graph regularization
Authors: G Kirubavathi, K J Vijayavarsini, G S Vruthula Shruthi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInstance-wise feature selection aims at discovering the most relevant set of attributes for each particular sample, providing a natural direction for achieving adaptive learning on inputs of heterogeneous and high-dimensional nature. While recent neural-net models made impressive advances, all current methods share a common drawback: the selected feature mask is considered as a definite one. Such a degree of certainty with respect to selected features is hardly justified in case of imperfect input data, correlations between predictors, and small sample sizes. The resulting too confident interpretation of features’ relevance becomes highly problematic in critical applications, e.g., medical decision-making, risk assessment, and fault diagnosis. Despite such danger, all current methods of instance-wise feature selection have an important common drawback: they assume the selected feature mask to be definite, providing no way of estimating its reliability. In contrast, our paper proposes the first-ever approach that deals with the uncertainty inherent to feature selection itself, not just in model prediction. We propose UDIFS - a novel Uncertainty-aware Deep Instance-wise Feature Selection method with Adaptive Graph Regularization. The fundamental concept is to view feature selection as a stochastic process where, rather than fixing on a specific mask for each instance, a distribution over masks is learned using a Concrete (Gumbel-Sigmoid) distribution. By sampling multiple independent masks at inference time, we can estimate uncertainty in predictions and in feature selection through Monte Carlo estimation. The second key element of our approach is an adaptive Graph Laplacian regularization method which is applied directly to the sampled masks themselves (not the latent representations) in order to ensure that semantically similar instances have consistent explanations without having to resort to a GNN-based approach. We compare UDIFS (Graph) to eight other approaches grouped into four categories: instance-wise selectors such as INVASE, neural feature selectors like STG, LassoNet, CAE, c-STG for 2023, and MCDropout-FS. We show in experiments run on three diverse datasets: Car Evaluation (low dimensionality), Dexter (ultra-high dimensional text corpus), and IMDB Sentiment (high dimensional) that UDIFS (Graph) attains the lowest feature selection uncertainty among all baselines while maintaining classification accuracy. UDIFS is, to the best of our knowledge, the first approach to (i) directly incorporate uncertainty estimation in the feature selection phase itself instead of in predictive models, and (ii) directly use Graph Laplacian regularization on instance-wise feature selection masks instead of on latent space representations. The existence of a non-zero positive gap in terms of uncertainty between correctly and incorrectly classified instances indicates that the learned uncertainty signal holds true semantic meaning. This indicates a need for a paradigm shift; i.e., evaluation of explanations based on accuracy and reliability measures together. Introduction

The use cases for machine learning models have evolved from being restricted to benchmarks and experiments in labs to practical applications such as diagnosis in healthcare, risk assessments in finance, legal decisions, surveillance in industry, and autonomous systems. In the latter domains, not only do we need to ensure accurate predictions, but we also need a clear understanding of the reasoning behind these predictions. The process of explaining the reasoning is imperative for building trust and confidence in predictive models. Feature selection and explanation methods based on the traditional approach work in a global fashion where one needs to find a common set of relevant features across the dataset. However, in real-world scenarios, there is no guarantee that all instances utilize the same set of features for making predictions. Instance-wise feature selection (IWFS) addresses this problem where the objective is to select the most relevant features for each instance. Prior works, such as INVASE1, have proven the potential of deep neural networks for instance-wise feature masking. Other contributions followed, providing further improvements, including dynamic joint feature selection and classification2,3, instance-wise causal feature selection4, group-wise feature selection at an instance level5, and structured acquisition setups6. Together, these papers showed the potential of the IWFS approach for providing personalized explanations and adaptive computations.

Nonetheless, one assumption seems to be consistently overlooked throughout these advancements: most of the approaches that utilize IWFS are deterministic, in the sense that each model produces one mask per sample as a definitive output. This assumes certainty in the feature subset selection process, an assumption that is often unjustified when working with noisy, real-world datasets with many correlated features and limited samples, which frequently admit several different possible feature sets that can be used to justify the model’s decision. Meanwhile, the study of Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) shows how crucial it is to quantify predictive uncertainties to create reliable ML models. Bayesian methods in deep learning can be used to identify epistemic uncertainties (resulting from deficiencies in model) and aleatoric uncertainties (resulting from noise in data)7. Practical solutions, such as Monte Carlo dropout8,9, allow estimating uncertainty without altering the structure of the models. Monte-Carlo-related methods have shown promising results in various applications ranging from soil modeling10, anomaly detection11, battery state-of-health prediction12, perception by autonomous vehicles13, to hydrological forecasts14. Nevertheless, all of those methods deal only with uncertainty in model predictions; uncertainty in explanations (feature selections), which could occur, has not yet attracted sufficient attention.

The other problem lies in the stability of the explanations. Current IWFS models train separate feature masks for each sample. This means that two samples, which are very similar in semantics, will still receive different explanations due to the effects of initialization and gradient noise. Graph learning is a field where the issue of smoothness has long been considered important. Adaptive graph learning approaches15–18 have proven their importance in clustering17, semi-supervised feature selection16, temporal modeling19, multi-label feature selection18, and elsewhere. Yet, previous constraint-based approaches have only been studied for embedding or representation-level tasks but not for instance-wise explanation masks. The above observations thus lead us to pose the following research question: Is it possible to perform instance-wise feature selection while taking into account structural consistency and being uncertainty-aware?

Though there have been recent advances on instance-wise feature selection and uncertainty estimation, no prior method considers feature selection as an uncertain and stochastic problem, hence no prior evaluation takes into account the quality of their uncertainty estimates. This results in a major limitation of existing works: existing approaches consider the explanation of a prediction with a single feature subset and do not take into consideration the uncertainty of the choice of such feature subset. In our study, we formulate the problem of instance-wise feature selection as an uncertainty-aware explanation task. Instead of focusing on the choice of feature subsets for each prediction, we ask how reliably the selection of feature subsets is made. This rephrasing shifts the focus of the explanation towards its distribution which considers not only the importance of a feature but also its uncertainty. We further propose the problem of explanation-level structural consistency through graph-based regularizer for feature masks.

Novelty statement. The introduced UDIFS approach introduces two novel aspects which, to the best of our knowledge, have never been addressed before.

First novel aspect – modeling uncertainty during feature selection, not prediction. Existing approaches to uncertainty quantification (UQ)7,8,20 aim at measuring the confidence of a model in its prediction. In contrast, UDIFS seeks to measure the confidence in which features caused the prediction. Technically, UDIFS generates T different feature masks per input and outputs their variance . This allows for a finer grain control over the reliability of predictions. For example, a neural network can be highly certain in its prediction yet extremely uncertain about the features causing this prediction. This type of information is inaccessible to any existing IWFS and UQ method.

Second novel aspect – regularization using graph Laplacian on feature selection masks. Previous approaches to graph-regularized machine learning15,16,21 regularize latent space representations or predictions. UDIFS regularizes instance-wise feature selection masks using . This provides an interpretable graph smoothness term directly on feature selection masks.

Motivation

Machine learning’s growing use in critical safety applications calls for model results that are accurate and interpretable. It is essential for the decision making process of clinical decision support systems, factory fault detection systems, and risk assessments systems that not only the results be predicted by the algorithm but that the method behind these predictions be understood, justified, and assessed for validity. The concept of instance-wise feature selection has emerged because of the inherent drawbacks associated with the global approach to feature selection. Global feature selection is built on the foundation of the hypothesis that there must always exist some set of feature that applies to all instances. This hypothesis is frequently false, as identical patients may have entirely different risk factors for disease, or identical machinery in factories could have different failure reasons. Recent innovations in neural IWFS such as INVASE1, however, indicate that instance-wise feature selection and decision-making using reinforcement learning is possible.

Nonetheless, when considering all existing IWFS methods, a common assumption lies at the base: the explanations generated from the model are deterministic – only one feature set was used for each instance to make a decision, which means that the set alone is responsible for making the prediction. However, the claim is often not justified. Taking into account possible imperfections of models, data errors, and the possibility of feature dependencies, it is usually possible to find several other feature sets that would provide sufficient grounds for making the same decision about a certain instance.

The problem is even more relevant for safety-critical scenarios. Having made a prediction and an associated explanation, the following important question arises: how confident are we that the right features were used for each prediction? The confidence level will be impossible to estimate without uncertainty measures if we have two patients with almost identical data who received a similar but not identical set of features as their explanation. The causal methods in IWFS4 and feature grouping5 have made explanations more interpretable but remain limited to providing only one deterministic explanation for each example.

Great progress has been achieved in the area of UQ for addressing this challenge. One of the first approaches was the development of an epistemic-aleatoric uncertainty separation approach7, laying the grounds for uncertainty representation in DL. In recent surveys20,22, the need to represent uncertainty was mentioned as important. Monte Carlo Dropout approach was proved to be useful in several domains, from battery condition estimation12 to autonomous vehicles perception13. The limitation of such approaches is that they represent uncertainty in the predictions of the model, yet they lack uncertainty in explaining the model itself. The certainty with which the model has chosen certain features requires more attention.

In recent years, it was shown that the development of adaptive graph-based models results in the improvement of the prediction quality through imposing certain structural assumptions on them15–18. Several surveys of graph neural networks have indicated the benefits of structural smoothness in improving generalizability23. Nevertheless, these assumptions about structure held true for representations and embeddings but not for explanations at the instance level.

In conclusion, the progress described above has demonstrated an important connection between interpretability, uncertainty quantification, and structural learning. The goal of this research is to bridge this connection by means of stochastic explanations with quantifiable uncertainties and structured control.

Related work

This paper sits at the crossroads of three different areas of research that have undergone substantial development in the past decade but have yet to be synthesized in any meaningful way: instance-dependent feature selection, quantifying uncertainties in deep learning models, and graph learning adaptation. While substantial progress has been achieved in each of these areas, the convergence of these areas into a unified framework, especially when using graph data to provide an explanation mask regularization and quantify uncertainties, remains underexplored.

Adaptive graph learning

In addition to that, another promising approach in modern representation learning consists of exploring the relationships between objects rather than defining them. Wen et al.17 found out that joint optimization of the graph and node representations in multi-view learning leads to substantial robustness gains in the case of incomplete data. Additionally, adaptive graphs have been explored for semi-supervised classification problems in works done by Gao et al.16 and Chen et al.24, in which the method of iterative deep adaptive graph learning was utilized. Zou et al.15 proposed multi-view clustering with the use of adaptively generated graphs, while Qin et al.18 applied the approach to multi-label feature selection via label adaptation. Whereas, edge computing adaptive graphs have been designed in Li et al.19, and adaptive modeling has improved spatiotemporal fusion and time series forecast under challenging real-world scenarios25,26.

However, UDIFS diverges from the aforementioned approaches because of one major reason. The existing graph modeling techniques employ structural regularization on latent space representation or forecasting, whereas UDIFS has become the first framework to apply structural regularization at the level of instance explanation. Therefore, UDIFS facilitates the interpretation of graph smoothness with regard to feature selection for similar instances.

Uncertainty quantification in deep learning

Surely, the subject area was supplied with its theory by the work of Kendall and Gal7, which made the separation between epistemic uncertainty, which arises from the model itself, and aleatoric uncertainty, which appears due to data noise. The concept of estimating uncertainties by means of Monte Carlo dropout without regard to neural network architecture was formulated by Loquercio, Segu, and Scaramuzza8, the technique was described in detail qualitatively by Seoh9. General insights into uncertainty estimation in the context of deep learning are provided by Segu et al.27; the Monte Carlo simulations for uncertainty estimations were introduced earlier by Papadopoulos and Yeung28.

Many surveys have discussed the necessity of modelling uncertainties, among which there is the one by Abdar et al.20 concerning Bayesian approaches and ensembles, as well as Tyralis and Papacharalampous22, who considered the machine learning side of the issue. It is noted that Monte Carlo dropout is efficient when used for a number of research purposes: soil spectroscopy modelling10, remaining residual lifetime estimation for lithium-ion batteries12,29, satellite anomalies detection11, and streamflow predictions14. In terms of methodology, attention must be paid to the approach of calibration of the dropout by Ahmadian et al.30 and to the approach of Masksembles proposed by Durasov et al.31. Meanwhile, Miok et al.32 explored Monte Carlo dropout in the context of data generation. On the other hand, Blundell et al.33 provided the Bayesian interpretation by using weight distributions, while Lakshminarayanan et al.34 demonstrated that deep ensembles have good capabilities for the calibration of the uncertainty in predictions.

The similarity between the two above is that both try to determine the degree of uncertainty in the result of the respective models. The factor that both do not take into consideration is the degree of uncertainty in the decision-making process. While models are able to give absolutely certain results, they can be extremely uncertain about the factors leading to the results.

Instance-wise feature selection

In the study conducted by the authors, Yoon et al.1 showed how reinforcement learning is used to conduct dynamic instance-wise feature selection through INVASE, a process where deterministic binary masks are generated per instance, and no suggestion is made concerning uncertainty or consistency among neighboring instances. Warahena Liyanage et al.2 further developed their previous research in this field and devised a framework for conducting dynamic joint feature selection and classification in correlated feature spaces. Masoomi et al.5 proposed instance-wise grouping of correlated features to capture their correlated nature. On the other hand, group-wise supervised feature selection was developed by Xiao et al.35, and Peng et al.36 carried out probabilistic feature selection using copulas. The use of instance-wise causal feature selection for rotating machine fault diagnostics has been examined by Guo et al.4. Sequential feature selection algorithms have been studied by Ekanayake et al.6, whereas other works on feature selection have considered the dual aspect of features and expert decision acquisition from a single-instance viewpoint6. In addition, three well-known global feature selection approaches have also been included as baseline solutions.

The model LassoNet by Lemhadri et al.37 uses a linear hierarchical structure, which relies on the sparsity defined by the norm. The Concrete Autoencoder38 by Balin, Abid, and Zou was designed based on the reparametrization approach, using differentiable Gumbel-softmax allocation as follows:

Positioning of UDIFS

Nonetheless, on closer inspection, we can see that there are two major issues with the aforementioned approaches. The first one is the fact that all the current instance-wise feature selection methods are deterministic. In other words, the selection of the mask for each individual instance is assumed to be the ground truth, and no uncertainties regarding its choice are considered. Another problem that arises is the fact that the adaptive learning graphs lead to better generalization abilities. Unfortunately, this approach has not been applied for the creation of explanation masks using feature selection algorithms. The proposed technique of UDIFS solves both aforementioned issues at once.

It should be noted that the wide applicability of the proposed solution described in UDIFS has been proven in several complex prediction tasks. For example, the modelling of spatiotemporal interactions and multi-granularity temporal features resulted in a considerable increase in air quality prediction accuracy compared to many different baseline models25. Another advanced forecasting task that has been successfully handled by applying adaptive graph convolutions and interpolation attention for making accurate forecasts based on partially incomplete data has been multivariate time series forecasting26. In terms of batteries, fast charging optimization and prediction of Remaining Useful Life can be done based on uncertainty-aware machine learning techniques, namely, by employing ensemble surrogate-assisted multi-objective optimization45 and gated non-stationary transformers supplemented with meta-learning in case of RUL prediction intervals when training datasets are limited29. As for musculoskeletal modeling, it was shown that adding physics-based constraints as soft constraints in a transfer learning approach enables quick generalization to new subjects46, similar to what is achieved by applying graph Laplacian constraints in UDIFS. Moreover, this technique was further improved by running parallel training for local models and aggregating them to obtain a global model with error weighting47. Optimizations in UDIFS are done with Adam optimizer48 in PyTorch49. Statistical significance of results is evaluated using Wilcoxon signed-rank test50. Together, these results demonstrate that combining structure considerations along with uncertainty measures yields better results across tasks— a concept that is UDIFS innovative in exploring explainability masks.

Stochastic instance-wise feature selection

Instead of picking one particular mask at all times, UDIFS quantifies the relevance of each feature in terms of the probability of picking that feature. In other words, a feature with high selection probability will be picked repeatedly across different rounds; a feature with low selection probability will never be selected in any round; and finally, a feature with an intermediate probability is truly ambiguous about its importance.

When and why graph regularization helps

The utility of graph regularization is heavily contingent on the intrinsic data structure and degree of ambiguity in features. Graph regularization is especially advantageous when dealing with examples that are semantically similar but have varied feature selection due to random noise or stochasticity in the learning model. In such instances, the adaptive graph ensures consistency by promoting similar instances to have similar feature masks, hence minimizing variance and stabilizing the explanation process.

This phenomenon is highly pronounced when working with high dimensional or sparse datasets (e.g., Dexter and IMDB), where numerous feature sets may be able to adequately account for the same outcome. In the absence of structural assumptions, the model may choose distinct feature sets across Monte Carlo iterations, resulting in considerable uncertainty.

In situations where data points have distinct differences or where classes have well-defined boundaries, heavy application of graph regularization might oversimplify information specific to each instance, which could adversely affect the classification process. These phenomena justify why should be chosen at intermediate values, as depicted in Fig. 6. In contrast, overuse of graph regularization would result in decreased accuracy.

In summary, the application of graph regularization is maximized in scenarios where there are existing relationships between data points and high feature selection uncertainty.

Discussion

The following are the three insights drawn from the empirical findings: What does graph regularisation accomplish? Why is accuracy not enough as a measure of evaluation? How does UDIFS compare?

What graph regularization actually achieves

Structural smoothing is mainly achieved by minimizing random variance in the structure of selected features among similar instances that emerge due to noise in the data or optimization dynamics. Smoothing is a means of stabilizing the generated explanations without necessarily improving the predictive performance. This demonstrates that the process of graph regularization can be regarded as a technique for ensuring explanation stability, which is different from the common optimization techniques. Contrary to earlier techniques that applied graph regularization on latent variables, UDIFS uses graph regularization on explanations directly.

Why accuracy is not the right objective

Instance-wise feature selection tries not just to interpret the prediction made by the model, but also to quantify how confident the model is in its explanation. As shown in our experiments, two models can perform similarly well, yet exhibit drastically different degrees of explanation uncertainty and structural consistency. Failing to consider the uncertainty leads to an overly optimistic view on the trustworthiness of explanations20,22. While prediction accuracy and explanation reliability are two separate objectives that complement each other, both need to be considered.

For instance, when comparing our UDIFS (Graph) method against c-STG (2023), we see that the accuracy difference between the two on the Car Evaluation dataset is 0.9654 - 0.9312 = 0.0342; however, the uncertainty difference between the two is 0.0198 - 0.0040 = 0.0158, which is considerably larger. The difference in accuracy of the two algorithms is 0.0198 for the IMDB dataset, while UDIFS (Graph) provides an explanation that is 7.8× less uncertain.

Comparison against c-STG (2023)

Since c-STG is the latest instance-aware algorithm, it is reasonable to perform a detailed comparison between them. While both approaches learn the gate parameters on a per-instance basis, they differ by three key factors. First, while c-STG learns a population-level Gaussian noise with a common σ, UDIFS learns the instance-wise logit values, which are then used with Gaussian noise for greater expressiveness. Second, there is no way to aggregate the uncertainty from multiple Monte Carlo runs with c-STG to see how uncertain a choice is for a specific instance; on the other hand, UDIFS directly calculates the variance . Finally, there is no structural requirement for the mask learned by c-STG; however, in UDIFS, the graph Laplacian makes sure that similar instances are explained similarly.

Practical implications

For critical areas like healthcare, finance, and legal matters, the validity of the explanations provided by the model plays a vital role in making decisions. However, invalid explanations might encourage the user to rely on faulty reasoning processes. In light of this, our solution offers an effective means to detect uncertain explanations, thus allowing humans to examine the reasoning process more carefully. The uncertainty heat maps illustrated in Fig. 8 are an example of such an interface.

Limitations

However, despite its practical utility, there are a number of limitations of the framework that deserve further study.

Hyperparameters: Sensitivity and interactions: In contrast to previous work, the presented framework uses several different regularization terms (with corresponding hyperparameters: and ). While this helps gain greater insight into the model, it requires additional care during optimization. Furthermore, as these hyperparameters are not independent, their interactions can cause complex trade-offs between the different properties of the models. For example, while increasing reduces the variance, it also tends to oversmooth instance-specific explanations. Finding principled ways of selecting optimal values remains an open problem.

Dependence on embedding quality for constructing graph: To construct the graph, cosine similarity is used as an adaptive distance metric, which depends entirely on the quality of the representation produced by the encoder. As a consequence, if representations cannot faithfully encode semantics, the graph is likely to impose incorrect structural assumptions, leading to biased explanations.

Approximation using mini-batches: Graph generation is done on a mini-batch scale, which helps improve computational efficiency. Although feasible, this approach will not ensure that global correlations between data instances are captured; there may be issues with inconsistent regularization across batches. Developing a method to extend the algorithm to work using global graphs or memory-based graphs is an interesting problem to investigate in the future.

Overheads due to computations and Monte Carlo simulations: The proposed approach entails some computational overheads in two ways: computing pairwise similarities () and performing Monte Carlo sampling while performing inference tasks. While feasible, both aspects may cause problems when dealing with large-scale datasets and real-world scenarios. One solution to mitigate the cost would involve decreasing sample size and developing deterministic approximations of uncertainty.

Large and high-dimensional data considerations: Even though mini-batch approach reduces costs, there could still be scalability issues with large-scale and highly dimensional data sets. Graph sparsification techniques, approximate nearest neighbour searching algorithms, and hierarchical clustering regularization are among possible strategies to address such issues.

Accuracy-Uncertainty-Stability Trade-off: The model inherently captures the trade-off among accuracy, explanation stability, and uncertainty. In specific scenarios (e.g., Dexter dataset), applying constraints via graph regularization might negatively impact the prediction performance. The exact regime where such a trade-off can be exploited is unclear at the moment.

Lack of dataset variability and testing in real-life scenarios: Experiments were carried out using carefully crafted tabular and textual datasets. Although they account for varying dimensional regimes, there might not be enough consideration of realistic high-stakes applications in domains such as medicine or genomics. More extensive testing using domain-specific, large-scale, noisy datasets is needed to confirm robustness. Moreover, the low performance of the algorithm on the IMDB data(57–62% across all methods) can be explained as an inevitable result of using sparse and binary bag-of-words TF-IDF vectorization, which, by default, disregards the word order. This is a limit for all the compared approaches due to how the data was created, not due to some inherent shortcoming of the UDIFS approach.

Scope of the baseline approaches: As noted, currently, the authors concentrate on neural feature selection approaches. Although this reflects the nature of the problem addressed, including some classical filter-based (mRMR, ReliefF) or decision-tree-based methods (Random Forest importance) could shed more light on the practical competitiveness.

Extension to additional modalities: Currently, experiments were performed on tabular and bag-of-words data. Extension to image, time-series, or multimodal data would likely require re-designing the network architecture for the encoder and graph formation components.

Conclusion

We presented UDIFS, an uncertainty-aware approach to instance-wise feature selection that leverages stochastic masking, uncertainty estimation through Monte Carlo sampling in the context of feature selection, and graph Laplacian regularization. To the best of our knowledge, UDIFS is the first method to (i) quantify and model uncertainty in the context of instance-wise explanations rather than through model prediction uncertainties, and (ii) use graph Laplacian regularization over explanation masks instead of latent space representations.

In light of reviewer feedback, we substantially enhanced our experimental evaluation to include eight approaches belonging to four different paradigms, including the state-of-the-art c-STG (2023) baseline. Experiments on three complementary datasets (Car Evaluation, Dexter, and IMDB) show that graph regularization helps lower the uncertainty in feature selection while improving its structural consistency; compared to all the baselines, UDIFS significantly lowers feature selection uncertainty while not falling behind in terms of classification accuracy.

There exists a significant correlation between prediction correctness and feature selection uncertainty over all datasets and approaches. This finding validates that the uncertainty signal is indeed semantically rich. The calibration plots additionally provide further support for the reliability of the uncertainty estimate produced by the algorithms. The results indicate that accuracy, uncertainty, and stability are independent yet complementary criteria when evaluating instance-wise feature selection approaches.

In addition to its technical impact, this paper also calls for a paradigm shift, whereby the assessment of instance-based explanation techniques should go beyond measures focused solely on accuracy and consider multiple objectives such as robustness, stability, and uncertainty-awareness. This paradigm shift becomes a necessity in safety-sensitive settings, where inaccurate explanations may have detrimental consequences.
