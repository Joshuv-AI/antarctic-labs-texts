Compensatory “rolling-like” tongue motion after circumferential tongue necrosis without reconstruction: A functional adaptation phenomenon
Authors: Atsushi Shudo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSevere self-inflicted tongue injury may result in extensive tissue loss and functional impairment. Although acute management strategies have been described, long-term functional adaptation following circumferential necrosis treated without reconstruction remains insufficiently characterized.Case presentationA 67-year-old woman with major depressive disorder developed circumferential tongue necrosis after repeated self-inflicted biting. Emergency management consisted of debridement and primary closure. Reconstructive surgery was deferred because of psychiatric instability and systemic risk. At 1-year follow-up, epithelial healing was complete, but tongue protrusion and segmental deformation were markedly restricted. Instead of linear protrusion or localized tip shaping, en bloc rotational displacement of the residual tongue was observed. This compensatory rotational displacement may reflect altered traction vector dynamics of preserved extrinsic muscles under circumferential intrinsic scar contracture.DiscussionCircumferential scar contracture appeared to restrict intrinsic muscle-mediated three-dimensional deformation while permitting limited extrinsic muscle-driven displacement. Altered traction vector dynamics may explain the characteristic rotational movement pattern. Despite persistent fine motor impairment, basic speech intelligibility and oral intake were preserved through compensatory mechanisms.ConclusionConservative management of circumferential tongue necrosis may allow partial long-term functional adaptation under selected conditions. Recognition of characteristic compensatory movement patterns may assist in reconstructive decision-making.

Introduction

Oral self-injurious behavior involving the tongue has been reported in association with psychiatric disorders, neurodevelopmental syndromes, and psychostimulant exposure 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. Injury severity ranges from superficial lacerations to extensive necrosis with potential airway compromise and long-term dysfunction.6

While previous literature has focused primarily on acute management and reconstructive strategies,7 detailed descriptions of long-term biomechanical adaptation after circumferential necrosis healing without reconstruction have not been well characterized. Existing publications emphasize hemostasis, tissue viability, and reconstructive decision-making, with minimal attention to post-healing movement patterns or compensatory motor adaptation.

Because the tongue functions as a muscular hydrostat, coordinated intrinsic and extrinsic muscle activity is essential for three-dimensional deformation, articulation, and swallowing.8 Circumferential scar contracture may fundamentally alter this functional architecture.

The present report describes a clinical example illustrating long-term functional adaptation after circumferential tongue necrosis treated conservatively, with emphasis on characteristic movement patterns and their biomechanical implications.

Case presentation

This case illustrates functional sequelae after circumferential tongue necrosis managed without reconstruction. A 67-year-old woman with major depressive disorder developed severe tongue injury following repeated self-inflicted biting during psychiatric hospitalization. The patient inflicted self-injury twice; the second episode occurred two days after the initial event, and she was referred to our hospital on the same day as the second injury. Persistent bleeding prompted referral to oral and maxillofacial surgery. Examination revealed circumferential congestion and partial marginal necrosis of the tongue (Fig. 1). Emergency management under local anesthesia included hemostasis, debridement, and primary closure (Fig. 2). Surgical debridement was performed twice (on the day of presentation and three days later). No clinical evidence of local infection or systemic complications was observed. Re-epithelialization was achieved within 10 days. Follow-up evaluations were conducted at 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months after injury. Serial photographic documentation was obtained. At 1-year follow-up, tongue protrusion was markedly limited, extending only approximately 2–3 mm beyond the anterior dentition and failing to achieve palatal contact without compensatory mandibular elevation. Speech intelligibility was generally preserved, although articulatory precision for tongue-tip consonants was reduced. Speech intelligibility could not be assessed immediately after injury due to severe depressive symptoms and absence of phonation. At one year, speech was intelligible in conversation, although occasional words required careful listening. A blenderized diet was tolerated without aspiration. Instrumental swallowing assessments (FEES or VFSS) were not feasible due to psychiatric hospitalization. At one year, the repetitive saliva swallowing test (RSST) and modified water swallowing test (MWST) showed no functional impairment. The patient remained hospitalized in a psychiatric facility during follow-up and continued antipsychotic medication therapy. No recurrent self-injury was reported. No formal speech or myofunctional rehabilitation was provided. Although comprehensive quality-of-life assessment was challenging in the context of psychiatric hospitalization, the Health Utilities Index Mark 3 (HUI3) indicated Level 1 for speech (“able to be understood completely”) and Level 2 for emotion (“somewhat happy”)9. Additional surgical intervention was declined.

Discussion

Conservative management of tongue lacerations has been reported as a viable option in selected acute cases.7,10 However, circumferential necrosis differs from localized injury in that it may induce global volumetric reduction combined with circumferential scar contracture.

The principal functional finding in this case was severe loss of segmental deformation capacity. Under physiological conditions, intrinsic tongue muscles generate localized shape changes, such as narrowing, thickening, elongation, and tip shaping, independent of gross positional displacement.11 The near absence of such deformation suggests that circumferential scarring markedly restricted intrinsic muscle extensibility.

In muscular hydrostatic systems, circumferential constraint can reduce deformational freedom by limiting radial expansion and longitudinal redistribution of tissue volume. Increased circumferential tension may alter internal force transmission and restrict segmental shape change, thereby favoring mass displacement over localized deformation.

Although no clinical evidence suggested overt destruction of the extrinsic musculature, protrusive movement remained markedly limited. The genioglossus muscle is the principal protrusor of the tongue.8 In the present case, attempted protrusion resulted in rotational displacement rather than linear anterior translation. Circumferential scar tension may have altered effective traction vectors, converting anteriorly directed force into rotational motion within the oral cavity. In addition to circumferential contracture, primary geometric shortening caused by debridement and marginal tissue loss likely contributed to limited anterior translation. Distal tissue loss and anterior tethering at the closure margin may have mechanically restricted protrusive excursion independent of secondary scar remodeling.

Moreover, posteriorly directed force vectors generated by the genioglossus and related extrinsic muscles may have acted against the shortened anterior segment, favoring rotational rather than linear displacement. Thus, the observed en bloc movement may reflect the combined effects of structural shortening and altered force transmission rather than adaptive motor reorganization alone.

This characteristic kinematic pattern may be described descriptively as “rolling-like” motion. For descriptive consistency, “rolling-like” motion refers to mass-like en bloc rotational displacement of the residual tongue observed during active movement attempts, rather than segmental intrinsic shaping. Compensatory mandibular movement was also observed, consistent with coordinated oral motor adaptation mechanisms described in studies of mastication and swallowing.11

Functionally, fine articulatory precision was compromised, yet basic speech intelligibility and swallowing safety were preserved. These findings suggest that extrinsic muscle-driven displacement, together with mandibular compensation, may sustain limited functional capacity despite intrinsic restriction. Rehabilitation literature following glossectomy has similarly demonstrated that functional communication and swallowing can be partially restored through compensatory mechanisms and targeted therapy, even in the setting of substantial tissue loss12. In contrast, no formal rehabilitation was implemented in the present case, suggesting that spontaneous motor adaptation may also contribute to functional preservation.

The proposed biomechanical interpretation should be regarded as inferential. Imaging studies (MRI or ultrasonography) or electromyographic analyses were not performed to confirm intrinsic muscle integrity or scar distribution. The observed “rolling-like” motion is therefore presented as a clinically observed compensatory pattern rather than a mechanistically verified adaptation.

Given psychiatric instability and risk of recurrence, conservative management was selected. Although the cited review primarily addressed acute tongue lacerations, conservative management may be considered when airway stability and oral intake are preserved.7 Although systematic reviews have compared surgical and conservative management of severe tongue injuries, detailed functional outcomes in cases of circumferential necrosis managed conservatively remain sparsely reported. Therefore, it is unclear whether the degree of functional adaptation observed in the present case represents an expected outcome or an unusually favorable adaptation. The present example suggests that partial long-term adaptation may occur even after circumferential necrosis, although residual impairment should be anticipated.

Limitations include the single-case design and absence of quantitative motion analysis. Quantitative volumetric estimation of tissue loss was not possible due to the absence of pre-injury imaging. Objective range-of-motion measurements were also limited because of loss of the tongue tip and the presence of spontaneous mandibular coordination during attempted elevation. Therefore, functional documentation relied primarily on clinical observation and video recordings. Further accumulation of comparable cases with objective kinematic evaluation would clarify functional prognostic indicators and reconstructive thresholds.

Conclusions

Circumferential tongue necrosis treated conservatively may lead to severe restriction of intrinsic muscle-dependent deformation while permitting partial preservation of function through altered extrinsic traction dynamics and mandibular compensation. In clinically stable patients in whom airway patency and oral intake can be maintained, conservative management may allow partial long-term functional adaptation, although residual articulatory impairment should be anticipated. Recognition of characteristic compensatory movement patterns may assist in identifying functional thresholds, estimating reconstructive necessity, and informing the timing of surgical intervention.
