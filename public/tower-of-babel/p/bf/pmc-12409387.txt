Sustained Serotonergic Stimulation Platform for Peripheral Axonal Regeneration
Authors: Sara C. Chaker, Ling Yan, Jugal Kishore Sahoo, Cengiz H. Acikel, Isaac V. Manzanera Esteve, David L. Kaplan, Michael Levin, Vijay S. Gorantla, Wesley P. Thayer, Huseyin Karagoz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeLimitations remain in peripheral nerve injury treatments. Previous studies suggest that serotonergic signaling promotes nerve regeneration by facilitating reinnervation and modulating neuronal guidance. This study aimed to evaluate the potential of serotonergic peripheral neuroregeneration using Zolmitriptan, a serotonin receptor agonist.MethodsA total of 24 female Sprague-Dawley rats were divided into four nerve injury cohorts. Sciatic nerve transection and primary repair were performed in two groups, whereas a 1-cm nerve defect was created and repaired with the same nerve segment as an autograft in the other two groups. One primary repair group and one nerve graft group received 1 mL of Zolmitriptan directly to the nerve via sonicated silk protein gels. Control groups received gels without Zolmitriptan. At postoperative 8 weeks, the sciatic nerves were collected for histological analysis. Ex vivo diffusion tensor magnetic resonance imaging was also used to assess axonal regeneration.ResultsThe primary repair cohort treated with Zolmitriptan demonstrated robust regeneration, whereas the control cohort showed poorer regeneration. There was no statistical difference in regeneration between the treated and control autograft groups. When evaluating the regeneration rates of the primary repair groups, 80% of the axons successfully extended to the distal end in the Zolmitriptan group, compared with 57% in the control group. In the autograft groups, these rates were 65% for Zolmitriptan and 42% for the control. Electron microscopy supported the axonal counting results.ConclusionsThis pilot study suggests that Zolmitriptan may enhance peripheral nerve regeneration following transection injuries, warranting further investigation for clinical translation.Clinical relevanceThis study presents promising results regarding the potential of serotonin agonists to aid in peripheral nerve recovery. Additional investigation into these findings could inform new treatment strategies for peripheral nerve injuries.

Peripheral nerve injuries (PNIs) are a substantial challenge in trauma cases, often resulting from motor vehicle accidents, penetrating wounds, or crush injuries, leading to severe functional impairments. It has been reported that about 2% of all trauma patients have a peripheral nerve injury, and 73% occur in the upper extremity, with the ulnar nerve being the most frequently injured.1 The current gold standard for repair of complete transection of the nerve involves end-to-end microsurgical repair or with autologous nerve grafts when there is a deficit in length between the two ends and end-to-end anastomosis is not possible.2,3 However, functional outcomes after PNI remain suboptimal because microsurgical techniques cannot fully address the complex molecular and pathophysiological mechanisms of peripheral nerve regeneration.

When a nerve is damaged, various biological mechanisms are elicited to promote regeneration and repair of the nerve. These processes involve activating growth-promoting pathways, proliferating Schwann cells, and upregulating neurotrophic factors, all of which are essential for guiding axonal regrowth and reestablishing functional connections.4, 5, 6 In addition to these classical mechanisms, emerging evidence suggests that modulation of the resting membrane potential can also act as a neuromodulator for axonal growth. Changes in membrane excitability have been shown to induce neural proliferation and regulate growth cone motility in axons, ultimately influencing axonal pathfinding and elongation.7, 8, 9, 10 Serotonergic signaling has been shown to exhibit a neuroregenerative effect in relation to promoting reinnervation, neural outgrowth, and modulation of neuronal guidance downstream of depolarization of the resting membrane potential.8 Although these mechanisms are primarily described in the context of the central nervous system (CNS), they provide a rationale for exploring serotonergic receptor activation as a potential therapeutic strategy in peripheral nerve injury.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the potential of serotonergic neuroregeneration using Zolmitriptan, a serotonin (5-HT) receptor agonist. Typically used for the treatment of migraines, Zolmitriptan acts upon the 5-HT1B/1D receptors, which promote vasoconstriction in intracranial blood vessels and inhibit proinflammatory neuropeptides to be released from the nerve endings.11 Although inflammation can impede the nerve regenerative process, the goal of this study was not to assess Zolmitriptan’s anti-inflammatory properties, but rather to explore its potential in maintaining a more stable and conducive environment for axonal regrowth. Despite growing evidence on serotonin’s role in neuroregeneration, its therapeutic potential in peripheral nerve injury remains underexplored. This study investigates its efficacy in a rodent model and potentially opens avenues for new adjunct treatments for PNI.

Materials and Methods

All animal handling, care, and experimental use were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, under the Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, to minimize pain and suffering.

Animals and surgical procedure

Twenty-four female Sprague-Dawley rats (250–270 g) were purchased from Envigo and were housed in ventilated cage racks under standard conditions (12-h light/dark cycle, 72°F). All rats were given access to food and water ad libitum. Rats were divided into two nerve injury repair groups and then subcategorized in each group between those who received Zolmitriptan or control, for a total of four cohorts, each containing six rats.

All surgeries were performed under 2% isoflurane anesthesia at 3 mL/min. Care was taken to prevent hypothermia in the subjects. The sciatic nerve was exposed by a 2-cm incision at the ischial notch parallel to the longitudinal axis of the left hind leg and dissected to expose the nerve proximally and distally to the trifurcation of the sciatic nerve. All animals were closed in a standard fashion using 5-0 sutures and placed on a homothermic blanket system until fully recovered. Specific nerve injury and repair were carried out in relation to the group the animal was randomly assigned to and are outlined below. The senior author performed all surgeries.

Groups 1 and 2 (cut and repair)

Twelve rats underwent standard transection of the sciatic nerve and immediate repair. The sciatic nerve was sharply transected 1 cm from the trifurcation and underwent epineurium repair using 9-0 Nylon sutures. Six rats were placed in group 1 and received 1 mL of Zolmitriptan administered directly onto the nerve following coaptation via sonicated silk gels. Group 2 (n = 6) was designated as controls and received empty sonicated silk gels administered directly on the repaired nerve.

Groups 3 and 4 (autograft)

Twelve rats underwent a nerve injury repaired with a 10-mm autograft. A 1-cm sciatic nerve segment was harvested as the distal transection being 1 cm from the trifurcation. The resected section of sciatic nerve was then reversed 180° and reattached to the defect. The autograft was coapted to the proximal and distal stumps of the sciatic nerve using 9-0 Nylon sutures. Six rats were placed in group 3 and received 1 mL of Zolmitriptan administered directly onto the nerve following coaptation via sonicated silk gels. Group 4 (n = 6) was designated as controls and received empty sonicated silk gels administered directly on the repaired nerve.

Preparation of sonicated silk hydrogels

Sonicated silk hydrogels were used to deliver the Zolmitriptan to the nerve. Sonicated silk hydrogels were used specifically due to the ability to control the delivery of molecules through fine-tuning of the hydrogel. The release profile can be changed by the pore size, whereas smaller pores slow down the release of the therapeutic.12 The use of sonication is another parameter that controls the gelation time of the hydrogel, allowing for more consistent gel structure and properties.13 In a previous pilot study (unpublished), it was determined that in vitro Zolmitriptan release parameters of the gel were to be able to release Zolmitriptan for up to 30 days, demonstrating sustained release through this mechanism. Two batches of sonicated hydrogels were prepared, one with Zolmitriptan-loaded silk hydrogels and one with only silk hydrogels. Control silk hydrogels were prepared by sonicating 1 mL of 6% weight/volume 30-minute extracted silk fibroin protein for 22 seconds at 10% amplitude. After sonication, the pre-gel solution was placed in ice and pulled into 1-mL insulin syringes and allowed to gel in the syringe. Zolmitriptan-loaded silk hydrogels were prepared by sonicating 1 mg/mL Zolmitriptan in the 6% silk solution prepared in the same way as above. Similar to the silk hydrogel, after sonication, 1 mL of the pre-gel solution was pulled into insulin syringes and allowed to gel in the syringe. Both batches of gels were UV-treated overnight for sterilization.

Behavioral assessment and tissue preparation

Following nerve injury and repair, animals in all groups were allowed to recover for 8 weeks. At the end of the study, animals performed a walking track assessment to calculate the sciatic foot index (SFI) for each rat. Measurements of footprints were taken, and the SFI was calculated using the formula derived by Bain et al.14 An SFI score of −100 was considered total impairment, whereas a normal gait was an SFI score between −11 and +11.15

At 8 weeks after surgery, following walking track analysis, animals were killed, and the injured sciatic nerve was harvested. All nerve samples were fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde/3% glutaraldehyde for 24 hours and then washed for at least 1 week in phosphate-buffered saline to remove excess fixation and then immersed in 1 mM gadopenetate dimeglumine (Magnevist; Berlex) at 4°C for 36 hours before imaging to reduce spin-lattice relaxation and corresponding scan times. Diffusion tensor imaging acquisition and analysis were conducted according to our previously published protocol.16

Diffusion tensor imaging

Diffusion tensor imaging is a magnetic resonance imaging modality that measures the directionality of water molecules in tissues.17 As electrophysiological nerve assessment studies are limited in its ability to quantify nerve recovery, this method was employed to supplement this study’s physiological and morphological analyses for axonal continuity and nerve degeneration visualization.

Morphological studies

Following magnetic resonance imaging acquisition, nerve samples were postfixed in 1% phosphate-buffered osmium tetroxide for 1 hour at room temperature. Samples were then embedded in resin, and semithin sections (1 μm) from the proximal and distal regions of the repair sides were stained with toluidine blue for axon counting. Thin sections were observed under electron microscopy.

Statistical analysis

For each cohort (groups 1, 2, 3, and 4), the total number of axons proximal and distal to the injury site was manually counted from the images captured by the electron microscopy. Differences between the two sites were then evaluated using an independent t test. A statistically significant difference was interpreted as poorer regeneration, indicating fewer axons reaching the distal end. Relative rates of regeneration were calculated by dividing the mean number of distal axons by the mean number of proximal axons for each group, multiplied by 100. All analyses were performed using Stata version 18.5 (StataCorp). Statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

Results

All animals reached the study end point without any signs of self-mutualization or foot ulcers.

Histologic results

Figures 1 and 2 present histological images, whereas Table 1 reports the mean number of myelinated axons at proximal and distal sites. For animals that received a complete nerve transection and immediate repair, when treated with Zolmitriptan, the regeneration rate was 80%, which means 80% of the axons successfully extended to the distal end, compared with 57% in the control group. The Zolmitriptan-treated group exhibited significantly higher axonal regeneration than the control (P < .05), supporting its potential role in nerve repair. In the autograft groups, these rates were 65% for Zolmitriptan and 42% for the control groups. Discussion

The present study aimed to evaluate the potential of serotonin-mediated neuroregeneration using a serotonin receptor agonist, Zolmitriptan. Histological analysis demonstrated a greater effect of Zolmitriptan on myelinated axon quantity with an equal number of axons proximal and distal in animals that received a complete nerve transection and immediate repair with Zolmitriptan treatment, suggesting robust regeneration. In the autograft groups, however, the effect of Zolmitriptan was not statistically significant although the regeneration rate was higher in the Zolmitriptan treatment group than control. The investigation of serotonergic neuroregeneration in the peripheral nervous system has yet to be fully elucidated in the literature.

Although the role of 5-HT in axon growth in the peripheral nervous system remains less understood, its role in the CNS has been thoroughly examined in the literature.8,18, 19, 20, 21 In invertebrates, Barreiro-Iglesias et al22 found that intraperitoneal injections of 5-HT following a complete spinal cord injury in zebrafish led to an increase in newly generated motor neurons. However, in goldfish, neurite regeneration was decreased by 5-HT in an optic nerve crush injury.23 Contradictory results in the literature regarding 5-HT’s role may be attributed to 5-HT receptors and the variety of types that may have varied influences on neuroregeneration following injury. Importantly, however, in Xenopus laevis tadpoles, Zolmitriptan induced large amounts of outgrowth of the optic nerve of ectopic eye grafts.8 This suggests a receptor-mediated mechanism for promoting axonal regeneration. Therefore, as a selective agonist of 5-HT1B/1D receptors, Zolmitriptan may influence regenerative processes through modulation of these specific serotonin pathways.

Investigation into improved treatments and techniques for peripheral nerve injury has proliferated in recent years. Exogenous administration of nerve growth factor and other cytokines has been a key research area, as these molecules are naturally released following a PNI and often aid in recovery and axonal regeneration. Most notably, transforming growth factor-β1 was reported to improve regeneration in vivo in a rat tibial nerve injury model.24 Moreover, nerve conduits for PNIs with substantial defects that would require grafting have been developed and investigated in their role for promoting nerve growth and recovery. These strategies aim to modulate the local microenvironment to promote axonal guidance and growth. Therefore, in this context, the results of our study suggest that Zolmitriptan, through activation of serotonin receptors, may offer a complementary pharmacologic approach to enhance nerve repair. Although research has shown that structural solutions such as conduits made with certain materials can promote greater and faster regeneration than autografts alone, the complexity of nerve regeneration limits the improvement of regeneration through these mechanisms. This underscores the need for more nuanced approaches for PNI recovery, including receptor-targeted therapies such as Zolmitriptan that may modulate the regenerative microenvironment.25

In the present study, we found limited results in the improvement of peripheral nerve regeneration following the application of Zolmitriptan. However, the greatest improvement was seen in the primary repair model rather than the autograft model. In this context, the percent regeneration observed in the primary repair model treated with Zolmitriptan was nearly double that reported in the literature, as de Ruiter et al26 reported a 42% axon regeneration rate following direct coaptation in the same nerve injury model. The difference in improvement between the two repair groups could be due to the complexity of regeneration in the graft cohort. Serotonin has been noted to promote axonal guidance in addition to its neuromodulator roles in the CNS.27 Given that axonal realignment is more straightforward in primary repairs, Zolmitriptan may facilitate a more direct pathway for axon regeneration and continuity in that setting. In contrast, autografts introduce two coaptation sites and a longer regeneration distance, increasing the likelihood of axonal misalignment and disruption of guidance cues. Therefore, misalignment or disorganization within the graft may have interfered with these mechanisms, thereby limiting Zolmitriptan’s effect in the autograft experimental groups. However, we also acknowledge that methodological factors such as graft tension or suture variability could also have contributed to the observed differences.

Moreover, the limited effect of Zolmitriptan seen in the present study could have been dampened by the dose used. One milliliter of Zolmitriptan was administered one time at the surgical nerve repair in the respective cohorts. This dose was based on a preliminary unpublished study evaluating the effect of Zolmitriptan in a rodent optic nerve transection model. Although this dose was effective in demonstrating the serotonergic-mediated nerve regeneration in the preliminary study, the effect may likely be dose-dependent and mediated differently outside the CNS. Moreover, in the autograft group, the 1-mL dose was administered across the two coaptation sites, which could have potentially led to a reduced effective dose per site. Future studies should explore dose-response relationships, as serotonergic effects may be dose-dependent, with potential nonlinear regeneration outcomes. There are several strengths to this study. To begin, this is the first study to our knowledge to investigate the use of a serotonin agonist for peripheral nerve regeneration. Given the limitations of current treatment modalities for PNIs, the exploration of Zolmitriptan to supplement these treatments to promote axonal growth is warranted. Additionally, the inclusion of different surgical repair methods allowed for a detailed investigation into the application of a serotonin agonist, optimizing the translational capacity of the study. However, this study is not without its limitations. Firstly, with the study end point of 8 weeks, long-term effects and recovery were not investigated. Although nerve regeneration mechanisms typically slow down following this period, it is important to note that functional and histological changes can continue beyond 8 weeks. This could also explain the observed differences in outcomes between the groups with some groups demonstrating histological improvement without corresponding functional improvement. Additionally, although the sample size is adequate for preliminary conclusions, it may have limited the statistical power to detect more subtle differences or variations in regeneration. Although the study was adequately powered based on an a priori analysis assuming a large effect size, it may have been underpowered to detect smaller, yet potentially meaningful, differences between groups. For example, there was a noticeable difference in the proximal-distal variation among the different groups, yet the statistical significance could not define the cause of this difference. This may be due to the dependent variables (average number of axons) having higher variability, requiring a larger sample size in each group. However, as this study faced difficulties in the trial process and limitations in the project budget, a larger sample size was unattainable. Future studies with larger sample sizes for a more robust statistical analysis will be carried out based on these results to fully elucidate the benefit of a serotonin agonist in peripheral nerve recovery.
