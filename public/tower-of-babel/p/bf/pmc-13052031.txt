The Effect of Nursing Practice Environment, Individual and Occupational Characteristics on Nurses' Attitudes Towards Medical Error: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Şehrinaz Polat, Nilgün Göktepe, Begüm Yalçin, Emine Türkmen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study was conducted to examine the effect of nursing practice environment individual and occupational characteristics on nurses' attitudes towards medical error.DesignCross‐sectional study.MethodsThe questionnaire was used for data collection in a tertiary university hospital from 4 March to 30 April 2022. The questionnaire included the “Information Form”, the “Practice Environment Scale‐Nursing Work Index (PES‐NWI)” and the “Medical Error Attitude Scale”. The population of this study included 1329 nurses. A total of 355 nurses who voluntarily completed the questionnaires constituted the study sample. Convenience sampling was used to select participants. Since the study was conducted at a single hospital, the survey was emailed to all nurses at the institution to ensure broad accessibility. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess the relationships between continuous variables. Multiple linear regression analysis was employed to examine factors influencing the scores on the Medical Error Attitude Scale. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional ethics committee, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. The findings were reported following the STROBE guidelines.ResultsThe mean score of the PES‐NWI was 2.60 ± 0.45 and the mean score of the Medical Error Attitude Scale was (3.50 ± 0.39). Regression analysis was performed to identify factors influencing nurses' attitudes towards medical errors. According to the results of the multiple regression analysis, age (p < 0.001), duration of experience in the institution (p < 0.001), educational status (p < 0.001), general satisfaction with patient safety in the unit (p < 0.05) and two subdimensions of the PES‐NWI—“Nurse manager ability, leadership and support of nurses” (p < 0.05) and “Staffing and resource adequacy” (p < 0.05)—were significant predictors. These six variables collectively explained 12.8% of the total variance in the Medical Error Attitude Scale score (R 2 = 12.8%, F = 7.454, p < 0.001).ConclusionsThe study concluded that both the nursing practice environment and certain individual and occupational characteristics of nurses impact their attitudes towards medical errors. In line with this study's results, we recommend that institutional managers and nurse managers make necessary arrangements to improve nurses' attitudes towards medical errors, as well as to develop institutional strategies designed to prevent medical errors in their units/institutions, thereby improving patient safety and quality of care.No Patient or Public ContributionThe participants of this study were nurses and this contribution has been explained in the data collection section. There was no patient contribution in this study.

Introduction

Approximately one in every 20 patients experiences preventable harm in the healthcare environment (Panagioti et al. 2019). Globally, one out of every four to five healthcare professionals commits medical errors, with prevalence rates ranging between 0.35% and 6%, leading to patient harm (Khammarnia et al. 2021; Lind et al. 2020; Panagioti et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2023). A systematic review and meta‐analysis of studies conducted in Iran from 2005 to 2019 revealed medical error rates of 31% among physicians and 37% among nurses (Khammarnia et al. 2021). Another study indicated that 12% of preventable medical errors are severe or result in death (Panagioti et al. 2019). In the United States, medical errors cause approximately 250,000 deaths annually (Makary and Daniel 2016). Patients who experience adverse events and medical errors have significantly longer hospital and ICU stays, undergo more invasive interventions and have higher in‐hospital mortality rates compared to other patients (Ahmed et al. 2015; Cantor et al. 2022; Mangus and Mahajan 2019). Additionally, patient harm is associated with substantial financial losses (Cantor et al. 2022; Slawomirski and Klazinga 2022; Welling and Takala 2023).

Nurses have a distinctive yet frequently unnoticed role in recognizing, averting and alleviating medical errors (Gaffney et al. 2016). Various factors contribute to the incident of medical errors, which Ghezeljeh et al. (2022) categorized into four main groups: non‐organizational difficulties, organizational location, specificity of the care environment and individual causes (Ghezeljeh et al. 2022). The study investigated how the nursing practice environment influences nurses' attitudes towards medical errors.

Background

The nursing practice environment examines the situations in the workplace that can either facilitate or impede professional nursing practices (Swiger et al. 2017). Characteristics of a positive practice environment include having an adequate number of qualified nurses, access to professional development and autonomy for nurses, supportive communication and cooperation among the health care team and nurses and involvement of nurses in leadership and management (Aiken and Fagin 2018; Kocaman et al. 2018; Lake 2007; Swiger et al. 2017). A conducive nursing practice environment equips nurses with essential physical, mental and social resources (Huang et al. 2021).

Recent studies continue to show that more favourable nursing practice environments are associated with greater nurse autonomy, which can make nursing staff feel appreciated and motivated to contribute actively to patient care. Studies using the Practice Environment Scale of the Nursing Work Index (PES‐NWI) also demonstrate links between supportive work environments, nurse empowerment and positive nurse outcomes such as job satisfaction and intention to remain in the hospital (Al‐Ghraiybah et al. 2024; Göktepe et al. 2021; Lake et al. 2024; Paguio et al. 2020; Taketomi et al. 2024). It also reduces medication errors (Paguio et al. 2020; Swiger et al. 2017), patient falls (Paguio et al. 2020) and patient mortality rates (Aiken et al. 2014, 2017), thereby increasing patient perceptions of and satisfaction with the quality of nursing care (Aiken et al. 2021; Paguio et al. 2020).

While researchers have shown that favourable practice environments yield positive outcomes for patients, nurses and institutions (Aiken et al. 2017; Al‐El‐Gazar, Abousoliman, et al. 2024; Paguio et al. 2020; Pereira et al. 2024), there is a paucity of studies investigating the relationship between the practice environment and attitudes towards medical errors. Recognizing that attitudes frequently precede behaviour (Brown et al. 2020), comprehending the factors shaping nurses' attitudes towards medical errors is pivotal for error prevention.

Attitudes towards medical errors in recent literature are likewise described as multidimensional constructs that integrate cognitive beliefs, emotional reactions and behavioural tendencies towards error recognition, reporting and prevention. These attitudes are influenced by factors such as nurses' attitudes towards reporting, perceived behavioural control, subjective norms, expectations regarding reactions from superiors, colleagues and patients, prior experiences of errors, knowledge levels, ethical sensitivity, perceived organizational culture and expectations about the consequences of reporting (Braiki et al. 2024; Mendonca and Schmidt 2020; Pehlivan and Yeşilyurt 2024). However, studies indicate that many medical errors go unrecognized by staff, who often harbour negative attitudes towards them due to fear of repercussions for reporting (Mendonca and Schmidt 2020; Moreno‐Leal et al. 2024). Consequently, medical error reporting rates remain low. Improving employees' attitudes towards medical errors is essential for enhancing error reporting (Moreno‐Leal et al. 2024). A study involving newly graduated nurses emphasized the importance of fostering a positive practice environment and supporting nurses' personal development to achieve optimal organizational outcomes (Wang et al. 2024).

All healthcare services involve inherent risks, and medical errors often occur when preventable and unintended mistakes by healthcare professionals result in harm to patients during treatment (Panagioti et al. 2019). Nurses, frequently juggling multiple tasks, are required to follow institutional patient safety protocols, which include assessing patients for harm and risk, devising care plans, conducting monitoring and surveillance, providing support and cooperating with other healthcare professionals (Vaismoradi et al. 2020). Given their constant presence at the patient's bedside and regular interactions with various members of the medical team, including physicians, pharmacists, families and other healthcare professionals, nurses play a crucial role in ensuring timely coordination and communication of the patient's condition to the team. Nurses are widely recognized as central actors in preventing medical errors across multiple domains of care, including medication safety, surveillance for patient deterioration and coordination of the healthcare team. Their continuous presence at the bedside, involvement in monitoring, communication and adherence to safety protocols enables them to detect risks early and intervene to prevent errors made both by themselves and by other team members (Alrasheeday et al. 2024; Alqhtani et al. 2025; Albeshri et al. 2024; Vaismoradi et al. 2020). Awareness among nurses regarding the risks associated with medical errors and their positive attitude towards errors can significantly contribute to reducing such errors and enhancing the quality and safety of healthcare services.

Design

A cross‐sectional design using an anonymous online survey was conducted.

Samples and Setting

The study was conducted at a public university hospital in the AAAA region of BBBB, which serves patients from all areas. The population comprised 1329 nurses employed at the hospital. The sample size was determined using the known sample formula “(n = N·t 2·p·q/d 2·(N − 1) + t 2·p·q)”, with a minimum requirement of 299 nurses. The inclusion criteria were (1) being a nurse in the hospital where this study was conducted and (2) volunteering to participate in the study. Since the study was conducted in a single hospital, questionnaires were sent to all nurses in the hospital to increase the sample size, and 355 nurses who completed the questionnaires constituted the entire sample. Convenience sampling was used to select participants. Post hoc power analysis using the G Power 3.1 program, indicated a standardized effect size of 0.05 α, 95% power, R 2 of 12.13% and six predictors, confirming the adequacy of the sample size. Reporting followed the “Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement” guidelines.

Data Collection

In this descriptive and cross‐sectional study, data were collected between March 4, 2022, and April 30, 2022. The questionnaire form was prepared as Google Forms and sent to the nurses via email. The Director of Nursing Services of the hospital where the study was conducted was contacted and asked to email the survey link to all nurses in the sample. After the approval of the director of nursing services, the distribution of the questionnaire by e‐mail was started. No name or e‐mail information was requested in the questionnaire. The questionnaire was designed to ensure that the participants answered all the questions.

Instruments

The “Information Form”, the “Practice Environment Scale‐Nursing Work Index (PES‐NWI)” and the “Medical Error Attitude Scale” were used in the study.

Information Form

The form given to the nurses who participated in the study collected information on their age, gender, marital status and educational background. Additionally, it gathered data on their duration of occupational experience, length of tenure in the current unit, work schedule and position. Furthermore, the form included two questions aimed at assessing nurses' satisfaction levels: one concerning their perceived workload in the department (categorized as high, balanced, or low workload), and another regarding their general satisfaction with the unit's patient safety practices (rated on a scale from 1 to 5, ranging from “not satisfied at all” to “very satisfied”).

Practice Environment Scale‐Nursing Work Index (PES‐NWI)

The scale used in the study, originally developed by Lake (2002), was adapted into Turkish and validated by Türkmen et al. (2011). Permission to use the scale was obtained by email from the author who conducted the adaptation. This 4‐point Likert‐type scale comprises five subscales, with answers varying from (4) strongly agree to (1) strongly disagree. To compute subscale scores, the item scores within each subscale were aggregated, and the sum of the scores was divided by the number of items in the subscale. The mean score for each subscale falls between one and four. For the total scale score, the average scores of the five subscales were combined and divided by five, yielding a scale score ranging from one to four. Higher scores on the scale indicate more favourable perceptions of the practice environment. The Cronbach's α coefficient for the total scale was 0.82 in Lake's original development (0.71–0.84 for the subscales), 0.94 in the Turkish adaptation study by Türkmen et al. (2011) (0.80–0.87 for subscales) and 0.93 in the current study (0.71–0.93 for subscales). These coefficients indicate high internal consistency reliability for the scale and its subscales across different studies.

The Medical Error Attitude Scale

The Medical Error Attitude Scale employed in the study was formulated by Güleç and Seren‐İntepeler (2013) and comprises three subscales: “medical error perception” (two items), “approach to medical errors” (seven items) and “causes of medical errors” (seven items). Responses to the 16 items on this five‐point Likert‐type scale vary from (1) Strongly disagree to (5) Strongly agree. The overall scale score ranges between one and five. Two items on the scale (items 10 and 13) are reverse‐coded. The scores of these items were reversed, and then all item scores were summed to obtain a raw score. The scale's cut‐off point is three: attitudes towards medical errors below an average score of three are considered negative, while scores of three and above are considered positive attitudes. Negative attitudes suggest a limited awareness of the significance of medical errors and error reporting, while positive attitudes reflect a heightened awareness. During internal consistency testing, Cronbach's α reliability coefficient for the overall scale was determined to be 0.75. The internal consistency reliability of the subscales was 0.74 for “medical error perception”, 0.62 for “approach to medical errors”, and 0.60 for “causes of medical errors” (Güleç and Seren‐İntepeler 2013). In the current study, Cronbach's α reliability coefficient was 0.77 for the overall scale and 0.88, 0.66 and 0.69 for the subscales, respectively. These coefficients indicate acceptable internal consistency reliability for the scale and its subscales in both studies.

Ethical Review

Prior to the commencement of the research, ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of a public university hospital (Date: 03.01.2022, Number: 680998). Additionally, permission was obtained from the hospital administration where the research was carried out (Date: 03.03.2022, Number: 788750). This study was conducted in accordance with the World Medical Association's Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent and were informed that their anonymity would be protected. The informed consent form included the information that “participants have the right not to participate in the study or to withdraw from the study at any time after participation, that the information obtained from the study will be used solely for research purposes,” and that their personal information will be kept confidential, but that anonymized data may be used for publication purposes. The first page of the data collection tool contained detailed information about the research's purpose, scope and ethical considerations. The voluntary consent form on the first page of the online survey included the options “I agree to participate in the study” and “I do not agree to participate in the study.” Consent was obtained from all participants who agreed to participate in the study, and only nurses who selected the “I agree to participate in the study” option could proceed to the remaining questionnaire pages. Permission to use the relevant scales was obtained from the authors who adapted them for use in the study. It is worth noting that there was no hierarchical relationship between the participants and the researchers involved in the study. To ensure anonymity, the questionnaire did not include any identifying information such as participants' names or contact details.

Data Analysis

The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) program was used for statistical analyses. There were no missing data. The results were evaluated at the 95% confidence interval, and significance was accepted at p < 0.05. Skewness and Kurtosis tests were used to assess the normality of scores from a continuous variable in statistical methods. Descriptive analyses were employed to examine the individual and occupational characteristics of the participating nurses and the scores they obtained from the scales. Cronbach's α values were calculated to assess the scales' reliability. The relationship between two continuous variables was analysed using Pearson's correlation coefficient. Lastly, multiple linear regression analysis was employed to examine factors influencing the scores on the Medical Error Attitude Scale. Using backward elimination, all variables were initially entered into the model, and those significant at the 0.05 level were retained.

Discussion

This study revealed that several factors significantly influenced nurses' attitudes towards medical errors. Specifically, nurses' age, duration of experience in the institution, educational status, general satisfaction with patient safety practices in the unit, “nurse manager ability, leadership and support of nurses”, as well as “staffing and resource adequacy”, were found to have an impact on nurses' attitudes towards medical errors.

The study's results showed that as nurses' age increased, their medical error attitude scores decreased. In other words, younger nurses were found to be more responsive to medical errors. Mushi et al. (2025), in their study on associated factors and predictors of medication errors across Saudi healthcare settings, found that younger workers under 35 had significantly lower odds of committing errors than older workers, similar to our study. Contrary to our result, while many studies report no correlation between nurses' age and their attitudes towards medical error (Alemdar and Yilmaz 2020; Aktan and Atay 2021; Dinçer et al. 2023; Kandemir and Yüksel 2020; Kavuran and Çetinkaya 2022; Kirca et al. 2020; Önler et al. 2021; Özyer et al. 2020), while others indicate that as nurses' age increases, medical error attitude scores also increase (Büyük et al. 2021; Doğan and Kebapçı 2025; Kandemir and Yüksel 2020; Ulusoy and Tosun 2020; Wang et al. 2023). In the study by Bastani et al. (2024), it was found that as nurses' age increased, the average score for determinants affecting medication errors decreased. In their study conducted in Iran, Mohebifar et al. (2025) reported that medication administration errors decreased with increasing nurse age. The current study finds that nurses' medical error attitude scores decrease with age, which underscores the importance of refresher training on medical errors for older nurses. Younger nurses' heightened awareness of medical errors may reflect the fact that newly graduated nurses or those in the early years of their careers are more responsive and sensitive to them.

This study also found that nurses with more years of experience in the institution exhibited more positive attitudes towards medical errors. Contrary to this finding, some studies in the literature (Aktan and Atay 2021; Özyer et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2025) report no difference between the duration of experience in the institution and attitude towards medical errors. However, other studies have shown that employees with four or more years of experience (Kavuran and Çetinkaya 2022) and those with 13 or more years of experience (Özen et al. 2019) exhibit more positive attitudes towards medical error. This may be because more experienced nurses are more likely to adopt and apply institutional patient safety practices. Although there are few studies examining the relationship between nurses' length of experience and their attitudes towards medical errors, a review of the literature indicates that medication errors decrease as nurses gain more professional experience. In the 2024 study by Bastani et al. (2024), it was determined that as the experience of the participating nurses increased, the average score of determinants affecting medication errors decreased. Alrasheeday et al. (2024) on medication error reporting among nurses in Saudi Arabia reported that nurses with 10 years or more of experience had higher rates of medication error reporting. The Mohebifar et al. (2025) study reported that medication administration errors decreased as nurses gained experience. Li, Yue, et al. (2025) reported that nurses aged 26–35 and those with 6–10 years of experience were the most common groups to experience Medication Administration Errors (MAE). Li, Liu, et al. (2025) in their study in China, having more years of professional practice was associated with lower odds of self‐reported MEs.

The current study's results revealed that nurses' educational status affected their attitudes towards medical error, with nurses holding bachelor's or postgraduate degrees displaying more positive attitudes. While some studies report no connection between these two factors (Alemdar and Yilmaz 2020; Özyer et al. 2020; Kandemir and Yüksel 2020; Ulusoy and Tosun 2020; Wang et al. 2023; Kirca et al. 2020), Gök and Sarı (2017) found that nurses holding undergraduate or postgraduate degrees exhibited more positive attitudes towards medical error. Similarly, both Aktan and Atay (2021) and Büyük et al. (2021) reported that nurses with a postgraduate degree have higher medical error attitude scores, and Araby et al. (2018) indicated that medical errors decrease as nurses' educational level increases. Nield et al. (2025) also noted that nurses with higher levels of formal education (doctoral‐prepared vs. master's‐prepared) had higher scores on the Medical Error Attitude Scale. Li, Liu, et al. (2025) found in their study in China that having a bachelor's degree or higher was associated with a lower likelihood of self‐reported ME. In addition, some studies consistently show that hospitals with a higher proportion of bachelor‐educated nurses have lower complication and mortality rates, as well as shorter lengths of stay and fewer readmissions. These studies support the view that a better‐educated nursing workforce contributes in a measurable way to the improvement of patient outcomes (Lasater et al. 2021; Schnelli et al. 2024), and every 10% increase in the ratio of undergraduate nurses has been reported to result in a 9% decrease in patient deaths (Lasater et al. 2021). According to current studies, undergraduate nursing education positively affects nurses' attitudes towards medical errors, and as nurses' educational level increases, their professional competencies and awareness of medical errors also increase.

According to this study's results, nurses' general satisfaction with patient safety in the unit positively affected their attitudes towards medical errors. Although no studies to date have focused directly on this topic, some indicate a correlation between patient safety practices in nurses' institutions/units and medical error training/outcomes. Existing research supports the associations between patient safety culture, safety climate, proneness to medical errors and reporting behaviours. In addition, training programmes for nurses focusing on patient safety and medical errors have been shown to enhance safety awareness and promote safe nursing practices, while reducing proneness to errors and negative attitudes towards reporting them. (Alemdar and Yilmaz 2020; Alsahli et al. 2024; Alsobou et al. 2025; Kandemir and Yüksel 2020; Kim and Kim 2025; Mert et al. 2025; Park and Yeom 2025). In the study by Bastani et al. (2024) on medication errors and their relationship with patient safety culture, it was reported that as patient safety culture improves, medical errors decrease and that nurses' efforts to strengthen patient safety culture positively influence the reduction of medication error incidence within hospital units. Similarly, Alsobou et al. (2025) found a positive correlation between patient safety culture and nurses' attitudes towards incident reporting among Jordanian nurses. In the study by Nasiri et al. (2022), patient safety culture was also found to be directly related to nurses' medication errors. The current study's results may indicate that nurse satisfaction with positive interventions regarding patient safety in the unit and the approaches of team members/colleagues have a positive impact on individual attitudes towards medical errors.

This study also found that “nurse manager ability, leadership and support of nurses”, which is one of the PES‐NWI subscales, had a positive effect on nurses' attitudes towards medical error. Although no studies were found in the literature that focused precisely on this topic, Palweni et al. (2023) reported that nurse managers' leadership styles influence patient safety, Chegini et al. (2020) demonstrated that leadership coaching behaviour affects error reporting, and Güneş et al. (2020) highlighted that fear of being blamed by management adversely impacts the reporting of medication errors. Moreno‐Leal et al. (2024) argued that management behaviour influences the reporting of medical errors and near misses in healthcare organizations. In the meta‐analysis by Lake et al. (2024), the leadership and support subscale of the PES‐NWI was identified as one of the highest‐scoring dimensions and was emphasized as contributing to improved patient safety outcomes. In the scoping review by Moraca et al. (2024) on nursing leadership style and error management culture, similar to the present study, a positive relationship between nursing leadership and error management was observed, with reduced errors and increased benefits. In the systematic mixed‐methods review by Braiki et al. (2024) on factors influencing the reporting of medication errors and near misses among nurses, leadership style, safety climate and organizational trust were identified as significant factors. In the study by Cui et al. (2025), medication errors were significantly associated with “manager expectations and actions to promote patient safety” and “management support for patient safety.” On the other hand, Lappalainen et al. (2020) reported a moderate and statistically significant correlation between nurse managers' transformational leadership and medication safety. Similarly, in the study by Kim et al. (2020), transformational leadership was positively associated with perceived benefits of system use and reduced medication errors. Recent studies conducted in different countries indicate that nurses' medication error reporting rates increase in the presence of a non‐punitive, learning‐oriented culture, trust‐based relationships and safe reporting systems. Conversely, factors such as fear of blame, concern about disciplinary action, hierarchical power distance and loss of face are identified as key barriers that hinder nurses from reporting errors (Alrasheeday et al. 2024; Ravi et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2025). In addition, Dyab et al. (2018) emphasized the importance of improved participation in medication error reporting and of a culture of non‐incriminating reporting, and Aydeniz and İntepeler (2021) found that employees expect their managers to provide support, assistance, understanding and a non‐judgmental approach when reporting errors. Research indicates that a supportive attitude and a fair, system‐focused approach by managers can strengthen nurses' intentions to report errors. Nurses particularly expect their managers to adopt a non‐blaming, understanding stance that offers help and guidance when errors occur. In settings where these expectations are not met, the tendency to report errors has been shown to decrease (Najafpour et al. 2021; Ravi et al. 2024; Svitlica and Konstantinidis 2024; Tuvor et al. 2025). Our findings are consistent with those of El‐Gazar, Shawer, et al. (2024), who found that decent working conditions encourage nurses to take greater ownership of their work, enhancing their vigour and improving nursing behaviours. Consistent with our study's results, a supportive practice environment and adequate resources provide a basis for nurses to develop more constructive attitudes towards medical errors. In their study, El‐Gazar and Zoromba (2025) found that favourable working conditions strengthened nurses' sense of responsibility and belonging towards their organization, thereby increasing their willingness to report errors internally. This finding is consistent with our observation that nurses who experience positive working conditions and greater satisfaction with patient safety demonstrate more constructive and reporting‐oriented attitudes towards medical errors. Together with the findings of Zoromba et al. (2025), which showed that patient‐initiated mistreatment undermines caring behaviours through psychological disengagement but that managerial support and positive social interactions mitigate this, our study's result that staffing/resource adequacy and managerial support enhance attitudes towards medical errors suggests that structural and relational resources in the nursing practice environment both play a protective role in sustaining a culture of patient safety. Furthermore, our study found that nurse manager leadership and support are associated with more positive attitudes towards errors. Evaluated alongside the results of El‐Gazar, Baghdadi, et al. (2024), which show that ambidextrous leadership enhances creativity via psychological safety, this suggests that leadership practices are a key organizational resource that strengthens performance, error reporting and safety‐focused innovative behaviours. This study also found that staffing and resource adequacy, another subscale of the PES‐NWI, had a positive impact on nurses' attitudes towards medical errors. According to the literature, a lack of staffing and resources is among the leading causes of medical errors, suggesting a correlation between attitudes towards medical error and the number of patients receiving daily care (Aktan and Atay 2021; Dall'Ora et al. 2022; Lasater et al. 2021; Uchmanowicz et al. 2024). In the study by Nasiri et al. (2022), among the subdimensions of the nursing work environment, the adequacy of nursing staff was found to have a direct, significant relationship with nurses' medication error rates. In the study by Li, Yue, et al. (2025), environment, equipment, and infrastructure, working settings, and workload and workflow were identified as important factors influencing medication administration errors. The literature shows that when patient numbers are high, the rate of medical error increases (Eraslan and Bozkurt 2023) and that nurse shortages result in both increased medical error and effort‐reward imbalances (Wang et al. 2023). In the systematic review by Rashdan et al. (2025) on human factors frameworks used to analyse contributory factors to medication errors, one of the organizational influences highlighted was resource management and inadequate resource management was identified as a high‐frequency contributory factor. Supported by the literature, the current study indicates that adequate staffing and resources in the nursing practice environment positively impact nurses' attitudes towards medical errors.

Limitations

The data in this study were collected from nurses at a single hospital, limiting the study's generalizability. Secondly, data collection during the COVID‐19 pandemic, when nurses experienced changes in working conditions (such as high patient loads and excessive workloads), may have affected the nursing practice environment. Lastly, the data in this study are based on nurses' self‐reports, which may be considered a limitation. The fact that the data were collected solely based on participating nurses' self‐reports may lead to social desirability bias. The cross‐sectional design of the study prevents the establishment of causal relationships.

Furthermore, because the sample was selected from a single institution, the generalizability of the results is limited. The observed homogeneity in the distribution of scale scores may have made it challenging to identify specific relationships. Finally, the low explained variance suggests that other individual or institutional factors may also play a role in attitudes towards medical errors. Another limitation of this study is that research on attitudes towards medical errors is limited, and the majority of studies on the subject have been conducted in Türkiye, which may limit the discussion of the research findings.

For future research, it is recommended that other psychosocial, organizational and cultural variables that affect nurses' attitudes towards medical errors be considered. Longitudinal studies to clarify causal relationships and repeated studies with larger samples in different clinical settings would be beneficial. An in‐depth examination of nurses' experiences and opinions using qualitative methods may provide a more comprehensive view of the subject. Furthermore, the impact of organizational support mechanisms and leadership practices on attitudes towards medical errors can be evaluated in intervention‐based studies.

Conclusions

This study revealed that nurses' attitudes towards medical errors, which play a critical role in delivering high‐quality and safe patient care, are shaped by both individual/occupational characteristics and factors related to the nursing practice environment. The findings showed that age, length of institutional experience, educational background and overall satisfaction with patient safety in the unit are significant predictors of attitudes regarding medical errors. Furthermore, supportive nurse manager leadership and adequate staffing/resources were associated with more positive attitudes towards errors. However, the relatively low variance explained suggests that other unmeasured factors may also be influential. Given the cross‐sectional design and reliance on self‐report data, future research should consider longitudinal and mixed‐method designs, expand institutional diversity and examine additional psychosocial and organizational variables to deepen understanding and improve safety culture in clinical settings.

In line with the findings of this study, we recommend that institutional managers and nurse leaders make the necessary arrangements to improve nurses' attitudes towards medical errors and develop institutional strategies designed to prevent medical errors in their units/institutions, thereby reducing patient safety problems and improving quality of care.
