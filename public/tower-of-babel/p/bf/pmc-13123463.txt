Distinctive domestication of farmhouse beer yeasts preserved pre-industrial genetic and phenotypic diversity
Authors: Peter W. Bircham, Andrea Del Cortona, Lars Marius Garshol, Mohammed T. Tawfeeq, Beatriz Herrera-Malaver, Sofie Mannaerts, Jeroen Cortebeeck, Robbe Nolmans, Brigida Gallone, Anton Gorkovskiy, Michael Abrouk, Jan Steensels, Kevin J. Verstrepen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySince its inception over 10,000 years ago, beer brewing has transformed from a domestic activity based on uncontrolled spontaneous fermentations into a highly optimized industrial process that uses pure single-strain starter cultures of Saccharomyces yeasts. Along with the storage of frozen yeast stocks, this shift halted the domestication process of beer yeasts and led to a massive loss of beer yeast biodiversity. However, a few traditional farmhouse brewers in Northern and Eastern Europe still rely on artisanal fermentation methods, using mixed cultures of yeasts that are passed on from one fermentation to the next. We genetically and phenotypically analyzed 1,760 isolates from 44 traditional European farmhouse ale yeast cultures from Norway, Latvia, Lithuania, and Russia. We find that farmhouse cultures harbor remarkable genetic diversity, from near-monocultures to intricate communities of over 30 different S. cerevisiae variants. Farmhouse yeast genomes exhibit clear geographic structuring but also reveal signs of admixture alongside lineage-specific genomic features, such as a horizontally transferred gene cluster in Baltic strains. Large-scale phenotyping reveals that farmhouse yeasts harbor domestication signatures that are distinct from those of industrial beer yeasts, including maintenance of a sexual cycle, higher stress tolerance, and more diverse flavor profiles. Together, our study reveals the complex structure and diversity of farmhouse cultures and provides a reservoir of new yeast strains that can propel the next wave of beer innovation.

Introduction

Beer brewing has been deeply intertwined with human development and culture.1,2 Since its inception over 10,000 years ago, the production of beer spread across the globe, yet it remained a small-scale domestic practice, with farmers brewing beer for their families and neighbors, often using grain grown on their own land.3 Typically, a portion of the yeast from each fermentation was harvested to initiate the next fermentation batch throughout the brewing season. These cycles of continuous cultivation and re-inoculation of yeasts, called “backslopping,” effectively isolated the yeasts from their natural habitats and drove adaptation to the man-made environment, a process referred to as “domestication.”4,5,6 Beer yeasts have followed several domestication trajectories, most clearly illustrated by the divergence between conventional ale and lager lineages. Genomic surveys indicate that major ale groups fall into at least two broad clades: Beer1, consisting of three geographically delineated subclades (Belgium/Germany, UK, and US), and Beer2, which clusters nearer wine lineages, consistent with secondary domestication from other fermentation environments.7 Both ale clades share typical domestication signatures, such as amplification of MAL loci, enabling efficient maltose utilization in the wort.7,8 Interestingly, the origin of lager yeast, Saccharomyces pastorianus, entailed an additional domestication step via interspecies hybridization between a brewing-adapted Saccharomyces cerevisiae ancestor from the Beer1 “Belgium/Germany” clade and the cold-tolerant species S. eubayanus.9,10

From the 13th century onward, brewing gradually shifted from a purely domestic activity to a more specialized industrial process that took place in breweries that catered to multiple households.11,12 A key innovation occurred in 1883 at the Carlsberg Brewery, where Emil Christian Hansen developed a method for isolating and propagating pure yeast cultures.13 Along with the invention of mechanical refrigeration that allowed storing yeast stocks in frozen form, the isolation of pure cultures gradually led to the replacement of traditional backslopping practices with the use of cryopreserved stocks derived from a single, defined yeast strain.14 Moreover, the widespread distribution of the most efficient strains among brewers resulted in the dominance of only a small number of yeast strains.10 This industrialization of brewing influenced the evolution and domestication of brewing yeast in three main ways. First, as fermentation became more controlled and standardized, yeasts were selected to grow efficiently in pure cultures at specific temperatures (typically between 10°C and 20°C). Moreover, continuous cycles of fermentation likely reduced the need for cells to sporulate and survive periods of starvation and drought. Many of today’s industrial beer yeasts have therefore lost the capacity for sexual reproduction, including sporulation and mating.7,15 Lastly, the adoption of pure cultures caused a massive decline in beer yeast diversity, because only a few different yeasts were isolated and subsequently spread over different breweries in different parts of the world, causing a clear genetic bottleneck.5,7,9,10,16

In certain less-densely populated regions, however, industrial beer brewing did not completely replace traditional, small-scale farmhouse brewing practices. Some households or small artisan breweries are still producing beer in the traditional way, fostering unique environments without the selective pressures typically associated with industrial brewing or the adoption of pure cultures and frozen stocks. Interestingly, each region’s distinct traditional brewing techniques and yeast management practices have led to separate, divergent evolutionary paths, giving rise to multiple unique domesticated lineages that show evidence of adaptation to specific conditions. In Belgian “Gueuze” beers, for example, new interspecific hybrids emerged, such as S. cerevisiae × Saccharomyces kudriavzevii.9 These hybrids show improved cold tolerance, likely as an adaptation to the prolonged fermentation and barrel aging typical of gueuze farm breweries. They also display resistance to organic acids, which may result from co-cultivation with lactic and acetic acid bacteria in the barrel. In Northern and Eastern Europe, however, vastly different farmhouse brewing practices are used. Fermentations are typically started at exceptionally high temperatures (30°C–42°C) and with a high initial sugar content, up to 28° Plato (i.e., approximately 280 g/L). In some cases, unboiled wort is used, and juniper branches may be added during the process.3,17,18 Moreover, yeasts are still harvested and dried on straw, cloth, or specially constructed wooden equipment or stored wet in a jar or bottle after each fermentation round, practices that resemble those used in pre-industrial Western Europe to preserve yeast between brewing seasons.3 The yeast is kept in this form until it is used to inoculate the next fermentation batch, a period that can range from a few days up to a few months. Although these farmhouse cultures have not yet been investigated in great detail, recent studies showed that these strains are genetically distinct from industrial Western European beer yeasts.19,20,21,22

Here, we report the first large-scale systematic investigation of farmhouse yeast diversity of Northern and Eastern Europe by genetically and phenotypically characterizing 1,760 isolates from 44 distinct cultures. Our study reveals that farmhouse yeast cultures typically consist of complex S. cerevisiae populations, ranging from near-monoculture to cultures of over 30 distinct strains. Many of the strains show specific adaptation toward environmental conditions encountered in farmhouse beer production but also share domestication traits with industrial beer strains. Genetic investigation revealed geographical delineation of farmhouse yeasts but also uncovered patterns of strain exchange between neighboring farmhouses. Moreover, it highlighted the importance of admixture and horizontal gene transfer (HGT) in shaping the evolution of farmhouse yeasts. Together, our results reveal how the distinct domestication route of farmhouse yeasts from Northern and Eastern Europe resulted in a vast genotypic and phenotypic diversity that far exceeds that of the industrial beer yeasts. These results not only offer novel insights into the composition and characteristics of fascinating ancient brewing yeast populations but also open routes toward the selection of yeasts with specific properties that may be valuable for various industrial processes.

Results

Farmhouse yeast cultures harbor mixed populations of S. cerevisiae

Samples were obtained from 44 Northern and Eastern European farmhouse yeast cultures, including those from Western and Eastern Norway, Latvia, Lithuania, and Russia (Figure 1A; Table S1). For each, 40 random isolates were obtained and assigned a unique identifier “NNRXX,” where NN is the culture number from the Farmhouse Yeast Registry (Table S1), “R” denotes a random isolate, and “XX” is the isolate number (1–40). Over 98% (1,738/1,760) of isolates are S. cerevisiae. All non-cerevisiae isolates are Pichia membranifaciens, a common fermentation spoiler.26 As farmhouse cultures are continuously exposed to the environment, for example, in open fermenters or during traditional storage (Figure S1), this suggests strong selective pressure for S. cerevisiae during brewing and/or preservation.

Genetic fingerprinting of all 1,738 S. cerevisiae isolates identified 557 unique strains. Of these, 23 strains showed strong similarities to known industrial strains (Figure S2A). Although it is in principle possible that farmhouse cultures are the original isolation source of these industrial yeasts, their phylogenetic placement as a distinct outgroup (see further) and historical records27 favors the alternative hypothesis that these isolates originate from commercial sources and were introduced relatively recently into farmhouse cultures. As these isolates therefore do not represent authentic farmhouse yeasts, we define them as “allochthonous yeast” and highlight them as such in the figures.

Culture compositions varied widely, ranging from near-monocultures with >80% of the population consisting of a single dominant strain (e.g., #3, #16, and #43) to highly diverse mixtures containing up to 33 strains (#5), with a median of 14.5 strains per culture (Figure 1B). To assess how brewing practices might shape strain diversity, we analyzed the correlation between diversity and variables such as wort (substrate) temperature during the early fermentation phase (“pitching temperature”), total fermentation time, and preservation method (Figure 1C). Of these, only the preservation method showed a significant effect on diversity, with cultures preserved by drying (e.g., on cloth or straw) showing greater strain diversity than those stored in liquid (e.g., in jars or bottles) (p = 0.014, Mann-Whitney U test; Figure S2B).

Plotting all pairwise genetic similarities of isolates originating from the same culture shows that some cultures (e.g., #6, #14, #29, #39, and #46) were composed of only closely related strains, likely originating from a single ancestral strain that diversified over time (Figure 1D), while others (e.g., #27, #28, #40, #41, #44, and #68) consisted of genetically distinct populations coexisting within the same environment.

To explore genetic relationships among farmhouse yeast cultures, we used the fingerprinting data to generate a cladogram of all isolates (Figure 2A).23 The cladogram reveals that isolates from the same culture tend to form separate subclusters. However, it also shows signs of strain sharing or common ancestry between cultures, especially in cultures from the same region. Next, we further investigated culture overlap, i.e., the presence of identical or near-identical strains appearing in different cultures (Figure 2B). No overlap was found with any Russian, Baltic, or South-East (SE) Norway cultures. By contrast, overlap was common within, and sometimes between, NW and SW Norway cultures, even between those separated by large distances (e.g., #3 and #44, over 250 km). Network analysis revealed several geographically coherent clusters. For example, #11 and #18 (both from Hornindal) share isolates, as do #27 and #28 (both from Ål). Likewise, #12, #13, AA, and AB form a cluster, spanning two villages ∼27 km apart. Other overlaps were less straightforward: #2 and #35 share strains, despite their NW and SW Norway origins, suggesting occasional longer-range exchange (or shared ancestry) beyond local geography. Importantly, geographical proximity did not always translate into shared strains, and several patterns indicate culture-specific histories. Some cultures from neighboring farms (< 10 km apart) show no strain overlap at all (e.g., #46 and #68). Similarly, #2, currently used in Dyrvedalen, shows no overlap with other cultures in that valley, and several Western Norway cultures (#14, #69, AC, and AE) show no overlap at all with others. These non-overlap cases are nevertheless broadly consistent with known culture histories. For example, the absence of overlap for AC may reflect its documented split from #2 in 1975.

While most farmhouse cultures that are still in use today are not cryopreserved, a few cultures have been sampled and frozen over the past years. Whereas these frozen stocks are not used to start new fermentations, they do offer a unique opportunity to investigate the recent evolution of the cultures, revealing that at least some farmhouse cultures are still evolving rapidly. For example, cultures AA and AB originate from the same farmhouse brewer (Stavset) but were sampled at different times. AA was isolated and cryopreserved in the 1960s, while AB is the brewer’s current active culture. Although the isolates are genetically related and show strain overlap, only two of the strains are identical between the two populations (Figure S2C), highlighting substantial strain turnover in farmhouse yeasts over decades.

For more information on the origin of farmhouse yeast cultures and historical links between brewers that help explain some of the connections between the cultures, we refer to Garshol et al.27

Geography and admixture shape the phylogeny and population structure of farmhouse yeast

To investigate genetic relationships within and between cultures, we performed whole-genome sequencing on 37 representative isolates from 22 farmhouse yeast cultures of diverse geographical origins (Figures 1B and 2A). After de novo assembly and annotation, a coalescent species tree was built from codon alignments of 1,390 single-copy nuclear genes shared by all isolates and the outgroups (Figures 3A and S3). We also included 7 previously sequenced Western Norway kveik strains19 and 159 industrial strains from various niches (beer, wine, bioethanol, sake, spirits, and wild isolates).7 These industrial strains cluster into five clades: “Beer1,” “Beer2,” “Wine,” “Asian” (mainly from sake and bioethanol), and “Mixed” (mainly beer and bread yeast). While previous studies established that farmhouse yeasts form a genetic cluster distinct from known industrial strains,19,20 the large number of isolates sequenced in this study reveals a more nuanced structure within this group (Figure 3A). Multiple genetically distinct subclusters start to emerge, often reflecting geographical origins. For example, while the NW and SW Norway (kveik) strains form a separate clade, their internal structure includes subclusters containing strains from both regions, in line with the genetic fingerprinting data (Figure 2B). Strains from Central-East (CE) Norway, as well as the Baltic strains, form separate lineages. Particularly intriguing is the placement of two isolates, 39R20 from Russia and 16R23 from Lithuania, within the broader Asian cluster. These strains group closely with, yet remain distinct from, Asian sake and bioethanol strains, suggesting possible historical connections or gene flow events between Eurasian fermentation traditions. Moreover, these are not rare variants within these cultures, as fingerprinting data show that those isolates (or those with near-identical patterns) in both cases comprise >95% of the population. Lastly, in line with results from the PCR-based fingerprinting, some isolates are very closely related to industrial beer (e.g., 7R25 and 38R16), bread (e.g., 40R1 and 40R2), and wine (e.g., 40R14) yeast. As previously defined, we classify these strains as allochthonous yeasts.

To evaluate whether the observed clade structure was influenced by strain sampling, we constructed an expanded phylogeny including 35 previously published farmhouse ale strains from Preiss et al.20 (Figure S4). The inclusion of these strains did not alter the overall topology or our main conclusions, as all additional isolates clustered within clades identified in the primary analysis. It did, however, provide a higher resolution of Baltic farmhouse yeast diversity, which now appears to split into multiple clades. Strains from our dataset grouping in the Asian clade were not detected in the Preiss et al. collection. Notably, the expanded analysis revealed additional allochthonous strains in #39 and #42, where our initial sequencing detected only original farmhouse strains, highlighting the importance of sequencing multiple isolates per culture to capture the full population diversity.

Next, we analyzed nucleotide diversity (π) and population structure in farmhouse yeasts using 162,800 polymorphic sites. Principal-component analysis (PCA) on SNP data confirmed farmhouse yeasts as a distinct cluster from other niches (Figure 3C) and showed geographic differentiation within farmhouse yeasts (Figure 3D). The farmhouse yeast population shows a higher π than the species-wise median (6.64E-3 vs 3.6E-3),28 and all farmhouse subpopulations, except SW Norway, showed higher π (CE Norway = 3.99E-3, SW Norway = 1.06E-3, NW Norway = 4.12E-3, Lithuania = 4.85E-3, Latvia = 5.03E-3, Russia = 4.72E-3) than other industrial clades (Beer1 Britain = 3.13E-3, Beer1 US = 2.31E-3, Beer1 Belgium/Germany = 3.12E-3, Mixed = 4.35E-3, Wine = 1.59E-3, Beer 2 = 2.95E-3, Asia = 2.39E-3)7 (Figure 3B). This indicates that each of these regions harbors a larger genetic diversity than the ensemble of the common industrial strains.

Population structure at optimal K (K = 15 full dataset, Figure 3E; K = 5 farmhouse only, Figure 3F) corresponds with the major phylogenetic lineages. Within farmhouse yeasts, distinct subclusters corresponded to (1) Baltic states and Russia, (2) CE Norway, (3) SW Norway, (4) NW Norway, and (5) allochthonous yeasts. However, geography does not fully explain structure, with 41R15 and Hornindal1 (originating from NW Norway) showing SW ancestry, while 8R19 and Hornindal2 show admixed ancestry from NW and SW Norway. Several SW Norway strains (2R23, 14R6, 14R30, 19R28, and 44R32) showed gradients toward Baltic and CE Norway populations. Also, the profile of strains originating from the same culture sometimes differed: in #41 and Hornindal (NW Norway), Hornindal1 and 41R15 were pure SW Norway; 41R10 was pure NW Norway; and Hornindal2 was mixed NW, SW, and Baltic. In #44 (SW Norway), 44R7 was pure SW Norway, and 44R32 was mixed NW, SW, Baltic, and CE Norway.

Signatures of domestication and hybridization in farmhouse yeast

In order to further investigate the domestication trajectory of farmhouse yeasts, we examined genetic signatures often associated with industrial beer yeast domestication.

In contrast to wild yeasts but similar to most industrial beer yeasts, farmhouse yeasts are tetraploid (Table S3).7,29 Patterns of aneuploidies and large structural variations were usually restricted to subpopulations, with some exceptions (Figure S5). For example, a chromosome IX duplication was found in 15 strains, including 6/10 SW Norway and 4/14 NW Norway strains. Partial or full duplications of chromosomes I, III, and VI appeared in 3/8 Baltic strains. Such poly- and aneuploidies are typically associated with domesticated yeast lineages.4,30,31 Furthermore, farmhouse yeasts show a remarkable level of heterozygosity, with an average of 71,812 heterozygous sites per strain. Strong heterozygosity is consistent across farmhouse subclusters, except for allochthonous yeasts, and is strongest in Baltic strains. Next, by exploring the unmapped contigs of farmhouse yeast genomes, we discovered a ∼15 kb HGT event comprising seven genes from Zygosaccharomyces parabailii in 5/8 Baltic strains (Figure 4C). This HGT event appears unique to Baltic farmhouse yeasts and intriguingly overlaps with an event reported in other, distantly related S. cerevisiae strains32 (Table S4). Interestingly, synteny is conserved among the farmhouse yeast genomes but differs from the organization in the putative donor Z. parabailii genome (Figure 4C). While HGT is a common mechanism for DNA transfer in bacteria, its occurrence is less frequently reported in eukaryotic microorganisms.33,34,35 Previous studies have described the role of HGT in yeast domestication, most notably in wine yeast lineages, where transferred genes enhanced peptide36 and fructose37 metabolism. However, the seven horizontally transferred genes found in the Baltic strains do not appear to have similar functions. Several of the transferred genes are linked to cellular processes relevant in the farmhouse brewing process, including tolerance toward various stressors (SKN738 and SET539) and amino acid biosynthesis (BAT140); however, further investigation is required to determine the potential selective advantage that these genes might confer.

Loss of phenolic off-flavor (POF) production is a hallmark of beer yeast domestication and is caused by inactivation or deletion of PAD1 and/or FDC1, encoding phenylacrylic and ferulic acid decarboxylases, which convert ferulic acid into 4-vinyl guaiacol, a spicy clove-like aroma compound.41,42,43 In industrial ale7 and lager9 yeasts, mutations or structural variations in these genes eliminate POF. Strikingly, 95% of NW and SW Norway (“kveik”) strains are POF-, in line with previous observations19 (Figure 4B). Moreover, even genetically unrelated farmhouse strains clustering with Asian yeasts share mutations (FDC1w168fs and PAD1W102∗ in 16R23, FDC1K78∗ in 39R20) with the other farmhouse yeasts, suggesting parallel evolution or, more likely, admixture. Intriguingly, CE Norway strains show subpopulations with different POF phenotypes; 3/6 have intact PAD1 and FDC1 and are POF⁺, while 2/6 closely related strains carry FDC1 nonsense mutations (FDC1w168fs and FDC1Q154∗) and are consequently POF- (Figure 4B). The other strain contains both a functional and a non-functional copy of both genes and therefore remains POF+. This mutational pattern might indicate involvement of hybridization (followed by loss-of-heterozygosity) within this lineage.

Many of the observations above (population structure, ploidy, heterozygosity, HGT, and POF mutational patterns) point toward the importance of sexual reproduction in farmhouse yeast evolution. In line with these observations, most farmhouse isolates show a functional sexual cycle (see Figure S6A), a trait that largely eroded in the main domesticated beer lineage due to genome decay.7 This is somewhat surprising given that farmhouse strains are typically tetraploid, a state often assumed to complicate canonical meiosis and the production of viable segregants (Table S3). To determine whether polyploid farmhouse strains can nevertheless complete meiosis and yield viable progeny, we sporulated three polyploid (two tetraploid and one triploid) farmhouse strains (7R7 (4n; NW Norway), 28R8 (4n; CE Norway), and 42R20 (3n; Latvia)) and performed tetrad dissections. While 42R20 did not yield viable spores, segregants of the other two strains were generally diploid and displayed either a single (matA or matα) or double (matA/α) mating type, showing the mating capability of these strains (Figure S6A). Importantly, evidence for sporulation is not limited to laboratory induction: microscopic investigation of the initial farmhouse cultures indeed indicates the occasional presence of spores (in #39, Figure S6B). While laboratory conditions that promote sporulation are well established,44 this represents (to our knowledge) the first evidence of sporulation of industrial yeasts in their habitat.

Phenotypic robustness and fermentation performance of farmhouse yeast

Traditional beer production involving backslopping provides an ideal environment for yeast domestication.45 Fermentation and storage practices in farmhouse brewing differ markedly from industrial processes,3,17,46 and these differences may have driven distinct evolutionary trajectories. To investigate how such conditions have shaped farmhouse yeast physiology, we assessed the growth of all identified farmhouse strains and 159 industrial strains under various stress conditions, focusing on phenotypes potentially relevant in farmhouse yeast domestication (for example, tolerance toward ethanol and high/low temperatures and growth in low nitrogen conditions) (Table S5). In addition, we evaluated fermentation efficiency and metabolite production of a subset of 168 farmhouse strains in two distinct beer fermentation settings. These conditions were chosen to represent environments typically encountered in farmhouse brewing (high temperatures and high final ethanol titers) and current industrial beer production (lower temperatures and ethanol titers).

First, supervised PCA was performed to visualize phenotypic diversity under stress conditions. When the set of industrial strains was included and “genetic clade” was used as the supervising variable, this approach effectively separated farmhouse strains from the various industrial clades (Figure 5A). Differences between farmhouse subgroups were also observed, with SE Norway strains being the least and Latvian strains the most temperature tolerant.

Next, we compared the beer fermentation performance of industrial and farmhouse yeasts using a subset of 168 randomly selected strains, including all sequenced strains. Fermentations were conducted under conditions resembling industrial ale production (“industrial conditions”: 16°P wort, 16°C) and traditional farmhouse brewing (“farmhouse conditions”: 22°P wort, 30°C) (Figure 6A). Chemical analysis of the resulting beers revealed substantial variability in flavor compound production across isolates. For instance, isoamyl acetate, the primary contributor to beer fruitiness, varied by over an order of magnitude in the farmhouse condition, reaching concentrations up to 9.54 ppm, approximately 15 times its reported flavor threshold in beer (0.6 ppm)47 (Table S6). Fermentation efficiency also differed markedly between conditions. Under industrial conditions, only 4.3% of isolates consumed more than 95% of fermentable sugars within 7 days, whereas this proportion increased to 26.5% under farmhouse conditions, despite the higher initial sugar content in this condition (Figure 6B), suggesting specific adaptation of the farmhouse yeasts to the fermentation conditions used in farmhouse brewing. Together, these results reveal that farmhouse yeast strains combine broad stress tolerance with high phenotypic and metabolic diversity in fermentations that likely correspond to the various selective pressures these yeasts experience during cycles of farmhouse brewing.

Discussion

Our findings highlight the distinct domestication trajectory of farmhouse yeasts and demonstrate that, unlike modern industrial beer production, traditional European farmhouse brewing practices maintain genetically diverse S. cerevisiae populations. This diversity is not only apparent across different farmhouses but also within cultures, where multiple lineages can coexist. Intraspecific diversity within communities is well documented for bacteria in natural environments (e.g., soil48,49,50), but remains largely unexplored in yeasts. Such mixed communities are particularly prevalent in fluctuating environments, where the diversity can help buffer the effects of environmental perturbations and enhance community resilience.51 Given that farmhouse brewing practices involve extremely variable conditions, with repeated cycles of rapid growth in a chemically complex substrate (wort) with widely varying temperatures and nutrient levels, combined with harsh storage conditions in between fermentation cycles where cells may experience starvation and desiccation, the persistence of strain-level diversity in these communities may be under selection. These heterogeneous populations thus offer a tractable natural system to further investigate subspecies diversity, population dynamics, and adaptive evolution in highly variable yet cyclic environments.

Despite non-sterile conditions, farmhouse cultures contained almost exclusively S. cerevisiae yeast (> 98%), highlighting the strong selective conditions of the brewing environment. However, the presence of horizontally transferred genes in Baltic strains from four cultures (#16, #42, #45, and #46) reflects occasional interaction with wild yeast species. Previous studies have highlighted the importance of interspecific exchange of DNA through hybridization in the origin and evolution of beer yeast. For example, interspecific hybridization has led to the emergence of traditional lager beer yeasts (S. cerevisiae × S. eubayanus),52,53 while S. cerevisiae × S. kudriavzevii and S. eubayanus × S. uvarum hybrids were prevalent in Belgian gueuze beers.9 Interestingly, apart from the allochthonous strains 7R25 and Muri, which are S. cerevisiae × S. uvarum × S. eubayanus triple hybrids likely originating from a commercial yeast source, our results indicate that interspecific hybrids are perhaps not as prevalent in European farmhouse yeast cultures. Still, several observations point toward (occasional) intraspecific outcrossing events. The absence of extensive homozygosity (Figure 4A) indicates that selfing is infrequent, and maintenance of heterozygosity is likely driven by occasional hybridization between divergent strains, followed by asexual propagation within farmhouse brewing systems.54 In line with this observation, population structure analyses reveal signs of admixture in several isolates, reflecting contributions from divergent populations (e.g., Asian and farmhouse, Figure 3E), which suggests that intraspecific hybridization, rather than shared ancestry alone, explains these patterns. These observations highlight that admixture is a recurrent feature of brewing-associated yeasts, as similarly also reported for domesticated industrial beer strains in population-genomic studies.55,56 However, in farmhouse cultures it could reflect ongoing strain exchange and occasional outcrossing from backslopping, rather than a few historical admixture events followed by clonal propagation.

Supporting these indications of outcrossing events, many of the farmhouse yeast strains evaluated (72%) retained a functional sexual cycle. Interestingly, this trait is largely lost in some domesticated lineages; strains from French dairy, African beer, and sake clades sporulate very poorly,15 while sporulation is even fully absent in other clades, such as the Beer1-Belgium/Germany ale yeast.7 This is likely a result of a relaxed selective pressure on the mating process, as modern brewing practices do not give rise to conditions that would stimulate sporulation of the yeasts. A functional sexual cycle is retained, however, in other lineages, such as many non-domesticated (wild) and wine yeasts. Like wine strains, farmhouse yeasts undergo prolonged periods of nutrient scarcity (in vineyards between harvests for wine yeast and during yeast storage in farmhouse strains), which might trigger sporulation,44 while bursts of nutrient availability during fermentation may stimulate spore germination and mating.

Large-scale growth and fermentation assays revealed substantial phenotypic diversity among farmhouse yeasts. In contrast to most industrial ale yeasts, farmhouse strains exhibit broad thermotolerance, demonstrating robust growth across both low and high temperatures. This phenotype likely reflects their unique life history. Farmhouse fermentations are typically initiated at elevated wort temperatures (30°C–37°C, occasionally even up to 42°C17), followed by prolonged storage at ambient temperatures, which can approach freezing in winter. It has been hypothesized, however, that this thermotolerance in farmhouse strains comes at a metabolic cost, particularly a reduced ability to efficiently ferment all wort sugars. Specifically, deleterious mutations in the bidirectional sugar transporter gene AGT1 have been identified previously in kveik strains.57 Disruption of this transporter may lead to reduced trehalose export, resulting in increased intracellular concentrations, which provides stress resistance. However, the AGT1 mutations also result in reduced uptake of the wort sugar maltotriose, leading to incomplete fermentations.58 Consistent with this trade-off, we observe a modest yet significant negative correlation between total ethanol production (a direct proxy for sugar utilization) under farmhouse conditions and tolerance to high temperature (41°C) (Pearson R = −0.22, p = 0.0079).

Together, our large-scale genetic and phenotypic characterization of traditional European farmhouse yeast cultures shows how these yeasts have followed a different domestication trajectory from the other yeasts in human-driven food fermentations. Specifically, farmhouse beer yeasts likely represent a direct continuation of the domestication process that was started with the first beer-like fermentations some 10,000 years ago, when beer brewing was performed at smaller scales, in less controlled conditions, without the use of purified cultures, and with longer periods in between successive fermentations during which the yeasts were conserved in semi-starved and/or dried form. Our findings not only provide valuable insights into non-industrialized beer yeast communities and evolution but also provide new resources to diversify industrial brewing with unique strains rooted in centuries-old traditions.

Experimental model and study participant details

Experiments were conducted using S. cerevisiae. All the farmhouse yeast cultures used in this study are listed in Table S1 and all isolated strains used in the phenotyping experiments are listed in Table S5.

Farmhouse cultures and yeast isolation

Samples from 44 farmhouse ale cultures were analyzed in this study (Table S1). Samples were obtained from the farmhouses in either dried or liquid form. Prior to yeast isolation, dried samples were rehydrated in sterile water, liquid samples were used as such. Serial dilutions of each culture was plated onto YPD2% agar (1% w/v yeast extract, 2% w/v peptone, 2% glucose w/v and 2% agar w/v) supplemented with chloramphenicol (0.01%w/v) to inhibit bacterial growth and grown at 30°C for 72 hours. 40 random colonies were picked for each sample. Isolates from these samples are named according to the scheme ‘NNRXX’ where ‘NN’ is the number of the culture in the farmhouse ale registry (Table S1, https://www.garshol.priv.no/download/farmhouse/kveik.html), ‘R’ stands for ‘random isolate’, and ‘XX’ is a sequential number (1–40).

Method details

Interdelta DNA fingerprinting

Genomic DNA extraction of the isolates for PCR-based fingerprinting was carried out in a 96-well format using a TissueLyzer II (Qiagen, Venlo, The Netherlands) according to the manufacturers protocol (ether extraction). Interdelta bands were amplified using primers δ12 (5′-TCAACAATGGAATCCCAAC-3′) and δ21 (5′-CATCTTAACACCGTATATGA-3′).23 PCR amplification was carried out using a Mastercycler Nexus (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) as follows: 3 min at 95°C, 9 cycles of 25 s 94°C, 30 s 45°C, 90 s 72°C, 21 cycles of 25 s 94°C, 30 s 50°C, 90 s 72°C, and a final step of 10 m at 72°C. PCR products were analyzed by capillary electrophoresis (QIAxcel Advanced System, Qiagen, Venlo, The Netherlands). Banding patterns were analyzed using BioNumerics 7.1 (Applied Maths, Sint-Martens-Latem, Belgium). Clustering of Interdelta patterns was performed using curve based Pearson’s correlations with 4% optimization and 1% curve smoothing to generate a similarity matrix (% identity between strains).

Species identification by ITS sequencing

Strains showing an Interdelta Profile were subsequently subjected to a species PCR, to be able to discriminate between different Saccharomcyes species (and their hybrids). Species PCRs were performed as outlined in.59

Strains not showing an Interdelta Profile (indicative of non-Saccharomyces yeast species) were further characterized by PCR amplification of the fungal ITS2 region with the primers ITS4 (5′-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3′) and ITS1f (5′-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAACTAA-3′) and subsequent sanger sequencing as previously described.60

Analysis of culture overlap

To investigate to what extent the same strains could be found in different cultures, all isolates were genetically fingerprinted and scored for similarity (see Interdelta DNA Fingerprinting). A custom-made Python script analyzed the output, grouping together all strains found by traversing up the tree from a strain node to the last internal node before a set cutoff distance was reached, then including all strains reachable below that internal node at a distance less than the cutoff. This way, strains that are the same (or very similar) across different cultures can be identified. The resulting overlap structure was visualized using GraphViz 11.0.0 (20240428.1522).

Genome Sequencing and Analysis, Phylogenetic and Population Structure Analysis

DNA isolation

Single clones were isolated after streaking of YPD2% agar plate, and single colonies were picked up. After 24 hours growth in liquid YPD2% media with chloramphenicol, cells were spun down, washed in TE buffer and resuspended in Y1 buffer (Qiagen, Venlo, The Netherlands). Spheroplasts were obtained by treating the resuspended cell with Zymolase (Seikagaku, Tokyo, Japan) in TRIS acetate - EDTA (TE) buffer solution + 5% (w/v) glucose. Genomic DNA for Illumina short reads sequencing was isolated with 20-G genomic tip (Qiagen, Venlo, The Netherlands), following the manufacturer protocol.

Genomes de novo assembly and annotation and ploidy estimation

For each sequenced DNA library, quality of the reads was assessed with FastQC 0.11.8 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/, last accessed on 09/02/2025). Clean reads were obtained by trimming low quality reads (Phred score< 20) with Trimmomatic v.0.33.61 Reads shorter than 50 bp were discarded. The presence of eventual interspecific hybrids was evaluated by aligning the trimmed reads to a custom set of reference genomes, composed by Saccharomyces cerevisiae S228C vR64.2.1,62 S. eubayanus FM1318 v1.0,53 S. kudriavzevii FM1066 v1.0,63 S. mikatae IFO 1815 v1.0,64 S. paradoxus UFRJ50816 v1.065 and S. uvarum U3s1 v1.0.66 Reads were aligned with Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA mem, v0.7.12-r103967), mapping files were handled with samtools v1.9 (11) and the mean coverage was calculated along a 10kb non-overlapping sliding window with bedtools v.2.25.68

For each sequenced strain, de novo genome assembly was performed with Megahit v1.1.4 with standard parameters.69 A summary of the assembly metrics is reported in Table S2.

Makerpipeline v2.31.10 was used to annotate genomes.70 To increase the quality of genome annotation, protein sequences from additional 19 non-S. cerevisiae yeasts were integrated in the gene prediction step (Table S2). Moreover, coding sequences from S. cerevisiae reference genome v.R64.2.1 and from the 1,011 pangenome sequencing project29 were supplied as ESTs.

Ploidy estimation of the sequenced farmhouse yeasts was performed based on k-mer frequency method.71 For each of the sequencing libraries we counted the 41 nt k-mers and estimated their frequency with ntCard v.1.2.2.72 The k-mers frequencies distributions were trimmed for frequencies below 10 and plotted with (R version 4.3.2, http://www.r-project.org), and the number of peaks in the distributions, corresponding to an approximated estimate of the genomic ploidy, was identified manually.

We further assessed the ploidy levels of the newly sequenced individuals using three complementary computational approaches: (1) nQuire with ‘create’, ‘denoise’, and ‘lrdmodel’ steps,73 which statistically evaluates allele frequency distributions, (2) PloidyFrost,74 which infers ploidy based on genome-wide topological features derived from De Bruijn graphs, and (3) Smudgeplot,75 which evaluates k-mer co-occurrence and multiplicity patterns to identify the most likely ploidy state and heterozygosity structure of the genome.

Horizontal Gene Transfer detection

Presence of Horizontal Gene Transfer (HGT) in the sequenced isolates was detected with a custom pipeline. Briefly, for each isolate, the predicted proteins were aligned to the NCBI non-redundant protein database with DIAMOND v0.9.22.123,76 preserving taxonomic information for each hit sequence with parameters --sensitive --max-target-seqs 5—taxonmap prot.accession2taxid.gz --taxonnodesnodes.dmp (prot.accession2taxid.gz and nodes.dmpavialble at https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pub/taxonomy/, last accessed on 09/02/2025). For each hit protein, the full taxonomic annotation was recovered from the corresponding taxid using the ETE3 package for Python.77 Query proteins with at least one hit to a Bacterial protein were retained as potential HGT candidates for the downstream analysis.

A more refined sequence similarity search was performed for the potential HGT candidates with blast+ v2.2.31 against the NCBI non-redundant protein database in order to exclude eukaryotic proteins wrongly assigned to Bacteria. In addition to that, taxonomic assignment of the flanking genes for each potential HGT candidate was assessed with a custom Python script.

Reference-based alignment and Variant Calling

Reference-based alignment to S. cerevisiae reference genome S288C was used to identify Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) and short insertions and deletions (InDels), Copy number Variants and infer population structure. Clean reads were mapped to the S. cerevisiae reference genome S288C with the BWA mem, using the default parameters except for -K 100000000. Mapped reads were sorted with samtools, and duplicated reads, non-primary alignments and non-properly paired reads were flagged with MarkDuplicates tool from the Broad Institute Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK v4.0.11.078). GATK HaplotypeCaller was used to identify SNPs and InDels in gVCF mode with --base-quality-score-threshold 30 and --min-base-quality-score 30, and the resulting gvcf files were merged with the gvcf files obtained from the 156 sequenced strains of,7 using the GATK GatherVcfs tool. The genotype was called on all 44 + 156 strains simultaneously with GATK GenotypeGVCFs strains to minimize false positive calls. Low quality variants were filtered with GATK VariantFiltration tool on the resulting merged vcf file applying the following filters: minimum base quality score of 20, a standard minimum confidence threshold for calling of 50 and a standard emitted confidence of 20. Sites with total quality by depth < 2.00 and mapping quality < 40, genotype quality < 30 and genotype depth < 5. Bcftools v1.979 was used to handle the large merged vcf file, and SnpEff (v3.3)80 was used to annotate and predict the effect of the variants. The final set of SNPs included a total of 162,800 polymorphic sites accounting for a total of 12,086,970 SNPs across all strains.

Copy Number Variants detection

Copy-number variations (CNVs) were identified on the reference-based alignments using CNVnator v0.4.1.81 For each sequenced strain, CNVnator was run in windows of 1000bp and 500bp, and to improve the robustness of the call, only CNV regions that were called with both the 1000bp and the 500bp window were retained for downstream analyses. Overrepresented and Enriched biological processes, molecular functions and pathways were inferred on the genes interested by CNV using the clusterProfiler package v4.17.0 in R.82

Phylogenetic inference

Nucleotidic sequences of the predicted farmhouse yeast genes were combined with the genes of the 156 yeast strains present in7 and clustered together using CD-HIT v.4.8.183 using a similarity threshold of 98% and minimum alignment coverage of 95%. From there, gene clusters corresponding to 1,347 single copy genes shared between all yeast strains from7 were selected. Then, the corresponding nucleotidic sequence of the S. kudriavzevi orthologs was added to each of the 1,347 gene clusters, working as an outgroup to root the phylogenetic tree. The nucleotidic sequences of each of the 1,347 gene clusters were translated to the corresponding amino acid sequences, and then aligned using MAFFT v.7.27184 with flags --localpair --maxiterate 1000 --ep 0.123. Nucleotidic sequences were aligned according to the corresponding amino acid alignments using PAL2NAL.85

For each of the 1,347 gene clusters, a phylogenetic single gene tree was obtained by inferring a ML tree with IQ-tree v.1.6.8,86 using the MG+F1X4 codon model and performing 1,000 ultrafast bootstraps and 1,000 SH-like approximate likelihood ratio test.87 In order to handle incomplete-lineage sorting and prevent some common ML artefacts based on analyses of concatenated gene alignments, the coalescent-based algorithm ASTRAL v.5.7.888 was used to reconstruct the final phylogenetic tree. For each of the 1,347 genes, the 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap trees were used in addition to the final single gene ML tree reconstructed, for a comprehensive phylogenetic space of almost 1.5 million single gene trees. The coalescent-based analysis was performed on these single gene trees allowing gene resampling across 100 multi-locus bootstrapping replicates.

Population structure

Population structure was inferred from the final set of SNPs using only biallelic variants. Biallelic variants were identified with plink v1.989 with the following parameters --indep-pairwise 50 5 0.5 --maf 0.05 --biallelic-only --geno 0.25. The model-based Bayesian algorithm fastSTRUCTURE v1.090 was used to infer the population structure of all 44 + 156 yeasts strains, varying the number of ancestral populations (K) between 1 and 20 using the simple prior implemented. K = 15 was found to be the optimal K. For inferring the population structure of the 44 farmhouse yeasts, an independent run was performed using a varying number of ancestral populations from 1 to 10. For farmhouse yeasts, K = 5 was found to be optimal. The corresponding district plots derived from the Q estimates were generated with the pophelper v2.3.1 R package.91 Principal Component Analysis of the population structure was performed with plink and the components were visualized with PCAtools package for R.

Investigation of the Yeast’s Sexual Life Cycle

Sporulation was induced on minimal sporulation medium [1% (w/v) KAc, 0.05% (w/v) amino acids, 2% (w/v) agar] after pre-growth in YPD2%. Plates were incubated at 23°C for 10 days.

Propidium iodide staining for ploidy determination

DNA content of the parent strains and segregants was determined by staining cells with propidium iodide, as described previously92

High-throughput phenotypic screening

All strains (i.e. all unique isolates of each culture, totalling 660 strains) were tested in high-throughput spotting assays in several conditions. These conditions were selected to investigate potential domestication traits in farmhouse yeasts. All isolates were evaluated on YPD2% agar for (i) temperature tolerance (8°C - 12°C - 37°C – 39°C - 41°C; as farmhouse brewing often involves high-temperature fermentation processes and low-temperature storage conditions), (ii) ethanol tolerance using increasing concentrations of ethanol (8 - 10 - 12% v/v; as farmhouse brewing often yields higher ethanol titers compared to regular ale or lager brewing), (iii) copper tolerance using 0.1mM copper (as farmhouse ales are sometimes made using copper brewing equipment). Stressor concentrations were selected based on previous pilot experiments with a broader concentration range (data not shown). Additionally, nitrogen dependency was assessed, since it has been hypothesized previously that farmhouse yeasts have a higher nitrogen demand compared to industrial beer strains. This was assessed on medium containing 0.19%w/v yeast nitrogen base without amino acids and ammonium sulphate, supplemented with varying concentrations of ammonium sulphate. H2S production was measured on Biggy agar medium, as described earlier.93 While this phenotype is likely not directly linked to yeast domestication, it supplements the data of the fermentation experiments, as it is considered an important off-flavour in beer. Growth on YPD2% agar on 20°C was used as a control condition.

Prior to the experiment, the 96-well microtiter plates containing the isolates (stored at −80°C in YPGly25% (1% w/v yeast extract, 2% w/v peptone, 25% glycerol w/v)) were thawed and spotted on YPD2% agar and incubated at 30°C for 48 hr. Then, isolates were arrayed into 1536 colony format on YPD2% agar plates using a ROTOR HDA robot (Singer Instruments, UK). Yeast arrays were grown at 20°C for 48h before being replica pinned onto the various environmental conditions. After replica pinning, all plates were sealed using plastic paraffin film and all plates (except for plates used in the thermotolerance assays) were incubated at 20°C.

Phenolic off-flavour (POF) production

Screening for 4-VG production was assessed by measuring 4-VG production of each strain in medium enriched in the 4-VG precursor, ferulic acid. Strains were pregrown on YPD2% agar, a single colony was picked and directly inoculated in a GC vial filled with 5mL of test medium (YPD2% supplemented with 100mg L-1 ferulic acid). Vials were capped (but not completely closed) and wrapped with plastic paraffin film and statically incubated at 30°C for 3 days. Next, 4-VG concentration was measured using GC-FID (see further). Strains were scored as 4-VG+ if the concentration produced was significantly higher compared to the non-inoculated fermentation medium.

Production of beer wort

A commercial Belgian pilsner malt was used to produce wort in the 5hl pilot brewery of KU Leuven. Fifty kilograms of coarse milled pilsner malt was mixed with 0.9hl reversed osmosis brewing water containing 80 mg/l CaCl2. Mashing occurred at 50°C and pH 5.7. The following brewing scheme was used: 52°C for 15 min, 62°C for 55 min, 72°C for 30 min, 78°C for 1 min (rise in temperature at 2°C/min). After this, the wort was filtered in a lauter tun filter and sparged with 2hl sparging water (extract of last running’s 2°P). The extract of the combined sweet wort was 17.3°P at the beginning of boiling. Wort boiling was conducted for 72 min at atmospheric boiling in a boiling kettle with internal boiler. At the end of boiling, 0.2mg/l Zn2+ ions were added. The wort was clarified in a whirlpool as follows: filling for 6 min, rest for 20 min, emptying in 20 min at 95°C. Iso-α-acids (25mg/L) extract was added to the wort. The wort was then diluted to two gravities to perform the fermentation experiments: 22°P and 16°P. 35ppm of isohumulone was added to obtain 25 bitterness units (BU), and 50mg/L chloramphenicol was added to avoid bacterial contamination.

Lab-scale beer fermentations

Yeast strains were inoculated into 5ml liquid YPD2% (1% w/v yeast extract, 2% w/v peptone and 2% w/v glucose) and grown at 30°C, with shaking in a roller drum for 24 hours. 500μl from the 5ml test tubes were pitched in Erlenmeyer flasks with 50ml of YPD6% (1% w/v yeast extract, 2% w/v peptone and 6% w/v glucose) and incubated at 30°C, 200rpm for 72 hours. Cell density of each Erlenmeyer flask was counted with an automated cell counter (Bio-Rad, USA). 150ml of wort (wort gravity based on the required condition, either 16°P or 22°P) is inoculated at a rate of 107 cells/ml into a 250ml bottle fitted with a water lock. Fermentation conditions were selected to represent farmhouse brewing conditions (high temperature, high final ethanol titers) and processes linked to current industrial beer production (lower temperatures and lower final ethanol titers). The 16°P bottles were incubated at 16°C (magnetically stirred, 150rpm) and the 22°P bottles were incubated at 30°C (magnetically stirred, 150rpm). After fermentation the samples were centrifuged at 3000rpm for 3 min and the supernatant was used for metabolite analysis.

Metabolite analysis

Samples for ethanol analysis were analyzed by Alcolyzer beer ME (Anton Paar GmbH), following manufacturer recommendations. SO2, glycerol, acetic acid and pH were analyzed by Gallery Plus Beermaster (Thermo Scientific), following manufacturer recommendations. The samples were prepared for measuring these compounds by filtering 0.5ml using a 0.2μm syringe filter and a 1ml syringe. Headspace gas chromatography coupled with flame ionization detection (HS-GC-FID) (Agilent Technologies, USA) was used for the quantification of yeast aroma production. The GC was calibrated for 16 important aroma compounds, including esters (ethyl acetate, isobutyl acetate, propyl acetate, isoamyl acetate, phenyl ethyl acetate, ethyl propionate, ethyl butyrate, ethyl hexanoate, ethyl octanoate, ethyl decanoate), higher alcohols (isoamyl alcohol, isobutanol, butanol, phenyl ethanol), acetaldehyde and 4-VG. The GC was equipped with a headspace autosampler (PAL system, CTC analytics, Switzerland) and contained a DB-WAXETER column (length, 30 m; internal diameter, 0.25 mm; layer thickness, 0.5 μm, Agilent Technologies, USA) and N2 was used as the carrier gas. Samples were heated for 25 min at 70°C in the autosampler. The injector block and FID temperatures were both kept constant at 250°C. Samples of 5mL filtered fermentation medium were collected in 15 ml glass tubes containing 1.75 g of sodium chloride each. These tubes were immediately closed and cooled to minimize evaporation of volatile compounds. The oven temperature was held at 50°C for 5 min, after which it increased to 80°C at 4°C min-1. Next, it increased to 200°C at 5°C min-1 and was held at 200°C for 3 min.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The exact number of strains and/or biological replicates for each strain for all experiments are included within their respective figure legends. When statistical analyses were performed, the tests used are described in the main text and/or figure legends.

In the high throughput phenotyping experiments, colony sizes were measured after 4 and 7 days of growth (depending on the condition). Plates were imaged using a Sony ILCE-6400 camera with a 50 mm lens and backlit with a light panel, based on which colony sizes were determined. Batch and positional effects were corrected for in R loess regression.

Results of GC measurements were analyzed with the Agilent Chemstation software (Agilent Technologies, USA). Each sample contained 100μl of 2-heptanol (final concentration of 1ppb PrSAc and 2.44ppm 2-heptanol) as internal standard.

Sporulation efficiency (%) was quantified by visually inspecting 100 random cells of the population, and determining whether they formed tetrads.

The ploidy of segregants was determined using propidium iodide staining, and were analyzed using Attune NxT Flow Cytometer (Thermo Scientific) software, as described previously.92
