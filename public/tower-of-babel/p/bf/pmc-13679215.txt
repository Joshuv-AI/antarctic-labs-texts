Cystic pulmonary amyloidosis with lymphoid interstitial pneumonia in Sjögren’s disease: a case report
Authors: Jinzhou Chen, Songtao Ren, Jing Huang, Ting Yang, Guangbing Lu, Hua Fang, Xiaojing Deng, Bo Zhang, Sixian Liu, Liangdong Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInterstitial lung diseases are commonly seen in association with Sjögren’s disease, especially lymphoproliferative disorders, such as lymphoid interstitial pneumonia. In occasional and rare cases, pulmonary amyloidosis with cysts, nodules, and/or ground-glass opacites as radiological features, can also become a complication of Sjögren’s disease. On the other hand, diffuse cystic lung disease, without nodules, concomitant with chylous fluid accumulation in female leads to initial suspicion of lymphangioleiomyomatosis. Interestingly, it’s challenging to differentiate if diffuse cystic lung lesion absent of nodules, chylous fluid accumulation and Sjögren’s disease happen simultaneously.Case presentationA 34-year-old female, with diffuse cystic lung disease, chylous reflux lymphedema and Sjögren’s disease, was eventually diagnosed with cystic pulmonary amyloidosis with lymphoid interstitial pneumonia, secondary to Sjögren’s disease, and chylous reflux lymphedema as comorbidity. The case presented with atypical pulmonary amyloidosis radiological features of lung lesion, where diffuse cysts existed without nodules or ground-glass opacites, apart from the scarcely seen size of the biggest cyst. The patient was diagnosed with CPA with LIP, secondary to SjD. Clinical symptoms were relieved after thoracoscopic lobectomy of the right lower lobe, where located the biggest cyst. The immunosuppressants that she had been taking were recommended again for disease stabilization.ConclusionCystic pulmonary amyloidosis is a rare complication of Sjögren’s disease, while lymphoid interstitial pneumonia is comparatively well-known. There can be cystic pulmonary amyloidosis cases where the lung lesion only consists of diffuse, thin-walled, and variably-sized cysts, with the absence of nodules or ground-glass opacities, which warrants a lung biopsy. Clinicians should be alerted to the cyst-only radiological features of the cystic pulmonary amyloidosis with Sjögren’s disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12890-026-04528-0.

Introduction

The prevalence of interstitial lung disease in Sjögren’s disease (SjD) is variably reported from 9 to 90% [1–3], which is greatly influenced by the means of ascertainment and detection. In terms of diffuse cystic lung disease (DCLD), lymphoid interstitial pneumonia (LIP) is the mostly commonly seen lung lesion secondary to Sjögren’s disease [4]. Pulmonary amyloidosis has been reported in rare cases of Sjögren’s disease, with diffuse cysts, nodules and ground-glass opacities as radiological features [5].

In this report, we present a case of a SjD patient, with cystic pulmonary amyloidosis (CPA) and LIP, which only have diffused, different-sized, and thin-walled cysts, with the absence of typical amyloidosis nodules or ground-glass opacities. The atypical radiographic features of CPA, with LIP, secondary to SjD, in the case increased the difficulty of early diagnosis and led to an initial misdiagnosis as lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM). The atypical radiological features and scarcely seen cyst size in the case make it worth reporting.

Case report

A 34-year-old female was referred to our hospital for a 5-month progression of dyspnea and cough, with a chronic dyspnea course of 9 years. She had chylous reflux lymphedema on her left leg since the age of 9, and had been having it until two surgical procedures at 17. It got worse again when she was 19 during her first pregnancy, and afterwards, she hadn’t received any further relevant treatment ever since. At the age of 23, she had her second child. At the age of 25, she had her first dyspnea and cough, and was found with diffuse, thin-walled, and different-sized lung cysts by high-resolution CT. Two years later, at the age of 27, she had hemoptysis for the first time, with prominent ground-glass opacity of low lung zone predominance along with the cysts. She recovered one week after with hemostasis treatment, and the ground-class opacity had mostly disappeared. At the age of 28, she was first diagnosed with SjD with the manifestation of xerostomia by labial gland biopsy, SPECT imaging of salivary gland, and seropositive anti-ANA (1:320), SS-A/Ro52 (+++), SS-A/Ro60 (+++), and SS-B (+). She was then diagnosed with DCLD with Sjögren’s disease and chylous reflux lymphedema, suspicious of LIP or LAM. No precise diagnosis was established due to the absence of a biopsy. Since then, she had been treated with mycophenolate mofetil, tacrolimus, total glucosides of paeony, methylprednisolone, and N-acetylcysteine. She had been symptomatically stable until she stopped taking the medicines by herself one month before her third pregnancy at the age of 32. She started to take them again 15 months after because of deterioration of cough and dyspnea. She was then referred to our hospital, another 5 months after, due to progressive dyspnea and cough. She had a history of exposure to porcelain and no history of smoking.

On admission, she was 156 cm in height and 45 kg in weight. Her peripheral oxygen saturation was 97% with nasal catheter oxygen inhalation of 2 L/min; respiratory rate was 21 breaths/min. She was with a normal temperature (36.8◦C) and tachycardia (89 beats/min) with a BP of 96 mmHg/60 mmHg. Examination of skin and joints identified no abnormal changes. Auscultation of the chest showed bilateral inspiratory hyperresonance throughout the lower lung fields, without Velcro crackles or moist rales. She had her left swollen leg in a stocking.

She had mild leukopenia (WBC counts, 3.40 × 109/L) and anemia (RBC counts, 3.44 × 1012/L, hemoglobin levels 99.00 g/L), with a normal level of blood platelets on admission. Arterial blood gas analysis showed a pH of 7.45, a PaCO2 of 37.9 mmHg, and a PaO2 of 76.8 mmHg on room air (not located in a high-altitude region). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was 26 mm/h. No abnormalities of liver or renal functions were found. C-reactive protein, blood coagulation function, and electrocardiogram (ECG) were normal. The peripheral serum kappa light chain M protein and lambda light chain M protein were normal 6 months before admission.

The HRCT of the lung from admission showed thin-walled, round, diffuse, lower lobe predominant cysts in different sizes, with no obvious lung nodules and ground-glass opacities. The largest cyst, which was 70.6 mm (Fig. 1), was found bigger than 16 months before admission, and her age of 25, when she was first found with diffuse cysts.

Pulmonary function tests after hospitalization revealed an FVC of 2.65 L (82.2% predicted), an FEV1 of 1.88 L (67.4% predicted), a FEV1/FVC% of 70.92%, a total lung capacity of 4.76 L (102% predicted), a ratio of residual volume to total lung capacity of 148%, and the diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide of 6.25 mmol/min/kPa (73.4% predicted).

Bronchoalveolar lavage was not conducted, as a thoracoscopic lung biopsy and thoracoscopic lobectomy of the right lower lobe were conducted instead, for diagnosis and improving pulmonary function. On histological examination, some areas showed deposition of red-stained material and small foci of calcification, surrounding which, fibrous tissue proliferation was observed, accompanied by lymphocytic cell aggregation. Immunohistochemistry showed Congo red staining positivity and apple-green birefringence in a polarized microscope, with kappa and lambda light chain deposition (Fig. 2). The patient was diagnosed with CPA with LIP, secondary to SjD, due to the positive Congo red stains and lymphocytic cell aggregation. The patient stated that the dyspnea was relieved after thoracoscopic lobectomy of the right lower lobe. The patient was suggested to take a PET/CT scan and bone marrow biopsy for lymphoma detection, and mass spectrometry-based proteomic analysis for amyloid subtyping. Lymphedema specialists were recommended as well for her chylous reflux lymphedema. She is now under out-patient follow-up and further examinations.

Discussion and conclusions

CPA has now been considered as one of the rare cystic lung diseases (RCLD), along with light chain deposition disease (LCDD), low-grade metastatic neoplasms and infections, apart from those relatively well-known cystic lung diseases, which are Langerhans cell histiocytosis (PLCH), Birt-Hogg-Dube syndrome (BHD), LIP and LAM [6].

Amyloidosis is known by misfolded proteins that deposit in the extracellular matrix as fibrils [7]. Typically, five kinds of amyloidosis are relevant in pulmonary amyloidosis, and they are systemic light chain (AL) amyloidosis, systemic autoimmune (AA) amyloidosis, systemic wild-type (senile) amyloidosis, hereditary transthyretin (TTR) amyloidosis, and localized AL amyloidosis, which are mostly seen in CPA [8]. Cysts in pulmonary amyloidosis are often associated with Sjögren’s disease, where concomitant nodular opacities are often found, with diffuse cysts in rare cases, which are often round, thin-walled, multiple, and in varying sizes, and which have an even distribution or a lower lobe predominance [8]. The presented case has a comparatively large cyst on the lower right lobe, more than 7 cm, with other different-sized and diffused cysts, and without apparent nodules or ground-glass opacities, which indicates the rarity of the presented case. Two different hypotheses illustrate the formation of cysts in pulmonary amyloidosis. Airway narrowing from inflammation or amyloid deposits may result in a ball valve phenomenon [9]. Otherwise, capillary disruption caused by an ischemic process with amyloid deposition may lead to alveolar wall destruction and cyst formation [10, 11].

The diagnosis of CPA requires lung biopsy, through either transbronchial cryobiopsy or thoracoscopic lung biopsy suggestively. A Congo-red-stain positivity and apple-green birefringence when viewed by polarizing microscopy are necessarily required for diagnosis, along with occasional calcification or ossification, and clusters of plasma cells with monoclonal staining for kappa or lambda immunoglobulin light chains [12]. After the diagnosis of amyloidosis, mass spectrometry-based proteomic analysis is the preferred method for amyloid subtyping, with a higher sensitivity and specificity, which affects the following treatment, compared to immunohistochemistry staining [13]. CPA with Sjögren’s disease suggests worse radiological progression and worse clinical outcomes, in which case mucosal-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphomas should be investigated [14]. The pathological diagnosis of the patient was CPA, with atypical hyperplasia of lymphocytes and plasma cells in some areas, and clonal expansion peaks detected by IgK gene rearrangement analysis. It was necessary to differentiate between indolent small B-cell lymphoma and MALT lymphoma with plasma cell differentiation. The evidence-based treatment of CPA concomitant with Sjögren’s disease remains unclear. However, some cases reported that immunosuppressants and hydroxychloroquine in treating Sjögren’s disease showed benefits in stabilizing the lung lesion and cyst enlargement [15].

Generally, DCLD concomitant with SjD leads to an initial suspicion of LIP; otherwise, with chylous accumulation, to LAM. Interestingly, clinical manifestations and radiological features of lung lesions may lead to a primary diagnosis before confirmation by lung biopsy:LIP, a lymphocytic infiltration disorder, is often associated with connective tissue diseases, particularly Sjögren’s disease [16]. It’s often found radiologically with ground-glass opacities, centrilobular and subpleural nodules, bibasilar reticulation, and peribronchovascular interstitial thickening, in addition to diffuse thin-walled and round cysts, which predominantly distribute in the lower lung fields. Eccentric vessels in the outer perimeter of cysts are characteristic of LIP [17]. Cysts with multiple irregular nodules should raise the suspicion of LIP with amyloidosis.LAM, a systemic disease due to infiltration of neoplastic LAM cells, is often associated with cystic lung lesion, abdominal tumors, and chylous fluid accumulations [18]. In women with compatible cystic lung disease, the additional presence of any one of the following disorders is sufficient to establish a diagnosis of LAM without a lung biopsy: tuberous sclerosis complex, elevated serum vascular endothelial growth factor D (VEGF-D; >=800 pg/ml), renal angiomyolipoma, lymphangioleiomyoma, or chylous effusions. Spontaneous pneumothorax can be the first symptom of LAM in some cases and often recurs. The chylous effusions include chylothorax and chylous ascites, with chylous pericardial effusions, chyloptysis, and chyluria in occasional cases [18], but don’t include chylous reflux lymphedema, the situation of the presented case. LAM cysts are also thin-walled, diffusely and uniformly distributed, round, 2-30mm in size but in similar size, usually upper lobe predominant, often tiny and peripheral, typically devoid of internal structure, and are in some cases accompanied by ground-glass opacities and other thoracic lymphatic manifestations, including septal thickening, thoracic duct dilatation, mediastinal lymph node enlargement, and rarely, cystic lymphangioleiomyomas [17]. Nodules are rarely seen in LAM, which caused the initial misdiagnosis of the presented case.PLCH is generally a smoker’s disease, although a non-smoking female PLCH case was once reported [19]. In active disease, nodules along with thick-walled and irregular cysts predominates, in addition to ground-glass opacities. BHD is an autosomal-dominant disorder and is usually characterized by fibrofolliculoma, lung cysts, and renal tumors [12]. Histologically, deposition of non-structured red-stained material can be seen in both LCDD and PCA under hematoxylin and eosin stains. Nonetheless, LCDD does not bind Congo red or exhibit apple-green birefringence under polarized light [20]. SjD and amyloidosis both belong to lymphoproliferative disorders. There are cases reporting CPA secondary to Sjögren’s disease [21]. However, the presented case shows a manifestation of cystic lung lesion, three years earlier than the onset and diagnosis of Sjögren’s disease, which warrants future studies on the concomitant pathogenesis and mechanisms of CPA with Sjögren’s disease. No obvious evidence or knowledge shows the connection between the lung lesions/CPA and chylous reflux lymphedema at this stage in this case, due to lack of biopsy in the left leg. Nonetheless, there are some case reports showing common lymphedema caused by primary amyloidosis [22–24], and chylothorax and chylous ascites caused by cardiac amyloidosis [25–27]. Therefore, clinicians should be alert to the possible associations between amyloidosis and chylous fluid accumulation.

Lastly, CPA is a rarely seen complication of Sjögren’s disease. Clinicians should be alert to the atypical radiological features of the presented case, where only diffuse, thin-walled, variable-sized cysts exist, without apparent nodules or ground-glass opacities. The size of the presented case is also scarcely reported. Radiological features and manifestations of DCLD helps with initial diagnosis, although a lung biopsy is usually necessary in the end.
