Re-exploration of all ATG genes
Authors: Kentaro Furukawa, Tamara Ginevskaia, Tomotake Kanki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMost autophagy-related (ATG) genes have been identified and characterized through studies using the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae. However, there are no studies that comprehensively compare the contribution of each ATG gene to non-selective bulk autophagy and various types of selective autophagy, including the cytoplasm-to-vacuole targeting (Cvt) pathway, mitophagy, reticulophagy, and pexophagy. Our recent study quantified these types of autophagy in all atg mutants and showed that (i) autophagy is not completely impaired in specific deletants such as lacking the components of the two ubiquitin conjugation-like (UBL) systems, traditionally regarded as essential for autophagy in yeast, and (ii) residual autophagic activity is especially prominent in mutants of the Atg8 UBL system, which display small autophagic body-like vesicles at a low frequency. Alongside these findings, our comprehensive analysis suggested a link between mitophagy and pexophagy, and a differential contribution of Atg proteins to cargo specificity. Studies over three decades using the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae have revealed the fundamental processes underlying autophagy. The core autophagy-related (ATG) genes are involved in autophagosome formation and can be classified into six groups: (i) the Atg1 kinase complex, (ii) the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) complex, (iii) the Atg9-positive vesicle, (iv) the Atg2–Atg18 complex, (v) the Atg12 ubiquitin conjugation-like (UBL) system, and (vi) the Atg8 UBL system. In addition, numerous ATG genes involved in the selective autophagy of specific organelles, including mitochondria, the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and the peroxisome have also been identified in the last 15 years. To date, a total of 45 ATG genes, including some specific to Komagataella phaffii (formerly Pichia pastoris) and Schizosaccharomyces pombe, have been reported. Although the functions of each individual ATG gene are well characterized, there are no comprehensive studies quantitatively analyzing the contribution of each gene to non-selective bulk autophagy, or to various types of selective autophagy. Therefore, this merits the re-analysis of all atg mutants (total 36 mutants: atg1Δ–atg24Δ, atg27Δ, atg29Δ, atg31Δ–atg34Δ, atg36Δ, and atg38Δ–atg42Δ) under unified experimental conditions.

We therefore performed comprehensive quantification of non-selective bulk autophagy, the cytoplasm-to-vacuole targeting (Cvt) pathway, mitophagy, reticulophagy/ER-phagy, and pexophagy in all S. cerevisiae atg mutants by monitoring autophagic processing or maturation of green fluorescent protein (GFP)-fused Pgk1 (Pgk1-GFP), aminopeptidase I (Ape1), Idh1-GFP, Sec63-GFP, and Pex14-GFP [1]. Among the essential core ATG genes, we found that non-selective bulk autophagy, the Cvt pathway and pexophagy were not completely impaired in strains lacking Atg13, the members of the Atg8 UBL system and the members of the Atg12 UBL system, while mitophagy and ER-phagy were almost absent in the same mutants. As the Atg8 UBL system mutants showed substantial residual autophagic activity, we further analyzed atg3Δ and atg8Δ cells. Electron microscopy analysis revealed that atg3Δ and atg8Δ cells (in a vacuolar protease-defective pep4Δ prb1Δ background) could form round vesicles resembling autophagic bodies of bulk autophagy in the vacuole, although their number and size were extremely small compared with those in wild-type cells upon nitrogen starvation (Figure 1). Although Atg8 plays multiple roles during autophagosome formation, including binding several Atg proteins and cargoes, and mediating membrane fusion and phagophore expansion, this result suggests that Atg8 itself, or lipidation of Atg8, is not absolutely essential for autophagic pathways, at least in the case of small cargos. The complete defects in mitophagy and ER-phagy shown by atg8Δ cells are consistent with previous reports that Atg8 determines the size of autophagosomes.

In contrast to wild-type cells, Atg8 UBL system mutants atg3Δ and atg8Δ cannot form sufficient size and number of autophagosomes upon the induction of autophagic pathway, resulting in a strong impairment of these processes. However, Atg8 UBL system mutants display residual autophagic activity and autophagic body-like vesicles in vacuoles in the case of small cargos such as cytosolic proteins and peroxisomes.

To understand why bulk autophagy is not completely compromised in some atg mutants, we investigated the involvement of microautophagy, which entails the direct engulfment of cytoplasmic components by vacuolar membrane invagination. To this end, we analyzed Ape1 maturation in atg3Δ cells with or without Vps4 or Vps27, the components of the endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT), which are thought to facilitate certain types of microautophagy. Remarkably, Ape1 maturation was nearly absent in both atg3Δ vps4Δ and atg3Δ vps27Δ double-mutant cells, suggesting that microautophagy is one of the reasons for the residual autophagic activity in Atg8 UBL system mutants. However, vps4Δ and vps27Δ single-mutant cells also showed reduced Ape1 maturation. Therefore, the involvement of microautophagy alongside other unidentified autophagic processes in the residual autophagic activity in Atg8 UBL system mutants requires further investigation.

Our comprehensive analysis provides additional important information. For instance, we found that pexophagy was strongly impaired in mitophagy-defective atg32Δ cells, suggesting that the mitophagy receptor Atg32 is also involved in pexophagy, or that mitophagy and pexophagy influence each other. Similarly, it has been reported that BNIP3L/NIX, a mitophagy receptor in mammals, regulates both mitophagy and pexophagy. We also found that some atg mutants showed differential contributions to cargo specificity. For example, disruption of the sorting nexins, Atg20 and Atg24, strongly suppressed mitophagy and pexophagy but not ER-phagy. In fact, recent reports have shown that stabilization of the edge of isolation membrane (phagophore) by these sorting nexins is necessary for the autophagic sequestration of large cargoes, such as fragments of peroxisomes and mitochondria. Thus, our datasets will be useful for comparisons of autophagy between yeast and other organisms, and for validation of cargo specificity. In our experimental setup nitrogen starvation was used to induce autophagy. Carrying out the same analyses by employing different conditions such as carbon or phosphate depletion to trigger autophagic pathways, is likely to provide different results and thus allowing to understand the contribution of each Atg protein in the regulation of autophagy in response to different stimuli. In addition to our comprehensive analysis of budding yeast, similar analyses in other organisms, such as fission yeast, worms, and mammals, will pave the way to a complete understanding of autophagic pathways.
