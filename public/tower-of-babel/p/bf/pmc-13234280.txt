Synthetic data Using Population Profiles for cardiOvascular Risk facTors (SUPPORT) in mainland China
Authors: Qiuping Liu, Yu Guo, Mengxi Lu, Yifan Zhou, Xinrong Gao, Xun Tang, Pei Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Publicly available synthetic population datasets often lack detailed health information, limiting their utility in disease modeling. To address this gap, we present the SUPPORT (Synthetic data Using Population Profiles for cardiOvascular Risk facTors) dataset, a large-scale cross-sectional resource comprising 777,358,492 synthetic individuals aged 35–84 across seven geographic regions of China, anchored to the year 2020 demographic structure. Each synthetic individual possesses a detailed profile of sociodemographic attributes and major cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors, including blood pressure, cholesterol levels, body mass index, and a history of diabetes. The population was constructed using iterative proportional fitting, multivariate normal distribution sampling, and multiple imputation, integrating data from China’s Seventh National Population Census (2020), the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, and numerous health surveys. Technical validation against census statistics and independent cohorts, including the China Kadoorie Biobank, confirmed that the dataset accurately replicates marginal sociodemographic distributions and adequately approximates cardiovascular risk profiles of real-world populations. The open-source SUPPORT dataset can be extended with additional attributes, providing a publicly available resource to enable robust, individual-level modeling of CVD.

Background & Summary

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of global disease burden1. Large-scale individual-level data are essential for understanding disease trends and population heterogeneity, which is crucial for improving CVD prevention and treatment by ensuring generalizability and robust findings2,3. However, access to such data is often limited; nationwide population studies typically release only aggregate statistics due to privacy concerns, while local-level datasets usually lack the necessary sample size for comprehensive analysis. This scarcity of large-scale, individual-level data presents a significant challenge for researchers, particularly in vast and diverse countries such as China, where population profiles vary considerably by region4. Synthetic populations, defined here as micro-level representations that statistically replicate the joint distributions of real individuals, offer a promising solution to this data-access challenge5. While some generative models require training on sensitive individual-level records, methods that reconstruct populations from publicly available aggregate data (e.g., census summaries) are generally considered to offer relatively stronger privacy protections6.

Although synthetic population datasets are available for several countries, such as Canada7, the United States (US)8,9, the Netherlands10, the United Kingdom (UK)11, and Sweden12, they have primarily focused on sociodemographic attributes. In recent years, a growing number of initiatives have also released synthetic health datasets, including Medicare Claims Synthetic Public Use Files in the US13, the synthetic cardiovascular cohort and the synthetic COVID-19 dataset from the UK Clinical Practice Research Datalink14, synthetic cancer data from Public Health England National Disease Registration Service (the Simulacrum)15, and microdata from Israel’s National Registry of Live Births16. These efforts illustrate the expanding landscape of synthetic health data resources. However, synthetic data resources specifically targeting CVD risk factors are still relatively scarce, and most focus on populations from high-income countries. Openly available population-level synthetic datasets that integrate major CVD risk factors remain uncommon, particularly for low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) such as China, despite these countries bearing a disproportionate burden of CVDs across regions17. Consequently, an open-source synthetic dataset that integrates major CVD risk factors for the Chinese population is needed. Furthermore, existing data for the Chinese population18 may not accurately reflect the demographic profile captured in the latest nationwide population census (the seventh)19. This Data Descriptor describes the generation of a synthetic population for seven geographic regions in China, addressing these gaps by combining up-to-date census data with key information on CVD risk factors.

Two primary methodologies are typically used to generate synthetic populations: synthetic reconstruction (SR) and combinatorial optimization (CO)7. SR techniques construct a population from aggregate data, such as census tables or summary statistics, by first estimating the joint distribution of the desired attributes7–9. In contrast, CO methods require a representative sample of individual-level microdata. These methods generate a population by selecting and duplicating individuals from the sample, and iteratively optimizing the fit between the synthetic population characteristics and the known marginal totals of the real population.

The data available for this project primarily consisted of two distinct types: comprehensive aggregate statistics from census and literature sources, and a large-scale but geographically localized microdata cohort. This common data scenario, lacking a nationally representative microdata sample, renders CO methods unsuitable. Therefore, we employed the synthetic reconstruction approach8,10,18 to generate cross-sectional individual-level population data, namely the Synthetic data Using Population Profiles for cardiOvascular Risk facTors (SUPPORT) dataset for the Chinese population. The following sections detail the data generation workflow, final data record structure, technical validation procedures, and usage notes for this dataset.

Methods

Overview

We generated a synthetic population of individuals aged 35–84 across seven major regions of China (Central, East, North, Northeast, Northwest, South, and Southwest) for the year 2020. For each synthetic individual, the dataset includes a comprehensive set of attributes. Sociodemographic variables include sex (male or female), age (in single-year increments and 5-year groups for stratification), region, settlement type (urban or rural), ethnicity (58 categories), and educational level (nine categories). The key cardiometabolic markers include systolic blood pressure (SBP, mmHg), total cholesterol (TC, mmol/L), and body mass index (BMI, kg/m²). Health status and behavior variables capture current smoking status (yes/no), history of diabetes (yes/no), and history of CVD (yes/no). Medication history includes the use of lipid-lowering and antihypertensive drugs. We followed an eight-step procedure to construct the dataset, comprising data preprocessing (steps 1–5, Fig. 1) and variable creation and assignment (steps 6–8, Fig. 2). To ensure the reproducibility of our data generation pipeline, detailed metadata for all third-party input parameters including their sources (e.g., study name and DOIs or URLs), parameters extracted from the source, data collection year(s) or survey period, are provided in Tables 1 and 24,20–45. Step 1: Adjustment of the sociodemographic data

To generate sociodemographic attributes, we employed Iterative Proportional Fitting (IPF)9, a standard SR technique, using China’s Seventh National Population Census (2020) as the primary data source19. (Table 1) We processed the raw census tables to generate the required inputs for the IPF algorithm: specifically, stratified marginal distributions (by age group, education, and ethnicity across regions) and joint distributions (age-group-by-education and age-group-by-ethnicity at the national level). These inputs were stratified by settlement type and sex. This stratification was essential for preserving the demographic structure within each subgroup and for creating lower-dimensional contingency tables for the fitting process. The adjustments to the raw census tables primarily involved three parts. First, multiple non-urban settlement types were aggregated into a single ‘rural’ category to align with our ‘urban/rural’ classification. Second, age groups and population counts were aligned across variables (age, education, and ethnicity) to ensure consistency between the marginal and joint distributions. Third, the tables were split by settlement type (urban/rural) and sex, creating separate input files for each stratum. Further details of this preprocessing workflow are provided in Fig. 1.

Step 3: Collection of CVD risk factor data

We synthesized the core CVD risk factors (SBP, TC, BMI, smoking, and diabetes) by sampling from multivariate normal distributions specified for each region, age group, and sex stratum. The parameters for these distributions (Table 4) were derived from two distinct input types: mean/prevalence values and covariance matrices. The means (for continuous variables) and prevalence (for binary variables) were collated from a wide array of authoritative sources to ensure regional accuracy and data validity. These sources included major nationwide surveys, such as the China Chronic Disease and Risk Factor Surveillance (CCDRFS)22–26,44,45,47, the China Patient-Centered Evaluative Assessment of Cardiac Events (PEACE) Million Persons Project4,27,28, and the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS)29–31. Due to a lack of accessible public microdata, the covariance matrix defining the interrelationships between CVD risk factors could not be estimated at the regional level. Instead, we derived a sex- and age-specific covariance matrix using data from the Chinese Electronic health Records Research in Yinzhou (CHERRY) study48. This approach inherently assumes that while the marginal means and prevalences of risk factors vary geographically, the underlying correlation structure among these risk factors remains uniform across all seven diverse regions of China. This assumption aligns with those made in global studies, such as the WHO cardiovascular risk prediction models49, which premise that baseline exposure levels can differ geographically, but the biological associations among risk factors remain largely invariant. The covariance matrix was constructed by first computing the Pearson correlation coefficients between all pairs of risk factors (yielding a correlation matrix) to capture linear relationships, extracting the standard deviation (SD) of each variable to quantify marginal variability, and combining them to derive the final covariance matrix using the MBESS package in R.

Medication history was imputed using an approach derived from established methodologies50,51, conditioning on the previously synthesized variables to address the complete missingness of medication data in our primary simulation. By combining CHARLS29–31 data with SUPPORT across regions, we used observed correlations in CHARLS, along with synthesized variables in SUPPORT, to impute missing values. The datasets were preprocessed separately prior to appending. In SUPPORT, variables representing histories of antihypertensive and lipid-lowering medication use were created and initialized as missing. Because the generation of upstream variables (age, SBP, BMI, TC, current smoking status, and history of diabetes) involved stochastic components, five distinct random seeds were used to generate five independent synthetic datasets per region. To reduce the propagation of stochastic variability before imputation, these datasets were pooled. For continuous variables, the pooled value was the mean across the five datasets; for binary variables, a majority rule was applied. For the CHARLS data, the 2015 survey wave was utilized to construct a harmonized variable library ensuring consistency with SUPPORT. Given the relatively small unweighted CHARLS sample, resampling with replacement was conducted among participants with available blood sample data, using CHARLS blood sample weights as sampling probabilities, to generate a synthetic cohort of 100 million individuals with unique identifiers. This expanded CHARLS population was stratified into seven geographic regions and appended to the pooled SUPPORT datasets. The resulting combined dataset, comprising individuals with complete risk factor information and those missing medication histories, established the analytical framework for subsequent imputation.

Step 4: Construction of multivariate normal distributions for CVD risk factors

Using the mean or prevalence vectors and covariance matrix from step 3, we defined the parameters for the multivariate normal distributions of CVD risk factors for each subgroup (stratum) defined by region, 5-year age group, and sex. (Fig. 1) Each stratum was assigned a specific mean vector (using the collated means/prevalence) and the covariance matrix (derived from the CHERRY study). Sampling from these defined distributions, using functions such as mvrnorm from the MASS package in R, was performed in step 7. This overall approach ensures that the stratum-specific target means and covariance structure are preserved within each subgroup.

Step 5: Preparation of input tables for CVD history

We prepared the input data to synthesize a binary CVD history variable, which would be assigned in step 8 using the IPF method. This step ensures that the final prevalence of CVD history in the synthetic data aligns with epidemiological benchmarks from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study20,21. (Fig. 1) We prepared two sets of inputs. First, we created a national-level contingency table for age group by CVD status (yes/no) to serve as the ‘seed’ table, capturing the fundamental age-CVD association. It was derived by applying GBD’s age- and sex-specific national prevalence rates to the 2020 census population counts. Second, we generated regional-level marginal target totals for the IPF. These included: (a) the population count for each age group within each region (from the census) and (b) the total count of individuals with CVD history in each region (by applying GBD’s region- and sex-specific prevalence to the regional census totals).

For each of the seven regions, we generated sociodemographic profiles separately for each stratum (defined by settlement type and sex). (Fig. 2) This was achieved using a three-dimensional IPF algorithm to fit the joint distribution of 5-year age group (a), educational level (e), and ethnicity (g). Step 7: Create and assign CVD risk factor attributes

Once the sociodemographic shell for the population was created, we assigned CVD risk factor values by sampling from the multivariate normal distributions defined in step 452,53. (Fig. 2) For each individual, a single draw was taken from the specific multivariate normal distribution corresponding to their stratum (defined by region, age, and sex). This draw yielded a vector of values. For continuous variables (e.g., SBP, TC, and BMI), the sampled values were directly assigned. For binary variables (e.g., current smoking and history of diabetes), sampling from the multivariate normal distribution yielded a continuous latent variable for each individual. To mathematically map these continuous values to individual-level probabilities, the latent values were transformed to the [0, 1] interval using the standard normal cumulative distribution function. Binary outcomes (0 or 1) were subsequently generated via Bernoulli sampling by comparing each individual’s mapped probability against a random draw from a uniform distribution53. To impute medication history, we used the merged CHARLS and SUPPORT datasets to implement multiple imputation via the mice package in R. Sex-stratified logistic regression models were used to predict the use of antihypertensive drugs (based on age, settlement type, and SBP) and lipid-lowering drugs (based on age, settlement type, and TC). Five imputed datasets were generated for each medication type and merged back into the main synthetic sets using unique IDs, yielding five complete datasets for downstream analyses.

Step 8: Create and assign CVD history attributes

In the final step, a binary CVD history status (History_CVD) was assigned to each synthetic individual using the IPF outputs prepared in step 5. (Fig. 2) For each sex and region, a two-dimensional IPF algorithm was run. This algorithm used the national-level contingency table (age groups by CVD status) as the seed and iteratively adjusted it to match the regional-level marginal totals for the age distribution and the regional-level marginal totals for CVD prevalence. This resulted in a final contingency table (age groups by CVD status) for each region and sex. Individuals within each stratum (e.g., 40–44-year-old males in the East region) were then probabilistically assigned a ‘1’ (having a history of CVD) or ‘0’ (no CVD history) to match the cell counts in this final fitted table.

Technical Validation

Technical validation was designed to assess the correspondence of the statistical properties of the synthetic population with those of observed real-world data. We validated the synthetic data at three levels: the distribution of individual variables, composite indicators derived from multiple variables, and the joint distribution among variables. For individual variables, we evaluated the goodness-of-fit by comparing the population counts, means, and prevalence of key characteristics in the synthetic dataset with their corresponding values in the target population. To validate composite indicators, we applied the 2019 WHO non-laboratory risk prediction models49 to estimate the 10-year risk of coronary heart disease and stroke for each individual, based on age, sex, SBP, BMI, and smoking status. To quantify the concordance between individual variables and composite indicators, we calculated the Relative Absolute Error (RAE)7 for each variable category i (e.g., the ‘male’ category for the variable ‘sex’). For the joint distribution of variables, we calculated Pearson correlation coefficients among the continuous CVD risk factors in both the synthetic population and the observed populations, and compared the results between the two.

Sociodemographic data validation

We validated the sociodemographic attributes of the synthetic data against the official statistics from China’s Seventh National Population Census. This validation involved a two-stage process dictated by the structure of the public census data. First, the census provides regional data for education and ethnicity only as aggregates across the entire population (ages 0–100 + ). Therefore, we initially validated the distributions of these two variables in a preliminary synthetic population covering the whole age range. This step ensured that our generation process accurately replicated the specific aggregate distributions. Second, after confirming the fidelity of the education and ethnicity data, we filtered the dataset to our target population (aged 35–84). Within this final population, we validated the distributions for settlement type and 5-year age groups against their corresponding census totals.

Internal validations of CVD risk factors

To enhance univariate fidelity, we characterized the national-level distributions of key continuous CVD risk factors (SBP, TC, and BMI) using sex-stratified histograms and compared them with mean levels reported in national survey data22,44,45. We then conducted internal validation of the joint distribution of Age, SBP, TC, and BMI using large regional data from the CHERRY cohort and nationally representative data from the CHARLS study. All populations were stratified by sex and age (under 60 years vs. 60 years and above). Because the CHERRY cohort is geographically restricted to East China, we validated the East China SUPPORT data against CHERRY, covering the entire synthetic age range. Conversely, we validated the national-level SUPPORT data against CHARLS; however, since CHARLS primarily includes individuals aged 45 and older, the lower age limit for the under-60 group was set at 45 years for both datasets in this comparison.

External validations of CVD risk factors

National-level univariate external validation was conducted using other nationally representative surveys that were not used to derive parameters for data generation27,28,55–57. We performed an external validation of the individual and composite CVD risk factors against the China Kadoorie Biobank (CKB) study58,59, a large, independent population-based cohort not used in the data-generation process. We compared our synthetic data to published results from ten CKB recruitment sites spanning urban and rural settlements, matched to our study regions based on geography and an established regional classification scheme60. To ensure a valid comparison, we harmonized our synthetic dataset by applying the same inclusion criteria (ages 40–79) and exclusion criteria (having a history of CVD) as the CKB study59. We calculated the 10-year predicted risks for coronary heart disease (CHD) and stroke using the WHO non-laboratory-based risk model49, applying the original calibration coefficients from the CKB study59. All risk factor levels and predicted risks from our synthetic data were age-standardized using the Sixth National Population Census structure to mirror the CKB methods and allow for direct comparison. Furthermore, external validation of the national-level joint distribution of variables was conducted using data from the China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS) cohort61. The CHNS is a nationally representative, publicly accessible open cohort widely used in epidemiological research62. For this validation, we applied the identical population stratification, CVD risk factors, and metrics utilized during the internal validation phase.

Sociodemographic validation results

The distributions of population counts for settlement type and 5-year age groups in the synthetic population were nearly identical to the Seventh National Population Census totals. The distribution of education levels also demonstrated a strong fit with the census data (Fig. 3), with the most significant discrepancy being a minor RAE of 0.04% for those with post-secondary non-tertiary education in the East region. For ethnicity, the synthetic data generally mirrored the observed distributions. (Fig. 4) The RAE for the Han ethnic group was less than 0.01% in all seven regions. As expected, the RAE values were larger for ethnic groups with smaller population counts; for instance, the Nu ethnic group in Northwest China (observed count: 25) had an RAE of 92.00%. The SUPPORT dataset represents a cross-sectional population anchored demographically to the year 2020, with CVD risk factor parameters drawn from studies spanning 2013 to 2020. It is designed for research applications requiring individual-level baseline data, such as disease modeling or healthcare simulations. Synthesizing a simulated population is typically the foundational step in international microsimulation research62–66. This dataset can support diverse modeling applications, including evaluating hypertension or diabetes management strategies in low- and middle-income countries, comparing the health and economic impacts of multi-risk factor control strategies, evaluating precision treatment trade-offs, or simulating the population-level cardiovascular benefits of environmental interventions. It serves as a foundational resource that can be enriched by incorporating additional variables via imputation67 or made dynamic by modeling population growth68 or longitudinal risk trajectories69.

Crucially, SUPPORT is a synthetic dataset derived fundamentally from aggregate parameters and a uniform covariance structure, so it cannot be used to discover novel epidemiological associations or underlying causal relationships. It is strictly unsuitable for studying intrinsic longitudinal trajectories, causal outcomes dependent on true clinical histories, or time-varying exposures. Furthermore, the dataset does not capture post-2020 structural shifts in healthcare, such as COVID-19 disruptions or the widespread adoption of novel cardiometabolic medications.

Finally, users should note the limitations of the generation process. Input parameters were derived from heterogeneous sources with varying survey years, potentially introducing temporal inconsistencies. Some source studies reported risk factors in broad 10-year age bands, necessitating the assumption of uniform levels across adjacent 5-year age groups. Additionally, small sample sizes for older adults and certain ethnic minority groups in the source data decrease precision in these demographics, requiring cautious interpretation and adequate statistical power. We strongly encourage researchers to validate analyses using independent survey data when possible, and we note that emerging machine learning techniques, such as generative adversarial networks (GANs)70,71, offer promising avenues for synthetic data generation in the future. Studies utilizing these data are required to cite this Data Descriptor.
