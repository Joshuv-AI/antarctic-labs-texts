Robotic-assisted total hip arthroplasty outperforms manual technique in obese and overweight patients: a prospective comparative study
Authors: Chaoqun Yu, Zian Zhang, Chang Liu, Zhenchao Huang, Xinzhe Lu, Yusi Gao, Haining Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the increasing prevalence of obesity, there is growing awareness of the impact of overweight and obesity on total hip arthroplasty (THA). Research exploring the accuracy of acetabular component orientation in THA between robotic-assisted and manual techniques across different BMI categories is insufficient.MethodsThis prospective study evaluated 221 patients who underwent THA with a Robotic Interactive Orthopaedic Arm system and 252 patients who underwent manual THA between March 2022 and January 2024. The patients were divided into four groups according to their BMI. We analysed whether there were differences in the accuracy of acetabular component positioning between robotic-assisted THA and manual THA across different BMI categories.ResultsIn the overweight group, robotic-assisted THA achieved a significantly higher rate of abductions within the target range (73/6) than manual THA (62/28) (p = 0.000). Both abductions and anteversions within the target range were also significantly more frequent in the robotic-assisted THA group (69/10) than in the manual THA group (56/34) (p = 0.000). Among the obese patients, robotic-assisted THA showed a perfect record for anteversions within the target range (29/0), markedly outperforming manual THA (39/6) (p = 0.040).ConclusionIn the overweight (24 kg/m² ≤ BMI < 28 kg/m²) and obese (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m²) groups, robotic-assisted THA demonstrates significantly greater accuracy in acetabular component positioning compared to manual THA. This indicates that robotic-assisted technology may provide a more precise positioning of the acetabular component in overweight and obese patients.Keywords: Robotic-assisted total hip arthroplasty, Body mass index, Overweight, Obesity, Acetabular component positioning

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) can greatly improve the quality of life of patients with end-stage hip disease. Some studies have shown that the accuracy of acetabular implantation is closely related to postoperative dislocation, prosthesis loosening, periprosthetic fracture, the complications described above, and the prosthesis survival rate [1–5]. The outcomes of THA are influenced by various factors, including those related to the surgeon and the patient. Barrack et al. [6] noted that a low surgical volume by the surgeon and high body mass index (BMI) of the patient can increase the risk of improper placement of the acetabulum. Charalambous et al. [7] also pointed out that for patients requiring THA, it is advisable to select an experienced surgeon for the operation. Obesity presents a significant challenge for surgeons. It prolongs the operation time and increases the risk of dislocation [8–10]. However, with the rapid development of digital orthopaedic technology, CT-based robot-assisted THA is changing this situation. Robot-assisted THA can effectively reduce errors related to patient experience, improper surgical positioning, and complex acetabular orientation [11–13]. Research exploring the accuracy of acetabular component orientation in THA between robotic-assisted and manual techniques in different BMI categories is insufficient and requires support from more centers and larger sample sizes. The purpose of this study was to analyze whether there are differences between robotic-assisted and manual THA in populations having different BMI categories.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the medical ethics committee of our hospital The study adhered to the principles of informed consent. All participants received a detailed introduction to the study before it began and were informed that they had the right to withdraw their consent at any time without any adverse consequences. This prospective study evaluated 221 patients who underwent THA using Robotic Interactive Orthopaedic Arm (RIO) system (MAKO Surgical Corporation, Fort Lauderdale, FL, USA) and 252 patients who underwent manual THA between March 2022 and January 2024. All patients underwent surgery by the same surgical team using the Accolade II cementless prosthesis system (Stryker, Mahwah, NJ, USA) All the patients underwent THA using the modified Hardinge approach.

The inclusion criteria for this trial were as follows: (1) age between 18 and 80 years, regardless of sex; (2) planned unilateral primary THA; (3) surgical indications(primary or secondary osteoarthritis of the hip joint, avascular necrosis of the femoral head, rheumatoid arthritis involving the hip joint, femoral neck fractures, etc.) for THA; (4) ability of the subject/guardian to understand the purpose of the trial, demonstrate sufficient compliance with the trial protocol, and sign the informed consent form.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) uncontrollable infection in the hip or other parts of the body; (2) inability to tolerate surgery due to other diseases; (3) neuromuscular dysfunction leading to postoperative joint instability or abnormal gait; (4) severe osteoporosis, metabolic bone disease, radiation bone disease, or joint tumour; (5) known allergy to implant materials; and (6) other conditions deemed unsuitable for participation by the investigators.

We divided the patients into 4 groups: Group A(< 18.5 kg/m²);Group B(18.5 kg/m² ≤ BMI < 24 kg/m²);Group C(24 kg/m² ≤ BMI < 28 kg/m² );Group D(BMI ≥ 28 kg/m²) according to BMI in Chinese adults [14]. The sample size for each group was calculated using the function “pwr.chisq.test(w = NULL, df = NULL, power = NULL, sig.level = NULL)” in R version 4.4.1 (2024-06-14 ucrt). ‘w’ represents the effect size, ‘df’ represents the degree of freedom, ‘sig.level’ represents the significance level, and ‘power’ represents the power of the test. ‘w’ was set to 0.6 based on previous studies [15, 16]; In this study, the degree of freedom ‘df’ was calculated as \(r-1)*(c-1)\), where ‘r’ is the number of rows and ‘c’ is the number of columns, with ‘r = 4’ and ‘c = 3’; the desired power is 0.80, and the significance level is 0.05. The calculation yielded a sample size \( N = 55.52343 \). To account for a 10% attrition rate, the minimum sample size per group was calculated to be at least 62.

We evaluated the postoperative anteroposterior (AP) pelvic radiographs of each patient. The target ranges for abduction and anteversion for THA were defined according to Lewinnek et al. [2] (30° to 50° and 5° to 25°, respectively). We analysed whether there were differences in the accuracy of acetabular component positioning and postoperative complications between robotic-assisted THA(R-THA) and manual THA(C-THA) across different BMI categories.

Surgical procedures

In the R-THA group, all procedures were performed with the assistance of the MAKOplasty Total Hip Application System (version 3.0), which was used to guide neck resection, acetabular reaming, and acetabular component positioning.

In the C-THA group, the surgeon marked the femoral osteotomy site based on the predetermined distance from the lesser and greater trochanters. The angle and orientation of the acetabular component were guided by specific intraoperative landmarks, including the transverse acetabular ligament, the anterior and posterior walls of the acetabulum, and the anterior superior iliac spine. To enhance the precision of the acetabular component placement, an external alignment guide was attached to the handle of the cutting-edge reamer and acetabular impactor.

Clinical and radiographic assessment

The data were repeatedly measured by three professionally trained physicians according to established measurement methods.

As shown in the Fig. 1a, The anteversion angle was calculated using the Pradhan algorithm [17]. The Pradhan algorithm uses an anteroposterior radiograph to measure the anteversion angle of the acetabular cup. The metal edge of the cup appears as an ellipse, with “D” representing the length of the long axis of the ellipse. By marking a point at one-fifth of the long axis and drawing a perpendicular line intersecting the ellipse, we can measure the length “p” from the intersection point to the long axis. The anteversion angle can then be calculated using the formula: Anteversion = arcsin(p/0.4D).

(a) Measurement method of anteversion. (b) Measurement method of abduction

As shown in the Fig. 1b, the abduction angle α is the angle between the line connecting the lowest point of both teardrops and the long axis of the ellipse.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 25.0 (IBM, USA). A significance level of p ≤ 0.05 was set, and a 95% confidence interval was used. Measurement data were presented as mean(standard deviation)[mean(SD)]. The independent samples t-test was used to compare differences between the two groups. The chi-square test was used to analyze the comparative data for the anteversion and inclination of the acetabular cup in relation to the target range. Fisher’s Exact test was used to compare postoperative complications. Using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient to assess the degree of inter-rater reliability.

Result

The inter-observer reliability for the measurement of abduction angle was high, with an Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) of 0.962. Similarly, the anteversion angle measurements demonstrated excellent reliability, with an ICC of 0.973.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first prospective comparative study to examine the anteversion and abduction of acetabular components in robotic-assisted THA compared with conventional manual THA across different BMI categories. Our study revealed significant statistical differences between R-THA and C-THA in patients with a BMI between 24 and 28 kg/m² and in those with a BMI > 28 kg/m². This finding highlights the potential of robot-assisted technology in enhancing surgical precision, especially in overweight and obese patients.

As obesity becomes increasingly prevalent, there is growing awareness of the impact of overweight and obesity on THA. Elson et al. [18] pointed out a significant correlation between obesity and insufficient anteversion, a high BMI is the most significant risk factor formalpositioning. Barrack et al. [6] also indicated that the odds of missing the target increased by ≥ 0.2 for every 5 kg/m² increase in BMI. Obese patients typically have more subcutaneous fat and other soft tissue, which may obscure or alter the palpation of bony landmarks, making accurate intraoperative positioning challenging. The surgical field may be more difficult to expose in obese patients, limiting the surgeon’s view and operating space, and increasing the difficulty of the surgery. Some patients with high BMI and hip dysplasia often have more severe femoral head dislocation and changes in the surrounding anatomical structures of the hip joint than other patients, which also increases the difficulty of the surgery. Conventional THA is not the best treatment option for these patients.

Based on preoperative CT data, the robotic system can precisely determine the actual position of the patient’s pelvis in space and provide reference values for anteversion and abduction relative to the patient’s pelvis. It is not influenced by the patient’s posture or soft tissue, factors which can often introduce errors in traditional surgical methods [19, 20]. Theoretically, robotic-assisted THA should offer greater accuracy in acetabular component positioning than manual THA, especially in patients with higher BMIs. Gupta et al. [21] and Imai et al. [22] have demonstrated that robotic-assisted THA can maintain the accuracy of acetabular component positioning even in patients with higher BMIs. Building upon their work, our study further validated the advantages of robotic-assisted THA by increasing the sample size and conducting a comparative analysis with manual THA across different BMI categories. To the best of our knowledge, this approach has not been previously adopted.

Previous studies have indicated that experienced surgeons can enhance the accuracy of acetabular component positioning in THA [6, 23, 24]. However, this improvement in accuracy is limited. Our study shows that in the lower BMI group (including the underweight group with BMI < 18.5 kg/m² and the normal range group with 18.5 kg/m² ≤ BMI < 24 kg/m²), experienced surgeons can achieve a similar level of accuracy with manual THA as with robotic-assisted THA. Nevertheless, when the BMI is ≥ 24 kg/m², robotic-assisted THA significantly outperforms manual THA in terms of the accuracy of acetabular component positioning. This finding underscores the unique value of robotic-assisted THA when dealing in patients with a higher BMI.

Previous studies have often defined individuals with a higher BMIs as obese [6, 7, 21, 25]. However, the definition of obesity should consider the fact that individuals of different ethnicities and regions may have different healthy BMI thresholds. Globally, although the incidence of obesity is on the rise, the proportion of patients with obesity in the THA population remains relatively small. Conversely, the overweight population constitutes a larger proportion of patients. Zhang et al. [26] retrospectively assessed that robotic-assisted THA achieved better outcomes in patients with a BMI of ≥ 28 kg/m² compared to manual THA, while other BMI groups were not included in the analysis. In this study, we categorised the patients into four groups based on the BMI standards for Chinese adults [14]: underweight, normal weight, overweight, and obese. This grouping method is more reflective of the actual situation of our study participants and helps to assess the impact of different BMI categories on surgical outcomes more accurately. However, due to the small proportion of obese patients in this study, the sample size was insufficient to support further subgroup analyses. Since there are differences in the definitions of obesity among different populations, we adopted BMI classification standards suitable for the Chinese population in our study design. This helped us identify and compare the impact of different BMI categories on the accuracy of the acetabular component position more precisely.

In this study, we performed THA on all patients using a unified modified Hardinge approach and did not include patient groups operated on using the anterior or posterior approaches. We recognise that different surgical approaches may impact the surgical process to varying degrees depending on the BMI, which could also contribute to the differences in the study results.

Another limitation of this study is that we used X-rays to manually measure postoperative anteversion and abduction of the acetabular component. Although these data were repeatedly measured by three professionally trained physicians according to established measurement methods, there is still a possibility of intra-observer error due to the subjectivity of the measurers, technical proficiency, environmental factors, physiological state, and fatigue or lack of concentration.

Therefore, we recommend that future studies consider include different surgical approaches and adopt more objective measurement techniques, such as three-dimensional CT scanning or automated image analysis software, to reduce human error and improve the accuracy and reliability of the measurements. Using these methods, the impact of different BMI categories on surgical outcomes can be assessed more accurately, thereby providing more precise guidance for clinical practice. In addition, there are currently few reports on imageless navigation technology for THA. However, imageless navigation technology has demonstrated benefits in the field of knee replacement. Stefano et al. [27]introduced that an imageless navigation system can improve the accuracy and clinical outcomes of total knee replacement. Fabio et al. [28, 29]introduced a novel imageless robotic technique for total knee arthroplasty that not only enhanced the precision of tibial and femoral component alignment but also contributed to better pain perception and an improved range of knee motion. In the future, image-less robotic technique for total hip arthroplasty should be developed and compared with existing techniques.

Conclusion

In the overweight (24 kg/m² ≤ BMI < 28 kg/m²) and obese (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m²) groups, robotic-assisted THA demonstrates significantly greater accuracy in acetabular component positioning compared to manual THA. This indicates that robotic-assisted technology may provide a more precise positioning of the acetabular component in overweight and obese patients.
