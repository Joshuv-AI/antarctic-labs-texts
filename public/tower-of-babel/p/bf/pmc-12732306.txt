Association between intraoperative hypotension and motor evoked potential change in ossification of posterior longitudinal ligament and/or ossification of ligamentum flavum surgery
Authors: Tun Liu, Wentao Wang, Huaguang Qi, Jia Li, Bin Guo, Kuo Jiang, Songchuan Zhao, Ruiguo Wang, Jitao Zhang, Gang Wu, Gang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntraoperative hypotension (IOH) is a recognized contributor to changes in motor evoked potential (MEP) during spinal surgeries. Additionally, it is essential to precisely define IOH across different surgical phases. However, there is limited data on optimal IOH thresholds for predicting MEP changes in thoracic ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) and/or ossification of the ligamentum flavum (OLF) surgery. We aim to determine the IOH thresholds for predicting MEP changes during surgical treatment for OPLL and/or OLF based on different surgical phase.MethodsData collected included demographic information, surgical details, mean arterial pressure (MAP) values, and MEP signals. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was employed to determine the MAP thresholds. A comparative analysis was performed to evaluate IOH episodes occurring during predecompression versus postdecompression surgical phases, specifically in the early and later stages. Additionally, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the association between surgical variables and MEP change.ResultsThe MAP thresholds for predicting changes in MEP at the early surgical stage were determined as follows: 70 mmHg for patients with combined OPLL and OLF, 66 mmHg for OPLL patients, and 65 mmHg for OLF patients. However, it is recommended that MAP at the later surgical stage should be elevated to exceed 75 mmHg, 73 mmHg, and 71 mmHg in patients diagnosed with combined OPLL and OLF, OPLL, and OLF, respectively. A stronger correlation was observed between MAP variability ratio and MEP amplitude reduction ratio (ARR) during postdecompression surgical phase. At the early surgical stage, the administration of ephedrine bolus was identified as a risk factor for predicting MEP change (odds ratio [OR]=1.13, p<.01). At the later stage, the risk-factors included ephedrine bolus (OR=1.09, p<.01), estimated blood loss (per 100 mL) (OR=1.23, p=.02), and patients with combined OPLL and OLF (OR=12.12, p<.01).ConclusionsWe determined cutoff values for MAP to predict changes in MEP in patients undergoing surgical treatment for OPLL and/or OLF based on different surgical phases. Compared to the early surgical stage, patients exhibit less tolerance to IOH at the later surgical stage. A stronger correlation was observed between the MAP variability ratio and MEP ARR at the later surgical stage. Additionally, we identified surgical factors that are associated with a higher probability of MEP change.

Introduction

Intraoperative hypotension (IOH) is a risk factor for postoperative complications in spinal fusion surgery [[1], [2], [3]]. Although there is no universally accepted threshold for IOH, an absolute mean arterial pressure (MAP) of ≤65 mmHg is commonly used as a criterion in noncardiac general surgery [1,2]. This is because 65 mmHg is generally considered the lower limit of human autoregulation. Moreover, IOH across different surgical phases should be precisely defined due to the distinct underlying mechanisms [3,4] involved. Südfeld et al. [4] demonstrated that early IOH is particularly relevant to major complications. Additionally, Glassman et al. [1] identified that IOH occurring within the first hour of surgery is associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications in spinal surgeries. Intraoperative fluid administration is one method to address IOH. However, excessive fluid administration may lead to respiratory complications [5]. The administration of vasopressors is the other method to increase MAP, although this may compromise splanchnic perfusion [6]. Therefore, it is crucial to accurately refine and manage IOH according to the different surgical phases.

Surgical decompression is the standard treatment for patients with thoracic spinal myelopathy. It was demonstrated that myelopathy patients experience a greater degree of “anesthetic fade” [7]. A notable challenge in spinal surgery is the progressive suppression of MEP signals or even false-positive changes—a phenomenon termed “anesthetic fade” [[7], [8], [9]]. Although MEP signals deterioration occur most frequently during posterior decompression, the majority of changed MEP waveforms demonstrated recovery after intraoperative interventions [10]. Furthermore, surgical treatment for thoracic ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) and/or ossification of the ligamentum flavum (OLF) differ in both surgical outcomes [[11], [12], [13]] and MEP change thresholds [12,13]. The intraoperative estimated blood loss (EBL) can be substantial [12,14]. So, induced hypotension is favored as a strategy to reduce EBL, improve surgical field visibility, and potentially shorten operative duration [1]. However, IOH is a major contributing factor to MEP signal change and may lead to spinal cord dysfunction [15,16]. Consequently, raising MAP is a standardized response to address intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM) changes [16,17]. MAP is advocated to be maintained above 80 mmHg during spinal deformity surgery [18]. However, robust evidence from evidence-based medicine is currently lacking, and there is limited data on the optimal MAP threshold for predicting MEP change in the surgical treatment for OPLL and/or OLF patients. It is clinically crucial to determining optimal MAP thresholds in OPLL and/or OLF surgery [13]. These cutoff values can assist the surgical team to achieve a delicate balance between minimizing EBL and maintaining adequate perfusion in the thoracic spinal cord, thereby exerting a less inhibitory effect on MEP signals.

Our primary endpoint was to determine the optimal IOH thresholds for predicting changes in MEP amplitude in the upper extremities of the patients during surgical treatment for OPLL and/or OLF based on different surgical phases. Our secondary endpoint was to identify the surgical factors associated with a higher probability of MEP change based on different surgical phases.

Methods

Ethics

The study was approved by Ethical Committee and Institutional Review Board (IRB, No. 2025-KY-119-01) from our hospital of our university (Chairperson: Prof. Yan) on 29th June 2025, and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. In light of the retrospective nature of our research and the use of completely deidentified data, the need for informed consent was waived.

Patients

A total of 207 patients who underwent surgical treatment for OPLL and/or OLF at our hospital from March 2014 to March 2025 were identified. Electrical medical records (EMR) were retrospectively reviewed to collect demographic data, surgical data, MAP values, and MEP signals.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients aged from 18 to 75 years. (2) patients who underwent radial artery cannulate for continuous MAP monitoring upon arrival in the operating room, with measurements recorded continuously in the EMR system.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients in whom arterial line insertion was unsuccessful in the operating room, or invasive arterial blood pressure (ABP) recordings could not be documented in the EMR; (2) patients who were unable to obtain stable MEP baseline waveforms in the upper limbs; (3) patients who suffered from the stroke with residual neurological disorders; (4) patients with either preexisting hypertension diagnoses or newly identified hypertension during the hospitalization; and (5) patients with either current cervical spondylosis or a history of surgical or nonsurgical treatment for cervical spine disorders.

Anesthesia management

Anesthesia was induced and maintained according to the protocol described in our previous study [13]. Anesthesia was induced by 0.02 mg kg-1 midazolam, 0.4 mg kg-1 sufentanil, 2.0 mg kg-1 propofol, and 0.50 mg kg-1 rocuronium. Propofol-remifentanil-based total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) exerts less influence on synaptic transmission because it has a minimal effect on the MEP amplitude [5]. Therefore, anesthesia was maintained by 4.0 mg kg-1 h-1 propofol and 0.25 μg kg-1 min-1 remifentanil. To ensure that the muscle relaxants did not interfere with the IONM signals, we assessed the real-time train-of-four ratio before eliciting the MEP signals.

MEP signals acquisition and monitoring strategies

MEP waveforms were acquired as described [3,12,19]. MEP signals were obtained by an independent team of neurophysiologists from the deltoid, brachioradialis, and abductor pollicis brevis (APB) muscle for the upper extremities and the the flexor hallucis brevis and abductor hallucis (AH) for the lower limbs [20,21] by the needle electrodes. We chose the APB muscle to minimize the possible inhibitory effects from the lesions in the thoracic spine and/or surgical maneuver on the MEP amplitude in the lower limbs [22]. MEP change is defined as any change in the MEP signals recorded from the distal muscles in either upper extremity limb.

The parameters used for obtaining MEP signals were as follows: constant voltage (220 V−360 V); multiple trains (5−8 pulses); and a duration of 300 ms. Band-pass filter was set from 10 Hz to 1500 Hz. The time base was 100 ms.

MEP signals were measured at eight time points: T1, when surgical field exposure was completed, which we defined as the “baseline value” [12,19]; T2, when ready for decompression; T3, immediately after decompression; T4, 10 minutes post decompression; T5, 20 minutes post decompression; T6, 30 minutes post decompression; T7, 40 minutes post decompression; T8, 50 minutes post decompression; T9, 60 minutes post decompression; T10, at the start of surgical muscle closure. Furthermore, MEP signals were obtained before and after critical surgical procedures or according to our specific needs [12,19]. In our previous studies, MEP signals were obtained before and after critical surgical procedures or according to our specific needs as followings [4,6]: (1) Intraoperative signals were documented even when MEP remained unchanged, particularly when the surgical team suspected potential iatrogenic spinal cord injury. (2) Additional MEP recordings were obtained during episodes of substantial blood loss or abrupt hypotension (MAP≤65 mmHg).

This protocol ensured a continuous and comprehensive assessment of neural integrity throughout all critical phases of surgery. Necessary measures were taken to keep the recorded limb muscles warm.

MEP amplitude reduction ratios (ARR) compared to the corresponding baseline value were calculated [12,19]. MEP change is defined as a >50% drop in amplitude from the baseline value in the distal muscles of either upper limb of the patients [23,24].

MAP and artifact removing methods

Upon arrival in the operating room, invasive ABP was continuously monitored via arterial catheterization connected to pressure transducers, with automated recordings at 3-minute intervals in our EMR system. Artifacts were removed as followings: (1) MAP≤20 mmHg or MAP≥250 mmHg; (2) systolic blood pressure (SBP)≤diastolic blood pressure (DBP)+5 mmHg; (3) abrupt changes in SBP≥80 mmHg in 1 minute, abrupt changes in SBP defined by ≥40 mmHg within 2 minutes [5].

Definition of arterial hypotension

Arterial hypotension was defined as a MAP≤65 mmHg [1,2]. Upon reaching this threshold, a bolus dose of ephedrine (ranging from 3 mg to 6 mg) was administered immediately to raise blood pressure. Simultaneously, this information was documented in our EMR.

Based on the different surgical phases, MAP values were divided into two subgroups: the early stage (during the predecompression surgical phases), and the later stage (during the postdecompression surgical phases).

Surgical parameters

Surgical data including age, body mass index (BMI), operative time, EBL, preoperative motor status in the lower extremities, OPLL and/or OLF pathologies, and ephedrine consumption were also identified and noted.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS 28.0 software (SPSS 28.0) and GraphPad Prism v10.0 (GraphPad Software). All measurement data and enumeration data are reported as mean±standard deviation (X±SD). When the data were normally distributed, independent two-sample T-tests were used. When the data were skewed, the Mann−Whitney U test was used. Chi-square testing or Fisher’s exact test was also used.

To determine the optimal MAP thresholds, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used. Correlation between surgical variables and MEP ARR was determined by the Pearson correlation coefficient.

We performed univariate logistic regression to explore the relationship between different surgical variables and MEP change. If variables had a p-value of <.2 in the univariate regression analysis, it was analyzed in multiple logistic regression [19,25].

Factors associated with MEP signals change

Univariate analysis revealed that at the early surgical stage, factors associated with a higher probability of MEP change included age (p<.01), operative time (p<.01), EBL (p<.01), preinduction MAP (p<.01), and ephedrine consumption (p<.01). At the later surgical stage, the factors associated with a higher likelihood of MEP change included age (p<.01), preoperative motor status in the lower extremities (p<.05), EBL (p<.01), patients with combined OPLL and OLF, and ephedrine consumption (p<.01) (Table 2).

Multiple logistic regression analysis revealed that at the early surgical stage, ephedrine bolus administration was a risk factor for MEP change (OR=1.13, 95% CI=[1.06, 1.21], p<.01). Discussion

IOH is a contributing factor that triggers changes in MEP amplitude during spinal surgery [18,26]. It is clinically crucial to identify optimal IOH thresholds that minimize intraoperative blood loss while avoiding excessively low MAP levels that could inhibit MEP signals. This retrospective study was conducted to explore the MAP thresholds necessary for maintaining reliable MEP signal quality during surgical treatment for patients with OPLL and/or OLF. Our findings demonstrate that MAP thresholds vary depending on different surgical phases. Compared to the early surgical stage, patients exhibit less tolerance to IOH at the later surgical stage.

Surgical decompression is the standard treatment for patients with thoracic spinal myelopathy. It was demonstrated that myelopathy patients would experience a greater degree of “anesthetic fade” [27]. The progressive decrease in MEP amplitude during spinal surgery was called “anesthetic fade” [1,2]. The attenuated waveforms could be interpreted erroneously as spinal neurological injury, which can weaken the diagnostic value and prognostic value of MEP [3,4]. In clinical trials, it has been demonstrated that the following factors are independent high-risk factors for false positive MEP changes [4]: long duration of operation, bleeding volume exceeding 500 mL, total propofol dosage exceeding 1,550 mg, or continuous propofol infusion lasting 4 hours.

However, an optimal intraoperative blood pressure during the spinal surgery is also essential, and an adequate perfusion in the spinal cord demonstrates greater resilience to intraoperative neurological injuries [5]. Thus, maintaining adequate MAP and perfusion serves as a prophylactic neuroprotective strategy. Our future research will be dedicated to investigating this issue.

Although MEP signals deterioration occur most frequently during posterior decompression, the majority of changed MEP waveforms demonstrate recovery following intraoperative interventions [10]. Furthermore, a previous study identified that 45% of intraoperative MEP changes were unrelated to surgical procedures [28]. Funaba defined these signals as nonattributed neurophysiological changes to distinguish them from intraoperative neurological injury related to MEP changes (ie true positive). Nonattributed changes may be more likely to be false positives.

So, a comparative analysis was conducted to evaluate IOH thresholds for predicting MEP change during the predecompression versus postdecompression surgical phases.

Ephedrine administration was a surrogate parameter for IOH

The causes of IOH in OPLL and/or OLF surgery are multifactorial. The most common contributors include significant surgical blood loss, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) status, extensive bone manipulation, and decompression and/or fusion procedures [11]. Although there are no widely recognized thresholds for IOH, an absolute MAP value of ≤65 mmHg has been accepted as a criterion in noncardiac general surgery [1,2].

In our study, the administration of ephedrine boluses was associated with MEP change during both the early and the later surgical phases. We considered intraoperative ephedrine consumption to be a surrogate marker for anaesthesia-related hypotension, as a bolus dose of ephedrine was administered immediately when MAP fell to ≤65 mmHg [1,2] to raise blood pressure.

The consistent identification of ephedrine administration as a high-risk factor during both early and later stages of surgery highlights the significant influence of IOH on MEP signals in the surgical treatment of OPLL and/or OLF.

Reasons for difference in MAP thresholds based on different surgical phase

Patients with OPLL and/or OLF typically experience prolonged operation time and massive EBL [11,12]. A notable challenge in spinal surgery is the progressive suppression of MEP signals or even false-positive changes—a phenomenon termed “anesthetic fade” [[7], [8], [9]]. Additionally, clinical trials have identified several independent high-risk factors for false-positive MEP changes (37,38,41): prolonged operation duration, massive EBL (exceeding 500 mL), total propofol dosage exceeding 1,550 mg, or continuous propofol infusion for 4 hours [29,30]. As the surgical procedure advances, MEP waveforms become increasingly vulnerable to change, particularly at the later stages that involve extensive bone manipulation, decompression and/or fusion. Consequently, patients demonstrated reduced tolerance to IOH thresholds in later surgical phase.

Why only absolute MAP values were used

Previous studies use both the absolute and the relative MAP thresholds [2]. However, we exclusively adopted absolute MAP thresholds. This choice was influenced by two primary factors. First, by eliminating the dependency on preoperative baseline MAP values, absolute MAP thresholds are more easily integrated into our decision support systems and more effectively applied in educational contexts in our hospital affiliated to our university. Second, previous study defined the baseline MAP value as the average MAP value over the six months before the surgery, excluding measurements taken during hospitalizations [2]. Given the lack of such stringent and standardized MAP data in our study, we chose to focus solely on absolute MAP values.

The role of IOH thresholds

The MAP cutoff value should not be interpreted as a strict threshold below which MEP change in the upper extremities will occur. In contrast, this value only serves as a hemodynamic marker to guide intraoperative MAP management strategies during OPLL and/or OLF surgery. Patients whose MAP values fall below the threshold presented a higher likelihood of experiencing a decline in MEP amplitude. However, this does not imply a direct causal relationship. Although definitive MAP cutoff values may not be universally applicable to all types of spinal surgeries, our aim is to cast light on determining MAP thresholds that could assist the anesthesiologists in managing MAP during OPLL and/or OLF surgery with MEP monitoring.

Limitation

Firstly, neither MAP nor MEP signals were continuously recorded. MEP signals were obtained before and after critical procedures or according to specific needs, ensuring effective monitoring during the critical surgical stages. The MAP recordings strategies employed in our study facilitated a relatively precise evaluation of the hemodynamic-neurophysiological interplay. However, we acknowledge that the 3-minute recording intervals may fail to capture transient yet clinically significant hypotension episodes, potentially compromising the accuracy of the optimal blood-pressure thresholds. Secondly, the duration of IOH was not included as a variable to assess its influence on MEP signals. Future prospective studies will aim to explore this relationship. Thirdly, the retrospective design inherently carries a risk of information bias, particularly regarding the completeness and accuracy of EMR data for continuous physiological parameters such as MAP and detailed surgical events. Undetected data gaps or inaccuracies could influence our results. Due to the nature of our retrospective study and observational design, the sample size is relatively small. Multi-center prospective studies with larger sample sizes should be conducted to externally validate our findings. Fourthly, a potential limitation is the exclusion of hypertensive patients, which may limit the generalizability of the results to OPLL and/or OLF patients. Our future study will focus on determining the IOH thresholds for preoperative hypertensive patients to broaden the applicability of our findings, in order to optimize hemodynamic management during critical surgical phases in surgical treatment for OPLL/OLF patients. Ephedrine administration is a therapeutic response to—rather than a direct measure of—IOH (defined as MAP ≤65 mmHg). While it reflects an episode of IOH that required treatment, we don't quantify the duration or depth of the hypotensive event prior to intervention. This limitation could potentially bias the association between the severity of hypotension and changes in MEP signal.

Conclusions

We determined the MAP cutoff values for predicting changes in MEP during surgical treatment for OPLL and/or OLF surgery based on different surgical phases. Compared to the early stage, patients exhibited decreased tolerance to IOH for maintaining MEP stability in the later surgical stage. Furthermore, a stronger correlation was observed between the MAP variability ratio and MEP ARR at the later stage.
