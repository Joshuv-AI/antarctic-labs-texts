Monolayer Penta-BeAs2: A Promising 2D Materials for Toxic Gas Sensor with High Selectivity
Authors: Suchanuch Sringamprom, Thanasee Thanasarnsurapong, Teeraphat Watcharatharapong, Wisit Hirunpinyopas, Weekit Sirisaksoontorn, Jariyanee Prasongkit, Adisak Boonchun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe discovery of novel materials with high gas sensing selectivity is a key driver of gas sensor technology. Based on the recently reported two-dimensional (2D) pentagonal BeP2, we introduce penta-BeAs2 as a new member of the pentagonal family of materials for toxic gas sensors based on density functional theory (DFT). For electronic applications, a band structure calculation showed that penta-BeAs2 has indirect band gaps of 0.38 and 0.79 eV, using GGA–PBE or HSE functionals. Stability analysis confirmed that penta-BeAs2 is dynamically, mechanically, and thermally stable. The adsorption of toxic gases (CO, NO, and NO2) and nontoxic gases (H2, N2, H2O, and CO2) on the penta-BeAs2 monolayer was studied. The contrasting adsorption behavior observed between toxic and nontoxic gases on penta-BeAs2 (physisorption vs chemisorption) underscores its high selectivity for toxic gas-sensing applications. Adsorption energies for toxic gases fall within a moderate range (0.4–0.8 eV), indicating good reversibility and short recovery times at room temperature. Additionally, the quantum transport properties of penta-BeAs2 were studied using the nonequilibrium Green’s function (NEGF) approach, confirming strong sensitivity and selectivity toward toxic gases.Keywords: penta-BeAs2, two-dimensional, toxic gas sensor, selectivity, first-principles calculations

Introduction

Increasing industrial activity has significantly increased the emission of toxic gases, posing a major threat to living organisms. These gases originate from natural sources and human activities. Gas sensors are crucial tools for detecting these harmful gases. In 2004, graphene, a two-dimensional material with unique properties, including a high surface-to-volume ratio, was discovered.1 The high surface-to-volume ratio provides a vast area for gas molecules to interact with the sensor material, significantly increasing the adsorption sites and potentially enhancing the sensitivity and selectivity of gas sensors based on graphene. This discovery sparked significant interest in exploring other 2D materials for gas sensing applications.2,3 Subsequently, numerous materials, such as phosphorene,4 molybdenum disulfide (MoS2),5 and tungsten disulfide (WS2)6 have been investigated as potential as gas sensors due to their promising properties and performance.

Building on the success of the honeycomb structure of graphene, Zhang et al. have proposed a novel theoretical material with a pentagonal structure, the so-called penta-graphene.7 This led to the exploration of new materials in this family.8 These pentagonal materials have a special wrinkled structure and a large surface area, which might allow their electrical properties to be controlled, making them potentially useful for high-performance gas sensors.

In a recent study by Wei et al.,9 the gas-sensing properties of the monolayer penta-BCN have been examined. It exhibited adsorption capabilities toward various gases, including CO, H2S, NH3, NO, and NO2. Notably, NO2 displayed the strongest adsorption energy, leading to a significant bandgap change that exceeded 80%.

In 2022, Jiang et al.10 investigated penta-BeP2 and discovered its remarkable ability to chemisorb toxic gases (CO, NH3, NO, and NO2) with high adsorption energy. This strong interaction suggests high sensitivity to these harmful molecules. Conversely, penta-BeP2 exhibited minimal interaction with common ambient gases (H2, N2, H2O, O2 and CO2). However, the calculated recovery time for toxic NO gas at room temperature was very high, 662.39 s. This recovery time showed that efficient desorption of toxic gases (CO, NO, and NO2) required heating to 400 K.

This study explored the potential of penta-BeAs2 for gas sensor applications. First, we showed that penta-BeAs2 are dynamically, mechanically, and thermally stable. Furthermore, the adsorption energy of toxic gases (CO, NO, NO2) is significantly higher compared to that of nontoxic gases (H2, N2, H2O and CO2). Charge density calculations supported this finding, indicating a stronger interaction between penta-BeAs2 and toxic molecules. Additionally, recovery time calculations based on adsorption energies demonstrated that penta-BeAs2 rapidly desorbed gas molecules at room temperature, 300 K. We analyze the quantum transport properties of penta-BeAs2 to evaluate its sensitivity and selectivity, demonstrating its ability to detect toxic gases. These combined results—high sensitivity toward toxic gases, excellent selectivity, and rapid recovery—strongly suggest that penta-BeAs2 is a prime candidate for use in advanced gas sensors capable of precisely detecting and monitoring harmful gases.

Computational Method

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed with Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE)11 exchange–correlation functional, implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP),12,13 A kinetic energy cutoff of 500 eV was used for plane-wave expansion. To model layered materials accurately, the DFT-D3 functional developed by Grimme14 was used to account for long-range van der Waals (vdW) interactions. During geometry relaxation, the process continued until the cumulative sum of the Hellmann–Feynman forces reached 0.02 eV/Å, while maintaining energy convergence at a threshold of 10–6 eV. A k-point grid of 7 × 7 × 1 was used to integrate over the first Brillouin zone within the Γ-centered Monkhorst–Pack scheme.15 A vacuum size of 20 Å was added perpendicular to the crystal layer to avoid interactions with neighboring images. In dynamical stability analysis, the phonon calculations, implemented in the PHONOPY package,16 were performed using the finite-displacement method within a 5 × 5 × 1 supercell with a single Γ point.

The quantum transport was investigated using the nonequilibrium Green’s function (NEGF) method,17 as implemented in the QuantumATK package.18 The system is divided into three sections: a scattering region positioned between semi-infinite left (L) and right (R) electrodes. The calculations employed the PBE exchange–correlation functional.11 Valence electrons are described using a local basis set with a double-ζ plus polarized (DZP) configuration for all elements. To account for long-range interactions, van der Waals (vdW) forces were included via the Grimme DFT-D3 method,19 incorporating semiempirical corrections. The real-space grid employed a mesh cutoff of 200 Ry, and the Brillouin zone integration was performed using the Monkhorst–Pack scheme with a 3 × 1 × 1 k-point mesh.

Results and Discussion

The penta-BeAs2 structure was constructed by mimicking the atomic arrangement of penta-BeP2, substituting the P atoms with the As atoms, as shown in Figure 1a. The lattice constants of penta-BeAs2 are a = b = 5.50 Å, with a buckling height (h) of 1.74 Å. Meanwhile, the lattice constants of penta-BeP2 are a = b = 5.24 Å, with a buckling height of 1.51 Å.20 Comparing with penta-BeP2, the lattice constant is larger and the bucking height is higher.

Furthermore, ni refers to the number of atoms (Be and As) within the unit cell. The calculated cohesive energy per atom of the penta-BeAs2 is −6.45 eV/atom. The negative value of the cohesive energy indicates that the pentagonal structure of BeAs2 is more energetically favorable than that of the separated gaseous atoms. Earlier research has stated that the cohesive energy of pentagonal graphene is −6.84 eV/atom. For penta-BeP2, the cohesive energy is −3.40 eV/atom.20 However, in our test calculation, the cohesive energy of penta-BeP2 was −4.63 eV/atom. Therefore, the cohesive energy per atom of penta-BeAs2 is smaller than that of penta-BeP2. This result suggests that penta-BeAs2 has a stronger attraction between atoms and will be more stable in solid form compared to penta-BeP2. Additionally, for ternary pentagonal structures, such as penta-BXN (where X = C, Si, Ge, and Sn), the previously calculated cohesive energies of penta-BCN, BSiN, BGaN, and BSnN are −7.69, −6.83, −6.24, and −5.84 eV/atom, respectively.21

Dynamic stability was verified through phonon dispersion analysis. A dynamically stable crystal structure exhibits only positive phonon frequencies throughout the Brillouin zone. Conversely, imaginary frequencies (negative values) in the phonon spectra indicate that the structure experiences nonrestorative forces upon atomic displacement, signifying instability. The calculated phonon dispersion of the penta-BeAs2 is presented in Figure 2. There were no imaginary frequencies across the Brillouin zone, confirming its dynamic stability. However, it is important to acknowledge the presence of insignificant negative frequencies near the Γ point, visible only upon close inspection. These are likely artifacts and can be eliminated using functionals such as LDA.

The thermal stability of penta-BeAs2 under high temperatures was investigated using ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations. Supercells were created by duplicating the unit cell along the [100] and [010] directions The AIMD simulations were conducted from 600 to 700 K for 5000 fs, using a time step of 1 fs. The swing of potential energy at 600 K is shown in Figure 4a. The AIMD simulation revealed a significant collapse in the structural geometry at 700 K, as shown in Figure 4b. These results indicate that penta-BeAs2 remains thermally stable up to at least 600 K, as evidenced by the minimal fluctuations in the potential energy and the stable crystal geometry.

To identify the best stable adsorption arrangements for different gas molecules on the penta-BeAs2 monolayer, several representative high-symmetry adsorption sites on the surface were considered, as shown in Figure 1. These sites include top positions (T1–T3), bridge positions (B1–B2), and the hollow site (H) located above the center of a five-membered ring. For each adsorption site, three distinct orientations for each gas molecule were explored: parallel and perpendicular to the surface (as an example, with the O atom either above or below the C atom for CO). The calculated adsorption energies Ead for different gas molecules on the penta-BeAs2 monolayer, as presented in Table 2, revealed a clear distinction between toxic and nontoxic gas molecules. The adsorption energies of nontoxic gases H2, N2, H2O, and CO2 are −0.082, −0.122, −0.229, and −0.176 eV, respectively. These values indicate weak physical adsorption, as shown by the relatively large adsorption distances ranging from 3.08 to 3.83 Å. This implies weak van der Waals interactions between the penta-BeAs2 surface and nontoxic gas molecules. In contrast, the adsorption energies of toxic gas molecules, ranging from −0.359 to −0.814 eV, were significantly stronger for CO, NO, NO2. These stronger interactions were reflected in the shorter adsorption distances of 2.03 to 2.84 Å, suggesting the formation of chemical bonds between the penta-BeAs2 surface and the toxic-gas. Oxygen, a common gas in the environment, can stabilize in three distinct ways on the penta-BeAs2 surface, according to our calculations. First, oxygen can weakly physisorb with an energy of −0.112 eV at a distance of 3.31 Å, as shown in Figure 5h, which is close to earlier findings (−0.127 eV) for penta-BeP2. Second, it can chemisorb with an energy of −1.162 eV and a distance of 2.23 Å. Finally, oxygen can react with the surface, dissociating and forming new material, with a significantly higher adsorption energy of −6.623 eV, as shown in Supporting Information Figure S1.

In many sensor materials, O2 can easily interact with the surface, which can reduce the selectivity for target gases.22−26 Due to oxygen’s high reactivity and its ability to irreversibly bind to penta-BeAs2, it may hinder the sensor’s ability to detect other gases. This suggests that penta-BeAs2 would need an extremely low oxygen or oxygen-free environment to function effectively as a gas sensor. Therefore, we will not include oxygen in our future studies.

The Bader charge analysis (Q) is shown in Table 2, the negative Bader charge (Q) indicates a transfer from the penta-BeAs2 surface to the absorbed gas molecule. As expected, the magnitude of the charge transfer, reflected by |Q|, is greater for toxic gases than nontoxic gases. This observation is consistent with the stronger adsorption energies observed for toxic gases, suggesting a more significant chemical interaction between penta-BeAs2 surface and the toxic gas molecule.

Before the interactions between penta-BeAs2 surface and absorbed gas molecules were considered further we first examined the electronic band structure alongside the partial density of states (PDOS) of pure penta-BeAs2, as illustrated in Figure 6. The band structure computed with the PBE and HSE06 functionals, are shown as solid red and black lines, respectively. With the PBE functional,11 the calculated band structure shows an indirect band gap of 0.38 eV. Because conventional DFT methods commonly underestimate the band gap, the Heyd–Scuseria–Ernzerhof (HSE06) hybrid functional27,28 was employed to overcome this issue.27,28 This HSE06 approach yielded a wider indirect band gap, 0.79 eV. As shown in Figure 6 the valence band maximum (VBM) is located at the Γ point for the band structure calculation, whereas the conduction band minimum (CBM) is located along the Γ – X path. The PDOS obtained from the HSE06 functional is plotted alongside the band structure in Figure 6. The VBM and CBM were mainly dominated by the Be-p and As-p states, similar to the behavior observed in penta-BeP2 where the VBM and CBM are mainly dominated by the Be-p and the P–p state.

Next, the interactions between the penta-BeAs2 surface and absorbed gas molecules were studied. The total density of states (TDOS) of the systems with absorbed molecules and the projected density of states (PDOS) of molecules of particular interest were calculated and are presented in Figures 7 and S3. The NO molecule represents toxic gases, while the N2 molecule represents nontoxic gases. The PDOS of NO showed a prominent peak near the Fermi level (Ef), indicating strong bonding interactions with the top layer of penta-BeAs2. Conversely, the peak for the nontoxic N2 molecule is located far from Ef (around 3 eV), suggesting a weak interaction. The TDOS and PDOS of the remaining toxic and nontoxic gases are shown in Figure S3. To confirm more detail, the electron localization function (ELF) for all gases is presented alongside the corresponding PDOS plot. The ELF measures electron pairing and indicates bonding. Higher ELF values were observed between the NO molecule and the surface, indicating strong bonding interactions between the NO molecule and the top surface of the penta-BeAs2. Conversely, the low ELF values observed between the N2 molecule and the surface indicated nonbonding interactions. Similar trends were observed for the other gas molecules (see Figure S3).

The calculated recovery times τ of the penta-BeAs2 monolayer upon adsorption of CO, NO, and NO2 molecules are shown in Figure 8 for 300, 400, and 500 K. At room temperature (300 K), the recovery times τ for penta-BeAs2 with CO, NO, and NO2 toxic gases were 0.107 μs, 0.167 ms and 4.64 s, respectively. Notably, NO2 adsorption has the longest recovery time. As expected, the recovery time generally decreases with increasing temperature. At 400 K, the calculated recovery times were within 2 ms. Furthermore, the recovery time for NO2 on penta-BeAs2 (4.64 s) was significantly lower than penta-BeP2 (111.82 s).10

Previously, penta-BeP2 has been reported to have the longest recovery time for molecule adsorption of 662.39 s for NO at 300 K.10 Here, NO adsorption on penta-BeAs2 exhibited a much faster recovery time of only 0.167 ms at the same temperature. This significant improvement demonstrated the superior performance of penta-BeAs2 for detecting NO. Overall, faster recovery times were observed for all toxic gas molecules (CO, NO, and NO2) on penta-BeAs2 compared to penta-BeP2, further highlighting its promise as a reversible sensor in environmental toxin detection.

Figure 9b shows the zero-bias transmission spectra T(E, Vb = 0) for pristine penta-BeAs2 and the device with various adsorbed gas molecules; i.e. NO2, NO, CO, and CO2. These gases were identified for their promising adsorption interactions, as described above [see Table 2].

The zero-bias sensitivity as a function of energy is illustrated in Figure 9c, which effectively simulates tuning the Fermi energy via a gate voltage. A very high sensitivity (>80%) is observed near the conduction and valence band edges, indicating that the device is highly responsive to gas adsorption in these regions. However, the sensitivity values in these regions are quite similar, making it challenging to distinguish between different gases. For gate voltages in the range of 1–1.5 eV, the adsorption of various gas molecules leads to the suppression of conduction channels in distinct ways, contributing to sensitivity variations. At a gate voltage of 1.3 eV, it is possible to differentiate between the four gases.

The I–V characteristics, as shown in Figure 9d, reveal distinct profiles for different gas molecules, enabling clear differentiation between the adsorbed target gases. Notably, NOx and COx molecules can be distinguished based on their I–V curves. For NO and NO2, the current is higher than that of the pristine device, indicating a significant increase in conductance due to gas adsorption. In contrast, the currents for CO and CO2 are lower than those of the pristine device. The I–V curves for CO and CO2 exhibit an exponential increase with applied bias voltage, while the I–V curve for NOx shows a distinct plateau around Vbias ≈ 0.7–0.8 V, likely indicating current saturation due to gas adsorption effects. For NOx, as the bias increases, new states may be induced within the bias window, activating additional conduction channels.

The bias-dependent sensitivity, as shown in Figure 9e, is calculated using eq 8, where the current at each bias voltage replaces the conductance. At Vbias = 0.6 V, the sensitivity for NO exceeds 800%, while for NO2, it reaches around 280% at Vbias = 0.7 V. This indicates that the device is highly sensitive to NO and NO2 at lower bias voltages, making it ideal for detecting these gases in this bias voltage range. For CO and CO2, the sensitivity can be further enhanced by adjusting the gate voltage, allowing for better control over detection depending on the bias conditions. This tunability suggests that the penta-BeAs2 device offers not only high sensitivity but also good selectivity for different gas molecules, making it a promising candidate for gas sensor application.

Conclusions

Using DFT calculations, penta-BeAs2 is introduced as a novel 2D pentagonal structure for gas sensors. This new material, penta-BeAs2, was dynamically, mechanically, and thermally stable through comprehensive verification methods, including phonon dispersion analysis, elastic constant calculations, and molecular dynamic simulations. Due to its characteristics, the penta-BeAs2 is classified as a semiconductor. It possesses an indirect band gap of 0.38 eV within GGA-PBE or of 0.79 eV within HSE06.

In terms of molecular gas adsorption, penta-BeAs2 demonstrates strong interactions with toxic gas molecules, such as CO, NO, and NO2, while showing negligible interaction with nontoxic gas molecules, such as H2, N2, H2O and CO2. These findings suggest high selectivity for detecting toxic gases. The moderate range of the adsorption energy between −0.359 and −0.814 eV results in a short recovery time for toxic gas desorption on penta-BeAs2, which can be further reduced by heating to 400 K, making it an ideal reversible sensor. Moreover, the I–V characteristics of the penta-BeAs2 device confirm its ability to detect toxic gases with exceptional sensitivity and selectivity. The device shows strong sensitivity to NO and NO2, making it well-suited for detecting these gases. For CO and CO2, tuning the gate voltage can significantly improve sensitivity, enabling better flexibility and fine-tuning of the sensing performance.
