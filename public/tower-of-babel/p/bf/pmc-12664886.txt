MetaLigand provides a prior-knowledge-guided framework for predicting non-peptide ligand mediated cell-cell communication
Authors: Ying Xin, Yang Jin, Cheng Qian, Seth Blackshaw, Jiang Qian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryNon-peptide ligands (NPLs), including lipids, amino acids, carbohydrates, and non-peptide neurotransmitters and hormones, play a critical role in ligand-receptor-mediated cell-cell communication, driving diverse physiological and pathological processes. To facilitate the study of NPL-dependent intercellular interactions, we introduce MetaLigand, a tool designed to infer NPL availability and NPL-receptor interactions using transcriptomic data. MetaLigand compiles data for 233 NPLs, including their biosynthetic enzymes, transporter genes, and receptor genes, through a combination of automated pipelines and manual curation from comprehensive databases. The tool integrates both de novo and salvage synthesis pathways, incorporating multiple biosynthetic steps and transport mechanisms. Comparisons with existing tools demonstrate MetaLigand’s ability to account for complex biogenesis pathways and model NPL availability across diverse tissues and cell types. Furthermore, analysis of single-nucleus RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) datasets from age-related macular degeneration samples revealed that distinct retinal cell types exhibit unique NPL profiles and participate in specific NPL-mediated pathological cell-cell interactions.Keywords: cell-cell communication, ligand-receptor interaction, non-peptide ligand, metabolite, single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics

Introduction

Cell-cell communication (CCC) is fundamental to the organization and functioning of multicellular organisms, enabling cells to coordinate activities for biological processes and homeostasis.1 A primary mechanism of CCC is ligand-receptor interaction (LRI), where a ligand binds its specific receptor to trigger intracellular signaling cascades, modulate cellular actions, and regulate gene expression. The complexity of such interactions underpins the intricate functional machinery of metazoans, demanding systematic exploration for deeper understanding.

Advances in computational pipelines and databases have enhanced the modeling of CCC, enabling the profiling of its quantitative, dynamic, and heterogeneous aspects in tissues. Single-cell sequencing technologies now allow unbiased, simultaneous sequencing of cells within tissues, offering a comprehensive view of transcriptomic landscapes.2 Single-cell transcriptomic analyses have proven particularly useful for investigating LRIs, using gene expression levels of ligands and receptors (LRs) as proxies for their availability.3,4 Tools such as NicheNet,5 CellPhoneDB,6 CellChat,7 and LRLoop8,9 have greatly advanced our understanding of LRI roles in cellular physiology, development, and disease progression.

However, these methods primarily focus on peptide ligands encoded by single genes, neglecting non-peptide ligands (NPLs), molecules like non-peptide hormones, neurotransmitters, amino acids, lipids, and carbohydrates. Since NPLs are not genome-encoded or transcribed into mRNA, they are difficult to analyze directly from transcriptomic data and have been largely overlooked in computational studies. The production of small-molecule ligands involves sequential enzymatic reactions transforming precursor molecules, with additional steps such as transporter-mediated shuttling or vesicular release for hydrophilic ligands.10 Modeling NPL availability based on the expression of genes involved in these processes, such as synthetic enzymes, and transporters, opens the possibility of transcriptomic-based analyses of NPL-mediated CCC.

Previous attempts to predict NPL-based LRIs include MEBOCOST11,12 and NeuronChat,13 which model the NPL availability based on curated database of relevant gene sets of enzymes and transporters. Other tools such as CellPhoneDBv5,14 MRCLinkdb,10 and MetalinksDB15 were based on the similar concept and were incorporated into pipelines such as CellChatv216 and LIANA+.17 These methods often rely on manual curation of the enzymes and reactions from databases such as the Human Metabolome Database (HMBD)18 and Metabolic Atlas,19,20 which provide gene-metabolite associations but lack detailed metabolic synthetic pathways that may involve multiple sequential reactions. Furthermore, they typically overlook alternative ligand production routes, such as salvage pathways, where pre-existing molecules serve as precursors and are imported into cells via influx transporters.21,22 Finally, existing models have not been extensively validated for NPL availability predictions across tissues and cell types.

In this study, we present MetaLigand, an R-based, customizable bioinformatics tool for profiling CCC via NPL-receptor interactions using transcriptomic data. MetaLigand compiles data for 233 NPLs, integrating gene sets for biosynthetic enzymes, transporters, and receptors. Biogenesis pathway information is extracted from genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) in the Metabolic Atlas database,19,20 followed by manual curation. MetaLigand’s predictions of NPL availability can be supported by the literature and mass spectrometry (MS) datasets. The tool enables users to infer, visualize, and analyze NPL-dependent LRIs across bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq), and spatial transcriptomic data.

Results

Data collection pipeline

MetaLigand integrates data through an automated pipeline combined with manual curation (Figure 1). The database compiles NPL-receptor interactions across human, mouse, and zebrafish species. Small molecule-protein interactions are sourced from databases such as BindingDB,23 Cellinker,24 BioGRID,25 STITCH,26 CTD,27 and CellphoneDBv5.14 Endogenous metabolite data are retrieved from the HMDB database.18 Receptor genes are obtained from OmniPath,28 CellTalkDB,29 Cellinker,24 Cell-Cell Interaction Database,30 NicheNet,5 and Network Analysis Toolkit for Multicellular Interactions (NATMI),31 with cross-referencing against GeneCards32 and HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee (HGNC)33 databases.

Curation of the MetaLigand database

MetaLigand integrates data through an automated pipeline combined with manual curation. Small-molecule metabolites, human receptor genes, and small molecule-protein interactions are collected from multiple databases. Small molecule-protein interactions are then filtered to include only those between human endogenous metabolite NPLs and their receptors, resulting in 13,441 interaction pairs involving 534 NPLs and 1,040 receptors. These 534 NPLs are further queried in the GEM of Metabolic Atlas database for relevant metabolic pathway reactions and enzymes with predefined criteria to identify those involved in metabolite synthesis, secretion, and transport. This auto-generated NPL database is then manually curated further using the literature and databases such as TCDB. MetaLigand includes 233 NPLs for humans, 232 for mice, and 205 for zebrafish, as well as 904, 900, and 989 receptors that interact with the NPLs for humans, mice, and zebrafish, respectively.

Small molecule-protein interactions are filtered to include only those between human endogenous metabolite NPLs and their corresponding receptors, resulting in 13,441 interaction pairs involving 534 NPLs and 1,040 receptors (Figure 1). These 534 NPLs are further processed through an automated pipeline to identify synthetic enzymes and membrane transporters involved in their biogenesis and bioavailability. Synonyms for each NPL are gathered from HMDB,18 and corresponding GEM names are used to query the Metabolic Atlas database19 for relevant metabolic pathway reactions and enzymes. The reactions are filtered using predefined criteria to identify those involved in metabolite synthesis, secretion, and transport. This auto-generated NPL database is curated further using literature and databases such as the Transporter Classification Database (TCDB).34

We excluded NPLs that cannot be endogenously synthesized (e.g., linolenic acid and tryptophan). However, some of these metabolites were included as precursors for downstream NPLs. For instance, MetaLigand did not include tryptophan as an NPL because its availability cannot be directly inferred from gene expression profiles. However, we included tryptophan as a precursor of kynurenine. In total, MetaLigand includes 233 NPLs for humans, 232 for mice, and 205 for zebrafish (Figure 1). It also contains 904 receptors for humans, 900 for mice, and 989 for zebrafish that interact with the NPLs (Figure 1).

Overview of MetaLigand

In the MetaLigand database, approximately 60% of NPLs are associated with synthetic enzymes, while the remaining NPLs are linked to both synthetic enzymes and membrane transporters (Figure 2A). Biochemically, most NPLs included in MetaLigand are lipids, amino acids, and nucleic acids, with smaller but functionally significant groups represented by non-peptide hormones and neurotransmitters (Figure 2B). Each NPL is associated with one or more genes encoding five key protein groups: (1) enzymes for NPL synthesis (synthetic step 1), (2) enzymes for synthesizing NPL precursors (synthetic step 2), (3) transporters for secreting NPLs toward the extracellular space and/or loading the synaptic vesicles (efflux step), (4) uptake transporters for importing NPL precursors from extracellular spaces (influx step), and (5) NPL receptors (Figure 2C).

The availability of each NPL is modeled based on the expression levels of the genes in the first four groups (Figure 2D). (1) Within each group, gene expression is summarized using “OR logic” (arithmetic mean). (2) NPL synthesis predictions consider the final two steps of the synthesis pathway (synthetic step 1 and synthetic step 2), which are linked by “AND logic” (geometric mean), ensuring that sequential catalytic reactions must both be active to produce the final NPL. (3) Salvage synthesis pathways, where cells recycle or import NPL precursors from the extracellular space (influx step) and process them into final NPL products using last-step enzymes (synthetic step 1), are also included. The de novo and salvage pathways are connected by “OR logic.” (4) The secretion of synthesized NPLs often requires efflux transporters (efflux step), which are linked to synthesis pathways using “AND logic.” For NPLs not associated with transporters in referenced databases, it is assumed that these NPLs are directly released into the extracellular space after biosynthesis.

Regarding the specificity of association between NPLs and their relevant gene sets (synthetic enzymes, transporters, etc.), an example from the mouse model shows that 618 (61.1%) of the 1,012 enzyme and transporter genes in MetaLigand are uniquely associated with the production of a single NPL (Figure 2E). In total, 125 NPLs (53.9%) are linked to fewer than five genes, while 195 NPLs (84.1%) are represented by fewer than 15 genes (Figure 2F). Similar trends are observed in human and zebrafish models (Figure S1).

An NPL atlas for mice

Using MetaLigand, we analyzed a public single-cell sequencing dataset including 975 cell types in 36 tissues.35,36,37 This analysis generated a cell-type resolution NPL atlas for the mouse. The distinct patterns of NPL availability reflect the functional heterogeneity of various tissues (Figure 3A). For example, the nervous tissues such as the brain, neocortex, and retina exhibit similar patterns of certain neurotransmitters. The adrenal gland demonstrates specialized utilization of particular steroid hormones. Additionally, metabolic organs showed a large number of NPLs. For example, using a scaled score cutoff of 2, we identified 39, 19, and 22 NPLs in the kidney, intestine, and adipose tissue, respectively. In contrast, structural tissues such as bone and muscle exhibited zero NPLs (Figure 3A).

The biosynthesis of each NPL category demonstrated unique patterns across different tissues (Figure 3B). For instance, neurotransmitters are abundant in neuronal tissues, such as the brain and retina, and a few peripheral organs such as the kidney; vitamins are most prevalent in the kidney and retina; steroid hormones accumulate primarily in the adrenal glands and testes; eicosanoids are most abundant in the nasal mucosa, where they are produced during inflammatory responses. Many of these observations are supported by previous studies.38,39,40

We then examine the dynamics of NPL availability across 10 developmental stages in 15 tissues using the Mouse Cell Atlas (MCA) v.1.0, v.2.0, and v.3.0 datasets,36,37,41 which provide single-cell resolution gene expression data across diverse cell types and organs in mice (Figure 3C). In addition to the high cell-type specificity, such as the predicted high availability of neurotransmitters like GABA and glutamate in brain-originated cell types (Figure 3D), and that leukotrienes and lipoxins are predominantly produced by immune cells including CD8+ T cells, macrophages, and microglia, many NPLs exhibited development stage-dependent patterns. For example, hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acids like 15-HETE, key pro-inflammatory mediators, showed increased availability across various cell types in aged lung tissue, consistent with previous findings.42 Similarly, cholesterol derivatives also displayed elevated availability in aging lung tissue, aligning with the upregulation of cholesterol biosynthesis observed in aged lungs and its involvement in lung fibrosis43 (Figures 3C and 3E).

Comparing MetaLigand to other methods

To evaluate the performance of MetaLigand, we compared it to four computational methods and their associated databases: MRClinkDB,10 CellphoneDBv5,14 NeuronChat,13 and MEBOCOST.12 MetaLigand includes a significantly larger number of NPLs with receptors and integrates a more biologically meaningful model overall (Figures 4A and 2D). While NeuronChat utilizes multiple synthetic enzyme genes, it is limited to only eight neurotransmitters. In contrast, MRClinkDB, CellphoneDBv5, and MEBOCOST cover a broader range of NPLs but model NPL availability based only on a single step in the synthetic pathway (Figure 4B).

(E) Comparison of neurotransmitters predicted by MetaLigand, MRClinkDB, CellphoneDBv5, NeuronChat, and MEBOCOST in different mouse brain regions with literature reports and mass spectrometry (MS)-based metabolomics data. Confirmed NPL availabilities by literature or MS data are checked.

First, we assessed MetaLigand’s capability to capture the physiological distribution and availability of neurotransmitters in nervous tissues using the mouse brain atlas44 (Figure 4C). MetaLigand, CellphoneDBv5, NeuronChat, and MEBOCOST successfully detected all eight neurotransmitters across brain regions, while MRClinkDB failed to detect acetylcholine. Notably, MetaLigand and MEBOCOST accurately identified high glutamate availability in the thalamus, a region enriched with excitatory glutamatergic neurons.45,46 Furthermore, MetaLigand, MRClinkDB, CellphoneDBv5, and MEBOCOST also successfully identify high glycine availability in the medulla oblongata, a region rich in inhibitory glycinergic neurons,47 whereas NeuronChat failed to detect glycine in this region.

Beyond neurotransmitters, we compared predictions for steroid hormones, a particularly challenging category for transcriptomics-based modeling due to their ubiquitous biosynthetic enzymes facilitating diverse NPL production. We used the MCA v.1 gene expression atlas in mouse organs for the prediction41 (Figure 4D). MetaLigand shows progesterone, testosterone, and corticosterone present in adrenal glands, ovaries, and testis, all of which are the major steroidogenic tissues.48 In contrast, CellphoneDBv5 predicts high testosterone in almost all organs, including the stomach, which is a non-steroidogenic tissue. Similarly, MRClinkDB incorrectly predicts higher testosterone in the bladder and kidney, and MEBOCOST predicts higher testosterone in the bladder. NeuronChat, being limited to neurotransmitters, was incapable of characterizing steroid hormones.

MetaLigand’s consideration of both de novo and salvage synthesis pathways further enhanced its predictive accuracy. For example, dopamine can be synthesized de novo in dopaminergic neurons or via salvage pathways in non-neuronal tissues such as the kidney and intestine.49 MetaLigand addresses this by including two synthesis steps for de novo synthesis and influx transporter genes for salvage pathway. As a result, it identified high dopamine availability in the brain, kidney, and intestine (Figure 4D). In comparison, NeuronChat, which does not account for salvage pathways, failed to profile dopamine production in the kidney and brain. Similarly, the prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) and prostaglandin D2 (PGD2) can be synthesized from the uptake of the precursor prostaglandin H2 (PGH2), both of which are highly involved in inflammation, pain sensation, and other biological processes in various tissues.50,51 MetaLigand can detect PGE2 and PGD2 in a wide spectrum of organs such as the liver, kidney, and pancreas, enabling comprehensive modeling of NPL availability and dynamics (Figure 4D).

In addition to computational predictions, metabolite availability is often quantified using MS.52 To validate MetaLigand’s NPL predictions, we applied MetaLigand, MRClinkDB, CellphoneDBv5, NeuronChat, and MEBOCOST to the adult mouse brain atlas44 and compared the predictions to MS-based metabolomics data obtained from the mouse brain.53 Focusing on neurotransmitters, MetaLigand’s prediction showed strong agreement with MS measurements and literature reports (Figure 4E). For instance, MetaLigand identified high glycine availability in the medulla and pons, consistent with MS data and previous studies showing glycinergic neurons in these regions.53 To quantitatively evaluate performance using MS results as benchmarks, we calculated the area under the curve (AUC) for sensitivity and specificity. The AUC values were 0.64, 0.53, 0.49, 0.45, and 0.54 for MetaLigand, MRCLinkDB, CellPhoneDBv5, NeuronChat, and MEBOCOST, respectively. When literature evidence was used as a benchmark, the AUC values were 0.86, 0.61, 0.84, 0.71, and 0.62, respectively.

When we examined closer on some discrepancies between our prediction and MS measurements, we observed that MS sometimes fails to distinguish the synthetic origins and active sites of the NPLs. For example, MetaLigand predicted dopamine synthesis in the midbrain, while MS data indicate high level of dopamine in the basal ganglia (Figure 4E). This discrepancy likely arises because dopaminergic neurons, located in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc) and ventral tegmental area (VTA) of the midbrain,54,55 release dopamine to target regions in the basal ganglia, where it regulates motor behavior.56 This example highlights MS’s limitations as a definitive benchmark for NPL prediction.

Identifying differential NPL-mediated LRIs in age-related macular degeneration

Finally, we used MetaLigand to study the NPL-mediated cell-cell interactions in age-related macular degeneration (AMD), which is a progressive neurodegenerative disease and a leading cause of blindness worldwide.57 To sustain constant visual signal transduction, photoreceptors require active metabolism to meet high energy demands and continuously synthesize their outer segment structures. Retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, along with glial cells such as Müller cells, play essential roles in maintaining this homeostasis by supplying nutrients and clearing waste. Consequently, a more comprehensive characterization, involving diverse metabolite types, multiple cell types, pathological temporal dynamics, and intercellular communications, is essential for uncovering finer mechanisms of AMD.58

We applied MetaLigand to a public single-nucleus RNA-seq dataset comprising cells from the retina, RPE, and choroid of seven control and six AMD donor eyes59 (Figure 5A). The analysis of NPLs across nine major retinal cell types revealed the number of NPLs available in each cell type (Figure S2A). Notably, different cell types exhibited distinct panels of NPLs, suggesting that unique sets of small molecules may serve as ligands for intercellular communication (Figure 5B).

When comparing AMD to controls, we identified differential NPL regulation between AMD and controls. RPE cells and cones exhibited a greater number of down-regulated than up-regulated NPLs in AMD. In contrast, rod photoreceptors, various interneurons, Müller glia cells, and retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) showed increased NPL availability in AMD compared to controls (Figure 5C). This observation suggests a potential compensatory homeostatic response by these cells to mitigate vision loss during AMD progression. Several of the identified differential NPLs are associated with AMD pathology. For example, ornithine was down-regulated in cones and has been implicated in RPE and photoreceptor survival in AMD.60,61 Similarly, glucosamine, which was down-regulated in Müller cells, is considered a nutrient with potential protective effects against AMD.62,63,64 In contrast, methionine, which was up-regulated in Müller cells, has been suggested as a potential risk factor for AMD development65,66,67 (Figure 5D).

We identified differential NPL-mediated LRIs between AMD and controls, among six of the major cell types that make direct contacts with one another in adult retina. Figure 6A illustrates the predicted availability of NPLs in sender cells and the gene expression levels of corresponding receptors in receiver cells. As expected, differential NPLs such as glucosamines, 4-hydroxyproline, L-methionine, and mevalonic acid are implicated in these differential LRIs. To further explore these interactions, we calculated the total number of differential NPL-mediated LRIs between retinal cell types (Figures 6B and 6C). Cone cells displayed the highest number of down-regulated LRIs among all cell types in AMD (Figure 6C) and the lowest number of up-regulated LRIs (Figure 6B). Notably, major cell types that lost connections with cone cells under AMD conditions include RPEs, bipolar cells, and Müller glia cells (Figure 6C). A comprehensive summary of cell-type-pair-specific LRIs is provided in Figure S2B. Furthermore, Figures 6D and 6E highlight significant up- and down-regulated LRIs specific to certain cell types. Up-regulated, highly cell-type-pair-specific LRIs predominantly originate from Müller cells, RPEs, and rods (Figure 6D). Conversely, down-regulated, highly specific LRIs are most frequently observed between RPEs, cone cells, horizontal cells, and Müller cells (Figure 6E).

Finally, MetaLigand supports simultaneous inference of peptide ligand- and NPL-mediated CCC through an integrative analytical framework. Using the peptide ligand- and NPL-receptor databases included in MetaLigand, we examined the intercellular interactions between RPEs and rods. Among the differential interactions identified between AMD and control samples, we further detected significantly enriched interactions between RPEs and rods (p < 0.05) (Data S1). The predicted differential and enriched interactions from RPEs to rods correspond with numerous signals associated with AMD. For instance, the trans-fatty acid-mediated interactions, such as those involving elaidic acid, have been correlated with AMD development.68,69 Increased production of hydrogen peroxide by RPEs is implicated in the progression of AMD, which is characterized by elevated oxidative stress-induced cellular damage.70,71 Additionally, it is widely recognized that the bone morphogenetic protein family is closely involved in AMD progression by compromising the blood-retina barrier integrity of the RPEs, inducing epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and cell migration and facilitating angiogenesis.72,73,74 On the other hand, among the predicted interactions from rods to the RPEs, it has been reported that retinal synthesis and retinal-mediated intercellular signals, which are essential for vision, diminish in AMD. This reduction may contribute to the advancement of AMD and visual impairment.75,76,77

To compare MetaLigand with existing methods, we applied each tool to the control samples using their default settings, focusing on interactions from RPEs to rods as an example. MetaLigand identified a substantially larger number of significantly enriched interactions from RPEs to rods (263 pairs; adjusted p value < 0.05) than the other methods: NeuronChat (0), CellphoneDBv5 (1), MRClinkDB (3), and MEBOCOST (14). The overlap among tools was minimal; only the interaction between gamma-aminobutyric acid and GABRB3 was identified by both MetaLigand and MEBOCOST, with all other predictions being unique to each tool (see Data S2). These differences likely stem from the varying sizes of the underlying databases and the scope of curated interactions, such as the inclusion of transporters and nuclear receptors. Specifically, MetaLigand’s curated NPL-receptor database comprises 4,268 interactions, while the databases underlying NeuronChat, CellphoneDBv5, MRClinkDB, and MEBOCOST contain 120, 226, 681, and 560 interactions, respectively.

Discussion

MetaLigand is a computational tool designed to infer NPL availability and NPL-receptor interactions from transcriptomic data, including bulk RNA-seq, scRNA-seq, and spatial transcriptomics. By curating an extensive list of NPLs and their receptors, MetaLigand effectively addresses challenges in modeling NPL availability using gene expression data. Its integration of synthetic enzymes and transport proteins into the predictive model makes it highly robust for analyzing complex cellular processes. Additionally, MetaLigand is compatible with popular computational tools for single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics (Figure S3). The full package, including NPL availability estimation and NPL-associated CCC models, is available as an R package at https://github.com/jinyangye119/MetaLigand.

When compared to existing methods like NeuronChat,13 MRClinkDB,10 and CellphoneDBv5,14 MetaLigand offers several key advantages. First, it incorporates both de novo and salvage synthetic pathways, providing comprehensive insights into NPL biosynthesis across cell types and tissues. Second, MetaLigand uses the geometric mean rather than the Euclidean norm to summarize genes involved in synthetic and transporter pathways, enhancing the biological relevance of its model. For ligands like neurotransmitters and steroid hormones, this approach captures the coordinated expression of synthetic enzymes and transporters, addressing disruptions that can lead to ligand accumulation or deficiency. In contrast, tools like MRClinkDB compute NPL availability by subtracting catalytic enzyme expression from synthetic enzyme expression, which can result in inaccuracies. For instance, MRClinkDB failed to detect acetylcholine in the brain due to this limitation.

NPLs can be predicted at the tissue, cell type, or single-cell level, depending on the source of gene expression measurements. Conclusions drawn from different resolutions may vary. For example, testosterone was detected at high levels in the ovary at the tissue level (Figure 4D). However, when analyzing at the cell type level using single-cell data,37 testosterone availability is highest in testis Leydig cells. Females naturally produce a certain level of testosterone in the ovary, primarily in theca cells, but also in granulosa and luteal cells (Figure S3F).

When analyses are performed at the single-cell level, the availability of NPLs in individual cells can be visualized through dimension-reduction analysis tools such as uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) (Figures S3C and S3D). However, excessive zero values could be shown in many cells due to the inherent sparsity of single-cell data. To address this issue, users can either apply MetaLigand to cluster-level pseudo-bulk expression profiles or use scRNA-seq imputation methods prior to analysis. Both strategies may help reduce potential information loss caused by dropouts.

The availability of each NPL is modeled based on the expression levels of genes in the first four functional groups. Within each group, enzymes contribute independently to synthesis or transport reactions; therefore, we apply “OR logic” using the arithmetic mean, which reflects the overall activity of each step. Similarly, at the pathway level, the de novo and salvage pathways contribute independently to NPL synthesis and are also combined using “OR logic”. In contrast, for sequential steps where all components must be sufficiently active for successful NPL synthesis and secretion, we use “AND logic” with the geometric mean to model the dependency across all coordinated steps (e.g., synthetic steps 1 and 2, synthesis step 1 and the influx step, or combinations of synthesis and efflux steps). However, other mathematical operations exist to represent “AND” and “OR” rules, such as minimum and maximum gene expression, respectively.78 MetaLigand offers these operations as optional choices to be selected based on the user’s specific criteria.

A key advantage of MetaLigand is its automated pipeline for gene identification. By automatically identifying genes involved in the synthesis and transport of NPLs from comprehensive genomic and metabolomic databases, MetaLigand can collect a more exhaustive gene list than tools relying solely on manual curation. This automated approach improves the accuracy and scope of NPL detection, as seen in MetaLigand’s accurate identification of testosterone in steroidogenic tissues. Tools like CellphoneDBv5 and MRClinkDB missed important enzymes (e.g., HSD3B and CYP17A) and inaccurately predicted testosterone in non-steroidogenic tissues, such as the stomach.

Limitations of the study

Current computational methods, including MetaLigand, can only predict the potential presence of NPLs in specific cell types or tissues. Quantifying NPLs from gene expression data is not feasible due to the lack of information on enzymatic efficiency involved in NPL synthesis. As a result, our predictions reflect the “availability” rather than the actual “abundance” of NPLs. While we cannot compare the availability across different NPLs, we can still compare the relative availability of a given NPL across different conditions (e.g., disease versus control). Another limitation of MetaLigand is that it models NPL production (e.g., production of testosterone by ovarian cells) but does not quantify downstream NPL consumption (e.g., conversion of testosterone into estrogens in the ovaries).

Method details

Data collection pipeline

A total of 3,088 unique metabolites were collected from HMDB (Version 5.0)18 using the “Browsing metabolites” page (https://hmdb.ca/metabolites) with the filtering terms “Endogenous” and “Detected and quantified”. All human receptors were first collected from 6 databases including OmniPath,28 CellTalkDB,29 Cellinker,24 Cell-Cell Interaction Database,30 NicheNet,5 and NATMI-ConnectomeDB202031 (Figure 1). After manual curation to filter out non-receptor proteins (e.g., calcium channels and metal ion transporters), 2,837 receptors were retained.

The collected human metabolites and receptors were then connected based on metabolite-protein interactions obtained from 6 databases: CTD,27 BindingDB,23 Cellinker,24 BioGRID,25 STITCH26 and CellphoneDBv514 (Figure 1). This resulted in 13,441 interaction pairs between 534 NPLs and 1,040 human receptors. Human receptor genes were then converted to mouse and zebrafish receptor genes based on orthologs using the ENSEMBL BioMart tool.79

To address the issue of varying metabolite nomenclature, NPLs were mapped to synonyms from the HMDB database to obtain HMDB accession IDs. HMDB accession IDs were used to query the genome-scale metabolic models (GEM) database19 for genes encoding synthetic and transport enzymes for each NPL.

Human, mouse, and zebrafish genes were collected from GEM-human, GEM-mouse, and GEM-zebrafish databases, respectively. Genes encoding synthetic enzymes or transporter proteins were collected from GEM model XML files using the following criteria: 1) Synthetic genes: filtered based on the reaction term “produce” and excluding the subsystem term “transport reactions”, 2) Efflux transporter genes: filtered by subsystem term “transport reactions” and compartment term “cytosol to extracellular”. The resulting gene list was then manually curated for accuracy. NPLs with shared synthetic genes (e.g., fatty acids, steroid hormones), genes for synthesizing NPL precursors (Synthetic step2) were included using the same filtering criteria. For NPLs with known salvage synthetic pathways (e.g., dopamine), genes encoding influx transporters for NPL precursors were incorporated by filtering for the subsystem term “transport reactions” and the compartment term “extracellular to cytosol”.

Generating Non-Peptide Ligand Atlas for Mouse

The processed single-cell RNA-seq datasets MCA 1.0, 2.0, 3.0 were downloaded directly from the Mouse Cell Atlas (https://bis.zju.edu.cn/MCA/). Downstream analysis was performed using Seurat v5.2.1 in combination with MetaLigand. The data were normalized with the NormalizeData function using the “LogNormalize” method and default parameters when needed. MetaLigand was then applied following the standard pipeline described in the online vignette (https://github.com/jinyangye119/MetaLigand/tree/main). For the Meta_matrix function, the species parameter was set to “mouse”; all other parameters were left as default. Average NPL availability for each cluster were computed using AverageExpression.

Integrating peptide ligands and NPLs

MetaLigand enables simultaneous inference of both peptide ligand- and NPL–mediated cell–cell communication within a unified and integrative workflow. It supports multiple scoring methods for ligand–receptor interactions, including 'cpdb' (average expression of ligand and receptor, as in CellPhoneDB6,14,80,81,82), 'natmi' (product of ligand and receptor levels, used in NATMI31), and 'scsigr' (a regularized product score introduced by SingleCellSignalR83). Users can choose the method for the calculation.

We also implemented a statistical enrichment analysis for ligand-receptor interactions in MetaLigand. This permutation-based approach generates a background distribution of interaction scores by randomly shuffling cluster labels. Each observed interaction score is then compared against this background to calculate a p-value for the predicted interaction. This allows users to evaluate whether specific ligand-receptor interactions are significant in particular cell types relative to random expectation.

Application of MetaLigand to snRNA-Seq data in AMD

To demonstrate the usage of MetaLigand, we provide a detailed protocol for analyzing ligand-receptor interactions in age-related macular degeneration (AMD) as an example. The processed single-nucleus RNA-seq dataset—including the gene count matrix, metadata, and UMAP coordinates—was downloaded directly from the Single Cell Portal (accession: SCP2012). Downstream analysis was performed using Seurat v5.2.1 in combination with MetaLigand.

A Seurat object was first created using the CreateSeuratObject function with the gene count matrix and metadata. Cells labeled as “QC_pass” = FALSE or missing values for “cell_type__ontology_label” (as specified in the metadata) were removed using the subset function. The data were normalized with the NormalizeData function using the “LogNormalize” method and default parameters. UMAP coordinates were added to the Seurat object using CreateDimReducObject.

MetaLigand was then applied to the resulting Seurat object, following the standard pipeline described in the online vignette (https://github.com/jinyangye119/MetaLigand/tree/main). For the Meta_matrix function, the species parameter was set to “human”; all other parameters were left as default. Average gene expression levels and predicted NPL availability for each cell type and disease status were computed using AverageExpression. Differential availability analysis was performed using FindMarkers with the Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test, comparing AMD versus control samples across all cell types using the following criteria: detection rate >10% in the corresponding cell type under either condition, the absolute value of the average log2 fold change >0.2, and the adjusted p-value from the Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test <0.05.

In addition to differential NPL-mediated interaction analysis among six major cell types, we ran the standard cell–cell communication pipeline to predict both peptide ligand– and NPL-mediated interactions, focusing on those between retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells and rods in AMD and control samples. The getLRIpval function was used with LRI.method = “scsigr” (SingleCellSignalR-based scoring83) and numperm = 101.

Differential interactions were defined using the following criteria: either the ligand or receptor must be available or expressed in at least 10% of cells within the corresponding cell type under either condition, show an absolute average log2 fold change greater than 0.5, and have an adjusted p-value below 0.05 (Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test). Additionally, the interacting partner was required to be expressed in at least 10% of cells in the relevant cell type, and its expression change could not be in the opposite direction of its counterpart. A summary of all predicted and differential interactions enriched between RPEs and rods (permutation-based p-values <0.05) is provided in Data S1.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Differential expression/availability analysis was performed using the function FindMarkers in the R package Seurat v5.2.1 with the Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test. Enriched interactions between each pair of cell types were identified by the permutation-based p-values (<0.05).
