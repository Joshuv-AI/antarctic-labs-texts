Computational identification of PTPRJ peptide targeting the Epstein Barr virus latent membrane protein 1
Authors: Dayang-Sharyati D A Salam, Hwang Siaw San, Xavier Chee Wezen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe treatment of Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-associated malignancies is increasingly recognizing the necessity of personalized medicine strategies to enhance therapeutic outcomes. This research addresses this need by computationally investigating potential peptide inhibitors targeting the latent membrane protein 1 (LMP1), a key oncoprotein in EBV-related cancers. The study specifically focuses on the C-terminal activating region (CTAR) of LMP1, a critical domain for its oncogenic signalling. To identify promising inhibitors, a comprehensive computational methodology was employed, encompassing Unidock virtual screening, PYRX molecular docking, AMBER molecular dynamics simulations, and CPPTRAJ analysis. This integrated approach led to the identification of a Protein Tyrosine Phosphatase Receptor Type J (PTPRJ) agonist peptide derived from the CancerPPD database as a potential inhibitor of EBV LMP1. PTPRJ is a known tumour suppressor involved in regulating cellular proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis. The findings suggest that the identified peptide agonist may exert its inhibitory effect on LMP1 by activating PTPRJ, thereby influencing cancer cell behaviour and the tumour microenvironment. This research presents a significant starting point for the development of novel therapeutic interventions tailored for EBV-related cancers. The application of computational methods underscores a strategic approach to tackle the complexities of personalized medicine in the context of these malignancies. Furthermore, the focus on the LMP1 CTAR region as a therapeutic target highlights the critical role of this viral protein in driving oncogenesis. The integration of diverse computational tools in this study signifies a rigorous and multifaceted strategy for identifying potential drug candidates.Keywords: Epstein–Barr virus, EBV latency, Latent membrane protein 1, Peptide, Nasopharyngeal carcinomaSubject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery, Oncology

Introduction

The interaction of peptide inhibitors at the C-terminal activating regions (CTAR) of the Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) latent membrane protein 1 (LMP1) is a vital area of interest due to the oncoprotein’s significant role in EBV-associated malignancies. LMP1 is a multifunctional membrane protein that activates several downstream signalling pathways related to cell survival, proliferation, and immune evasion, particularly through its CTAR domains, making it a promising target for therapeutic intervention. Thus, understanding the molecular interactions between peptide inhibitors and these CTAR regions is crucial. CTAR domains of LMP1 engage with various signalling proteins to transduce signals that promote oncogenic processes. CTAR interacts with TNF receptor-associated factors (TRAFs) and initiates signalling cascades that activate transcription factors such as NF-κB, MAPK, and AP-1, which are pivotal in cell proliferation and survival1–3. CTAR also activates similar signalling pathways but has distinct interactions, further illustrating the complexity of LMP1 signalling. Studies indicate that disrupting these interactions using specific peptide inhibitors can significantly impair LMP1’s function, corroborating the therapeutic potential of targeting CTAR regions3,4.

The ability of peptide inhibitors to interfere with LMP1-TRAF complexes, particularly the LMP1-TRAF6 interaction, emphasizes a novel approach for therapeutic intervention3,4. By disrupting these vital interactions, researchers aim to impair the survival and proliferation of EBV-infected cells. For instance, inhibitors like dehydroxymethylepoxyquinomicin (DHMEQ), which targets NF-κB pathways, have shown promise in inducing apoptosis in EBV-infected cells5. This approach exemplifies how precise molecular targeting can result in the death of cancerous cells while preserving healthy tissues. Moreover, the immunosuppressive effects of LMP1 also highlight the importance of peptide inhibitors. LMP1 not only promotes B-cell activation but also suppresses T-cell responses, allowing infected cells to evade immune surveillance6–8. Peptide inhibitors targeting the CTAR domains may enhance T-cell responses against EBV-positive tumours, thus promoting effective immune clearance of infected cells9,10. The potential for these inhibitors to modulate immune responses makes them attractive candidates for combinatorial therapies alongside existing cancer treatments.

The study of LMP1’s structural dynamics, particularly at the CTAR regions, reveals insights into how modifications influence its oncogenic properties and immune evasion capabilities. For instance, modulation of phosphorylation states and interactions at CTAR domains can be achieved using specific peptide sequences, which may alter LMP1’s signalling outcomes11–13. Understanding these interactions at the molecular level facilitates the design of peptides that could effectively disrupt malignancy-promoting signals without significant off-target effects. Furthermore, the genetic variability of LMP1 among different EBV strains poses challenges for the development of broadly effective inhibitors. Variational analyses indicate that certain LMP1 polymorphisms can alter the peptide binding capabilities, influencing inhibitor efficacy14,15. This observation requires the careful selection of target peptides and necessitates extensive characterization of various LMP1 variants in relation to their function and interaction profiles.

In clinical contexts, translating these findings into effective therapies hinges on robust preclinical models that accurately mimic human disease. For example, utilizing transgenic mice expressing human LMP1 provides a platform for assessing the efficacy of peptide inhibitors10,16. These models can elucidate the immune response dynamics and the therapeutic window of peptide inhibitors in an environment that approximates human pathology. The development of delivery systems for peptide inhibitors also remains a critical research area. Effective delivery mechanisms that can ensure the stability and bioavailability of these peptides in vivo will facilitate their practical application17–19. Current research is increasingly focused on nanoparticles and other innovative delivery systems that improve the pharmacokinetic profiles of peptide inhibitors by enabling sustained release and targeted action at specific sites. Despite these advances, existing peptide inhibitors face significant limitations, including poor stability, low bioavailability, and challenges related to specificity and off-target effects. These issues can reduce therapeutic efficacy and complicate experimental screening efforts. In contrast, computational screening methods offer distinct advantages by enabling the rapid and cost-effective identification of promising peptide candidates from large libraries. Techniques such as molecular docking and virtual screening facilitate efficient exploration of binding interactions and conformational flexibility, allowing researchers to prioritize compounds for experimental validation. Moreover, these computational approaches enhance the understanding of structure-activity relationships and support rational design strategies, ultimately accelerating the drug discovery process.

As research progresses, there is increasing recognition of the need for personalized medicine approaches in treating EBV-associated malignancies. The patient-specific profiles of LMP1 expression and activity can inform the selection of peptide inhibitors, tailoring therapeutic interventions to maximize efficacy based on individual tumour characteristics20,21. This shift towards precision therapies signifies a promising evolution in the management of EBV-related cancers. Targeting peptide inhibitors to the CTAR regions of EBV LMP1 presents a compelling strategy to combat EBV-linked malignancies. The intricate interactions of LMP1 with intracellular partners underscore the potential of tailored peptide therapies to impair oncogenic signalling pathways, promoting apoptosis and restoring T-cell functionality. Therefore, this study aims to computationally screen chemical libraries and perform molecular docking simulations to identify promising inhibitors targeting the CTAR region of LMP1, which can serve as a starting point for further experimental validation.

Methods and experiment

Screening compounds

A virtual screening campaign was conducted to identify potential LMP1 protein inhibitors using the Advanced Enamine and CancerPPD databases22,23. This involved docking 3,143 compounds from CancerPPD and 1.8 million from Advanced Enamine. A novel peptide, B1.12, developed by Ammous-Boukhris et al., was also included as a control17. The initial screening utilized UniDock, a molecular docking software, to assess the binding affinities of these compounds to specific regions of the large LMP1 protein structure (CTAR and TMD)24. During the UniDock screening, key parameters included the definition of the docking grid, scoring functions, and ligand flexibility. The docking grid was centred to encompass the binding site fully, with dimensions carefully chosen to cover all relevant regions. UniDock supports multiple scoring functions such as vina, vinardo, and ad4, and docks ligands flexibly while keeping the receptor rigid to optimize computational efficiency. Due to UniDock’s spatial limitations, the focus was on a targeted area for more efficient screening. This phase predicted binding modes and affinities based on scoring functions, with the accuracy of the binding energy assessment being crucial for selecting promising candidates. The top-scoring compounds from the UniDock screening were then subjected to a more detailed docking study using PyRx, which docked them against the entire LMP1 protein structure for a more refined evaluation of their inhibitory potential.

Molecular docking

Both ligand and protein preparations were completed before initiating the docking procedure to ensure accurate and reliable docking results. The protein-ligand docking process using PyRx involves several key steps25. First, the 3D structure of the protein is obtained and cleaned. PyRx uses AutoDockTools to prepare the protein, converting it into the necessary PDBQT format for AutoDock Vina. Next, the ligand is prepared, and optimized, especially for its geometry, before being converted to the PDBQT format as well. Open Babel is used for file format conversion and energy minimization to ensure the ligand is ready for docking. Once both the protein and ligand are prepared, a grid box is defined to cover the protein’s binding site. For docking, we defined the grid parameters with the grid centre located at coordinates (x = 3.8401, y = 7.2583, z = 15.0038). The grid box dimensions were set to encompass the binding site adequately, with sizes of 155.5704 Å along the x-axis, 72.0499 Å along the y-axis, and 91.6882 Å along the z-axis. AutoDock Vina, accessed via PyRx, is then used for docking. The software explores different ligand orientations and conformations within the grid box, calculating binding affinities for each predicted pose. After docking, PyRx presents the results, including binding poses, docking scores, and RMSD values. For docking parameter optimization, we performed redocking calculations to validate the docking protocol. This involved adjusting parameters such as exhaustiveness, grid spacing, and scoring function settings to minimize the RMSD between the docked pose and the protein structure. The redocking results demonstrated that our protocol could reproduce the binding pose within an RMSD threshold of 2 Å, consistent with established benchmarks The pose with the highest binding affinity is selected for further analysis using molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with AMBER.

Molecular dynamic simulation

The complete LMP1 sequences (386 amino acids) were obtained from UniProt, and the protein used here was based on our previous study26. After identifying promising ligands through docking studies, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using the AMBER package to explore the protein-ligand complex’s dynamic behaviour27. These simulations provide insights into the interactions and conformational changes upon ligand binding. The protein-ligand complex was modelled with a lipid membrane using the CHARMM-GUI membrane builder28. The lipid membrane environment, 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-phosphatidylcholine (POPC) was used to model the lipid bilayer membrane. POPC is a zwitterionic and neutral phospholipid widely employed in molecular dynamics simulations because it is a major component of biological membranes and forms stable bilayers. Including POPC in simulations provides a realistic membrane context that can influence protein conformation and ligand interactions, which is important for membrane-associated proteins like LMP1.The simulations used the AMBER FF14SB force field for the protein, Lipid21 for the membrane, and Gaff for the ligands. The TIP3P water model was used to solvate the system, and electrostatic interactions were calculated with a 10 Å Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) cutoff. A 2 fs timestep and SHAKE constraints were applied to maintain bond lengths. The system underwent a two-phase minimization: 20,000 steps of steepest descent and 10,000 steps of conjugate gradient. It was then heated from 0 K to 300 K over 200 ps in an NVT ensemble, equilibrated for 2 ns in an NPT ensemble, and subjected to a 250 ns production run. This lipid membrane building and MD simulation protocols followed our previously established methodology29.

Analysis

Trajectory analysis was conducted using CPPTRAJ in AMBER 20, while Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD) and PyMOL were used for visualization and evaluation of the MD simulations, both in real-time and post-processing30–32. All calculations were conducted with the initial protein-ligand complex structure serving as the reference for both RMSD and RMSF analyses. The RMSD (Root Mean Square Deviation) was calculated by least-squares fitting each frame of the simulation trajectory to the reference structure, typically the initial minimized or equilibrated conformation, using the protein backbone atoms (N, Cα, and C) for alignment. This metric quantifies the average deviation of atomic positions over time and provides insights into the structural stability of the complex throughout the simulation. The RMSF (Root Mean Square Fluctuation) was determined by measuring the fluctuation of individual atomic positions around their average coordinates across the trajectory, offering residue-level information on flexibility and mobility relative to the reference structure. Additionally, the radius of gyration (Rg) was calculated as the root mean square distance of protein atoms from the centre of mass, serving as an indicator of the overall compactness and folding state of the protein during the simulation. CPPTRAJ was also used to process hundreds of trajectory frames, which were subsequently utilized for binding energy calculations using the MM-GBSA method. This approach combines molecular mechanics with generalized Born and surface area solvation models to estimate the free energy of binding between the protein and ligand.

CancerPPD compounds (3343 and 3148) exhibit the most stable ligand binding

Figure 4b displays the Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) of the five different ligands (Ligand 1 to Ligand 5) over a 250-nanosecond simulation, where lower RMSD values indicate a more stable binding pose within the target molecule. Analysis of the ligands reveals that Ligand-3 (Green) and Ligand-5 (Purple) exhibit the most stable binding, consistently showing low RMSD values (generally below 2 Å) across all replicates. Ligand-1 (Blue) also demonstrates relatively good stability with RMSD mostly under 3 Å, although it shows some fluctuations in the final replicate. Ligand-2 (Orange) displays moderate stability with RMSD fluctuating between 2 and 4 Å. In contrast, Ligand-4 (Red) demonstrates significant instability with high and fluctuating RMSD values often exceeding 5 Å. Overall, the consistent and low RMSD profiles of Ligand-3 and Ligand-5 across the simulations identify them as the most promising candidates with the strongest and most stable interactions with the target molecule.

Enamine and both CancerPPD compounds express superior compactness

Figure 4c shows the radius of gyration (Rgyr) over time for the five ligands in three replicates. The radius of gyration, a metric for assessing molecular compactness and structural stability during molecular dynamics simulations, reveals that lower and more stable Rgyr values correlate with greater compactness and stability. Ligand-1 (Blue), maintains consistently low and stable Rgyr values, ranging approximately from 40 to 50 Å, across all three systems, suggesting robust structural stability with minimal fluctuations. In contrast, Ligand-2 (Orange) exhibits higher Rgyr values, around 50 to 60 Å, and experiences significant fluctuations, especially during the initial simulation period, indicating lower stability and increased flexibility. Similarly, Ligand-4 (Red) demonstrates stable and low Rgyr values, comparable to Ligand-2, with minimal fluctuations, indicating good compactness and stability. Ligand-3 (Green) displays moderate Rgyr values, roughly 45 to 55 Å, with some fluctuations that stabilize after an initial phase, showing better stability than Ligand-2 but less than Ligand-1. Notably, Ligand-5 (purple) shows the lowest Rgyr values, approximately 35 to 45 Å, across all systems, signifying excellent compactness and minimal fluctuations, thereby making it the most stable ligand. Ligand-2 (Orange), on the other hand, demonstrates the least stability due to its high Rgyr values and significant fluctuations. Consequently, Ligand-5 (Purple) emerges as the best-performing ligand due to its superior compactness and stability, followed by Ligands 1 and 3, which exhibit similar but slightly less compact behaviour.

Among the ligands analysed, Ligand 2 exhibited the most favourable ∆TOTAL of − 97.15 ± 1.80 kJ/mol, characterized by strong electrostatic interactions (EEL = − 3073.31 ± 45.44 kJ/mol) effectively counterbalanced by solvation effects (EGB = 3024.85 ± 45.99 kJ/mol), a typical characteristic of charged ligands. Its minimal standard error (± 1.80 kJ/mol) signifies high statistical confidence. Ligand 5 ranked second with a ∆TOTAL of − 90.37 ± 2.76 kJ/mol, although its larger standard error suggests lower precision compared to Ligand 2. Ligands 3 and 1 showed moderate binding affinities, with ∆TOTAL values of − 54.21 ± 1.68 kJ/mol and − 30.52 ± 2.10 kJ/mol, respectively, and Ligand 3 demonstrated better precision. In contrast, Ligand 4 displayed the weakest binding (∆TOTAL = − 9.12 ± 3.02 kJ/mol) and high variability among replicates, rendering its results less reliable. Given that differences exceedingly approximately 6 kJ/mol between ligands are considered statistically significant, the 6.78 kJ/mol difference between Ligand 2 and Ligand 5, coupled with non-overlapping error margins, confirms Ligand 2’s superior binding. Consequently, Ligand 2 is identified as the most promising candidate due to its strongly negative ∆TOTAL and high statistical precision, followed by Ligand 5 as a secondary option. Ligands 1, 3, and 4 are deemed less favourable. These results underscore the effectiveness of the MM/GBSA method in ranking ligands based on their binding affinity.

Discussion

This study employed a combined computational approach, including Unidock virtual screening, PYRX molecular docking, AMBER molecular dynamics simulations, and CPPTRAJ analysis, to identify a Protein Tyrosine Phosphatase Receptor Type J (PTPRJ) agonist peptide from CancerPPD as a potential inhibitor of EBV LMP1. This integrated strategy allowed for a comprehensive investigation of protein-ligand interactions, facilitating the identification of potential drug candidates. PTPRJ is a receptor-like protein tyrosine phosphatase known for its significant tumour-suppressing activities through mechanisms that regulate cellular proliferation, migration, and angiogenic processes. This phosphatase has been involved in the negative modulation of receptor tyrosine kinase signalling, which is integral to tumour development, making PTPRJ a focal point in cancer research Bilotta et al.33. The discovery of peptide agonists that specifically activate PTPRJ represents a promising therapeutic avenue, given their potential to enhance the tumour-suppressive functions of this phosphatase, thereby affecting cancer cell behaviour and the tumour microenvironment34.

The PTPRJ agonist peptides, such as PTPRJ-pep19, have been shown to bind to the extracellular portion of PTPRJ, effectively increasing its enzymatic activity. This interaction leads to the inhibition of cancer cell proliferation and the suppression of angiogenesis, which are crucial factors in tumour growth and metastasis35,36. For instance, various studies have demonstrated that these peptide agonists can inhibit in vitro tube formation in endothelial cells, showcasing their capacity to combat the vascularization processes that facilitate tumour expansion37,38. A notable aspect of PTPRJ peptides is their preferential effectiveness on cancerous endothelial cells compared to normal cells. This selective action hints at a potential therapeutic window where PTPRJ agonists could diminish tumour growth with reduced side effects on surrounding healthy tissues39. The modulation of PTPRJ activity via these agonist peptides not only restores the tumour suppressive effects of the phosphatase but also triggers apoptosis in malignant cells, rendering them susceptible to treatment37.

The significance of PTPRJ agonists transcends mere tumour suppression as they may also influence immune responses within the tumour microenvironment. By modulating the signalling pathways that PTPRJ engages in, these agonists could potentially alter the interactions between tumour cells and immune cells, thereby enhancing anti-tumour immunity34. This aspect of PTPRJ peptide agonists can be particularly relevant in the context of cancers associated with viral infections, such as those involving EBV. The interaction of EBV LMP1 with cellular signalling pathways is analogous to the signalling pathways that PTPRJ modulates. Consequently, enhancing PTPRJ activity through agonist peptides may counteract some of the pro-tumorigenic signals elicited by EBV-infected cells40.

For example, since LMP1 induces oncogenic signalling, PTPRJ agonist peptides could theoretically act synergistically to downregulate these signals, making it a potential adjunct therapy for EBV-associated cancers. Research on the intersections between PTPRJ function and the signalling pathways hijacked by EBV could reveal novel therapeutic strategies that leverage PTPRJ agonists41. Moreover, as microRNAs like miR-155 have been shown to target PTPRJ, the interplay between these small regulatory RNAs and PTPRJ agonist peptides opens additional avenues for exploration 39. Understanding how to counteract the effects of these microRNAs while enhancing PTPRJ activity through peptide agonists could yield a multifaceted approach to treating EBV-related malignancies. The challenge lies in developing PTPRJ agonists that exhibit both efficacy and specificity, especially given the complexities of the tumour microenvironment and the significant biological variability among cancer types. Continued exploration of the mechanistic pathways associated with PTPRJ and its agonists, alongside the effects of EBV and its latent proteins, could greatly contribute to the establishment of innovative therapeutic strategies42.

Apart from that, LMP1’s ability to modulate host cellular pathways makes it a key therapeutic target, especially through the targeted disruption of its distinct regions. One important aspect of LMP1’s role is its effect on gene expression via epigenetic changes, which offers another potential avenue for treatment. LMP1 has been shown to induce long-lasting epigenetic modifications, including those affecting the Polycomb Repressive Complex (PRC) and its key components like EZH220,43. Understanding how LMP1 influences these mechanisms opens up the possibility of developing small molecules to block these interactions, potentially reversing the oncogenic changes caused by the virus and restoring normal cell function. LMP1 also interacts with the NF-κB signaling pathway, which is crucial for cell survival and tumorigenesis. Studies have shown that aspirin can inhibit this pathway in EBV-infected cells, highlighting the therapeutic potential of dampening LMP1’s activity to promote apoptosis in cancer cells, such as those found in nasopharyngeal carcinoma44.

Additionally, LMP1 plays a critical role in immune evasion, influencing the host immune response by upregulating checkpoint molecules like PD-L145. Targeting these pathways could make tumors more sensitive to immune checkpoint blockade therapies, offering a novel strategy to enhance anti-tumor immunity. LMP1 also contributes to metabolic reprogramming in cancer cells by promoting glucose transport and lipogenesis, processes necessary for rapid tumor growth46,47. Inhibiting these metabolic pathways, such as using fatty acid synthase inhibitors to block LMP1-induced lipogenesis, presents a promising therapeutic approach for cancers driven by LMP1 expression, including certain hematological malignancies.

A key limitation of this study lies in the inclusion of intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) within the RMSD analysis. While this approach may introduce elevated flexibility and potentially obscure finer structural deviations, it was a necessary decision. The CTAR of LMP1, our primary target for inhibitor binding, is largely composed of these IDRs; consequently, their exclusion would have removed the main binding interface from our analysis. Therefore, RMSD was calculated for the entire protein to preserve the crucial context of ligand interactions. Future work could address this by employing alternative approaches, such as ensemble-based or disorder-aware metrics, to more accurately capture the dynamic behavior of IDR-containing binding sites.

Beyond methodological refinements, the multifaceted involvement of LMP1 in oncogenic signaling, immune modulation, and metabolic reprogramming underscores the essential need for multi-targeted therapeutic strategies. Small-molecule inhibitors, peptide-based therapies, or combination treatments that specifically target LMP1-dependent pathways hold significant promise for innovative interventions in EBV-related cancers. As our understanding of LMP1’s diverse functional domains continues to grow, it could profoundly transform the therapeutic landscape for diseases linked to Epstein-Barr virus. Furthermore, agonist peptides, like the one identified in this study, represent a new frontier in cancer treatment, offering targeted therapies that could modulate tumor growth, enhance immune responses, and counteract the malignant effects of EBV.

Conclusion

The management of EBV-associated cancers is shifting toward personalized medicine, aiming to optimize treatment based on individual patient profiles. A promising approach involves targeting the CTAR regions of EBV’s LMP1 with peptide inhibitors to disrupt oncogenic signalling, promote apoptosis, and restore T-cell functionality. One key finding was the identification of an agonist peptide, known for its tumour-suppressive properties, which could regulate cellular processes like proliferation and angiogenesis. The discovery of PTPRJ agonists suggests a novel therapeutic pathway to enhance tumour-suppressive activity and modify the tumour microenvironment. This research highlights the potential for precision therapies in EBV-related cancers, offering personalized treatments that maximize efficacy based on tumour-specific characteristics. Further testing is needed to confirm these findings for clinical use. Targeting LMP1 with agonist peptides not only aims at inhibiting a single oncoprotein but also impacts crucial signalling pathways, immune surveillance, and the effectiveness of traditional therapies, positioning them as a promising new frontier in treating EBV-related cancers.
