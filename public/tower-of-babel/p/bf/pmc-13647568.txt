Performance evaluation of domain-specific and general-purpose AI models for chest radiograph interpretation: a comparative study
Authors: Tae-Hoon Kim, Jun Hyung Hong, Jihun Hyun, Sang Gook Song, Eun Ju Yoon, Eunji Jeong, Jin Woong Kim, Hyung Joong Kim, Hyun Chul Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChest radiography remains the most widely used imaging modality worldwide; however, its interpretation is inherently challenging because of overlapping anatomical structures and subtle findings. Recent advances in multimodal large language models (LLMs) have enabled automated radiology report generation, yet their clinical performance relative to domain-specific medical AI systems remains insufficiently validated.ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the performance and clinical applicability of a domain-specific multimodal AI model (M4CXR) compared with a general-purpose LLM (ChatGPT-4o) for chest radiograph interpretation.MethodsIn this retrospective study, 500 anonymized chest radiographs from a single tertiary care center were analyzed. Four board-certified radiologists independently evaluated AI-generated reports from both models. Key outcomes included key finding detection (categorized as complete, partial, or inconsistent), report generation time, and report discrepancies assessed using the RADPEER scoring system. Agreement between original and M4CXR-assisted RADPEER scores was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients and weighted Cohen’s kappa. Statistical analyses included paired t-tests, and chi-square tests.ResultsM4CXR demonstrated significantly higher report consistency than GPT-4o, with complete concordance observed in 55.8% versus 19.8% of cases, and lower inconsistency rates (25.2% vs. 46.4%, P<.001). The use of M4CXR significantly reduced report generation time compared with unaided interpretation (16.3 ± 12.9 s vs. 179.2 ± 50.4 s, P<.001). RADPEER-based discrepancy analysis revealed no significant differences between original and AI-assisted interpretations. Agreement between original and M4CXR-assisted RADPEER scores showed good reliability (ICC = 0.701), and weighted kappa analysis showed substantial agreement (κw = 0.652).ConclusionsDomain-specific multimodal AI model evaluated in this study demonstrated higher diagnostic consistency than the general-purpose LLM evaluated under the study conditions. These findings suggest the potential of specialized AI models as viable assistive tools, while highlighting the complementary utility of general-purpose LLMs in broader clinical contexts. Future integration should prioritize human–AI collaboration and prospective multi-center validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12880-026-02557-z.Keywords: Chest radiography, Artificial intelligence, Multimodal learning, Radiology report generation, Clinical decision support

Introduction

Clinical decision-making in medicine frequently occurs under conditions of diagnostic uncertainty, time constraints, and incomplete clinical information [1]. Among imaging modalities, chest radiography (CXR) remains the most widely performed diagnostic examination worldwide. However, current interpretative practices face significant challenges due to the heavy workload of radiologists and the indiscriminate ordering of chest X-rays. These factors often lead to critical delays in reporting and, even when completed, may result in a decline in interpretive quality. Despite its accessibility and relatively low cost, accurate interpretation of chest radiographs remains challenging even for experienced radiologists because of overlapping anatomical structures, subtle radiographic findings, and heterogeneous disease presentations [2]. Diagnostic discrepancies in CXR interpretation are well documented and may lead to delayed or inappropriate clinical management, particularly in emergency settings where rapid decision-making is required.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have introduced new opportunities to assist clinicians in complex diagnostic workflows [3]. In particular, the development of large language models (LLMs) following the introduction of the Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT) architecture [4] has enabled machines to perform increasingly sophisticated tasks involving natural language understanding, knowledge synthesis, question answering, clinical documentation and mental health [5, 6]. Moreover, multimodal LLMs have expanded these capabilities by integrating visual information with textual reasoning [7], thereby enabling potential applications in medical image interpretation.

Several AI systems have been developed to automatically generate radiology reports from imaging data. Domain-specific models trained on medical datasets have demonstrated promising performance in structured report generation and abnormality detection. One such system, M4CXR (Exploring Multi-task Potentials of Multi-modal Large Language Models for Chest X-ray Interpretation), integrates image encoding with language generation to produce structured interpretations of chest radiographs. Preliminary studies suggest that such systems may improve reporting efficiency and assist clinicians in high-volume clinical environments [8, 9].

In parallel, general-purpose multimodal LLMs such as ChatGPT (GPT-4o) and Google Gemini (Gemini and Gemini Advanced) have recently demonstrated the ability to analyze visual content and generate clinically relevant textual descriptions [10, 11]. Although these models were not originally developed for medical imaging interpretation, their flexible architecture raises the possibility that they could support radiological analysis in a broad range of clinical scenarios.

Despite these advances, several important challenges remain before LLM-based systems can be safely integrated into routine clinical practice. Concerns include hallucinated findings, algorithmic bias, insufficient external validation, and unclear regulatory pathways for diagnostic AI systems. Furthermore, the relative advantages of specialized medical AI models (e.g., M4CXR) compared with general-purpose LLMs (e.g., ChatGPT-4o, Gemini) remain poorly understood in real-world radiology workflows.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to systematically evaluate the performance and clinical applicability of M4CXR and general-purpose LLMs in chest radiograph interpretation. By comparing agreement with reference report, key finding detection, and reporting efficiency, this study aimed to clarify the potential roles and limitations of LLM-based systems in supporting radiological decision-making in emergency settings.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The protocol for this study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of the Chosun University Hospital (No.2025-03-003). Written informed consent was waived by the University Hospital IRB committee due to the use of anonymous archival data including CXR data and radiological reports. The requirement for informed consent was waived for the examination required in this study.

Input dataset for deep learning radiological interpretation

Posteroanterior (PA), anteroposterior (AP), lateral chest radiographs were retrieved from the institutional PACS database and anonymized using a standardized de-identification pipeline developed by Infinitt (INFINITT Healthcare, Seoul, Republic of Korea). The anonymization process removed all patient-identifiable metadata from Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) headers in accordance with the de-identification recommendations of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA).

Following anonymization, each image was assigned a sequential numerical identifier, and exported in DICOM format for further analysis. Figure 1 illustrates the anonymization workflow, and Table 1 summarizes the DICOM tags removed during the de-identification process.

A total of 1,903,079 anonymized chest radiographs were initially collected between January 2003 and December 2024. After excluding 61,948 records, the remaining 1,841,131 chest radiographs were partitioned into training, validation, and test sets at a ratio of 90:5:5, respectively. The 500 radiographs used for comparative performance evaluation were randomly sampled exclusively from the held-out test dataset and were not included in the training or validation datasets used for M4CXR development. Each selected radiograph represented a unique examination accession, thereby avoiding duplicate image selection. Follow-up examinations were retained in the study cohort to reflect routine clinical practice and the diversity of real-world imaging scenarios. Difficult or rare cases were not intentionally enriched in the study cohort; instead, cases were randomly sampled from the held-out test dataset to reflect the distribution and diagnostic complexity encountered in clinical practice. Chest radiograph interpretation of M4CXR and ChatGPT

To enable automated interpretation of anonymized chest X-ray (CXR) images, this study employs M4CXR, a cloud-based clinical research platform for CXR analysis developed by Deepnoid Inc. (Seoul, Republic of Korea) and accessible through the M4Labs online platform [12]. M4CXR is designed building on a series of prior studies in medical vision-language modeling, including multi-task multimodal large language modeling for CXR interpretation [13], retrieval-augmented radiology report generation [14], and explainable vision-language alignment for radiology [15]. Together, these foundations allow the platform to integrate visual information extracted from CXR images with textual medical knowledge, and to generate radiology reports that describe relevant findings in a clinically meaningful manner. By drawing on retrieval-augmented generation strategies, the platform is also designed to maintain consistency between the generated reports and the input images, thereby aiming to improve consistency between generated reports and image findings and to reduce the likelihood of unsupported statements.

In this study, M4CXR is used to support automated report generation from anonymized chest radiographs. Users upload anonymized CXR images through a web-based interface and receive text-based interpretation results, which are returned in the form of free-text radiology reports.

In contrast, ChatGPT-4o is a general-purpose multimodal LLM developed by OpenAI Inc. (California, USA). Unlike domain-specific medical AI systems, GPT-4o is trained on broad, heterogeneous data rather than curated chest radiography datasets [16].

To generate radiological interpretations using ChatGPT-4o, a strategic prompt design was employed to navigate the model’s inherent constraints on providing professional medical judgments [16].

Regarding image input, chest radiographs were provided to ChatGPT-4o through its multimodal interface, with each case uploaded as a standard portable network graphics (PNG) image converted from the original anonymized DICOM file, because the interface does not support native DICOM files for image interpretation. Image conversion was performed using a standardized lossless pipeline without additional image enhancement or diagnostic content modification. The process extracted the original 16-bit pixel data, applied the standard Values of Interest (VOI) Lookup Table (LUT) transformation for grayscale rendering, and rescaled the image to an 8-bit intensity range (0–255) for display. Because PNG uses lossless compression, the conversion preserved grayscale information without introducing compression artifacts, thereby minimizing the likelihood of clinically meaningful image degradation. All images were processed using the same standardized processing. For cases with multiple views (e.g., PA and lateral views), images were uploaded sequentially within the same session, and the model was instructed to consider all provided views collectively before generating a unified interpretation. No clinical history, laboratory data, prior reports, demographic information, or additional metadata were provided to either M4CXR or GPT-4o. All interpretations were generated exclusively from chest radiographic images.

To ensure consistency across cases, a standardized prompting protocol was applied. The complete prompt used for GPT-4o was as follows: “You are a professional radiologist. Please interpret this chest radiograph and provide a radiology-style report. Focus only on image-supported clinically significant findings. Avoid speculative diagnoses. Structure the output into Findings and Impression.” This approach was adopted to reduce variability in response style and improve comparability across outputs.

Model parameters and session settings were controlled to the extent permitted by the interface. The temperature parameter was maintained at the default setting (deterministic configuration not explicitly adjustable in GPT-4o interface), and each case was processed in an independent session to prevent contextual carryover effects. The “Chat history & training” option was disabled to ensure data privacy and to avoid unintended model adaptation.

No repeated sampling or multiple inference runs were performed for the same image. Each case was evaluated based on a single model-generated report to reflect real-world clinical usage scenarios and avoid selection bias.

Radiology-specific terminology was evaluated according to standard thoracic radiology reporting conventions used in clinical practice and aligned with the RadLex Radiology Lexicon (RSNA) whenever applicable. Minor wording differences or synonymous expressions were regarded as equivalent when conveying the same clinical meaning. Because all reference reports, AI-generated reports, and radiologist evaluations were conducted in English. No translation procedures were required, thereby minimizing language-related variability.

Finally, minimal post-processing was applied to the generated reports. Specifically, outputs were collected verbatim without content modification, except for standardized formatting adjustments (e.g., separation of Findings and Impression sections) to facilitate blinded qualitative evaluation by radiologists.

Dataset for comparing interpretation performance

A total of 500 PA, AP, and lateral chest radiographs were randomly selected in the department of Radiology from a single tertiary care center (Chosun University Hospital, Gwangju, Republic of Korea) range from January 2003 to December 2024. This study focused exclusively on adult patients, ranging in age from 19 to 98 years (median age: 48.5 ± 7.1 years). Among the 500 radiographs included in the comparative evaluation, 184 (36.8%) were classified as normal and 316 (63.2%) as abnormal according to the reference radiologist interpretations. Pediatric patients were excluded, as pediatric chest radiographs present inherent interpretative challenges related to age-specific anatomical variability and require specialized interpretative expertise.

This study implemented specific inclusion criteria to ensure optimal image interpretability: digital PA / AP / lateral chest radiographs of diagnostic quality were included, with all images acquired in standard positions and accompanied by complete clinical documentation. Cases were excluded if they involved suboptimal quality images, including non-digital or portable radiographs of nondiagnostic quality, or showed significant motion artifacts or improper positioning. To maintain an unbiased assessment of radiographic complexity, disease-specific quota sampling was not implemented. Eligibility screening was performed by four board-certified radiologists with more than 10 years of thoracic imaging experience (Fig. 2).

Analyzing readings by LLMs

Four board-certified radiologists independently reviewed the chest radiographs. To minimize potential bias, image order was randomized prior to evaluation. All assessments were performed independently, and findings were recorded using anonymized case identifiers to ensure objectivity.

To evaluate the workflow efficiency of M4CXR assistance (Fig. 3), interpretation times were randomly measured and compared between two conditions: interpretation with M4CXR assistance and interpretation without AI-assistance. For the M4CXR condition, the recorded time represented the interval required for the system to automatically generate a preliminary radiology report from an input chest radiograph. For the radiologist condition, the recorded time represented the interval required for a radiologist to independently interpret the image and produce a complete report without AI support. Therefore, the reported comparison reflects automated AI-generated report production versus conventional radiologist-generated reporting, rather than a combined AI-assisted radiologist workflow.

The quality of AI-generated reports (M4CXR and ChatGPT-4o) was evaluated using the following metrics:

Key finding detection rate: The percentage of clinically relevant key findings identified by the AI model compared with the reference radiology reports. Key findings were defined as radiographic abnormalities explicitly described in the reference report and considered clinically important for diagnostic interpretation or patient management. Concordance assessment was performed at the report level rather than on an individual lesion basis. In cases containing multiple abnormalities, agreement was determined according to the overall concordance of clinically relevant key findings between the AI-generated report and the reference report. ‘Complete consistency’ was defined as identification of all key findings, ‘partial consistency’ as identification of some but not all key findings, and ‘inconsistency’ as failure to identify one or more principal findings or substantial disagreement with the reference interpretation.

The original clinical radiology report issued in routine practice was used as the pragmatic reference standard. Because routine clinical reports do not represent an absolute diagnostic gold standard, cases with discordance between AI-generated outputs and reference reports, as well as randomly sampled cases, were reviewed independently by experienced radiologists as part of the discrepancy assessment process. These reviews served to characterize discrepancy patterns rather than establish a consensus reference diagnosis. No formal consensus adjudication was performed, as the primary objective was to evaluate report concordance and RADPEER score agreement under routine clinical interpretation conditions. Accordingly, the primary endpoints were defined as report concordance and discrepancy patterns rather than formal diagnostic accuracy. Report discrepancies were assessed according to the American College of Radiology’s RADPEER scoring system (updated 2016; Supplementary Table 2) [17]. Scores were categorized as follows:

Score 1: Concur with interpretation (No discrepancy); Score 2: Discrepancy in interpretation / not ordinarily expected to be made (understandable miss); and Score 3, Discrepancy in interpretation, should be made most of the time.

In the present study, RADPEER was applied to characterize the potential clinical significance of discrepancies between AI-generated reports and reference radiologist interpretations rather than to assess standalone AI diagnostic accuracy.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA) and MedCalc version 23.5.1 (MedCalc Software Ltd., Mariakerke, Belgium).

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Differences in report generation time between AI-assisted and unaided interpretations were evaluated using the paired t-test after confirming normality of distribution. When normality assumptions were not satisfied, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied. Categorical variables, including key finding detection categories (complete consistency, partial consistency, and inconsistency), were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate.

Interpretation agreement among radiologists was assessed using the weighted Cohen’s kappa statistic for categorical variables. For continuous variables such as RADPEER scores, reliability was evaluated using the Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) [18]. The ICC values were classified as poor (< 0.40), moderate (0.40 to < 0.60), good (0.60 to < 0.80), and excellent (0.80–1.00).

A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study evaluated the clinical performance and applicability of LLM-based systems for chest radiograph interpretation by comparing a domain-specific multimodal model with general-purpose LLMs. Our findings demonstrate that the specialized model achieved higher report consistency and improved reporting efficiency without increasing clinically meaningful discrepancies.

M4CXR system demonstrated higher report consistency in key finding detection compared with general-purpose LLMs. This difference likely reflects the domain-specific training strategy of the model, which incorporates medical imaging data and radiology report structures. Previous studies have consistently shown that models trained on curated radiology datasets show higher concordance than general-purpose LLMs in image-based diagnostic tasks [8, 9], particularly in abnormality detection and localization [8, 19]. In contrast, general-purpose LLMs are optimized for broad language understanding and reasoning, which may limit their capability for detailed visual interpretation [20]. Nevertheless, although M4CXR achieved a substantially lower inconsistency rate than GPT-4o, a proportion of reports remained partially concordant or inconsistent with the reference reports. Because discrepancy patterns were evaluated at the report level, the present study was not designed to quantify lesion-specific errors or classify discrepancies according to clinical severity. Importantly, no significant differences in RADPEER scores were observed between original and AI-assisted interpretations, suggesting that the observed inconsistencies were not associated with a measurable increase in clinically meaningful diagnostic discrepancies. These findings indicate that while domain-specific models improve overall diagnostic consistency, residual disagreement remains an important consideration for clinical implementation and reinforces the need for continued radiologist oversight, particularly when AI-generated reports are used to support rather than replace clinical decision-making. Taken together, these findings support a complementary framework in which domain-specific models provide greater report-level concordance and workflow efficiency, whereas general-purpose LLMs offer broader flexibility across diverse clinical applications. Future studies should further investigate the clinical significance of residual discrepancies through lesion-level analyses and standardized evaluation frameworks.

The primary objective of this study was not to benchmark all available medical vision–language models, but rather to compare a domain-specific radiology model with a widely accessible general-purpose multimodal LLM that is increasingly being adopted by clinicians and researchers in everyday practice. GPT-4o was therefore selected because of its broad accessibility, multimodal capability, and growing use in healthcare-related applications, making it a clinically relevant comparator for real-world implementation scenarios. Nevertheless, recent medically specialized vision–language models, including CheXagent, Med-PaLM M, RadFM, LLaVA-Med, and Med-Flamingo, have demonstrated promising performance by leveraging domain-specific medical knowledge and large-scale multimodal training datasets [21–25]. These models are specifically designed to improve medical image understanding, visual reasoning, and radiology report generation, and therefore represent important benchmarks for future research. Accordingly, future studies should include direct comparisons among multiple specialized medical vision–language models (e.g., CheXagent, Med-PaLM M, RadFM, LLaVA-Med, and Med-Flamingo) and general-purpose multimodal LLMs using standardized evaluation frameworks and external validation datasets to better define their respective clinical roles and generalizability.

Regarding workflow efficiency, the significant reduction in report generation time observed highlights the potential clinical utility of AI-assisted reporting. The LLaVA-based framework enabled near real-time generation of preliminary reports, with a mean response time of approximately 16 s. Such automated draft generation has the potential to optimize radiological workflows by providing preliminary findings for clinician verification and refinement [26]. Previous studies have established that multimodal architectures, which integrate visual encoders with LLMs, deliver robust performance on benchmark datasets such as MIMIC-CXR and IU-Xray. By aligning visual features with linguistic representations, these models enable the autonomous production of structured clinical reports [27–29]. Consequently, such efficiency gains may be particularly relevant in high-volume clinical environments or emergency settings, where timely interpretation is critical for patient management. Moreover, the workflow evaluated in the present study primarily evaluated automated AI-generated preliminary reporting by the AI system alone. In real-world clinical practice, however, AI-generated draft reports are typically reviewed, verified, and modified by radiologists before final reporting. Therefore, future prospective studies should evaluate a true human–AI collaborative workflow, incorporating both AI-generated preliminary reports and subsequent radiologist review, to better assess the practical impact of AI-assistance on clinical efficiency, diagnostic accuracy, and workflow integration.

In the discrepancy analysis based on the RADPEER score, there were no significant differences between original and AI-assisted interpretations. Although RADPEER was originally developed for radiologist peer review, it was applied in this study to characterize the potential clinical impact of discrepancies between AI-generated reports and radiologist interpretations. These findings suggest that AI-assisted reporting did not introduce a measurable increase in clinically meaningful diagnostic discrepancies. Furthermore, the observed agreement between original and M4CXR-assisted RADPEER scores was good, as indicated by an ICC of 0.701 and a weighted Cohen’s kappa of 0.652. These findings indicate a relatively consistent evaluation framework across reviewers and support the reliability of the evaluation framework.

Despite these promising findings, several challenges must be addressed before the routine clinical implementation of LLM-based systems, including algorithmic bias, hallucinated findings, and limited validation across diverse patient populations. A major concern in the clinical deployment of multimodal LLM-based systems is the risk of hallucinations, whereby models generate plausible but image-unsupported statements. Previous studies have reported that such hallucinations may result in factual inconsistencies, anatomically incorrect descriptions, or unsupported diagnostic conclusions, potentially leading to clinically misleading interpretations if not carefully verified by clinicians [30, 31]. Although hallucinations were not evaluated as a separate outcome measure in the present study, future investigations should adopt predefined operational criteria and perform lesion-level analyses to distinguish fabricated image-unsupported findings from diagnostic omissions or misclassifications. Such standardized evaluation frameworks would facilitate more rigorous assessment of the clinical reliability and safety of multimodal LLMs. Recent studies have shown that transformer-based architectures such as BART and XLM can achieve high similarity scores compared with physician reports [32, 33], highlighting both the rapid progress in this field and the importance of rigorous comparative validation. Algorithmic bias is another potential limitation, particularly when models are trained on datasets that do not adequately represent diverse patient populations [34]. Furthermore, most LLM-based radiology systems have not yet undergone large-scale prospective validation or received regulatory approval for routine clinical use.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design is restricted to fully capture clinical complexity of real-world clinical workflows and decision-making processes. In addition, the study was primarily designed to evaluate overall report concordance and diagnostic consistency rather than lesion-specific error patterns. Consequently, discrepancies were assessed at the report level and were not prospectively categorized according to overcalling, undercalling, disease subtype, or RADPEER severity category. Although this approach enabled a comprehensive assessment of overall reporting performance, it did not allow detailed characterization of lesion-level errors or their potential clinical impact. Therefore, future studies should incorporate lesion-level analyses, including evaluation of false-positive and false-negative findings, disease-specific discrepancy patterns, and urgent or actionable abnormalities requiring immediate clinical attention. Second, the evaluation dataset was derived from a single tertiary care center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other institutions, imaging vendors, patient populations, and acquisition protocols. Broader external validation across different institutions, imaging vendors, acquisition protocols, and patient populations is necessary to establish model robustness and facilitate workflow optimization, particularly in resource-limited clinical settings. Third, the rapid evolution of LLM architectures implies that performance characteristics may change with future model iterations. Therefore, the findings of the present study should be interpreted within the context of the specific model versions evaluated. Finally, this study focused exclusively on chest radiography and therefore further validation across other imaging modalities is warranted. Future studies should include prospective multicenter validation, comparisons with emerging medical vision-language models, and optimization of human–AI collaborative workflows to ensure the safety, reliability, and generalizability required for routine clinical radiology practice.

Conclusion

This study suggests that the domain-specific multimodal AI system M4CXR shows higher report consistency and key finding concordance than a general-purpose LLM for chest radiograph interpretation, while significantly improving reporting efficiency.

AI-assisted interpretation did not increase notable discrepancies and showed good agreement with radiologist assessments, supporting its potential utility as an assistive tool in clinical reporting workflows.

Rather than replacing radiologists, LLM-based systems are best utilized as assistive tools that enhance efficiency and support structured reporting. Future integration of AI into clinical radiology should emphasize human–AI collaboration, ensuring that technological advancements augment, rather than replace, clinical expertise.
