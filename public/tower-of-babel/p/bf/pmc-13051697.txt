Is Obinutuzumab the Answer to Primary Membranous Nephropathy Resistant to Rituximab?
Authors: Kajaree Giri, Richard J Glassock, Fernando C Fervenza
Published: 2026

See Clinical Research on Article 103728

Membranous nephropathy (MN) is the most common cause of nephrotic syndrome in adults without diabetes mellitus worldwide. Historically, patients who needed immunosuppression were treated with cytotoxic drugs or calcineurin inhibitors, which were effective but associated with a significant risk of toxicity in the long-term. The treatment landscape changed after the seminal work by Remuzzi et al.1 in 2002 reporting on the successful use of rituximab in 8 patients with MN. Since then, a number of studies have documented the efficacy of rituximab in inducing proteinuria remission in MN,2 with ≥ 80% of the patients reaching complete remission (CR) or partial remission (PR) of proteinuria at 24 months.3 In a recent retrospective study of 159 patients with MN (108 [75.5%] had phospholipase A2-receptor [PLA2R]-antibody–associated MN), 140 patients (88.1%) achieved CR or PR after a median follow-up of 62.6 months.4 Failure to respond to rituximab was observed in only 11.9% of patients, with only 7 of 159 (4.4%) developing end-stage kidney disease, with an estimated renal survival of 97% (95% CI: 94%–100%) and 95.4% (95% CI: 91.2%–99%) at 5 and 10 years, respectively.

Accordingly, the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes guidelines recommend rituximab as a first-line therapy for the management of patients with MN at moderate or high risk of progression.5 Patients with high anti-PLA2R levels (> 150 RU/ml) are considered less likely to respond to rituximab; however, these same patients responded to a second course of rituximab.6 As such, ≤30% of patients are said to be “resistant” to rituximab and need rescue therapy. Retrospective studies have shown that these patients respond to obinutuzumab, a glycoengineered, fully humanized anti-CD20+ B cells monoclonal antibody, with enhanced antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity and direct B-cell killing.7

In the article by Trillini et al.,8 the data from the first prospective, single-arm, single-center, open-label trial have been presented to evaluate the response to obinutuzumab rescue therapy in rituximab-resistant and/or -intolerant adults with primary MN who had an estimated glomerular filtration rate > 30 ml/min per 1.73 m2 and proteinuria > 3.5 g/24 h despite optimized therapy with renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibition and no anti–B-cell therapy for > 6 months.8 Rituximab intolerance was defined as any previous acute grade III or IV hypersensitivity reactions requiring advanced care or delayed serum sickness syndrome that was deemed a potential risk upon patient reexposure. Resistance to rituximab was defined as the failure to achieve CR (urinary protein excretion < 0.3 g/d [urinary protein-to creatinine ratio < 300 mg/g] or PR (urinary protein excretion < 3.5 g/d or urinary protein-to creatinine ratio < 3500 mg/g with ≥ 50% reduction from baseline values) of the nephrotic syndrome, with serum albumin concentration < 3.5 g/dl, along with failure to achieve complete circulatory depletion of anti-PLA2R autoantibodies in anti-PLA2R positive patients.

The primary outcome was CR or PR of proteinuria at 12 months. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of relapses after initial CR or PR, time-dependent changes in 24-hour proteinuria, measured glomerular filtration rate, serum albumin, IgG fractional clearances, anti-PLA2R titers, along with serious and nonserious adverse events, among others. In patients with PLA2R-related disease, a serum anti-PLA2R levels to < 2.0 RU/ml by Euroimmun enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay was considered as immunological remission.

Twenty adults (age range: 45–71 years; 9 had detectable circulating anti-PLA2R and 1 patient had THSD7A-related disease) received 3 doses (1000 mg each) of obinutuzumab between March 2022 and February 2024. Overall, patients had received 60 rituximab infusions (1–8/patient) before obinutuzumab administration. Although a number of patients had experienced periods of CR or PR in different occasions following previous nonspecific immunosuppressive treatments including other anti CD20 monoclonal antibodies (e.g., ofatumumab), no one had achieved even a transient CR or PR after the most recent rituximab administration, and in all 9 patients with circulating anti-PLA2R, antibodies were never fully depleted despite complete peripheral B-cell depletion.

The authors reported that at 12 months, 16 patients met the combined end point, and 4 had CR; at the last available follow-up, 17 patients had reached the combined end point over a median (interquartile range) of 9 (6–12) months, and 5 had achieved CR. No patient relapsed after remission. Median (interquartile range) 24-h proteinuria decreased from 5.7 (4.7–8.1) to 1.3 (0.5–2.7) g/24 h. Albumin and IgG fractional clearances decreased, whereas serum albumin increased from 2.9 ± 0.6 g/dl to 3.9 ± 0.3 g/dl. Dyslipidemia, hypocalcemia, and hypogammaglobulinemia improved significantly, and glomerular filtration rate stabilized. At 12 months, total B cells and circulating anti-PLA2R antibodies were depleted. Similar findings were observed in the cohort of 10 patients who completed 24 months of follow-up. Treatment was safe and well-tolerated. As with the treatment with rituximab, hypogammaglobulinemia was not an issue likely because reduced urinary IgG losses as patients go into remission, counterbalanced obinutuzumab-induced reduction of IgG production.

The authors maintained that the median time from the last rituximab administration to the first obinutuzumab infusion exceeded 1 year to negate any post–rituximab carry-over effect. Three patients who were initially reported to be rituximab-intolerant showed remarkable decrease in proteinuria and albuminuria and concomitant improvement in serum albumin levels, after obinutuzumab administration. In addition, the authors noted that there was progressive reemergence of circulating B cells after initial depletion, but it was not associated with increase in proteinuria or reappearance of anti PLA2R antibodies, suggesting that obinutuzumab can eradicate autoreactive B-cell clones. However, we have observed relapses after CR in patients with MN treated with obinutuzumab, suggesting that autoreactive B-cell clones can persist.

These are very encouraging results and when added in the context of a formal prospective study, confirm and extend previous retrospective evidence indicating obinutuzumab as an effective therapy for patients with MN irrespective of anti-PLA2R status or resistance to rituximab.

However, we would like to point out that the definition of “resistance” to rituximab used in the study suffers from some significant limitations as follows: (i) as previously demonstrated by the authors,9 it takes ≥ 18 to 24 months or longer for proteinuria to reach its nadir. Therefore, timing needs to be taken into account in the definition of “resistance,” and in Trillini et al.8 the last rituximab administration preceded the first obinutuzumab administration by a median (interquartile range) of 13.1 (8.8–18.6) months, which could be considered a short interval in some patients; (ii) resistance requires proof that before obinutuzumab treatment, proteinuria had reached a plateau or was increasing despite patient being B-cell depleted (i.e., 0 cell/μl). In the study, baseline median proteinuria was 5.7 [4.70–8.15] g/24 h, but what were the proteinuria levels 6 to 12 months earlier? (iii) In anti-PLA2R positive patients, definition of resistance requires demonstrations that anti-PLA2R levels had reached a plateau or were increasing despite full B-cell depletion. However, as can be seen in Figure 5 of the study, none of the patients were B-cell depleted at the start of obinutuzumab. In our opinion, only patients who fail to have a decrease in anti-PLA2R levels or in whom levels were increasing despite persistent B cell depletion can be called resistant. Clinical proteinuria is a poor marker of treatment resistance, because it can be a manifestation of severe glomerular capillary injury with irreversible scarring. This is an important caveat, because many trials of novel agents or procedures are underway to remediate so-called treatment-“resistant” MN. Enrollment in these trials should document fulfilment of the definitions suggested above and never be considered resistant, unless a course of obinutuzumab has been tried, as demonstrated by Trillini et al.8 analyzed here.

Nevertheless, the study demonstrates that obinutuzumab has the potential to become the first-line therapy for any patient with primary MN. This hypothesis is being investigated by the MAJESTY trial, a phase 3, randomized, open-label, multicenter study which mirrors the design of the MENTOR trial, and compares the effects of obinutuzumab to tacrolimus in 142 naïve patients with primary MN and nephrotic syndrome (Clinical Trial Gov: NCT04629248). On February 15, 2025, Genentech announced that the study met its primary end point of CR at 2 years, with no safety signals identified. This marks a significant milestone in this autoimmune disease, with obinutuzumab likely to become the first approved treatment for primary MN. Therefore, the nephrology community should celebrate because it is likely that soon apparently treatment-resistant patients with MN will be a case of the past, with truly resistant patients a very rare encounter.
