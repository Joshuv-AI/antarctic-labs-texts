Neddylation is indispensable for early meiotic progression in spermatocytes via destabilizing HORMAD1 by SCF ubiquitin E3 ligase during synapsis
Authors: Ningyuan Tang, Qinghua Guo, Wenzhen Zhao, Li Wang, Rong Fu, Zhixiang Xin, Zongqin Zhang, Yue Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe organized chromatin configuration in meiosis prophase I is crucial for spermatogenesis and male fertility, involving a series of tightly coordinated events mediated by numerous proteins. Neddylation, a ubiquitin-like post-translational modification, conjugates NEDD8 to substrate proteins and thus regulates protein degradation via activating Cullin-RING E3 ligases. Despite its importance in other cellular processes, its role in meiosis remains elusive.MethodsWe inhibited neddylation using intratesticular MLN4924 treatment and generated germ cell-specific Nedd8-depleted male mice to study its meiotic role. Meiotic progression and chromosomal synapsis were assessed via markers of meiotic progression especially the assembly of synaptonemal complexes. Substrate-specific role of neddylation in meiosis was dissected to identify its functional targets both in vivo and in vitro.ResultsNedd8 deficiency in spermatocytes caused meiotic arrest at the zygotene stage, leading to azoospermia and infertility. Spermatocytes showed disrupted synapsis, with persistent HORMAD1 on unsynapsed axes and defective assembly of synaptonemal complex central elements. Mechanistically, neddylation mediated SKP1-Cullin1-FBXO47 complex-dependent ubiquitination and degradation of HORMAD1, a prerequisite for proper synapsis.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that neddylation is essential for spermatocyte development and reveal its critical role in regulating synaptonemal complex dynamics, probably depending on SCF complex-mediated HORMAD1 degradation. Background

Spermatogenesis, a fundamental biological process involving the production of fertile spermatozoa within the testicular seminiferous epithelium, comprises the mitotic division of spermatogonia, the meiosis of spermatocytes, and the morphogenesis of spermatids [1, 2]. Meiosis is a specialized cell division characterized by a single round of DNA replication followed by two successive rounds of chromosome segregation, ultimately generating haploid gametes [3–5]. Prophase I of the first meiotic division is notably extended and is subdivided into the leptotene, zygotene, pachytene, diplotene, and diakinesis substages based on chromosomal behavior. Aberrations during meiotic prophase I often lead to meiotic arrest or defective chromosome segregation, resulting in clinical outcomes such as infertility, subfertility, miscarriage, or congenital birth defects [6].

The structural hallmark of meiosis is the formation of the synaptonemal complex (SC), a tripartite proteinaceous structure assembled along homologous chromosome axes. The SC synapses homologous chromosomes to facilitate subsequent double-strand breaks (DSBs) repair and homologous recombination [7–9]. The SC consists of three parallel components: two lateral/axial elements, transverse filaments, and a central element. The axial elements (AEs; termed lateral elements once the SC is fully formed) comprise the cohesin complex, HORMA-domain (HORMAD) proteins, and the axis core proteins SCP2 and SCP3. These proteins form filamentous structures that coat the homologous chromosome axes and organize chromatin into an array of loops. Specifically, cohesins bind DNA while axis core proteins bind cohesins, mediating the assembly of extended coiled-coil filaments that anchor DNA loops [10–12]. HORMAD proteins bind to these axis core proteins to promote the stable alignment of recombining chromosomes and the formation of the SC. AEs begin to assemble during the leptotene stage, prior to SC formation, which initiates during zygotene. During synapsis, paired AEs of homologous chromosomes are linked by central elements (CEs, or transverse filaments) along their entire length, providing the structural framework necessary to promote crossovers at recombination sites [13–16].

The orderly progression of meiotic prophase I depends on coordinated protein synthesis and degradation to ensure proper chromatin remodeling, including pairing, synapsis, and recombination. In mammals, the protein composition of the axis shifts during synapsis; for instance, HORMAD components localized to unsynapsed axes are evicted upon successful synapsis. Conversely, the abnormal accumulation of HORMAD1 on chromosome axes is associated with the failure of SC formation, suggesting that HORMAD proteins may antagonize the assembly of the central element [17]. TRIP13 and SKP1 are established regulators essential for removing HORMAD proteins from the axes to ensure meiotic progression. However, the mechanisms governing axial element homeostasis during synapsis remain poorly understood [18].

Neural precursor cell expressed, developmentally down-regulated 8 (NEDD8) is a ubiquitously expressed 81-amino-acid ubiquitin-like protein that mediates the process of neddylation [19]. This post-translational modification involves the covalent attachment of the C-terminal glycine residue of NEDD8 to the ε-amino group of lysine residues on target proteins. The process is executed via a sequential three-step enzymatic cascade. NEDD8 is activated through two pathways: the canonical route facilitated by the NEDD8-activating enzyme E1 (NAE) and an atypical pathway mediated by the ubiquitin-activating enzyme 1 (UBA1). Activated NEDD8 is then transferred via NEDD8-conjugating E2 enzymes (UBC12 or UBE2F) and specific NEDD8-E3 ligases to the substrate [20, 21]. Because neddylation is intricately linked to cell cycle regulation and gene expression, its dysregulation in spermatogonia—which rely on precise transcription factor and signaling cascades—is often associated with impaired differentiation or tumorigenesis [22, 23].

In this study, we identified a novel role for neddylation in spermatogenesis using intratesticular MLN4924 treatment and a conditional knockout mouse model in which Nedd8 was deleted in pre-meiotic germ cells. Our results demonstrate that defective NEDD8 modification disrupts early spermatocyte development, leading to meiotic arrest and synapsis failure at the zygotene stage, consequently resulting in azoospermia and male infertility. Furthermore, we found that the ubiquitin E3 ligase activity of the SKP1-Cullin1-F-box (SCF) complex, regulated by neddylation, probably played a role in the clearance of HORMAD1 during SC assembly. Strikingly, our findings characterize neddylation as a master regulator of ubiquitin E3 ligases, orchestrating the protein degradation required for correct and orderly chromosome synapsis during meiotic prophase I.

Methods

Animals

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the International Guidelines for the Protection of Animals Involved in Biomedical Research, and the experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (No. A2019-029 and A2022-048). All mice were housed under pathogen-free conditions with free access to water and food, temperature and humidity control, and constant light and dark cycles at the Animal Center of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine.

The Nedd8f/f, Stra8-Cre mice were generated by crossing Stra8-Cre mice and Nedd8f/f mice. Nedd8 flox mice (Strain S-CKO-03918) and Stra8-Cre mice (Catalog C001536) were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences Inc Co, Ltd. (Suzhou, China). Male littermates Nedd8f/f were served as controls. The Cul9f/f, Stra8-Cre mice were generated by Crossing Stra8-Cre mice and Cul9f/f mice. Cul9 flox mice were generated by Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc Co, Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Male littermates Cul9f/f were served as controls. Genotyping was performed by PCR with tail genomic DNA using primers in Supplementary Table S1.

The construction of in situ MLN4924 treatment

After adult male mice were euthanized, a small incision in lower abdomen was made and the testis was exposed and received bilateral intratesticular injections: one testis was injected with 10 µL of 20 µg/mL MLN4924 (MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA) diluted in endotoxin-free saline solution (experimental group), while the contralateral testis received the same volume of sterile saline (control group). Morphological analyses were performed 10 days or 21 days after operation to assess the development of spermatogenetic cells and the progression of spermatogenesis.

Histological, immunofluorescence and chromosome spread analyses

For histological analysis, testes were fixed in Bouin's solution at room temperature overnight, embedded with paraffin, and sectioned. Testicular sections with 5-μm thickness were then performed hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining with standardize processes. For immunohistochemistry (IHC), testicular paraffin sections were dewaxed and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval by boiling for 15 min in citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0). Then, sections were stained with primary antibodies (supplementary Table S2) followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody staining and DAB visualization. Digital images were captured under a microscope (Olympus BX53, Japan).

Chromosome spread analysis was performed as previously described [24]. Briefly, the testis was torn and dispersed by tweezers to separate the seminiferous tubules in the dish containing ice-cold HEB (30 mM Tris, 50 mM sucrose, 17 mM trisodium citrate dihydrate, 5 mM EDTA, 5 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF). Incubate the seminiferous tubules in HEB on ice for approximately 1.5–2 h, and then transfer a group of 4 to 6 seminiferous tubules containing 30 μL of ice-cold sucrose (100 mM) to a dry, clean uncharged microscope slide. Gently pull and mash the tubules between the tips of the tweezers to form a semitranslucent microscope slide with seminiferous mashes. Then cells were pre-soaked with paraformaldehyde solution containing Triton X-100 and sodium borate.

For immunofluorescence, 8-μm-thick frozen testicular sections or chromosome spread slides were prepared and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 5% goat serum in PBS and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies (supplementary Table S2), respectively. After washing, the slides were incubated with responding secondary antibodies, followed by incubated with DAPI Fluoromount (Yeasen, China). The fluorescence signals were detected under a fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss LSM-510, Germany).

Plasmids

V5-HORMAD1 was purchased from Genscript Biotechnology Inc. FLAG-FBXO47, MYC-HORMAD1, MYC-CUL1, MYC-CUL1-K720R mutant plasmids were generated by using ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd. Shanghai, China) and Mut Express II Fast Mutagenesis Kit V2 (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All of the mutant plasmids were confirmed by DNA sequencing (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. Shanghai, China). All constructing Primers and Mutant Primers used in this study were listed in supplementary Table S3.

Cell culture and transfection

HEK293T cells were obtained from National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures and cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (GIBCO Laboratories, Grand Island, USA) containing 10% FBS and penicillin/streptomycin (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA), at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Plasmid DNA transfections in HEK293T cells were carried out using Hieff Trans® Liposomal 2000 Transfection Reagent (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). For each well of a 6-well plate, 1–2 μg of plasmids and 30 μL of Liposomal were used for transfection. Cells were collected 24–48 h after transfection for further analysis. Treatments of MG132 (2 μM for 6 h) (Sigma, St. Louis, USA) and MLN4924 (2.5 μM for 48 h) were performed 6 h after transfection.

Western blot analysis

Tissues or cells were homogenized in an ice bath in RIPA Lysis Buffer (EpiZyme, Shanghai, China) containing a protease inhibitor mixture (EpiZyme), and proteins were extracted as described [25]. Protein concentrations were then determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). The details of Western blot were described before [26]. Protein samples (20 μg) were separated using 4–15% denaturing polyacrylamide gels and then transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% BSA and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies listed in supplementary Table S2.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and co-IP assays

For idenetification of the NEDD8 modified proteins, denature IP assay was applied. Specifically, testicular tissues from wildtype adult mice were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with 1% SDS and boiled at 95 °C for 5 min, then diluted 10 times with RIPA buffer to reach 0.1% of the final SDS. The lysate was further incubated with NEDD8 antibodies (Proteintech) and performed IP assay according to the standard procedures as follows.

For co-IP assay, protein samples were washed with PBS then harvested and lysed directly in RIPA lysis buffer. Lysates were incubated with primary antibody CUL1 (Proteintech, Wuhan, China), SKP1 (Proteintech), HORMAD1 (Proteintech) was added with rotation at 4 °C overnight. Then antibody-protein complexes were incubated respectively with Protein A/G MagBeads, FLAG-beads or MYC-beads (Yeasen) with rotation at 4 °C for 4 h. After subsequent washing and elution, the precipitated proteins were analyzed by immunoblot analysis or LC–MS/MS analysis.

LC–MS/MS analysis

NEDD8-conjugated proteins were identified via immunoprecipitation and LC‒MS/MS analysis. The LC–MS/MS analysis was performed as described [27, 28]. All MS/MS spectra were searched using Maxquant software against the human International Protein Index (IPI) database (version 3.68). The processed dataset is available and listed in supplementary Table S4.

Data-Independent Acquisition (DIA) proteomics analysis

The testes were frozen immediately with liquid nitrogen and then prepared for liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS) and DIA proteomics analysis by Chi-Biotech Inc Co, Ltd (Shenzhen, China). Proteomics analysis was performed as previously described [29]. Data were processed in MaxQuant (UniProt database, USA) with tryptic specificity. LFQ normalization and FDR < 1% were applied. This dataset is available and listed in supplementary Table S5.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA), and results are presented as mean ± SD. Group comparisons were made using Student’s t-test where appropriate. The data were transformed for normality by taking logarithms when data were not normally distributed. The comparison of acrosome reaction rate and sperm deformity was assessed by chi-square analysis. Differences were considered statistically different when P < 0.05 (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ****P < 0.0001, ***P < 0.001).

Results

Inhibiting neddylation by MLN4924 induced spermatogenic arrest at the initiation of meiosis

Pevonedistat (MLN4924), a NAE specific inhibitor that blocks NEDD8 activation and subsequent neddylation [30], was introduced to investigate the effect of neddylation on spermatogenesis. Unilateral testis of adult mice was treated with MLN4924 by intratesticular injection, while the contralateral testis was injected with the same volume of saline as control (Fig. 1A). After 10 days of MLN4924 treatment, HE staining of testicular sections showed that most leptotene and zygotene as well as partial pachytene spermatocytes disappeared in the seminiferous epithelium, while other types of spermatogenetic cells still orderly arranged and the morphology of the seminiferous epithelium appeared normally (Fig. 1B). Notably, the interval time needed for the development of these missing spermatocytes was approximately 10 days, consistent with MLN4924-treated period, suggesting that neddylation was especially essential for the initial development of primary spermatocytes. Meanwhile, according to the morphology of cell nuclei stained by HE staining, we found that short-term MLN4924 treatment did not induce apoptosis, suggesting that inhibition of neddylation blocked the development or meiosis progression of spermatocytes rather than causing the apoptosis of spermatocytes.

To confirm the stages of spermatocytes, IHC staining of the phosphorylated Histone H2AX (γH2AX) and DMC1 was applied to indicate the leptotene, zygotene and pachytene spermatocytes. The results showed that spermatocytes ranging from preleptotene to mid pachytene stages still lacked in MLN4924 treated testes (Fig. 1C-D). Whereas, the signals distribution of markers of differentiating spermatogonia including SOHLH2 and P53, kept consistent between the control testes and MLN4924 treated testes (Fig. 1E). We also performed immunoblotting to analyze the expression levels of some spermatogonia related proteins and spermatocyte related proteins. The expression level of spermatogonial proteins including THY1, GFRA1 and SOHLH2 had no significant difference between MLN4924 treated testes and control testes, in accordance with that the spermatogonia remained the same in two groups identified by IHC staining. However, two meiotic proteins SCP3 and DMC1, were nearly absent in MLN4924 treated testes (Fig. 1F).

Additionally, chromosome ploidy analysis of the testicular cells implied that the ratios of haploidy (1N) and diploidy (2N) had no significant change between MLN4924 treated testes and control testes. However, the proportion of tetraploidy (4N, mainly representing primary spermatocytes) significantly decreased after MLN4924 treatment, indicating that inhibiting neddylation mainly blocked the development of spermatocytes (Fig. 1G).

Taken together, based on the loss of spermatocytes in the seminiferous tubules induced by short-term treatment of MLN4924, we speculated that the neddylation was especially crucial for the initial development of primary spermatocytes during spermatogenesis (Fig. 1H).

In addition, we further performed HE staining of the seminiferous epithelium at 21 days after MLN4924 treatment (supplementary Fig S1A). This duration time covered the meiosis of spermatocytes and initial spermiogenesis of spermatids. HE staining of testicular sections showed that most spermatocytes and round spermatids were absent in MLN4924 treated testes, and meanwhile, the leptotene spermatocytes were developed and appeared in the seminiferous epithelium again, suggesting that MLN4924 inhibition is reversible (supplementary Fig S1B). Above all, short-term inhibition of neddylation mainly blocked the initial development of primary spermatocytes.

(supplementary Fig S2A-B). Our successful deletion of NEDD8 protein in testes model was confirmed by Western blot, genotyping and immunofluorescence analyses (Fig. 2A, supplementary Fig S2C-D).

The Nedd8 cKO mice were healthy on the basis of their normal body weight (supplementary Fig S2E). But both male and female were absolutely infertile when mated with wildtype mice, associated with gonadal degeneration as estimating by histopathologic analysis via HE staining on testes and ovarian (Fig. 2B, supplementary Fig. S2F). Furthermore, we found that the size of the testes in 8-week-old Nedd8 cKO mice was significantly reduced in comparison to those in Nedd8f/f littermates (Fig. 2C). Such defect in testicular development displayed a progressive decrease in the testis index (the ratio of testis weight to body weight) since postnatal day 12 (P12), a timepoint zygotene spermatocytes occurred in the first wave of spermatogenesis, indicating that dysregulation of neddylation may disrupt the development of spermatocytes, probably interrupting the generation of zygotene spermatocytes (Fig. 2D).

NEDD8 modification is essential for meiosis of spermatocytes

To investigate the effect of neddylation on spermatogenesis, histological analyses were performed on adult Nedd8 cKO testes. HE staining of testicular sections from 8-week-old Nedd8 cKO mice showed severe defects in spermatogenesis, particularly loss of spermatocytes and spermatids in the seminiferous epithelium, and such spermatogenesis arrest caused devoid of mature spermatozoa in the cauda epididymis (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, IHC staining of DMC1 was applied to indicate the different stages of spermatocytes, that is DMC1 was slightly stained in the nuclei of preleptotene and leptotene spermatocytes, and enriched in the nuclei of zygotene spermatocytes. However, testicular sections of Nedd8 cKO mice were unable to identify the different stages of seminiferous tubules owing to the spermatogenesis arrest. Based on DMC1 staining, we found that there was preleptotene and leptotene spermatocytes but no zygotene spermatocyte existed in the seminiferous epithelium of Nedd8 cKO testes, instead there was some leptotene-like or zygotene-like spermatocytes with abnormal plaques signal in the nuclei (Fig. 2F).

Immunofluorescence staining of γH2AX and SCP3 on spermatocyte-chromosome spread slides was also performed to analyze the developing stages of spermatocytes. In the leptotene stage, SCP3 signal exhibited a thin-line structure that distributed along the chromosome axes and γH2AX signal became detectable along the chromosomes. In the zygotene stage, SCP3 signal on two thin chromosome axes became paired locally to form a thick line, and meanwhile γH2AX signal on chromosomes intensively increased. In the pachytene and diplotene stages, SCP3 signal exhibited thick lines on fully paired chromosomes and partially paired X–Y bivalent, while γH2AX signal was restricted to the X–Y sex chromosomes. In contrast, Nedd8 deficient spermatocytes exhibited an apparently normal leptotene, but cannot successfully develop into the zygotene stage and obviously absent in pachytene and diplotene stages (Fig. 2G). Specifically, Nedd8 deficiency resulted in the frequent appearance of leptotene-like or zygotene-like spermatocytes, in which SCP3 distribution presented as aggregated particles or patches accompanying with its linear pattern (Fig. 2G-H), indicating abnormal chromatin structure. Meanwhile, γH2AX present in DSBs was localized to the chromatins at the leptotene stage, and visibly decreased and scattered at the zygotene stage in Nedd8 deficient spermatocytes. Similarly, the single-strand DNA-binding protein RPA2 did not localize to the chromatin axis in Nedd8 cKO leptotene spermatocytes (supplementary Fig. S3A), indicating that meiotic DSBs were generated but DSB processing was impaired in Nedd8 deficient spermatocytes.

Overall, these results demonstrate that neddylation is essential for the completion of meiosis in male mice, whereas NEDD8 deficiency disrupts the development of spermatocytes and might arrest them at zygotene stage (Fig. 2I).

NEDD8 deletion leads to meiosis arrest at the stage of zygotene

To intently investigate more detail in the development of spermatocytes, we harvested testes from mice at P18 when the first wave of spermatogenesis generated the diplotene spermatocytes in the seminiferous epithelium. As expected, histological analysis of testicular sections from Nedd8 cKO mice at P18 exhibited degenerative seminiferous tubules containing scant spermatocytes, particularly loss of zygotene and pachytene spermatocytes in comparison to Nedd8f/f counterparts. Meanwhile, we found that there were leptotene-like and zygotene-like spermatocytes associated with aberrant chromatin filaments in the seminiferous epithelium of Nedd8 cKO mice (Fig. 3A).

Differential protein expression profiles of testes analyzed by comprehensive proteomics

To validate the aberrant expression of AEs or CEs related proteins and other potential change of protein expression in zygotene spermatocytes resulted from NEDD8 deficiency, DIA proteomics was performed on testes of P12 Nedd8 cKO and Nedd8f/f mice. We identified 715 differential expressed proteins (DEPs) with fold change > 1.5 and p-value < 0.05 between two groups, in which there were 609 down-regulated proteins and 106 up-regulated proteins. As shown in volcano plot and heatmap clustering analyses (Fig. 4H-I), a series of meiosis related proteins including SCP3, SCP1 and DMC1 were down-regulated in Nedd8 cKO group. In addition, gene ontology (GO) analysis showed that the DEPs mainly enriched in chromosome, spindle and kinetochore, and involved in the processes of meiosis I, negative regulation of nuclear division, and DNA binding and nucleotide binding (Fig. 4J). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis displayed that motor proteins and cell cycle represented a leading role that neddylation played in meiosis (Fig. 4K). Notably, the differential protein profile of Nedd8 cKO testis suggested that neddylation had a crucial role in meiosis processes.

Neddylation involved in chromosome pairing and assembly of synaptonemal complex

We next sought to determine the distinct process regulated by neddylation that was required for the development of zygotene spermatocytes. When leptotene spermatocytes develop to the zygotene stage, the synapsis proceeds orderly, during which the central elements of synaptonemal complex are assembled to facilitate the physical connection between the paired homologs, crucial for DSBs repair and homologous recombination. By performing immunofluorescence of spermatocyte chromosome spreads to detect HORMAD1, a compound of AEs, we observed that HORMAD1 localized in the unsynapsed chromosomal axes at the leptotene stage and it gradually disappeared in the synaptic axes where the CEs of SC assembled at the zygotene stage. On the contrary, HORMAD1 accumulated persistently on the unsynapsed axes throughout the whole zygotene stage in Nedd8 cKO spermatocytes (Fig. 5A). Thus, statistical analysis showed that the co-localization rate of HORMAD1 and SCP3 in Nedd8 cKO spermatocytes at the zygotene stage was significantly higher than that in Nedd8f/f spermatocytes (Fig. 5B). Accordingly, the SCP1 signal which represents the progress of synapsis was loaded onto the synaptic axes at the zygotene stage in Nedd8f/f spermatocytes, whereas it exhibited a scattered distribution instead of linear distribution on chromosomes of Nedd8 cKO spermatocytes at the zygotene stage (Fig. 5C). Statistically, the linear co-localization rate of SCP1 and SCP3 in Nedd8 cKO spermatocytes at the zygotene stage was markedly lower than that in Nedd8f/f zygotene spermatocytes (Fig. 5D). We assumed that such irregular accumulation of HORMAD1 on the chromosomes in Nedd8 cKO spermatocytes led to the failure of SCP1 loading to form CEs. Meanwhile, immunoblotting analysis also showed the irregular accumulation of HORMAD1 and down-regulated SCP1 in Nedd8 cKO testes at P10, P12 and P18 (Figs. 3D, 5E-G, supplementary Fig. S3E).

Taken together, meiosis process in Nedd8 cKO spermatocytes was disrupted at the zygotene stage and therefore resulted in abnormal chromosome morphology as well as aberrant SC structure. Therefore, we confirmed that neddylation was essential for the development of spermatocytes, especially for chromosomal synapsis.

Degradation of HORMAD1 depends on neddylation of the SKP1-CUL1-FBXO47 protein complex

To investigate the mechanism underlying the neddylation in meiosis, we performed immunoprecipitation to enrich NEDD8 conjugated substrates in wild-type mice at P14, when the first wave of spermatogenesis harvests a cluster of zygotene spermatocytes. NEDD8 modified substrates isolated by immunoprecipitation were then identified by qualitative mass spectrometry. The results indicated that some canonical neddylated E3 ubiquitin ligases including CUL1, CUL2, CUL3, CUL4A, CUL4B, CUL5, CUL7, and CUL9 had NEDD8 modification in testes (Fig. 6A). Using STRING analysis, we constructed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network, the PPI network of Cullin-RING E3 ligase family and neddylation-related proteins comprised of 11 nodes and 53 edges, suggesting NEDD8 and its functional related proteins broadly distributed within testis and may contribute to meiosis progression and spermatocyte development (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining of these cullins on wide-type mouse testes revealed the predominant localization of CUL1, CUL2, CUL3, CUL4A, CUL4B, CUL5 and CUL9 in spermatocytes (supplementary Fig S4A). Since previous studies reported that CUL4A involved in the development of spermatocytes at pachytene/diplotene stage and CUL4B contributed to the development of post-meiotic spermatids [31, 32], rather than that at zygotene stage as found in Nedd8 cKO mice, we supposed that other cullins might involve in the development of zygotene spermatocytes. At first, we attempted to investigate the potential role of CUL9 in meiosis by using the Cul9f/f, Stra8-cre (Cul9 cKO) mice. However, Cul9 cKO mice displayed normal male fertility and regular morphology of the seminiferous tubules (supplementary Fig S4B-C), suggesting that CUL9 was dispensable for meiosis.

Subsequently, we focused on another cullin member CUL1, and then the presence of the CUL1-NEDD8 conjugation in testis was confirmed via denaturing IP and immunoblotting. The result showed that CUL1 isolated by IP appeared as high-molecular-weight bands, representing the covalently modified forms of CUL1 (Fig. 6C).

Recently, Wang et al. demonstrated that HORMAD1 degradation was dependent on the ubiquitination by the SCF complex [17]. Therefore, we hypothesized that neddylation is essential for SCF complex to serve its E3 ligase function (Fig. 6D). To identify the crucial role of neddylation in SCF degrading HORMAD1, we confirmed the modification of NEDD8 on CUL1 essential for CUL1-SKP1 interaction (supplementary Fig S4D) and the interaction between HORMAD1 and SKP1 in mice testes in vivo (Fig. 6E). Meanwhile, we also found that MYC-tagged CUL1 expressed in HEK293T cells had a NEDD8-conjugated band, which was disappeared after MLN4924 treatment (Fig. 6F). Therefore, these results indicated that CUL1 was modified by NEDD8 both in vivo and in vitro.

To investigate the impact of neddylation on HORMAD1 degradation, MYC-tagged CUL1 (or K720R-mutated CUL1), V5-tagged HORMAD1 and FLAG-tagged FBXO47 were co-expressed in HEK293T cells, since both FBXO47 and HORMAD1 were absent in HEK293T cells. Either MLN4924 treatment or K720R-mutation could impede the NEDD8 modification on MYC-tagged CUL1 (Fig. 6G). Strikingly, MLN4924 treatment disrupted the degradation of V5-tagged HORMAD1 and thus led to its accumulation. Whereas, when expressed a mutant MYC-CUL1-K720R which actually eliminated the NEDD8 conjugated site of CUL1, it also disrupted the degradation of V5-tagged HORMAD1, in consistent with that caused by MLN4924 treatment (Fig. 6G). The results indicated that neddylation was essential for HORMAD1 degradation mediated by CUL1 in vitro.

To further examine the effect of neddylation on the HORMAD1 ubiquitination, we co-expressed HA-ubiquitin, MYC-HORMAD1 and FLAG-FBXO47 in HEK293T cells. Subsequently, MYC-HORMAD1 was immunoprecipitated with the anti-MYC antibody and poly-ubiquitinated HORMAD1 was detected by Western blotting with anti-HA antibody. As expected, the poly-ubiquitinated HORMAD1 was significantly reduced in the presence of MLN4924 treatment (Fig. 6H), suggesting inhibition of NEDD8 modification on SCF complex perturbed HORMAD1 poly-ubiquitination process. Collectively, these results demonstrated that neddylation was essential for the degradation of HORMAD1 by ubiquitination in vitro, therefore potentially involving in the sequential assembly of SC during synapsis (Fig. 7).

Discussion

The orderly progression of meiosis depends on the coordination of protein synthesis and degradation. Protein ubiquitination in meiosis has been extensively studied in recent years and indicates a defined role in spermatocyte development. In this study, we identified that a kind of ubiquitination-like post-translational modification, neddylation was essential for the first meiotic cell division in spermatocytes by maintaining chromosomal synapsis, mirroring its requirement for meiosis in oocytes. Defective neddylation by loss of NEDD8 starting at meiotic entry led to sterility and azoospermia with completely developmental arrest of spermatocytes at the zygotene stage. Moreover, our findings indicated that neddylation determined the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of SKP1-CUL1-FBXO47 complex was probably responsible for HORMAD1 degradation. Therefore, Nedd8 deficient spermatocytes failed to appropriately degrade HORMAD1 and assemble the CEs, ultimately leading to faulty initiation of synapsis.

Neddylation, a pivotal post-translational modification that conjugates NEDD8 to the lysine residue of a substrate, is frequently amplified in cancer cells to sustain cell proliferation, with its canonical mechanisms underlying the activation of ubiquitination enzymes, DNA replication machinery and the mTOR pathway [33]. To systematically investigate the function of neddylation on spermatogenesis, we employed two mouse models including in situ intratesticular injection of the neddylation inhibitor MLN4924 and deletion of Nedd8 in pre-meiotic spermatogonia. Remarkably, our results showed that defective neddylation resulted in the developmental blockage of spermatocytes with meiotic arrest approximately at the zygotene stage, thus suggesting neddylation is indispensable for the initial development of primary spermatocytes. However, serious studies related to ubiquitination in meiosis showed that the E3 ligases such as RNF212B, RNF126 and Trim41 [34, 35], mainly involved in the development of pachytene spermatocytes, apparently later than our findings [31, 32, 34, 36]. Since neddylation serves as a pioneer signal for ubiquitination in the canonical pathway, this regulatory process occurs much earlier in the spatiotemporal development of spermatocytes [37]. Nevertheless, recently Xi et al. reported that Nae1−/−, Stra8-Cre mice with neddylation defect in spermatocytes lead to meiosis arrest at the pachytene stage [38]. Indeed, the dynamics of meiotic progression differ between Nae1-deficient and Nedd8-deficient spermatocytes. The possible explanation for such difference may refer to the different protein lifespan, that NAE1 is longevous, whereas NEDD8 undergoes faster turnover. Therefore, deleting Nedd8 gene by Stra8-Cre in pre-meiotic spermatogonia could reduce the content of NEDD8 protein in time and exert its effect in zygotene spermatocytes.

Synapsis started at the zygotene spermatocytes is one of the key events in meiosis prophase I, crucial for the subsequent DSBs repair, homologous recombination and crossovers. There is a checkpoint of meiosis in response to synapsis defects, which is required to halt cell cycle progression and lead to prophase delays [39]. Specifically, synapsis requires the assembly of the SC by loading the CE between the homologous chromosomes to form the zipper-like structure, meanwhile requires degradation of some lateral elements associated proteins such as HORMAD1 on the unsynapsed chromosomes. It is reported that abnormal accumulation of HORMAD1 on chromosome axes was associated with the failure of SC formation. Notably, in Nedd8 deficient spermatocytes, increased levels of HORMAD1 and its failure to dissociate from the unsynapsed chromosomes, combined with diminished level of SCP1, together suggested that the failure of HORMAD1 degradation was the primary cause of the subsequent defective SC assembly, ultimately leading to synaptic failure and meiotic arrest.

It also warrants noting that neddylation shares partially overlapping substrates with ubiquitination, and modulates diverse cellular processes through regulating the activity of ubiquitin enzymes. In mammalian cells, Cullin family members are the best characterized substrates of neddylation, through which leads to activating the Cullin-RING ligases (CRLs) and subsequently promoting the ubiquitylation pathway [21, 22]. According to NEDD8-IP and LC-MS/MS profiling of NEDD8 conjugated substrates in testes, we identified multiple Cullin-RING ligase including CUL1, CUL2, CUL9 etc. Since CUL4A/CUL4B contributed to the development of meiosis process, we dedicated in unwinding the potential functions of these Cullin-RING ligases. In our study, it showed that CUL9 was unessential for meiosis of both sexes. Thus, more attention was focused on the function of CUL1, which was reported that SKP1-CUL1-F-box complex critically governed HORMAD1 degradation [17]. However, the role of neddylation in CUL1 dependent HORMAD1 ubiquitination and degradation was still unclear.

Here, we uncovered that the E3 ligase activity of SCF complex requires neddylation of CUL1. Either inhibiting neddylation by MLN4924 or mutating the neddylation site of CUL1, resulted in significant accumulation of HORMAD1, which was associated with its impaired polyubiquitination and degradation by SCF complex. Our findings demonstrated that neddylation was essential for the SCF complex-mediated HORMAD1 degradation by ubiquitin–proteasome system. Besides the SKP1-CUL1-F-box complex, PCH2/TRIP13 was reported to involve in removing HORMAD proteins from synapsed chromosome axes by altering the conformation of HORMADs [18, 40, 41]. Notably, although both loss of SKP1 and TRIP13 in mouse spermatocytes resulted in HORMAD accumulation on AEs, their phenotypes are not completely consistent. TRIP13 deficiency allows homologous chromosomes to pair and partially synapsis [18], while SKP1 deficiency prevents the chromosomal synapsis in consistent with our findings, suggesting that SCF complex associated ubiquitylation is more important for the development of spermatocytes. Otherwise, it is reported that TRIP13 promotes protein deubiquitylation by binding the deubiquitinase USP7 [42], therefore both ubiquitylation and deubiquitylation may equilibrate the appropriate protein stability during synapsis.

However, the molecular mechanisms that neddylation involves in spermatogenesis or meiosis, and their impact on these processes are still not fully understood. Our findings account for the partial role of neddylation in spermatocytes, especially in the events occurred after entry into meiosis that encounter a checkpoint of meiosis. We believe neddylation exerts a relatively broad regulatory effect in meiosis, in which CUL1 mediated downstream pathway is just one of the important pathways involved. Likewise, CUL1 may target numerous substrate proteins responsible for orderly control of protein dynamics in meiosis. Our study probably complements the SCF complex-mediated degradation of HORMAD protein during synapsis. Therefore, it would be important to identify more substrates of neddylation and unveil the exact mechanism for regulating protein stability by ubiquitin E3 ligases in spermatocytes in future studies.

Conclusions

In summary, the results obtained in this study revealed a crucial role of neddylation in meiosis and demonstrated that NEDD8 modification governed SCF activity was probably involved in HORMAD1 degradation which was required for the assembly of the SC during synapsis.
