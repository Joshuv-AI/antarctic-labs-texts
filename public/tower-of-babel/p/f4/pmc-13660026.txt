Knowledge of essential newborn care among caregivers in Ga West Municipality, Ghana
Authors: Eunice Akosua Brobbey Agyemfra Fianko, Kwaku Owusu-Ansah, Samuel Dery
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEssential newborn care (ENC) is a key strategy for reducing neonatal morbidity and mortality. Caregivers’ knowledge of recommended newborn care practices influences the quality of care provided during the neonatal period. This study assessed knowledge of essential newborn care and factors associated with good knowledge among postnatal mothers in the Ga West Municipality of Ghana.MethodsA facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 351 postnatal mothers attending child welfare clinics. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire assessing knowledge across four domains of essential newborn care. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and knowledge levels categorized as poor (< 40%), average (40–80%), or good (> 80%). Simple logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with good knowledge, and variables significant at p < 0.05 were included in a multivariable logistic regression model using backward stepwise selection.ResultsMost respondents demonstrated average overall knowledge of essential newborn care (85.42%), while 14.29% had good knowledge and 0.29% had poor knowledge. Knowledge was highest for breastfeeding and vaccination but comparatively lower for eye and cord care and neonatal danger signs. In the adjusted analysis, mothers aged 35 years and above (AOR = 2.76, 95% CI: 1.31–11.18), married mothers (AOR = 3.50, 95% CI: 1.10–11.19), mothers with tertiary education (AOR = 3.42, 95% CI: 1.62–7.20), and Christian mothers (AOR = 6.85, 95% CI: 1.96–23.91) were significantly more likely to have good knowledge of essential newborn care.ConclusionsAlthough most mothers demonstrated average knowledge of essential newborn care, important gaps remain in eye and cord care and recognition of neonatal danger signs. Maternal age, marital status, educational attainment, and religion were identified as independent predictors of good knowledge. Findings should be interpreted considering the facility-based cross-sectional design and reliance on self-reported data.

Background

Neonatal mortality remains a major public health challenge despite substantial progress in reducing under-five mortality globally. An estimated 2.3 million newborns die each year within the first 28 days of life, accounting for nearly half of all deaths among children under five years [1]. Most of these deaths occur during the first week of life and are largely attributable to preventable causes [2, 3]. The burden is disproportionately high in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, where neonatal mortality rates remain among the highest worldwide [1, 4].

Essential newborn care (ENC) comprises evidence-based interventions designed to promote newborn survival and healthy development. These interventions include immediate and exclusive breastfeeding, hygienic cord and eye care, thermal protection, immunization, and the early recognition of neonatal danger signs [5]. The World Health Organization recommends the adoption of these practices as part of routine maternal and newborn healthcare services to reduce preventable neonatal morbidity and mortality [5]. Studies have demonstrated that appropriate implementation of essential newborn care practices contributes significantly to improved neonatal survival and health outcomes [4, 6, 7].

Mothers and other primary caregivers play a critical role in implementing essential newborn care practices. Their knowledge of recommended newborn care influences the quality of care provided at home, timely recognition of neonatal illnesses, and care-seeking behaviour. Adequate knowledge has been associated with improved newborn health outcomes, while poor knowledge may contribute to harmful practices, delayed treatment, and increased risk of neonatal complications and death [8–10].

Studies conducted in low- and middle-income countries have reported varying levels of caregiver knowledge regarding essential newborn care. While awareness of breastfeeding and immunization practices is generally high, gaps have been identified in areas such as cord care, eye care, and recognition of neonatal danger signs [8, 10, 11].

In Ghana, efforts to improve maternal and newborn health have contributed to reductions in child mortality; nevertheless, neonatal deaths continue to account for a substantial proportion of under-five mortality [12]. Although newborn care education is routinely delivered through antenatal, delivery, and postnatal services, evidence on caregivers’ knowledge of essential newborn care remains limited in some settings. Existing Ghanaian studies have primarily focused on health professionals, with comparatively fewer studies centering caregivers, particularly postnatal mothers who are primary providers of newborn care at home [6, 13, 14]. In addition, most focus on newborn care practices, neonatal danger signs, or specific components of newborn care, with relatively few examining knowledge across multiple domains of essential newborn care [6, 7, 9, 15, 16]. Understanding caregivers’ knowledge and the factors associated with good knowledge is important for informing interventions aimed at improving newborn care practices and neonatal health outcomes. Therefore, this study assessed the level of knowledge of essential newborn care among postnatal mothers in the Ga West Municipality and examined the factors associated with good knowledge.

Methods

Study design, setting and participants

A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted among postnatal mothers attending child welfare clinics in selected health facilities within the Ga West Municipality of the Greater Accra Region, Ghana. The municipality comprises both urban and peri-urban communities and is served by several public and private health facilities that provide maternal and child health services, including antenatal, delivery, postnatal, and child welfare services.

The study population comprised postnatal mothers attending child welfare clinics during the study period. Eligible participants were mothers who had delivered a live baby and were within 28 days postpartum. Mothers who were critically ill or unable to participate in the interview at the time of data collection were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination and participant recruitment

Assuming a 95% confidence level, a margin of error of 5%, and an estimated 35.1% prevalence of good knowledge of essential newborn care from a previous study, the minimum required sample size was determined [11, 17, 18]. To account for possible non-response, an additional 10% was added, resulting in a final sample size of 351 participants.

The municipal hospital and two health centres providing child welfare services within the municipality were selected for study. Eligible postnatal mothers attending child welfare clinics during the study period were approached consecutively and invited to participate until the required sample size was achieved. The purpose and procedures of the study were explained to all eligible participants, and informed consent was obtained before enrolment.

Data collection and measurement of variables

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire adapted from previously validated instruments used in similar studies [10, 11] The questionnaire captured information on sociodemographic characteristics, obstetric history, sources of newborn care information, and knowledge of essential newborn care based on World Health Organization guidelines, which include assessment of danger signs, breastfeeding, eye and cord care and vaccination [5]. The questionnaire was pretested in a health facility outside the study area to assess clarity, relevance, and internal consistency. Trained research assistants conducted face-to-face interviews with eligible participants using Google Forms.

The primary outcome variable was overall knowledge of essential newborn care. Knowledge was assessed across four domains: breastfeeding, eye and cord care, neonatal danger signs, and newborn vaccination. Breastfeeding knowledge was assessed using three items (timing of initiation, exclusive breastfeeding, and timing of complementary feeding). Eye and cord care knowledge was assessed using three items related to signs of eye infection, importance of cord hygiene, and signs of cord infection. Knowledge of danger signs was measured using two items (common danger signs and importance of focusing on danger signs), while vaccination knowledge was assessed using three items related to the importance and timing of neonatal immunization (BCG and Polio).

Responses to knowledge questions were scored with each correct response coded as 1 and incorrect or “don’t know” response as 0, and aggregated to generate composite scores for each participant. Based on the overall knowledge score obtained, participants were categorized as poor (< 40%), average (40–80%), or good (> 80%). Domain-specific knowledge scores were similarly generated for each of the four essential newborn care domains.

Independent variables included maternal age, marital status, educational level, occupation, religion, parity, antenatal care attendance, place of delivery, mode of delivery, and other relevant sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics.

Statistical analysis

Data were exported from Google Forms into Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) for analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and knowledge of essential newborn care. Continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges, as appropriate, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Simple logistic regression analyses were initially performed to assess the association between each explanatory variable and good knowledge of essential newborn care. Crude odds ratios (CORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values were reported. Variables that were statistically significant at the 5% level (p < 0.05) in the simple logistic regression analyses were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model.

The final multivariable model was developed using a backward stepwise selection procedure with a significance level of p < 0.05 for variable retention. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs), 95% confidence intervals, and p-values from the final model were reported. Prior to model fitting, variables and categories were assessed for model estimability. Some categorical variables and categories were omitted from the crude and adjusted logistic regression analyses due to collinearity with other explanatory variables, sparse observations, or failure of the model to generate stable parameter estimates. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ghana Health Service Ethics Review Committee (GHS-ERC: 077/07/23). Permission was also obtained from the participating health facilities before commencement of data collection. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and confidentiality and anonymity were maintained throughout the study.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of caregivers

A total of 351 postnatal mothers were included in the study (Table 1). The majority were within the reproductive age group of 25–34 years, were married, and engaged in informal occupations, particularly trading. Most respondents had attained at least basic or secondary education, while a smaller proportion had tertiary education or no formal schooling. Christianity was the predominant religion among participants.

With respect to reproductive and maternal health characteristics, antenatal care utilization was high, with most mothers reporting four or more ANC visits during their most recent pregnancy. The majority also delivered in health facilities and had previous childbirth experience. Information on essential newborn care was commonly received during antenatal visits, with midwives serving as the primary source of education. Topics most frequently covered included maternal nutrition during pregnancy and exclusive breastfeeding practices. Overall, the findings indicate that the study population had relatively good access to maternal health services and opportunities to receive newborn care information.

Eye and cord care: Knowledge related to eye and cord care was more variable. While mothers demonstrated relatively good awareness of some signs of eye and cord infections and the importance of maintaining cord hygiene, important gaps remained in the recognition of several infection-related signs. Overall, this domain reflected less consistent knowledge compared with breastfeeding practices.

Neonatal danger signs: Mothers were able to identify some common neonatal danger signs such as fever, excessive crying, and vomiting/diarrhoea; however, recognition was not uniform across all signs. Knowledge was stronger for commonly observed symptoms than for signs requiring urgent medical attention such as poor feeding and breathing difficulty, suggesting potential gaps in timely identification of neonatal illness. Most respondents acknowledged the importance of recognizing danger signs in preventing adverse newborn outcomes.

Vaccination: Knowledge of newborn immunization was generally favorable. Most mothers understood the benefits of vaccination and demonstrated good awareness of the recommended timing of birth-dose vaccines, although knowledge of some specific benefits of immunization was less common.

Domain-specific knowledge of essential newborn care

Figure 1 illustrates caregivers’ knowledge across the four domains of essential newborn care and overall knowledge. Knowledge was highest for breastfeeding with 92% having good knowledge, followed by vaccination with 49.6% having good knowledge. In contrast, knowledge of neonatal danger signs and eye and cord care was predominantly in the average category, with a notable proportion of caregivers demonstrating poor knowledge, particularly for eye and cord care (Fig. 1a). Although domain-specific knowledge varied considerably, most caregivers were classified as having average overall knowledge (85.42%), with relatively few attaining good overall knowledge (14.29%) (Fig. 1b).

Factors associated with overall knowledge

Table 3 shows the factors associated with good knowledge of essential newborn care. After adjusting for potential confounders, mothers aged 35 years and above were significantly more likely to have good knowledge compared with those younger than 25 years (AOR = 2.76, 95% CI: 1.31–11.18). Married mothers had higher odds of good knowledge than single mothers (AOR = 3.50, 95% CI: 1.10–11.19). Tertiary education was independently associated with good knowledge (AOR = 3.42, 95% CI: 1.62–7.20), and Christian mothers were more likely to demonstrate good knowledge than Muslim mothers (AOR = 6.85, 95% CI: 1.96–23.91). Other variables that were significant in the crude analyses did not retain significance in the adjusted model.

Discussion

Overall knowledge of essential newborn care

This study assessed knowledge of essential newborn care among postnatal mothers attending child welfare clinics in the Ga West Municipality of Ghana. Although most mothers demonstrated average overall knowledge, relatively few attained good knowledge. This finding suggests that while mothers are generally exposed to newborn care information through maternal and child health services, important knowledge gaps remain in specific domains of newborn care. Adequate maternal knowledge is critical for promoting recommended newborn care practices and facilitating early recognition of neonatal illness, which are essential for reducing neonatal morbidity and mortality [7, 19]. Similar variations in maternal knowledge of essential newborn care have been reported in Ghana, Ethiopia, and Nigeria, where knowledge levels differed across specific domains of newborn care [8, 10, 11, 15, 16].

Breastfeeding knowledge

Breastfeeding was the domain with the highest level of knowledge among respondents. Most mothers demonstrated awareness of early initiation of breastfeeding, exclusive breastfeeding during the first six months of life, and the appropriate timing of complementary feeding. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Ghana and Ethiopia that reported relatively high maternal knowledge and awareness of recommended breastfeeding practices [20–22]. The high level of knowledge observed may reflect the strong emphasis placed on breastfeeding counselling during antenatal, delivery, and postnatal care services, as well as national and international campaigns promoting exclusive breastfeeding. The findings suggest that breastfeeding messages are being effectively communicated through the healthcare system.

Eye and cord care knowledge

Knowledge of eye and cord care was comparatively lower than knowledge of breastfeeding. Although many mothers were aware of the importance of maintaining cord hygiene and could identify some signs of infection, important gaps were observed in the recognition of eye and cord infection symptoms. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have reported lower knowledge levels for infection prevention and cord care practices compared with other components of essential newborn care [8, 10, 11]. Given that neonatal infections remain a significant contributor to neonatal morbidity and mortality in low-resource settings [2, 19], inadequate recognition of infection-related signs may delay care-seeking and increase the risk of adverse outcomes. These findings suggest the need for greater emphasis on practical demonstrations and counselling regarding hygienic cord care and identification of neonatal infections during routine maternal and child health services.

Knowledge of neonatal danger signs

Knowledge of neonatal danger signs was also suboptimal. While mothers readily identified common symptoms such as fever and excessive crying, recognition of critical danger signs such as poor feeding and breathing difficulty was less consistent. Similar deficiencies in the recognition of neonatal danger signs have been reported among caregivers in Ghana and other low- and middle-income countries [9, 23]. Early recognition of neonatal danger signs is a key component of newborn survival because it facilitates timely healthcare seeking and reduces delays in accessing treatment [5]. The findings therefore highlight the need for more structured and standardized counselling on neonatal danger signs during antenatal care, postnatal care, and child welfare clinic visits. Improving caregivers’ ability to recognize serious symptoms may contribute to earlier treatment-seeking and better neonatal outcomes.

Vaccination knowledge

Vaccination knowledge was generally favourable, with most mothers demonstrating awareness of the importance of newborn immunization and the timing of birth-dose vaccines. This finding is encouraging and may reflect the effectiveness of Ghana’s Expanded Programme on Immunization and routine health education provided through maternal and child health services. Similar findings have been reported among caregivers in urban Ghana, where knowledge and positive attitudes toward childhood immunization were generally high [24]. However, some gaps were observed regarding the broader benefits of immunization in preventing neonatal and childhood illnesses. Strengthening educational messages around the public health importance of vaccination may further improve caregiver understanding and support timely vaccine uptake.

Factors associated with knowledge of essential newborn care

The multivariable analysis identified maternal age, marital status, educational attainment, and religion as independent predictors of good knowledge of essential newborn care. Older mothers were more likely to demonstrate good knowledge than younger mothers, possibly reflecting greater childcare experience and repeated exposure to maternal and child health services. Similar associations between maternal age and newborn care knowledge have been reported in previous studies [8, 10, 11].

Educational attainment emerged as one of the strongest predictors of good knowledge. Mothers with tertiary education were significantly more likely to possess good knowledge than those with lower levels of education. This finding is consistent with studies from Ghana, Nigeria, and Ethiopia that identified education as a key determinant of newborn care knowledge and practices [6–8, 16, 22]. Higher educational attainment may enhance women’s ability to access, understand, and apply health information received through healthcare services and other information channels.

Married mothers also demonstrated higher odds of good knowledge compared with unmarried mothers. This may be attributable to increased social, emotional, and financial support, which may facilitate healthcare utilization and exposure to health information. Religion was also independently associated with knowledge. Although the mechanisms underlying this relationship remain unclear, differences in social support structures, health information dissemination, and healthcare-seeking behaviours may partly explain the observed variation.

Interestingly, variables such as occupation, parity, antenatal care attendance, place of birth, and mode of delivery did not remain statistically significant after adjustment for confounding factors. This suggests that their effects may have been mediated through other sociodemographic characteristics.

Implications for practice and policy

The findings highlight the need for strengthened and more comprehensive newborn care education within maternal and child health services. While breastfeeding and immunization messages appear to be effectively communicated, greater attention should be devoted to eye and cord care and recognition of neonatal danger signs. Healthcare providers should utilize antenatal care visits, delivery discharge counselling, postnatal services, and child welfare clinics as opportunities to reinforce key newborn care messages. Particular attention should be directed toward younger mothers and those with lower educational attainment, who may be at increased risk of inadequate knowledge.

Strengths and limitations

This study contributes to the growing body of evidence on essential newborn care knowledge in Ghana by examining multiple domains of newborn care among postnatal mothers. Assessing breastfeeding, eye and cord care, neonatal danger signs, and vaccination separately enabled the identification of specific knowledge gaps that may inform targeted interventions.

However, several limitations should be considered. Knowledge was assessed using self-reported responses and may therefore be subject to recall and social desirability bias. The facility-based design may limit the generalizability of the findings to mothers who do not routinely access health services. In addition, the cross-sectional nature of the study precludes causal inference regarding the observed associations between participant characteristics and knowledge of essential newborn care.

Conclusions

This study found that although most postnatal mothers in the Ga West Municipality demonstrated average knowledge of essential newborn care, important knowledge gaps remained in specific domains, particularly eye and cord care and the recognition of neonatal danger signs. In contrast, knowledge of breastfeeding and newborn vaccination was relatively high, suggesting that messages related to these practices are being effectively communicated through maternal and child health services. Maternal age, marital status, educational attainment, and religion were identified as independent predictors of good knowledge, highlighting the influence of sociodemographic factors on caregivers’ understanding of essential newborn care.

The findings underscore the need for strengthened and more comprehensive newborn care education throughout the continuum of maternal and child healthcare with particular emphasis, infection prevention, cord and eye care, and recognition of neonatal danger signs. Targeted educational interventions, especially for younger mothers and those with lower levels of education, may help improve caregivers’ knowledge and promote optimal newborn care practices.
