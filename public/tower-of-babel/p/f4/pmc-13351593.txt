Disease‐Duration–Specific Percentiles for Prospective Subtyping of Parkinson's Disease: A PPMI‐Based Study
Authors: Ahmed Negida, Nitai Mukhopadhyay, Brian D Berman, Seyed‐Mohammad Fereshtehnejad, Matthew J Barrett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParkinson's disease (PD) is clinically heterogeneous, with variable progression rates that complicate clinical trial design. The data‐driven diffuse malignant (DM), intermediate (IM), and mild‐motor predominant (MMP) subtyping model has prognostic value but lacks disease duration–specific thresholds for prospective use in disease‐modifying trials.ObjectiveTo define year‐specific percentile thresholds for key motor and non‐motor measures within the first 5 years after diagnosis to enable real‐time PD subtyping and assess progression patterns across subtypes.MethodsWe analyzed de‐identified PPMI data (downloaded April 22, 2026) from 1030 individuals with idiopathic PD. For each disease year, we computed percentiles for a composite motor score (MDS‐UPDRS II + III + PIGD) and non‐motor measures (MoCA, RBDSQ, SCOPA‐AUT). Thresholds were set at the 75th percentile for motor, RBDSQ, and SCOPA‐AUT, and the 25th percentile for MoCA, and applied annually to classify DM‐, IM‐, and MMP‐PD. Subtype stability (years 1–5) and progression were assessed using 25 predefined PPMI milestones. Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression models evaluated time to first milestone.ResultsPercentile thresholds worsened progressively over time, paralleling cohort‐level decline. DM‐PD prevalence ranged from 19.2–20.5% (IM 41.8–44.4%; MMP 35.1–38.8%). At baseline, clinical measures differed significantly across subtypes. Compared to MMP‐PD, DM‐PD (HR 3.03; 95% CI: 2.30–3.97) and IM‐PD (HR 1.48; 95% CI: 1.19–1.84) showed faster progression.ConclusionsWe establish disease duration–specific percentiles for prospective application of the DM/IM/MMP subtyping model, supporting patient stratification and enrichment in disease‐modifying trials.Keywords: diffuse‐malignant, Parkinson's disease, subtypes, strartification, trial enrichment

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder affecting selectively vulnerable neuronal populations including dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta, cholinergic neurons in the basal forebrain and pedunculopontine nucleus, and noradrenergic neurons in the locus coeruleus. 1 , 2 Despite numerous clinical trials of disease modifying agents over the past decades, there are still no proven and effective interventions to stop or slow disease progression. One reason why disease‐modification trials in PD fail is the substantial heterogeneity of disease symptoms and rates of progression, which make it challenging to demonstrate disease modification. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Another reason is the slowly progressive nature of the disease, which makes it difficult to demonstrate benefit using the conventional 1‐ or 2‐year disease‐modification clinical trial design. 3 , 4 , 5 , 8 , 9 Both of these obstacles would be overcome by subtyping PD to identify individuals with rapid disease progression. 7 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12

Several hypothesis‐based and data‐driven models for PD subtyping have been proposed over the past decade, 13 but many lack external validation and longitudinal stability. One promising subtyping solution is the data‐driven model that was first proposed in 2015 14 and was validated in 2017. 15 Using cluster analysis of longitudinal clinical data, researchers defined three distinct subtypes of PD: diffuse malignant (DM), intermediate (IM), and mild motor predominant (MMP). Subsequent studies validated these subtypes by multimodal imaging 16 and demonstrated subtype stability over time. 17 , 18 Observational studies validating or employing this subtyping approach span different worldwide populations including patients from Canada, 14 the US, 15 , 18 the UK, 19 China, 16 and the Netherlands. 17

DM‐PD patients tend to have more prominent early motor and non‐motor symptoms, faster disease progression, rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (RBD), mild cognitive impairment, orthostatic hypotension, and worse response to levodopa treatment. 15 , 19 , 20 The potential value of this subtyping model was recently demonstrated in a secondary analysis of the phase 2 PASADENA clinical trial. 21 Prasinezumab, a monoclonal antibody against aggregated α‐synuclein, slowed motor progression in patients with DM‐PD but not in non‐DM‐PD with an adjusted 1‐year change from baseline in the MDS‐UPDRS Part III of −7.8 vs −0.77, respectively. 22 This finding supports the idea that prospective subtyping of PD patients and selecting individuals with faster disease progression (ie, DM‐PD) for disease‐modification trials will increase the ability to demonstrate disease‐modifying benefits.

In prior studies, subtyping was performed retrospectively using percentile thresholds applied post hoc to baseline severity scores from drug‐naïve patients in the early stages of the disease. To enable prospective application of this subtyping model in clinical trials, the objective of this study was to define disease duration‐specific percentile thresholds using the PPMI dataset and to evaluate their ability to predict different rates of disease progression.

Methods

Study Design and Ethics

We conducted an observational analysis using data from the Michael J. Fox Foundation's Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI). The PPMI is an ongoing longitudinal, multicenter study, which aims to verify biomarkers of PD progression. 23 The institutional review board at each PPMI site approved the study and participants provided informed consent according to local regulations. This secondary analysis of publicly available, de‐identified data was exempt from VCU IRB review.

Study Participants

In this study, we included all confirmed PD patients with all data collected across all visits up to 10 years from PD diagnosis. Details regarding PPMI methodology are available online (https://www.ppmi-info.org/study-design/). Participants in the PPMI study were 30 years or older and met the following criteria at their baseline visit: (1) had been diagnosed with PD in the last 2 years, (2) would not need to begin any medications for PD in the next 6 months, (3) had at least two listed symptoms (resting tremor, bradykinesia, or rigidity), (4) with a Hoehn and Yahr Stage ≤2 at baseline, and (5) had a positive dopamine transporter scan demonstrating a dopamine transporter deficit. The study data included eligible patients from all records available in the database as of April 22, 2026. We selected participants with confirmed primary diagnosis of idiopathic PD at baseline, cohort definition of PD, who were untreated at baseline. We excluded participants with any pathogenic variants in LRRK2, GBA, VPS35, SNCA, PRKN, PARK7, and PINK1 and those for whom the diagnosis was later switched to an atypical parkinsonism.

Motor and Non‐motor Assessments

We extracted the following data: PD diagnosis history, Movement Disorder Society‐Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS‐UPDRS) and its subscales, Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT), REM Sleep Behavior Disorder screening questionnaire (RBDSQ), and Scales for Outcomes in Parkinson's Disease–Autonomic (SCOPA‐AUT).

Calculating Subtyping Percentiles for Years since PD Diagnosis

We followed the same data‐driven subtyping criteria as previously reported. 15 A composite motor score of the MDS‐UPDRS II and III scores and the postural instability and gait difficulty to tremor (PIGD/TD) ratio (all off medications) was calculated for all visits. Three non‐motor scores were chosen: MoCA for cognitive performance, RBDSQ for RBD, and SCOPA‐AUT for autonomic functions. These four scores represent the main motor and non‐motor categories that were used for data‐driven subtyping. Then, PD duration at each study visit was calculated by subtracting the PD diagnosis date from the visit date. We assigned an ordinal variable (years since PD diagnosis) for all the study visits (ie, years since PD diagnosis = 1 assigned to visits where PD duration at the study visit was ≥0 but <1 year). Multiple visits within the same disease year were averaged to obtain annual composite scores. We calculated the subtyping percentiles for each year since PD diagnosis. Subtyping percentiles were the 75th percentile of the composite motor score, RBDSQ, and SCOPA‐AUT and the 25th percentile of the MoCA since it has a different score direction (lower scores indicate impairment).

Determination of PD Data‐Driven Subtypes

Using the percentiles calculated in the previous step, we applied the subtyping criteria defined by Fereshtehnejad et al, 15 as follows: DM‐PD was assigned if (1) the composite motor score and at least one non‐motor score was beyond the subtyping percentile or (2) all non‐motor scores were beyond the percentiles. MMP‐PD was defined as those patients with the composite motor and all non‐motor scores less than the percentile thresholds. IM‐PD was assigned to patients who did not fit either of the two former subtypes. Since the percentiles were disease‐duration specific, the study patients had multiple subtype assignments year‐by‐year (one subtype assignment for each year since PD diagnosis that data were available). The year‐specific cutoffs for each scale, including the composite motor z‐score threshold and raw 75th‐percentile values for the three motor components, were applied to assign each patient's subtype.

PPMI Disease‐Progression Milestones

We assessed PD progression using the 25 progression milestones previously established for the PPMI, which are organized into six domains: “walking and balance,” “motor complications,” “cognition,” “autonomic dysfunction,” “functional dependence,” and “activities of daily living.” 24 Each milestone represents a significant clinical event or marker of disability, chosen for its relevance in tracking disease progression. These milestones were systematically recorded at specific intervals, a quarterly assessment in the first year and semiannual follow‐ups thereafter. This structured milestone‐based framework was used to monitor both motor and non‐motor symptom progression in the PD subtypes. 24

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables, including MDS‐UPDRS subscores, MoCA, UPSIT, RBDSQ, SCOPA‐AUT, and age, were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical variables, such as sex, were summarized as frequencies and percentages. For group comparisons across the three PD subtypes (DM, IM, and MMP), we constructed linear regression models for continuous variables (as age, education years, and clinical scores) and logistic regression models for categorical variables (as sex) including PD subtype and disease duration at visit as predictors. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to compare the time to first occurrence of any disease progression milestone among the three PD subtypes. Log‐rank tests were used to assess differences in survival curves. A sensitivity analysis was also performed by excluding individuals who had met any progression milestone at baseline to ensure that results were not confounded by pre‐existing disability. All analyses were conducted using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA), Jamovi (version 2.6.2.0 for Mac OS), and Prism (version 10.5.0 for MacOS).

Comparison of the Clinical Characteristics of the Subtypes

Because disease duration at baseline was variable at the time of enrollment to the PPMI, we assigned each patient a disease duration‐specific subtype based on the year of enrollment in PPMI. Sufficient clinical score baseline data for 1 or 2 years from diagnosis were available to subtype 921 patients (DM = 173, IM = 398, and MMP = 350). The age at PD diagnosis and age at enrollment were significantly different between the three subtypes with DM‐PD having an older age (Table 2). No significant difference was observed in sex distribution or education level (by years). Time to Reach Disease Progression Milestones

Patients classified as DM‐PD at baseline had significantly faster time to reach disease progression milestones compared to the IM‐PD and MMP‐PD (Fig. 4A). Sensitivity analysis removing patients who met any progression milestones at baseline showed the same finding (Fig. 4B). We performed Cox proportional hazards regression to evaluate the association between baseline PD subtype (classified using disease‐duration–specific percentiles), baseline motor severity (MDS‐UPDRS part 3, off medications), and age at diagnosis with the time to reach progression milestones. In the multivariable model, using the MMP subtype as the reference, both IM and DM subtypes were associated with a significantly increased hazard of reaching progression milestones (IM subtype: HR 1.48, 95% CI [1.19–1.84], p < 0.001, and DM subtype: HR 3.03, 95% CI [2.30–3.97], p < 0.001). Age at diagnosis (HR 1.02 per year, 95% CI [1.01–1.03], p = 0.001) and baseline MDS‐UPDRS part 3 (off) score (HR 1.02 per point, 95% CI [1.01–1.03], p < 0.001) were each independently associated with a higher risk of disease progression. The model had a concordance index of 0.678, indicating moderate discrimination. The likelihood ratio test was significant (χ 2 = 151.8, df = 4, p < 0.001). Sensitivity Analyses

Of the 921 patients with a baseline subtype assignment, 226 (24.5%) had a Conclusion‐of‐Study record indicating they did not complete PPMI follow‐up. Dropout was numerically higher in IM‐PD (27.4%) than in DM‐PD (25.4%) or MMP‐PD (20.9%), but the overall association between baseline subtype and dropout did not reach statistical significance (χ 2 = 4.38, df = 2, p = 0.112; pairwise odds ratio for DM‐PD versus MMP‐PD = 1.29, 95% CI 0.84–1.99, p = 0.265). Among dropouts, the most frequently recorded reasons were non‐PD‐related health issues (n = 54), death (n = 46), and loss to follow‐up (n = 46); the full per‐reason breakdown by subtype is shown in Table S1. Because higher‐progressing DM‐PD patients are among those most likely to drop out, the observed Cox hazard ratios for DM‐PD versus MMP‐PD are, if anything, conservative estimates of the true subtype effect on progression.

An additional sensitivity analysis examined whether any single component of the PPMI motor‐complication domain was driving the subtype gradient by decomposing it into its three constituent endpoints: troublesome dyskinesia (MDS‐UPDRS items 4.1 and 4.2 > 3; m7), functional impact of motor fluctuations (item 4.4 > 3; m8), and complex motor fluctuations/off‐time (item 4.5 > 3; m9) (Table S2). The troublesome‐dyskinesia endpoint showed the largest point estimate for DM‐PD versus MMP‐PD (HR 10.25, 95% CI 1.20–87.75, p = 0.034) but with very wide confidence intervals reflecting its low absolute frequency in this untreated‐at‐enrollment cohort (13 events combined across the three subtypes). Neither the functional‐impact endpoint (HR 1.05, 95% CI 0.66–1.67, p = 0.838) nor the complex‐fluctuations endpoint (HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.52–1.45, p = 0.594) was significantly differentiated between subtypes. The composite PPMI motor‐complication domain therefore appears to derive its prognostic signal from the dyskinesia endpoint rather than from motor fluctuations per se, but this observation requires confirmation in longer‐followed cohorts where dyskinesia is more common.

Because the primary analysis derived the composite motor score from OFF‐medication MDS‐UPDRS Part III, we repeated the full subtyping pipeline using ON‐medication MDS‐UPDRS Part III to obtain a parallel set of year‐specific cutoffs and subtype prevalences (Table S3). OFF‐ and ON‐medication subtype assignments were concordant across disease years from Year 2 onward (linear‐weighted κ 0.63 to 0.84; Table S4), and ON‐state subtype assignments showed Year‐1‐to‐Year‐5 stability comparable to the OFF‐state analysis (linear‐weighted κ = 0.59; Table S5).

Because RBD‐positive status can be operationalized either as a year‐by‐year continuous severity score (the primary analysis) or as a qualitative baseline classification carried forward across follow‐up, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using the latter operationalization (Table S6–S8). When baseline RBD‐positive status (defined as Year‐1 RBDSQ greater than the Year‐1 75th‐percentile cutoff of 6.00) was locked at baseline and applied to all follow‐up years, subtype assignment changed for only a moderate fraction of patients (concordance with the primary annual reclassification gave linear‐weighted Cohen's κ in the range 0.43 to 0.92 across Years 1–10); Year 1–Year 5 stability was higher under the locked scheme (linear‐weighted κ = 0.64 versus 0.43 under the primary annual scheme); and the multivariable Cox hazard ratio for DM‐PD versus MMP‐PD was essentially unchanged (HR 2.71, 95% CI 1.86–3.94). This supports a locked‐baseline‐RBD operationalization for prospective disease‐modifying trial designs, which would be both practically simpler and statistically equivalent.

We additionally tested whether substituting two fixed, clinically established RBDSQ cutoffs for the within‐year 75th‐percentile threshold would alter the subtyping or its prognostic value (Tables S9–S11). Under both an RBDSQ ≥5 cutoff and a high‐specificity RBDSQ >6 cutoff, subtype assignment was concordant with the primary analysis at moderate to high rates (linear‐weighted κ range 0.46 to 0.91 for RBDSQ ≥5 and 0.55 to 0.92 for RBDSQ >6, across Years 1–10), and the multivariable Cox hazard ratio for DM‐PD versus MMP‐PD remained nearly identical to the primary analysis (HR 2.72, 95% CI 1.92–3.87 under RBDSQ ≥5; HR 2.71, 95% CI 1.86–3.94 under RBDSQ >6). These findings support the substitution of fixed clinical RBDSQ thresholds for percentile‐based thresholds in practice settings where same‐year reference data are unavailable.

Discussion

The initial work defining the DM, IM, and MMP subtypes used baseline data from the PPMI. Subsequent studies either applied the same percentile cutoff values or imputed the 75th subtyping percentiles retrospectively in their cohorts. Prospective clinical trials require disease duration‐specific percentile cutoffs for real‐time patient stratification. This study addresses this gap by determining disease duration‐specific percentiles for the motor and non‐motor scores required for data‐driven subtyping of PD (Table 4). Further, this work is focused on the first 5 years from PD diagnosis date, when disease‐modifying therapies are commonly tested. In this study, we observed an increase in the motor and non‐motor scores indicative of disease progression of the overall cohort. This progression was reflected in the increase of the 75th percentiles of motor and non‐motor scores and slight reduction in the 25th percentile of MoCA over time.

In our study, DM‐PD represented 19.2% to 20.5% of the population. Prior studies on early PD reported similar rates. In the original validation study by Fereshtehnejad et al, 15 52 out of 421 patients (12.4%) had DM‐PD. In a Dutch longitudinal cohort, 59 out of the 499 patients (12%) had DM‐PD. 17 The Queen Square Brain Bank autopsy study reported DM‐PD in 18 out of 111 patients (16.2%). 19 However, these studies did not subtype patients on an annual basis with reference to the disease diagnosis date. This study expands prior work by calculating subtyping thresholds annually from the time of diagnosis, allowing longitudinal stratification.

DM‐PD patients reached the PPMI progression milestones faster than other subtypes even after a sensitivity analysis removed those who already met any milestones at baseline. Consistent with our findings, the Queen Square Brain Bank autopsy study reported earlier development of milestones (HR 10.90; 95% CI, 5.51–21.58) and reduced survival (HR 3.65; 95% CI, 1.98–6.75) in the DM‐PD subtype compared to non‐DM‐PD. 19 However, they examined different disease milestones, including recurrent falls, wheelchair dependence, dementia, and care home placement. Our study examined the 25 PPMI progression milestones which span the six domains of “walking and balance,” “motor complications,” “cognition,” “autonomic dysfunction,” “functional dependence,” and “activities of daily living.” 24 , 25

While multiple subtyping models for PD have been proposed, ie, the clinical subtypes tremor‐dominant vs. PIGD, 26 , 27 the pathological subtypes brain first vs. body first, 28 , 29 and the recently described PACE and progression subtypes, 30 , 31 many lack subtype stability or are not easily applicable in clinical trial settings. The Fereshtehnejad model remains among the most pragmatic and widely validated data‐driven approaches, and our work enhances its utility by providing dynamic, year‐specific thresholds rather than static baseline categorization.

This study presents the first disease duration‐specific thresholds for the Fereshtehnejad data‐driven subtyping model using a large, well‐characterized cohort. By analyzing longitudinal data from the PPMI study, we provide relevant scale cutoffs that allow annual reassignment of subtypes from the time of diagnosis. These findings have important implications for both clinical practice and research. In clinical settings, disease duration‐specific subtyping may help identify patients at risk of faster progression who could benefit from closer monitoring, early interventions, or referrals to multidisciplinary care. For research, these thresholds provide a practical framework for enriching disease‐modifying trials with patients more likely to demonstrate treatment effects within short follow‐up periods. This is especially relevant given the historical challenges in demonstrating disease modification in PD due to its slow and heterogeneous progression.

Strengths and Limitations

A specific limitation of our subtyping approach concerns the longitudinal use of the REM Sleep Behavior Disorder Screening Questionnaire (RBDSQ) as a continuous severity measure. The RBDSQ was developed and validated as a screening tool for the presence or absence of RBD rather than as a graded rating scale, and prior work has not established a consistent relationship between RBDSQ score and PD prognosis once RBD is established. Several RBDSQ items capture nonspecific sleep disturbances (eg, insomnia and disrupted sleep maintenance) that may evolve due to causes other than RBD itself. In addition, as PD progresses and REM sleep architecture deteriorates, patients with established prodromal RBD may paradoxically report fewer dream‐enactment behaviors—not because their underlying synucleinopathy has improved, but because they generate fewer dreams to enact. The cumulative effect is that, at longer disease durations, increasing or decreasing RBDSQ scores may not faithfully track the clinical RBD phenotype, and the utility of percentile‐based RBDSQ cutoffs as a longitudinal subtype marker may attenuate. From a clinical standpoint, the most prognostically meaningful RBD classification is therefore qualitative (RBD‐positive versus RBD‐negative). For prospective subtyping in disease‐modifying trials, an alternative framework that warrants future evaluation is to lock RBD status at baseline (RBD‐positive if the patient meets clinical or polysomnographic criteria for RBD at study entry) and to carry that status forward across follow‐up, rather than re‐classifying RBD status annually using a continuous score. We retained the continuous RBDSQ cutoff in the present analysis to remain fully comparable with the original data‐driven subtyping framework, but future iterations of the model should incorporate this nuance.

The study utilizes data from the PPMI, a large cohort of early PD patients with homogenous standardized assessments. The use of PPMI's milestone‐based outcome framework allows robust comparisons of disease trajectories across subtypes. Additionally, the large sample size and multicenter design increase the generalizability of our findings within de novo idiopathic PD populations. Unlike prior studies, we did not use patient enrollment in the PPMI as time zero, we used PD diagnosis date as the index date to give motor and non‐motor score percentiles that are disease‐duration specific. This study has several limitations. First, the derived percentiles are specific to the PPMI cohort, which primarily includes de novo, medication‐naïve PD patients, potentially limiting generalizability to patients at more advanced disease stages or those with genetic variants. Second, although we assessed longitudinal changes and outcomes, the findings have not yet been validated in external cohorts. Third, our model relies on the availability of specific motor and non‐motor scales (eg, SCOPA‐AUT, RBDSQ, MoCA), which may not be consistently collected in other datasets or clinical settings. Fourth, our subtyping percentiles were limited to the first 5 years since PD diagnosis because of the smaller sample size in subsequent years. However, the restriction to the first 5 years from PD diagnosis is most relevant for enrollment in disease‐modification trials. Finally, while our percentile‐based subtyping reflects disease severity and predicts progression, it does not account for potential underlying biological differences between subtypes, which may be further clarified by future biomarker‐based studies.

Future Directions

Future studies should externally validate these percentiles in independent PD cohorts with longer follow‐up and greater racial, ethnic, and genetic diversity. Additionally, incorporating biological, imaging, and digital biomarkers into subtyping algorithms may help uncover mechanistic or biological underpinnings of the DM‐PD subtype and improve predictive accuracy. Ultimately, combining clinical trajectories with multimodal data could enable a precision medicine framework for PD.

Conclusions

This study provides disease duration‐specific thresholds for prospective PD subtyping using the Fereshtehnejad criteria. DM‐PD patients, defined by these criteria, exhibit faster disease progression and earlier milestone acquisition. These findings support the use of subtyping in stratified recruitment for disease‐modification trials. To support clinical operationalization, we provide year‐specific reference statistics (mean, SD, and percentile breakpoints) for all six subtyping scales as Table S12 and as a freely accessible interactive web calculator at https://negidamd.github.io/data-driven-pd-subtype-calculator/.
