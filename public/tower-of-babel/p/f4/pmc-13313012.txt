Antioxidant Therapy Reverses Sound Stress-Induced Opioid Resistance in a Mouse Model of Mechanical Allodynia
Authors: Satoka Kasai, Ayaka Furuta, Junpei Kuroda, Miho Takagi, Toshiki Sawai, Daisuke Yamada, Akiyoshi Saitoh, Kazumi Yoshizawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMechanical allodynia is associated with psychological stress, and environmental stimuli may exacerbate it in individuals with pain; however, the effects of analgesics on stress-induced mechanical allodynia remain unclear. Therefore, the present study aimed to determine whether oxidative stress contributes to opioid resistance development under stress-induced allodynia and to evaluate whether antioxidant therapy can restore opioid efficacy.MethodsSound stress was induced using the distress vocalizations of a mouse whose tail was pinched with an artery clip. Mice were subjected to a 4 h sound stress protocol and subsequently tested for mechanical allodynia using the von Frey test under control, sound-stress, inflammatory (CFA), and corresponding drug-treatment conditions (n = 6 per group). The effects of morphine (1 and 3 mg/kg; an opioid agonist), celecoxib (10 and 20 mg/kg; a selective COX-2 inhibitor), and N-tert-butyl-α-phenylnitrone (PBN; 75 and 150 mg/kg; a reactive oxygen species scavenger) were evaluated.ResultsSound stress induced mechanical allodynia (P < 0.01) but did not affect plasma prostaglandin E2 or corticosterone levels 24 h post-exposure. However, it increased the expression of oxidative stress-related genes in the brain (P < 0.05). Celecoxib and PBN each reduced mechanical allodynia (P < 0.05), whereas morphine did not. In mice with CFA-induced mechanical allodynia, sound stress reduced the analgesic efficacy of both morphine and celecoxib, but not PBN (P < 0.01). Morphine and PBN combined produced stronger analgesia than morphine alone at the same dose.ConclusionThese findings suggest that sound stress-induced hyperalgesia is mediated by oxidative stress and that antioxidants such as PBN are effective both as stand-alone treatments and adjuvants that restore or enhance the analgesic effect of morphine. The study highlights the role of physiological stress in pain modulation and underscores the therapeutic potential of antioxidant analgesics in stress-related mechanical allodynia.

Introduction

Pain sensitivity is influenced not only by physiological factors but also by psychological stress and environmental stimuli. For instance, mice exposed to restraint stress before spared nerve injury showed increased allodynia,1 and both healthy individuals and those with fibromyalgia experienced thermal pain sensitivity following acute psychosocial stress.2 These findings highlight the critical role of psychological stress in modulating pain hypersensitivity.

In addition to psychological stress, environmental stimuli including lights, natural scenes, sounds, videos, and virtual reality have been shown to modulate pain perception.3,4 For example, postoperative patients in bright hospital rooms required 22% less opioid-equivalent analgesic medication than those in dim rooms,5 and exposure to bright light improved pain sensitivity in patients with fibromyalgia.6 Natural sounds and visual stimuli also promote pain relief and enhance patients’ sense of control over their pain.7,8 However, certain environmental stimuli, such as intense light, can worsen pain.9 Thus, although environmental factors can be powerful modulators of pain, their effects can be bidirectional and context dependent.

In clinical settings, patients are often exposed to unavoidable mechanical and anthropogenic sounds, including noise, monitor beeps, and alarms from various medical devices, conversations among staff, and the cries or groans of other patients. Importantly, several clinical studies have demonstrated that noise exposure exacerbates pain perception in humans. For example, postoperative patients exposed to ward noise exhibit increased pain scores and greater use of analgesic medications.10 Similarly, high intraoperative noise levels have been shown to aggravate postoperative pain and increase postoperative analgesic requirements in patients undergoing abdominal surgery.11 These findings underscore the relevance of investigating how auditory stress affects mechanical allodynia and the efficacy of analgesics.

Psychological and environmental stress activates the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) and rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM), leading to enhanced sympathetic nerve activity and increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation via hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis activation, both in the vascular endothelium and the central nervous system.12–15 Oxidative stress enhances pain sensitivity and amplifies central sensitization by promoting neuroinflammation following peripheral nerve injury, dorsal horn neuron hyperexcitability, and mitochondrial dysfunction.16–21 Accordingly, antioxidant therapy has a theoretical basis for suppressing the pain cascade, and its adjunctive effects have been noted in oxidative stress-related disorders, such as diabetic neuropathy, fibromyalgia, and chronic inflammatory pain.22–24 However, the effects of antioxidants on oxidative stress-induced pain triggered by environmental factors remain unclear.

This study focused on sound stress as a clinically relevant environmental factor. We hypothesized that exposure to sound stress induces mechanical hypersensitivity and diminishes analgesic efficacy via an oxidative-stress-dependent mechanism. By scientifically characterizing pain responses to stress-inducing sounds commonly encountered in healthcare environments, this study aims to determine whether oxidative stress contributes to the development of opioid resistance under stress-induced pain-hypersensitivity conditions, and to evaluate whether antioxidant therapy can restore opioid analgesic efficacy in this model.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Morphine hydrochloride hydrate was purchased from Shionogi & Co. Ltd. (Osaka, Japan), whereas celecoxib and PBN were purchased from the Tokyo Chemical Industry (Tokyo, Japan) and Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), respectively. Morphine was prepared in saline and administered subcutaneously at doses of 1 or 3 mg/kg. Celecoxib was dissolved in a solution consisting of 5% dimethyl sulfoxide (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Co., Osaka, Japan), 5% Tween 80 (Tokyo Chemical Industry), and 90% saline and administered intraperitoneally at 10 or 20 mg/kg; this vehicle had previously been shown not to affect pain responses in mice.25 PBN was also dissolved in saline and administered intraperitoneally at 75 or 150 mg/kg. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was administered to control groups as an inactive placebo, and PBS injection alone did not affect pain responses. The selected doses for each compound were based on previously published protocols.26

Animals

Seven-week-old male C57BL/6N mice (~22g) were obtained from Japan SLC (Shizuoka, Japan) and used in all behavioral and molecular analyses. The animals were maintained at the Tokyo University of Science’s Institute for Experimental Animals under standardized laboratory conditions: room temperature of 23 ± 1 °C, a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 8:00 a.m.), and unrestricted access to food and water. Mice were housed in standard cages containing sawdust bedding. Mice were randomly allocated to experimental groups after confirming that they were healthy and of comparable body weight, without considering baseline data or other factors. The sample size for each group (n = 6) was determined based on previously published protocols.27 All experimental procedures complied with the national guidelines issued by the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology of Japan for the ethical treatment of animals in research institutions. The study protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Tokyo University of Science (Approval IDs: Y21036 and Y22028). To ensure animal welfare, surgeries were performed under isoflurane anesthesia, and every effort was made to reduce pain and discomfort in a dedicated procedure room within the animal experimentation facility at Tokyo University of Science. The room is maintained under clean and temperature-controlled conditions. Animals were anesthetized with isoflurane, and tissue collection was performed in accordance with the approved institutional animal care protocol.

Experimental Design

The overall experimental design and group composition are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. In brief, male C57BL/6N mice (7 weeks old) were randomly allocated to experimental groups according to the purpose of each experiment. The number and type of groups varied among analyses, ranging from two to four groups per experiment, with six animals in each group (n = 6), except for one treatment involving CFA-treated mice (n = 5).

Behavioral assessments using the von Frey test were conducted in control and sound stress mice. Depending on the experimental objective, analyses were performed using either two or four group comparisons including pre- and post-exposure conditions. Additional independent cohorts were used for serum redox analysis. The animals used for qPCR analysis represented a separate cohort that did not undergo any behavioral testing.

Drug effects under the Sound stress condition were evaluated using independent cohorts. The Sound stress + PBS group (n = 6) was shared among analyses evaluating different pharmacological interventions, whereas each drug-treated group was tested independently (n = 6 each). The Control group was shared as a reference group across analyses.

For the CFA-induced chronic inflammatory model, CFA and CFA + Sound stress groups (n = 5, 6, respectively) were used. Vehicle treated groups (n = 6 each) were shared across multiple pharmacological analyses, whereas each drug-treated group (Celecoxib, Morphine, and PBN) consisted of independent animals (n = 6), except for the 3 mg/kg morphine-treated CFA + sound stressgroup, which were also included in another comparison.

Recordings of Cries

Cry recordings were obtained by securing a single mouse in a restraint apparatus and applying a 4 cm artery clip to its tail to induce pain-related vocalizations. To minimize animal distress and adhere to ethical standards, we recorded the vocalization only once from a single mouse and reused this brief recording throughout all experiments. Sounds were captured using the UltraSoundGate system (Avisoft Bioacoustics, Nordbahn, Germany). For use as auditory stress stimuli in subsequent experiments, only the audible frequency components (<20 kHz) of the recorded vocalizations were extracted to model clinically relevant sound stress conditions.

The mice were placed in a soundproof box (240 × 385 × 260 mm, ISB-1, Melquest, Toyama, Japan) with cages (six mice per cage, 136 × 208 × 115 mm, CL-0113-4, CLEA Japan, Tokyo, Japan), and each sound stimulus was played repeatedly for 4 h using a linear PCM recorder (PCM-A10; Sony Co., Tokyo, Japan) at 80 dB volume.

Our purpose was to examine how clinically relevant stress-inducing sounds influence pain perception and analgesic efficacy. For this reason, using recordings of genuine vocalizations emitted by mice during a mild nociceptive stimulus was essential, rather than artificial sounds such as white noise, which do not convey emotional salience. To promote the 3Rs principle (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement), the recorded distress vocalizations have been deposited in the Figshare repository (https://figshare.com/s/5b9576349dc73ce28b7d) for reuse, allowing other researchers to reuse it and avoid additional animal experimentation.

von Frey Test

A separate cohort of naive mice, distinct from those used for cry recordings, underwent 4 h exposure to 80 dB sound stress (day 0) inside a soundproof chamber. Mechanical sensitivity was then assessed at 0, 30, and 60 min post-exposure. For the inflammatory pain model, 5 μL of complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA) was injected into the plantar region of the right hind paw on day 0. Another group of CFA-treated mice, also different from those used for cry recordings, was exposed to the same sound stress protocol on day 6, and tactile thresholds were measured one day later (day 7). Mechanical hypersensitivity and analgesic responses to sound stress were evaluated using von Frey filaments. Tactile threshold was assessed as previously described,25 using a calibrated series of von Frey filaments (0.07–2.0 g, Aesthesio®, DanMic Global, San Jose, CA, USA). The 50% withdrawal threshold was calculated based on the up–down method,28 beginning with a 0.6 g filament. If the animal was responded, a filament with lower-force was applied, and the sequence continued until a change in response was observed. Four additional stimuli were delivered following the up–down algorithm, and the 50% threshold was then calculated using established equations.29 Mechanical sensitivity was assessed using a fixed low-force von Frey filament (0.4 g) to the mid-plantar region of the inflamed hind paw, selected a priorito avoid ceiling effects observed with higher-force filaments (which elicited withdrawal responses in all animals). As 0.4 g is commonly considered an innocuous tactile stimulus in naïve mice, this readout operationally reflects mechanical allodynia (increased withdrawal responses to a non-noxious stimulus). As previously described,25 mice were placed individually in wire-mesh-floor plastic cages and allowed to habituate for 60 min. The filament was pressed perpendicularly against the plantar surface for 3–5 s with enough force to cause slight bending. A positive response was recorded if the mouse lifted its paw. Each paw was stimulated 10 times at 5 s intervals, and the number of responses was recorded.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Blood samples were collected from Control and Sound stress groups at two time points from independent animals: immediately before sound exposure (0 h) and 24 h after the exposure. The Control group was handled in the same manner but without sound exposure. The animals used for blood collection were not subjected to the von Frey test. Anesthesia was induced and maintained with isoflurane vapor delivered in oxygen at a nominal concentration of 2–3% (v/v). Isoflurane was applied onto a cotton pad placed in a small induction chamber, and mice inhaled the vapor until the desired anesthetic depth was reached. Blood samples were collected under deep anesthesia immediately before euthanasia from the abdominal aorta into tubes containing heparin-Na+ and centrifuged (1710 × g, 10 min, 4 °C) to obtain plasma. Plasma prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) and corticosterone concentrations were measured using a PGE2 enzyme immunoassay kit (Arbor assays, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) and corticosterone enzyme immunoassay kit (Arbor assays), according to the manufacturer’s instruction.

Redox Assay

The oxidation–reduction potential in the serum was analyzed using a Redox Analyzer (RedoxSYSTM, Aytu BioPharma, Englewood, CO, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR)

Brain tissues (prefrontal cortex [PFC], amygdala, thalamus, and medulla oblongata) were collected from mice 24 h after 4 h of sound exposure. Tissue collection was performed only in the Control and Sound stress groups, which did not receive CFA injection or von Frey test, to assess the central effects of sound stress independent of peripheral inflammation. Samples were removed from the mice and stored in Gene Keeper RNA and DNA stabilization solution (Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan). Total RNA was extracted using ISOSPIN Cell & Tissue RNA (Nippon Gene) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentrations were quantified using a Nanodrop LITE (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Complementary DNA was synthesized from total RNA using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT master mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Gene expression analysis was performed using Thunderbird Next SYBR qPCR mix (Toyobo) on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using standard curve analysis and normalized to β-Actin as an internal control. The primers used in this study are listed in Table 1. Statistical Analysis

Sample sizes were determined based on precedent set by our previous work.27 Using the pooled SD, the effect size was estimated as d ≈ 3.8; with n = 6 per group and α = 0.05 (two-tailed), the achieved power was >0.999, supporting the adequacy of our sample size.

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). In all datasets, we clarified that normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed before applying statistical analyses. For comparisons between two groups, Mann–Whitney U-test was used. For multiple group comparisons, two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. The QQ plot of the residuals showed that the points fell approximately along the reference line, indicating that the residuals follow a normal distribution. The plot of residuals versus predicted values showed no obvious pattern or heteroscedasticity. Furthermore, to ensure the robustness of our results against potential violations of sphericity in the repeated measures design, we applied the Geisser-Greenhouse correction to all calculated P-values (ε = 0.8885). Behavioral time-course data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test, while pharmacological intervention studies were analyzed using two-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically meaningful. In all figures, significance is denoted as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001. Results

Mice exposed to sound stress for 4 h exhibited mechanical allodynia at 0, 30, and 60 min (Figure 1A, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 vs control mice, two-way ANOVA post hoc Bonferroni test). No differences were observed between control and sound-stressed mice in plasma PGE2 (Figure 1B) or corticosterone (Figure 1C) at either 0 or 24 h post-exposure. A slight elevation in PGE2 levels was observed in the control group at 24 h; however, this variation was within the expected range of biological variability and did not reach statistical significance. No behavioral or inflammatory changes were detected in control animals, indicating that this minor fluctuation does not reflect a meaningful physiological effect. Similarly, serum levels of ROS and total antioxidative capacity were unchanged (Figure 1D and E).

To investigate changes in gene expression, qPCR was performed using the PFC, amygdala, thalamus, and medulla oblongata. Expression of oxidative stress-related genes was significantly upregulated in the brains of sound-stressed mice (Figure 1F–I, Superoxide dismutase 1 [Sod1], P < 0.05, in the PFC, amygdala, and thalamus, P < 0.01, in the medulla oblongata; Catalase [Cat], P < 0.05, in the PFC, thalamus, and medulla oblongata, P < 0.01, in the amygdala; Glutathione peroxidase 1 [Gpx1], P < 0.05 in the thalamus; Heme oxygenase 1 [Ho1], P < 0.05, in the amygdala and medulla oblongata). The c-Fos expression, as a marker of activated neurons, was also significantly increased in the amygdala of mice exposed to sound stress (P < 0.01). Additionally, Cyclooxygenase2 (Cox2) and cAmp-response element binding protein (Creb) were significantly increased in the PFC of mice exposed to sound stress (Cox2, P < 0.01, in the amygdala and thalamus; Creb, P < 0.01 in the PFC).

The effects of analgesics on sound-induced mechanical allodynia were then examined. Morphine (1 or 3 mg/kg) had no effect (Figure 2A), whereas celecoxib (20 mg/kg) and PBN (150 mg/kg) significantly recovered sound stress-induced mechanical allodynia (Figure 2B and C; #P < 0.05, ##P < 0.01, ###P < 0.001 vs sound stress + PBS group, two-way ANOVA post hoc Dunnett’s test).

To evaluate the interaction of sound stress and inflammation, CFA was injected into the right hind paw (plantar surface) of the mice. On day six post-injection, mice were exposed to 4 h of sound stress, and mechanical allodynia was assessed the following day using the von Frey test (Figure 3A). Both CFA-treated mice and those subjected to CFA plus sound stress exhibited significantly reduced withdrawal thresholds on day 7 (Figure 3B; ****P, < 0.0001 vs day 0; CFA + sound stress-treated mice on day 7 ####P, < 0.0001 vs day 0; Bonferroni test), with no significant differences in mechanical allodynia between CFA-treated mice and those exposed to sound stress. Administration of morphine (1 and 3 mg/kg) significantly alleviated CFA-induced mechanical allodynia (Figure 3C; #P < 0.05, ##P < 0.01, ####P < 0.0001 vs CFA + PBS group, two-way ANOVA post hoc Dunnett’s test), but only 3 mg/kg morphine affected mechanical allodynia induced by CFA and sound stress (Figure 3D; ##P < 0.01, ###P < 0.001 vs CFA + PBS group, two-way ANOVA post hoc Dunnett’s test). Administration of 10 and 20 mg/kg celecoxib also significantly recovered CFA-induced mechanical allodynia (Figure 3E, #P < 0.05, ##P < 0.01 vs CFA + PBS group, two-way ANOVA post hoc Dunnett’s test), but no effect was observed in mice subjected to CFA and sound stress (Figure 3F). The administration of PBN (150 mg/kg) significantly reduced mechanical allodynia in both mice injected with CFA and those exposed to sound stress (Figure 3G and H, ##P < 0.01, ###P < 0.001 vs CFA + PBS group, two-way ANOVA post hoc Dunnett’s test). The combined administration of morphine (3 mg/kg) and PBN (75 mg/kg) significantly improved mechanical allodynia in mice injected with CFA and exposed to sound stress (Figure 3I, ##P < 0.001 vs CFA + sound stress + PBS group, two-way ANOVA post hoc Dunnett’s test).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that sound-stress exposure induces mechanical allodynia, and that administration of the free radical scavenger PBN ameliorates this effect. Notably, PBN reversed allodynia in mice with CFA-induced inflammation and enhanced the analgesic effects of morphine. These results suggest that sound stress may be linked to increased oxidative stress and that antioxidant intervention may help restore analgesic efficacy.

Several studies have supported the relationship between sound exposure and oxidative stress. For example, white noise exposure disrupts the redox balance in the rat hippocampus39 and impairs memory formation together with changes in oxidative stress-related gene expression.40 In our study, the absence of an increase in plasma PGE2 levels following acoustic stress exposure (Figure 1B), together with the lack of changes in plasma oxidative stress markers (Figure 1C and D), suggests that sound stress-induced mechanical allodynia is not mediated by systemic alterations in prostaglandin signaling or redox balance. Furthermore, the brains of mice exposed to sound stress exhibited significant upregulation of various oxidative stress response genes, such as Sod1, Cat, Gpx-1, and Ho-1 (Figure 1F–I). Because corticosterone levels increase immediately after sound exposure in rats,41 peripheral oxidative stress may have been transiently elevated at the early phase of exposure. However, our findings suggest that peripheral oxidative stress is not directly involved in the development of mechanical allodynia; rather, oxidative stress within the central nervous system appears to play a critical role in mediating sound stress-induced mechanical allodynia.

Oxidative stress is defined as an imbalance between the production of oxidants and antioxidant defenses.42 The observed upregulation of antioxidant enzyme genes such as Sod1, Cat, Gpx-1, and Ho-1 suggests a compensatory response to the increased oxidative burden. In particular, HO-1 degrades heme to carbon monoxide, iron, and biliverdin, all of which possess antioxidant properties, and HO-1 is also a transcriptional target of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2), a key redox-sensitive transcription factor that translocates to the nucleus under oxidative stress and activates several antioxidant enzyme genes.43,44 Although Nrf2 mRNA expression levels did not change in the brains of mice exposed to sound stress, the increase in Ho-1 mRNA expression implied that the Nrf2 protein may have translocated into the nucleus and was transcriptionally active.

These findings suggest upregulation of the oxidative stress response in brain regions involved in pain perception and emotional processing, such as the amygdala. Indeed, we also observed increased expression of c-Fos, an activated neuronal marker, and its transcriptional regulator Creb, in the amygdala. These molecules are typically associated with neuronal activation related fear and anxiety responses.45 Fear stimuli promote neuroinflammation46 and induce oxidative stress-related gene expression.47 Therefore, the enhanced oxidative stress response observed in this study may be mediated, at least in part, through fear-related neural circuits, such as the amygdala. Moreover, oxidative stress induces neuronal hyperexcitability within the central nervous system,48 suggesting that such hyperexcitability could contribute to increased pain sensitivity and mechanical allodynia development.

When mice with CFA-induced inflammation were exposed to sound stress, the von Frey thresholds decreased to a measurably low limit, and the analgesic effects of morphine and celecoxib were attenuated compared with those observed in mice treated with CFA alone (Figure 3B–F). These findings suggest that sound stress diminishes the analgesic efficacy of morphine and celecoxib under inflammatory conditions. As indicated in Figure 1F–I, this phenomenon is likely associated with an increase in oxidative stress within the brain.

In contrast, under sound stress exposure alone, morphine did not exhibit analgesic efficacy against mechanical allodynia, whereas celecoxib did (Figure 2B). This discrepancy can be explained by the differences in their pharmacological mechanisms. Morphine exerts its analgesic effects by binding to μ-opioid receptors located along the ascending nociceptive pathway, thereby inhibiting pain transmission.49 However, increased oxidative stress in the brain induces neuronal hyperexcitability in the central nervous system,48 and such sound stress-induced central hyperexcitability, independent of the ascending pathway, may account for the lack of morphine efficacy in non-inflammatory conditions and its reduced effect under inflammatory conditions. The restoration of the diminished analgesic effect by PBN co-administration (Figure 3I) supports this hypothesis. These findings indicate that oxidative stress induced by sound exposure contributes to the reduced analgesic efficacy of morphine.

Clinically, this study suggests that the concomitant use of antioxidants may enhance the efficacy of opioids in patients experiencing sound stress-induced pain hypersensitivity. This therapeutic approach represents a potentially useful strategy to improve pain management while avoiding unnecessary escalation of opioid dosage. In humans, environmental noise exposure influences both pain intensity and perception; high levels of environmental noise in hospital wards or operating rooms increase postoperative pain and analgesic consumption.10,11 Future studies are needed to determine whether other antioxidants can reproduce the effect observed with PBN, namely the restoration of the analgesic efficacy of morphine attenuated by sound stress, or whether similar outcomes can be achieved under different stress models. Furthermore, clinical studies will be essential to verify whether such antioxidant co-therapy can improve opioid responsiveness in patients under stress conditions. Nevertheless, our findings suggest that strategies such as combining antioxidant therapy with analgesics or reducing environmental noise exposure may help alleviate stress-induced pain resistance.

To clarify our selection of analgesic agents, celecoxib was chosen instead of a non-selective COX1/2 inhibitor such as ketorolac for the following reasons. First, non-selective COX inhibitors strongly inhibit COX1, which can influence gastrointestinal, renal, and platelet function even after a single administration, potentially altering pain behavior independently of sound stress. Second, our study aimed to compare analgesics with primarily central mechanisms of action (morphine and PBN). Celecoxib exhibits partial brain penetrance and central COX2 inhibition, allowing a mechanistically consistent comparison across drugs acting on central pathways.50,51 Finally, the use of a COX2 selective inhibitor minimized confounding peripheral effects and enabled a clear evaluation of the interaction between sound stress, inflammation, and oxidative stress-related pain modulation.

Celecoxib, in addition to its COX2 inhibitory action, possesses intrinsic antioxidant properties.52,53 Celecoxib attenuates oxidative stress and enhances the activity of antioxidant enzymes, such as glutathione and superoxide dismutase (SOD).53 Therefore, celecoxib may have alleviated allodynia induced by sound stress through its antioxidant effects within the central nervous system. However, in the presence of inflammation, although celecoxib has been reported to exhibit brain permeability,50,51 excessive oxidative stress and COX2 overexpression may have overwhelmed the antioxidant and COX2 inhibitory capacities of celecoxib. These factors may explain why celecoxib lost its anti-allodynic efficacy when sound stress was combined with CFA-induced inflammation. Given the potential antioxidant properties of celecoxib, co-administration of celecoxib with morphine could have enhanced the attenuated analgesic efficacy of morphine observed under inflammatory conditions with sound stress exposure. This possibility warrants further investigation in future studies.

In this study, we limited the acoustic stimulation to the human-audible frequency range (<20 kHz) to model clinically relevant sound stress conditions, such as noises commonly encountered in hospital environments. Although mice can perceive audible and ultrasonic frequencies used for intraspecies communication,54,55 the present study focused on auditory stimuli within the frequency range perceptible to humans. In our previous study using ultrasonic pain-related vocalizations,56 such sounds were also found to enhance pain sensitivity and prolong pain duration under inflammatory conditions, suggesting that both audible and ultrasonic frequency ranges can modulate pain perception in mice. Therefore, the present findings support the concept that auditory stress, regardless of frequency range, can influence pain pathways. However, whether ultrasonic pain-related vocalizations also induce oxidative stress responses, and the specific molecular mechanisms underlying these effects, remain to be elucidated.

Exposure to ultrasonic pain-related vocalizations enhances pain sensitivity and prolongs inflammation-induced pain in mice,56 suggesting that neuroinflammation occurs in the hypothalamus under such conditions. Therefore, hypothalamic inflammation also developed in the present study using audible pain-related sounds, may have contributed to the enhanced pain sensitivity observed. In addition, neurotransmitter systems play crucial roles in pain modulation, but we did not examine potential changes in neurotransmitter levels or the involvement of the endocannabinoid system in this study. These remain important issues for future research.

Moreover, whether the observed effects were specific to pain-related sounds within the audible range, or whether similar responses would be elicited by other sound types with comparable acoustic characteristics, remains uncertain. The contribution of emotional salience to the observed pain enhancement also remains to be clarified. Future studies comparing different categories of sounds and evaluating their emotional relevance will be essential to address these limitations.

Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, although we observed increased expression of oxidative stress-related genes, direct biochemical evidence of oxidative damage, such as lipid peroxidation or protein carbonylation, was not assessed. Second, we did not measure oxidative stress-related gene expression or PGE2 levels after drug administration or CFA injection. Therefore, whether the antioxidant (PBN) or analgesics (celecoxib and morphine) directly modulate oxidative stress pathways in the brain remains unclear. Future studies are warranted to determine whether pharmacological treatments can reverse the molecular alterations induced by sound stress or inflammation. Third, although changes in gene expression were analyzed in the specific brain regions, we did not perform region-specific quantification of oxidative stress or neuronal activity, which may provide detailed insights. Forth, the involvement of the amygdala and the role of fear in mediating oxidative stress and pain responses remain speculative and require direct validation using targeted approaches, such as pharmacological inhibition or region-specific genetic manipulation. Fifth, this study was conducted using only male mice. Previous studies have reported sex differences in both pain sensitivity and stress responses,57 which may involve distinct neuroendocrine and immune mechanisms.58 Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted with caution regarding their generalizability across sexes, and future studies should include female mice to clarify potential sex-specific differences. Finally, this study was not performed under fully blinded conditions owing to limited research personnel, although reproducibility was verified by repeated measurements and confirmation by multiple experimenters. This partial lack of blinding may have introduced experimenter bias and should be considered a limitation of the present study. Moreover, future studies should focus on clarifying the cellular sources and localization of oxidative stress, elucidating the causal relationship between stress-induced emotional responses and mechanical allodynia, and exploring the therapeutic potential of other antioxidant compounds. Investigating whether similar mechanisms occur under chronic stress or in human conditions could help translate these findings into clinical pain management strategies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings indicate that sound stress-induced mechanical allodynia is mediated, at least in part, by oxidative stress mechanisms. Antioxidant (PBN) administration reversed behavioral hypersensitivity and restored the analgesic efficacy of morphine under these conditions. These findings suggest that our findings provide a rationale for exploring antioxidant-based therapies as a means to improve pain management in stressful clinical environments.
