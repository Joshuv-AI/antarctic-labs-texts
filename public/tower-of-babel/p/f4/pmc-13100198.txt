Stem cells resume asymmetric division upon niche re-entry through reactivating the centrosome orientation checkpoint
Authors: Muhammed Burak Bener, Autumn Twillie, Naiya Patel, Mayu Inaba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In Drosophila testis, germline stem cells (GSCs) typically divide asymmetrically, keeping one daughter cell within the niche for self-renewal and placing the other daughter cell outside the niche to undergo differentiation. This is the primary mechanism for maintaining a stable number of stem cells in the niche. However, it has been shown that small fraction of GSCs continuously leave the niche, necessitating an additional mechanism to compensate for this loss and maintain a constant number of GSCs. Dedifferentiation and symmetric renewal have been shown to replenish lost GSCs during regenerative and physiological conditions. However, it remains unknown whether dedifferentiated GSCs reacquire native GSC behaviors. Using long-term live imaging, we trace the cells following dedifferentiation and show that dedifferentiated GSCs appear to reacquire at least one aspect of native GSC behavior, the centrosome orientation checkpoint, a GSC-specific checkpoint mechanism that ensures oriented spindle to achieve asymmetric stem cell divisions. Our findings provide important insights into the robust mechanisms ensuring asymmetric division in the niche.

Introduction

Stem cells play a vital role for tissue maintenance by continuously producing differentiating cells. The maintenance of stem cells throughout life is therefore critical to organismal health, and the loss of stem cells underlies many pathological conditions, including tissue degeneration or declining tissue functions in physiological aging1–3. The Drosophila testis serves as an excellent model system to study stem cell maintenance in vivo, offering unambiguous identification of stem cells, their differentiating progenies, and supporting cells4. In each testis, 8–12 germline stem cells (GSCs) are located around a single cluster of niche cells called the hub5,6. The GSCs usually divide asymmetrically by forming perpendicularly oriented spindles toward the hub-GSC interface7–11. As a result, one daughter cell remains adjacent to the hub, and the other daughter cell is displaced from the hub to become a gonialblast (GB)7. The GB then undergoes four rounds of transit amplifying cell divisions (2-to-16-cell spermatogonia, or SGs), and the 16-cell SGs become spermatocytes, enter two rounds of meiotic divisions, and ultimately give rise to mature sperm12.

In GSCs, asymmetric division is carefully regulated by a multilayered mechanism. Wild-type GSCs are polarized and rarely form misoriented spindles following the precise centrosome positioning in interphase. After the centrosome duplication in S-phase, the mother centrosome remains anchored near the hub-GSC interface, while the daughter centrosome migrates to the opposite pole. This centrosome positioning ensures the mitotic spindle forms along the correct axis7,8. Several systemic factors, such as aging and starvation, disrupt this precise centrosome positioning, increasing the frequency of centrosome misorientation in GSCs9,13. Despite this increased centrosome misorientation, the frequency of observed spindle misorientation during mitosis remains low9,13. This observation led to the discovery of a GSC-specific “centrosome orientation checkpoint” (COC) that acts as a surveillance mechanism, monitoring the centrosome positioning during interphase, prior to the mitosis11,14–16. If the COC detects misoriented centrosomes, it arrests the cell cycle in the late G2 phase until centrosomes are re-oriented16. Several factors have been implicated in the COC pathway. E-cadherin, which anchors GSCs to the hub, localizes specifically to the hub-GSC interface and provides polarity cues to the GSC15. The polarity protein Bazooka/Par-3 (Baz) acts as a downstream factor of E-cadherin11. Baz forms a small “patch”-like structure at the hub-GSC interface. This Baz-patch serves as a docking site for the apical centrosome, with an increased frequency of Baz-centrosome docking observed in the late G2 phase of the cell cycle. The proper capturing of apical centrosome by Baz-patch is monitored by COC mechanism11. Ectopic or precocious centrosome capturing by Baz causes COC clearance and allows mitosis to proceed, resulting in spindle misorientation11.

While asymmetric stem cell division can maintain a stable number of GSCs, GSC loss also occurs at a certain frequency, and thus, compensatory mechanisms are required to replenish the pool9,17–21. Previous studies suggested that “symmetric-renewal” and “dedifferentiation” are the mechanisms for replenishing lost stem cells18. Symmetric renewal in which both daughter cells of a GSC division become two GSCs, occurs following GSC division, and both of the daughter cells stay attached to the hub18,22,23. The same study also identified another mechanism called “dedifferentiation,” where differentiated cells migrate back to the niche and regain stem cell characteristics18.

To understand how tissues maintain a proper number of stem cells throughout an organism’s lifetime, it is essential to understand these mechanisms. However, because original and replenished GSCs are morphologically indistinguishable, the mechanism that allows differentiating cells to reenter the niche remains unclear and challenging to assess using fixed samples. In this study, we adapted a long-term live imaging methodology that enabled us to monitor the behavior of stem cells following these replenishing events. Specifically, we found that dedifferentiated GSCs rapidly reorient their centrosomes and resume mitosis after reattachment to the niche. We uncover evidence suggesting that dedifferentiated cells are likely arrested in G2 by reactivating COC prior to niche re-entry, thereby ensuring spindle orientation at the subsequent mitotic division. These results provide insight into the cellular regulatory mechanism that enables dedifferentiating cells to reenter the niche as functional GSCs. Given parallels in regulatory paradigms between the testis system and other stem cell systems, these findings could have broader relevance to understanding stem cell niche maintenance mechanisms.

Results

Lost GSCs are replenished through several mechanisms in the Drosophila testicular niche

To understand how stem cells behave to maintain homeostasis, we conducted long-term live imaging to monitor the niche, including 4–5 GSCs on average, GBs, and early differentiating SGs. By utilizing the GaAsP detector, which minimizes phototoxicity, we successfully obtained a high-resolution time-lapse series without photo-bleaching for ~16 h, while acquiring z-stacks (10–15 optical sections with a z-interval of 1.5–2 µm) every 10 min (see Methods). Additionally, we used the preset tiling function for the automated acquisition of 10–15 testes in the same imaging session.

With this condition, we obtained a total of ~1400 h of imaging of the steady-state niche using several different markers (Fig. 1A). In these live imaging experiments, GSCs divided every ~14 h on average, close to what has been estimated previously23,24. We observed that cell divisions occurred consistently throughout the 16-hour imaging period with no detectable changes in frequency, indicating that the tissue remained healthy for the entire imaging period (Table S1, Movie S1). The estimated cell cycle time was 14.29 h on average, as 480 mitosis were observed in a total of 6860 h of monitoring (1372 h recorded for the niche containing an average of 5 GSCs). This length is consistent with the previous report18. In this study, we employed multiple fluorescent markers across separate lines to monitor events occurring in the niche, thereby ensuring that different fluorescently tagged proteins do not artificially influence the results. GFP-αTubulin (αTub) was expressed under the germline-specific driver, nanos (nos)-Gal4, to visualize the mitotic spindle8 (Figs. 1, 2 Table S1-S3). We use fly-FUCCI cell cycle reporter expressing lines (Table S4, Fig. 3). We used membrane-bound GFP (myristoylated- or myr-GFP) directly under the nos promoter (nos-myrGFP, generated in this study) to visualize the cell cortex (Fig. 1, Figs. S1, S2, Table S5, S6). Further to test the consistency with previous studies, we also included the nosGal4-driven UAS-GMA (Expresses the actin-binding domain of moesin tagged with GFP, used in ref. 18) (Fig. S3A), as a bright cell cortex marker. Sqh-mCherry (driven by its own promoter, used in refs. 22,23, Fig. S3B-D) was also used in combination with nos-myrGFP as a marker for the midbody located between GSC and GB pair before cytokinesis, thus we can use it for identifying “symmetric renewal”. Using these lines, GSCs were readily identified as GFP-αTub, myr-GFP- or GMA-positive cells surrounding the hub.

Using these experimental settings, we conducted live observations and analyzed the patterns of GSC divisions. As expected, asymmetric division occurred most frequently, consistently seen in ~70% of the total observed events across different markers (Fig. 1B, Movie S2–3). Symmetric renewal was also observed infrequently but at a detectable frequency (~5%, Fig. 1A), similar to the frequency observed in previous report18. Such ‘symmetric renewal’ events were sometimes associated with misoriented spindle. In certain cases, with GFP-αTub expressing GSCs, we observed their mitotic spindles were formed parallel to the hub-GSC interface, and both daughter cells remained in the niche (Fig. 1C, left, Movie S4), as reported previously18. Other times, the symmetric renewal was observed after the formation of oriented spindles. In such cases the daughter cell, once displaced from the hub, swiveled back while still connected to the GSC and reattached to the hub, as observed by Sheng et al.18 (Fig. 1C, right, Movie S5).

Consistent with the previous reports18,22, we barely observed dedifferentiation events from SG stages (2-cell or 4-cell SG stages, Movie S6, Fig. S1). However, we noticed unexpected and striking events of dedifferentiation from single GBs: ~10% (Fig. 1A) of the time, a GB, after being pinched off from the GSCs, moved back and regained attachment to the hub (Fig. 1D, Movies S7–8, Fig. S2, Movie S9). After reattachment, all detected dedifferentiated cells remained attached to the hub for over 10-time frames (100 min), suggesting that dedifferentiation from GB is undoubtedly contributing to stem cell replenishment.

This class of dedifferentiation (from GB) is clearly distinguishable from the “symmetric renewal” in which replenishment occurs from GBs while they are still connected to GSCs, and has never been seen in previous studies. We observed such dedifferentiation events consistently throughout the imaging period, indicating that long-term imaging did not impact the dedifferentiation potential (Table S1). Importantly, this class of event would have been impossible to detect in fixed samples, because GB and GSCs are morphologically indistinguishable, with the only distinction being their attachment to the hub. Moreover, in previous live imaging studies, dedifferentiation from GB was underestimated because it was either not distinguished from the symmetric renewal cases22 or can be hard to distinguish in 2D imaging18.

Notably, the different results observed among studies are unlikely to be caused by the usage of different genotypes. We tested a range of fluorescent markers, including genotypes used in previous studies, such as nosGal4 > UAS-GMA used in Sheng et al.18, Fig. S3A, Movie S10), and Sqh-mCherry (used in Roach et al.22, Fig. S3B, Movie S11). We continued to observe the dedifferentiation events from post-cytokinetic GB stage in which midbody remnant is no longer present (Fig. S3C, Movie S12). This stage was clearly distinguishable from symmetric self-renewal cases (Fig. S3D, Movie S13). Note that there is a variation of the frequency of dedifferentiation ranging 6–17% (averaging to ~10% across all lines, Fig. 1A), likely because of the low sample size we can obtain from each live imaging session.

Consistent with the frequency of niche gain events, including symmetric renewal and dedifferentiation, loss events were also observed. It was often seen immediately after the division, where both daughter cells differentiate and leave the niche (symmetric differentiation, Fig. 1E, Movies S14–15) and also seen without link to the division event (defined simply as “loss”, Fig. 1F, Movies S16–17). Interestingly, for all observed loss events without associated divisions (n = 37 total), we failed to detect previous mitosis. This may indicate that the “loss” occurs in senescent cells or in cells at a certain cell cycle stage (such as G2), which is why we could not capture the last mitosis within 16-hour of the live session.

Dedifferentiation rate increases during niche regeneration

Previous works demonstrated that dedifferentiation occurs frequently under regenerative conditions17–19. Therefore, we also expanded our analyses to a regeneration condition where we imaged niche replenishment after forcibly depleting GSCs using a previously established method17. In this method, the niche is depleted by expressing the differentiation factor bam under the control of the heat-shock (hs) promoter. After 6 repeated cycles of heat shock, the majority of GSCs leave the niche, resulting in an average number of GSCs of ~2 GSCs /testis (n = 41), as compared to ~9/testis (n = 104) in the control group without heat-shock treatment. Following the last heat-shock treatment, flies are returned to room temperature to allow regeneration of the niche (hereafter, defined as “regeneration phase”). During this phase, the GSC number recovers within 5 to 7 days through dedifferentiation and becomes comparable to the niche before heat-shock treatment17. We started recordings from a 32-h to a 48-h time point after the last heat shock, where dedifferentiation events were most frequently observed in a previous report18.

At the start of imaging, the average number of GSCs was ~6 per testis (n = 23), as compared to an average of ~10 GSCs per testis (n = 30) in steady-state. Notably, we still detected asymmetric division at the highest frequency even after GSCs were depleted (3/16 h on average, Tables S1, S2, Fig. S4, Movie S18). As reported previously18, we observed a slight increase in both “gain” events (0.4/16-h of symmetric renewal, 1/16-h of dedifferentiation on average, Fig. 2A, B, Movie S19). The change in the rate of symmetric renewal from steady-state condition was not statistically significant (p = 0.24 Fisher’s exact test). In contrast, dedifferentiation rate (23 events per 23 recordings, Table S2) was significantly increased (20 events per 30 recordings, Table S1) (p < 0.0001, Fisher’s exact test), indicating that differentiating daughters are responsive to niche occupancy status and are likely induced to dedifferentiate as a consequence of the GSC loss. Dedifferentiated cells are in late G2 phase

While analyzing the live recordings, we realized that almost all detected dedifferentiated GSCs undergo mitosis with correctly oriented spindles after reattachment to the hub. In the recovery phase from GSC depletion, all observed 21 dedifferentiated GSCs were divided with correct orientation (Table S1). Similarly, 15 out of 16 divisions of dedifferentiated GSCs in steady-state conditions were correctly oriented (Table S2). No significant differences were detected in the frequency of spindle orientation between dedifferentiated GSCs and native GSCs under steady-state conditions (Table S1, p ≥ 0.05, Fisher’s exact test). The recovery of correct spindle orientation is consistent with that observed in a previous study in which dedifferentiated GSCs were distinguished by genetic marking9. However, we unexpectedly observed that the time between niche reattachment and the subsequent mitotic division was remarkably short in dedifferentiated GSCs (Fig. 3A, B). If we assume that dedifferentiation can randomly occur during any phase of the cell cycle, we would expect the time between reattachment and the 1st mitotic entry (see ”Methods” for defining the reattachment point) to be randomly distributed across ~14 h, the cycle length of male GSCs as estimated in previous works23,24. We observed that the mitotic entry of native GSCs already present in the niche at the start of imaging showed random distribution throughout the time of imaging periods (the average time to mitosis was 7.61 ± 4.39 h n = 128 in the steady-state phase, 6.8 ± 4.16 h, n = 59, in the regeneration phase) (Fig. 3A, B, Tables S1, 2). In contrast, the average time between hub reattachment and the 1st mitotic entry of dedifferentiated GSCs was drastically short in most of observed “dedifferentiation” cases (2.29 ± 1.88 h, n = 16 in the steady-state, 2.1 h ± 1.72, n = 21 in the regeneration) (Fig. 3A, B, Tables S1, 2).Fig. 3Dedifferentiated cells are in the late G2 phase.A, B Each dot represents one traced cell in long-term live imaging, the cell undergone dedifferentiation event (dediff. It must be noted that we also attempted to follow native GB behavior as a control because majority of dedifferentiation was observed from GBs. However, tracing GB throughout the imaging periods was found to be challenging because they are not tethered to the hub. Based on the previous study, GBs likely have similar rate of cell cycle to GSCs’24 Therefore, we used the data from native GSCs as the control instead of GBs.

The observation that dedifferentiated GSCs promptly undergo mitosis upon reentering the niche suggested two possibilities: first, dedifferentiated GSCs have a much shorter cell cycle than original GSCs whose cell cycle time has been estimated as ~14 h23,24; second, dedifferentiated GSCs are biased for a specific cell cycle phase. If dedifferentiated GSCs have a short cell cycle, we should observe the next round of mitosis again soon after the 1st division. To test this possibility, we followed dedifferentiated cells whose 1st division occurred within ~7 h from the start of imaging so that we could follow their behavior for at least ~9 more hours. We did not detect any of these cells undergoing 2nd cell division in the rest of the recording period (Table S3), suggesting that dedifferentiated GSCs are unlikely to have a shorter cell cycle than native GSCs.

To test the second possibility that dedifferentiated GSCs are in a specific cell cycle phase, we conducted live imaging during the recovery phase using Fly-FUCCI (fluorescent ubiquitination-based cell cycle indicator)25 expressed under the nosGal4 driver. We observed that at the moment of niche reattachment, all observed dedifferentiated GSCs were in G2 phase (n = 13) (Fig. 3C, Movie S20, Table S4), indicating that dedifferentiated cells are biased to be in a specific cell cycle phase in late G2 at the moment of dedifferentiation.

Centrosome orientation checkpoint is reactivated in dedifferentiated cells prior to reattachment to the hub

Next, we wondered if the observed G2 phase bias has any functional correlation to the dedifferentiation machinery or just a coincidence or consequence of unrelated factors. It is known that GBs remain attached to GSCs for several hours after mitosis due to delayed abscission18,23. If dedifferentiating cells are selected from single GBs after abscission, the G2 phase bias may be simply due to this stage preference. However, we observed the short time between hub reattachment and mitotic entry not only in steady-state conditions where single GB is almost the sole source of the dedifferentiation, but also seen in regeneration phases, even after complete depletion of the GSCs, where mixed stage of cells are the source of the dedifferentiation17, indicating that the cell cycle bias is unlikely reflecting stage preference and cell type availability. Instead, these observations suggest the possibility of a functional link between cell cycle regulation and the dedifferentiation machinery.

Moreover, our time-to-mitosis score (Fig. 3A, B) shows a narrow time distribution toward the end of the G2, with its peak at around two hours (the average time after reattachment to mitosis was 137.5 min in steady-state or 126.4 min in regeneration) relative to the entire G2 phase length (estimated as 8–9 h, shown in Fig. 3A, B), indicating that the dedifferentiating cells are in narrow window of late G2 phase. This cell cycle bias is reminiscent of the late G2 arrest caused by the centrosome orientation checkpoint (COC), observed explicitly in stem cell populations7,8. Since our observations indicate that dedifferentiating cells are likely in the late G2 phase, we hypothesized that COC may arrest these cells until their niche re-entry. The COC monitors centrosome orientation in the GSCs and arrests the cell cycle in late G2 if centrosomes are misoriented to ensure proper asymmetric cell division (Fig. 4B). A previous study using ex vivo treatment of testes with the microtubule depolymerizing drug colcemid showed that COC is specifically active in GSCs but not in differentiating cells (GBs and SGs)16. Cells without COC arrest in metaphase when treated with colcemid and exhibit the mitotic cell marker phospho-histone H3 (PH3), whereas cells with active COC arrest at the late G2 phase (no PH3 staining) when treated with colcemid16. Therefore, the frequency of PH3-positive cell reflects the absence or presence of an active COC16.

Consistent with the previous study16, GB and SG populations show higher PH3-positive rates than GSCs, indicating that COC is specifically active in GSC populations (Fig. 4C–F). However, during the recovery period after the GSC depletion, GBs exhibited a significantly lower rate of PH3-positive cells than the control. We scored approximately ~40% PH3 positive GBs are reducing to ~20%. This suggests the emergence of the population of GBs that are activated for COC and competent to dedifferentiate. Although it is difficult to estimate exact frequency of the COC-arrested cells due to the ambiguity of the labeling method, the data indicate that the COC is likely reactivated in these cell populations located outside of the niche (Fig. 4C–F, Fig. S5).

Notably, we did not observe a decrease in the PH3-positive rate in 2-cell SGs (Fig. 4E), consistent with the idea that single GB is the major source of dedifferentiation.

To assess functional relationships between COC and dedifferentiation, we tested the dedifferentiation ability of a RNAi line against Bazooka/Par3 (Baz) which has been functionally validated in a previous study11. Baz is an essential component of the COC and forms foci at the hub-GSC interface and serves as a docking site for the apical centrosome. Baz-centrosome docking serves as an indicator of correct centrosome orientation, and it triggers the release of the COC for mitotic progression11. After the forced depletion of GSCs, the recovery of GSCs was significantly slower in Baz RNAi testes than in the control testes (Fig. 4F, Fig. S6). These data indicate that the Baz-mediated G2 arrest is likely required for dedifferentiation. Ectopic Baz foci capturing GB centrosomes were occasionally observed in steady-state and regeneration conditions (Fig. 4G). Future studies are necessary to understand whether or not the ectopic Baz-centrosome association is required for dedifferentiation through activating COC clearance.

These results suggest that reactivation of the COC may be occurring in the dedifferentiating cell population and it may be functionally promoting dedifferentiation. This way, cells ensure the spindle orientation and asymmetric division in their subsequent division after their niche reentrance.

Discussion

In several stem cell systems, lost stem cells can be replenished through dedifferentiation26. However, it remains unclear whether a common mechanism underlies this process, specifically, how the stem cell niche senses vacancy and transmits signals to differentiating cells to switch their fate back toward stemness. In addition, the functional properties of dedifferentiated stem cells relative to native stem cells remain poorly understood.

In this study, using long-term live imaging of the Drosophila testicular niche, we show that dedifferentiating GSCs exhibit a strong bias toward late G2 phase and typically undergo cell division shortly after reattachment to the hub. This cell-cycle bias is imposed by reactivation of the centrosome orientation checkpoint (COC), which is a polarity checkpoint active specifically in stem cells, indicating that the dedifferentiating cells regain at least one stem cell-specific characteristic, the COC, prior to niche reentry.

In steady state conditions, COC prevents mitotic entry when centrosomes are not correctly positioned, thereby ensuring correct spindle orientation and asymmetric division9,16. Our live monitoring revealed that majority of dedifferentiated GSCs also divide with correctly oriented spindles, similarly to normal GSCs, indicating that the reactivation of COC prior to niche reentry prevents mitotic entry of dedifferentiated cells until correct establishment of apical polarity and centrosome positioning. Supporting this idea, we observed prompt centrosome repositioning in these dedifferentiating cells upon niche reentry.

Our findings suggest the existence of at least two unidentified signals. First, under normal conditions, the majority of GBs do not possess COC16. Because GSC depletion induces widespread COC activation in GBs located outside the niche, there must be a signal that senses stem cell loss and activates COC in these cells. The identity of this signal remains unknown. Notably, COC reactivation also occurs in dedifferentiating GBs under steady-state conditions, suggesting that this signal may act locally in response to niche vacancy.

Second, we propose an additional signal that is activated in dedifferentiated GSCs upon niche reattachment to promote rapid centrosome reorientation, and release cells from COC-mediated arrest, enabling immediate mitotic entry. Future studies will be required to define the nature of these signals and how they coordinate dedifferentiation and asymmetric division.

Coupling dedifferentiation to COC activation can be an effective strategy to ensure the spindle orientation of newly replenished stem cells. Although, we could not determine if this COC reactivation is required for dedifferentiation machinery or it is a consequence of reactivating stem-like feature in dedifferentiating cells prior to niche reentry, we show that the polarity protein Baz, a key component of centrosome positioning in the COC pathway11, is required for efficient dedifferentiation. Because Baz has multiple cellular functions other than COC, it remains unclear whether Baz depletion affects centrosome orientation and dedifferentiation through distinct mechanisms. Thus, whether the observed G2 arrest is mechanistically coupled to migration or instead serves to prevent premature mitotic entry before niche reattachment remains unsolved. Future experiments manipulating cell-cycle progression independently of migration will be critical to distinguish between these possibilities.

Lastly, our work suggests that there are no significant differences in proliferative capacity between dedifferentiated and native GSCs. Previous studies have shown that dedifferentiated GSCs behave differently from native GSCs, although they have reported conflicting results9,19. For example, Cheng et al.9 showed that dedifferentiated cells frequently exhibit centrosome misorientation and reduced mitotic activity9, whereas Herrera et al. reported that dedifferentiated GSCs induced by repeated feeding–starvation cycles display increased mitotic activity19. The discrepancies among studies likely arise from differences in the cellular origin or experimental conditions used to induce dedifferentiation. Notably, earlier studies primarily examined dedifferentiated cells derived from 4- to 8-cell spermatogonial cysts, whereas our study focused on earlier-stage germline blast cells (GBs), which we identified as the major source of dedifferentiation. Using a previously established lineage-tracing method, which marks dedifferentiated stem cells originated from 4-to 8-cell stages, we observed a high frequency of centrosome misorientation in dedifferentiated GSCs consistent with an earlier study (Fig. 5D)9 In contrast, we observed prompt reorientation of the centrosomes in the dedifferentiated GSCs from GB stage. This indicates that dedifferentiated cells exhibit distinct properties depending on their stage of origin.

In summary, our results highlight that asymmetric division is subject to exceptionally stringent regulation via activating polarity checkpoint prior to the niche reentry. Furthermore, we demonstrate that dedifferentiated stem cells fully regain the capacity for asymmetric division and normal proliferation rate, despite potential disturbances in the inheritance of intrinsic factors. Given the high frequency of dedifferentiation under physiological conditions, identifying the mechanisms that specifically regulate this process will be essential for understanding how tissues are maintained over the long term.

Materials and methods

Fly husbandry and strains

Flies were raised on standard Bloomington medium at 25 °C (unless temperature control was required). 0- to 7-day-old adults were used for all experiments unless specific ages of flies were noted. The following fly stocks were obtained from Bloomington Stock Center (BDSC); nosGal4 (BDSC64277)27; Ubi-mRFP.nls, FRT40A (BDSC34500); UAS-myr-GFP (BDSC32197); Fly-FUCCI, pUASp-GFP.E2f1.1-230, UASp-mRFP1.CycB.1-266 (BDSC55100); hs-bam (BDSC24636); bamGal4 (FBtp0111994); BazRNAi (BDSC31523), UAS-GMA (BDSC31775), yw (BDSC189), and Sqh-mCherry (BDSC99923). UAS-GFP-αTub28, c587Gal429, bamGal4 (FBtp0111994), and ubi-Asl-Td-tomato30; UAS-FLP9; Baz-GFP Flytrap11; were kind gifts from Yukiko Yamashita. nos-FRT-mCherry-stop-FRT-Gal4, UAS-GFP31 was from Inaba lab stock.

Long-term live imaging

Live imaging was performed following a previously established technique32. The testes were dissected into 1X Becker Ringer’s solution32 and then mounted onto a 35 mm Glass Bottom Dishes (Nunc). 500 μL of 1 mg/mL poly-L-lysine (Sigma) was pipetted onto the coverslip portion of the imaging dish and incubated for 5- to 7 h at room temperature. Then, the poly-L-lysine solution was replaced with the Becker-Ringer’s solution. Testes were dissected in the Becker Ringer’s solution and mounted onto a poly-L-lysine layer with the tip of the testes oriented toward the bottom. Next, Becker Ringer’s solution was slowly removed, and pre-warmed (room temperature) Schneider’s Drosophila medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and glutamine–penicillin–streptomycin (Sigma) was added to the dish.

Time-lapse imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM800 confocal microscope with a 63× oil immersion objective (NA = 1.4). The system was equipped with Airyscan, which utilizes a 32-channel gallium arsenide phosphide photomultiplier tube (GaAsP-PMT) area detector. Z-stacks were acquired with a 1.5–2 µm interval, comprising 10–15 optical sections. Images were acquired every 5–10 min for 16 h. The microscope’s preset tiling function was used for automated acquisition of up to 10 testes per imaging session.

GSCs were identified near the apical tip of the testis as GFP-αTub or myr-GFP positive cells surrounding the hub, which does not express GFPs. In cases of uncertainty, we utilized the electronically switchable illumination and detection module (ESID) to confirm the hub’s location, as via this method, the hub is identifiable as encircled by a halo (Fig. S7).

Image analyses

Airyscan time-lapse images were processed by using Zen software (Zeiss) and the images were manually traced for each z-stack to detect dedifferentiation events. Recordings were started at 32-h recovery point after 6 times heat shock treatment. The hub area was defined as a circular region with autofluorescence located near the testis tip and surrounded by fluorescent-labeled germline stem cells. Dedifferentiation events were noted when germline cells, originating from a location more than one cell layer away from the edge of the hub, reattached to the hub and maintained their approximately same position for at least 10 frames (100 min). When a dedifferentiated cell was first detected at the edge of the hub, the time point was defined as the time of “reattachment”. For dedifferentiated cells, the time to mitosis was determined by measuring the time between the reattachment and nuclear envelope breakdown. For native GSCs, the time to mitosis was determined by measuring the time between the start of imaging and nuclear envelope breakdown. The division orientation in GSCs was determined by checking the positioning of two daughter cells after cell division. If, following division, one daughter cell remained attached to the hub while the other daughter cell is displaced from hub, the division orientation was judged as “oriented”. 3D reconstruction of the images was performed by using Imaris 9.5 (Oxford Instruments Group). Movies were annotated using Veed.io.

Immunofluorescence staining

Testes were dissected in 1x phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Fisher Scientific) and fixed in 4% formaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences) in PBS for 30 min. Next, testes were washed in PBS with 0.2% TritonX-100 (Fisher Scientific) (PBST) for at least 60 min, followed by incubation with primary antibody in 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma) in PBST at 4 °C overnight. The next day, samples were washed for 60 minutes (three times for 20 min each) in PBST, incubated with secondary antibody in 3% BSA in PBST at room temperature for 2 h, and then washed for 60 min (three times for 20 min each) in PBST. Samples were then mounted using VECTASHIELD with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Vector Lab).

The primary antibodies used were as follows: rat anti-Vasa (1:20; developed by A. Spradling and D. Williams, obtained from Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank (DSHB); mouse anti-hu-li tai shao (Hts) (1:20, 1B1; DSHB); mouse-anti-Fasciclin III (FasIII) (1:40, 7G10; DSHB); mouse anti-γ-Tubulin (1:400, GTU-88; Sigma-Aldrich); rabbit anti-phosphorylated (Thr3) histone H3 (1:200; clone JY325, Upstate). AlexaFluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam) were used at a dilution of 1:400.

GSC depletion and recovery

GSC depletion and recovery were performed following the previously described method with modifications17. Approximately 0- to 3-day-old adult flies carrying hs-Bam (BDSC24636) transgene were raised at 22 °C and heat-shocked in a 37 °C water bath for 30 min twice daily in vials with fly food. Vials were placed in a 29 °C incubator between heat-shock treatments. After 6-time treatments, vials were returned to 22 °C for recovery. Live-imaging recordings were started at a 32-h time point after the last heat shock treatment. For fixed samples, testes were dissected at the desired recovery time points.

Generation of nos-myrGFP transgenic line

For nos-myrGFP construction, we cut nos-MCP plasmid33 (kind gift from Takashi Fukaya) with Bam-HI and XhoI and assembled by Gibson assembly (NEB) following manufacturer’s instruction with myrGFP PCR fragment amplified using Addgene: pJFRC12-10XUAS-IVS-myr::GFP as a template with following primers:

Nos-myrGFP-F: 5’-CCCGGATTTTCGCCCTCGAGATGGGCAACAAATGCTGCAG-3’

Nos-myrGFP-R: 5’-AAGTGGGCGTGACGCGGATCCTTACTTGTAGAGCTCATCCA-3’

The resultant plasmid was verified by sequencing and transgenic flies were generated using strain 9750 by PhiC31integrase-mediated transgenesis (BestGene Inc.). The plasmid is deposited to Addgene (ID: 253595).

Short-term live imaging

Dissected testes were placed into Schneider’s Drosophila medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and glutamine–penicillin–streptomycin and placed onto Gold Seal Rite-On Micro Slides’ 2 etched rings with media, then covered with coverslips. Images were taken using a Zeiss LSM800 Airyscan with a 63× oil immersion objective (NA = 1.4), with 8–12 z-stacks (interval 1.5 µm). Electronically switchable illumination and detection module (ESID) detector was used for bright-field image acquisition. For all short-term live imaging experiments, imaging was performed within 30 min and no time-lapse imaging was performed using this method.

Genetic marking of dedifferentiated GSCs

UAS-FLP flies were crossed with BamGal4, nos-FRT-mCherry-stop-FRT-Gal4, UAS-GFP flies at 25 °C. Centrosome orientation of dedifferentiated GSCs was examined by using 7-day-old males. Testes were dissected and processed for immunofluorescence staining with rat anti-Vasa and mouse anti-γ-Tubulin. Centrosome orientation of GSCs were assessed for GFP-positive and GFP-negative populations according to a previously established scoring criterion7. The centrosome orientation in GSCs was determined “misoriented” when neither of the two centrosomes was located in close proximity to the hub-GSC interface during interphase.

Colcemid treatment

Colcemid treatment was performed as described previously16. Testes were dissected and transferred to Schneider’s Drosophila medium (Gibco) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and glutamine–penicillin–streptomycin (Sigma). Colcemid (Calbiochem) was added at a final concentration of 100 μM. Samples were incubated on a nutator at room temperature for 4.5 h, and then processed for immunofluorescence staining with rat anti-Vasa, mouse anti-Hts, mouse anti-FasIII and rabbit anti-phosphorylated (Thr3) histone H3.

Statistical analysis and graphing

No statistical methods were used to predetermine the sample size. The experiment values were not randomized. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. All experiments were independently repeated at least 3 times to confirm the results. Statistical analysis and graphing were performed using GraphPad prism9. The illustrations were generated by using BioRender.com or Adobe Illustrator. Fisher’s exact test was performed using Python 3.11 for comparison of spindle misorientation rates. Numerical values for all graphs are provided in Supplementary Data1.
