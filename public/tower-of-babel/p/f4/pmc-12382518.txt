Toddlers’ and preschoolers’ movement behaviours and mental health: protocol for the MOvement behaviours and irritaBILITY in early childhood (MOBILITY) prospective cohort study
Authors: Sophie M Phillips, Matthew Bourke, Shauna M Burke, Graham J Reid, Jason Gilliland, Patricia Tucker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionMental health conditions are a major public health challenge. Though typically diagnosed in adolescence, these conditions may arise during early childhood. Considerable evidence shows that engagement in healthy movement behaviours (ie, regular physical activity, reduced sedentary behaviour and sufficient sleep) is associated with a lower risk of mental health conditions in adolescents and adults. However, whether these behaviours have the same protective effect during early childhood is less clear. The MOvement behaviours and irritaBILITY in early childhood (MOBILITY) study was established to investigate the association between 24-hour movement behaviours and trajectories of early childhood mental health.Methods and analysisMOBILITY is a prospective observational cohort study of toddlers’ and preschoolers’ (aged 2–4 years) movement behaviours and mental health, which will be assessed at three data collection points (baseline and 6- and 12-month follow-ups). Mental health indicators (ie, irritability, internalising, externalising and prosocial behaviour) will be assessed via parent-report questionnaires, and movement behaviours will be assessed via ActiGraph accelerometers and sleep diaries. Primary analyses will include using: (1) compositional data analysis to examine prospective relationships between overall movement behaviour compositions and trajectories of mental health indicators, (2) isotemporal substitution modelling to determine whether the prospective association between independent movement behaviours and trajectories of mental health indicators is contingent on other movement behaviours and (3) the ‘Goldilocks’ approach to determine optimal time use compositions to promote favourable trajectories in mental health symptomology.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval for this study was received from the Health Sciences Research Ethics Board at Western University, London, Ontario, Canada. The findings of this novel research will have implications for informing updates of evidence-based movement behaviour guidelines, as well as the development and implementation of preventive mental health interventions. Study findings will be disseminated via publications and conference presentations for academic audiences, infographics and short videos for community partners, early childhood educators and parents, as well as actionable recommendations for physicians and healthcare providers.Keywords: MENTAL HEALTH, PUBLIC HEALTH, PAEDIATRICS

Background

In 2019, the WHO adopted 24-hour movement behaviour guidelines for young children,1 in line with those already adopted by other countries, including Canada, Australia, New Zealand and South Africa.2,5 These guidelines state that toddlers and preschoolers (aged 2–4 years) should: (1) engage in 180 min of physical activity each day (including 60 min of moderate to vigorous physical activity (MVPA) for preschoolers aged 3–4 years), (2) limit the amount of time spent sedentary (eg, no more than 60 min of screen time each day) and (3) toddlers (aged 2 years) should have 11–14 hours of good quality sleep each day, while preschoolers (aged 3–4 years) should have 10–13 hours of good quality sleep.1 The protective effect of engaging in physical activity, especially MVPA, limiting the amount of time spent sedentary and getting an adequate amount of sleep on the mental health of school-aged children and adolescents is well established.6 7 However, there is less evidence available pertaining to the mental health benefits of these behaviours in toddlers and preschoolers.

Mental health conditions, including anxiety and depression, represent a major global public health concern among young people worldwide.8 9 The burden of common mental health conditions, including depression and anxiety, increases from early childhood to a peak in adolescence and young adulthood.10 With this, most youth will experience severe impairment indicative of a mental health condition at some point.11 12 Further, the COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated the incidence of mental health challenges and conditions globally,13 14 including among children and adolescents.15 Although mental health conditions are usually diagnosed in later childhood and early adolescence, evidence suggests that certain conditions (eg, oppositional defiant behaviour) may emerge much earlier,16 17 and indicators of mental health conditions may begin to manifest before children display symptoms of a diagnosis.18 19 As such, early childhood has been recognised as a critical and malleable time for the identification and prevention of mental health conditions.20 21

There is a growing body of research highlighting key markers and indicators of mental health in early childhood, which may also be predictive of mental health conditions in later life.17 22 23 Irritability in early childhood, for example, is a candidate marker and transdiagnostic of a range of later mental health conditions, including anxiety, depression and oppositional defiant disorder,24,27 with chronic irritability at age 3 predicting the onset of these conditions by age 6.28 Similarly, other studies have found that irritability at age 3 is associated with symptoms of anxiety at age 9,16 and both internalising and externalising problems in adolescence.29 Among young children, irritability is characterised as responding with anger, temper tantrums or chronic low mood that is disproportionate to a situation.30 It is a form of emotional dysregulation that may be reliably differentiated between clinical/non-clinical presentations of early life mental health in children as young as 12 months22 and is posited as a reliable marker of vulnerability to later mental health challenges.17 Temper tantrums are a common manifestation of irritability in early childhood—based on a sample of 1490 young children living in the USA, nearly all (83.7%) had temper tantrums sometimes; however, only 9% had daily temper tantrums.30 Thus, it is possible to differentiate between normative misbehaviours and clinical levels of irritability.27 Indeed, recent work with large samples (n=3347 children residing in the USA) of preschoolers (aged 3–5 years) showed that one in five demonstrated clinically significant levels of irritability.31 Irritability prevalence may increase throughout early childhood but has relatively high within-person stability during this time.27 32 However, generally, the prevalence of irritability tends to decrease as children enter later childhood and have a greater ability to self-regulate their behaviour.23 33 Children who do not display a decrease in irritability may be at an even greater risk of developing a diagnosable mental health condition.23 33 For example, children who have consistently high and/or increasing irritability from age 3 to 11 years are significantly more likely to be diagnosed with a major depressive disorder or anxiety, compared with their peers with lower levels of irritability.23 There is evidence that early intervention can reduce irritability from ages 2–4 years, which in turn lowers the risk of internalising and externalising problems at aged 10.5 years.34 In summary, irritability symptoms are a risk factor for the development of psychopathology that can be identified at a young age,17 offering the opportunity for early intervention and prevention of mental health challenges both during the early years and later in life.

The literature on treatments and preventive measures for irritability during early childhood is still sparse.35 There is limited but promising evidence on the use of parenting programmes to help minimise chronic early childhood irritability and subsequently lower the risk of mental health conditions in later childhood.34 Of note, there is substantial evidence from studies conducted with school-aged, adolescent and adult populations linking movement behaviours (specifically increased physical activity and improved sleep) with the prevention and treatment of mental health conditions, including anxiety and depression.67 36,39 As for young children (preschool-aged), when examined as independent behaviours, each of the movement behaviours (physical activity, sedentary behaviour and sleep) is associated with early life psychosocial health (ie, internalising, externalising and prosocial behaviours).40,43 Similarly, studies using continuous measures of 24-hour movement behaviours have shown that there may be a prospective relationship between independent movement behaviours and mental health indicators in young children. For example, research examining accelerometer-derived physical activity found that higher levels of MVPA at 6 years were related to fewer symptoms of depression at ages 6 and 8.44 Additional research has demonstrated that greater amounts of screen time in young children (aged 3–4 years) are related to increased externalising problems from 145 to 7 years later.46 In terms of sleep, during early childhood, longer night-time sleep duration is associated with fewer emotional and behavioural problems at age 6.47 However, inconclusive results have been found in studies that have examined adherence to movement behaviour guidelines and early life (aged <5 years) mental health indicators (eg, anxiety, depression, self-regulation, internalising, externalising and prosocial behaviours).48,50 Among these, however, a recent meta-analysis demonstrated a dose-response relationship between the number of movement guidelines met and more positive psychosocial health.50 Notwithstanding, authors often dichotomise children into two, often unbalanced, groups: (1) those who achieve the movement guidelines and (2) those who do not, significantly decreasing the power of their analyses.51

In recent years, there has been an increased interest in examining relationships between compositions of 24-hour movement behaviours on health and developmental outcomes across the life course.52 The idea behind the compositional approach is that time spent in one behaviour may modify the health-related influence of time spent in any of the other behaviours;53 therefore, it is important to consider which movement behaviours are replacing others and how these relate to health and development outcomes.54 For example, if a child increases their sedentary time by 30 min per day, they will consequently engage in 30 min less physical activity or sleep during that day. To date, very few studies have examined compositions of 24-hour movement behaviour on mental health indicators in young children.55 There is some recent evidence, using cross-sectional analyses with a relatively small sample of young children (N ≤100), showing mostly null findings between movement compositions and socioemotional well-being.56 Similarly, a recent longitudinal study examining associations between 24-hour movement behaviours of preschoolers and psychosocial health (eg, inhibitory control, self-regulation, mental health) at 5 years of age did not report any associations over time, although some cross-sectional relationships were observed between movement behaviours and inhibitory control (ie, children’s ability to follow opposing instructions).57 There is more substantive evidence in later childhood (eg, 9–12 years), whereby researchers have found that increasing either MVPA or sleep duration relative to screen time was associated with lower depressive symptoms, while increasing screen time relative to MVPA or time sleeping was associated with higher depressive symptoms 1 year later.58

Despite irritability being the most robust early marker of common mental health conditions,19 to date, no studies have examined prospective associations between 24-hour movement behaviours in relation to irritability as well as other mental health indices, such as internalising, externalising and prosocial behaviours; common measures of psychosocial health in early childhood (eg, 35 59). Examining movement behaviours in relation to mental health using compositional analyses has three potential advantages. First, it allows for an examination of the effects of displacing time between separate behaviours on health outcomes, which provides a more accurate reflection of the potential benefits or harms of engaging in each behaviour, compared with examining each variable independently. Second, it yields a more nuanced overview of possible intervention targets,60 and third, it allows for an examination of the optimal movement behaviour compositions for more positive mental health trajectories.61

Study objectives

The primary objective of this study is to examine the prospective association between young children’s 24-hour movement behaviour compositions (ie, physical activity, sedentary time and sleep) and trajectories of early life mental health symptoms (ie, irritability). The second objective is to determine whether prospective associations between independent movement behaviours and early life mental health symptoms are contingent on other movement behaviours. Third, this study will determine the optimal movement behaviour compositions in a 24-hour day for the promotion of positive mental health trajectories among young children. The purpose of this report is to describe the recruitment procedures and study protocol of the MOvement behaviours and irritaBILITY in early childhood (MOBILITY) study.

Methods and analysis

Study design

MOBILITY is a prospective cohort study of young children (aged 2–4 years at baseline) residing in Ontario, Canada. Children’s 24-hour movement behaviour compositions (ie, physical activity, sedentary time and sleep) and mental health indicators (ie, irritability, internalising, externalising symptoms and prosocial behaviour) will be assessed at three times over 1 year (baseline and 6- and 12-month follow-up).

Patient and public involvement

Community partner collaborators who work closely with families and young children in London, Ontario, Canada, have been instrumental in the recruitment and data collection procedures for the MOBILITY study. These community partners have a vested interest in early childhood development and are keen to use the results for their early support services and health and well-being programmes (that are inclusive of mental health).

Participants

Sample size

The study is powered to detect a small-to-moderate association (r2=0.09) between movement behaviour compositions (based on accelerometry data) and mental health symptoms (irritability), with 80% power and a 0.05 error rate. As well as an accelerometer compliance rate of 75% and an accelerometer retention rate of 70% over the three time points, which are based on the average of previous longitudinal studies assessing physical activity of young children using accelerometers.49 62 Thus, a minimum sample size of 220 young children will need to be recruited at baseline to yield a final sample of 154 participants with valid data.

Eligibility and recruitment

Recruitment for the MOBILITY study began in August 2023 and is ongoing. It is anticipated that all data will be collected by December 2026 (accounting for the 12-month follow-up time point). An opportunity sampling method with a multirecruitment approach is being employed. This includes distributing study information throughout Ontario via: (1) childcare centres; (2) settings that young children and their parents attend (eg, family centres, community centres, libraries, playgroups, dentists and family physician offices) and (3) social media advertisements on platforms typically accessed by parents (eg, Facebook and Instagram). Families with at least one parent or legal guardian who lives in Ontario and can read and write in English are eligible to take part with their young children. Participating children must be aged between 2 and 4 years old at baseline. Children diagnosed (based on parent report) with a developmental disability (eg, cerebral palsy, autism spectrum disorders, Down syndrome and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder) will be excluded. Children with developmental disabilities will be excluded, as these children constitute a highly heterogeneous population in terms of diagnoses and severity, which can reduce statistical power and would limit the ability to detect meaningful associations concerning these groups of children. Further, the standardised measurement tools used in this study were primarily developed and validated for use with children without disabilities—applying these same tools to children with varying disabilities could incur a disservice to this group of children by potentially leading to inaccurate data. Studies exploring the relationships between movement behaviour and mental health in young children with disabilities are warranted; however, this would require a different study targeted at this population. Families will receive a small token of appreciation, in the form of a $C40 gift card, for their participation at each time point.

Procedures

Data will be collected at three time points over the course of 1 year: baseline, 6 months and 12 months. Parents who wish to take part with their children will be asked to sign and return a consent and contact form to the research team either paper-based or online through Qualtrics. Participants will then be provided with a baseline study package, in paper form, delivered in person or via mail, which contains study materials including: (1) questionnaire (inclusive of mental health indicator questionnaires), (2) accelerometer and instruction sheet and (3) sleep diary. In addition, an ‘activity monitor sticker chart’ and stickers will also be provided for children to help increase compliance.63 At follow-up assessment time points, the study package (including the same materials as baseline, with a slightly shorter parent questionnaire that omits demographic questions) will again be delivered (in person or via mail with prepaid return postage) by the study team.

Measures

The primary outcome measures for the present study are children’s 24-hour movement behaviours and irritability as a key mental health indicator. Given that much of the extant literature examining early life mental health has focused on internalising, externalising and prosocial behaviours (ie, 64), we will also examine these factors as secondary early life mental health indicators. At all time points (baseline, 6 months and 12 months), additional assessments of children’s movement and health will be used, including (a) children’s health status, (b) parental-reported child activity levels, including contextual information on sedentary behaviour and screen time, (c) childcare hours and arrangements, (d) quality of parent–child relationship, (e) parents’ own self-reported activity levels, screen time and mental health and (f) parent employment status and family income. At baseline only, demographic information on the child, parent and family characteristics will be collected. Outcome measures and tools

24-hour movement behaviour

24-hour movement behaviours (physical activity, sedentary time and sleep) will be measured at each time point (baseline, 6 months and 12 months) using ActiGraph GT3X+ accelerometers. This device is the most widely used and extensively validated accelerometer in young children.65 Children will be asked to wear the accelerometer on their right hip, attached with a nylon belt around their waist continuously for 24 hours a day for seven consecutive days, except for when they are completing water-based activities (eg, bathing and swimming). Parents will be provided with an information package explaining how to correctly place the accelerometer on their child, which also includes a quick response code to an instructional video on how to use the accelerometer. Parents will be asked to complete a daily diary to state the time at which the device is removed. Accelerometer data will be collected in 15 s epochs and analysed using validated cut points for young children:66 ≤100 counts per minute for sedentary time, between 101 and 1679 counts per minutes for light physical activity and ≥1680 counts per minute for MVPA.66 67 Parents will be asked to complete a daily diary, reporting the times that children: (1) go to bed, (2) go to sleep, (3) wake up, (4) get out of bed, (5) wake during the night and (6) nap each day; this information is routinely used when measuring sleep.68 69 Visual identification of accelerometer sleep data will be conducted, using the sleep diaries to determine the start and end of each sleep bout. We will also apply a recently validated sleep algorithm to the data to compare accuracy.70 If the two methods (visual inspection and the sleep algorithm) produce comparable outcomes on baseline data, which will be assessed using the same statistical analyses as outlined in the original validation study,70 we will proceed with using the algorithm for all time points. Otherwise, we will conduct visual inspection on all datasets. In addition, parents will be asked to report daily screen time, as per the current WHO guidelines, which suggest that young children (aged 2–4 years) should engage in no more than 60 min of screen time each day.1

Young children’s mental health

Early life mental health indicators of the young children will be measured using two validated questionnaires, which parents will be asked to complete at all three time points. The primary indicator of interest is irritability symptoms, measured using the Temper Loss subscale of the Multidimensional Assessment of Preschool Disruptive Behavior.30 71 The scale consists of 22 items ranging from normative expressions (eg, ‘Lose temper or have a tantrum to get something s/he wanted’) to intense, dysregulated tantrums (eg, ‘Hit, bite, or kick during a temper tantrum’). Responses are recorded on a 6-point Likert scale (0=never to 5=many times each day), and an overall composite score will be calculated for each child. This assessment has displayed excellent construct validity and test-retest reliability (intraclass correlation=0.80),71 as well as good convergent validity with a range of emotional problems and mental health conditions in young children (r=0.49 to 1.00).27 30

Internalising and externalising symptoms, as well as prosocial behaviour, will be assessed using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) for preschool children.59 This scale contains 25 items, which assess conduct problems, hyperactivity, emotional problems, peer problems and prosocial behaviour. Responses are recorded using a 3‐point Likert scale (0=not true, 1=somewhat true, 2=certainly true). Appropriate items will be reverse scored, and items will be summed to compute totals for three subscales: (1) externalising (ie, conduct problems, hyperactivity), (2) internalising (ie, emotional problems, peer problems) and (3) prosocial behaviours. The SDQ is among one of the most widely used metrics for evaluation of mental health risk across childhood.35 72 73

Demographic information

Parents will be asked to complete a demographic questionnaire at baseline to assess their children’s age, sex, gender, ethnicity and Indigenous status; parental age, sex, gender, employment status, education (of both parents) and relationship to child in the study and their family situation (ie, single- or double-parent household), number of people in household, family income and household postal code. Demographic questions will only be asked at baseline, with the exception of employment status and income, which will be asked at all three time points.

Children’s health status

Parents will be asked three open-ended questions relating to their child’s health status: (1) whether their child has any conditions that affect their ability to play or be active, (2) whether they suffer from sleep problems and (3) whether their child has any illness at the time of measurement that may affect their normal behaviours. This is in line with parental perspectives of movement behaviour measurement, suggesting that these are important attributes to capture to help contextualise their children’s data.63

Childcare hours and arrangement

Parents will also be asked to report their children’s childcare arrangement, including how many hours per week their child attends childcare, given the known differences in movement behaviour patterns of children depending on the type of childcare they attend.74

Parental-reported physical activity and sedentary time activities

Parents will also be asked to proxy-report their children’s typical daily physical activity, including minutes of higher intensity and outdoor play. In addition, parents will be asked to report context-specific sedentary behaviour of their children. This includes questions on types of screens used, screen time limits, reasons for screen use (eg, educational, to calm a child), times when screens are used, coviewing with a parent, duration of time spent playing and other daily activities while sedentary and sedentary time while travelling. The questions are based on a tool previously developed with parents and carers of young children,75 as well as contextual questions used in the international Sunrise study.76 Though these data will not be included in the primary analyses, they will be used to help contextualise any identified associations, in line with previous research identifying the importance of understanding activities engaged in while sedentary in relation to early life mental health outcomes.77

Children’s sleep habits

Children’s sleep habits will be assessed using the bedtime resistance, sleep onset delay and night-time waking subscales of the Children’s Sleep Habits Questionnaire.78 This is a commonly used and evaluated tool in young children, demonstrating reasonable validity.69 This contains 10 items, with responses recorded on a 3-point Likert scale (1=Rarely, 2=Sometimes, 3=Usually). Appropriate items will be reverse-scored in line with the original scoring sheet, with higher scores indicating more disturbed sleep.

Parent–child relationship

Given the importance of the early parent–child relationship on children’s mental health,79 we will assess the quality of parent–child relationships using the short form version of the Parent–Child Relationship Scale.80 This scale contains 15 items, with responses recorded on a 5-point Likert scale (1=Definitely does not apply to 5=Definitely applies). Conflict questions are reverse-scored, and overall composite scores are calculated for each parent. Seven of the items relate to parent–child relationship closeness (eg, ‘My child spontaneously shares information about himself/herself’), and eight relate to parent–child conflict (eg, ‘My child and I always seem to be struggling with each other’).

Parent physical activity and screen time

There are strong dyadic correlations between young children’s movement behaviours, including levels of sedentary behaviour, screen time and physical activity and those of their parents.81 As such, parents will also be asked to self-report their physical activity using a validated single-item tool,82 as well as hours and minutes of their recreational screen time for weekday and weekend days, using two modified questions from the Canadian Health Measures Survey.73

Parental mental health

Parental mental health has been shown to be associated with child internalising and externalising symptoms in both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies.83 As such, we will measure parental mental health via the self-reported K6 questionnaire.84 Responses on this 6-item questionnaire are recorded on a 5-point Likert scale (0=None of the time to 4=All the time), and an overall composite score will be calculated for each parent. This tool has been shown to be valid for determining both moderate and severe mental distress.85

Data analysis plan

Data on participant attributes such as demographic characteristics will be summarised descriptively as frequency and percentages for categorical variables and means and SDs for continuous variables. Participant characteristics for those who complete the follow-up time points and those who drop out will also be explored.

To assess the primary aims of the MOBILITY study, data will be analysed using compositional data analysis60 with isotemporal substitution modelling,60 as well as the ‘Goldilocks’ approach to determine optimal time use compositions to promote favourable trajectories of mental health outcomes.61 These analyses will be conducted using the statistical analysis software packages SPSS and R. Rather than using raw movement behaviour data in analyses, the composition of children’s movement behaviours will be expressed as specific isometric log-ratio coordinates called pivot coordinates. Doing so means that rather than examining the absolute association between a single behaviour and an outcome (eg, the association between sleep and irritability), it is possible to examine the relative association between a behaviour as a ratio of any combinations of other behaviours with an outcome. The pivot coordinates will be included as independent variables in a linear regression model, and symptoms of mental health (irritability, internalising, externalising and prosocial behaviour as separate analyses) at 6 and 12 months will be entered as the dependent variables. Variables known to be a strong predictor of mental health in early childhood will be entered as covariates in the analyses as needed (ie, parent–child relationship). There are three steps to assess the primary objectives: 1. examining prospective relationships between overall movement behaviour compositions and trajectories of early life mental health. The coefficient of multiple determination (R2) and overall significance will be estimated from the overall compositional data analysis model60 to determine if there is a significant prospective association between 24-hour movement behaviour compositions and mental health at each time point. 2. Modelling whether the prospective association between independent movement behaviours and trajectories of early life mental health is contingent on other movement behaviours. To do this, compositional isotemporal substitution models will be conducted60 to estimate if the prospective association between spending more time engaged in specific movement behaviour on trajectories of mental health is contingent on which other movement behaviour it displaces. 3. Determining the optimal movement behaviour composition at baseline to promote positive trajectories in early life mental health symptoms. We will use the Goldilocks approach to determine optimal time use compositions to promote favourable trajectories in early life mental health.61 The ‘Goldilocks day’ approach refers to finding the ideal balance and optimum amount of time in each movement behaviour on health outcomes. The isotemporal models described above will be used to estimate trajectories in mental health symptoms for every possible combination of movement behaviours. The limits of estimated compositions will be truncated at 99% CIs for the sampling distribution of each movement behaviour. The results for mental health indicators will be sorted from most to least favourable, and the compositional mean for each movement behaviour from the top 5% of results will be calculated to describe the optimal time use composition to improve symptoms of each mental health outcome.

Secondary analysis will be conducted to determine if changes in 24-hour movement behaviours covary with changes in mental health symptoms across time. To achieve this, a multilevel model will be estimated. The model will be estimated with disaggregated within-person and between-person effects of 24-hour movement behaviour compositions.86 Aitchison’s perturbation87 will be used to centre 24-hour movement behaviours on each participant’s individual geometric mean across assessments. Pivot coordinates will be calculated based on the within-person centred movement behaviours at each assessment, and separate between-person coordinates will be calculated based on a participant’s geometric mean for each movement behaviour across assessments. Compositional isotemporal substitution models will be estimated to determine how substitutions between individual movement behaviours across assessments relate to changes in mental health outcomes.

Finally, we will also examine prevalence estimates of meeting thresholds for the 2019 WHO 24-hour movement guidelines,1 including proportions of children who meet independent and combinations of the guidelines and those who do not meet any recommendations and examine this in relation to early life mental health outcomes. Though not our primary aim, we will examine whether there are any differential associations over time between meeting the 24-hour movement behaviour guidelines and mental health symptomology. Children will be categorised as either meeting or not meeting: (1) independent movement behaviour guidelines of sleep, screen time or physical activity; (2) combinations of any two movement behaviours; (3) the number of guidelines met or (4) meeting the integrated 24-hour movement guidelines. Linear regression models will be performed in R, for both baseline cross-sectional data, as well as longitudinal data, including adjusting for covariates and baseline outcomes, as outlined previously by others.88

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Ethical approval has been provided by the Health Sciences Research Ethics Board at Western University, London, Ontario, Canada (REB# 122059). Parents and guardians of participating children provide written consent for themselves and their children, either by signing and returning a completed paper consent form to the research team or completing an online consent form via Qualtrics.

Dissemination

Findings will be disseminated to academic audiences through peer-reviewed journal articles and conference presentations. In addition, findings will be disseminated through infographics and short videos, cocreated with our community partners (eg, Families First Community Action Program for Children, Merrymount, Childreach), to be shared among the families in their care, as well as displayed online and via social media to reach a wider audience of early childhood educators and parents. Broader engagements will include a series of actionable recommendations for physicians and healthcare providers shared through our Children’s Health Research Institute connections. Finally, participating families who expressed interest in receiving findings of the study will be sent all publications from this project. Authorship for peer-reviewed journal articles will follow the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors’ defined authorship criteria.89

The funding sources have had no role in the design of the study and will not have any role during its execution, analyses, interpretation of the data or decision to submit results.
