Association of FHR5 With Disease Severity in ANCA-Associated Vasculitis
Authors: Yan-Jie Li, Su-Fang Chen, Zhi-Ying Li, Li Zhu, Su-Xia Wang, Ming-Hui Zhao, Mark A Little, Min Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlternative complement activation is pivotal in antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis (AAV) pathogenesis. Growing evidence highlights an important role of complement factor H (FH) in AAV. FH-related protein 5 (FHR5) functions as a competitive antagonist of FH. This study aimed to investigate the association of FHR5 with disease severity and renal outcomes in patients with AAV.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, plasma FHR5 levels were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay in 154 patients with AAV at active stage and 55 patients in remission. Renal FHR5 deposition was assessed using immunohistochemistry (IHC) in 71 of the 154 patients. The association of plasma FHR5 levels and renal FHR5 deposition with clinicopathological parameters was analyzed.ResultsPlasma levels of FHR5 were significantly higher in patients with active AAV than in those in remission and healthy controls, and correlated with increased inflammatory parameters and more severe renal damage. Positive FHR5 staining was observed in the glomeruli of 49 (69%) renal specimens. Patients with moderate or strong glomerular FHR5 deposition (grade > 1+) demonstrated significantly worse baseline renal function, more severe histopathological kidney injury, and poorer treatment response than those with negative or few deposition of FHR5 (grade ≤ 1+). Multivariable Cox regression analysis indicated that moderate or strong glomerular FHR5 deposition was independently associated with an increased risk of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD).ConclusionBoth plasma levels and glomerular deposition of FHR5 were elevated in patients with AAV and associated with more active disease and more severe renal damage. Glomerular FHR5 deposition was an independent risk factor for ESKD in AAV.Keywords: ANCA, FHR5, vasculitis

AAV is a group of life-threatening systemic autoimmune diseases characterized by pauci-immune necrotizing small vessel vasculitis and the presence of circulating autoantibodies against neutrophil cytoplasmic constituents, particularly proteinase 3 and myeloperoxidase. It comprises granulomatosis with polyangiitis, microscopic polyangiitis and eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis.1 Although AAV was traditionally characterized as pauci-immune (negative or few depositions of Ig or complement),2, 3, 4 cumulative evidence from both animal studies and clinical investigations has demonstrated that overactivation of the complement system via the alternative pathway (AP) plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis.5, 6, 7, 8 However, the underlying mechanisms of AP dysregulation are not fully clear. There is growing evidence highlighting the important role of complement regulatory proteins,9, 10, 11, 12, 13 in particular, FH,9, 10, 11, 12 in AAV.

The regulatory activity of FH can be antagonized by FHRs (FHR1–5), which share high sequence similarity with FH but lack the complement regulatory domains.14 The balance between FH and FHRs may determine the outcome of the AP activation and regulation, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of complement-mediated diseases such as Ig A nephropathy and C3 glomerulopathy.15,16 Interestingly, a recent study highlighted an important role of the FH/FHR balance in AAV by showing that the levels of plasma FHR1, FHR2, and FHR5 were significantly increased; whereas the levels of plasma FH were significantly decreased in patients with active AAV compared with controls.12 In contrast to the recently reported association between FHR1 and disease severity of AAV,12,17 the role of FHR5 in AAV remains poorly understood. Notably, significant association between elevated FHR5 levels and disease progression has been reported in some complement-mediated diseases, including IgA nephropathy and C3 glomerulopathy, indicating that FHR5 may serve as an important contributor to the AP dysregulation.18, 19, 20 This study aimed to investigate the association of FHR5 with disease severity and renal outcomes in AAV by assessing plasma FHR5 levels and renal FHR5 deposition in a cohort of patients with AAV with regular follow-up.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

One hundred fifty-four patients newly diagnosed with active AAV, confirmed by renal biopsy at Peking University First Hospital between 2018 and 2023, were enrolled in the study (the entire cohort). All patients fulfilled the 2012 Chapel Hill Consensus Conference definitions for AAV.1 Patients with secondary vasculitis or other comorbid renal diseases were excluded. The recruitment process is shown in Figure 1. Baseline clinical information was recorded, and disease activity was measured using the Birmingham Vasculitis Activity Score.21 Plasma samples were collected before the initiation of immunosuppressive treatment and stored at −80 °C until use. Of the 154 patients, 98 patients achieved complete remission22 after immunosuppressive therapy, and plasma samples were available for 55 patients (the remission cohort). Plasma samples from 72 sex-matched and age-matched healthy blood donors were collected as healthy controls.

Flow diagram illustrating patient enrollment. EGPA, eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis; ELISA, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay; IHC, immunohistochemistry.

Because the protein antigenicity of paraffin-embedded tissues may decline with prolonged storage, 71 of the 154 patients diagnosed between 2022 and 2023 were consecutively included in the IHC analysis (the IHC cohort) to minimize potential measurement bias from antigenic variability and to ensure a more accurate assessment of renal FHR5 deposition. Renal tissues from the histologically normal part of nephrectomized kidneys (due to renal carcinoma) from 19 age-matched and sex-matched individuals served as normal controls. These control tissues were confirmed normal by light microscopy, immunofluorescence, and electron microscopy.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (Approval No. 2025-1551). Informed consent was obtained from each patient or guardian.

Renal Histopathology and Treatment Strategies

Renal histopathological assessment is detailed in the Supplementary Methods, and related supplementary references were cited (Supplementary References). Treatment strategies are detailed in the Supplementary Methods.

Measurement of Plasma FHR5

Plasma FHR5 levels were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay as previously reported,20,23,24 with details provided in the Supplementary Methods. To validate assay specificity, FH and other FH family proteins (FHR1–4) were applied to assess potential cross-reactivity (Supplementary Figure S1).

IHC Staining for FHR5 in Renal Specimens

Renal deposition of FHR5 was detected using IHC as previously described,18,19 with details provided in the Supplementary Methods. Antigen staining intensities were graded as 0 (negative), +/- (minimal), 1+ (mild), 2+ (moderate), or 3+ (strong), as described previously; staining was considered “positive” for scores of 1+ to 3+.18 To evaluate the association of FHR5 staining intensity with clinicopathological parameters in patients with AAV, the patients were divided into 2 groups based on the intensity of immunostaining as described previously2,3,25,26: a moderate or strong FHR5 deposition group (grade > 1+) and a group with negative or few FHR5 depositions (grade ≤ 1+). Biopsies containing <10 glomeruli, or < 2 nonsclerotic glomeruli were excluded from analysis to ensure adequate histopathological evaluation.18

Details are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

The Definition of Systemic and Renal Treatment Response

The systemic and renal responses to immunosuppressive treatment were defined as previously described.22,27,28 Complete remission was defined as the absence of disease activity attributable to active vasculitis, and partial remission was defined as disease activity scores decreased ≥50% without new manifestations attributable to active vasculitis. Treatment resistance was defined as unchanged or increased disease activity after 4 weeks of standard therapy in patients with acute AAV, or <50% reduction in disease activity scores after 6 weeks of treatment, or chronic persistent disease defined as the presence of at least 1 major or 3 minor items on the disease activity score after 12 weeks of therapy.22

Renal response was evaluated 6 months after immunosuppressive therapy initiation. Because early death precluded this assessment, patients who died within 6 months were excluded from this analysis. Complete renal recovery was defined as normalization of kidney function (serum creatinine [Scr] < 133 μmol/l) and resolution of hematuria, whereas partial recovery was defined as stabilization or improvement in renal function with Scr ≥ 133 μmol/l, without the need for maintenance dialysis. Treatment failure was defined as progressive deterioration of kidney function with persistent active urinary sediment despite immunosuppressive therapy.27,28

Outcome

All patients were followed up in the outpatient clinics. The study end point was ESKD, defined as the need for maintenance dialysis or kidney transplantation. Accordingly, the follow-up time, representing the renal survival time for each patient, was calculated from the date of AAV diagnosis to the time point of reaching ESKD or death (if it occurred before ESKD), or to the last follow-up.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with R software (version 4.4.3, R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Quantitative variables were presented as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range (IQR) according to the normality of data. Between-group differences in normally distributed quantitative parameters were assessed using the t test or analysis of variance as appropriate. Continuous variables with skewed distribution were tested using Mann-Whitney U tests or Kruskal-Wallis tests when appropriate. Paired variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Qualitative variables were presented as number (%) and differences between 2 groups were analyzed using the chi-square test. To compare differences between semiquantitative variables, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied. Pearson’s correlation coefficient or Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used for correlation analysis as appropriate based on the normality of data. Regarding correction for multiple comparisons, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to control false discovery rate (FDR); FDR-adjusted P-values were applied for correlation analyses.

All patients, including those who died early, were included in the survival analysis. Kaplan-Meier curves were depicted to show the cumulative incidence of outcomes and the difference between the groups was tested using the log-rank test. Cox regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association of glomerular FHR5 deposition with the risk of ESKD. Candidate variables included in the multivariable Cox regression were selected by univariable survival analysis when the P value was <0.05. Age and sex were forced into multivariable Cox models because they were potential confounding factors, as previously described.29 Regarding censoring, (i) participants who were lost to follow-up or completed the study without reaching ESKD were censored at their last visit, and (ii) participants who died before reaching ESKD were censored at the date of death.30 In standard time-to-event analyses, such individuals classified as right censored were considered “at risk” up to the time of censoring; however, they were not counted as events and did not contribute to the risk set at later time points. For Cox regression models, the proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residual tests, and the multicollinearity among the covariates in the multivariable Cox regression was assessed using the variance inflation factor. Considering the limited sample size, as well as zero events in some variable categories, Firth's penalized Cox regression was applied in survival analyses.31 To address the concern regarding competing risks, we performed sensitivity analyses using Fine-Gray competing risk regression, treating death as a competing event for ESKD. A 2-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General Data of Patients With AAV

Baseline characteristics of the 154 patients with AAV (the entire cohort) are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Among these patients, 59 (38.3%) were male and 95 (61.7%) were female, with an age of 60.1 ± 13.9 (range: 15–83) years at diagnosis. One hundred forty-five patients (94.2%) and 6 patients (3.9%) were positive for myeloperoxidase-ANCA and proteinase 3–ANCA, respectively; the remaining 3 patients (1.9%) were negative for ANCA. The median levels of Scr and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) were 252.6 (IQR: 171.4–401.9) μmol/l and 18.7 (IQR: 11.0–33.0) ml/min per 1.73 m2, respectively. The level of baseline Birmingham Vasculitis Activity Score was 16.5 ± 5.1. Among the 154 patients with AAV, 98 patients achieved complete remission22 after immunosuppressive therapy, whereas plasma samples of 55 patients were available (the remission cohort). The characteristics of the remission cohort are presented in Supplementary Table S2. Among these patients, 25 were male and 30 were female, with an age of 58.8 ± 11.7 (range: 25–79) years at diagnosis. The levels of Scr and eGFR at sampling in remission stage were 141.5 (IQR: 104.0–177.0) μmol/l and 40.5 (IQR: 30.5–60.2) ml/min per 1.73 m2, respectively. The Birmingham Vasculitis Activity Score levels of all the 55 patients in remission stage were 0.

Baseline data of the 71 patients stained for FHR5 in renal specimens (the IHC cohort) are presented in Supplementary Table S3. No significant difference in age (60.8 ± 12.4 vs. 59.2 ± 14.7 years, P = 0.446), sex (male: 38.0% vs. 41.7%, P = 0.635), serum FHR5 levels (5.8 [IQR: 3.6–7.3] vs. 5.6 [IQR: 4.6–8.1] μg/ml, P = 0.131), or renal C3 deposition (grade > 1+, 34.3% vs. 44.6%, P = 0.173) was observed between patients in the IHC cohort and the remaining patients of the entire cohort. Although baseline eGFR were significantly lower in patients in the IHC cohort (12.6 [IQR: 7.0–20.8] vs. 24.1 [IQR: 12.5–39.9] ml/min per 1.73 m2, P < 0.001), disease severity within this cohort was well-distributed across the ANCA kidney risk score32 categories: low (33.8%), moderate (22.5%), high (33.8%), and very high risk (9.9%), thereby enabling the exploration of the association between renal FHR5 deposition and disease severity and outcomes.

Plasma FHR5 levels in Patients With AAV and Controls

Plasma FHR5 levels were measured in all 154 patients with AAV at active stage (the entire cohort). The levels of plasma FHR5 were significantly higher in patients with AAV at active stage than in those in remission and healthy controls (5.6 [IQR: 4.3–7.8] vs. 5.0 [IQR: 3.8–6.8] μg/ml, P = 0.004; 5.6 [IQR: 4.3–7.8] vs. 3.8 [IQR: 3.0–4.3] μg/ml, P < 0.001, respectively; Figure 2a). Consistently, among the 55 patients with AAV with sequential plasma samples of both active stage and remission, plasma levels of FHR5 were significantly higher in patients at active stage than in those in remission (5.8 [IQR: 4.8–8.5] vs. 5.0 [IQR: 3.8–6.8] μg/ml, P = 0.002; Figure 2b). In addition, significantly higher plasma FHR5 levels were observed in patients with AAV achieving remission, than in healthy controls (5.0 [IQR: 3.8–6.8] vs. 3.8 [IQR: 3.0–4.3] μg/ml, P < 0.001) (Figure 2a).

Association Between Plasma FHR5 Levels and Clinicopathological Parameters in Patient With AAV

Next, the association of plasma FHR5 levels with clinicopathological parameters in patients with AAV at active stage was explored. Elevated plasma FHR5 levels significantly correlated with higher levels of inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (r = 0.389, FDR-adjusted P < 0.001; and r = 0.498, FDR-adjusted P < 0.001, respectively; Figure 3a and b), as well as more severe renal damage, including higher Scr, lower eGFR, and higher proportion of cellular crescents and glomeruli with fibrinoid necrosis in renal histopathology (r = 0.184, FDR-adjusted P = 0.028; r = −0.169, FDR-adjusted P = 0.036; r = 0.219, FDR-adjusted P = 0.011; and r = 0.213, FDR-adjusted P = 0.011, respectively; Figure 3c–f). In addition, significant correlations between plasma FHR5 levels and coagulation indices were observed, including D-dimer, fibrin degradation products and platelet count in peripheral blood (r = 0.364, FDR-adjusted P < 0.001; r = 0.409, FDR-adjusted P < 0.001; and r = 0.176, FDR-adjusted P = 0.033, respectively; Figure 3g–i).

Renal FHR5 Deposition in Patients With AAV and Controls

Among the 154 patients, renal FHR5 deposition was assessed using IHC in 71 patients (the IHC cohort). Consistent with the previous study,19 no obvious FHR5 staining in the tubulointerstitium was detected. As described above, glomerular FHR5 staining intensity was graded as 0 (negative), +/- (minimal), 1+ (mild), 2+ (moderate), or 3+ (strong), the proportions of patients with AAV in each category were 9 (12.7%), 13 (18.3%), 28 (39.4%), 12 (16.9%), and 9 (12.7%), respectively (Figure 4). No obvious FHR5 staining was observed in the glomeruli of the normal control kidney tissues.

Association Between Glomerular FHR5 Deposition and Clinicopathological Parameters in Patients With AAV

To evaluate the association of FHR5 staining intensity with clinicopathological parameters in patients with AAV, the patients were divided into 2 groups as described above: a moderate or strong FHR5 deposition group (grade > 1+, n = 21) and a group with negative or few FHR5 depositions (grade ≤ 1+, n = 50).

Patients with moderate or strong FHR5 deposition in glomeruli demonstrated more severe renal damage, indicated by higher levels of urinary protein (2.5 [IQR: 1.4–6.0] vs. 1.2 [IQR: 0.8–2.0] g/24 h, P = 0.001), lower eGFR (8.1 [IQR: 6.4–13.4] vs. 16.0 [IQR: 9.9–23.7] ml/min per 1.73 m2, P = 0.007), and higher Scr (542.3 [IQR: 368.1–669.3] vs. 291.1 [IQR: 226.3–468.9] μmol/l, P = 0.003), as well as a higher proportion of patients requiring dialysis at diagnosis (47.6% vs. 16.0%, P = 0.005).

Histologically, the distribution of histopathologic classification proposed by Berden et al.33 was significantly different between the 2 groups (P = 0.038). In patients with moderate or strong FHR5 deposition, the mixed category was more frequent whereas the focal category was less common (Supplementary Table S3). Consistently, patients with moderate or strong FHR5 deposition had a significantly lower proportion of normal glomeruli (6.5% [IQR: 1.9%–12.6%] vs. 29.9% [IQR: 15.0%–48.8%], P < 0.001) and higher proportion of fibrous crescents (28.6% [IQR: 7.9%–59.2%] vs. 7.3% [IQR: 0%–29.4%], P = 0.009) in renal specimens. The ANCA kidney risk score, which incorporates the percentage of normal glomeruli, interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy, and baseline Scr, has been validated as a predictor of ESKD in patients with AAV.32 The ANCA kidney risk score risk stratification differed significantly between the 2 groups of patients (P < 0.001). Patients with moderate or strong FHR5 deposition were predominantly classified as very high or high risk, while showing a lower proportion of low risk (Supplementary Table S3).

Association Between Glomerular FHR5 Deposition and Treatment Response

The systemic response to treatment was significantly worse in patients with moderate or strong FHR5 deposition than in those with negative or few depositions of FHR5 (P < 0.001). Specifically, the proportions of complete remission, partial remission, and treatment resistance were 4 (19.0%), 11 (52.4%), and 6 (28.6%) in the moderate or strong FHR5 deposition group, versus 41 (82.0%), 7 (14.0%), and 2 (4.0%) in the negative or few FHR5 depositions group, respectively.

Regarding renal response to treatment evaluated at 6 months, 2 patients who died within 6 months were excluded from this analysis because early death precluded this assessment. Therefore, the association between glomerular FHR5 deposition and renal response to immunosuppressive treatment was analyzed in the remaining 69 patients. Six months after initiation of the induction therapy, patients with moderate or strong FHR5 deposition demonstrated significantly poorer renal response than those with negative or few FHR5 depositions (P < 0.001). Among the 20 patients with moderate or strong FHR5 depositions included in the analysis, only 4 (20%) achieved recovery of renal function, including 1 (5%) and 3 (15%) patients achieving complete and partial recovery, respectively; the remaining 16 patients (80%) experienced treatment failure. In contrast, of the 49 patients with negative or few FHR5 depositions, 11 patients (22.5%) achieved complete recovery, 33 (67.3%) achieved partial recovery, and 5 (10.2%) experienced treatment failure.

FHR5 Colocalized With C3b/iC3b/C3c in Glomeruli of Patients With AAV

Considering that previous studies have shown that FHR5 promotes surface complement activation by competing with FH for binding to tissue-bound C3b, we next assessed the relationship between glomerular deposition of FHR5 and C3b/iC3b/C3c. Patients with moderate or strong deposition of FHR5 exhibited more intense staining of C3b/iC3b/C3c, than those with negative or few depositions of FHR5 (P = 0.008; Supplementary Table S3). The colocalization of FHR5 and C3b/iC3b/C3c on glomeruli in patients with AAV was further evaluated using double immunofluorescence staining. FHR5 predominantly deposited along glomerular capillary loops in renal specimens of patients with AAV. In the same tissue sections, C3b/iC3b/C3c exhibited a similar staining pattern. Notably, colocalization of FHR5 and C3b/iC3b/C3c along the glomerular capillary area was observed (Figure 5).

Association Between Glomerular FHR5 Deposition and Renal Outcome

All the 154 patients with AAV (the entire cohort) were regularly followed up for a median duration of 25.0 (IQR: 11.8–58.5) months. During the follow-up, 37 patients (24.0%) progressed to ESKD. In univariable Cox survival analysis, plasma FHR5 levels at active stage did not show significant association with the risk of ESKD (P = 0.357, hazard ratio [HR]: 0.95, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.86–1.06), whereas higher plasma FHR5 levels during remission were marginally associated with increased risk of ESKD (P = 0.067, HR: 1.23, 95% CI: 0.99–1.53). We analyzed the association between glomerular FHR5 deposition and renal outcomes. Of the 71 patients stained for FHR5 in renal specimens (the IHC cohort), during a median follow-up time of 15.0 (IQR: 8.0–25.0) months, 18 patients (25.4%) progressed to ESKD. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that patients with moderate or strong FHR5 deposition had significantly poorer renal survival than those with pauci-deposition of FHR5 (log-rank P < 0.001; Figure 6). In univariable Cox analysis, moderate or strong glomerular FHR5 deposition was significantly associated with an increased risk of developing ESKD (P < 0.001, HR: 10.98, 95% CI: 3.98–36.86; Supplementary Table S4). Other variables associated with the risk of ESKD identified by the univariable Cox analysis included age, baseline eGFR, 3 histological variables (% of normal glomeruli, interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy, and glomerular C3b/iC3b/C3c deposition), and the 2 prognostic classifications (ANCA kidney risk score and Berden classification). Because of the strong correlations among variables, including baseline eGFR, percentage of normal glomeruli, and the 2 prognostic classifications, 2 distinct multivariable models were constructed, following the approach of a previous study.34 Multivariable Cox analysis showed that glomerular FHR5 deposition was independently associated with the risk of ESKD in patients with AAV after adjusting for other candidate risk factors (P = 0.018, HR: 4.79; 95% CI: 1.30–19.46 in model 1; P = 0.003, HR: 7.39; 95% CI: 1.91–35.71 in model 2, respectively; Table 1). In addition, sensitivity analysis using Fine-Gray competing risk regression, treating death as a competing event, demonstrated consistent findings with the primary Cox regression analysis (Supplementary Table S5).

Discussion

Dysregulation of the AP, particularly the balance between FH and FHRs, has been increasingly recognized in the pathogenesis of AAV.9,10,12 Our current study showed that plasma FHR5 levels were significantly elevated in patients with AAV compared with healthy controls, which is consistent with the recent study by Lucientes-Continente et al.12 Further, we found that elevated plasma FHR5 levels were associated with more active disease and more severe renal damage, and moderate or strong glomerular FHR5 deposition was associated with poorer treatment response and worse renal outcome of patients with AAV, further supporting a potential role of FHR5 in disease progression of AAV. Notably, only glomerular FHR5, but not plasma levels of FHR5, showed a significant association with renal survival. A previous study has demonstrated a critical role of local FH in regulating surface complement activation and protecting glomerular endothelial cells from complement-mediated inflammation and injury.35 Therefore, we hypothesized that in patients with AAV, glomerular deposition of FHR5 antagonizes this protective effect by competing with FH for endothelium recognition and surface-C3b binding, because of its high sequence similarity to FH within the surface recognition domains. Interestingly, consistent with the finding by Lucientes-Continente et al,12 we observed that plasma FHR5 levels were significantly higher in patients with AAV in remission than in healthy controls, suggesting incomplete resolution of complement dysregulation during remission. This observation aligns with previous evidence that persistent immunoinflammatory activity exists despite clinical remission and contributes to progression of chronic kidney disease.36 Consistently, patients with higher plasma FHR5 levels during remission tended to have worse renal outcomes in our current study. Taken together with the finding that moderate or strong glomerular FHR5 deposition was independently associated with poorer renal survival, it seems feasible that persistent elevation of FHR5 may create a microenvironment of sustained complement activation and contributes to the progression of renal dysfunction in AAV.

The role of FHR5 in the development of AAV is unclear. The interaction between complement activation and neutrophil activation is crucial in the pathogenesis of AAV.7,8 Activation of the complement system acts as a positive feedback amplification of ANCA-induced neutrophil activation, promoting oxidative burst and degranulation of neutrophils, as well as the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (termed “NETosis”), resulting in endothelial damage.8 Moreover, moderate or strong glomerular deposition of C3b/iC3b/C3c, important products of complement activation, has been reported to be associated with more severe renal damage and poorer outcomes in AAV.26,37 Our current study observed colocalization of FHR5 with C3b/iC3b/C3c along glomerular capillary loops in patients with AAV, as well as significant association of glomerular FHR5 deposition with renal injury and ESKD. Given that FHR5 antagonizes the ability of FH in negatively regulating C3 activation,38 we hypothesized that FHR5 may contribute to renal injury in AAV by deregulating the AP activation. Interestingly, in addition to its well-established role as a key regulator of the AP, our previous study revealed that FH can directly inhibit ANCA-induced neutrophil activation.11 FHR5 has been reported to bind to neutrophils39; whether FHR5 competes with FH for neutrophil binding, and its potential role in neutrophil activation, warrants further investigation. In addition, in our current study, plasma FHR5 levels were found to be correlated with platelet counts and coagulation indices, including D-dimer and fibrin degradation products, suggesting its association with the hypercoagulable state in patients with AAV.40 This association was in line with a recent study showing that higher plasma FHR5 levels were associated with increased thrombin generation potential, and that recombinant FHR5 enhanced platelet activation in vitro.41 Moreover, platelet activation was able to trigger AP activation and amplify neutrophil activation.8,42 Collectively, we speculate that FHR5 serves as an important molecule bridging the activation of complements, coagulation system, and neutrophils.

The critical role of complement overactivation in AAV makes it an attractive therapeutic target. Following the successful application of avacopan, an oral C5a receptor antagonist, in AAV,12,43 other complement-targeted therapies such as iptacopan targeting factor B are emerging and under evaluation.44 Of note, defects in complement regulation have attracted increasing attention in recent years. Given that plasma FH levels were decreased in patients with active AAV and associated with disease severity,9,12 together with the findings on FHR5 in the current study, we suggest that the balance between FH and FHR5 is important, supporting FH and FHR5 as novel AP-specific therapeutic targets in AAV.

Our study has several limitations. First, majority of patients recruited in the current study were myeloperoxidase-ANCA positive because of the predominance of myeloperoxidase-AAV in Chinese patients45,46; thus it remains unclear whether our findings can be extrapolated to patients with proteinase 3–AAV. Second, given the retrospective design and limited sample size, the prognostic value of glomerular FHR5 deposition needs to be validated by large-scale prospective studies.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that FHR5 levels were elevated both in circulation and renal specimens in patients with AAV, and were associated with more active disease and more severe renal damage. Moreover, glomerular FHR5 deposition was an independent risk factor for ESKD in AAV. These findings provide new insights into complement dysregulation in AAV and suggest FHR5 as a potential therapeutic target.
