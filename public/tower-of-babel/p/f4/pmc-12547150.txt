ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study highlights the growing significance of flowers, especially roses, in the global agricultural market, where they are cultivated for both personal enjoyment and commercial purposes. Among these, roses are considered one of the most popular and widely cultivated flowers. However, rose cultivators often encounter substantial challenges due to diseases that affect the plants, which can lead to significant economic losses in the agricultural sector. Timely and accurate detection of these diseases is crucial to mitigating their impact, potentially saving millions of dollars in crop losses. The dataset utilized in this research consists of 10,000 high-quality images collected from an initial set of 3113 images taken from several rose gardens located in Amin Model Town, Khagan, Ashulia, and Savar, Bangladesh. The data collection process spanned from October 30 to November 6, 2024. These images are categorized into four distinct classes: Healthy Leaf, Black Spot, Leaf Hole, and Dry Leaf, representing various stages of disease development in rose plants. The images were captured using a Vivo IQOO Z9x phone, ensuring high resolution and detailed imagery necessary for research analysis. This dataset serves as a valuable resource for researchers and developers working on creating efficient algorithms for the early and accurate identification of rose leaf diseases. By leveraging machine learning and image processing techniques, these algorithms could significantly enhance disease detection and prevention, helping to safeguard crops and reduce economic losses in the agricultural sector.Keywords: Rose leaf disease, Plant pathology, Image classification, Smart farming, Deep learning, Machine learning dataset, Ornamental horticulture

Background

The Rose is considered the symbol of beauty and holds a significant place among all flowers [1]. The rose flower is not only commercially valuable but also holds an emotional place in people's lives. The Rose is seen as the symbol of love; it has unparalleled value for its charm and versatility in both decorative and economic uses [2]. Rose cultivation is considered a vital component of the agricultural economy in countries such as Bangladesh. The diseases of rose plants significantly impact cultivation, as rose plants are highly vulnerable to diseases [3]. Moreover, cultivators face significant losses due to the lack of early disease detection mechanisms, as well as environmental factors.

As the fields of Deep learning and Machine learning (ML) are now more advanced, they promise to provide significant solutions in identifying and managing plant diseases [[4], [5]]. For agricultural applications, accurate datasets are crucial for training the models [6]. The existing datasets often lack variety and region-specific information, which limits their utility. There are 3113 images of rose leaves in the dataset collected from Amin Model Town, Khagan, Ashulia, Savar, Bangladesh. The dataset is divided into four classes, of which three classes indicate the disease classes: dry leaf, black spot, and leaf holes. It aims to bridge the data gap and support the development of AI-powered tools for disease detection and informed agricultural decision-making. The dataset will be helpful in sustainable rose production and agricultural innovation [7].

A similar study, presented as RoseNet by Sazzad et al. [8], features a dataset of 917 images of rose leaves. The dataset was intended to help the machine learning models in the development of automated systems to identify the diseases affecting rose leaves. However, their dataset was not trained using machine learning algorithms. On the other hand, our study consists of a significantly larger dataset. Our dataset includes 3,113 high-quality images that were augmented to reach a total of 10,000 images, with 2,500 images assigned to each class (healthy, black spot, leaf hole, and dry leaf) and also pre-processed to guarantee a balanced and thorough representation for efficient model training and evaluation.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Theoretical knowledge gathering

Before we started gathering the dataset, we studied rose plants and their leaves in detail. We looked through academic publications, agricultural forums, and internet blogs to learn more about common illnesses that affect rose leaves, such as black spots, leaf holes, and dry leaf conditions. This information collection included signs, causes, and methods of detection for these illnesses. Agricultural websites and earlier studies on rose leaf health and disease control were among our sources of information. This preliminary stage was essential in providing us with the underlying knowledge we needed to guarantee the precision and applicability of the data that was gathered.

Leaf selection

Suitable leaves were collected for the dataset after gaining theoretical knowledge about rose leaf diseases. The selection process focused on ensuring that the collected leaves accurately represented all the categories in the dataset (See Fig. 3). Rose plants with accessible branches and sufficient leaves were prioritized to ensure diversity and quality in the dataset. Only mature leaves were chosen, as diseases like black spots and dry leaves predominantly affect mature leaves. Diseased leaves were selected based on visible symptoms, with a significant portion of the leaf exhibiting the specific condition being documented. This approach ensured that the dataset captured a comprehensive and precise representation of each disease type.

Sample image of leaf disease.

Image capturing of the collected leaves

The leaves were sorted carefully into their respective categories after the fieldwork was complete: healthy, black spot, leaf hole, and dry leaf. Each leaf was placed on a clean, white background to ensure a consistent and distraction-free setting. Care was taken to handle the leaves gently to prevent damage or distortion that could affect the dataset's quality.

High-resolution images were captured using a Vivo IQOO Z9x phone, ensuring clarity and precision for machine learning applications. The device was positioned approximately 30–40 cm from the target leaf to maintain consistent framing and detail. Images were taken under natural light conditions whenever possible to avoid shadows and ensure accurate color representation. The resolution of each image was maintained at 3264 × 2448 pixels with a 72-dpi resolution presented in Table 6.

Image processing and classification

The images in this dataset were initially captured with high-quality settings using a resolution of 3264 × 2448 pixels. To guarantee consistency and sharpness, every picture was shot against a white backdrop. For researchers' convenience and ease of use, each image was methodically tagged with descriptive file names that matched its respective classification, such as “healthy1” or “black_spot1”.

Model validation

In this study, we applied transfer learning using two pre-trained CNN architectures, ResNet50 and VGG16, to classify rose leaves into four categories: Black Spot, Leaf Holes, Dry Leaf, and Healthy Leaf.

The dataset contained 3113 images, which were divided into 80 % for training and 20 % for validation to ensure robust model development and evaluation. Both models were trained with a batch size of 32 and a learning rate of 0.0010. The ResNet50 model was trained for 10 epochs, while the VGG16 model was trained for 15 epochs, which allowed stable convergence without significant overfitting.

To verify the training and testing phases, we plotted the accuracy and loss curves for both training and validation. These plots provided a clear view of the learning behavior of the models and helped in identifying potential issues such as overfitting or underfitting. Furthermore, we used a confusion matrix to analyze the class-wise performance and a performance matrix (including metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score) to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the classification results.

As part of our future work, we intend to expand the dataset by incorporating more diverse leaf samples from different regions and seasons, which will further enhance the accuracy of our label annotations through expert reviews. Additionally, we are exploring the development of a real-time disease detection system, utilizing mobile and IoT-based apps. This system will empower farmers to detect diseases in their crops efficiently and in real-time, potentially revolutionizing smart farming and supporting more sustainable agricultural practices.

Limitations

This study faces several limitations that may impact the generalizability and accuracy of the results. Firstly, the images were captured using a cell phone camera, which may lead to issues related to image quality and resolution. Additionally, the data collection process was limited to a single location within a short time frame of approximately one week, which could restrict the diversity of the dataset. The dataset used in this study contains only four classes: Leaf Holes, Black Spot, Healthy Leaf, and Dry Leaf, which may not fully capture the wide range of potential leaf diseases. Moreover, a significant limitation is the absence of annotations in the dataset images, which could affect the precision of disease classification and further model refinement.

Ethics Statement

While collecting the data, it was made sure that no plants or animals became infected. The work procedure does not involve any human subjects, animal experiments, or data from social media platforms. Throughout the data collection process, the authors strictly adhered to data ethics standards.
