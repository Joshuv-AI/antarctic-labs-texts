Dental Management of a 9‐Year‐Old Child With Suspected CHARGE Syndrome: A Case Report and Brief Literature Review

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEarly recognition of syndromic features by dental professionals can lead to timely diagnosis and intervention, especially when systemic anomalies have been overlooked. Preventive and individualized dental management, supported by behavioral guidance, can achieve long‐term stability even in medically complex patients, including those with CHARGE syndrome.

Introduction

CHARGE syndrome is a rare, complex, multisystem congenital disorder first described by Pagon et al. in 1981. The acronym CHARGE stands for coloboma, heart defects, choanal atresia, growth retardation, genital abnormalities, and ear anomalies [1]. Its prevalence is estimated at 1 in 12,000–15,000 live births, with most cases occurring sporadically. Clinical manifestations are highly variable and may involve multiple organ systems. Choanal atresia or stenosis can cause respiratory distress and may be life‐threatening in neonates, particularly with bilateral obstruction [2]. Ocular coloboma may result in visual impairment or blindness, and involvement of the vestibulocochlear nerve can lead to hearing loss and balance disorders. Additional cranial nerve dysfunction may contribute to anosmia, facial dysmorphism, and feeding difficulties [3].

Other reported systemic features include congenital heart defects, growth retardation, developmental delay, tracheoesophageal fistula, renal anomalies, immune dysfunction, scoliosis, limb or facial asymmetry, and cleft palate [2]. Although intellectual disability is not universal, combined sensory impairments often result in significant learning difficulties [4]. Most cases are associated with mutations in the CHD7 gene on chromosome 8q12. Diagnosis during childhood remains challenging due to the wide variability in clinical presentation [3]. While systemic features of CHARGE syndrome are well documented, its oral and dental manifestations remain less clearly defined, as reported in previous studies [5, 6]. This case report describes the dental management of a child suspected of CHARGE syndrome, emphasizing the importance of multidisciplinary care and individualized treatment planning.

Case History

A 9‐year‐old boy presented in 2025 to the Pediatric Dentistry Department with a chief complaint of pain in the left mandibular region. Family history revealed that the father was a chemical warfare veteran. The patient was the second child of non‐consanguineous parents; the first child was healthy. No history of maternal exposure to teratogenic agents, radiation, or other relevant risk factors was reported. Parental ages at conception were 46 years (father) and 36 years (mother).

Prenatal and Perinatal History

Prenatal ultrasound at 30 weeks and 5 days of gestation revealed an estimated fetal weight at the 30th percentile and abdominal circumference at the 20th percentile, consistent with mild intrauterine growth restriction; however, values remained within acceptable clinical limits. The pregnancy progressed to full term, and delivery was performed via cesarean section. Birth weight was 2.55 kg, length 46 cm, and head circumference 34 cm. Neonatal adaptation, including feeding and respiration, was unremarkable.

Developmental History

The first primary tooth erupted at 7 months of age. Developmental milestones were achieved within normal limits, including walking at 15 months and first spoken words at 16 months. At present, the patient demonstrates proportionate growth slightly below average for age (height: 132 cm, weight: 26 kg). No significant growth delay was identified, although periodic follow‐up is recommended. The child was described by his family as socially active, talkative, and academically capable. He was not receiving any medications and was under follow‐up by pediatric cardiology and otolaryngology (ENT) services.

Medical History

A cardiac murmur was detected on day three of life by a pediatric cardiologist. At 2 months of age, mild unilateral congenital hydronephrosis was identified on renal ultrasound. By 3 months, the cardiac murmur had decreased in intensity. Audiological assessment revealed severe conductive hearing loss in the right ear (approximately 60%) associated with complete atresia of the external auditory canal. At 6 years of age, mild right‐sided pyelectasis was reported, and at 9 years, echocardiography revealed a 3 mm patent foramen ovale. Overall, systemic evaluation demonstrated congenital cardiac anomalies, renal involvement, and unilateral conductive hearing impairment.

Methods (Investigations, Differential Diagnosis, and Treatment Planning)

Investigations

The patient presented with poor oral hygiene and had no previous dental experience; this was his first dental visit. Written informed consent for the publication of clinical photographs was obtained from the patient's parents. The chief complaint was pain in the left mandibular region. Extraoral examination revealed midface hypoplasia, lower eyelid scleral show, incompetent lips, and facial asymmetry due to right‐sided facial nerve palsy. Microtia was also observed. Skeletal assessment suggested a Class II pattern with mandibular deficiency (Figure 1). Facial analysis showed an increased lower facial height relative to the upper face. Lateral profile assessment indicated mild mandibular retrusion, with the chin position approximately 5 mm posterior to the Frankfort horizontal plane reference. Lip position was within acceptable limits, although mild lip incompetence was noted. The nasolabial angle was slightly reduced (~90°–95°), consistent with midface deficiency. At rest, lip separation exceeded 2 mm, confirming lip incompetence (Figures 1 and 2).

Intraoral examination revealed normal soft tissues. The patient was in the mixed dentition stage with a Class I molar relationship. Multiple carious lesions were detected in primary teeth B, D, G, J, K, L, S, and T. All erupted permanent teeth were caries‐free. Enamel hypoplasia was observed in several primary teeth. Dental calculus was present in the posterior quadrants. The patient was classified as high caries risk based on caries risk assessment (CRA). Midline deviation to the left was noted. Maximum mouth opening was within normal limits. Overbite was 2 mm, and overjet was increased by 4 mm (Figure 3). Panoramic radiography confirmed the presence of all permanent tooth buds. No eruption delay was observed. Severe root resorption was noted in primary tooth K, along with carious involvement of multiple primary teeth (Figure 4).

Differential Diagnosis

Based on the systemic and craniofacial findings, the following syndromes were considered in the differential diagnosis: DiGeorge syndrome, VACTERL association, Kallmann syndrome, Kabuki syndrome, Goldenhar syndrome, Renal‐coloboma syndrome, Smith–Lemli–Opitz syndrome, Joubert syndrome, Cat eye syndrome, holoprosencephaly spectrum disorders, and velocardiofacial syndrome.

Genetic Evaluation

To further evaluate the suspected diagnosis and exclude overlapping syndromic conditions, the patient was referred for chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA). The analysis was performed using array‐based comparative genomic hybridization (array‐CGH) technology. The results revealed no clinically significant chromosomal abnormalities or aneuploidy (Figure 5). These findings helped exclude major chromosomal syndromes and supported further clinical correlation for a suspected CHARGE‐related phenotype.

Treatment

A multidisciplinary treatment plan was established in collaboration with pediatric cardiology and orthodontics. Initial management included oral hygiene instruction, professional prophylaxis, fluoride varnish application, dietary counseling, and fissure sealants for permanent molars #3, 14, 19, and 30. A preventive resin restoration was performed on tooth J, and an amalgam restoration was placed on tooth A. Primary teeth B, S, and T required vital pulp therapy due to deep carious lesions. Following caries removal, pulpotomy with mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) was performed, and all teeth were restored with stainless steel crowns to ensure long‐term durability.

Tooth B required pulpotomy after carious pulp exposure in the absence of signs or symptoms of irreversible pulpitis or periapical pathology. Teeth S and T showed extensive carious involvement approaching the pulp but remained asymptomatic; therefore, vital pulp therapy was indicated according to pediatric endodontic guidelines.

Tooth K was extracted due to advanced root resorption and compromised periodontal support. Tooth L was initially managed with pulpotomy; however, intraoperative findings of hyperemic pulp tissue with uncontrolled bleeding and inadequate coronal structure led to extraction.

Following the premature loss of teeth K and L, a lingual arch appliance was placed to maintain arch length and prevent mesial drift of permanent molars (Figures 6 and 7). An orthodontic consultation was also performed to monitor space management and future occlusal development. The patient was classified as high caries risk based on CRA; therefore, a preventive maintenance program was established, including three‐month recall visits, semiannual fluoride therapy, and reinforcement of oral hygiene measures. Behavioral management using the tell–show–do technique was employed throughout treatment. Although initial cooperation was limited, the patient's behavior improved progressively over five short appointments (15–20 min each), allowing completion of all planned procedures. Endocarditis prophylaxis was not indicated according to the pediatric cardiology consultation. The patient was followed up for 12 months with satisfactory clinical outcomes (Figures 8, 9, 10).

Discussion

CHARGE syndrome is a rare multisystem disorder with highly variable clinical manifestations. While systemic features such as cardiac defects, renal anomalies, and cranial nerve dysfunction are well documented, craniofacial and dental manifestations remain underreported and poorly defined [5, 6]. Diagnosis is often delayed because of phenotypic variability and the absence of a clear genotype–phenotype correlation [7, 8]. Approximately 80% of children with CHARGE syndrome have congenital heart defects of varying severity. Additional systemic manifestations include renal anomalies, urinary tract abnormalities, and reflux [9]. Multidisciplinary evaluation and coordinated long‐term care are therefore essential for optimizing outcomes in these patients [2, 3, 10].

The molecular basis of CHARGE syndrome is primarily attributed to pathogenic variants in the CHD7 gene located on chromosome 8q12. CHD7 encodes a chromatin‐remodeling protein involved in the regulation of gene expression during embryogenesis, particularly in neural crest cell development. Disruption of these processes contributes to the multisystem abnormalities of CHARGE syndrome [11]. Despite the established role of CHD7, a consistent genotype–phenotype correlation has not been demonstrated [5, 6].

Although molecular testing improves diagnostic accuracy, CHARGE syndrome remains primarily a clinical diagnosis supported by genetic findings [12]. Pathogenic CHD7 variants are identified in approximately 70%–80% of patients fulfilling established clinical criteria [13]. Chromosomal microarray analysis cannot detect most sequence‐level CHD7 variants, including point mutations and small insertions or deletions [14]. Therefore, patients with a strong clinical suspicion of CHARGE syndrome may require additional investigations using next‐generation sequencing approaches [15].

In the present case, chromosomal microarray analysis did not reveal clinically significant copy‐number variations. Although CMA does not always detect pathogenic variants in individuals with CHARGE syndrome, it remains the recommended first‐line genetic test [14]. According to Blake's diagnostic criteria, the patient fulfilled two major criteria (facial nerve palsy and characteristic ear anomaly) and several minor criteria, supporting the designation of suspected CHARGE syndrome (Table 1) [10].

Historically, several diagnostic frameworks have been proposed because of the wide phenotypic spectrum of CHARGE syndrome. Blake et al. introduced one of the most widely accepted sets of criteria [5, 10]. Various syndromes, including DiGeorge syndrome, VACTERL association, Kabuki syndrome, Goldenhar syndrome, renal‐coloboma syndrome, Smith–Lemli–Opitz syndrome, Joubert syndrome, cat eye syndrome, and velocardiofacial syndrome, should be considered in the differential diagnosis because of overlapping manifestations [5, 16].

Oral and dental manifestations in CHARGE syndrome are heterogeneous. Reported findings include delayed or ectopic eruption, tooth agenesis, prolonged retention of primary teeth, taurodontism, enamel defects, increased plaque accumulation, feeding difficulties, cleft lip and palate, xerostomia, temporomandibular joint problems, and increased susceptibility to dental caries [17]. In contrast, the present patient exhibited normal eruption timing without agenesis but demonstrated enamel hypoplasia affecting the primary teeth, poor oral hygiene, and multiple carious lesions (Tables 2 and 3). Cranial nerve involvement contributes substantially to oral and functional complications in affected individuals. Facial nerve palsy has been reported in approximately 40% of patients with CHARGE syndrome and may contribute to facial asymmetry and compromised oral function. Hearing impairment is also common and may complicate communication during dental care [18].

Dental management in patients with suspected CHARGE syndrome is often complicated by systemic comorbidities, sensory impairments, airway considerations, and behavioral challenges. Short appointments, individualized behavioral guidance techniques, and close communication with medical specialists are essential components of successful care [19]. Preventive strategies, including parental supervision of oral hygiene, dietary counseling, professional prophylaxis, fluoride therapy, and regular recall visits, should be initiated early because of the increased risk of oral disease [19, 20]. In the present case, despite years of specialist follow‐up, the possibility of an underlying syndromic diagnosis had not previously been raised. This observation highlights the important role of pediatric dentists in recognizing patterns of craniofacial and dental anomalies that may indicate broader systemic conditions and in facilitating timely referral for multidisciplinary evaluation [19, 21].

Behavioral difficulties have been described in children with CHARGE syndrome and may influence treatment acceptance and compliance [22]. In this patient, cooperation improved progressively through the use of the tell–show–do technique, allowing comprehensive treatment to be completed without sedation or general anesthesia.

The present report contributes to the limited literature regarding dental management in children with suspected CHARGE syndrome. In addition to describing craniofacial and oral findings, it provides detailed information on preventive and restorative interventions, behavioral guidance strategies, space management considerations, and 12‐month follow‐up outcomes. Such reports may help clinicians recognize unusual presentations of syndromic conditions and support the development of evidence‐based approaches to oral healthcare in medically complex pediatric patients.

Conclusion

CHARGE syndrome is a rare multisystem disorder with significant variability in craniofacial and dental manifestations, requiring individualized and multidisciplinary management. Early recognition of associated oral findings, combined with preventive and minimally invasive dental strategies, is essential for maintaining oral health and improving long‐term outcomes in affected children. This case underscores the importance of comprehensive dental evaluation in identifying previously unrecognized syndromic conditions. Pediatric dentists may play a key role in the early suspicion of complex systemic disorders, particularly when craniofacial and dental anomalies coexist with unexplained medical findings. Although genetic confirmation was not obtained in this case, clinical diagnosis based on established criteria, together with coordinated dental and medical care, enabled effective management and satisfactory functional outcomes.
