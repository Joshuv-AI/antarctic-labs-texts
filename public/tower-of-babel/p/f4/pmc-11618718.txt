Parental SES and family support as predictors of educational level: Testing the buffering effect of effortful control and peer support
Authors: Matthijs Fakkel, Margot Peeters, Susan Branje, Gonneke W J M Stevens
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionExposure to family risk factors increases adolescents' chances of attaining a lower educational level. However, some adolescents attain a high educational level despite being exposed to family risk factors such as a lower parental socioeconomic status (SES) or receiving less family support.MethodUsing data from the Dutch TRAILS cohort study (N T1 = 2175; M age = 11.1, SD = 0.55, 50.8% female), we investigated if higher levels of effortful control and peer support can buffer against the negative effects of a lower parental SES and less family support on educational level. Two multinomial logistic regressions were performed (from early to mid‐adolescence and from mid‐adolescence to young adulthood) with post hoc tests to contrast four ordinal educational levels: practical vocational, theoretical vocational, higher general, and (pre‐)university.ResultsAdolescents with a higher parental SES were consistently more likely to end up at a higher educational level, but family support was hardly associated with educational level. Neither effortful control nor peer support buffered the associations of parental SES and family support with educational level. Effortful control did have a positive direct (compensatory) effect on the educational level.ConclusionWe conclude that other individual competencies or more structural changes may be more helpful buffers for reducing socioeconomic inequalities in educational attainment.Keywords: parental support, peer relationships, self‐evaluation, SES, self‐monitoring, self‐regulation, social class

INTRODUCTION

Even in some societies that are considered meritocracies, inequalities in educational opportunities are persistent or on the rise—after decades of improvements (Inspectie van het Onderwijs, 2016, 2020; OECD, 2016). The considerable impact of family background factors on the educational attainment of adolescents is considered by some as unfair and economically inefficient (De Beer & Van Pinxteren, 2016; Heckman, 2006). For example, the educational attainment of adolescents from a lower socioeconomic background tends to be obstructed by limited access to study materials and certified teachers (Brandén et al., 2023; Conger & Donnellan, 2007). Similarly, adolescents who receive less socioemotional family support throughout their academic endeavors tend to end up at a lower educational level than adolescents who receive plenty of such support (Wentzel, 1994). These inequalities in opportunities contribute to different educational attainments despite similarities in educational potential across adolescents from different family backgrounds (Fergusson et al., 2008; Zumbuehl et al., 2022).

Some adolescents, however, attain a higher educational level despite being exposed to family risk factors. Such resilient adolescents may possess certain assets like effortful control or resources like peer support that help negate the potential negative impact of family risk factors on their educational level. As a result of these sources of resilience, adolescents from a lower socioeconomic background may have more similar odds of attaining higher education as adolescents from a higher socioeconomic background (Kong, 2020).

While macro‐level policies and interventions are the primary means to provide equality of opportunity (e.g., sufficient funding), such isolated attempts are at risk of having a limited positive or negligible impact—to the extent that educational inequalities keep rising despite policy implementations (Exalto et al., 2023). Even effective policies are generally implemented to serve another (later) cohort of adolescents than the one in which the educational inequalities were originally observed (Oliver et al., 2014). It is thus argued that overcoming inequalities of educational opportunities for example as the result of differential family risk factors requires combining multiple sources of resilience (e.g., individual, social, and societal; Evans et al., 2013). More so than children, adolescents have a characteristic desire for agency and control over their educational outcomes (Haffejee & Theron, 2019). Hence, identifying individual sources of resilience can provide opportunities for adolescents to independently attenuate the negative effects of family risk factors on their educational attainment. Given the complex and resource‐demanding nature of longitudinal developmental research required for these types of research questions, relatively little is still known about the extent to which individual adolescent sources of resilience can attenuate the negative effects of family risk factors on educational attainment (Shifrer & Pals, 2021).

Utilizing high‐quality longitudinal data of the TRAILS cohort, the aims of this study are twofold: (1) to identify the extent to which family background factors (i.e., parental SES and family support) affect educational attainment in mid‐adolescence and young adulthood; and (2) to identify the extent to which adolescent resilience factors (i.e., effortful control and peer support) can negate the associations between family risk factors and educational attainment.

Parental SES and family support as predictors of educational attainment

Characteristics of the family environment can serve as both protective factors and risk factors. Risk factors refer to all aspects of adolescents' lives that provide challenges and obstacles that require additional assets or resources to overcome, otherwise resulting in unfavorable outcomes (Fergus & Zimmerman, 2005). Family risk factors can come in a multitude of forms, most prominently in terms of lacking material or social resources, such as having parents with a lower SES or receiving less family support than desired. Growing up with risk factors in the family environment can potentially deprive adolescents of opportunities for optimal participation and development, and eventually in the attainment of an educational level below their potential. Family protective factors may be reflected in terms of sufficient resources and stimulation for optimal cognitive and noncognitive development, educational participation, and ultimately translate to better odds to attain higher educational levels (Ryberg et al., 2017). The resulting inequalities in educational attainment go on to persist in occupational status and income, in health and well‐being, and in terms of broader societal participation (Kromydas, 2017; Mackenbach, 2012; Raghupathi & Raghupathi, 2020).

Parents with a lower SES generally have fewer financial resources to invest in the academic development of their adolescents, such as basic school equipment or extracurricular activities (Conger & Donnellan, 2007). Furthermore, parents with a higher SES generally have a better understanding of the educational system and its requirements which can help adolescents at crucial moments in their educational trajectory, such as during school transitions (Dumont et al., 2019; Forster & van de Werfhorst, 2020; Lareau & Cox, 2011). While macro‐level and school‐level policies may offer partial solutions to such inequalities related to differences in material resources across families (e.g., laptop sponsoring), utilizing such facilities tends to be complex, time‐consuming, and stigmatizing (Simons et al., 2018). Furthermore, such structures mostly compensate for basic facilities, while adolescents from a higher SES background have access to extensive facilities. For example, the annual expenses for additional private tutoring in the Netherlands have risen from approximately €30 million in 1995 to over €200 million in 2016, and are mostly spent by parents with a higher SES (Elffers, 2019). Other important risk factors experienced in a low SES context include the lack of a quiet place at home for studying and the threat of violence in the neighborhood (McLaughlin & Sheridan, 2016; Mills‐Koonce et al., 2016).

Exact estimates between parental SES and educational attainment in young adulthood can differ across studies as a result of using different SES measures (Black & Devereux, 2011; OECD, 2018; Rea, 2023). Using one or few indicators of low SES can result in an overestimation of participating families with a low SES (Fakkel et al., 2020). For a low parental SES to qualify as a valid family risk factor, it is therefore important to combine multiple indicators, such as mother's and father's educational attainment, occupational status, and family income (Fergus & Zimmerman, 2005). Such an approach also reduces measurement error and improves model parsimony (Fergusson et al., 2008). In contrast, educational attainment in young adulthood is highly predictive of occupational status, career opportunities, income, and wealth trajectories in later adulthood, and is commonly perceived as a solid indicator of young adulthood SES—while acknowledging that some mobility is still possible (Erola et al., 2016).

Besides parental SES, the extent to which adolescents receive family support is also positively associated with educational level (Conger & Donnellan, 2007). A supportive family is characterized by positive communication habits, a positive handling of unexpected events, and acceptance and trust toward one another (Miller et al., 2000). Adolescents with more family support tend to have more constructive discussions with their parents, receive more encouragement, and receive help to overcome challenges in school, while also being held accountable for their actions and decisions (Kohl et al., 2000; Suizzo & Stapleton, 2007). Such family support fosters a sense of control, belonging, and security in adolescents (Surjadi et al., 2011). In contrast, adolescents who receive less family support are more likely to feel unmotivated, insecure, and have lower self‐efficacy (Bradley et al., 2001; Fan & Williams, 2010). Throughout one's school career, adolescents are likely to experience numerous educational challenges and even setbacks as inevitable stepping stones in their intellectual and psychological development (Roeser et al., 2000). In a highly supportive family environment such challenges are generally overcome, which helps boost school performance over time, but in a low supportive family environment, such experiences can slow down, discourage, and limit adolescents' attainment of a higher educational level, despite having the potential.

Most parents try to be as supportive as possible, but stress, tension, child‐rearing difficulties, parent–adolescent conflicts, health issues, marital issues, and other aspects of life can lower the levels of family support at crucial moments. Measures of reported emotional climate at home and the stress levels of parents also often fall under the umbrella term of family support (Conger & Donnellan, 2007; Martin et al., 2010). As such, a lower parental SES and less family support may be considered family risk factors for adolescents to attain a lower educational level, whereas higher parental SES and more family support may be considered protective factors to attain a higher educational level.

Effortful control and peer support as buffers against parental SES and family support

In addition to top‐down research that identifies mechanisms linking family background factors to educational attainment, important insights may be obtained from bottom‐up research that identifies individual‐level resilience factors which can flexibly and effectively be applied to weaken these complex associations (Adamecz et al., 2023; Shifrer & Pals, 2021). Resilient adolescents are characterized by the ability to achieve favorable outcomes, such as a higher educational level, despite having been exposed to risk factors that would generally lead to less favorable outcomes, such as attaining an educational level below their potential (Fergus & Zimmerman, 2005; Wright & Masten, 2005). Adolescents may derive resilience from assets within themselves or resources such as their family or the broader social environment. Previous research identified assets such as perseverance and self‐efficacy to be predominant in resilient adolescents (Afifi & Macmillan, 2011; Smokowski et al., 1999). Similarly, resilient adolescents possessed considerable resources such as teacher and school support and community support relationships (Brooks, 2006; Davies et al., 2011). Such sources of adolescent resilience can have a moderating, protective effect that attenuates the association between family background factors and educational attainment, or alternatively have a direct, compensatory effect on educational attainment—independent of the negative effects of risk factors (Fergus & Zimmerman, 2005).

Resilient adolescents have been argued to be particularly adept at (1) recognizing a potentially risky situation, (2) aligning behaviors with a long‐term goal, and (3) developing a strong sense of self‐efficacy (Dillon et al., 2007); all of which are closely related to the concept of effortful control. Effortful control refers to the capacity to deliberately and voluntarily suppress a dominant response and instead perform a subdominant response (Kochanska et al., 2000). Closely related to the concept of self‐regulation, adolescents with high levels of effortful control are particularly adept in attentional control, inhibitory control, and behavioral activation (Capaldi & Rothbart, 1992). As such, adolescents with more effortful control are better capable of resisting temptation, overcoming distractions, and working toward long‐term goals on a day‐to‐day basis (Maloney et al., 2012).

Effortful control can enable adolescents to down‐regulate distracting thoughts and feelings, and focus attention on positive long‐term goals (Lengua et al., 2008). Similarly, adolescents with more effortful control may be able to mitigate the negative effects of less family support by being emotionally and attentionally flexible when an unexpected event or conflict occurs within the family (Loukas & Roalson, 2006; Thompson et al., 2020). Hence, adolescents with more effortful control may be less affected by family risk factors in their educational attainment.

Besides effortful control as a resilience asset, adolescents may utilize peer support as a resilience resource against family risk factors. Peer support refers to the behavioral confirmation and affection that adolescents receive from classmates and friends, and serves as an important source of social well‐being (Ormel et al., 1999). Having more peer support may serve as a source of resilience against the negative effects of family risk factors such as a lower parental SES or less family support on the educational level of adolescents (Bradley et al., 2021; Winne & Nesbit, 2010). Adolescents with more peer support frequently experience kind words of encouragement, and feel connected and engaged, whereas adolescents with less peer support have fewer such experiences, and may even be subject to bullying (Hombrados‐Mendieta et al., 2012; Wentzel et al., 2016). Peer support may boost adolescents' sense of self‐efficacy and sense of belonging, thereby serving as a potential buffer against possible negative effects of a lower parental SES or less family support on the educational level (Chen et al., 2017; Malecki & Demaray, 2006; Rodgers & Rose, 2002). Potentially, negative associations between family risk factors such as low parental SES and low family support with educational attainment can be attenuated by adolescent sources of resilience such as effortful control and peer support, though previous research has not comprehensively assessed these specific associations.

The Dutch educational system

Educational stratification happens relatively early in the Netherlands at around the age of 11–12, compared to the OECD average of age 14, and the average age of 16 in Scandinavian countries. Based on a standardized national cognitive test and teacher advice, adolescents are recommended to go to one of four educational tracks after primary school: (1) lower vocational track, (2) intermediate vocational track, (3) higher vocational track, (4) academic track. These secondary education tracks continue into tertiary education, with some opportunities for mobility (see also Schmengler et al., 2021).

In educational systems marked by early tracking, such as in the Netherlands, a relatively strong association is observed between parental education and adolescents' school performance (Van de Werfhorst & Mijs, 2010). Adolescents from a higher SES are more likely to already be showing their competence at an early age after having received plenty of stimulation and opportunities for development, whereas adolescents from a lower SES may require more time to display their potential. Furthermore, higher tracks require longer to be completed, thus resulting in adolescents in vocational education entering the labor market around the age of 20 years old and adolescents in university education around the age of 22–25.

The combination of factors that underlie the association between parental SES and educational attainment and family support and educational attainment may be complex and various, to some extent differing between socioeconomic strata and even between seemingly similar families. In this study, we aim to explore specific adolescent sources of resilience, that is, effortful control and peer support, that may be flexibly and effectively applied across situations so that adolescents with a lower parental SES or less family support have similar odds of attaining higher education as adolescents with higher parental SES and more family support.

Present study

In this study, we assessed effortful control and peer support as moderators in the associations of parental SES and family support with educational level (see Figure 1). Research questions and hypotheses were preregistered at OSF: [anonymized]. We first assessed if lower parental SES (RQ1.1) and less family support (RQ1.2) are risk factors for the educational level that adolescents attain. Next, we assessed if the association between parental SES and educational level was mitigated by effortful control (RQ2.1) and peer support (RQ2.2). Similarly, we assessed if the association between family support and educational level was mitigated by effortful control (RQ3.1) and peer support (RQ3.2).

Diagram of tested associations, from early to mid‐adolescence (Model 1) and from mid‐adolescence to young adulthood (Model 2).

We expected to find that adolescents with a lower parental SES (H1.1) and less family support (H1.2) attain a lower educational level. We also expected that effortful control (H2.1) and peer support (H2.2) buffer against (i.e., mitigate) the effect of parental SES on educational level. Similarly, effortful control (H3.1) and peer support (H3.2) were expected to buffer against the effect of family support on educational level.

All associations of interest were examined in two developmentally different age periods (i.e., early to mid‐adolescence and mid‐adolescence to young adulthood) coinciding with two different stages of education (i.e., secondary education and tertiary education): from age 11 to age 16 (Model 1) and from age 16 to age 26 (Model 2).

METHOD

Participants and procedure

Data from three waves (T1–T3–T6) of the TRacking Adolescents' Individual Lives Survey (TRAILS) were used, when participants were approximately 11, 16, and 26 years old. TRAILS is a population‐based prospective cohort study focused on adolescents and their families from Groningen, the northern region of the Netherlands (Oldehinkel et al., 2015). Eligible adolescents were identified through municipality registers and registers of 122 participating schools (i.e., 90.4% of the schools accommodating 90.3% of eligible children in the selected municipalities). Adolescents and their families were first informed about (the purpose of) the TRAILS study through brochures and school visits, after which telephone calls were made to inquire about participation and answer any remaining questions. This recruitment approach resulted in the inclusion of 2229 out of an eligible 3134 adolescents and their families (71.1%). Written informed consent was obtained from both adolescents and their parents before inclusion in the study. Ethical approval for TRAILS was obtained from the Dutch national ethics committee Central Committee on Research Involving Human Subjects (#NL38237.042.11).

Adolescents in the baseline sample (N = 2229; M age = 11.1, SD = 0.55; 50.8% girls) were followed between 2000 and 2017 with assessments roughly every 2.5 years, from age 11 (T1) to age 26 (T6). For this study, data from T1, T3 (age 16), and T6 were used. The majority of participants identified as having a Dutch ethnicity (89.4%) and the sample has a higher socioeconomic status than the Dutch average (Fakkel et al., 2020).

Measures

Educational level

Adolescents self‐reported their educational level at age 16 (T3) and at age 26 (T6). Educational level was measured using a single item, and (ordinally) categorized as: (1) practical vocational education, (2) theoretical vocational education, (3) higher general education, and (4) (pre‐)university education (Peeters et al., 2021). At age 16 (T3), the educational level in secondary school is established for most adolescents in the Dutch educational system (Schmengler et al., 2022). At age 26 (T6), most adolescents have completed their tertiary level of education, so this measure reflected educational attainment. If educational level was missing at T3, data from T2 (age 14) were used. Similarly, if educational level was missing at T6, data from T5 (age 22) were used.

Parental SES

Parental socioeconomic status was assessed at T1 using five indicators: mother's and father's educational attainment, mother's and father's occupational level based on the International Standard Classification of Occupations (ISCO‐88), and family income. A single SES variable was created by averaging the items after standardization, with a higher score indicating a higher SES. This SES variable has previously been found to explain 61.2% of the variance in the five indicators with good internal consistency, α = .84 (Amone‐P'Olak et al., 2009; Vollebergh et al., 2005).

Family support

Family support was assessed by combining (shortened versions of) the Family Assessment Device (FAD; Epstein et al., 1983) and the Parental Stress Index (PSI; Abidin, 1983; de Brock et al., 1992). Both the FAD and the PSI were filled out by the primary parent at T1 and T3. The FAD measures family functioning and patterns of interactions among family members. The FAD consists of 12 items (e.g., “We avoid talking about our fears and worries”), that can be scored from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 4 (Strongly agree). A higher score on the FAD indicates poorer family functioning.

The PSI measures the relative stress in the parent–adolescent relationship. The PSI consists of 24 items (e.g., “My child seems more difficult to take care of than other children”), that can be scored from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 6 (Strongly agree). A higher score on the PSI indicates the parent commonly feels more stressed about raising their adolescent. The FAD and the PSI are positively correlated (r = .4) and all 36 items combined have high internal consistency (Cronbach's α T1 = 0.90; α T3 = 0.91). Standardized sum scores of the FAD and the PSI were combined and recoded into a single variable, with a higher score indicating more family support.

Effortful control

The primary parent reported on their adolescent's effortful control by filling out the effortful control‐subscale of the Early Adolescent Temperament Questionnaire, at both T1 and T3 (EATQ‐R; Ellis & Rothbart, 2001; Hartman, 2000). This subscale consists of 11 items (e.g., “If my child has to do a difficult task, he/she starts immediately”), and can be answered from 1 (“Almost never true”) to 5 (“Almost always true”). The items have good internal consistency (α = .86). A standardized sum score was calculated, with a higher score indicating better effortful control.

Peer support

Peer support was measured by adolescents' self‐report on the Social Production Functions questionnaire (SPF; Lindenberg, 1996). Adolescents filled out 17 items at T1 and 11 items at T3 on behavioral confirmation and affection by classmates (e.g., “Many classmates are happy with me just the way I am”). Items were scored on a scale from 1 (“Never”) to 5 (“Always”). Internal consistency among items was good at both T1 (α = .93) and T3 (α = .84). Items were combined into a sum score, with a higher score indicating receiving more peer support.

Statistical procedure

Assumption checks and data cleaning

As a result of left‐skewed data, educational level was measured on an ordinal scale with four categories. Furthermore, the proportional odds assumption for educational level as an ordinal variable was found to be violated. Hence, a multinomial logistic regression was preferred over a polynomial logistic regression and over the pre‐registered continuous structural equation model (anonymized).

Peer support at age 11 and family support at age 16 were also non‐normally distributed. A total of 54 outliers were identified. These 54 adolescents on average had a lower parental SES (t = 3.25, df = 56.31, p < .01) and less family support (t = 3.81, df = 49.86, p < .001) than adolescents in the remaining sample. Excluding these outliers was an essential part of the data preparation to make our models converge. After applying a power transformation and the removal of 54 outliers in total (2.4% of total participants), linearity was established between continuous predictor variables and the logit of the outcome variable for both Model 1 and Model 2 (N = 2171). Alternative attempts to retain outliers, such as winsorizing, did not resolve the above issues with non‐normality. No multicollinearity was observed between our variables of interest (all VIF < 1.2).

Multiple imputation procedure

As a result of attrition and missing data across 15 years of data collection, observations on adolescents' educational level dropped from N = 2229 at T1 to N = 2017 at T3 (−9.5%) to N = 1517 at T6 (−24.8%). Similarly, observations on family support (−26.9%), effortful control (−24.3%), and peer support (−24.0%) dropped over time (from T1 to T3). Attrition was associated with being men (χ2(1) = 54.09, p < .001), being older (t(2226) = 2.24, p < .05), being from a lower SES background (t(1918) = −14.49, p < .001), having less family support (t (2036) = −2.11, p < .05), and having less effortful control (t(1981) = −5.88, p < .001) at baseline.

The missing data in our data set were not missing completely at random (Little's MCAR test; χ 2 = 1015.98 (490), p < .001). To minimize missing data bias we first performed multiple imputations on our data set (N = 2175) before analyzing the multinomial logistic regressions. Variables included in the multiple imputation procedure were parental SES at T1, age at T1; family support, effortful control, and peer support at T1 and T3; and educational level at T3 and T6 (i.e., 10 variables). Predictive mean matching was the preferred imputation method of choice to minimize bias and yield small confidence intervals (CI) while still maintaining power (Peeters et al., 2015; Van Buuren, 2018). A total of five imputed data sets was obtained and split for separate analyses, after which the results were pooled (Buuren & Groothuis‐Oudshoorn, 2011). Descriptive statistics (Table 1) and Pearson's correlations (Table 2) of standardized variables were obtained from the pooled imputed data sets. Zero‐order correlations between variables of interest vary in strength, with both weaker associations (e.g., between peer support and educational level) and stronger associations (e.g., between parental SES and educational level).

While performing our analyses on complete cases only (i.e., N = 1517) would yield estimates based on truly observed data, such an approach is not necessarily deemed most optimal. Potential downsides for example include a highly selective final sample, less power, and less robust findings (i.e., less generalizable). Estimating multiple imputations tackles these issues to a considerable extent, though also has the important downside of being based on the assumption that data is not missing at random (Peeters et al., 2015; Pepinsky, 2018). By reporting both approaches we offer multiple perspectives from which readers may draw their own conclusion.

Main analyses

Two separate multinomial logistic regressions were performed as main analyses, for Model 1 and Model 2 (Figure 1). For both multinomial logistic regressions we performed (1) a model fitting procedure and (2) a model testing procedure. For the model fitting procedure, we used a nested model approach to assess the individual contribution of the two main predictors (i.e., parental SES and family support) and each of the four interaction terms (i.e., between parental SES and effortful control; family support and effortful control; parental SES and peer support; and family support and peer support) in Model 1 and Model 2. Using a backward elimination method, the model fit of the full model (four main effects and four interaction effects) was compared to a nested model excluding the particular interaction effect of interest (four main effects and three interaction effects) (Tutz et al., 2015). If the full model had a better fit to the data than the nested model (based on the likelihood ratio test), the interaction term would be retained for model testing (see Table 3).

RESULTS

Contrary to our expectations, the model fitting procedure indicated that none of the interaction effects improved the model fit (Table 3). Hence, none of the interaction effects were included in the final Model 1 and Model 2 for the model testing procedure (i.e., post hoc group comparisons). In Model 1 (Table 4), educational level at age 16 was predicted by parental SES and family support at age 11, while controlling for main effects of effortful control and peer support at age 11. Based on the likelihood ratio test (χ 2(9) = 937.19, p < .001), Model 1 had a good fit to the data, and explained a considerable part of the variation in educational level at age 16 (Cox‐Snell R 2 = 0.35; Nagelkerke R 2 = 0.37; McFadden R 2 = 0.15).

In our model testing procedure, post hoc group comparisons were made to assess if lower parental SES (RQ1.1) and less family support (RQ1.2) are risk factors for the educational level that adolescents attain. As expected (H1.1), parental SES was consistently positively associated with educational level at age 16 and age 26 (in 12 out of 12 post hoc comparisons). Contrary to our expectations (H1.2), family support at age 11 and age 16 was mostly not associated with the educational level at age 16 and age 26 respectively (in 9 out of 12 comparisons). Moreover, family support at age 11 and age 16 was in part negatively associated with educational level at age 16 and age 26 (in 3 out of 12 comparisons)—opposite to our expectations. In particular, adolescents who reported higher levels of family support were more likely to end up in practical vocational education instead of in (pre‐)university education (both at age 16 and age 26).

Though the main effects of effortful control and peer support were not part of our hypotheses, their inclusion and interpretation as control variables are relevant for understanding our (null) findings. Effortful control (at age 11 and age 16) was consistently positively associated with educational level (at age 16 and age 26) (in 12 out of 12 comparisons). Peer support at age 11 and age 16 was mostly not associated with educational level at age 16 and age 26 (in 10 out of 12 comparisons), or negatively associated (in 2 out of 12 comparisons). More specifically, adolescents who reported higher levels of peer support at age 11 had increased odds of ending up in practical or theoretical vocational education instead of preuniversity education at age 16.

To test the robustness of our findings, we performed two sensitivity analyses (per model): a multinomial logistic regression on complete cases, and a multinomial logistic regression without influential cases. Results of the sensitivity analyses were mostly similar to the original outcomes; more details can be found in Supplementary Materials A and B. Rerunning our models with the highest level of education from either parent as indicator of SES instead of the composite measure also did not considerably affect our findings or conclusions. Last, when we ran our models with the FAD or the PSI separately instead of the combined measure of family support, less variance in educational attainment was explained. All analyses considered, parental SES and effortful control both have a moderate to strong positive effect on educational attainment, whereas other associations are nonsignificant.

For exploratory purposes, we also assessed the interaction between parental SES and family support to identify potential buffering effects of family support on the association between parental SES and educational level. Including the interaction effect between parental SES and family support at age 11 did not explain more variance in educational level at age 16 than a model without this interaction effect, χ 2(27) = 6.51, p = .10. Similarly, including the interaction effect between parental SES and family support at age 16 did not explain more variance in educational level at age 26 than a model without this interaction effect, χ 2(27) = 1.00, p = .80. Hence, family support does not attenuate the association between parental SES and educational level throughout adolescence.

DISCUSSION

In this study, individual adolescent sources of resilience did not buffer the negative effects of family background factors on educational attainment. More specifically, adolescents with a lower parental SES were more likely to attain a lower educational level. Higher levels of peer support did not serve as a protective factor against the negative effect of low parental SES on educational attainment. Higher levels of effortful control also did not serve as a protective factor, but effortful control did have a positive direct (compensatory) effect on educational attainment. This direct positive effect of effortful control on educational attainment was similar for adolescents with a higher and a lower parental SES, thus in itself does not reduce socioeconomic inequalities. Receiving less family support was not found to be a risk factor for educational attainment. These findings were mostly consistent across adolescence and young adulthood. Other individual competencies or structural factors may be more helpful buffers for reducing unequal opportunities in education.

Our primary purpose was to investigate if adolescents who are exposed to family risk factors attain a lower level of education than adolescents who are not exposed to such family risk factors, and if certain individual resilience factors may weaken this association. Research that implies that higher educational levels are more favorable than lower educational levels is at risk of sounding stigmatizing (Simons et al., 2018). Aiming to have as many adolescents as possible attaining the highest educational level is not necessarily the goal of educational systems or society as a whole (De Beer & Van Pinxteren, 2016; Kromydas, 2017), though this may nonetheless be the experience of adolescents, contributing to heightened levels of academic pressure (Cosma et al., 2022). This current study aims to reduce the stigma—or not contribute to it—by making no explicit value judgments about differing types of education.

Theoretical implications

Considering that adolescents' effortful control does not compensate for the lack of opportunities associated with a low SES context, our findings nuance the idea that adolescents from disadvantaged backgrounds have similar opportunities to attain a higher educational level as adolescents from more privileged backgrounds (Duckworth & Schoon, 2012; Tough, 2012). Similarly, though strong peer support is generally considered as a positive factor for adolescent well‐being and school performance (Buchanan & Bowen, 2008; Martinot et al., 2022), it may in itself not serve as a partial buffer against structural inequality. Alternatively, strong peer support may in part be based on externalizing behaviors that promote popularity, such as skipping classes (Martinot et al., 2022; Wang & Degol, 2014). Such types of peer support may explain why adolescents with more peer support had an increased chance of ending up in theoretical or practical vocational education instead of (pre‐)university education, though the majority of our findings indicate no association between peer support and educational level.

Alternatively, the occasional negative associations between peer support and educational level may indicate that adolescents who experience more peer support feel more freedom to choose an educational level that matches their interests and talents perhaps more so than their intellectual aptitude or family expectations and pressures. Such an interpretation can have a meaningful contribution to the contemporary debate of school pressure and the shortage of vocationally educated workers in the Netherlands (see e.g., Cosma et al., 2022).

Most of our findings indicate that parental SES, parental support, and educational level are positively associated (e.g., Table 2). However, in some instances, we found that adolescents with a higher parental SES but less family support were more likely to end up in (pre‐) university education than in practical vocational education (Tables 4 and 5). This suggests that in certain low‐SES contexts adolescents are more strongly connected to their family whereas in certain high‐SES contexts adolescents may be pushed more toward individual independence (Stephens et al., 2014). Besides such a social strength‐based interpretation of the low SES context, it must be noted that some adolescents in a low SES context have health or developmental challenges underlying their extra parental support and that some adolescents in a high SES context have successfully converted their parental capital to their own capital. All findings combined, adolescents with a lower parental SES mostly reported less parental support and ended up in lower educational levels than adolescents with a higher parental SES.

Our finding that adolescents with a lower parental SES were more likely to be in a lower educational level is in line with earlier research indicating the still strong impact of adolescent's parental SES even in meritocratic societies (Sandel, 2021). Adolescents with a lower parental SES may have access to fewer material resources. Moreover, during school transitions, parents with a higher SES are generally better at advocating for their adolescents, which may result in admission to a more favorable educational level in case of ambiguity. As such, adolescents with a lower parental SES may end up in a lower educational level than adolescents with a higher parental SES, even when competencies are somewhat similar. However, it has also been argued that parents have a higher SES as a result of having more competencies, and that these competencies are genetically transmitted to their adolescents, subsequently resulting in the attainment of higher educational levels (Krapohl & Plomin, 2016).

Additionally, it must be noted that multiple mechanisms may underlie the association between parental SES and educational attainment, with the exact combination of factors differing to some extent between socioeconomic strata, or even between seemingly similar families (e.g., Ellis et al., 2017; Viner et al., 2012). For example, in a high SES context, family pressures to perform may contribute to adolescents ending up in a higher educational level, whereas in a low SES context, economic stress and chaos in the household may partly explain why adolescents end up in a lower educational level.

While parental SES may still be of considerable importance for the educational level that adolescents attain in young adulthood, we found that the educational level is mostly unaffected by family support. Despite experiences of family conflicts, misunderstandings, and tension at home (i.e., less family support), adolescents have a similar chance to end up in a higher educational level as adolescents with more family support. Our findings suggest that financial resources and experience that parents can invest in their adolescent may be more beneficial towards attaining a higher educational level than social‐emotional support.

Three characteristics of our data may nuance this conclusion. First, a large majority of parents reported high levels of family support, and only a small minority of parents reported moderate to low family support. It thus appears that the relatively low levels of family support that some adolescents experienced do not technically qualify as risk factors, according to Fergus and Zimmerman's (2005) definition: these adolescents were not severely limited in their academic endeavors nor did they require sources of resilience to compensate their moderate levels of family support. Second, our measure of family support relies on parent reports, whereas adolescents could report different experiences (Kevenaar et al., 2021). For example, parents might feel as if it is possible to openly discuss emotions at home, or family members to fully trust and accept each other, whereas adolescents might report otherwise. This null finding might not be extrapolated to adolescents with low family support, for which we missed representation. Third, for some comparisons in our multinomial logistic regressions adolescents with more family support had an increased chance of ending up in a lower educational level (e.g., practical vocational vs. university education)—opposite to the positive zero‐order correlations between family support and educational level. Our finding that after controlling for parental SES, effortful control, and peer support, the unique association between family support and educational level is negative in two instances may be indicative of a suppressor effect (Ludlow & Klein, 2014; Pandey & Elliott, 2010). Alternatively, there may be an optimal level of family support, beyond which support may be indicative of overly involved parents who allow insufficient opportunities for their adolescents to struggle and learn on their own (Castro et al., 2017) or may be explained by confounding factors, such as health issues or learning issues. Future research should include adolescent reports on family support and investigate samples with more variance in family support.

Strengths and limitations

The findings of our study must be interpreted in light of its inherent strengths and limitations. Strengths of our study include the longitudinal nature of the data, the large sample size, and the robustness of findings. An important limitation of our study is the attrition bias, which is mostly due to drop‐out over time. The attrition bias in our sample is reflected by a skewed drop‐out of older, male participants with a lower parental SES and less family support. At baseline, families with a lower SES were already unintentionally undersampled. For example, approximately only 7% of the TRAILS sample consisted of adolescents from the lowest income quintile in the Dutch population (Fakkel et al., 2020). The attrition bias further contributes to a limited generalizability of findings.

Though partly inevitable, we strived to keep this bias at a minimum by performing predictive mean matching. Also, a number of outliers that prevented a normal distribution and a number of influential cases that individually affected model estimates were removed from analyses—adding to a skewed attrition. However, the number of excluded cases was a small proportion (2.4%) and considered essential to obtain reliable model estimates. Given the consistency of findings across main analyses and sensitivity analyses, we believe these exclusions did not considerably impact our findings.

Regarding the educational level in young adulthood (T6), initial missings were replaced by known educational level 2 years (T5). Remaining missings were resolved in the predictive mean matching procedure. Though this “last observation carried forward” approach may potentially wrongly assume some individuals to still be in a certain educational level, we believe this approach is a better alternative than removing the participant all altogether or instant imputation, thereby ignoring available historic information. Overall, these limitations of our study have been kept at a minimum and have not considerably impacted our main findings and conclusions. However, considering how our sample predominantly consists of middle‐to‐high SES families, our findings could have potentially been more in line with hypotheses in a sample that better represents the Dutch population.

Though the Netherlands is a relatively wealthy country with one of the smallest income inequalities in the world, differences between socioeconomic strata are still tangible and meaningful in everyday life (Van Bavel & Frankema, 2017). For example, wealth inequalities in the Netherlands are among the highest in the world, with an increasingly clear distinction between homeowners and renters (Statistics Netherlands, 2021). As a result, Dutch families from lower income groups also experience a sense of precarity, and may often struggle to cover basic expenses, or to maintain sufficient savings or an emergency buffer, and lack opportunities for building wealth (Hoff et al., 2019). Such socioeconomic differences are hypothesized to be reflected in the family environment, in terms of tension, stress, and a poorer socioemotional climate. While these differences may be strongest between the lowest and the highest SES groups, they are likely to exist across the spectrum (Viner et al., 2012). Compared to other large Dutch cohort studies, TRAILS is socioeconomically fairly representative—especially regarding the middle to high SES spectrum (Fakkel et al., 2020).

While attaining a higher level of education among adolescents from lower SES families may be beneficial in numerous ways, including increased job opportunities, higher income, and better health, social mobility may also come with negative effects. For example, it is stressful to the extent that one can have physical symptoms (Chen et al., 2022). Additionally, one may lose social connections with family and friends while not yet having fully connected to the new social environment (Bourdieu, 2007; Daenekindt, 2017). While previous research suggests that effortful control and peer competence can help protect against psychological risks following social mobility such as chronic stress or social isolation, future studies are encouraged to assess specific moderation effects on these psychological risks.

In fact, adolescents may also purposefully choose a particular lower educational level. For example, because they aspire after a particular profession, feel like fitting in better with their social environment, for maintaining friendships or because of specific extracurricular activities (Gelderblom et al., 2023). Future longitudinal studies could opt to incorporate quantitative or qualitative data on educational decision‐making among adolescents (Volodina & Nagy, 2016). The intention of the current study is not to promote university over vocational educational levels, and it must be emphasized that adolescents should be facilitated to develop their competencies—regardless of their specific background or aspirations.

However, some adolescents experience family risk factors for their educational attainment, as a result of which they attain a lower educational level than those who are not exposed to such risks or a lower educational level than they could potentially attain under more favorable circumstances. In many modern societies, there are direct and indirect advantages for those who have completed a higher level of education compared to those who have not. For example, individuals with higher educational attainment directly experience more positive job prospects, with better opportunities for long‐term contracts with better compensation. Indirect benefits of being higher educated include having a higher life expectancy, more years of good health, as well as better access to important information than individuals with lower educational attainment (Mackenbach, 2012; Raghupathi & Raghupathi, 2020). It must be noted that attaining a higher educational degree is not a panacea for resolving structural inequalities, such as access to high quality food, health care, and job opportunities—which may remain limited to adolescents in a low SES context even after graduating university (McMaughan et al., 2020; Stephens et al., 2019). While the underlying mechanisms for these associations are thus more complex than implied here, the numerous positive associations with attaining a higher educational level may explain why in society many parents and adolescents strive to become higher educated (Boxer et al., 2011).

CONCLUSION

Adolescents with a lower parental SES benefit from having strong effortful control, but not more so than adolescents with a higher parental SES and strong effortful control. Family support was mostly unrelated to educational attainment, and peer support neither had a protective effect nor a compensatory effect on educational attainment. Effortful control may be an important target for intervention, though it is important to emphasize that effortful control has a considerable genetic component (Willems et al., 2018) and that developing effortful control is particularly challenging in a low SES context or in the absence of sufficient family support (Ellis et al., 2017). Our findings also urge researchers to focus on including and retaining more adolescents from a low SES background and who receive little to no family support. Last, considering that macro‐level policies and interventions are often not as effective as intended to reduce inequalities in educational opportunities (Exalto et al., 2023), social support was mostly unrelated to educational attainment, and effortful control did not attenuate the negative effect of being from a low SES background on educational attainment, we conclude that inequalities in educational opportunities are rigid and opportunities to overcome those are limited—despite good intentions of policy makers, teachers, parents, and adolescents themselves.
