Clinical Experience With Biologic Disease‐Modifying Therapy in Relapsing–Remitting Multiple Sclerosis: A Case Report and Review of Literature
Authors: Pandaramparambil Saidu Nazneen, Thinley Dorji, Sonam Yangzom, R. Vijayalakshmi Natarajan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBiological agents are highly effective in preventing/minimizing disability related to multiple sclerosis. However, in resource‐limited countries, the diagnosis and management of multiple sclerosis are limited by social and structural determinants of health. Support for patients to access these medicines through government schemes or insurance coverage is important in preventing disability.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis is the most common autoimmune demyelinating disorder in young adults [1]. It is characterized by inflammation, demyelination, plaque formation or scarring, and eventual neuronal loss, and can have a relapsing or progressive course. Though there are no large epidemiological studies regarding multiple sclerosis reported from India, with increased awareness regarding the disease, relatively easy availability of magnetic resonance imaging, and a significant increase in the number of neurologists, the current estimate of the prevalence of multiple sclerosis in the country is about 7 to 10/100,000 [2]. The figures may be even higher because a large section of the population in India still has no access to adequate medical facilities.

Early initiation of treatment for multiple sclerosis can slow the progression of disability and enhance long‐term clinical outcomes [3]. However, disease‐modifying therapies (DMTs) target relapse prevention rather than halting or reversing progression, and there is no consensus regarding how early and how aggressively multiple sclerosis should be treated [4]. While DMTs have been shown to slow the progression of disability and enhance long‐term clinical outcomes [5], DMTs are expensive and not covered by insurance, and there is limited government support (available mainly for defense personnel and other government employees in India). As a result, the majority of the patients must bear the expenses themselves to receive DMTs. We report a case of relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis with clinical experience in using the highly efficacious biologic DMTs in the treatment at a secondary care public sector hospital in India. This case highlights the indications for Natalizumab, monitoring of adverse events, and follow‐up of clinical response in relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis.

Case Presentation

History and Clinical Course

A 24‐year‐old male from Bihar, presented with lower limb weakness, paraesthesia, bladder symptoms, and imbalance in walking for a year and diplopia for 1‐month duration. At the onset of initial symptoms, he had intermittent fever for 1 week associated with myalgia, retro‐orbital pain, and headache. He noticed difficulty in lifting his right foot off the ground while walking/running. Two weeks later, he developed a tingling sensation and paraesthesia over bilateral leg and foot. He also had an electric shock‐like sensation on bending the neck forward. He had a feeling of walking on a sponge and would have fear of falling while walking in the dark. He also had difficulty in estimating the height of stairs. The symptoms gradually progressed to severe imbalance and swaying to either side while walking. He also had slippage of slippers with awareness at the same time. Two weeks into the illness, he also started to have urinary urgency and urge incontinence and progressed to having difficulty in holding the urine.

At this point, he was evaluated with magnetic resonance imaging and cerebrospinal fluid analysis that was normal and negative for oligoclonal bands. He was given a provisional diagnosis of Multiple Sclerosis and was treated with IV Methylprednisolone 1 g for 5 days followed by oral Prednisolone that was tapered over 2 months along with oral Dimethyl fumarate. After this therapy, he had improvement in walking and a decrease in imbalance but had no improvement in paraesthesia and urinary symptoms.

A few months later, he had a short febrile illness following which there was a worsening imbalance while walking. In addition, he had weakness in bilateral lower limbs initially involving distal muscles and later involving proximal muscles. When he presented to us in September 2022, he had binocular double vision, more on lateral gaze and for far objects. There was no further increase in urinary complaints and he could hold urine for 20 min. There was no history of constipation, sleep disturbances, altered sensorium or seizures or features of connective tissue diseases. He consumed a mixed diet, and had no history of alcohol or tobacco use. There was no family history of a similar illness. Examination

On admission, he was afebrile, pulse 78/min, BP 128/76 mmHg, with normal higher mental functions. His visual acuity was 6/9 in the right eye and 6/12 in the left eye, with pupils 3 mm bilaterally and a normal fundus examination. Extraocular movement examination revealed restriction of right eye adduction, with diplopia on left lateral gaze and grade 1 horizontal nystagmus in the left eye. There was no ptosis, and the convergence reflex was normal. Other cranial nerve examinations were normal.

Motor system examination revealed Grade 3 spasticity involving both lower limbs and Grade 1–2 spasticity involving both upper limbs. There was weakness involving hip muscles (Grade 4), knee flexors (Grade 4), and ankle dorsiflexors (Grade 4). Toe grip was normal bilaterally. Deep tendon reflexes were brisk bilaterally. His right plantar reflex was extensor and left was flexor. Cerebellar system examination revealed bilateral dysmetria on the Finger‐Nose‐Finger test and impaired Heel–Knee‐Shin in bilateral lower limbs. Sensory system examination revealed diminished vibration sense over the right medial malleolus and bilateral styloid process. Pain, temperature, and touch sensations were normal. He had a spastic ataxic gait and he walked with support. Skull and spine examination was grossly normal. Cardiovascular, respiratory system, and abdomen examination did not reveal any abnormalities.

Investigation Findings

Possibility of Relapsing Remitting Multiple Sclerosis or Neuromyelitis Optica spectrum disorders was considered highly likely, with the differential diagnosis being central nervous system vasculitis, connective tissue diseases, neuro‐sarcoidosis, neurosyphilis, and antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, and was evaluated accordingly. On evaluation, his basic laboratory evaluations were within normal limits (Table 1). ANA profile, antiphospholipid antibody syndrome profile, pANCA, cANCA, and rheumatoid factor were negative. Serum angiotensin‐converting enzyme levels and C‐reactive protein were within normal limits. Serum anti‐aquaporin four and anti‐myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibodies were negative. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis revealed four cells (all lymphocytes) with mildly elevated protein and mildly reduced sugar; gram stain, India ink, culture, and VDRL were negative; oligoclonal bands were positive with an IgG index of > 2. Visual Evoked Potential showed prolonged P100 latencies in both eyes, Brainstem Auditory Evoked Response was abnormal in both ears, and Somatosensory Evoked Potential was prolonged in bilateral upper and lower limbs.

Management

Based on the history of relapsing–remitting neurological illness and evaluation showing oligoclonal bands in the cerebrospinal fluid, impaired evoked potentials, and lesions involving periventricular, brainstem, juxtacortical, and short segments of cervicodorsal cords, the patient was diagnosed as a case of Relapsing–Remitting Multiple Sclerosis based on the Revised McDonald's Criteria.

His disease relapse was managed with Pulse Methylprednisolone 1 g IV for 5 days, followed by oral prednisolone. Considering the high frequency of relapses and high disease burden in imaging in this patient, he received highly effective biologic DMT. His oral Dimethyl Fumarate was stopped, and he received monthly infusions of Natalizumab 300 mg in 250 mL 0.9% NaCl over 1 h for 6 months. His oral steroids were gradually tapered and stopped. Natalizumab was made available after completion of all due administrative procedures, which took around 9 months from when the patient presented to us for the first time.

The patient did not have any disease relapse during Natalizumab and had zero residual neurological deficit by 8 months after initiation. John Cunningham (JC) virus antibody levels tested after six doses of Natalizumab revealed a very high titre (index value of 1.75). Considering the risk of JC virus‐induced Progressive Multifocal Leukoencephalopathy, Natalizumab was discontinued and switched over to Rituximab. However, he had one major disease relapse 6 months after discontinuation requiring pulse Methylprednisolone followed by a tapering dose of oral Prednisolone. Following this, he received Rituximab 1 g infused 2 weeks apart. Rituxan Therapy Test done 3 months later showed good CD20 cell depletion (Table 2). Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient is on regular follow‐up, is relapse‐free, and has resumed a normal life. He was followed up with serial Rituxan Therapy Tests every month from the 4th month to the 6th month (Table 2). The Rituxan Therapy Test done in the 6th month has shown recovery of CD20 cell levels. Based on this report, maintenance doses of Rituximab are being planned.

Discussion

There are mainly three clinical subtypes of multiple sclerosis—Relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis (RMS): 90% of MS cases are characterized by reversible episodes of neurological deficits that usually last for days or weeks, with substantial or complete neurological recovery and a neurologically stable state between each attack. Secondary progressive multiple sclerosis (SPMS) always begins as RMS; however, the development of permanent neurological deficits and the progression of clinical disability become prominent over time. Primary progressive MS (PPMS) is characterized by a progressive disease course from the onset.

Multiple sclerosis is more prevalent among women, with a female‐to‐male ratio of 3:1 [1]. Although a definite cause is still not known, well‐established risk factors include low sun exposure, passive or active smoking, obesity during adolescence, low vitamin D levels, and Epstein–Barr virus infection during early adulthood and adolescence [6]. The diagnosis of multiple sclerosis is based on the 2017 Revised McDonald Criteria, which is primarily clinical, with magnetic resonance imaging as a supportive, supplementary, or replacement tool to demonstrate dissemination of demyelinating lesions in space and time [7, 8]. Although not specific, multiple sclerosis can have Evoked Potential abnormalities in the form of marked delay in the latency of a specific Evoked Potential component (visual, auditory, somatosensory, or motor), which is suggestive of demyelination [9]. Oligoclonal bands in the cerebrospinal fluid are seen in more than 90% of patients [10]. The absence of oligoclonal bands has a very high negative‐predictive value [10, 11]. Similarly, longitudinal extensive transverse myelitis and the presence of bleeds or infarcts in magnetic resonance imaging are ‘red flags’ for alternate diagnosis [12]. However, the 2017 revisions to the McDonald criteria allow diagnosis of multiple sclerosis to be made if clinical or MRI criteria for dissemination in space are met and in the absence of atypical cerebrospinal fluid findings [8]. Poor prognostic factors include male gender, a late age at onset, a progressive course at onset, a short inter‐attack interval, motor, cerebellar, and sphincter involvement at onset, a high frequency of early attacks, a faster rate of disability accumulation, and a relevant early residual disability [13].

The treatment of MS involves symptomatic treatment, treatment of acute attacks, and prevention of relapses and new MRI lesion activity. Acute attacks are managed with methylprednisolone 500–1000 mg/day for 3 to 5 days, with or without a tapering course of oral prednisolone over 2 weeks [9] and DMTs are administered to relapsing forms of MS (RMS, SPMS with exacerbations). Though several agents are approved for the management of RMS, only Ocrelizumab is approved for the treatment of PPMS with a demonstrable decrease in disease progression [14]. Recently, Ocrelizumab has been made available in India. DMTs for RMS are categorized into modestly effective (Interferon β, Glatiramer acetate, Teriflunomide), moderately effective (Fingolimod, Dimethyl Fumarate), and highly effective agents (Ocrelizumab, Natalizumab, Alemtuzumab, Mitoxantrone) [9].

There are two main strategies of treatment initiation. The ‘escalation strategy,’ wherein moderately effective DMTs are used for disease control first, followed by stepping up to a more potent agent in the event of breakthrough disease activity. This strategy better manages the rare risk of adverse events associated with highly effective agents; however, there is no standardized guideline regarding the threshold of escalation. The ‘induction strategy,’ wherein highly effective DMTs are started first to achieve early and effective disease control, followed by de‐escalation and maintenance with moderately effective DMTs [15]. Among the highly effective DMTs, Ocrelizumab carries some increased risk of malignancy including breast cancer; Alemtuzumab carries increased risk of malignancies including thyroid cancer, breast cancer, lymphoma as well as an increased risk of autoimmune diseases like Grave's disease, thrombocytopenia, haemolytic anemia, and glomerulonephritis; and Natalizumab carries an increased risk of JC virus‐induced Progressive Multifocal Leukoencephalopathy during long‐term treatment [9]. Hence, it is recommended to test for JC virus antibody titres at six‐month intervals in patients receiving Natalizumab treatment. Practice guidelines recommend starting with Alemtuzumab, Natalizumab, or Fingolimod in people with highly active disease [16].

Natalizumab discontinuation increases the risk of rebound activity (increase in disease activity above the baseline), disease activity detected on magnetic resonance imaging, and relapse within 6 months of discontinuation [17]. As per retrospective cohort data analysis, switching from Natalizumab to Rituximab in patients with a high index of JC virus antibodies results in lower rates of clinical and radiological disease activity compared to switching from Natalizumab to Fingolimod [18], although ironically, Rituximab is yet to get a nod for approval in the treatment of multiple sclerosis. However, many clinicians use Rituximab off‐label in the treatment of multiple sclerosis. Considering its cost effectiveness and clinical response observed in off‐label use, further studies to evaluate the efficacy of Rituximab as a first‐line agent in the treatment of MS are a need of the hour.

Lack of or inadequate treatment of multiple sclerosis results in disability, loss of employment, and escalating treatment costs. Periods of relapses are associated with absenteeism from school/work, early retirement from job, while disability is also independently associated with increasing treatment cost [19]. Therefore, prevention of disability progression is one of the important factors in considering therapy with DMTs [16]. To narrow the access gap for biologic agents, alternate modalities for health financing should be explored for patients residing in low‐ and middle‐income countries.

Conclusion

Multiple Sclerosis carries significant societal and economic burden in a developing country like India. Though the prospects of treatment and outcome in MS have changed substantially since the arrival of DMTs, early diagnosis and initiation of DMTs in the early inflammatory phase of the disease where the DMTs are effective is still a challenge in India. Once the DMTs are initiated, adherence is a major challenge due to the cost of the treatment, especially that of biologic DMTs. As the majority of the patients are young and the risk of disability accumulation in MS increases as time progresses, every effort should be made to establish the diagnosis at the early stage and initiate DMTs without delay.
