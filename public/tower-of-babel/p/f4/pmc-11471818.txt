Towards a general computed tomography image segmentation model for anatomical structures and lesions
Authors: Xi Ouyang, Dongdong Gu, Xuejian Li, Wenqi Zhou, Qianqian Chen, Yiqiang Zhan, Xiang Sean Zhou, Feng Shi, Zhong Xue, Dinggang Shen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Numerous deep-learning models have been developed using task-specific data, but they ignore the inherent connections among different tasks. By jointly learning a wide range of segmentation tasks, we prove that a general medical image segmentation model can improve segmentation performance for computerized tomography (CT) volumes. The proposed general CT image segmentation (gCIS) model utilizes a common transformer-based encoder for all tasks and incorporates automatic pathway modules for task prompt-based decoding. It is trained on one of the largest datasets, comprising 36,419 CT scans and 83 tasks. gCIS can automatically perform various segmentation tasks using automatic pathway modules of decoding networks through text prompt inputs, achieving an average Dice coefficient of 82.84%. Furthermore, the proposed automatic pathway routing mechanism allows for parameter pruning of the network during deployment, and gCIS can also be quickly adapted to unseen tasks with minimal training samples while maintaining great performance.

Introduction

Medical imaging serves as an indispensable component of clinical diagnosis and treatment assessment in modern healthcare settings. The success of deep learning has accelerated accurate quantification of large imaging data and revolutionized the field of medical image analysis. Studies have demonstrated that deep learning methods can expedite and help physicians make more precise assessments and choose better treatment options1–4. Among various medical artificial intelligence (AI) applications, image segmentation is considered fundamental. For instance, accurate segmentation of tumors and organs at risk in medical images is essential for dose planning and delivery in radiation therapy5.

Given the diversity of medical images and variability of organ sizes and shapes to be segmented, the majority of image segmentation tasks6–12 are typically carried out in a data- and task-specific manner to achieve plausible performance. As shown in Fig. 1a, specific neural network models are tailored to particular segmentation tasks. These models typically fail to capture the relationship between tasks, and their performance may drop significantly when the input image data or the target segmentation deviate from those employed during training. It is also difficult to transfer the task-specific models to new unseen tasks. Alternatively, some studies adopt the multi-task learning (MTL) strategy13–17, which appends multiple heads to the end of a shared neural network model to generate predictions for different tasks (Fig. 1b). On the other hand, foundation models pre-trained on large-scale datasets have been a hot topic in the field of computer vision. Recently, the segment anything model (SAM)18 has drawn considerable attention as a powerful pre-trained network. It is constructed on a vast 2D natural image dataset comprising 11 million images and 1.1 billion masks and has shown promising performance on various segmentation tasks. Learned from such a large and diverse dataset and annotations, SAM gains better generalization, which is crucial for ensuring the model performance for different and even previously unseen tasks.

More recently, MedSAM19 has been proposed for medical images, however, neither SAM nor MedSAM considers input images in 3D, and they extract 2D slices from original volumes for segmentation. MedSAM suggests that the distribution shift from natural images to medical images can be overcome by using more medical images. Additionally, it is worth noting that there is a huge gap between 2D natural images and 3D or 4D medical volumes (e.g., computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)). Segmenting volumes slice-by-slice in a 2D manner is potentially vulnerable to maintaining 3D structural information, especially for tubular structures. It is commonly acknowledged in the medical imaging domain that 3D processing outperforms 2D ones and is preferred especially for application-oriented models. This suggests why almost no tubular-structure segmentation tasks are used in those SAM-based experiments. Finally, and more importantly, MedSAM or SAM rely on location cues (foreground points, boxes, or masks) and are unable to achieve full automation of the entire segmentation process.

In this paper, we propose a general CT image segmentation (gCIS) model that naturally supports multi-task CT image segmentation using text prompt inputs for automatic path routing. Trained using CT images and corresponding organ descriptions for 83 CT segmentation tasks, such as coronary artery segmentation with the prompt “coronary”, kidney segmentation with the prompt “kidney”, etc., our model can perform automatic segmentation of various organs, vascular structures, and lesions in 3D.

The structure of gCIS is shown in Fig. 1c, which is trained on a large number of multi-task datasets as a versatile and promotable solution for CT image segmentation. Unlike common task-specific AI models (Fig. 1a), gCIS is pre-trained on multi-task datasets and capable of feature extraction with a transformer-based encoder across various tasks. Compared to multi-head-based MTL (Fig. 1b), gCIS introduces a comprehensive automatic pathway (AP) routing mechanism to automatically determine the feature decoding routes for different tasks. This allows for gCIS to capture correlations between different tasks while enhancing the segmentation performance of each. This idea is similar to the network architecture search method20 to automate the discovery of optimal network structures. With AP routing, we can mitigate conflicts and maintain mutual benefits across various tasks.

Specifically, gCIS employs a transformer encoder to extract a variety of medical image features into a shared latent space across multiple tasks. Studies have shown that the Swin Transformer network is a great architecture for medical image segmentation, yields better performance on large-scale datasets21–23 and outperforms state-of-the-art convolutional networks after being pre-trained on large amounts of data. Considering the large number of CT scans in our dataset, we decided to use the Swin Transformer as our encoder. Furthermore, prompt-based automatic AP modules are used to adapt the model to the tasks. A series of AP modules each containing a learnable routing layer and M sub-pathway convolutional networks are used in the decoding procedure. The routing layer receives the prompt embedding of the input task description (e.g., “cardiac” indicating the cardiac segmentation) and determines automatically the decoding sub-pathways for the task. Herein, a Gumbel softmax function24 is employed to automatically select the dedicated sub-pathways based on the generated sampling weights. Therefore, a series of vectors responsible for sub-pathway selection within the AP modules along the decoding pipeline form the pathway of feature decoding networks for each individual task. As shown in Fig. 1c, the proposed decoder consists of seven AP modules arranged along the decoding pipeline. Deconvolutional layers connect the first five AP modules, gradually restoring the resolution of the feature maps. The final two AP modules operate at the original resolution to produce the final segmentation results. Each AP module contains a fully connected layer serving as its routing layer and M sub-pathways formed by residual blocks, where M is set to 6 in our work. This allows for effective feature fusion for different tasks, and the combination of sub-pathway selections at different decoding levels makes it efficient to fulfill various segmentation tasks. Pathway selection patterns of all samples can be clustered to analyze the unique characteristics of each task.

We built up the most extensive dataset so far for training gCIS, which contains 36,419 cases of CT scans with 64,674 annotations for 83 segmentation tasks. As shown in Fig. 2, we employed a dataset consisting of 58,499 masks for training and 6175 samples for testing these tasks. This is the largest dataset consisting of both non-contrast CT and contrast-enhanced CT so far for 3D medical image segmentation. This large-scale data training approach facilitates gCIS to learn transferable and generalizable representations in medical images, enabling it to migrate to other new medical image tasks. For an unseen task, the AP modules allow for the extraction of relevant sub-pathways based on the similarity between the unseen task prompt and an already-seen task prompt. The sub-pathways used can then be extracted to form a trimmed pre-trained gCIS network, providing an excellent starting point for fine-tuning using a relatively limited amount of annotated data while obtaining promising results. In summary, gCIS is a foundation 3D CT segmentation model with a unified framework that facilitates the concurrent training of multiple tasks. It provides a convenient framework for the rapid development of AI applications. This allows for parallel development of AI tasks and achieving remarkable performance in the short term. For a specific task or a single-task application, we can extract task-specific sub-pathways and form a readily deployable model. In other words, gCIS can trim the unused sub-pathways for an individual task without compromising its performance. A similar trimming approach can also be used for multi-task applications, offering exceptional flexibility for the deployment of single or multiple tasks while maximizing the benefits of extensive pre-training.

Results

In this section, we first present the comparative results between the proposed gCIS and other state-of-the-art methods, demonstrating the superior performance of this foundation model. Next, we introduce the ablation studies on the effectiveness of large-scale multi-task pre-training. Finally, we demonstrate the great performance of adapting gCIS to previously unseen tasks. We further describe the results in detail for each task and the performance of gCIS to new tasks in Supplementary Note 2.

A large-scale CT segmentation benchmark

We collected the largest dataset for 3D CT image segmentation tasks to date, which contains 36,419 CT scans with 64,674 annotations corresponding to 83 segmentation tasks throughout the entire body (Fig. 2). Altogether more than 11 million slices have been used, with an average of 317 slices per CT scan. 32,170 CT scans with 58,499 annotations are used as the training set, and the rest 4249 CT scans with 6175 annotations are used as the testing set. Because one CT volume may contain multiple masks, the split is based on volumes so that one volume cannot be in both the training and testing sets. Among the datasets, a total of 33,913 cases of anonymous in-house CT scans were collected from our collaborating hospitals, and IRB approvals were obtained by the Research Ethics Committees from those centers. Written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study. In addition, the rest 2506 volumes in the experiments came from multiple publicly available datasets. Details are provided in the Data Availability Statement and Supplementary Note 1.

Performance evaluation of gCIS across multiple CT image segmentation tasks

We conduct a comprehensive evaluation of gCIS by assessing its performance on segmentation tasks throughout the entire human body. The results for all 83 segmentation tasks are reported in Fig. 3a, b, and the detailed Dice/clDice25 scores are provided in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3. These tasks include a wide range of anatomical structures, such as organs, vessels, and tumors. The gCIS model yields satisfactory Dice scores for all the tasks, with the majority of mean Dice exceeding 80%. Even for tasks with limited training samples and challenging sizes, the gCIS model still achieves Dice scores over 60% (except for two tumor tasks). Overall, gCIS yields robust performance across tasks of varying sizes, shapes, and distributions. We also show the clDice scores for segmenting tubular structures in Fig. 3c. Within the context of these tasks, a certain degree of slight over- or under-segmentation of the tubular radius is considered acceptable, as it does not significantly impact clinical diagnosis. The general model achieves clDice scores over 70% for all the tasks, indicating its great generalizability for segmentation tasks of tubular or curvilinear structures. Notice that for the segmentation of liver vessels, gCIS model achieves a relatively lower clDice score of 68.82%. Figure 3d plots the distribution of t-SNE projected tasks using the initial prompt embeddings, while Fig. 3e depicts those of the AP pathways using gCIS. From these two figures, it is evident that the groups of tasks using gCIS exhibit more pronounced clustering compared to those using text prompt embeddings. In fact, such a difference indicates that the routing layers of the AP modules undergo further processing of the prompt embeddings, resulting in structurally meaningful sub-pathway selection after being trained on multiple tasks. Additionally, tasks involving smaller soft tissues and lesions (orange) appear to be more scattered than other groups. This phenomenon could be attributed to the inherent variability in the shape and appearance of smaller structures.

Comparison with state-of-the-art segmentation methods

To better demonstrate the effectiveness of the gCIS model, we compare its performance with the state-of-the-art models, including nnU-Net27, Cascaded Vb-Net5 and MedSAM19, each independently trained for individual tasks using the same datasets. For MedSAM19, the best performance is gained by using box prompts, wherein 2D bounding boxes tightly enclosing each separate contiguous region of the object of interest are used as inputs. Therefore, we place bounding boxes to tightly enclose all the separate contiguous regions based on the ground truth masks during automatic inferences. We remove the original limitation of three boxes since there could be more regions of interest in each slice, particularly for tubular structures. We repeate slice-by-slice segmentation for applicable slices of each testing volume.

Impact of multiple tasks on gCIS performance

To quantitatively validate the performance of gCIS, we conduct comparative experiments by training sole-path gCIS models (gCIS without AP modules) separately for each task. The routing layer is removed in each AP module, and only one sub-pathway is kept for the training of each task. Also, the model does not need prompt input for single-task training. The results of these sole-path gCIS models are shown in Table 1, and gCIS outperforms the sole-path gCIS in all the tasks for the metrics of Dice or clDice. The findings demonstrate that gCIS trained with data from various tasks and significantly (p < 0.05 for 10 out of 14 tasks) enhances the segmentation performance of individual task. Notably, obtaining results for sole-path gCIS on each task necessitates training the task-specific models, whereas gCIS achieves all these results using a single model. More discussion about the effect of high-volume data is shown in Supplementary Note 2. At the same time, for the segmentation of lower limbs artery, we use different prompts to test the performance of segmentation results as shown in Supplementary Note 3.

Generalization of gCIS to unseen tasks

Although it is a large dataset with a wide range of segmentation tasks, real clinical scenarios often present diverse applications that our training set cannot cover. It will be very time-consuming and resource-intensive to add the new data of each unseen task and update the entire model parameters according to the current training process. As discussed above, an active pathway can be extracted by using the text prompt of the new task from the gCIS model to form a trimmed gCIS, which can be further refined to adapt to the new task. To illustrate this approach, we use renal vascular segmentation as an example. The goal is to segment the renal artery in the arterial phase of CT images and the renal vein in the venous phase of the CT images. Our renal artery dataset includes 201 training and 28 testing cases, while the renal vein example includes 90 training and 9 testing cases. Notice that the images in arterial and venous phases are not paired, and neither the images nor the annotations have been used for gCIS training. We treat renal artery and renal vein segmentation as two distinct tasks.

The method for rapidly adapting to new tasks consists of three steps. The first step is to use new text prompts to select suitable sub-pathways from gCIS. Here, we use “renal artery” and “renal vein” prompts to choose the sub-pathways for renal artery and renal vein segmentation, respectively. Then, we keep the selected pathways and trim the remaining pathways from the gCIS network to reduce the size of our network. Finally, while fixing the parameters of the encoder, we refine only the parameters of the decoder with these specific pathways using the new training data. The training hyperparameters and loss function remain consistent with those used in the gCIS multi-task co-training.

The comparative results with nnU-Net and cascaded Vb-Net for the segmentation of renal artery using different training examples are shown in Table 2. We show the values of Topology Precision (Tprec) and Topology Sensitivity (Tsens)25 in this table, detailing the sensitivity and precision for segmentation results. It can be observed that our foundation model can achieve significantly better clDice scores in the one-shot and five-shot settings. As shown in Fig. 4, it is a quite challenging task for these individual AI models to provide meaningful segmentation results with only one or five training examples. However, our foundation model could deliver much better results with limited training cases. Discussion

By incorporating dozens of tasks and tens of thousands of CT volumes, we present a general CT image segmentation model by using a transformer-based encoder and an adaptive pathway module-based decoder. First, the transformer-based encoder extracts image features across all the tasks. Then, to address the inherent constraints associated with a shared decoder for multi-head networks in traditional MTL, gCIS introduces task flexibility through the routing of multiple sub-pathways at each decoder level, denoted as AP modules. These sub-pathways are dynamically and autonomously selected and learned to alleviate conflicts and maximize benefits across a spectrum of tasks. The selection of sub-pathways is initially guided by a text prompt encoder utilizing the encoded task descriptions22. Subsequently, the text embedding is further refined through learnable routing layers so that optimal sub-pathways are automatically determined based on not only prompt inputs but also the characteristics of image features for the tasks.

The advantage of the AP module within gCIS is twofold: first, it facilitates the automatic selection of sub-pathways, so that multiple tasks can share common features while retaining some task-specific parameters; second, for new tasks, the most analogous pathways can be automatically identified from the routing layer based on the text prompt and be further trained/refined for the new task. As demonstrated in Fig. 3d, e, in the gCIS model trained with 83 tasks, pathway clustering shows that similar tasks are naturally clustered together, indicating robust correlations among the segmentation tasks.

In general, when a network shares certain layers and parameters across multiple tasks and is trained simultaneously, that network is referred to as an MTL model. One popular approach is depicted in Fig. 1b. The advantage of sharing network parameters lies in the potential to extract common representation, which proves beneficial for related tasks or exhibits shared characteristics. This is particularly invaluable in fields such as natural language processing, computer vision, and healthcare, wherein tasks are similar. Furthermore, it is believed that, when the number of data or annotations is limited, MTL can enhance the model’s generalization performance by capitalizing on the information shared across tasks. However, one limitation of MTL is that not all tasks benefit equally from simultaneous training. Previous studies indicate that this strategy is effective when tasks are interconnected or share certain similarities. For disparate or unrelated tasks, MTL might lead to sub-optimal results compared to training single-task models. Thus, the MTL architecture necessitates thoughtful design to suit the various tasks and ensure that shared features prove to be beneficial for all tasks. For example, as discussed in the previous study28, multi-head structures within the common encoding and decoding networks could be designed with greater depth to effectively adapt to segmentation tasks.

In this study, we show that gCIS, equipped with deep adaptive pathway selective decoders, can be trained to effectively improve the performance of segmentation tasks and eliminate side effects of outlier tasks in the traditional MTL. This approach not only enhances the performance of existing tasks but also simplifies the process of creating new models for emerging objectives. In the realm of medical imaging, addressing new tasks typically demands annotating new data and devising and training distinct models. gCIS makes it feasible to streamline this procedure by using a relatively modest amount of annotated data and a few descriptive prompts pertaining to the specific task at hand. By evaluating all available pathways within the trained gCIS, one can select the most appropriate trained pathways and prompts as an initialization for a new task. Subsequently, the selected pathway can be refined using the available annotated data. This strategy not only expedites the development of new segmentation models but also seamlessly integrates them into gCIS’s existing model portfolio via pathway parameters. The mechanism for automatic pathway selection for new tasks and the incorporation of pathways into gCIS will be explored in our future work. For example, refining new gCIS models with added new tasks and merged pathways, as well as selecting multiple candidate pathways for joint optimization in new tasks, may be beneficial for boosting the performance and making the model robust to prompts. Additionally, the inclusion of image/mask pairs for routing may enhance those image-task-driven AP models.

Experiments are conducted by comparing with state-of-the-art 3D image segmentation methods. Although there is controversy surrounding whether 2D or 3D networks are more suitable for medical images, or if a hybrid approach combining both dimensions would be superior, it is widely recognized that 3D segmentation, particularly for vasculatures, provides fewer false positives and plays a crucial role in shape analysis in medical imaging29. Slice-based segmentation may prove inadequate or need subsequent 3D reconstruction for such tasks. Thus, this paper attempts to perform and compare image segmentation in a 3D fashion.

Our results reveal several significant trends. First, the multi-task gCIS model, enriched with AP modules, demonstrates superior performance compared to its single-task counterparts. This underscores the synergistic effect of utilizing more segmentation tasks and their associated data, suggesting mutual enhancements. Second, we present evidence of gCIS’s superiority over other state-of-the-art models optimized independently for each task. This distinction shows the comprehensiveness and effectiveness of the gCIS approach.

The study has several limitations. First, although this dataset currently stands as the largest 3D medical imaging dataset reported for training a segmentation model, it is important to note that the numbers of images and annotations are not balanced across tasks. This disparity could potentially impact the performance of specific tasks due to the widely acknowledged majority effects commonly observed in the deep-learning models. On the other hand, based on empirical experience, tasks such as segmenting larger regions generally require less data compared to delineating intricate structures like small lesions or detailed vessels. In fact, the results also reveal that performance does not necessarily correlate with the overall number of training samples used. Therefore, we devised a data sampling strategy during training to balance the probability of data sampling in different tasks, addressing both sample scarcities and emphasizing the segmentation of vessels and smaller objects.

Second, we introduce adaptive pathways for the multi-level decoder. Existing research suggests that within large MTL models, features may naturally organize into distinct clusters in their high-dimensional space. Similar tasks or images sharing analogous shapes or appearances might exhibit closely grouped features. Unsupervised learning with prior constraints in the feature space could enhance the model’s robustness. Notably, less common features within the long-tailed distribution should be retained to counteract any potential outlier influences on the majority of common tasks. It is in this context that feature space analysis, including sparse representation and constraining, coupled with unsupervised learning, emerges as a prevailing trend in extensive model studies.

Third, to facilitate the model’s versatility across diverse tasks, our study leverages the concept of a pre-trained text encoding model. We employ its language encoder to aid gCIS in the selection and prioritization of training-specific pathways tailored for distinct tasks. While this approach effectively reduces the search space for feature vectors, further investigations are warranted to scrutinize pathway features, feature clusters, as well as feature adaptation and amalgamation for novel tasks.

Finally, as networks continue to grow in size, the demand for smaller deployable models increases for practical applications. Our study illustrates that gCIS exhibits adaptability, allowing it to be pruned into smaller task-specific models characterized by reduced network parameters. Additionally, the procedure for trimming network parameters after training warrants further exploration, drawing from methodologies similar to those employed in other neural networks.

Methods

gCIS architecture

We design gCIS to tackle the challenge of multiple medical image segmentation tasks. As illustrated in Fig. 1c, gCIS consists of three sub-structures: (1) A transformer-based encoder that extracts features from various medical images and forms a common interactive latent space; (2) a prompt text encoder that provides prompt embedding to guide the sub-pathway selection through the routing layers; and (3) a decoder that processes data through different sub-pathways to generate the corresponding segmentation results based on automatic routing using the AP modules. We outline the respective details below:Transformer-based image encoder. Given the remarkable performance of Swin transformer23,30, we adapt it into the 3D structure as the encoder for gCIS, which consists of four stages, with each stage containing two transformer blocks, using regular-window-partitioning multi-head self-attention modules, respectively. It consists of four stages, and each stage contains two transformer blocks using regular-window-partitioning multi-head self-attention modules. The input of the Swin-transformer encoder is a patch embedding layer with a patch size of 2 × 2 × 2 and 48 output channels. Five-level downsampling operations with 1/2 scale are used in the encoder, which has a size of 8.06 million parameters in total to learn the image information from the large-scale dataset. Since it is trained in 2D images, only partial weights can be transferred from the ImageNet31 pre-trained weights. The rest of the parameters are randomly initialized.Prompt encoder. In our framework, we employ text prompts for each segmentation task, e.g., we use “pulmonary arteries” for pulmonary artery segmentation and “heart” for heart segmentation, and so on. To encode these text prompts into feature representation, we adopt a text encoder and load its pre-trained weights directly, which are pre-trained together with numerous images in ref. 22. The prompt embedding reflects effectively the meaning or content of the image and is passed to each AP module so that initial sub-pathways are determined (Fig. 1c). The text encoder we adopted comprises 63 million parameters, 12 layers, and a width of 512 with 8 attention heads.Decoder with automatic pathway modules. Deconvolutional layers connect the first five AP modules to restore the resolution of the feature maps gradually, and the last two AP modules process the feature map at the original resolution to generate the final segmentation results. Each AP module consists of a fully connected layer as its routing layer and also M sub-pathways formed by residual blocks. M is set to 6 in our work. Upon receiving the task-specific prompt embedding, the routing layer calculates the probability of selecting each sub-pathway and picks the most suitable sub-pathway to decode the input features. Training details

Data are pre-processed in the following steps. First, we conduct a “window width/level” (window: 1800, level: 0) operation to the Hounsfield units, i.e., the intensity is truncated to the range of [−900, 900] by mapping intensity values above 900 to 900 and those below −900 to −900. Second, we crop 96 × 96 × 96 patches from the normalized images to serve as inputs for network training. The patches are selected so that approximately 70% of crop centers fall within the ground-truth mask area, while the remaining 30% are located in the area of the background.

The networks are implemented using the PyTorch framework32, with memory usage and computation speed optimized using the automatic mixed precision (AMP) package. The training process employs the AdamW optimizer33 with a momentum of 0.9, weight decay of 0.00001, and an initial learning rate of 0.0002. For data augmentation, common strategies for deep-learning model training are employed, including rotation, scaling, flipping, shifting, and adding noise. For the adjustment strategy of the learning rate, the model is trained with cosine annealing with the WarmRestarts strategy, and the number of warmup epochs is set to 50 in the experiments. Throughout the training stage, a batch size of 220 is maintained. The loss function used for training is composed of focal loss34 and Dice loss35, each with a weighting factor of 0.5.

A total of 20 NVIDIA A40 graphics processing units are used for training. First, the model is trained for 3000 epochs with all the training data of 83 tasks. Three candidate spacing settings, i.e., 0.6 × 0.6 × 0.6 mm3, 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3 and 1.5 × 1.5 × 1.5 mm3, are used. During training, it has a 10% chance to choose among three candidate spacing settings randomly, and a 90% chance to choose the candidate closest to the original image spacing. This is a data augmentation strategy to make the network learn the different resolutions of images. Linear interpolation and nearest-neighbor interpolation methods are used for the resampling of images and the ground truth masks respectively. Subsequently, following the standard protocol of data pre-processing, we normalize the processed intensity values in the CT images to the range [0, 1]. Second, for each selected sub-pathway in AP modules, we only select the data of the tasks belonging to this sub-pathway to further update the parameters for another 1000 epochs of the corresponding sub-pathway with other parameters in the model fixed. In this stage, we only use the spacing setting closest to the original image spacing for training the parameters of selected sub-pathways.

Evaluation details

The performance is evaluated by two commonly used metrics Dice and clDice. Dice provides a globally averaged assessment of segmentation performance across all voxels, and clDice evaluates the topological degree of matching for tubular-structure tasks. Also, we show the values of Topology Precision and Topology Sensitivity25 in Tables 2, 3, and Supplementary Table S3, detailing the sensitivity and precision for segmentation results. For all the metrics, results are presented with mean and standard deviation (std). To compare the performance between different methods, a Student’s t-test is employed to determine whether any significant difference between the metrics of the two methods. For comparing gCIS with other methods, a small p-value (p < 0.05) suggests that there is a significant improvement in terms of Dice or clDice using gCIS.

Downstream tasks

gCIS can be trimmed for a downstream task with fewer parameters. Specifically, for each AP module, only one sub-pathway is selected for single-task deployment. For this purpose, the prompt embedding is passed into the softmax function to select the sub-pathway with the highest probability. Since there are originally M = 6 candidate sub-pathways in each AP module, the network trimming operation can reduce the parameters of AP modules to 1/6. In contrast, the original general model in the training of all tasks has 60.50 million parameters, while the trimmed model for the downstream task uses only 26.75 million parameters. We compare the number of parameters, computational complexity, and memory usage from four models: nnU-Net, Cascaded Vb-Net, sole-path gCIS (trimmed gCIS), and gCIS in Supplementary Note 5. It could be a great advantage to deploy the proposed gCIS model for downstream tasks in real clinical centers. Because only one pathway from each AP module is selected during training and inference for each task, all the parameters for the task are retained after pruning. Therefore, its performance will not be affected.
