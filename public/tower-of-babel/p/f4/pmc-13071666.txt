The potential role of pan-immune-inflammation value in differentiating pediatric osteomyelitis from soft tissue infections
Authors: Gang Ren, Xin Wang, Zhenjiang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDistinguishing between pediatric osteomyelitis (OM) and soft tissue infection (STI) remains a diagnostic challenge due to overlapping clinical and laboratory features. The pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV) is a new comprehensive biomarker calculated from counts of blood cells taken from outside the main part of the body, showing the overall condition of the immune system and inflammation in the body. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic utility of PIV in differentiating OM from STI in children.MethodsThe retrospective study conducted a review of past data involving children who visited Department of Orthopedics, Center for Children’s Health, Capital Medical University from January 2020 to May 2025. The cohort consisted of 59 children with OM and 310 with STI. Demographic, clinical, and laboratory parameters were systematically collected for all participants. PIV was calculated and analyzed.ResultsThe OM group showed higher PIV levels compared to the STI group (512.6±198.4 vs. 220.3±115.7, P<0.001). Multivariate analysis identified PIV as an independent predictor for OM [odds ratio (OR) =1.012, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.008–1.016, P<0.001]. The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) for PIV was 0.892 (95% CI: 0.845–0.939), with a sensitivity of 84.7% and specificity of 82.3% at the optimal cutoff value of 385.6. The combination of PIV with C-reactive protein (CRP) further improved diagnostic accuracy (AUC =0.926).ConclusionsPIV is a promising, readily available biomarker that shows potential to effectively differentiate pediatric OM from STIs, potentially aiding in early diagnosis and appropriate management upon further validation.Keywords: Pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV), osteomyelitis (OM), soft tissue infection (STI), pediatric, diagnostic biomarker

Key findings

• Pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV) was significantly higher in pediatric osteomyelitis (OM) than soft tissue infection (STI) (512.6 vs. 220.3, P<0.001), with area under the curve (AUC) of 0.892 for differentiation.

• Combining pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV) with C-reactive protein (CRP) improved diagnostic accuracy to AUC 0.926, outperforming other indices like neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and systemic immune-inflammation index.

What is known and what is new?

• Traditional markers like CRP and erythrocyte sedimentation rate lack specificity for distinguishing OM from STI; hematologic ratios like NLR show promise in inflammation.

• PIV, integrating neutrophils, platelets, monocytes, and lymphocytes, independently predicts OM with superior AUC over existing indices.

What are the implications, and what should change now?

• PIV has the potential to enable early, cost-effective differentiation, reducing misdiagnosis risks like chronic infection or unnecessary procedures, pending validation in larger studies.

• It is suggested to integrate PIV into routine diagnostic protocols for pediatric musculoskeletal infections to improve accuracy and management.

Introduction

Osteomyelitis (OM) and soft tissue infections (STI) represent two of the most common bacterial infections in the pediatric population, often presenting with remarkably similar clinical features including fever, localized pain, swelling, erythema, and functional impairment (1). Clinicians face immense difficulties in this diagnostic quandary, especially when it comes to emergency departments and primary care settings where accurate differentiation is essential for initiating suitable treatment (2). The consequences of misdiagnosis can be severe, with delayed treatment of OM leading to chronic infection, growth plate damage, permanent deformity, and long-term disability (3). Conversely, over-diagnosis of OM may result in unnecessary invasive procedures, prolonged antibiotic courses, and increased healthcare costs (4).

Current diagnostic approaches rely heavily on clinical suspicion supported by laboratory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and white blood cell (WBC) count (5). However, these markers lack sufficient specificity as they can be elevated in both conditions (6). Imaging modalities, particularly magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), have become the gold standard for distinguishing OM from STI due to their excellent soft tissue and bone marrow resolution (7). Nevertheless, MRI availability is often limited in resource-constrained settings, requires sedation in young children, and may not be immediately accessible in emergency situations (7). These limitations underscore the critical need for readily available, cost-effective biomarkers that can aid in early and accurate differentiation between these two clinical entities (8). In recent years, there has been growing interest in hematologic indices derived from complete blood count (CBC) parameters as potential biomarkers of inflammatory states (9,10). Indicators like the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) have been proven to be valuable tools in a range of inflammatory and infectious diseases. These composite indices offer advantages over single parameters by providing a more comprehensive reflection of the systemic inflammatory response and immune activation (11,12). However, their application in pediatric musculoskeletal infections, particularly for distinguishing between OM and STI, remains relatively unexplored and results have been inconsistent (13,14).

The pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV) represents a novel integrative biomarker that incorporates four key cellular components of the immune response: neutrophils, platelets, monocytes, and lymphocytes (15). Calculated using the formula (neutrophils × platelets × monocytes)/lymphocytes, PIV provides a holistic assessment of both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory pathways (16). Initially investigated in oncology as a prognostic indicator, PIV has recently shown promise in various infectious and inflammatory conditions, including sepsis and pneumonia (17,18). Its ability to capture the complex interplay between different immune cell populations makes it particularly attractive for assessing the severity and nature of infectious processes (19).

The pathophysiology of OM involves a more profound and localized inflammatory response compared to STI, with greater bone marrow involvement and systemic immune activation (20). The initial response to bone infection involves massive neutrophil infiltration followed by monocyte recruitment and platelet activation, all while lymphocyte populations may be relatively suppressed (20,21). This distinct immunopathological profile suggests that PIV, which integrates these cellular components, might serve as an ideal discriminator between these two conditions (22). Therefore, we hypothesized that PIV would be significantly elevated in children with OM compared to those with STI and would demonstrate superior diagnostic performance compared to traditional inflammatory markers and other hematologic indices. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (23) (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2025-aw-807/rc).

Methods

Study design and population

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Center for Children’s Health, Capital Medical University (No. SHELL2024063). Informed consent was waived by the ethics committee due to the use of anonymized retrospective data retrieved from electronic health records. We examined the electronic health records of pediatric patients under the age of 18 who were hospitalized with a diagnosis of OM or STI from January 2020 to May 2025. Patients were classified based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes for otitis media (M86.0-M86.9) and skin and STIs (L03.0-L03.9). The initial search identified 387 potential cases (68 OM and 319 STI). After thorough review by two independent investigators, 18 cases were excluded based on the following pre-defined criteria: (I) incomplete medical records or missing laboratory data (n=9); (II) presence of other inflammatory or autoimmune diseases (n=4); (III) recent surgery or trauma within one month (n=3); (IV) immunodeficiency or malignancy (n=2). The final analysis included 369 patients (59 OM and 310 STI).

Data collection

Thorough information was gathered from electronic health records utilizing a uniform data extraction document. Demographic information included age, gender, and comorbidities. Clinical characteristics included duration of symptoms before admission, presence and duration of fever, affected anatomical site, and physical examination findings. Laboratory parameters were obtained from the first blood test performed upon admission, including CBC (WBC, neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, and platelet counts), CRP, and ESR. All blood samples were processed using standardized automated analyzers (Sysmex XN-1000 for hematological parameters and Cobas 8000 for CRP measurements). Calculation of PIV and other indices

PIV was calculated using the formula below: PIV = (neutrophil count × platelet count × monocyte count)/lymphocyte count, where all cell counts were expressed as 109/L (15). The neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, and platelet counts were obtained directly from the automated CBC report. Additionally, several different established calculations were identified for the purpose of comparison, such as NLR, PLR, and SII (10,11).

Diagnostic criteria

The diagnosis of OM required a combination of clinical features (e.g., localized pain, swelling, warmth, limited range of motion), elevated inflammatory markers (CRP >20 mg/L or ESR >20 mm/h), and confirmatory imaging findings (MRI in 54/59 cases, CT in 5/59 cases) consistent with OM (e.g., bone marrow edema, periosteal reaction) (24). The MRI/CT images were interpreted by experienced pediatric radiologists who were blinded to the patient’s laboratory results, including PIV and other hematologic indices, to minimize incorporation bias. For STI, diagnosis was primarily clinical, supported by elevated inflammatory markers and typical physical examination findings. Imaging (ultrasound or MRI) was performed in 187 out of 310 STI cases (60.3%) when there was diagnostic uncertainty or to assess for abscess formation. Positive culture results from blood, tissue, or abscess aspiration, when available, further supported the diagnosis in both groups (25,26).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.2.0, with appropriate tests applied based on data distribution. The discriminative power of PIV and other biomarkers was assessed via ROC curve analysis, multivariate logistic regression, and DeLong test, with statistical significance set at P<0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The study population consisted of 369 children, of whom 59 were diagnosed with OM and 310 with STI. No significant disparities in age were seen between the two groups (OM: 7.2±3.5 vs. STI: 6.8±4.1 years; P=0.45) or gender distribution (male: 59.3% vs. 55.5%; P=0.58). However, children with OM exhibited a significantly longer duration of symptoms prior to admission compared to those with STI (5.8±2.4 vs. 3.2±1.9 days; P<0.001), as well as a longer duration of fever (4.5±2.1 vs. 2.8±1.6 days; P<0.001). The lower extremities were the most frequently involved site in both groups, although the distribution of infection sites differed significantly (P=0.02). As expected, conventional markers of inflammation, CRP and ESR, showed a notable increase in the OM group (both with a P value less than 0.001). Laboratory parameters and hematologic indices

Table 2 offers a comparison of laboratory parameters between the two groups. No significant difference was observed in the total WBC count (OM: 14.2±4.8 ×109/L vs. STI: 13.1±5.2 ×109/L; P=0.13). Multivariate logistic regression analysis

Univariate analysis identified several variables significantly associated with OM, including symptom duration, fever duration, CRP, ESR, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, NLR, PLR, SII, and PIV (all P<0.05). Given the limited number of OM events (n=59), to avoid overfitting, we constructed a parsimonious multivariate logistic regression model including only the most clinically relevant and non-collinear predictors based on univariate results and biological plausibility. The final model included symptom duration, CRP, and PIV. In this model (Table 3), both PIV [odds ratio (OR) =1.011, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.007–1.015, P<0.001] and CRP (OR =1.026, 95% CI: 1.014–1.038, P<0.001) remained significant independent predictors of OM, while symptom duration showed a trend (P=0.06). The variance inflation factors for all included variables were below 2.0, indicating no substantial multicollinearity. The model demonstrated adequate calibration, as assessed by the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (χ2=7.24, P=0.51).

To account for potential confounding by the time interval between symptom onset and laboratory sampling, we performed an additional multivariate logistic regression analysis adjusting for symptom duration. After adjusting for symptom duration, PIV (adjusted OR =1.011, 95% CI: 1.007–1.015, P<0.001) and CRP (adjusted OR =1.026, 95% CI: 1.014–1.039, P<0.001) remained significant independent predictors of OM.

Diagnostic performance analysis

The diagnostic performance of various biomarkers for discriminating between OM and STI is summarized (Table 4). PIV exhibited excellent discriminative capacity, with an area under the curve of 0.892 (95% CI: 0.845–0.939). This performance was significantly superior to that of NLR (AUC =0.815; P=0.01), PLR (AUC =0.782; P=0.003), and SII (AUC =0.834; P=0.03). The optimal cutoff value for PIV was determined to be 385.6, yielding a sensitivity of 84.7% and a specificity of 82.3%. CRP also demonstrated good diagnostic ability (AUC =0.865). However, the combination of PIV and CRP achieved the highest AUC of 0.926 (95% CI: 0.888–0.964), which was significantly greater than that of CRP alone (P=0.04).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the PIV serves as a novel and highly effective biomarker for differentiating pediatric OM from STIs. Our findings reveal several important aspects of PIV’s diagnostic utility. First, PIV was significantly elevated in children with OM compared to those with STI, reflecting the more intense systemic inflammatory response and immune activation associated with bone infection. Second, multivariate analysis identified PIV as an independent predictor of OM, even after adjusting for other inflammatory markers and clinical variables, suggesting it provides unique pathophysiological information. Third, PIV demonstrated excellent discriminative ability with an AUC of 0.892, outperforming other established hematologic indices such as NLR, PLR, and SII. Finally, the combination of PIV with CRP achieved superior diagnostic accuracy (AUC =0.926), suggesting that these markers provide complementary information about the inflammatory process.

The superior performance of PIV can be attributed to its comprehensive incorporation of multiple immune cell lineages that play distinct roles in the pathogenesis of musculoskeletal infections. Neutrophils play a vital role in combating bacterial infections as the primary defense mechanism, essential for containing both OM and STIs at the onset. However, in OM, the recruitment of neutrophils into the bone marrow space is particularly robust due to the rich vascular network and cytokine milieu, leading to more pronounced neutrophilia. Monocytes, which differentiate into macrophages and osteoclasts, play a dual role in bone infections by both combating pathogens and contributing to bone resorption. The significantly higher monocyte counts in OM patients observed in our study support their important role in bone infection pathophysiology. Platelets, while traditionally viewed as mediators of hemostasis, are increasingly recognized as key regulators of inflammation and immune responses (27). Activated platelets release numerous inflammatory mediators and interact with leukocytes through P-selectin and other adhesion molecules, amplifying the inflammatory cascade (28). Although absolute platelet counts did not differ significantly between groups in our study, their incorporation into the PIV formula likely contributes to its discriminative power by reflecting platelet activation status rather than mere numbers. Lymphocytes, particularly T cells, play a crucial role in modulating the immune response to infection. The relative lymphopenia observed in OM patients may reflect lymphocyte sequestration at the infection site, apoptosis due to inflammatory stress, or redistribution to secondary lymphoid organs (20). This lymphopenia contributes to the elevated PIV values and underscores the complex immune dysregulation occurring in bone infections. The integration of these four cell types into a single index allows PIV to capture the net balance between pro-inflammatory (neutrophils, monocytes, platelets) and anti-inflammatory or immunoregulatory (lymphocytes) forces, providing a more holistic view of the host’s immune status than any single parameter or simpler ratios (15,16).

The combination of PIV with CRP achieved the highest diagnostic accuracy in our study, highlighting the complementary nature of cellular and acute-phase protein biomarkers. CRP, produced by hepatocytes in response to IL-6 and other cytokines, provides information about the intensity of the systemic inflammatory response (29). Countless elements, including liver functioning, nutrition, and the timing of measurement, can affect CRP levels. PIV, derived from cellular components, may provide a more direct reflection of real-time immune activation and thus complement CRP in the diagnostic process. This synergistic effect suggests that a model incorporating both markers could be highly useful in clinical practice, potentially guiding the need for further imaging.

Our findings have important clinical implications. The availability of PIV from routine CBC data makes it an attractive, cost-effective tool that can be readily implemented in various healthcare settings, including those with limited access to advanced imaging. The optimal cutoff value of 385.6 identified in our study provides clinicians with a concrete threshold for decision-making. Children presenting with suspected musculoskeletal infections and PIV values above this threshold should undergo prompt imaging evaluation for OM, potentially reducing diagnostic delays and improving outcomes. Conversely, those with values below the cutoff might be managed more conservatively, avoiding unnecessary radiation exposure or MRI studies. Our study, despite its promising findings, has certain limitations that must be recognized. To begin with, the retrospective, single-center design introduces a risk of selection bias and restricts our capacity to manage all confounding variables, even after taking into account our multivariate adjustments. Second, the marked imbalance in group sizes (59 OM vs. 310 STI) may influence the stability of the ROC estimates and the derived cutoff value, potentially overestimating diagnostic performance in the smaller OM group. The OM group’s sample size, being notably diminutive, could potentially impede the generalizability of our discoveries and the accuracy of the estimated cutoff value; thus, external validation in a larger, multi-center cohort with a more balanced distribution is essential. Third, we only analyzed admission laboratory values and cannot comment on the dynamic changes in PIV during the course of treatment, which might provide valuable prognostic information. Fourth, the longer symptom duration observed in the OM group could have influenced the levels of inflammatory markers, including PIV and CRP. Although we statistically adjusted for symptom duration in a sensitivity analysis, residual confounding may still exist. The differences in PIV might partially reflect a more advanced stage of infection at presentation rather than an intrinsic pathophysiological distinction. Future studies with standardized sampling timepoints relative to symptom onset are needed to better delineate this relationship. Furthermore, the optimal PIV cutoff value of 385.6 was derived and tested within the same cohort without internal validation (e.g., bootstrapping or split-sample validation). This increases the risk of overfitting and may lead to an overoptimistic estimation of its diagnostic performance in new patient populations. Therefore, this specific cutoff should be considered preliminary and must be prospectively validated before clinical implementation. Future prospective studies should investigate serial PIV measurements to assess its utility in monitoring therapeutic response and predicting complications. Additionally, exploring the relationship between PIV and specific pathogens, as well as its performance in different pediatric age subgroups, would be valuable research directions.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the PIV demonstrates considerable promise as a diagnostic biomarker for differentiating pediatric OM from STIs. Its calculation relies solely on routine CBC parameters, rendering it highly accessible, cost-effective, and straightforward to implement across diverse clinical environments. The strong discriminative performance of PIV—especially when used in combination with CRP—indicates its potential to substantially improve early diagnostic precision and inform clinical decision-making. We advocate for further validation of these results through larger, prospective, multi-center studies before any clinical implementation can be recommended. Future research should also investigate the utility of PIV for monitoring treatment response and predicting clinical outcomes in pediatric musculoskeletal infections.
