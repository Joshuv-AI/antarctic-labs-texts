A Novel Role of Dexmedetomidine in the Modulation of Morphine Reward Memory via γ-Aminobutyric Acid Transporter-1
Authors: Jian He, Guanhua Lu, Wanyou He, Fancan Wu, Huailong Zhang, Wenyan Pan, Hongseng Xu, Fuyu Wang, Chengrui Zhuang, Keyi Deng, Manying Bian, Jingxin Wu, Jianxun Xia, Hanbing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Opioid use disorder (OUD) remains a major public health challenge with limited effective treatments. Opioid-induced reward memory, driven primarily by increased dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens (NAc), contributes to the development and relapse of OUD. Dexmedetomidine (Dex) has been reported to reduce dopamine release in the NAc, but its ability in OUD intervention remains unclear. This study aimed to determine whether Dex promotes extinction of morphine-induced reward memory.Methods:Adult male and female C57BL/6J mice were subjected to morphine-induced conditioned place preference (CPP) to evaluate reward memory. CPP scores were measured after systemic Dex administration or microinjection into the ventral tegmental area (VTA). Calcium imaging and whole cell patch clamp recordings were used to evaluate the excitability of dopaminergic and γ-aminobutyric acid–mediated (GABAergic) neurons in the VTA and D1-type medium spiny neurons (D1-MSNs) in the NAc. Extracellular γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and dopamine in the VTA and NAc were quantified using fiber photometry. Molecular docking and microscale thermophoresis were performed to validate the binding of Dex to GABA transporter-1 (GAT1).Results:Systemic and intra-VTA Dex administration facilitated the extinction of morphine-induced CPP and suppressed increased dopamine release with resultant hyperactivity of D1-MSNs in the NAc. In the VTA, Dex attenuated hyperexcitability of dopaminergic neurons by increasing extracellular GABA without affecting GABAergic neuron activity or GABA synthesis. Molecular and functional assays demonstrated that Dex competitively inhibits GAT1, reducing GABA reuptake. Intra-VTA bicuculline—but not idazoxan—blocked Dex’s proextinction effect, indicating a GABA receptor but not an α2-dependent mechanism.Conclusions:Dex facilitates the extinction of morphine-associated reward memory by competitively inhibiting GAT1, enhancing VTA GABAergic inhibition, and rescuing NAc D1-MSN activity. These findings reveal a novel GAT1-dependent, α2-independent mechanism by which Dex modulates opioid reward circuitry, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent for OUD.

Opioids, such as morphine and fentanyl, are widely used for the treatment of moderate to severe acute and chronic pain.1 However, their misuse can lead to opioid use disorder (OUD), which has been considered a public health issue termed the opioid epidemic.2 OUD is a chronic relapsing neuropsychiatric disorder characterized by compulsive drug seeking despite adverse consequences, which is correlated with maladaptive reward processing. The 2022 World Drug Report estimates that 1.2% of the global population aged 15 to 64 yr (approximately 61.3 million individuals) had OUD in 2020, underscoring the urgency of addressing this issue. Opioid agonists (methadone and buprenorphine) and antagonists (naltrexone) have been adopted to reduce cravings and alleviate withdrawal symptoms in OUD patients.3 Methadone maintenance treatment has been widely used to decrease the use of opioids4 and alleviate withdrawal symptoms,5 but patients often require prolonged—or lifelong—maintenance treatment, and tapering frequently triggers rebound withdrawal symptoms (e.g., anxiety, insomnia, intense cravings), heightening relapse risk.6 Injectable extended-release naltrexone, by blocking opioid effects, has demonstrated improved treatment adherence compared with oral formulations7; however, even long-acting naltrexone does not guarantee long-term abstinence.8 Consequently, novel approaches to treat OUD are needed.

Opioid-induced reward memory is the root driver of OUD.9 The mesolimbic dopamine pathway—which especially originates in ventral tegmental area (VTA) dopaminergic neurons and projects to the nucleus accumbens (NAc)—is central to reward and compulsive drug seeking.10,11 Opioids activate µ -opioid receptors on γ-aminobutyric acid–mediated (GABAergic) interneurons and inhibit their activity in the VTA. This disinhibition reduces the inhibitory input to dopaminergic neurons, thereby increasing their activity and dopamine release in the NAc, which induces reward-related memory and reinforces drug-seeking behavior.12

Dexmedetomidine (Dex), a highly selective α2-adrenergic agonist, is widely used for sedation and analgesia in clinical anesthesia and intensive care settings.13 Previous studies have demonstrated that Dex can decrease extracellular dopamine concentrations in the NAc of rats,14,15 suggesting that it may regulate opioid-induced reward memory. Accumulating evidence has further demonstrated that Dex can alleviate opioid withdrawal symptoms16–19 and attenuate the development of morphine tolerance in rats.20,21 These findings suggest that Dex is a potential pharmacologic candidate for OUD therapy.

This study aimed to evaluate whether Dex facilitates the extinction of morphine-induced reward memory and elucidate the underlying mechanisms involved. Based on previous evidence linking mesolimbic dopaminergic activity to opioid reward, we hypothesized that Dex promotes the extinction of reward memory by inhibiting the VTA–NAc dopaminergic pathway. To test this hypothesis, we employed a conditioned place preference (CPP) paradigm combined with fiber photometry and patch-clamp recordings to evaluate the effects of Dex on morphine-associated reward memory. Molecular docking and microscale thermophoresis were used to determine whether Dex directly interacts with γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) transporter-1 (GAT1) to increase extracellular GABA levels. This integrative approach enabled us to clarify how Dex influences the brain reward circuits involved in morphine reward memory, suggesting that Dex is a novel candidate for treating OUD beyond its traditional role in anesthesia.

Materials and Methods

Only a concise summary of the experimental procedures is provided in this section. Full and detailed protocols are available in the Supplemental Digital Content (https://links.lww.com/ALN/E442).

Animals

All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center (Guangzhou, China; approval No. D202303-48). Adult male and female C57BL/6J mice were obtained from Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center. For cell type–specific manipulations, C57BL/6J mice were crossed with glutamate decarboxylase 2 (Gad2)-Cre, dopamine transporter (DAT)-Cre, dopamine receptor D1-Cre, and dopamine receptor D2-Cre lines. Mice were maintained under a 12-h light/dark cycle at 22° to 25°C with ad libitum access to food and water. All experiments were performed in mice aged 8 to 10 weeks and weighing 20 to 25 g. This study enrolled a total of 511 mice. Among them, 59 mice were excluded due to inaccurate injection site (18 mice), incorrect fiber position (11 mice), poor postoperative recovery (6 mice), or unsuccessful establishment of CPP (24 mice). The remaining 452 mice were included in the data analysis. The sample size was determined based on previous studies and experience with feasibility of the experiment. Experimental units were randomly allocated to control and treatment groups using a computer-generated random sequence. Outcome assessment and data analysis were conducted in a blinded manner.

Chemicals and Viruses

Morphine hydrochloride, Dex, clonidine, GABA, idazoxan, and bicuculline were obtained from commercial suppliers. Primary and secondary antibodies, as well as adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors (rAAV-hSyn-DA3h, rAAV-hSyn-iGABASnFR, rAAV-EF1α-DIO-GCaMP6s), were purchased from Abcam (United Kingdom) or BrainCase Biotechnology (China). Detailed catalog numbers and sources are provided in the Supplemental Digital Content (https://links.lww.com/ALN/E442).

Conditioned Place Preference

The CPP test was conducted as previously described.22 CPP was performed in a two-compartment apparatus (35 × 20 × 30 cm). Baseline preference was assessed during 2 days, and mice with strong innate bias (more than 600 s in one chamber) or low locomotion (fewer than 20 crossings) were excluded. During conditioning, mice received saline in the preferred chamber for 45 min in the morning, followed 4 h or more later by daily morphine injections (10 to 50 mg/kg intraperitoneally) in the nonpreferred chamber for 6 days. Postconditioning CPP was tested 24 h after the last session. Extinction was evaluated by daily Dex or saline injections, with CPP testing every other day up to 14 days after detoxification. In CPP experiments, animals with baseline CPP more than 600 s or posttraining CPP less than 90 s were excluded.

Locomotion Test

Mice were injected with saline or Dex and 12 h later placed in an open field (50 × 50 × 50 cm) for 15 min. Total distance traveled was recorded via MATLAB (MathWorks, USA).

Naloxone-precipitated Withdrawal

Morphine dependence was induced by 6 days of escalating doses (intraperitoneal), as described in the Conditioned Place Preference section. On the final day, Dex (systemic or intra–locus coeruleus [LC]) was administered concurrently with morphine. Two hours later, naloxone (5 mg/kg intraperitoneally) was used to precipitate withdrawal, with three cardinal signs (jumps, rearing, tremors) scored by blinded observers for 20 min.

AAV Injection and Fiber Implantation

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (3% induction, 1 to 1.5% maintenance) and placed in a stereotaxic frame with body temperature maintained at 36°C. AAVs (200 nl, 2 × 1012 vg/ml) were unilaterally injected into the NAc or VTA at 60 nl/min, with the pipette left in place 10 min. Postoperative analgesia (meloxicam 5 mg/kg intraperitoneally) and erythromycin ointment were applied. Two weeks later, fiber optic cannulae (200 μm, 0.37 numerical aperture) were implanted 0.05 mm above injection sites and secured.

Fiber Photometry

Three weeks after AAV injection (GCaMP6s, DA3h, or iGABASnFR), fluorescence in NAc or VTA was recorded via fiber photometry. Signals were excited at 480 nm (less than 50 μW) and detected by a photomultiplier tube, then analyzed with custom MATLAB scripts.

Intracerebral Cannulation and Injection

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and secured in a stereotaxic frame. A craniotomy was performed, and unilateral 26-gauge guide cannulae were implanted into the VTA (anterior–posterior, –3.1 mm; medial–lateral, ±0.5 mm; dorsal–ventral, 4.5 mm coordinates) and secured with dental cement and skull screws. Postoperative analgesia (meloxicam 5 mg/kg) was given. Drugs were infused via a 33-gauge injector at 0.1 μl/min, with the injector left in place 5 min to prevent reflux.

Brain Slice Electrophysiology

Mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and transcardially perfused with ice-cold oxygenated choline-based cutting solution. Coronal slices (250 to 300 μm) containing VTA or NAc were prepared and incubated in oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid at 32°C for 30 min or more.

Green fluorescent protein–positive (GFP⁺) neurons were visualized with infrared differential interference contrast and 488 nm fluorescence. Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed using a Multiclamp 700B (Molecular Devices, USA) amplifier and Digidata 1550B (Molecular Devices). Current-clamp recordings used a K+-based internal solution (in mM, 130 K-gluconate, 10 KCl, 10 HEPES, 1 EGTA, 2 Na-GTP, 2 Mg-ATP, and 2 MgCl₂) to monitor spontaneous and evoked action potentials (0 to 200 pA, 20 pA steps). Voltage-clamp recordings used a Cs+-based solution (in mM, 135 Cs-methanesulfonate, 10 HEPES, 1 EGTA, 3.3 QX-314, 8 Na₂-phosphocreatine, 4 Mg-ATP, and 0.3 Na-GTP) to measure spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents at −70 mV; hyperpolarization-activated cation currents were induced from −60 to −130 mV. Signals were filtered at 10 kHz, sampled at 20 kHz, and analyzed with Clampfit 11.0 (Molecular Devices) and MATLAB. Neurons with access resistance greater than 20% were excluded. Experiments were performed at 22° to 24°C with experimenters blinded to conditions.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Brains were perfused with saline and 4% paraformaldehyde, postfixed (4°C, 12 h), cryoprotected in 20 to 30% sucrose, and coronally sectioned at 30 μm. Sections were blocked in phosphate-buffered saline with 3% donkey serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1 h, then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against glutamate decarboxylase 65 (GAD65, 1:500) and tyrosine hydroxylase (TH, 1:200). After washes, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) or Cyanine 3 secondary antibodies (1:200 to 1:400) for 2 h, mounted with antifade medium, and imaged on a Keyence fluorescence microscope (Japan).

Western Blotting

VTA tissues were homogenized. Proteins (20 µg) were separated on 10% sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride, blocked, and incubated with anti-glutamate decarboxylase 65/67 (GAD65/67) and glyceraldehyde‑3‑phosphate dehydrogenase overnight. After horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies and enhanced chemiluminescence development, bands were imaged and quantified with ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA), normalized to glyceraldehyde‑3‑phosphate dehydrogenase.

Microscale Thermophoresis

Microscale thermophoresis (MST) was performed as previously described.23 Lysates containing GAT1-GFP or GFP were diluted in 1 × Tris-NaCl-Tween 20 buffer to optimal fluorescence (2/3 and 1/100, respectively). Dex (2,000 μM) and GABA (8,000 μM) were subjected to a 16-point twofold serial dilution and mixed 1:1 with diluted lysates, followed by 15-min incubation at 25°C. Samples were loaded into Monolith NT.115 standard treated capillaries and measured using autoexcitation and high MST power. Kd values were derived using NanoTemper MO.Affinity Analysis (NanoTemper Technologies GmbH, Germany), with buffer controls correcting nonspecific binding and data normalized to fraction bound.

Statistical Analysis

No formal statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size; however, the sample sizes were consistent with those reported in a previous study in the field.22 All data from animals that completed the experimental protocol were included in the statistical analysis. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed via IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., USA). The normality of the distribution was verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test (α = 0.05), with homogeneity of variance assessed via Levene’s test. For normally distributed datasets, parametric analyses were applied, including repeated-measures ANOVA and one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multigroup comparisons. Nonnormally distributed data were analyzed via nonparametric methods, specifically the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc analysis. Within-group comparisons (e.g., pre- vs. postintervention) were performed via paired t tests. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Dex Facilitates the Extinction of Morphine-induced CPP

A mouse CPP model was established by conditioning with increasing doses of morphine according to a previous study (fig. 1A).22 After 6 days of morphine conditioning, the mice exhibited a significant preference for the morphine-paired chamber, as evaluated by CPP scores (fig. 1B). The mice with a CPP score of 90 s or more were divided into three groups: saline control, Dex 50 μg/kg, and Dex 100 μg/kg (intraperitoneal), with Dex or saline administered daily for 4 consecutive days. Both Dex groups presented accelerated CPP extinction and spent significantly less time in the morphine-paired chamber than did the saline controls (fig. 1, C and D). This proextinction effect persisted until day 14, indicating a lasting impact beyond the treatment period (fig. 1, E to I).

To rule out the possibility that these effects were due to the sedative property of Dex, an open field test was performed 12 h after Dex administration. The results revealed no significant difference in the total distance traveled within 15 min between mice injected with saline or Dex (fig. 1, J and K), indicating that Dex-induced CPP extinction is not attributable to sedative effects. Consistent with the findings of clinical case reports,17 Dex (50 µg/kg intraperitoneally) significantly attenuated naloxone-precipitated withdrawal symptoms in morphine-dependent mice, as evidenced by a reduction in vertical jumps, rearing, and body tremors (Supplemental Digital Content fig. S1, A to E, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E438).

Dex Attenuates Morphine-induced Dopamine Release and Activation of D1-type Medium Spiny Neurons in the NAc

As increased NAc dopamine is a key mediator of opioid-induced reward,10 we used fiber photometry to monitor dopamine dynamics. The mice received unilateral intra-NAc injections of the dopamine sensor rAAV-hSyn-DA3h 3 weeks before morphine conditioning. After 2 weeks, optical fibers were implanted, followed by 1 week of recovery and 6 days of escalating-dose morphine conditioning (fig. 2, A and B). Dopamine signals were recorded 5 min before and 20 min after saline, morphine, or morphine plus Dex (50 μg/kg) administration on day 7 (fig. 2C). Our results revealed that, compared with saline, morphine induced a sustained increase in NAc dopamine, as reflected by a greater area under the curve, whereas coadministration of Dex significantly attenuated this effect (fig. 2, D to F). These findings indicate that Dex suppresses morphine-induced dopamine release in the NAc, thereby providing a neural substrate for its proextinction effects. Given that dopaminergic axons in the NAc predominantly originate from the VTA,24 we further administered Dex directly into the VTA via guide cannulas. Local VTA microinjection of Dex markedly inhibited morphine-evoked NAc dopamine release (fig. 2, G to J), confirming that Dex modulates mesolimbic dopamine transmission through the VTA.

To investigate cell type–specific mechanisms, we focused on D1-type medium spiny neurons (D1-MSNs) in the NAc, a key population critical for reward processing. D1-Cre mice received rAAV-EF1α-DIO-GCaMP6s injection in the NAc, followed by unilateral fiber implantation. After recovery, the mice underwent 6 days of escalating-dose morphine conditioning (fig. 3, A to C). Fiber photometry was used to capture the calcium dynamics for 5 min before and 20 min after drug injection. Compared with saline administration, acute morphine administration (50 mg/kg intraperitoneally) robustly increased Ca2+ signals in D1-MSNs, which was significantly attenuated by coadministration of Dex (50 μg/kg intraperitoneally) (fig. 3, D to F). These findings demonstrated that Dex preferentially suppresses morphine-driven hyperactivity in D1-MSNs, which may underlie the observed facilitation of addiction memory extinction. In contrast, in adjacent D2-type medium spiny neurons (D2-MSNs), morphine reduced Ca2+ activity, and Dex did not further enhance this suppression; instead, D2-MSN activity in the morphine + dexmedetomidine group resembled saline levels, suggesting a partial restoration toward baseline (Supplemental Digital Content fig. S2, A to E, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E439).

To further explore the effects of Dex on NAc D1-MSNs, whole cell patch clamp recordings revealed that morphine conditioning increased the firing rate of evoked action potentials, which was effectively suppressed by Dex (fig. 3, G to J). The firing frequency of spontaneous action potentials serves as an indicator of neuronal excitability.25 Voltage clamp recordings further revealed elevated spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic current frequency in morphine-conditioned mice, which was also reduced by Dex (fig. 3, K and L).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that Dex can dampen the morphine-induced activation of D1-MSNs, highlighting its potential to disrupt rewarding memory consolidation involved in morphine addiction.

Dex Alleviates the Morphine-induced Hyperexcitability of VTA Dopaminergic Neurons

Since VTA dopaminergic neurons serve as the primary source of dopamine projections to the NAc,10 we targeted these neurons in DAT-Cre mice via stereotaxic injection of AAV-EF1α-DIO-GCaMP6s into the VTA and subsequent optical fiber implantation for cell type–specific fiber photometry. Compared with saline, morphine (50 mg/kg intraperitoneally) robustly increased calcium signals in VTA dopaminergic neurons (fig. 4, A to F). Critically, coadministration of Dex (50 μg/kg intraperitoneally) significantly attenuated the morphine-induced increase in calcium signals (fig. 4, D to F).

To further elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we performed whole cell patch clamp recordings of VTA dopaminergic neurons projecting to the NAc. These neurons were identified by their prominent hyperpolarization-activated cation current.26 DAT-Cre mice received unilateral VTA injections of AAV-DIO-GFP to genetically label dopaminergic neurons, followed by a 1-week viral expression period (fig. 4, G to I). Our electrophysiologic data revealed that chronic morphine exposure increased both the spontaneous and current-evoked firing frequencies of VTA dopaminergic neurons, which was effectively suppressed by Dex treatment (fig. 4, J and K; Supplemental Digital Content fig. S3, A to D, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E440). Acute slice recordings further revealed that local Dex (1 μM) perfusion reduced the frequency of spontaneous action potentials in morphine-conditioned dopaminergic neurons in the VTA, and this effect was reversed after a 5-min washout with artificial cerebrospinal fluid (fig. 4, L and M). These findings strongly confirmed that Dex effectively suppresses morphine-induced hyperactivity in VTA dopaminergic neurons.

Dex Inhibits Dopaminergic Neuron Activity in the VTA by Enhancing GABAergic Tone

Given the critical role of the GABAergic system in regulating dopaminergic function within the VTA, we employed fiber photometry to monitor GABA dynamics in vivo during morphine exposure. Initially, the mice received unilateral VTA injections of the GABA sensor rAAV-hSyn-iGABASnFR followed by optical fiber implantation (fig. 5, A to C). Our results indicated that morphine (50 mg/kg intraperitoneally) injection significantly reduced the GABA signal intensity, whereas Dex alone increased it (fig. 5, D to F). Notably, coadministration of Dex with morphine reversed morphine-induced GABA suppression (fig. 5, D to F).

As VTA GABAergic neurons provide key inhibitory afferents to VTA dopaminergic neurons,27 we investigated whether Dex directly modulates the activity of these GABAergic neurons. Using GAD-Cre mice expressing GCaMP6s, we performed fiber photometry to monitor VTA GABAergic neuron activity (fig. 5, G to I). The results showed that morphine (50 mg/kg intraperitoneally) significantly reduced the calcium signal intensity in VTA GABAergic neurons, which was not reversed by Dex (fig. 5, J to L). Whole cell patch clamp recordings further revealed that morphine-conditioned mice exhibited a reduced frequency of evoked firing of VTA GABAergic neurons, while Dex treatment failed to rescue it (fig. 6, M to O). These results indicated that Dex did not directly activate GABAergic neurons in the VTA.

To further assess whether Dex influences GABA synthesis, we measured the levels of glutamate decarboxylase 1 (GAD1), the enzyme responsible for GABA synthesis, in the VTA using Western blot analysis. Our findings showed no significant increase in GAD1 expression after Dex treatment (fig. 6, A and B), ruling out upregulation of the synthetic enzyme as a mechanism.

In summary, these results demonstrated that Dex did not directly enhance the activity of GABAergic neurons or upregulate GABA synthesis in the VTA. Instead, the observed increase in GABA levels likely arises from indirect mechanisms, such as alterations in GABA reuptake process.

Dex Disrupts GABA Reuptake by Modulating GAT1 Function

Since synaptic GABA clearance mainly depends on GAT1-mediated reuptake,28 we investigated the effects of Dex on GAT1 function. A structural study revealed Y60, Y140, and G65 as critical residues within the GABA-binding pocket in GAT1.29 Our molecular docking simulations revealed that Dex occupies this same pocket, forming hydrogen-bonding and π–π interactions with Y60 and Y140, with a favorable binding energy (–7.48 kcal/mol), suggesting competitive inhibition of GABA binding (fig. 6, C and D; Supplemental Digital Content fig. S4A, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E441).

To validate these predictions, we synthesized the full-length GAT1 protein in vitro and confirmed its integrity via sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (fig. 6E). Microscale thermophoresis showed that GAT1 binds to GABA with high affinity (Kd = 24.8 ± 2.1 μM; fig. 6, F and H). Notably, Dex alone also bound GAT1 with moderate affinity (Kd = 243.5 ± 18.7 μM), and its presence significantly reduced GABA binding affinity (Kd = 4,373 ± 356 μM; fig. 6, G and H). This 176-fold increase in the Kd value indicated that Dex competitively inhibits GABA binding to GAT1, thereby increasing extracellular GABA levels in the VTA.

Collectively, these findings indicate that Dex disrupts GABA reuptake by competitively inhibiting GAT1 function, thereby increasing extracellular GABA levels in the VTA and enhancing GABAergic inhibition of dopaminergic neurons.

Dex Facilitates Morphine-induced CPP Extinction via VTA GABAergic Modulation but Not the LC

Given that the LC is a canonical pharmacologic target of Dex, we tested its role in Dex-mediated extinction of morphine-induced CPP. Cannula-guided Dex administration infusion into the LC alleviated withdrawal symptoms (Supplemental Digital Content fig. S4, B to F, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E441) but failed to promote CPP extinction (fig. 7, A to D), suggesting that the LC is not the primary site mediating Dex’s effect on reward-related memory.

To determine whether VTA-targeted administration of Dex facilitates extinction of morphine-induced CPP, mice were subjected to a 6-day escalating dose of morphine conditioning (fig. 7E). After morphine conditioning, the mice received unilateral infusions of saline, Dex (0.2 μg), or clonidine (0.5 μg) into the VTA for 4 consecutive days (fig. 7, E and F). Only Dex significantly reduced the CPP score, whereas neither saline nor clonidine (another α2-adrenergic agonist) altered the CPP score (fig. 7, G and H; Supplemental Digital Content fig. S4, G to J, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E441), indicating a mechanism beyond classical α2 receptor activation.

To pharmacologically isolate the receptor(s) involved in the Dex effect, we preinjected the VTA with either the α2 antagonist idazoxan (0.5 μg) or the GABA antagonist bicuculline (10 ng) 30 min before systemic Dex (50 μg/kg intraperitoneally) injection in mice subjected to morphine-induced CPP extinction for 4 consecutive days (fig. 7, I and J). Idazoxan pretreatment did not alter the ability of Dex to facilitate CPP extinction; however, this effect was completely abolished by bicuculline (fig. 7, K and L).

Collectively, these results suggest that Dex promotes the extinction of morphine-induced CPP primarily through the modulation of GABA receptor–mediated neurotransmission within the VTA rather than via the α2-adrenergic receptor.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that Dex markedly facilitates the extinction of morphine-induced CPP through a novel mechanism based on GABAergic modulation in the VTA. Both systemic and intra-VTA administration of Dex rescue the excitability of VTA dopaminergic neurons, reduced dopamine release in the NAc, and suppressed the activation of D1-MSNs induced by chronic morphine exposure. Crucially this proextinction effect was mediated not by the classical α2-adrenergic pathway but through competitive inhibition of GAT1, which elevated extracellular GABA level and enhanced inhibitory tone in the VTA dopaminergic neurons.

Chronic opioid exposure suppresses LC noradrenergic neuronal activity, and immediate cessation leads to rebound hyperactivity of these neurons, causing typical withdrawal symptoms such as anxiety, agitation, and hypertension.30,31 In line with previous studies,17–19,32 we found that both systemic and intra-LC administration of Dex, similar to clonidine and lofexidine, can alleviate morphine withdrawal symptoms. Importantly, our data revealed a key distinction between Dex and other α2 agonists. Dex not only attenuated withdrawal symptoms but also markedly facilitated the extinction of morphine-induced CPP. Notably, this proextinction effect was observed after systemic or intra-VTA—but not LC—administration of Dex, and clonidine failed to replicate this effect, implicating that the regulation of withdrawal symptoms and the modulation of reward memory depend on distinct neural circuits. This finding is partly consistent with previous work demonstrating that Dex suppresses stress-induced nicotine seeking,33 highlighting its broader potential to disrupt maladaptive reward processes. Because reward memory is a key mediator of continued drug use and relapse after withdrawal, Dex-induced facilitation of extinction highlights its therapeutic potential in treating OUD.

The mesolimbic dopamine system, particularly the VTA–NAc circuit, is pivotal to opioid reward.10,11,34–36 Our results showed that morphine robustly activated the VTA–NAc dopaminergic pathway, leading to increased dopamine release in the NAc and selective activation of D1-MSNs. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that repeated opioid exposure preferentially enhances D1-MSN excitability in the NAc shell, whereas D2-MSNs are relatively spared.37,38 Importantly, Dex effectively suppressed these morphine-induced dopamine releases, reducing the hyperexcitability of NAc-projecting VTA dopaminergic neurons and rescuing D1-MSN activity. These results suggest that Dex attenuates morphine-associated reward memory by selectively dampening the VTA–NAc dopaminergic circuit, highlighting its therapeutic potential in opioid addiction.

Opioids inhibit GABAergic transmission in the VTA, thereby increasing dopaminergic activity and driving addiction.39,40 Consistent with these findings, we observed that morphine reduced GABA levels and suppressed VTA GABAergic neuron activity. Dex prevented the morphine-induced reduction in GABA but did not restore GABAergic neuron activity or upregulate GAD1 expression, indicating that elevated GABA arose from preserved extracellular levels rather than increased release or synthesis. GAT1, predominantly localized to axon terminals, mediates the reuptake of synaptically released GABA and thus regulates neuronal excitability in the central nervous system.41 Previous studies have demonstrated that enhancing GABAergic signaling can attenuate addictive behaviors and reduce drug craving.42–44 In addition, pharmacologic blockade of GAT1—for example, with tiagabine—elevates extracellular GABA and has been shown to reduce psychostimulant reward-related behaviors.45 Our molecular docking and MST assays revealed that Dex occupies key residues (Y60 and Y140, critical for GABA binding) in the GAT1 binding pocket, reducing GABA affinity by more than 170-fold and acting as a competitive inhibitor. Therefore, Dex can disrupt GABA reuptake by competitively inhibiting GAT1 function and enhancing GABAergic inhibition of VTA dopaminergic neurons. Moreover, intra-VTA administration of Dex—but not clonidine—facilitates morphine-induced CPP extinction, and this effect is abolished by the GABA receptor antagonist bicuculline but not by the α2 antagonist idazoxan. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that Dex promotes the extinction of morphine-associated reward memory primarily by inhibiting GABA reuptake within the VTA. This noncanonical, GAT1-dependent mechanism distinguishes Dex from other α2 agonists and informs a novel therapeutic target for OUD based on GABA clearance.

Considering the essential role of GABA in sedation, this mechanism may also contribute to Dex’s sedative effects, suggesting that GAT1 modulation could underlie both its reward-modulating and sedative properties. While the specific brain regions and relative contributions of this pathway to sedation versus reward memory extinction remain to be fully elucidated, these findings provide a novel intersection between Dex’s actions on GABAergic transmission, addiction-related neural circuits, and arousal regulation, providing new directions for future research and potential clinical applications in OUD.

Although fentanyl now predominates in OUD, morphine remains a clinically relevant and well-established model for studying opioid reward memory and extinction. Morphine-induced CPP provides a robust and extensively characterized paradigm for mechanistic investigations. As both morphine and fentanyl primarily act via μ-opioid receptors and engage overlapping reward circuits, our findings may have broader relevance, although direct validation in fentanyl models is needed.

There are several limitations of this study. First, our findings are derived from mouse CPP models, which primarily capture the associative reward memory and may not fully recapitulate the complexity of human OUD, highlighting the need for clinical trials. Second, Dex-only control groups were not included, so the direct effects of Dex on NAc dopamine release remain to be clarified. Finally, although both male and female mice were included, the sample size for each gender was too small to allow statistical analysis of potential sex-specific effects. Future work should prioritize clinical translation, focusing on whether Dex or selective GAT1 inhibitors can effectively reduce relapse and craving in patients with OUD. In addition, a deeper dissection of GAT1 regulation—including cell type–specific contributions, structural determinants of Dex–GAT1 interactions, and downstream modulation of VTA–NAc microcircuits—will be essential to establish GABA transporter modulation as a viable therapeutic strategy.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that Dex facilitates the extinction of morphine-induced reward memory by restoring GABAergic inhibition in the VTA, thereby suppressing the morphine-induced hyperactivity of dopaminergic neurons and downstream NAc D1-MSNs. This GAT1-dependent, α2-independent mechanism highlights Dex as a promising therapeutic candidate for OUD and underscores GABA transporter modulation as a novel intervention strategy.
