Improving carbon emission estimates through data fusion: Evidence from China’s construction industry
Authors: Jindao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryReliable carbon emission estimates are fundamental to designing effective climate mitigation policies. As one of the largest emitters in China, the construction industry attracts considerable attention. However, prevalent outliers in construction material statistics undermine emission estimates. This study enhances carbon emission estimates in China’s construction industry during 2004–2021 using a data fusion approach. The results indicate that outliers caused a cumulative overestimation of 7,396 million tonnes (Mt), concentrated in 2011–2014 and 2019. Pre-correction data suggest a 2012 peak at 5,337 Mt, yet corrected estimates demonstrate overall growth through 2021, reaching 4,380 Mt. After correction, emissions grew at 16.2% annually during 2004–2014 and then slowed to 3.4% annually. Cement share decreased from 58.4% to 43.0%, while steel increased from 25.0% to 33.6%. Provincial intensity varied 5.2-fold. The data fusion approach can be applied to other sectors or countries facing similar data challenges, helping policymakers to better understand actual emission trajectories.Keywords: carbon emissions, data fusion, input-output analysis, emission factor model, construction industry, climate change

Introduction

High-quality carbon emission estimates are essential for policymakers to formulate effective mitigation policies. However, the quality of statistical data has been a major barrier to accurate estimation of carbon emissions. The quality of China’s official statistics has attracted much concern.1,2,3,4,5,6 One widely discussed issue is long-lasting data discrepancies between national and provincial scale statistics on energy consumption, leading to substantial discrepancies in carbon emissions between these two levels. Guan et al.2 found that the difference in China’s carbon emissions in 2010 due to the discrepancy in coal consumption reached up to 1.4 gigatonnes, equivalent to Japan’s carbon emissions in 2010. In addition to energy consumption statistics, other statistics such as material consumption suffer from similar quality issues. These data issues affect the quality of carbon emission estimates.

A high-quality calculation of the carbon emissions in China’s construction industry is particularly important for China to understand the emissions and take appropriate emission reduction measures. As one of largest emitters, the construction industry generally emits over one-fourth of national carbon emissions in China.7,8,9 The emissions associated with construction materials dominated the industry’s carbon emissions.10,11 The quality of construction material statistics is highly relevant to the estimate quality of construction carbon emissions. Improving the quality of construction material statistics can help achieve more accurate estimates of construction carbon emissions. However, current construction material statistics have many extreme outliers or erroneous values. For example, official records12 show cement consumption in Jilin Province jumped from 19.5 million tonnes (Mt) in 2011 to 1,150 Mt in 2012, a nearly 59-fold increase in 1 year, which is clearly impossible. Failing to deal with these outliers can make the construction carbon emission estimates and subsequent analyses unreliable.

Existing studies have explored the carbon emissions in the construction industry in China using the emission factor method and input-output analysis but largely overlooked data quality issues. The emission factor method, which multiplies energy and material consumption by emission factors, is commonly used to estimate construction carbon emissions.13,14,15,16,17,18,19 Since the material consumption data can be only acquired from 2004 to 2021, most research focused on the construction carbon emissions during the period. The input-output analysis captures upstream supply chain emissions through transforming economic flows between industries into emission flows.20,21,22 The input-output analysis requires input-output tables with intensive data on interrelationships between industries, which are generally published every 5 years in China such as 2002, 2007, and 2017. However, most studies assume that reported statistics are accurate without verification. The outliers among statistics receive little attention in construction industry research, though such issues could substantially affect emission estimates.

Data quality issues in statistical systems can be addressed through data fusion approaches that leverage multiple auxiliary data sources for outlier detection and correction.23 For construction carbon emission estimation, the emission factor method is more amenable to such validation than input-output analysis. The intensive interrelationships and lack of time-series data make outlier identification in input-output tables quite challenging. In contrast, material consumption data can be cross-validated against correlated indicators such as construction output and construction area, which maintain relatively stable relationships with material demand.

Robust regression techniques, particularly Huber M-estimators, offer resistance to outlier influence when modeling relationships between target variables and auxiliary indicators.24 By assigning reduced weights to observations with large residuals, these estimators limit the leverage of extreme values on coefficient estimates and enable reliable prediction even when data contain substantial contamination.25 Detected outliers confirmed through multiple indicators can then be replaced with predicted values derived from models trained on non-outlier observations. Such data fusion methods have been applied in various areas such as ship fuel consumption estimates,26 real estate valuation,27 structural health monitoring,28 and methane emissions monitoring.29 Yet these methods have not been applied to construction material statistics.

Therefore, this study aims to improve carbon emission estimates in China’s construction industry during 2004–2021. Specifically, the study first identifies and corrects the outliers in provincial construction material consumption data through data fusion. Then, the study calculates the impact of outlier correction on construction carbon emission estimates for China. Finally, the study evaluates the emission characteristics of construction carbon emissions from both national and provincial perspectives. The data fusion approach provides a replicable methodology for enhancing data quality in carbon emission accounting, which is rather important for carbon emission analysis and policy formulation.

Results

Data fusion identifies 395 outliers with high model accuracy

Positive outliers dominate across five construction materials

Figure 1 shows the outliers among construction material data across 30 provinces and by material from 2004 to 2021. 395 outliers were identified across five construction materials, including 294 positive outliers (74%, where original values exceed predictions) and 101 negative outliers (26%, where original values fall below predictions). Heilongjiang, Jilin, and Tianjin had the largest numbers of outliers overall with the numbers of 24, 23, and 21, respectively. Steel showed the highest number of corrections (90 observations), followed by glass (79), aluminum (78), cement (75), and wood (73). As for steel consumption, the numbers of outliers in Beijing and Shaanxi were the largest two, both of which were 6. Heilongjiang and Guizhou had the largest two numbers of outliers in wood consumption. Jilin, Hebei, and Guangdong had the top three numbers of outliers in cement consumption (7, 5, and 4, respectively). Tianjin and Inner Mongolia exhibited the top two numbers of corrections in glass consumption (7 and 6, respectively). As for aluminum, Jilin, Heilongjiang, and Jiangsu had the largest three numbers of outliers (6, 5, and 5, respectively).

Huber regression achieves strong predictive performance for most provinces

Figure 2 displays the R2 values of Huber robust regression models by province and material. The Huber robust regressions demonstrate strong predictive performance across all materials. Training R2 values range from 0.00 to 1.00 (median 0.89), with material-specific means of 0.90 for steel, 0.88 for wood, 0.86 for cement, 0.74 for glass, and 0.79 for aluminum. These R2 values indicate that auxiliary indicators (construction area and construction output at constant prices) effectively predict material consumption patterns for most provinces. Only 4 out of 143 province-material combinations (2.8%) fell below the R2 threshold of 0.30 and required fallback to polynomial time trends, specifically cement (R2 = 0.25) and glass (R2 = 0.00) in Shanghai, glass in Beijing (R2 = 0.11), and aluminum in Tianjin (R2 = 0.27).

Data quality issues cause 12.9% cumulative overestimation and mask emission growth

Corrected data reveal growth deceleration during 2019–2021 instead of a false 2012 peak

Figure 4 presents the original and corrected total carbon emissions and intensity in China’s construction industry during 2004–2021. The corrected cumulative emissions totaled 49,857 Mt CO2, representing a 12.9% reduction from the original estimate of 57,253 Mt, a discrepancy of 7,396 Mt. 2011–2014 and 2019 were identified as the years with significant adjustments exceeding 15%. The 2012 correction was most extreme, with emissions reducing from 5,337 to 2,948 Mt, followed by 2011 from 3,872 to 2,553 Mt, 2013 from 4,182 to 3,192 Mt, 2019 from 5,276 to 4,231 Mt, and 2014 from 4,487 to 3,674 Mt. The corrected data showed that emissions experienced growth deceleration during 2019–2021, with the highest observed value of 4,380 Mt in 2021. During 2004–2014, emissions increased from 817 to 3,674 Mt, representing a 350% increase over the decade with an average annual growth rate of 16.2%. From 2015 onward, emissions continued to rise from 3,582 to 4,380 Mt in 2021, with an average annual growth rate of 3.4%.

Annual carbon emissions (top) and emission intensity in Mt CO2 per billion yuan at 2012 constant prices (bottom) comparing original (red) and corrected (blue) estimates from 2004 to 2021.

Original data indicated a 17.7% decline in emission intensity (defined as carbon emissions per unit of construction output value at 2012 constant prices; see Equation 10) from 2004 to 2021, decreasing from 0.0234 to 0.0193 Mt CO2/billion yuan in constant 2012 prices. Corrected data revealed a 10.2% reduction, from 0.0205 to 0.0184 Mt CO2/billion yuan. The most substantial intensity corrections also occurred in 2011–2014 and in 2019, with the same correction rates as the emissions. This corresponds to an annual average decline rate of 0.6% in corrected data compared to 1.1% in original data. The corrected emission intensity patterns similarly divided into two phases. During 2004–2014, intensity fluctuated considerably, declining from 0.0205 Mt CO2 per billion yuan in 2004 to 0.0197 in 2010 before experiencing volatility during 2011–2014, ultimately reaching 0.0210 in 2014. The 2015–2021 period demonstrated markedly different behavior, with intensity first declining to 0.0193 in 2015, further to 0.0185 in 2016, and subsequently stabilizing between 0.0184 and 0.0203 through 2021, showing no sustained downward trend.

Discussion

Summing the annual differences between original and corrected estimates, the outliers caused a 12.9% overestimation in cumulative construction emissions (7,396 Mt discrepancy), with corrections heavily concentrated during 2011–2014 and in 2019. The 2012 corrections were most extreme, reducing total construction carbon emissions from 5,337 to 2,948 Mt, a reduction of 2,389 Mt or 44.8% of the original value, followed by 2011 (1,319 Mt, 34.1%), 2013 (990 Mt, 23.7%), 2019 (1,045 Mt, 19.8%), and 2014 (813 Mt, 18.1%). The corrected emissions in 2012 are very close to those of Chen et al.,30 who found that the construction carbon emissions reached 2,945 Mt using the input-output method with different data. The 2017 estimates are also comparable, with 3,888 Mt in this study and 3,763 Mt in their study. Although Ni et al.14 applied construction material recycling coefficients that would reduce emission estimates, their results still exceeded the corrected values. This study does not incorporate recycling coefficients due to insufficient empirical evidence supporting their values. Corresponding to the emissions, the intensity was extremely anomalous during 2011–2014 and in 2019. The correction of the apparent 2012 peak indicates that post-2012 declines in original data reflect statistical errors rather than actual emission reductions, with direct implications for policy evaluation and emission forecasting.

The corrections in these significant years were further decomposed into provincial and provincial-material contributions. Taking 2011 and 2012 as examples, as shown in Figure 10, Jiangsu and Hebei dominated the 2011 reduction, accounting for 651 (49.4%) and 446 Mt (33.8%), respectively, while Jilin, Hebei, Jiangsu, Shandong, and Sichuan contributed 980 (40.9%), 432 (18.1%), 259 (10.8%), 249 (10.4%), and 179 Mt (7.5%), respectively, of the reduction in 2012. For the 2011 correction, cement dominated the reduction in both provinces, accounting for 71.5% of the Jiangsu reduction and 100.0% of the Hebei reduction, with steel (18.2%) and aluminum (9.6%) also contributing in Jiangsu. For the 2012 correction, cement dominated in Jilin (95.3%) and Hebei (99.6%), while aluminum led the Jiangsu reduction (64.2%) followed by steel (34.4%). Shandong showed contributions from steel (41.4%), cement (38.6%), and aluminum (18.1%), and Sichuan was almost entirely driven by steel (98.9%). These considerable impacts stem from outliers in provincial construction material consumption data. For example, cement emissions in Jiangsu and Hebei in 2011 were corrected substantially from 619 to 153 Mt and from 484 to 37 Mt, respectively. Therefore, greater attention should be given to improving provincial construction material consumption statistics.

Several identified outliers can be traced to known data quality events. In particular, the outliers observed in northeastern provinces during 2011–2014 reflect a period of widespread statistical data inflation in this region. Official investigations revealed that multiple provinces systematically inflated economic statistics during this period, with investment data containing at least 20% exaggeration in some provinces,31 and the Liaoning provincial government formally acknowledged fiscal data falsification for 2011–2014.32 The clustering of extreme outliers in these provinces and years thus provides independent quantitative corroboration of the documented statistical inflation.

During 2004–2014, emissions surged from 817 to 3,674 Mt (16.2% annual growth). The 2015–2021 period showed continued growth to 4,380 Mt in 2021 but at reduced pace (3.4% annually). The 2004–2014 surge coincided with unprecedented urbanization velocity, massive infrastructure construction under stimulus policies, and real estate market expansion. The post-2015 slowdown was because of economic “new normal” transition emphasizing quality over quantity, urbanization rate deceleration, and real estate market cooling policies. Especially between 2019 and 2021, the annual growth rates were 1.1%, 0.9%, and 2.6%, respectively. While the growth rates during 2019–2020 were notably low, the rebound to 2.6% in 2021 indicates that it remains uncertain whether emissions have peaked. Nevertheless, excessive construction that causes serious vacant housing may constrain future construction demand.33,34 The housing vacancy rate in China may have exceeded 30% since 2021.35

The emission intensity in the construction industry decreased from 0.0205 to 0.0184 Mt CO2/billion yuan between 2004 and 2021, with an annual average decline rate of 0.6% and a total decline rate of 10.2%. It should be noted that the decline in carbon emission intensity results from technological advances that reduce material consumption per unit of construction and lower the carbon intensity of building materials, as well as demand structure shifts toward construction types requiring less emission-intensive material inputs.36,37 The intensity decline between 2004 and 2021 likely reflects technological advances in production rather than shifts in material demand. Despite higher quality standards and more high-rise construction in 2021, both of which increase material use per building, intensity still fell. Yet the decline remained smaller than the rise in total emissions, suggesting continued reliance on carbon-intensive materials and limited uptake of low-carbon alternatives. Further reductions will depend on both developing cleaner technologies and creating incentives for their adoption.

Between 2004 and 2021, cement share dropped from 58.4% to 43.0% over the study period, while steel rose from 25.0% to 33.6%. This shift may be because of China’s urban transition from low-rise to high-rise development. High-rise structures demand greater steel for load-bearing capacity, whereas optimized engineering can lower concrete use per unit floor area.38 Such compositional change poses challenges for decarbonization given that steel production emits more CO2 per ton than cement.39,40 Without parallel advances in steelmaking technology, this trend risks negating emission reductions achieved elsewhere. Therefore, more efforts should be made to decrease the emission intensity of steel production.

Using median values as thresholds (93 Mt for emissions, 0.0158 Mt/billion yuan for intensity), provinces fall into four groups in 2021, as shown in Figure 11. Eleven provinces including Fujian, Zhejiang, Sichuan, and Hubei exhibited both high emissions and high intensity, together accounting for 63.7% of national construction emissions. These provinces should be priorities for mitigation efforts, as reducing their intensity could yield substantial emission reductions. Fujian stands out with both the highest emissions (498 Mt) and highest intensity (0.0377), indicating significant room for efficiency improvement. Four provinces—Jiangsu, Henan, Shandong, and Chongqing—achieved high output with relatively low intensity. Jiangsu emitted 470 Mt but maintained intensity at only 0.0155. As mentioned earlier, the low intensity may be because Jiangsu used material more efficiently and had a relatively low-carbon construction demand structure. These provinces demonstrate that high construction activity need not imply high intensity, and their practices may offer lessons for Q1 provinces. Four provinces—Gansu, Hainan, Ningxia, and Shanxi—showed low emissions but relatively high intensity. Their small construction sectors limit total emissions despite inefficient material use. Eleven provinces including Beijing, Shanghai, and Liaoning achieved both low emissions and low intensity. Beijing’s intensity of 0.0072 was the lowest nationally, likely reflecting its mature urban landscape dominated by renovation rather than new construction.

Finally, material composition exhibited substantial regional variation. In 2021, cement dominated in twenty-three provinces, steel in six (Hebei, Shanghai, Beijing, Shaanxi, Shandong, and Hainan), and aluminum only in Guizhou (40.4%). The high contribution by steel in Hebei and Shandong may be because they rank first and third in China’s steel production, and local availability makes steel a natural choice for builders. Steel’s high contributions in Shanghai and Beijing are probably because tall buildings that need steel frames are the common construction style in the two regions. Guizhou’s aluminum dominance is likely due to its rich bauxite reserves. The province ranks third nationally in bauxite reserves.41 This resource endowment supports local aluminum smelting capacity and ensures a convenient supply of aluminum products for the construction industry. This profile is further amplified by the interaction between electricity-intensive aluminum smelting42 and Guizhou’s thermal-power-reliant electricity generation mix.43 Such disparities among provincial material structure imply that province-specific measures instead of national measures should be taken to mitigate the construction carbon emissions. For example, Hebei builders might respond better to incentives for low-carbon steel. Cement-heavy provinces could gain more from promoting alternative binders. It should be noted that steel share generally increased in all provinces except Shanxi, Inner Mongolia, Ningxia, and Liaoning, while cement share decreased in all provinces except Hainan, Shanxi, and Gansu. More and more attention should be paid to steel in most provinces.

The robustness of the data fusion approach was validated through multiple tests. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the robustness of the outlier detection results to the Z score threshold choice. Four thresholds (3.0, 3.5, 4.0, and 4.5) were tested. The threshold of 4.0 was selected to balance detection power against false-positive risk (Table S1). Leave-one-out cross-validation was conducted for province-material combinations with multiple outliers. The correction direction was perfectly consistent (100%), and the median relative deviation between leave-one-out and full-model predictions was only 0.44%, indicating that the correction procedure is robust. For the four province-material combinations requiring quadratic time trend modeling (Shanghai-cement, Shanghai-glass, Beijing-glass, and Tianjin-aluminum), the aggregate corrections accounted for only 1.0% of the total national correction, indicating that methodological choices for these edge cases have negligible impact on main findings.

Regarding the peak emission year, the sensitivity analysis revealed that the corrected peak occurred in 2021 under all tested Z score thresholds (3.0, 3.5, 4.0, and 4.5) (Table S1). The consistent 2021 peak across thresholds reflects growth deceleration during 2019–2021, where annual growth rates ranged from 0.9% to 2.6%. Therefore, rather than identifying a single definitive peak year, the evidence more robustly supports the conclusion that construction emissions entered a growth deceleration phase during 2019–2021, with the highest observed value of 4,380 Mt in 2021. Whether this deceleration represents a true emission peak will depend on post-2021 trends driven by housing market dynamics and decarbonization policies.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, emission factors for fossil fuels, electricity, and construction materials were assumed uniform across all provinces and over time, without accounting for regional differences in material sources, production technologies, or the ongoing decarbonization of the electricity grid. As China’s electricity carbon footprint has decreased substantially,44,45 future research could incorporate province-specific and time-varying emission factors when such data become available. Second, material recycling was not considered in the emission calculations due to the lack of reliable recycling coefficients. Subsequent studies could integrate material recycling once robust coefficient values are established. Third, the study used the Regional GDP Deflator for 2020–2021 rather than the Construction Price Index to deflate the construction output due to data unavailability. Future studies can address these data issues when such data are available. Fourth, the root causes of individual outliers cannot always be definitively traced due to limited access to sub-provincial reporting records. Future research could collaborate with provincial statistical bureaus for targeted traceability analysis.

This study improved construction carbon emission estimates in China from 2004 to 2021 by integrating a data fusion-based outlier detection and correction approach with the emission factor method. Across 30 provinces, 395 outliers were identified, and these errors inflated reported emissions by a cumulative 7,396 Mt, a 12.9% overestimation concentrated in 2011–2014 and 2019. Correcting these outliers removed an apparent 2012 peak that proved to be a data artifact. The corrected data instead show emissions rising steadily from 817 Mt in 2004 to a highest observed value of 4,380 Mt in 2021. Growth was rapid through 2004–2014 at 16.2% per year before easing to 3.4% over 2015–2021 and decelerating further toward the end of the period. Cement and steel remained the leading emission sources. The cement share fell from 58.4% to 43.0%, while the steel share rose from 25.0% to 33.6%. Emission intensity in 2021 also differed 5.2-fold among provinces. Such regional and material heterogeneity indicates that province-specific rather than uniform mitigation measures are warranted. Beyond these findings, the data fusion approach can be applied to other sectors or countries facing similar data quality problems, as long as reliable auxiliary indicators are available. The improved estimates give policymakers a sounder empirical basis for emission accounting and mitigation planning.

Data fusion outlier detection and correction

The study implemented covariate consistency testing and ratio stability analysis to detect outliers in construction material consumption data. The covariate consistency testing employed Huber robust regression24 to model the logarithmic relationship between material consumption and auxiliary indicators. The Huber M-estimator was implemented using the HuberRegressor class from scikit-learn (version 1.7.2) in Python 3.13, with the tuning parameter ε = 1.35, a maximum of 200 iterations, and convergence tolerance of 10-5.

Two auxiliary indicators, namely construction area and construction output, were tested independently through the above steps. An observation is confirmed as outlier only when both indicators concur (dual-confirmation rule). This dual-confirmation requirement reduces false positive risk while capturing genuine outliers corroborated by multiple predictors.

The study employed a complete replacement strategy instead of conventional approaches that partially retain outliers through weighted averaging. This is justified because observations passing these stringent criteria are extremely unreliable. Furthermore, complete replacement avoids arbitrary weight specification while ensuring all corrected values derive from a consistent modeling framework trained on clean data.

Data collection and processing

The study collected the fossil fuel energy and electricity consumption from the Carbon Emission Accounts and Datasets (CEADs)50,51 and the construction material consumption from the China Construction Industry Statistical Yearbook.52 The emission factors for various fossil fuel energy were obtained from Liu et al.46 The emission factors for construction materials (cement, glass, steel, and aluminum) were obtained from the Standard for Building Carbon Emission Calculation,47 and the emission factor for wood was sourced from the China Product Life Cycle Greenhouse Gas Emission Factor Database.48 Provincial electricity emission factors were obtained from the Ministry of Ecology and Environment of China and National Bureau of Statistics of China.49 Two auxiliary indicators, namely construction area and construction output, were collected from the China Statistical Yearbook.53 Construction area refers to the total floor area of buildings under construction during the reporting period, measured in square meters.

To enable temporal and spatial comparisons, construction output was converted to 2012 constant prices. For construction output value, the study employed the Construction and Installation Engineering Fixed Asset Investment Price Index for 2004-201952 and the Regional GDP Deflator for 2020-2021 due to index discontinuity. The GDP Deflator was derived from nominal GDP and the GDP index (previous year = 100) published in the China Statistical Yearbook.53 The Tibet data were excluded due to incomplete data coverage.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using Python 3.13. Huber robust regression was used for outlier detection with a Z-score threshold of 4.0, requiring confirmation from both auxiliary indicators. Model fit was assessed using R2, with a minimum threshold of 0.30. n = 30 provinces × 18 years × 5 materials. Statistical details including number of outliers, R2 values, and correction magnitudes are reported in the results section and figure legends.
