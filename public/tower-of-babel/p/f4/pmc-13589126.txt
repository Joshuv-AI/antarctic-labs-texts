When dying worms look alike: Convergent pathology may create mechanistic illusion in plant-derived anthelmintic research
Authors: Natthawut Charoenphon, Schawanya K Rattanapitoon, Phornphitcha Pechdee, Nathkapach K Rattanapitoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe recent study by Devi et al. provided compelling ultrastructural and histological evidence of severe damage in Ascaridia galli following exposure to Meyna laxiflora seed extract, supporting its anthelmintic potential. However, the interpretation of such morphological alterations warrants further consideration. Similar cuticular and internal lesions have been reported in parasites exposed to chemically unrelated compounds with distinct molecular targets, suggesting that comparable structural phenotypes may represent convergent pathological endpoints rather than mechanistic signatures. This raises the possibility that morphology-based interpretations could inadvertently create a false mechanistic consensus in plant-derived anthelmintic research. Histopathology and scanning electron microscopy are powerful tools for demonstrating efficacy, but they may not reveal the initiating biological events underlying parasite death. Distinguishing convergent pathology from shared pharmacology is therefore essential for the rational development of botanical anthelmintics. Future investigations should focus on mechanism-oriented approaches capable of identifying upstream molecular and physiological disturbances preceding structural collapse. Understanding why dying worms look alike may prove as important as discovering new compounds capable of killing them.

Dear Editor,

We read with considerable interest the recent article by Devi et al. (2026), which demonstrated marked ultrastructural and histological alterations in Ascaridia galli following in vitro exposure to Meyna laxiflora seed extract. Their study provides compelling evidence of anthelmintic activity and adds to the growing interest in ethnoveterinary alternatives in response to concerns regarding drug resistance. Particularly noteworthy is the detailed visualization of cuticular disruption, degeneration of reproductive structures, and distortion of intrauterine eggs, which collectively highlight the profound susceptibility of the parasite to the extract.

Beyond these observations, however, the study raises a broader question regarding how morphological evidence is interpreted in plant-derived anthelmintic research. Severe surface destruction is often regarded as indirect support for a specific mechanism of action. Yet parasites exposed to chemically unrelated compounds frequently exhibit remarkably similar ultrastructural phenotypes. Wrinkling, erosion, blebbing, and disorganization of the cuticle have been reported after exposure to diverse phytochemicals and conventional anthelmintics with fundamentally different pharmacological targets (Challam et al., 2010). Such similarities suggest that morphological deterioration may represent a convergent pathological endpoint rather than a mechanistic fingerprint.

This distinction deserves attention because phenotypic convergence may inadvertently create mechanistic illusion. Distinct upstream disturbances—including neuromuscular dysfunction, oxidative imbalance, mitochondrial impairment, membrane destabilization, or metabolic collapse—can ultimately culminate in comparable structural manifestations. Consequently, worms dying through different molecular pathways may appear strikingly alike under scanning electron microscopy. Similar pathology, therefore, should not be interpreted as evidence of similar pharmacology.

The implications extend beyond the study by Devi et al. Morphological descriptions have become a common component of phytotherapeutic investigations and are frequently invoked to infer mode of action. If convergent pathological phenotypes are mistaken for mechanistic signatures, a false consensus may emerge regarding how plant-derived compounds exert their effects. Such assumptions could obscure distinct molecular targets and hinder the rational prioritization of promising candidates for further development.

Importantly, this perspective does not diminish the significance of the findings reported by Devi et al. Their work convincingly demonstrates that Meyna laxiflora seed extract damages A. galli. The unresolved question concerns what initiates the observed destruction. Histopathology and scanning electron microscopy reveal where injury becomes visible, but not necessarily where it begins. Resolving this distinction requires approaches capable of capturing earlier biological events preceding structural collapse.

As interest in botanical anthelmintics continues to grow, perhaps the next transition should be conceptual rather than descriptive. Instead of asking whether different compounds produce similar lesions, attention may need to shift toward whether similar lesions conceal fundamentally different mechanisms. Understanding why dying worms look alike may prove just as important as discovering new ways to kill them.
