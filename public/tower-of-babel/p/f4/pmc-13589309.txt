Neural evolution of complex motor behaviors: insights from Drosophila courtship song
Authors: Yun Ding, Joshua L. Lillvis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The remarkable complexity and diversity of acoustic signals used in social interactions across animal species offers a unique window into the neural basis of behavioral evolution. Among these, the Drosophila courtship song serves as a powerful system for investigating how context-dependent complex motor patterns are encoded and evolve over time. With the aid of advanced tools—including cell-type-specific manipulation and connectomics—researchers are beginning to uncover the circuit architecture responsible for song production. Comparative analyses of homologous neurons across species are further revealing how specific components of the song circuit contribute to the diversification of acoustic signals. In this review, we synthesize recent comparative studies in Drosophila with current knowledge of the courtship song circuit to discuss where and how neural circuits may have evolved to generate species-specific motor patterns and to highlight circuit principles and features that may underlie or shape the evolution of complex motor behaviors.

Introduction

Animals exhibit a staggering diversity of motor repertoires. These behaviors―from grooming, walking, swimming, and digging to highly elaborate courtship displays―are orchestrated by neural circuits that integrate sensory inputs and internal states to produce coordinated motor outputs. Acoustic signals used in social and sexual communication offer an especially rich framework for understanding how these neural computations are modified over evolution to generate diversity and novelty in complex motor patterns. These signals rely on intricately patterned motor actions that are inherently context-dependent, highly species-specific, often sexually dimorphic, and amenable to precise recording and quantification. Comparative systems―such as calls and songs in insects, fishes, birds, frogs, and mice―have begun to uncover the neural mechanisms underlying behavioral diversity and innovation [1]. A central question is whether there are principles as to where and how nervous systems evolve to generate behavioral diversity. Are particular circuit nodes (e.g., sensory, premotor, motor) or neural properties (e.g., intrinsic physiology, connectivity, neuromodulation) more amenable to evolutionary change? Here, we will discuss insights into these questions from recent work on the neural basis and evolution of Drosophila courtship song.

Drosophila species vibrate their wings to generate a variety of acoustic signals in different social contexts including courtship, aggression, sexual rejection, and copulation, with the male courtship song being the most extensively characterized acoustic signal [2]. In Drosophila melanogaster (D. melanogaster), the song consists of two key components―pulse song, made up of discrete wing beats [3], and sine song, defined by acoustic waveforms composed of continuous oscillations [4] (Fig. 1). These song signals are characterized by acoustic parameters such as carrier frequency, amplitude, cyclicity of pulse, and interpulse interval (IPI) [5]. Importantly, courtship song is not a fixed action pattern but a dynamically patterned motor output, with both song types and parameters modulated by ongoing sensory cues and internal states [6–9]. Thanks to a suite of resources, such as rich cell-type-specific genetic tools and most recently, whole central nervous system connectomes (e.g., Refs. [10–13]), the underlying neural circuits―from sensory perception to motor execution―are increasingly well dissected in the model species D. melanogaster (e.g., Refs. [14–19]).

Across the Drosophila genus, courtship song is ubiquitous yet remarkably species-specific in spectrotemporal patterns, exhibiting quantitative changes of song parameters as well as gains and losses of discrete song types (Fig. 1). These species-specific signals are thought to function both as indicators of male quality―shaping female mate choice―and as cues of species identity―contributing to reproductive isolation [20]. The remarkable diversity of male courtship songs across species has long been a major subject of quantitative genetics for dissecting the genetic basis of behavioral evolution [21]. Fueled by recent advances that enable labeling and comparing homologous neurons across species (See Table 1 for a summary of species comparisons of neurons in Drosophila courtship circuits), Drosophila species also emerged as a powerful model for investigating the neural basis of behavioral evolution [22]. The courtship song system thus provides a comprehensive model to study how complex behaviors are generated, coordinated, and modified through evolution at multiple levels of neural processing over a relatively well-defined 60-million-year phylogeny, enabling the identification of convergent and divergent features across different evolutionary timescales.

Neural circuit locus of courtship song evolution

Drosophila courtship behaviors, including song, are largely encoded by a few thousand neurons that express the sex-determination genes fruitless (fru) and/or doublesex (dsx) [28,29]. In the brain, these neurons integrate visual, olfactory, auditory, and gustatory cues, along with internal state, to guide moment-to-moment decisions in male flies about whether and how to court. The resulting commands are relayed to the ventral nerve cord (VNC)―the fly’s spinal cord analog―via descending neurons (DNs). These descending inputs, together with local sensory inputs, drive specific courtship actions patterned in the premotor and motor circuits of the VNC. In theory, changes to a variety of nodes in the courtship circuit may underlie the diversity of song behaviors that have been observed [30]. Here, we will walk through this circuitry (Fig. 2A), downstream of the sensory circuits whose evolution has been intensively investigated and summarized in Drosophila [22], to explore emerging circuit principles and features that shape and inform the neural circuit locus of courtship song evolution.

Decision-making circuits

A handful cell types integral to the decisions of whether and how to sing have been described [19,31–36]. The most prominent of these are the dsx- and fru-expressing P1 neurons, which are activated by female sensory cues to drive male courtship behaviors such as chasing and courtship song [32,35,37]. P1 neurons are a heterogeneous group of neurons that are identifiable across distantly related Drosophila species [26,38] and functional comparisons across multiple species within the D. melanogaster species group suggest a conserved role of P1 neurons in promoting or gating a courtship state [24]. Notably, sensory signals that feed into P1 neurons, such as sexual pheromones, are rapidly evolving across species [39]. Consequently, the perception and/or processing of these signals must have co-evolved so that P1 activity encodes a conserved arousal state towards conspecific females. Rigorous comparisons of pheromone circuits between species reveal that evolutionary changes in both sensory and central circuits contribute to these adaptations [24,25].

The precise decisions to produce distinct courtship actions, such as chasing, song, licking, and copulation attempts, appear to be encoded largely by neurons downstream of P1 [28,30,40]. Given a seemingly conserved function of P1 neurons in promoting an elevated courtship state, evolutionary changes to the output of P1 neurons may play a significant role in the diversification of courtship actions generated across species. For example, a recent study has demonstrated that, in D. subobscura males, the novel nuptial gift giving behavior of regurgitated crop droplets during courtship emerges from the recruitment of regurgitation-promoting insulin cells downstream of P1 neurons into the fru-expressing courtship circuit [26,38]. For male courtship song, the decision to sing is made by a pair of fru-expressing DNs named pIP10 that are also downstream of P1 and directly contact the premotor circuits of song in the VNC [15,16,19,32] (Fig. 2A). The pIP10 neurons are active during pulse and sine songs, and their activity is necessary and sufficient to produce both song types [15,16,19]. Notably, D. subobscura represents a rare case where males do not sing a courtship song [41]. The presence, connectivity, and function of pIP10 and other downstream song-related neurons remain unknown in this species.

Males dynamically modulate song type and song parameters depending on the social context [6–9]. In D. melanogaster, males are more likely to sing the louder pulse song, whose amplitude positively correlates with the distance to females, during chasing, whereas the quieter sine song is more common when the female is close and relatively still [6,7]. This choice of song type is encoded, at least in part, by DNs tuned to different social contexts: the fru-expressing pMP2 DNs contribute to specifying whether to sing pulse, as their activity is required, sufficient, and selective for generating pulse song [15,16]. That is, both pIP10 and pMP2 are active during pulse song production (Fig. 2A). While the DNs for sine song remain unknown, connectome data has revealed multiple candidates that may provide excitatory signals onto the downstream sine-generating circuit, suggesting that coincident activity of these DNs and pIP10 may generate sine song [15] (Fig. 2A). An alternative model suggests that the transition from pulse song to a more complex song of both pulse and sine, is driven by the inhibition of inhibitory DNs that regulate a mutually inhibitory pulse/sine rebound circuit in the VNC [19]. Regardless of the specific mechanisms that regulate the timing of pulse and sine production in D. melanogaster, differential DN activity is a critical element. Additionally, multiple DNs directly connect to VNC song neurons with functions in patterning song parameters [15], suggesting possible roles of differential DN activity in modulating song features such as pulse amplitude and carrier frequency, which vary in response to female behavior during courtship [7,8]. These DN inputs onto the VNC song circuit may encode species differences in the temporal organization and context-dependent modulation of song.

Evolutionary insights from comparing DNs across species

Given that the production of different song types within a species involves distinct DN activity, do species differences in song types produced reflect species differences in DN activity? A comparison between D. melanogaster and D. yakuba, two closely related species that sing distinct song types, offers some clues [9]. Unlike D. melanogaster, which produce pulse and sine songs, D. yakuba has lost sine song and instead sings two pulse song types: the conventional pulse song, generated via unilateral wing extension and vibration, and a second type called clack, which is generated by bilateral wing vibrations without major wing extension (Fig. 1). Surprisingly, at the behavioral level, the association between song type produced and social context has diverged [9]. Whereas D. melanogaster produces more pulse when far from a female and more sine when close, D. yakuba produces more clack when far from a female and more pulse when close (Fig. 3A). At the circuit level, this “mismatch” aligns with DN activity: optogenetic activation of pIP10 neurons at a lower level is more likely to drive pulse in D. melanogaster and clack in D. yakuba, whereas pIP10 activation at a higher level produces more sine song in D. melanogaster and more pulse song in D. yakuba. Thus, pIP10 appears to be activated in a similar manner in a similar social context between species, but the resulting song produced has diverged (Fig. 3A). Consistent with this framework, hybrid males of D. yakuba and its closest outgroup species that sing pulse and sine songs, D. teissieri, produce hybrid songs that are chimeras of the different song types that each species generates in a similar social context [27]. One hybrid song resembles a blend of D. yakuba clack song and D. teissieri pulse song (produced when far from a female), and the other resembles tightly time-locked production of D. yakuba pulse song and D. teissieri sine song (produced when close to a female) (Fig. 3B). Although it is unclear what role pMP2 and other DNs play in clack song generation, these results indicate that similar social context-dependent decision-making processes lead to distinct song types between species. Thus, in this case, downstream motor patterning circuits and their interfaces with DNs are the candidate neural loci of evolutionary changes that encode species differences in song types and song parameters.

As we will discuss premotor circuits separately below, it is worth noting here that the interfaces between upstream context-coding circuits and downstream premotor circuits may be particularly important nodes for evolutionary change [42]. In singing mice, the evolution of loud, highly structured songs likely involves the redeployment of an ancestral motor program―originally used for soft, unstructured ultrasonic vocalizations―into a new social context [43]. A key circuit innovation appears to be the expanded cortical projections to the midbrain hub for vocal initiation and modulation, and to the auditory cortex, which may underlie the auditory-motor coupling required for this context-sensitive turn-taking behavior [44,45]. In D. melanogaster, connectome data show that decision-making DNs are directly and strongly connected to key premotor circuit elements that are sufficient and necessary to drive specific song types [15]. In theory, evolutionary modifications at this interface may readily shift the social context in which a given song type is produced or may co-opt existing motor programs to new social contexts to generate novel song types.

Premotor circuits

Across invertebrates and vertebrate systems, premotor central pattern generators (CPGs), such as the hindbrain vocalization circuits in frogs, generate species-specific rhythmic motor patterns [46–48]. Evolutionary changes in the structure or modulation of CPGs are likely to contribute to the diversification of motor outputs across species [46]. In D. melanogaster, evidence from the connectome, functional imaging, and neurogenetic manipulation has identified a core premotor courtship song circuit composed of highly interconnected, sexually dimorphic neurons expressing fru and/or dsx [15,16]. This circuit is largely independent of the premotor flight circuit, but both circuits share an overlapping set of wing motor neurons and muscles [10,11,15,16,49] (Fig. 2B). The courtship song premotor circuit may also be separable, at least to a degree, from the circuits that pattern male-specific agonistic song in sexual competition [14]. This modular organization may facilitate the evolvability of premotor circuits by minimizing pleiotropic constraints.

Within the song circuit, the connectivity between pulse circuit and sine circuit is high and structured, with modular subcircuits that have overlapping yet semi-specialized components (Fig. 2a) [15,16]. While many cell types are multifunctional, a cell type is rarely required to generate both song types. Most of the core song neurons are required to produce one song mode, while having secondary roles in patterning the other song type. For example, TN1A and dMS2 neurons, two premotor nodes that are sufficient and necessary to generate sine song, have a phenotype in pulse song amplitude [15,32,50,51]. Such circuit nodes with key song functions and relatively limited pleiotropic effects are likely ideal neural substrates for song evolution. This may explain the recent discovery that the evolutionary loss of sine song in D. yakuba is caused, at least in part, by a loss of TN1A neurons [27]. Notably, silencing all TN1 neurons in D. yakuba does not lead to obvious song phenotypes. Here, the partially modular organization may have enabled sine song to be lost by eliminating key neurons with a primary function in sine song production without a broad circuit overhaul.

The highly interconnected nature of the premotor song circuit suggests the presence of a moderately sized pool of neurons upon which evolutionary modifications can act to yield specific changes in song. For example, IPI has evolved repeatedly across species and may be a critical element of signaling species identity. In D. melanogaster, silencing song neurons during courtship indicates that normal activity of at least three premotor cell types is essential for proper IPI patterning, and seven cell types modulate IPI when activated during song production, making them plausible candidates for IPI diversification across species (Fig. 2A) [15]. This aligns with a typically polygenic architecture underlying natural variation in complex motor behaviors [52]. Furthermore, the complex and highly connected song circuit may harbor latent activity―as suggested by the presence of a latent clack song circuit in D. melanogaster [9]―from which novel motor patterns can emerge [42]. This connectivity may also facilitate the flexible recruitment of circuit nodes, allowing evolutionary changes in song to arise through adjustments in connection strength rather than requiring the generation of entirely new connections.

Finally, an intriguing feature of the song circuit is its nested architecture, where the evolutionarily more recent sine song is generated by a subset of neurons embedded within the larger premotor circuit that produces the ancestral pulse song (Fig. 2a) [15,16]. Interestingly, leech locomotor circuits exhibit a similar organization, with the more recently derived swim network embedded within the ancestral crawling circuit [53]. The nested structure might reflect an evolutionary footprint from the ancient co-option and further specialization of ancestral circuit components to generate new motor behaviors, a potentially common evolutionary strategy for behavioral innovation. This hypothesis can be tested by tracing the presence and function of key sine-generating neurons―such as the TN1A neurons―in outgroup species that diverged prior to the origin of sine song.

Motor circuits

Song, flight, and other wing motor patterns are generated by the coordinated activity of a small number of ancient wing muscles, each of which is typically innervated by a single motor neuron [54]. Unlike the distinct DNs and premotor circuits for courtship song and flight, both behaviors engage an overlapping set of wing motor neurons and muscles [49]. Although manipulating individual motor neurons can lead to various song changes that mimic species differences (Fig. 2a) [17,49,51], any evolutionary changes to the motor neurons and muscles must not disrupt the ability of the animal to fly. This suggests that motor neurons and muscles may be evolutionarily more constrained than the premotor song circuit. Consistent with this, and in contrast with the highly sexually dimorphic premotor circuits, only one pair of wing muscles, hg1, is known to be sexually dimorphic in D. melanogaster [51]. The functional pleiotropy and evolutionary constraints on motor circuits present an interesting comparison with the sensory system, where peripheral neurons, especially chemosensory neurons, are often highly specialized and evolvable [22].

While the constraints on motor circuits suggest that they may not be the primary circuit nodes of song evolution, certain motor neurons and muscles, particularly ones important for song generation, may have evolved to support song evolution. Phylogenetically, the emergence of a larger hg1 muscle in males, which contributes to sine song amplitude, corresponds to the emergence of sine song [51]. Furthermore, an analysis of hg1-hg4 muscles across Drosophila species demonstrated that sexual dimorphisms in hg1–3 have evolved in some species [55].

Mechanisms of neural diversification in courtship song evolution

Evolutionary changes in various neural properties may alter circuit function and behavior. Broadly, these mechanisms can fit into several categories: changes in neuron number, changes in the intrinsic physiology of neurons, changes in connectivity between neurons, and changes in neuromodulation. The loss of TN1A neurons and sine song in D. yakuba provides an example of how a loss of neurons at a key node of the premotor circuit contributes to behavioral evolution [27]. Similarly, losses of lateral and medial giant neurons have occurred in correspondence with the loss of escape behaviors across decapod crustacean species [56].

At the intrinsic neuron physiology level, natural variation in the carrier frequency of sine song has been mapped to an intronic insertion of retrotransposon in a potassium channel gene, potentially via changes in its alternative splicing [57]. Although the exact neural consequence of this variation has not been determined, such a shift is likely to influence the intrinsic physiological properties of neurons. A major hallmark of ion-channel genes is their unusual complexity in alternative splicing [58], which likely offers an important layer of evolvability to these otherwise functionally highly constrained genes. Species differences in frog vocal behaviors have been linked to differences in neuron excitability, plausibly implemented by changes in ion channel composition [47]. Similarly, the production of electric signals from a spinal neuron derived electric organ in one lineage of electric fish is due to potassium channel gene duplication and expression differences [59].

Changes in neuron morphology are likely widespread across species. P1, pIP10, and TN1 neurons, despite being functionally distinct nodes of the song circuit with grossly conserved morphology, all exhibit species differences in specific arbors [9,24,27]. While these changes may be explained by developmental drift [60] rather than functional adaptation―as seen in the swimming circuits of related sea slug species and other systems [61–63]―their prevalence suggests that arbor-specific neuroanatomical changes are not rigidly constrained by either mutational mechanisms or selective processes. In theory, such changes can offer enormous evolvability for neural circuits to establish new connectivity while avoiding broad detrimental effects. While their significance in song evolution awaits further investigation, they have been implicated in other species-specific behavioral shifts and novelties in Drosophila [26,64,65]. For example, the connectivity between P1 neurons and insulin neurons that enables nuptial gift giving in D. subobscura resulted from new arborizations of insulin neurons caused by a gain of fru expression [26].

Finally, fine-grained cell types of dsx-expressing neurons, resolved by single-cell transcriptomics, appear overall very similar across closely related Drosophila species, while changes in their gene expression are pervasive [66]. A particular striking pattern, also recently observed in mice and nematodes [67,68], is the exceptional evolutionary turnover in gene expression of neuropeptide precursors and neuropeptide receptors in a highly cell-type-specific manner. Neuromodulatory genes, including neuropeptide signaling genes, have long been posited as a primary mechanism of behavioral evolution due to their function in coordinating context-dependent flexible behaviors by dynamically enabling circuit reconfiguration [69]. Species-differences in sea slug swimming behavior are linked to differences in serotonergic neuromodulation and serotonin receptor expression at a single cell type [70,71]. The characterization of these neuropeptideric differences across Drosophila species now provides a foundation to address their roles in the regulation and evolution of courtship circuits.

Outlook

As the Drosophila courtship song system begins to shed light on key nodes and adaptive features of neural circuits in behavioral evolution, the neural mechanisms underlying song evolution, and motor behavior evolution more broadly, are likely highly diverse. Despite the pursuit to derive general principles, identified species differences are best understood in the context of the neural architecture and computation relevant for the evolved behavior phenotype. The courtship song system provides an exciting opportunity for this endeavor. A largely untapped key strength of the system is the repeated evolution of song characteristics across phylogeny, offering natural experimental replicates of behavioral evolution that inform the extent and conditions under which evolutionary solutions are constrained and predictable.

Several main questions remain in understanding the circuit mechanisms underlying the evolution of sex-specific motor patterns. To what extent are the underlying circuits―particularly those involved in pattern generation―shared or specialized across different acoustic signals, such as male-specific courtship and agonistic songs as well as sex-shared sexual rejection songs [2]? How often are motor patterns generated in one social context co-opted for use in another during evolution? In addition, despite mounting evidence that core courtship song circuits are primarily male-specific or sexually dimorphic, paradoxically females appear to carry a latent circuit potential to produce male-like courtship behaviors [72,73]. To what extent do females share “homologous” or “paralogous” circuits involved in song generation, and how might evolutionary modifications to these circuits contribute to behavioral evolution, such as female singing during courtship in some species [2,74]? Finally, to what degree is the evolution of male courtship circuits driven by molecular-genetic programs that establish sexual dimorphisms of the nervous system?

Looking forward, recent advances in comparative single-cell transcriptomics (e.g., Refs. [27,66,75]), comparative connectomics (e.g., Refs. [45,63,76,77]), and large-volume functional imaging (e.g., Ref. [78]) are on the horizon to collectively provide a more comprehensive picture of how the wiring and functional configuration of neural circuits differ among species to generate divergent behaviors. The ability to label homologous neurons and cell types to compare their structure, physiological properties, and functions across species is expected to be a time-limiting but critical step for hypothesis testing. These efforts in Drosophila, together with other systems [1,22,52], will contribute to a general understanding of the divergent and evolvable features of neural circuits in the evolution of complex motor behaviors, as well as how behavior- or taxa-specific circuit architectures shape their evolutionary trajectories.
