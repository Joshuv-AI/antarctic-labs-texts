The correlation between intragenerational subjective social mobility and health: a scoping review
Authors: Kiarash Noorizadeh, Hossein Molavi Vardanjani, Jeyran Ostovarfar, Sadra Valiee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHealth and socioeconomic status (SES) are interrelated. Social mobility—a key concept in studying this relationship—is intra-generational mobility, where individuals are compared to their own past SES.MethodThis is a scoping review evaluating the effects of intra-generational social mobility especially downward mobility on health and health behaviors. All English studies released after 1995 that report the effect of social mobility on health or health behaviors were included.ResultMost of the studies evaluate mortality, mental disorders, physical diseases, and health behaviors as a health variable and use occupational class as a factor for determining social mobility. A significant number of studies stated that social mobility affects health and downward mobile people had worse health than stable high groups. However, some studies deny these effects. Several studies have examined the models and theories that show the relationship between health and social mobility including social causation, health selection, accumulation of disadvantages, and etc.ConclusionAmong the studies that investigated the models and theories justifying the relationship between health and social mobility, it seems that the social causation model and the accumulation of disadvantages model have more acceptance than the others.Keywords: Intra-generational mobility, Health, Health-related behaviors, Social causation

Introduction

In recent years, social mobility and its impact on various aspects of people’s lives have become a topic of interest. Social mobility refers not just to shifts in socioeconomic status (SES), but also to the broader social processes through which individuals navigate opportunity structures, constraints, and class boundaries over time [1]. In terms of a certain framework, social mobility refers to the movement of people between different levels of socioeconomic status SES, which is usually measured through objective indicators such as education, occupation, and income [2]. Although subjective measures of SES also exist, they are less commonly used in research on social mobility than objective indicators [3]. An example of a subjective instrument is the MacArthur Subjective Social Status Scale, in which respondents are shown a 10-step ladder, with each step representing a different position in the socioeconomic hierarchy of society (the first step representing the lowest SES level and the tenth step representing the highest). Individuals are asked to choose the step to which they belong based on their perception of their social status [4].

Social mobility, comprising intergenerational and intragenerational dimensions, reflects dynamic SES changes across generations and individual life courses [5]. Intergenerational mobility compares individuals’ SES with their parents’, revealing societal opportunity structures, while intragenerational mobility tracks lifetime SES trajectories, influenced by career progression (typically upward in early adulthood) and retirement transitions (often downward later in life). Crucially, these analyses distinguish absolute mobility (cohort-wide SES changes from economic shifts) from relative mobility (individual mobility compared to peers), with stability indicating unchanged SES position. Both dimensions fundamentally shape health outcomes, as mobility patterns - whether upward, downward, or stable - interact with structural inequalities across the life course [6, 7].

There is a distinguish between origin SES, often based on childhood circumstances like parents’ education or occupation, and destination SES, which reflects an individual’s current or achieved position in adulthood. This distinction matters because health outcomes might relate not just to where someone is now, but also to where they started—or the journey between the two [8, 9]. Mobility itself can be measured in different ways: some studies rely on objective data like occupational changes or income shifts, while others use subjective assessments, such as perceived position on a social ladder [10].

Health, like SES, is a multidimensional construct that can be evaluated through both subjective perceptions (e.g., self-rated health) and objective indicators (e.g., mortality, clinical diagnoses), each capturing distinct aspects of health-related experience and status [11, 12].To investigate the effect of social mobility on health, researchers employ diverse methods. Health outcomes, like SES, can be assessed subjectively (e.g., self-rated health questionnaires) or objectively (e.g., mortality rates, clinical diagnoses of mental or cardiovascular diseases). While subjective tools are practical, they may overlap with biases in self-reported mobility, whereas objective indicators offer more standardized but costly alternatives [13–16]. Health-related behaviors (e.g., alcohol use, diet) also mediate this relationship, often shifting with SES changes [14].

While the health selection and social causation hypotheses offer competing explanations for the relationship between SES and health, a fundamental methodological challenge persists in the study of social mobility and health wwhich is reverse causality. It is often difficult to determine whether socioeconomic status influences health outcomes or whether pre-existing health condition affect an individual’s social mobility trajectory. However, these pathways are context-dependent—economic shocks may restrict mobility opportunities regardless of health, while age-structural factors (e.g., retirement-related income drops) can independently worsen health outcomes [17, 18].

The accumulation model further clarifies how mobility trajectories interact with health over time. Prolonged exposure to low SES—whether due to limited intergenerational mobility or intragenerational declines—compounds biological wear-and-tear (allostatic load) and behavioral risks (e.g., chronic stress, poor diet). Conversely, upward mobility in early adulthood may delay health deterioration, but late-life downward mobility (e.g., job loss) can erase these benefits, highlighting the life-course sensitivity of health-mobility linkages [19–22].

Three core models explain the health-mobility relationship: [1] The Dissociative model posits that all mobility induces stress-related health risks [2], The Directional model links upward mobility to health gains through resource access and downward mobility to losses, while [3] The Acculturation model attributes health outcomes primarily to destination SES rather than mobility itself. These models show stronger predictive validity when using objective mobility measures (e.g., income/occupation trajectories) versus subjective reports [23–25].

In this scoping review, we take stock of both the theoretical and the empirical findings on how social mobility, particularly downward mobility, relates to health. Beyond summarising the field, we explicitly aim to carry forward what we learn here into a qualitative study in Iran. To do so, we identify the key constructs, measurement approaches, and contextual factors that recur across the literature and translate them into interview questions and thematic domains that can capture lived experiences, rather than only statistical associations.

Method

This study is a scoping review. Our research question was: What effects does social mobility, particularly downward mobility, have on health and health-related behaviors? We used PCC framework (population, concept, context) to guide our search strategy. No restrictions were applied to the population- all individuals, regardless of demographic characteristics, were considered eligible. The concept of the study was health and health related behaviors, while the context focused on intra-generational social mobility.

The search strategy was developed with the assistance of an experienced librarian and include keywords such as ‘social mobility’, ‘intra-generational’, ‘socioeconomic*’, ‘health’, and so on. The full search string is presented in Table 1. The search was conducted in four major electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and Web of Science. We limited the search to studies published in English after 1995, because during the mid-1990s, significant economic, technological, and healthcare system transformations occurred globally, reshaping patterns of social mobility and health behaviors. Moreover, methodological advancements in social mobility research, including the increased use of longitudinal data and refined measures of SES and health, became more prominent after this period [26, 27].

Search strategy

We include original studies that reported the effect of intra-generational social mobility on health outcomes or health related behaviors. Exclusion criteria were: (1) studies focusing on inter-generational mobility, (2) studies that did not examine health or health behaviors as outcomes, (3) review articles, and (4) studies that did not report findings related to downward mobility. The screening process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

PRISMA flow diagram

Title and abstract screening were performed independently by two reviewers. Full texts of potentially relevant studies were then reviewed for eligibility. Any discrepancies were discussed and resolved through consensus in team meetings. Data extraction was also conducted independently by two reviewers using a standardized form. A third reviewer cross-checked all extracted data for consistency and accuracy. We implemented several measures to enhance quality assurance. First, calibration exercises were conducted before the formal screening process, where reviewers independently screened a random sample of 20 studies and discussed discrepancies to harmonize interpretation of eligibility criteria. Second, dual independent screening and extraction processes were performed at all stages, followed by regular consensus meetings. Third, a senior researcher supervised the entire review process, providing oversight to ensure methodological consistency and resolve any complex cases. Although a formal risk-of-bias assessment was not performed, these rigorous procedures were applied to maximize the reliability and validity of the findings.

Beyond mapping previous studies, this review lays a clear foundation for the qualitative research that follows. Identifying the key constructs and measurement strategies in the literature enables us to design targeted interview questions and thematic frameworks, ensuring that qualitative insights are closely aligned with the trends and signals highlighted by quantitative findings.

Result

Studies are divided into two general categories based on how social mobility is evaluated. One part of the studies used subjective method for evaluating social class and then social mobility. They asked the participants that if social classes were a hypothetical ladder, which rung of the ladder would you consider yourself to be on? One-step and sometimes two-step movement between these rungs would be considered social mobility. The other part of studies used objective methods for evaluating social classes. The most prevalent determinant for social class was occupational classifications, but few studies used other variables such as income, educational level, or a composition of income, occupation, and educational level [4, 24, 28–33]. Data Summary and Characteristics of Relevant Findings of Reviewed Literature

Studies used different variables for evaluating health condition, but there are three major categories. Mortality, morbidity, and health behaviors. About 30% of studies used mortality for evaluating health. some of them used all-cause mortality and others specified the cause of death like cardiovascular, cancer, and etc [28, 33–40]. The second category was studies that used morbidity as a health variable. We can divide morbidity into psychological and physical conditions. Psychological morbidity like major depressive disorders (more common), mania, and panic disorder [4, 30, 32, 41–45]. physical morbidity like carotid intima-media thickness, acute coronary syndrome, hypertension, and oral health [23, 24, 29–31, 43, 44, 46–50]. Few studies used health behaviors as a determinant of health. Physical activity and smoking cigarette were examples of health behaviors that were used [34, 39, 51, 52].

The studies included in our review differed in how they measured key variables such as age and SES at origin and destination. Some relied on retrospective accounts of parental class to define origin SES, while others used prospective cohort data [53]. In studies where parental class was mentioned, it served only as a baseline reference to locate an individual’s own socioeconomic starting point, allowing researchers to track socioeconomic changes across that person’s own life course. This approach does not reflect intergenerational mobility; rather, it situates intragenerational shifts relative to the socioeconomic conditions from which individuals started. Our review therefore remains focused on within-lifetime mobility patterns, even when parental SES is used as a reference point. Age was typically recorded at baseline or follow-up, but not always adjusted for analytically. In many cases, destination SES was based on current occupation, income, or education, but the criteria for defining mobility were not consistently reported, which may contribute to variation in findings [9, 54].

The results of the effect of social mobility on health have been similar in some areas and somewhat different from each other. Most of the studies which evaluate mortality, stated that downward mobility increases the mortality and mortality rate of downward mobile people would be between their origin and destination group. Some studies stated that there is a significant relationship only in certain circumstances. For example, Uggla et al. in a study in Finland which evaluated both economic growth and recession conditions stated that downward mobility had a relationship with higher mortality risk in economic growth conditions and there is no relationship in an economic recession [28, 33, 34, 36–40].

In the case of psychological disorders, the results are more varied. Some of the studies stated that downward mobile people had a higher risk of psychological problems such as depressive symptoms but others didn’t find a significant association between social mobility and psychological disorders. Although several studies that rejected the relationship between social mobility and mental illnesses have admitted the existence of a relationship between low socioeconomic status and low income with mental illnesses [4, 30, 32, 42–45].

Studies that evaluate physical morbidity also had different results but less than psychological disorders. Most of the studies stated that downward social mobility increases the risk of physical morbidity such as acute coronary syndrome, hypertension, and physical symptoms like gastrointestinal, musculoskeletal, etc. But some studies didn’t find significant associations between these two [23, 29, 31, 43, 47–50]. One study reported a completely different result. Khairuddin et al. in a study used 1970 British birth cohort study, stated that both upward and downward social mobility were inversely associated with poor oral health and support the social maximization theory [24]. Jonsson et al., and Tran et al. stated that when evaluating social mobility as a dichotomous variable (absence vs. presence), there are no significant differences between people who had mobility and immobile people in terms of health condition [23, 33].

Most of the studies that were conducted on health behavior examined inter-generational social mobility, and fewer articles examined the effect of intra-generational social mobility on health behaviors. Some studies stated that upward mobility had negative effects on behaviors like increasing inactivity and cigarette smoking but downward mobility had not such effect. However, other studies found the opposite results. They stated that downward mobile people smoked more and had a higher mortality rate due to smoking-related cancer. These studies found no association between mobility and alcohol-related mortality [34, 39, 51, 52].

Some studies have shown that social mobility can reduce health inequalities in society because mobile people carry some of the characteristics of that social class with them to another class. For instance, Bartley et al. (2007), using longitudinal data from the Office for National Statistics Longitudinal Study, demonstrated that mobility between occupational classes over a decade did not exacerbate health inequalities but instead appeared to mitigate them. Their findings indicate that the persistence of certain protective factors from the class of origin can buffer or dilute health disparities among mobile individuals [39, 46, 55].

Several studies have examined the models and theories that show the relationship between health and social mobility. A significant number of them state that the health selection theory has little effect in explaining this relationship, and the social causation theory seems to have a greater effect. As a result, the social causation theory seems to have more acceptance than the health selection theory [32, 35–37, 41, 44, 56]. However, some believe that the hypothesis of health selection justifies this relationship [34, 40]. The second theory that has more acceptance is the accumulation of disadvantage (or cumulative model). They believed that long-term exposure to disadvantages and low SEP affects health [33, 39, 43, 47, 48]. Some studies support another model. For example, Khairuddin et al. in a study that evaluated self-rated oral health, support the status maximization hypothesis because upward mobile people adapt to their destination class and downward mobile people adapt to their origin class [24]. Houle et al. in a study evaluating psychological distress, support the acculturation model. Because their current class of people had more impact on their health [45]. At the end, some studies didn’t find significant difference between these models and none of them was superior to the other [23, 38].

Discussion

The stress and disorientation hypothesis suggests that people who experience social mobility—especially downward—may face psychological strain, identity conflict, or a loss of social anchors. In this view, it’s not just the SES level that affects health, but the mismatch between where people started and where they find themselves. This tension can lead to stress-related outcomes, particularly when individuals feel alienated from both their origin and destination groups [57]. Although some reviewed studies refer to this model implicitly, very few directly assess its psychological or behavioral mechanisms.

Although many studies draw on frameworks like social causation and health selection, they often fail to clearly distinguish between the effects of origin SES, destination SES, and the process of mobility itself. By definition, social mobility is relational and reflects the shift between origin and destination SES. Methodological approaches such as Diagonal Reference Models (DRMs), introduced in the early 1980 s, have been widely used to disentangle these dimensions, although they have faced critiques for their limited capacity to infer causality [54, 58, 59]. More recent and causally informative approaches, including Luo (2021) and Breen and Ermisch (2024), have been proposed to address these limitations [9, 58]. In this review, we acknowledge these methodological debates and highlight the importance of distinguishing between origin and destination SES while recognizing the limitations of current models in fully capturing the causal effects of mobility on health outcomes.

The relationship between health and social mobility is mutual. In this study, we focused more on the effect of social mobility on health. Significant number of studies found that downward social mobility had negative effects on health (mortality, mental and physical disorders, health behaviors, and etc.). Some studies negate the effects of social mobility on health at least in certain gender or condition. Billingsley (2009) stated that although downward mobility has immediate, short-term effects on both genders, unemployment has a longer-lasting impact on men following job loss [28].

While many studies report that downward social mobility is linked with poor health outcomes—including higher mortality—some findings challenge this view. For example, in another study when Billingsley et al. (2018), using DRMs to separate the effects of origin and destination SES, found no significant link between downward mobility and mortality. This suggests that traditional models may overstate mobility effects by not properly accounting for background and achieved class. As methodological tools become more refined, we can better isolate the specific role of mobility itself from the structural contexts that surround it [9, 53, 54].

In a study evaluating the effect of social mobility on atherosclerosis, Guimaraes et al. stated that intra-generational downward mobility unlike inter-generational, had no association with atherosclerosis. They explain this issue by saying that having a lower social class than their family may be more stressful for the individual, and on the other hand, more time is needed to develop atherosclerosis, which is provided in intergenerational mobility [47]. Meng et al. in a study evaluating the effect of income mobility on mental disorders, found no association between these two. They said that the effect of income mobility needs more time to show itself (more than the period of this study). Also, its effect may be mediated by other covariates [32]. In a study evaluating effect of social mobility on blood pressure in Brazil, Guimaraes et al. found no association between downward mobility and incidence of hypertension. They said that short time follow-up of participants is the main reason for that [31]. Hoven et al. in a study evaluating the effect of social mobility on depressive symptoms, stated that there was no consistent association between these two. They consider the cause of the result to be entering the origin and destination group information in the statistical analysis [42]. Knochelmann et al. in a study evaluating quality of life, found no association between social mobility and mental aspect of quality of life. They justify this result with the argument that lifetime exposure to disadvantages and allostatic load, affect physical functioning more than mental [43].

Among the studies that investigated the models and theories justifying the relationship between health and social mobility, it seems that the social causation model and the accumulation of disadvantages model have more acceptance than the others, although the influence of the other models such as Health selection cannot be neglected. However, in some cases, it is not possible to determine which model is better, so these models may operate together.

It is also important to recognize that downward movement in SES can have different meanings. Some individuals experience individual-level mobility, which involves changes in their socioeconomic position relative to others within a relatively stable societal structure. In contrast, others experience structural mobility, driven by macro-level changes, such as economic recessions or labor market disruptions, that reshape the entire socioeconomic system. These macro-level shifts may produce different health consequences than individual-level mobility, yet many studies conflate the two [26, 60]. Future research should strive to differentiate these experiences more clearly, ideally using longitudinal individual life-course data and macroeconomic indicators, such as unemployment rates or GDP trends.

One of the key challenges that remains is distinguishing the health effects of where people come from (origin SES), where they end up (destination SES), and the experience of moving between the two. Health disparities that appear to result from downward mobility may, in fact, reflect deeper, long-standing disadvantages linked to an individual’s early-life social background. At the same time, structural forces—such as economic recessions—can lead to downward shifts that are not driven by personal circumstances but by broader systemic changes. These overlapping factors, combined with inconsistencies in how mobility is defined and measured across studies, make it difficult to draw firm conclusions about causal mechanisms. Recognizing and addressing these complexities is crucial for developing more precise research frameworks and informing effective policy responses.

Our study has also more several limitations. First, is that an article may have been missed in the search we have done and excluded unintentionally. The second limitation is that only English-language articles were included in the study, while there were also some articles in other languages that, based on their summary, seemed to be related to our topic. The next limitation was that most of the articles were conducted in European countries such as Sweden and Great Britain, and the social conditions governing those countries may have influenced the results. So the results may not be generalizable to all countries. Finally, self-reported (subjective) social mobility is only one (and not the main) way to measure movements in SES. Furthermore, given its subjective and retrospective nature, it carries many risks in interpreting the results.

Conclusion

Several studies have shown that intragenerational social mobility, especially downward mobility, can have significant effects on physical and mental health and health-related behaviors. While many studies support the social causality model and the accumulation of inequalities as the main explanations for this relationship, some studies also consider the effects of this phenomenon to be limited or dependent on specific demographic and economic conditions. Downward social mobility is often associated with increased mortality rates, the incidence of mental and physical illnesses, and changes in health behaviors, although in some cases mixed results have been reported. Contextual factors such as gender, economic conditions, and the duration of the study also play a role in this relationship. Given the findings of this study, it seems that health policymakers should pay special attention to the effects of social mobility on health and adopt strategies to reduce its negative effects, especially in people with downward mobility. Also, there is a need for more research in different societies to better understand this relationship and develop more accurate models.
