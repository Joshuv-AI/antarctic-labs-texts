Global trends in lipoprotein(a) and high-sensitivity c-reactive protein testing in patients with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease
Authors: Pei-Lun Lee, Kuan-Yu Chi, Shao-Wei Lo, Yung-Fang Tu, Yu Chang, Raiza Rossi, Zafer Akman, Armin Nouri, Michele Nanna, Abdulla A Damluji, Michael G Nanna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNone.Keywords: Lipoprotein (a) High-sensitivity C-reactive protein Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease

Introduction

Contemporary management of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) has evolved beyond traditional risk-factor control toward modifying residual cardiovascular (CV) risk [1]. Atherogenic lipoproteins and systemic inflammation are two major mediators of this residual CV risk [1]. Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is a genetically determined lipoprotein particle with inherent proatherogenic properties [2], whereas high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) is an inflammatory marker with established prognostic value [1,3].

Given the growing recognition of residual CV risk and emerging therapeutic strategies, professional societies have endorsed universal screening for Lp(a) and hsCRP [3–5]. Despite these efforts, both biomarkers remain underutilized, and real-world data describing their implementation in ASCVD populations is limited. Therefore, we sought to evaluate the contemporary trends in Lp(a) and hsCRP uptake among patients with established ASCVD.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using TriNetX Global Collaborative Network, a federated real-world database comprising electronic health records from 168 healthcare organizations (HCOs), primarily based in the US, covering 160 million patients. TriNetX contains only de-identified, aggregate data; therefore, this study was compliant with the HIPAA and exempt from institutional review board approval.

We identified adults aged ≥18 years with ASCVD between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2025, using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes. ASCVD was defined as coronary artery disease (CAD) (ICD-10-CM: I20-I25), ischemic stroke (I63), transient ischemic attacks (TIA) (G45), or peripheral artery disease (PAD) (I73.9). To mitigate misclassification of ASCVD, patients were required to have prescriptions for statin and an antiplatelet agent (aspirin, clopidogrel, ticagrelor, or prasugrel), as well as serum low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol within six months before or after any ASCVD diagnosis. Eligible patients were identified at the time of their first qualifying ASCVD encounter. The enrollment period (2016–2025) was selected to reflect contemporary practice and capture trends in Lp(a) and hsCRP adoption following landmark trials (eg, COLCOT [6], LoDoCo2 [7], CANTOS [8]) and updated guidelines [4,5].

The primary outcome was the annual incidence and annual incidence rate of first-time Lp(a) and hsCRP testing. Annual incidence was calculated as the proportion of patients undergoing each test per calendar year from 2016 to 2025. The annual incidence rate was calculated as the number of patients tested each year divided by total person-time at risk, expressed as cases per 1000 patient-years. Person-time accrued from the first observation within each year until the earliest testing, death, or end of follow-up of corresponding calendar year. Patients with prior testing were excluded from subsequent annual analyses to avoid double counting. Temporal trends of the annual incidence rates were evaluated using log-linear regression, and the average annual percentage change (APC) with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) was derived from the regression slope.

We additionally examined annual incidences for both tests stratified by sex, race, and ethnicity, and assessed the prevalence of Lp(a) and hs-CRP testing in the first and last study year. Data were extracted on January 4, 2026, and all statistical analyses were performed using the built-in TriNetX analytic platform.

Results

A total of 2791,704 individuals met eligible criteria (mean age, 72.0 years; male, 59.0 %; White, 62.3 %; Black, 14.3 %; Asian, 6.1 %; Hispanic, 4.9 %; CAD, 77 %; stroke, 30 %; TIA, 13 %; PAD, 19 %; aspirin, 96 %; mean LDL, 82.6 mg/dL). Overall, Lp(a) testing was performed in 79,425 patients (2.8 %), and hsCRP testing in 184,972 patients (6.6 %). Patients with Lp(a) measurements were younger (67.8 vs 72.1 years), more often men (62 % vs 59 %) but less likely to be Black (11 % vs 14 %), received more intensive lipid-lowering therapy (ezetimibe, 30 % vs 12 %; evolocumab, 10 % vs 2 %), with lower serum LDL (mean level, 75.6 vs 80.4 mg/dL) and higher HDL (mean level, 48.1 vs 44.5 mg/dL) (all p < 0.001). On the other hand, individuals who underwent hsCRP testing were more likely to be Asian (22.3 % vs 4.9 %, p < 0.001), and less often to be White (51.5 % vs 63.1 %, p < 0.001). They also had greater utilization of lipid-lowering therapy (ezetimibe, 24 % vs 12 %; evolocumab, 4 % vs 2 %) with lower serum LDL (mean level, 78.0 vs 80.5 mg/dL) and higher HDL (mean level, 47.6 vs 44.5 mg/dL) (all p < 0.001).

Over the study period, annual incidences of both tests remained persistently low (<2 %). Lp(a) testing increased substantially from 0.13 % in 2016 to 1.65 % in 2025, with a marked acceleration post-2022 (Fig. 1A). In comparison, hsCRP testing increased modestly, from 0.75 % to 0.94 % (Fig. 1B). The annual incidence rate of Lp(a) increased from 1.32 to 23.02 per 1000 patient-years (APC, 16.1 %; 95 % CI, 11.6 %−20.7 %) (Fig. 1C), whereas hsCRP annual incidence rate rose from 7.94 to 13.10 per 1000 patient-years (APC, 6.0 %; 95 % CI, 4.1 %−7.8 %) (Fig. 1D). Overall prevalence of testing rose from 0.63 % to 5.30 % for Lp(a) and from 3.94 % to 9.49 % for hsCRP between 2016 and 2025.

(A) Annual incidence of Lp(a) measurement. (B) Annual incidence of hsCRP measurement. (C) Annual incidence rate of Lp(a) measurement. (D) Annual incidence rate of hsCRP measurement.

Testing practices stratified by sex, race, and ethnicity were generally consistent with those observed in the overall cohort. Asians exhibited the greatest increase in annual testing incidence, whereas Black patients experienced the slowest growth during follow-up (Lp(a): Asian, 0.14 % to 2.23 %; Black, 0.08 % to 1.15 %; Hispanic, 0.22 % to 1.66 %; White, 0.12 % to 1.57 %; hsCRP: Asian, 3.43 % to 4.45 %; Black, 0.45 % to 0.60 %; Hispanic, 0.43 % to 1.35 %; White, 0.55 % to 0.77 %).

Discussion

This large, real-world study yielded several key findings. First, both Lp(a) and hsCRP screening remain strikingly uncommon (Lp(a): 23 per 1000 patient-years; hsCRP: 13 per 1000 patient-years) among patients with ASCVD, despite their high risk for recurrent CV events. Second, hsCRP testing volume has shown minimal growth over time. Although screening for Lp(a) has increased relatively more, its absolute uptake remained very low. Third, subgroup analyses revealed that Black population was less likely to be tested. Collectively, our study delineates contemporary practice patterns in biomarker utilization and highlights important gaps in residual CV risk assessment among ASCVD patients.

Lp(a) screening has been limited by physician inertia, assay heterogeneity, test availability, and cost, though many of these barriers have been progressively addressed. The increase in Lp(a) testing observed in this study (∼1 per 1000 patient-years to 23 per 1000 patient-years) reflects growing recognition of its importance and is consistent with national registries data, which saw approximately 4-fold to 8-fold increases over a decade [9,10]. Nonetheless, absolute testing rates remain exceedingly low in ASCVD populations [9], underscoring the urgent need to integrate Lp(a) into secondary prevention strategies.

Conversely, hsCRP testing has not exhibited a comparable rise, despite robust evidence linking inflammation to adverse CV events, and the emerging anti-inflammatory therapies [3]. This likely reflects uncertainty regarding clinical application and interpretation in real-world settings, where results may be confounded by infection or autoimmune diseases [3]. Given the high prevalence of systemic inflammation in ASCVD, the ACC recently published a Scientific Statement promoting integration of hsCRP into clinical management [3]. Future studies should assess if these recommendations translate into increased clinical uptake.

Several limitations should be noted. Firstly, TriNetX data relies on retrospective claims-based coding data without clinical adjudication, introducing potential misclassification. Secondly, the stringent inclusion criteria may reduce generalizability of our findings to those not receiving statin and antiplatelet therapy. Thirdly, participating HCOs are predominantly academic centers, which may also limit generalizability to non-academic practice.

In conclusion, Lp(a) and hsCRP testing remain infrequently performed among patients with established ASCVD. These findings emphasize the need to enhance comprehensive CV risk assessment and equitable implementation of biomarker testing.
