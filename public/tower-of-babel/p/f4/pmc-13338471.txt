Correlation Between the Natural Course and Magnetic Resonance Imaging Features of Bacillus Calmette-Guérin-Related Granulomatous Prostatitis in Patients With Bladder Cancer
Authors: Ki Choon Sim, Deuk Jae Sung, Na Yeon Han, Yeo Eun Han, Beom Jin Park, Min Ju Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo evaluate magnetic resonance imaging features and the natural course of granulomatous prostatitis after intravesical Bacillus Calmette-Guérin therapy in patients with bladder cancer.MethodsUsing a radiologic report keyword search program, data were collected from magnetic resonance images between March 2018 and August 2023. A total of 241 baseline or follow-up magnetic resonance images of 64 patients with pathologically or clinically confirmed Bacillus Calmette-Guérin-related granulomatous prostatitis were retrospectively included. We investigated intravesical Bacillus Calmette-Guérin history, lesion size changes, and serum prostate-specific antigen changes through a chart review. Lesion imaging features were assessed on T1-. T2-, diffusion-, and contrast-enhanced T1-weighted images and classified based on enhancement degree and presence of diffusion restriction.ResultThe completely improved group (n = 12), incompletely improved group (n = 26), and recurrent inflammation group (n = 13) were identified according to temporal magnetic resonance imaging changes of granulomatous prostatitis. Patients who underwent radical cystectomy or were lost to follow-up were excluded due to unknown outcomes (n = 13). Among 64 patients, subtle increased T1 signal intensity with granulomatous inflammation was observed in 57 (89.1%). The baseline prostate-specific antigen level in the recurrent inflammation group was 2.00 ± 1.26 ng/mL, which was higher than the 1.0–1.1 ng/mL levels in the other groups (P = 0.050). Even if the lesion completely improved on magnetic resonance imaging, the serum prostate-specific antigen level increased by approximately 2.0 times compared to the baseline level. Lesion peak size (26.0 ± 15.0 mm) in the incompletely improving group was significantly larger than that in the other groups (14.8 ± 5.8 and 17.0 ± 9.1 mm) (P = 0.006). Among the 77 initial magnetic resonance images, type A (n = 30, strong enhancement and diffusion restriction), type B (n = 25, rim enhancement with diffusion restriction), and mixed type (n = 19) were the most common. In the last 50 magnetic resonance images, type C (n = 34; no enhancement or diffusion restriction) was predominant.ConclusionIn patients who underwent intravesical Bacillus Calmette-Guérin therapy, bladder magnetic resonance imaging frequently revealed subtle T1-hyperintense lesion in the prostate with diffuse or rim-like enhancement accompanied by markedly restricted diffusion, which is highly suggestive of Bacillus Calmette-Guérin-related granulomatous prostatitis and tends to spontaneously regress on follow-up imaging. Even with radiological improvement, prostate-specific antigen levels may remain elevated and should not be mistaken for malignancy.

INTRODUCTION

Bladder cancer is a global health concern, ranking approximately 6th–7th among malignancies in men worldwide and within top 10 cancers overall.1 Thus, long-term follow-up is essential after initial transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) treatment due to its high local recurrence rate.2 Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) is the standard treatment of high-risk non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) as an adjuvant treatment after TURBT.345 Intravesical BCG therapy aims to induce inflammation and activate the immune system of the bladder. Despite its clinical efficacy, intravesical BCG therapy has been linked to various adverse effects, including systemic, musculoskeletal, genitourinary, and dermatological complications, as well as granulomatous prostatitis (GP).2

BCG-related GP can occur after intravesical BCG treatment because urine reflux through the prostatic urethra involves the prostate. Its incidence is reported to range from 0.8–3.3%.67 Compared to the low incidence of GP (< 4%), an increase in prostate-specific antigen (PSA) after intravesical BCG treatment is reported to be up to 40%.689 Therefore, differentiation between prostate cancer and GP as the cause of PSA increase is necessary, and several studies have reported magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) features of BCG-related GP.610111213 However, most were published as case reports or case series, and only two studies reported MRI findings of BCG-related GP based on 24 and 31 cases, respectively.614 To date, no study has analyzed imaging or clinical features in a large cohort.

MRI is considered essential for initial diagnosis and follow-up examination of bladder cancer after TURBT, and multiparametric MRI has become the standard protocol.151617 The prostate is naturally included as a scan field in men and focal lesions showing abnormal T1 and T2 signal intensity (SI), and restricted diffusion are frequently observed. Given their potential to mimic prostate cancer, lesions identified on MRI examinations pose a considerable diagnostic challenge, particularly in accurately characterizing GP, which may develop as a sequela of bladder cancer treatment.

Thus, we aimed to analyze the MRI features of BCG-related GP in a large patient cohort and discover its clinical features and natural course.

METHODS

Study population and data collection

Our hospital database of radiologic reports on MRI study between May 2016 and August 2023 was queried with keywords such as “granulomatous prostatitis,” “BCG treatment,” granulomatous inflammation,” or “tuberculous prostatitis” using INFINITT Enterprise Search (INFINITT HEALTHCARE, Seoul, Korea). One hundred and ninety-three MRIs from 79 patients with suspected BCG-related GP were included as the initial cohort. We investigated whether TURBT, radical cystectomy, prostate biopsy, and intravesical BCG therapy could be performed through an electronic medical records review. In patients who underwent multiple MRI scans, all available scans were reviewed to assess the time course. Among them, 64 patients (81%) with 241 MRIs were confirmed to have BCG-related GP and were finally included.

MRI protocol

All MRI scans were performed using one of the following 3T scanners (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) with a phased-array body coil: MAGNETOM TrioTim, Skyra, Prisma fit, or VIDA. An intravenous antiperistaltic agent (5 mg/mL cimetropium bromide [Bropium, Bukwang Pharm Co. Ltd., Seoul, Korea] or 20 mg/mL scopolamine butylbromide [Hyoscine butylbromide, Jeil Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea]) was administered to reduce bowel motion. MRI sequences included T1-weighted, axial/coronal/sagittal T2-weighted, contrast-enhanced T1-weighted, and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI). Acquisition parameters are listed in Table 1.

Image interpretation

A consensus review of the MRI scans was conducted by two urogenital radiologists (with 23 and 13 years of experience in interpreting urologic MRI). Lesion characteristics were classified into types A, B, and C according to the study by Lee et al.,6 and cases presenting with mixed imaging findings were classified as mixed types X and Y. Type A lesions were considered as lesions showing diffuse enhancement. Type B lesions were enhancing lesions with a well-demarcated, poorly enhancing component. Type A and B lesions were considered lesions with significantly restricted diffusion. Type C lesions were considered to have poor rim enhancement or non-enhancement and low SI on high b-value DWI. The lesion SI on T1-weighted imaging was visually assessed and categorized as hyperintense, isointense, or hypointense.14 When available, the prostate signal on the baseline MRI was used as the reference standard; in the absence of a baseline study, the background prostate tissue on the current MRI was used for comparison. Temporal changes in the lesion SI were evaluated and classified as decrease in SI, fluctuating SI, or no significant change.

The enhancement patterns of prostate lesions were visually assessed on contrast-enhanced T1-weighted images. Each lesion was categorized into one of the following enhancement types: early arterial enhancement, delayed enhancement, persistent enhancement, delayed washout, rim-like peripheral enhancement, or poor enhancement. Lesions exhibiting two or more enhancement patterns were also included in the analysis.

Diagnostic criteria for GP

The authors diagnosed “possible GP” when typical MRI imaging features were present after recent intravesical treatment for bladder cancer through chart review and image review; “probable GP” when there was a comparable pre-BCG treatment MRI (normal appearance of prostate) or a post-BCG treatment follow-up MRI that increased diagnostic conspicuity and “definite GP” in patients with histopathologic confirmation.

Protocol of intravesical BCG therapy

The selection criteria for patients undergoing intravesical BCG therapy after TURBT for bladder urothelial carcinoma primarily depends on the risk stratification of NMIBC. At our institution, induction BCG therapy is generally performed within 4 weeks after initial TURBT, and intravesical BCG instillations are administered every 6 weeks. Maintenance BCG therapy was administered to some patients as three weekly intravesical instillations 3- and 6-months following induction.

Histopathological analysis

The histopathological evaluation was based on pathology reports generated by pathologists randomly assigned at diagnosis rather than by a single designated pathologist. Tissue specimens were routinely fixed in neutral-buffered formalin and embedded in paraffin blocks according to standard protocols.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. One-way analysis of variance with F-test was used for normally distributed data, and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-parametric data. Between-group comparisons with equal variances were performed using independent t-tests. Interquartile range (IQR) was calculated using the linear interpolation method. Categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Ethics statement

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Korea University Medical Center (approval number: 2023AN0003), and informed patient consent for research was waived. All research and data collection procedures adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

RESULTS

Clinical findings

Seventy-nine patients (193 MRIs) were initially identified as BCG-related GP based on a keyword search. Fifteen patients were excluded after a chart review because of prostate adenocarcinoma (n = 4), urothelial carcinoma with prostate invasion (n = 4), genitourinary tuberculosis (n = 1), or an unconfirmed history of intravesical BCG therapy. The remaining 64 patients (81%) with serial MRIs (n = 241), including baseline and follow-up scans not captured in the initial cohort, were finally analyzed.

A median of four MRIs (range, 1–9) were performed per patient. Only one MRI was available for eight patients: radical cystectomy (n = 5), followed by CT and cystoscopy only (n = 2), and lost to follow-up (n = 1). Additionally, five patients with two MRIs, but only one MRI (either before or after BCG therapy) were suitable for inclusion, making it impossible to assess the clinical course of the lesions. Thus, natural course of GP was determined in 51 patients (Table 2), among whom 38 (74.5%) showed gradual improvement over time (e.g., from imaging types A to B and B to C); of which, 12 exhibited complete lesion resolution, with a mean initial size of 14.8 mm (range, 6.9–27.0 mm). Their baseline PSA averaged 1.16 ng/mL, peaked at 3.85 ng/mL (3.32-fold increase), and declined to 2.27 ng/mL at last follow-up, remaining twice the baseline despite no residual imaging findings. In the remaining 26 with incomplete improvement, lesion size decreased from 26 mm (range, 10.6–74.7 mm) to 13 mm (range, 4.7–25.3 mm). Their baseline, peak, and final PSA levels were 1.1, 4.2, and 1.7 ng/mL, respectively (Table 2).

However, 13 patients (25.5%) developed new lesions despite improvement in existing lesions and were classified as having recurrent granulomatous inflammation, all of whom received maintenance BCG therapy after induction. The mean baseline, peak, and final PSA levels were 2.0, 4.8, and 3.1 ng/mL, respectively. Mean lesion size changed from 17 mm (range, 9.6–43.7 mm) to 15.7 mm (range, 0–36.1 mm). In one case, the lesion resolved completely and became immeasurable (Table 2).

The baseline PSA level in the recurrent inflammation group was 2.00 ± 1.26 ng/mL, which was higher than the 1.0–1.1 ng/mL levels in the other groups (P = 0.050). The incompletely improved group showed significantly larger peak lesion sizes (26.0 ± 15.0 mm) than the complete (14.8 ± 5.8 mm) and recurrent groups (17.0 ± 9.1 mm) (P = 0.006) (Table 2).

The interval from the first BCG instillation to post-TURBT MRI was 242 days on average (range, 36–1,645). For 49 of the 51 patients (2 excluded for biparametric magnetic resonance imaging [bpMRI]), the interval from post-TURBT to the last MRI was 810 days (range, 60–2,163). No significant group differences were found in MRI follow-up duration (P = 0.270) or BCG-to-MRI interval (P = 0.502) (Table 2).

Diagnosis of GP

According to the diagnostic criteria established by the authors, 3 out of 64 patients were assigned as possible GP (4.7%), 47 as probably GP (73.4%), and 14 as definite GP (21.9%). Among the 14 patients, histopathology was confirmed by radical cystectomy (n = 8) or prostate biopsy (n = 6).

Imaging patterns on last MRI

Fifty-one underwent at least one follow-up MRI and one patient was excluded because of bpMRI scan. The KJR-type distributions were type A (n = 5), B (n = 7), C (n = 34), mixed A and B (n = 2), and mixed B and C (n = 2) (Table 3). Interobserver agreement showed a Fleiss-Cohen weighted kappa of 0.945 (95% CI, 0.855–1.036; P < 0.001).

Dynamic enhancement analysis was performed in 50 patients with GP using the final MRI scan. Rim-like peripheral enhancement, alone or in combination with other patterns, was the most common finding (n = 23, 46%) and showed a statistically significant difference among the groups (P < 0.001). Complete resolution, with no visible lesions on dynamic imaging, was observed in 10 patients (20%).

Patterns of temporal change in lesion T1 SI

Among 64 patients, T1 SI confirming granulomatous inflammation was observed in 57 (89.1%) (Figs. 1B, 2B and 3B). After excluding 13 patients without available follow-up imaging, 44 of the remaining 51 patients (86.3%) demonstrated normalization of T1 high SI on follow-up imaging, concomitant with lesion regression (Figs. 1C, 2C and 3C).

DICUSSION

Bladder MRI is considered essential for the initial diagnosis and follow-up examination of bladder cancer after TURBT, and multiparametric MRI has become the standard protocol.151617 At our institution, bladder MRI is routinely performed in most patients with bladder cancer since 2018. During image interpretation, we frequently encountered incidental abnormal signal intensities or enhancements within the prostate. These unexpected findings often pose diagnostic challenges in terms of their clinical significance and interpretation. In this context, prostate-related abnormalities observed on MRI may be particularly relevant when considering the potential side effects of intravesical BCG therapy, a standard adjuvant treatment following repeat TURBT in certain intermediate- and all high-risk NMIBC cases.345 Although physicians may identify certain intravesical BCG therapy side effects based on clinical symptoms or laboratory findings, determining it solely from imaging studies is challenging for radiologists. This limitation hinders accurate interpretation of extravesical findings observed on follow-up MRI.

Lee et al.6 proposed an MRI pattern classification system based on 24 cases. Based on the lesion enhancement pattern and degree of diffusion restriction, the authors proposed types A, B, and C classification, reflecting progression from acute to chronic inflammatory stages. This consistent trend was evident across initial and final MRIs scans in our large cohort, reinforcing the robustness of findings (Table 3). However, a notable proportion of lesions exhibited mixed features that could not be clearly assigned to a single type, accounting for 24.7% of the initial and 18% of the final MRIs scans. Rim-like peripheral enhancement (KJR type B) was observed in 57.1% of cases. In comparison, when lesions were analyzed solely based on enhancement patterns, this feature was identified in 45.5% (35 of 77) cases. Overall, rim-like peripheral enhancement was characteristically present in approximately 50% of patients, with imaging findings suggestive of GP; supporting that this pattern is more commonly associated with benign conditions rather than prostate cancer and its recognition is important when differentiating using MRI. It is often related to the presence of central caseous necrosis, as seen in BCG-induced granulomatous inflammation.1819 Nevertheless, a subset of patients demonstrate non-rim-like enhancement patterns, such as early enhancement with washout or a plateau, overlapping with those seen in prostate cancer.2021 Subtle T1 hyperintensity (not bright high SI in the post-biopsy setting) in GP, as confirmed in the present authors’ study and the recent study by Durmaz et al.,14 is expected to be an important point in differentiating it from prostate cancer. In the post-biopsy setting, T1 hyperintensity is largely attributable to the presence of methemoglobin from hemorrhage,22 which typically manifests as a conspicuous signal alteration. By contrast, the subtle T1 hyperintensity occasionally encountered in GP has been postulated to reflect paramagnetic effects related to macrophage activity and associated necrotic byproducts,14 thereby producing a less pronounced signal change. Therefore, comprehensive evaluation using multiparametric MRI, including T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and DWI, remains essential. The normalization of increased T1 hyperintensity in the natural course of GP, first identified in this study, is expected to be a very important finding suggesting benignity.

GP following intravesical BCG therapy has been reported with a wide range of incidence (0.3–40%), and up to 95% of cases are asymptomatic and self-limiting.2324 Therefore, it is crucial to avoid misinterpreting post-TURBT surveillance MRI findings or PSA elevation as indicators of prostate cancer. Changes in serum PSA and lesion size were confirmed in all patients included (Table 2), with the highest increase observed in the incompletely improved group. PSA elevation associated with prostate inflammation is considered a physiological response and is consistent with findings from previous studies.10 Notably, even in patients in whom no residual prostatic lesion was observed on imaging, serum PSA levels remained approximately 1.5- to 2-fold higher than baseline values measured prior to TURBT. As expected, the recurrence group had the highest mean PSA level at the last follow-up; BCG maintenance therapy was the main contributor to recurrent granulomatous inflammation. However, baseline PSA level was nearly twice as high as that in the improving group (P = 0.050), suggesting that a higher initial PSA level may also predispose patients to recurrence. A larger peak lesion size was significantly associated with incomplete resolution (P = 0.006). Since the follow-up duration did not differ among the groups, peak lesion size may serve as a predictor of incomplete resolution.

Based on our data, we performed a subgroup analysis focusing on patients who exhibited a temporal evolution from acute inflammation—KJR imaging pattern 1 (diffusely enhancing lesions with restricted diffusion that are difficult to distinguish from prostate cancer, excluding those with KJR imaging pattern 2 or mixed patterns 1 and 2)—to the chronic phase of granulomatous inflammation, KJR imaging pattern 3, characterized by necrosis or complete regression indicated by the absence of enhancement and diffusion restriction (signal void), or by the complete disappearance of the lesion. Seventeen patients met this criterion, serum PSA levels increased from a baseline of 1.48 ng/mL to a peak of 4.43 ng/mL, subsequently decreasing to 2.67 ng/mL at the most recent assessment, supporting the interpretation of a benign, self-limiting inflammatory process. In this subgroup, the mean interval from the initial BCG instillation to the first MRI demonstrating KJR pattern 1 was 179 days (IQR, 85 –148 days), and the mean interval to the follow-up MRI demonstrating KJR pattern 3 was 731 days (IQR, 427–815 days). Based on lesion size, the mean for KJR pattern 1 lesions was 15.6 mm (IQR, 10.90–19.38 mm), and the mean during follow-up for KJR pattern 3 lesions was 4.27 mm (IQR, 0.00–7.38 mm). These findings suggest that GP in the acute phase typically becomes evident on MRI approximately 6 months after initiating the standard 6-cycle BCG induction therapy and may progress to chronic changes over a median interval of about 2 years after the initial instillation. In the study by Kim et al.,25 150 patients with bladder cancer underwent intravesical BCG therapy and 18-fluoro-2-deoxyglucose (F-18 FDG) positron emission tomography computed tomography, revealing increased F-18 FDG uptake within 1 year after BCG instillation (acute stage) and decreased uptake after 2–3 years (chronic stage). Similarly, our study showed that multiparametric MRI features of GP correlate with disease stage. At this stage, newly developed prostate imaging abnormalities or PSA elevations in patients with bladder cancer are more likely to reflect benign inflammatory changes than malignancies. Therefore, rather than pursuing immediate invasive procedures, such as prostate biopsy, careful follow-up with PSA monitoring or imaging may be a more appropriate management approach.

Since Vesical Imaging-Reporting and Data System introduction in 2018, both preoperative and postoperative bladder MRIs have been increasingly performed, leading to a higher frequency of incidental prostatic findings. In many cases, initial imaging interpretations tend to favor the diagnosis of prostate cancer, particularly when accompanied by elevated PSA levels, which further complicates the differential diagnosis. At our institutional report database, four of eight were ultimately diagnosed with prostate adenocarcinoma, while the remaining four were found to have direct invasion of urothelial carcinoma into the prostate and were therefore excluded. Although cases were limited, follow-up MRI consistently demonstrated an increase preexisting prostatic lesions size in these patients. This finding highlights the clinical importance of monitoring lesion progression, rather than simply noting the emergence of new lesions on surveillance MRI, in patients with bladder cancer. An increase in lesion size should prompt the consideration of potential malignancy, and in such cases, further diagnostic workup, including prostate biopsy, may be warranted to guide timely and appropriate management.

This study has several limitations. First, approximately 22% of cases had pathological confirmation. Therefore, the possibility of misclassification, such as inclusion of patients with concurrent prostate or bladder cancer among those clinically diagnosed, cannot be excluded. Through careful chart review and detailed imaging assessment, this study was able to analyze the imaging and clinical features of a larger cohort of GP than previously reported. Second, MRI scans were acquired using various scanners and protocols. The heterogeneity of imaging protocols precludes quantitative image analysis. To overcome this, we applied the diagnostic criteria proposed by Lee et al.,6 which are considered highly reproducible and reliable for imaging-based interpretation. Third, the time interval between BCG instillation and follow-up MRI varied considerably, largely because of the absence of established guidelines defining the optimal timing for surveillance MRI in this clinical setting. This considerable variation makes it challenging to establish consistent correlations between disease duration and imaging stage. Finally, the study cohort was selected based on radiological reports. Patients whose bladder MRI reports lacked specific comments regarding the prostate were excluded. This limitation hinders the accurate estimation of GP incidence following intravesical BCG treatment. A more accurate estimation of the true incidence may be achieved through a prospective study design that includes MRI data analysis of patients undergoing intravesical BCG therapy after TURBT.

In conclusion, abnormal SI lesions in the prostate are frequently observed on follow-up MRI in patients who have undergone TURBT and intravesical BCG therapy for bladder cancer, often accompanied by mild serum PSA elevation. BCG-related GP should be considered in the differential diagnosis of newly developed lesions demonstrating subtle T1 hyperintensity with diffuse or rim-like peripheral enhancement on contrast-enhanced images. Moreover, PSA levels may remain elevated despite radiological improvement of the lesions and should not be misinterpreted as evidence of malignancy.
