Cerebrospinal fluid volume outperforms traditional measures for predicting treatment failure in infant hydrocephalus
Authors: Marcia Harumy Yoshikawa, Mingzhao Yu, Julia Tatz, Hayleigh Tran, Henry A Feldman, Ariadna S Luviano, P Ellen Grant, Jason Sutin, Benjamin C Warf, Pei-Yi Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndoscopic third ventriculostomy with choroid plexus cauterization (ETV/CPC) offers an alternative to shunt placement for infant hydrocephalus, avoiding shunt-related complications when successful. However, approximately 40% of procedures fail, with 30% ultimately requiring shunt placement. Current risk stratification relies primarily on age and prior interventions, limiting ability to optimize treatment selection. Therefore, this study aimed to determine whether preoperative cerebrospinal fluid volume (CSFV) measurements independently predict shunt-dependence following ETV/CPC in infant hydrocephalus.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study was conducted from 2008 to 2024, with follow-up through 2024 and statistical analysis performed in January 2025. Infants who underwent ETV/CPC as the primary treatment for hydrocephalus and had magnetic resonance imaging within two weeks before surgery were included. The primary outcome was shunt-dependence, defined as progressive ventriculomegaly requiring shunt placement after ETV/CPC. Imaging measures included traditional frontal-occipital horn ratio (FOHR) and novel volumetric assessments: CSFV and CSFV/intracranial volume (ICV) ratio estimated using deep learning-based segmentation methods and the corresponding z-scores using age- and sex- adjusted normative curves. Cox proportional hazards regression identified independent predictors of shunt-dependence.ResultsA total of 188 infants (median age at surgery 60 days, 42.5% female) were included. Sixty-six (35.8%) patients became shunt-dependent over a median follow-up of 3.8 years as of January 2025. In multivariable analysis adjusting for age and prior temporary CSF diversion, both CSFV z-score (HR 1.10, 95%CI 1.02–1.20, p = 0.02) and CSFV/ICV ratio z-score (HR 1.07, 95%CI 1.01–1.13, p = 0.02) independently predicted shunt-dependence, while FOHR did not achieve statistical significance in adjusted models. Younger infants with higher CSFV z-scores demonstrated elevated failure rates.ConclusionsPreoperative CSF volume measurements provide superior risk stratification compared to traditional linear assessments for predicting shunt-dependence in infant hydrocephalus. These findings support integration of volumetric brain imaging analysis into clinical decision-making to optimize treatment selection and improve outcomes through more precise identification of candidates most likely to benefit from ETV/CPC versus primary shunt placement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12987-026-00819-0.Keywords: Hydrocephalus, Cerebrospinal fluid volume, Infant, Endoscopic third ventriculostomy

Introduction

Hydrocephalus in infants is characterized by abnormal enlargement of the cerebral ventricles and clinical signs of increased intracranial pressure. It is the most common cause for neurosurgery in the pediatric population in the United States [1, 2]. The standard treatment for hydrocephalus has long been placement of a ventriculoperitoneal shunt (VPS) to divert cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from the cerebral ventricles to other body cavities, most commonly the peritoneal cavity [1, 2]. However, VPS is associated with a reoperation rate of 40% − 50% within the first two years after the procedure as well as a lifelong risk of shunt malfunction [3, 4].

More recently, endoscopic third ventriculostomy combined with choroid plexus cauterization (ETV/CPC) has been introduced as an alternative treatment for infant hydrocephalus [3–9]. A successful ETV/CPC allows a life free of implanted devices and the associated morbidity of multiple reoperations, as surgical failures beyond the first year post-ETV/CPC are rare [3–9]. Introducing ETV/CPC as a treatment option for infant hydrocephalus is particularly important for underserved areas, where the limited access to timely neurosurgical care in cases of shunt malfunction is life-threatening. Additionally, a recent randomized controlled trial of ETV/CPC vs. VPS for infants with post-infectious hydrocephalus has demonstrated no difference in neurodevelopmental outcomes at one-, two-, and five-years post procedure [10–12]. Despite being a promising alternative to VPS, a subset of patients who undergo ETV/CPC fail the procedure with about one-third ultimately requiring shunt placement [4, 13]. Only age, prior shunt, and the frontal and occipital horn ratio (FOHR) have been identified as risk factors for ETV/CPC failure [4, 14, 15].

Thus, the clinical monitoring of ventricle size is often performed by measuring the FOHR, which is derived from linear measurements on axial brain imaging slices [16]. However, FOHR only approximates the volume of the complex 3D shapes in hydrocephalus and is dependent on how the measurements are performed. Additionally, FOHR is susceptible to variability from imaging acquisition factors such as head angulation, rotation, and slice thickness [17, 18]. While the quantitative assessment of CSF volumes might offer superior predictive performance, it requires image segmentation – which is largely limited to research studies due to laborious post-processing – hindering efforts to investigate the role of CSF volumes in predicting ETV/CPC outcome.

This study aimed to assess whether preoperative CSF volume (CSFV) is an independent factor that predicts shunt-dependence after ETV/CPC, and whether it outperforms FOHR. Identifying early predictors of shunt-dependence could optimize treatment decisions and decrease the number of hydrocephalus related operations.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a single institution and received ethical approval from the institutional review board at Boston Children’s Hospital.

Study population

Patients younger than 1 year who underwent ETV/CPC for hydrocephalus between 2008 and 2021 were identified and cross-referenced using departmental clinical and hospital billing databases [4]. Inclusion criteria were: (1) ETV/CPC as the initial treatment for hydrocephalus and (2) availability of an axial T2-weighted brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) within 2 weeks before surgery. Patients were excluded if (1) hydrocephalus was secondary to encephalocele, (2) significant imaging artifacts prevented accurate intracranial segmentation, and (3) intraventricular blood products occupied more than 5% of the ventricular volume as measured by intracranial segmentation.

Demographic and clinical data collection

Clinical and surgical information were obtained from the Neurosurgery Department database [4]. The primary outcome was shunt-dependence, defined as progressive ventriculomegaly after ETV/CPC requiring shunt placement. Secondary outcome was ETV/CPC failure, defined as need for any additional surgical procedure for hydrocephalus treatment or death related to hydrocephalus.

MRI data analysis

To obtain the brain parenchymal volume (BPV) and CSFV, intracranial segmentation was performed by a deep-learning model based on an encoder-decoder convolutional neural network architecture tailored to infant hydrocephalus images [19].

The segmentation model implemented was trained with 167 clinically acquired preoperative T2-weighted images of hydrocephalus patients aged 0 to 330 days. MRI protocol used included fast-spin echo (FSE) T2, T2 blade, T2 propeller, and fast recovery FSE (FRFSE) T2, with a mean slice thickness of 3.2 mm (range 1–5). Intracranial segmentation comprised three classes (CSF, brain parenchyma, and background) and was performed on axial slices spanning from the most superior slice where brain parenchyma or CSF were visible to the level of the Foramen Magnum (Supplementary Fig. 1). Structures inside the ventricles – such as choroid plexus and intraventricular blood products – were segmented as “CSFV” and included in the ventricle volume. For this reason, patients with significant amount of intraventricular blood were excluded from the outcome analysis, as described in the “Study population” section.

The automated segmentation process took approximately 0.8 s per scan using a NVIDIA A30 GPU and 20 s using a CPU (AMD Ryzen 9 7950X Processor 16-Core). Visual inspection and manual editing of model’s segmentation results were by trained staff (M.H.Y., H.T., and A.S.L.) using ITK-SNAP [20], following a standardized protocol and under the supervision of a senior pediatric neuroradiologist (P.E.G.), who reviewed all segmentations as needed to ensure consistency and accuracy. FOHR was measured by four board-certified neurosurgeons [4] as FOHR = (A + B)/ (2 x C) in which A is the greatest diameter of the frontal horns, B is the greatest diameter of the occipital horns, and C is the greatest biparietal diameter [16]. Intracranial volume (ICV) was calculated as ICV = BPV + CSFV. CSFV/ICV ratio was also calculated.

BPV and CSFV z-scores were generated from normative growth curves for age and sex from Peterson, et al. [21], with an updated computation of z-scores to enable the scores to be unbounded. The new z-score calculation was performed by the generalized method proposed by Rigby [22] but calculated in log-space to compensate for the numerical instabilities that arise from extreme values. A generalized additive model for location, scale and shape (GAMLSS) was used to model the CSFV/ICV ratio growth curves. The CSFV/ICV ratio model was developed using the NIH Pediatric MRI Repository dataset and the R-package GAMLSS [23]. A Beta distribution was used for constructing the CSFV/ICV ratio growth curves as it is the most suitable distribution for modeling proportions. The GAMLSS model was exported to python for z-score computation.

Selection of variables

Imaging variables were selected a priori based on two criteria: (1) biological plausibility as markers of CSF burden and ventricular size, and (2) prior published associations with ETV/CPC outcomes [3, 4, 24]. All candidate imaging predictors identified on these grounds — FOHR, CSFV, CSFV z-score, BPV, BPV z-score, CSFV/ICV ratio, and CSFV/ICV ratio z-score — were included in the analysis; no variables were excluded after inspection of the data. Age at ETV/CPC and prior CSF diversion were pre-specified as covariates in all multivariable models based on their established association with ETV/CPC outcomes, independent of their association with the outcome in this dataset.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]). Given the time-to-event nature of the outcomes, Cox proportional hazard regression was used to identify predictors of shunt-dependence and ETV/CPC failure. Hazard ratios, 95% confidence interval (CI) and p values were obtained using the Cox proportional hazards regression modeling. The validity of the Cox proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals test for all covariates included in the final models. No violations of the proportional hazards assumption were identified.

All continuous predictors (age at ETV/CPC, CSFV, BPV, z-scores, and ratios) were entered as continuous variables in the Cox models, while the binary covariate (prior CSF diversion) was entered as a categorical variable. To improve the interpretability of the hazard ratio, FOHR was rescaled by a factor of 10, so that the resulting hazard ratio reflects the change in hazard per 0.10-unit increase in FOHR.

Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied separately within each outcome (shunt-dependence and ETV/CPC failure) to control the false discovery rate (FDR) at 5% across all seven imaging predictors evaluated in the multivariable models (FOHR, CSFV, CSFV z-score, BPV, BPV z-score, CSFV/ICV ratio, and CSFV/ICV ratio z-score).

From the fitted Cox model cumulative risk curves were constructed for each pairwise combination of age and CSFV z-score corresponding to the 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 90th percentiles of age and z-score in the study sample. A 2-tailed alpha level based on p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using Stata version 19.5 and SAS 9.4.

Results

Study sample

265 patients underwent ETV/CPC as their initial surgery for treating hydrocephalus between 2008 to 2021 (Fig. 1). A total of 188 children were included, with a median (IQR) follow-up time of 46 (22 − 87) months. Most patients (n = 165, 87.7%) were followed for more than 12 months after ETV/CPC. Eight (4.2%) patients died and, among these, 1 (0.5%) death was related to hydrocephalus. Females accounted for 80 (42.5%). Myelomeningocele was the most common etiology, accounting for 51 (27.1%) cases, followed by post-hemorrhagic (n = 49, 26%), Aqueductal stenosis (n = 37; 19.6%), Dandy-Walker complex (n = 23; 12.2%), post-infectious hydrocephalus (n = 4; 2.1%), and other (n = 24; 12.7%) (Table 1). Etiologies included in the ‘Other’ category were: Alexander’s Disease, Idiopathic Communicating Hydrocephalus, Chiari 1 malformation, Crouzon Syndrome, Apert Syndrome, Supracerebellar Cyst, Posterior Fossa Cyst, Megalencephaly-polymicrogyria-polydactyly-hydrocephalus (MPPH) Syndrome, Cloverleaf Skull Deformity. Six patients with unknown cause of hydrocephalus were also included in the ‘Other’ category.

ETV/CPC outcomes

Twenty-one (11.1%) patients had temporary CSF diversion defined as either an external ventricular drain (EVD) or ventriculosubgaleal shunt (VSGS). The median (IQR) corrected age at ETV/CPC was 90 (10–164) days. Ninety (47.8%) patients failed the first ETV/CPC and the median time to ETV/CPC failure was 74 (38–157) days. Among which, 66 (73.3%) became shunt-dependent and 24 (26.6%) were successfully managed with additional ETV. The median (IQR) preoperative FOHR was 0.62 (0.57–0.66), BPV z-score was − 0.61 (-2.3–0.63), CSFV z-score was 4.7 (3.3–5.9), CSFV/ICV ratio was 0.41 (0.31–0.52), and CSFV/ICV ratio z-score was 6.24 (4.02–8.48) (Table 1). FOHR was significantly correlated with CSFV (Pearson, r = 0.56, p < 0.001) and CSVF/ICV ratio (Pearson, r = 0.81, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3).

The univariate Cox Regression analysis for shunt dependence showed that corrected age at ETV/CPC (p = 0.01), history of temporizing CSF diversion (p < 0.0001), FOHR (p = 0.01), BPV (p = 0.01), CSFV z-score (p = 0.03), CSFV/ICV ratio (p = 0.003), and CSFV/ICV ratio z-score (p = 0.008) were significantly associated with shunt dependence in the follow-up (Table 2). The multivariable Cox Regression analysis confirmed that CSFV z-score (p = 0.01), CSFV/ICV ratio (p = 0.01), and CSFV/ICV ratio z-score (p = 0.01) independently predicted shunt dependence when adjusted for corrected age at ETV/CPC and prior CSF diversion, while BPV (p = 0.98) and FOHR (p = 0.06) lost significance after adjustment for age and prior diversion (Table 3). Raw CSFV and BPV z-score were not associated with shunt dependence in either univariable or multivariable analyses.

Discussion

The ability to predict the likelihood of success for ETV/CPC treatment in any individual patient provides an important guide to choosing the best treatment for that patient, for counseling the family about risks and benefits of that treatment, and for managing expectations. ETV/CPC provides an alternative to the implantation of a shunt for the treatment of infant hydrocephalus and, when successful in the first 6 months after treatment, the requirement for any further hydrocephalus-related operations is far less likely than is the case for shunts [2–4]. In addition, ETV/CPC has been shown to be equivalent to shunt placement regarding developmental outcome and brain growth [10–12] and has a lower rate of postoperative infection [3, 5, 9, 11, 25–27]. But overall, ETV/CPC ultimately fails in about one-third of infants, with the majority failing within 6 months of surgery and requiring shunt placement [4, 5, 13]. It is crucial to improve the ability to accurately predict those infants whose likelihood of failure should exclude them from having ETV/CPC as their primary treatment in order to minimize the number of operations and to optimize their brain growth during this important period of development.

Younger age at treatment and a history of prior CSF diversion are widely recognized as factors that decrease the likelihood of success for both ETV alone [14, 15, 28] and ETV/CPC [4, 29–31], and are useful in helping to predict which patients have the best chance of success. We previously published the first report showing that a high FOHR (> 0.6) was also a significant predictor of ETV/CPC failure [4]. Here we present a conceptual advance by introducing the first volumetric assessment of preoperative CSF as an independent predictor of ETV/CPC outcome, demonstrating the superiority of these parameters – namely, the CSFV z-score, CSFV/ICV ratio and CSFV/ICV ratio z-score – over FOHR in the prediction of ETV/CPC success.

We had previously hypothesized that preoperative ventricular volume should impact ETV/CPC success, which is why we studied the effect of FOHR in our previous work [4]. The traditional bulk flow model suggests that ETV “bypasses” ventricular outflow obstruction by establishing communication between the ventricles and the subarachnoid space via the basal cisterns. And it would further imply that CPC functions to reduce the rate of CSF production, hence addressing a deficiency in CSF absorption. Although this model might account for the efficacy of ETV/CPC in all hydrocephalus etiologies it does not readily explain the significant negative effects of age and ventricular volume on the success of ETV/CPC that have been observed. On the other hand, in a hydrodynamic model of hydrocephalus (postulating that ventricular expansion is driven by intraventricular pulsations), ETV/CPC may function by modulating pulsatile forces rather than simply altering the balance between CSF production and absorption [1, 4, 7, 31–34]. That model suggests ETV functions as an intraventricular pulsation absorber. Infants have a relatively high brain compliance that decreases with age [35], such that ETV may prove inadequate in very young infants whose ventricles may distend more readily. We have previously proposed that CPC might augment the pulsation absorber effect of ETV by further reduction of the intraventricular pulsation amplitude, thus accounting for the increased success rates demonstrated for ETV in young infants when CPC is added [1, 4, 7, 33].

It is reasonable to assume that the ventricles follow, at least to some degree, the law of Laplace, which states, “the tension on the wall of a sphere is the product of the pressure times radius of the chamber, and the tension is inversely related to the thickness of the wall”, as expressed in the following equation.

Wall tension T= (pressure × radius) ÷ (2×wall thickness)

Thus, at a fixed pressure, larger ventricles (greater radius), which are typically associated with thinner cerebral cortices (reduced wall thickness), would logically increase the inflationary force, and – as does the higher ventricular compliance among infants – limit the effectiveness of ETV/CPC. There might thus be an upper limit of ventricular compliance (or, the ease with which the ventricles inflate) beyond which ETV/CPC is unable to arrest the process. Our unpublished data measuring intracranial pressure and ventricular pulsatility in infants while undergoing ETV/CPC suggests ETV decreases both the mean ICP and the intraventricular pulsation amplitude, while the subsequent CPC doubles the effect of reducing pulsatility. The hypothesis of a fixed upper bound of ventricular compliance beyond which ETV/CPC cannot be effective certainly requires more research for confirmation. But for now, this paradigm satisfies many observations and may ultimately enable further optimization of patient selection for ETV/CPC in order to maximize the chances for a favorable outcome and minimize the number of operations.

Because FOHR can be easily measured with little training, it has been widely used clinically. However, this linear measurement fails to capture the full extent of three-dimensional ventricular distortion and is originally validated against ventricle volumes with a small number of patients [16]. In addition, the linear measurements could be subjective and have high variations due to imaging acquisition factors. The current study, to our knowledge for the first time, examined its accuracy against ground truth (CSF volume) in a larger sample. We found a moderate correlation (r = 0.56, Supplementary Fig. 2) with CSF volume and the correlation improves when it is compared to the CSF/ICV ratio, a 3D version of FOHR (r = 0.81, Supplementary Fig. 3). This indicates that FOHR is a proxy of the ratio between the ventricle volume and the intracranial volume rather than a proxy of ventricle size. These two measurements are both related to the severity of ventriculomegaly but are not interchangeable in the context of evaluating the likelihood of treatment failure. We also noticed that this linear relationship does not hold well in cases with markedly enlarged ventricles.

The present study takes this work one step further by replacing the proxy of FOHR with the measured ventricular volumes of individual patients and then calculating the z-score to account for the child’s corrected age. The results showed CSFV z-score, CSFV/ICV ratio, and CSFV/ICV ratio z-score were independent outcome predictors, but FOHR and raw CSFV did not show the same effect size. We demonstrated that CSFV z-score provides a precise and individualized predictive parameter with a basis in physiology. The incorporation of age-adjusted z-scores addresses the critical issue of normal developmental changes in CSFV, providing a more precise assessment of pathological enlargement.

Current risk stratification for ETV/CPC has relied primarily on age at surgery and history of prior interventions, with limited incorporation of imaging findings beyond qualitative assessment of ventricular size. Previously reported success scores, though useful, have limited sensitivity and specificity for patient-specific clinical decision-making [27, 30]. Our volumetric approach represents a significant advancement in objective failure risk assessment. The interaction between age and CSFV z-score illustrated in Fig. 2 has important clinical implications: very young infants (< 10 days, 25th percentile in our cohort) with high CSFV z-scores (> 75th percentile in our cohort) demonstrate higher failure rates while older infants with smaller relative CSF volumes have substantially lower failure rates. This suggests that risk stratification should consider both factors simultaneously rather than each in isolation. Integration of CSFV z-scores with existing clinical risk factors (age, etiology, prior interventions) could form the basis for a comprehensive prediction model. Such a tool could stratify patients into risk categories: low-risk cases ideal for ETV/CPC, intermediate-risk cases requiring individualized decision-making, and high-risk cases where primary shunt placement might be preferred. This approach could aid in the optimization of treatment selection while maintaining shared decision-making with families.

Finally, although neuroimaging remains a cornerstone of hydrocephalus care, quantitative assessment of CSFV is still not readily available for routine clinical use [36]. Existing volumetric analyses typically rely on specialized software and trained personnel for image segmentation, which increases both time and cost [36, 37]. Moreover, these tools often demonstrate limited performance in clinical images of infant hydrocephalus due to the structural brain abnormalities and the variable quality of clinical scans [36]. In this study, we applied a deep-learning segmentation model tailored to the infant hydrocephalus population and able to handle image artifacts and noise commonly present in clinical scans. This approach substantially reduced the time required for accurate volume acquisition compared to manual segmentation, highlighting the urgent need for more precise, efficient, and widely available tools to enable real-time CSFV assessment during routine imaging review in this population.

Ultimately, simplified risk calculators incorporating age, prior CSF diversion status, and CSFV z-scores — similar to the risk analysis illustrated in Fig. 2 — could be developed to assist clinical decision-making. The goal is to make real-time volumetric assessment as widely accessible as current linear measurements while offering significantly superior predictive accuracy.

Limitations

This study has limitations intrinsic to its retrospective design and single-center nature. However, we included hydrocephalus patients treated with ETV/CPC at Boston Children’s Hospital over a 13-year period, encompassing a wide range of patient characteristics and hydrocephalus etiologies. It is worth noting that the neurosurgery department of Boston Children’s serves a diverse patient population, including referrals from other institutions and various geographical regions, making this cohort uniquely representative.

Additionally, because the model used for intracranial segmentation and volume calculation requires a T2-weighted MRI, several patients without MRI scans within two weeks from surgery were excluded. However, important baseline characteristics of included participants did not differ from the excluded participants - except for age at surgery, which differed due to the study selection criteria.

For real-world implementation, our findings will require external validation with varying access to neuroimaging, as the predictive model’s performance may differ in centers where MRI availability is limited or where imaging protocols vary significantly from our standardized approach. Our requirement for MRI within two weeks before surgery, while ensuring measurement accuracy, also highlights the dynamic nature of infant hydrocephalus. Progressive ventricular enlargement between imaging and surgery could potentially affect the predictive value of CSF measurements. Potential temporal changes between imaging and surgery represent additional limitations that should be considered in future prospective studies.

Conclusions

The current study introduces, for the first time, the CSFV z-score as an important factor in predicting the outcome for ETV/CPC and, in concert with corrected age and shunt history, further refines our ability to offer individualized patient care for the treatment of infant hydrocephalus. The volumetric assessment outperformed traditional linear measurement of ventricle size in predicting shunt dependence when adjusted for age at intervention and prior temporary CSF diversion status, providing a foundation for more precise risk stratification in clinical practice.
