In-situ growth of biomimetic ion-selective membranes via confined molecular encapsulation for superior fluoride/chloride separation
Authors: Qian Chen, Mei-Ling Liu, Sheng Jiang, Yu-Tong Zhang, Yue-Wen Jia, Wei-Xing Li, Shi-Peng Sun, Weihong Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiomimetic ion channels demonstrate potential for nanoscale molecular separations by leveraging their unique confined recognition capabilities. Metal-organic framework (MOF)-based mixed matrix membranes (MMMs) offer a promising platform that integrates the ångström-scale pores of MOFs with polymer processability. However, slow MOF nucleation kinetics and weak interfacial interactions impede precise channel formation. Here, we present a confined molecular encapsulation (CME) strategy that synchronously regulates MOF nucleation kinetics and interfacial interactions, transforming precursors into flexible gel-network metal-organic gels (MOGs) via supramolecular assembly. Molecular dynamics simulations and in-situ optical detection show that stronger MOG-polymer interactions and confined diffusion govern enhanced interfacial compatibility and uniform dispersion. Optimized MMMs deliver a F⁻/Cl⁻ separation ratio of 32.0 with ionic current rectification. COMSOL simulations demonstrate that synergistic coupling of aligned MOF arrays and uniform surface charge enables efficient ion differentiation. This CME strategy establishes a versatile nanoscale platform for fabricating high-performance monovalent ion-selective membranes and nanofluidic devices.Subject terms: Chemical engineering, Pollution remediation

Introduction

The precise separation of monovalent ions shows great potential for advancing sustainable nanomaterials, nanobiotechnology, etc.1,2. However, conventional membrane materials lack specific recognition sites and precise transport channels, rendering them ineffective to separate monovalent ions with similar physicochemical properties. A prime example is the challenging separation of fluoride (F−) and chloride (Cl−) ions. Excessive F− is ubiquitous in groundwater and surface water. It inhibits the production of phosphoryl transfer enzymes in humans, disrupting cellular metabolism, energy conversion, and ultimately compromising health3. The co-occurrence of F− and Cl− in aqueous environments, coupled with their analogous charges and similar ionic radii (F−: 0.133 nm; Cl−: 0.181 nm), further complicates effective separation using traditional membranes.

Nature addresses this challenge through evolved F−-selective biological ion channels. These channels achieve ultrahigh selectivity via phenylalanine residues within ångström-scale pores that specifically bind F−, combined with asymmetric nanostructures and surface charge distributions4. This asymmetry drives ion rectification, which is manifested as nonlinear current responses with distinct forward-reverse bias asymmetry, and this is a hallmark of nanofluidic ionic diodes. Ion rectification modulates ion dehydration energy barriers and transport kinetics, thereby enhancing separation efficiency5,6. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), featuring uniform ångström-scale channels and tunable functional groups7, offer an ideal platform for constructing biomimetic ion filters capable of selective ion recognition8. MOF channels can be analogized to the selective filters in biological ion channels. Through the rational introduction of specific functional groups onto the channel walls, the surface charge distribution and chemical environment can be precisely modulated, emulating the chemical interactions in biological channels to achieve ion recognition and selective transport. Furthermore, the highly ordered polar sites within MOF channels substantially lower the energy barrier for ion transport, which is in strong agreement with the ultrafast transport mechanism of biological ion channels. Typically, such bioinspired channels are fabricated via in-situ MOF crystallization within artificial nanochannels9–12. While yielding excellent separation performance, this approach is inherently limited to nanoscale fabrication areas3,13 and often requires complex etching processes that hinder scalability14. Incorporating MOFs into polymers to form mixed matrix membranes (MMMs) presents a promising route towards scalable biomimetic ion channels15,16. However, MOF agglomeration, discontinuous distribution, and poor interfacial compatibility within polymer matrices remain significant obstacles. These issues result in underutilization of their sub-nanometer channels and limited ion separation performance, with target ion separation ratios typically below 517.

Molecular encapsulation, an emerging strategy involving the entrapment and stimulus-triggered release of active components18,19, inspired our approach. Encapsulating MOF precursors within a polymer solution could enable in-situ MOF growth upon heating to form MMMs. Yet, a critical mismatch exists that involves polymer solidification kinetics typically far outpacing MOF crystallization. Premature membrane formation restricts MOF growth, leading to non-uniform dispersion. Metal-organic gels (MOGs), sharing MOFs’ chemical composition but forming interconnected 3D gel networks rather than discrete particle20,21, offer a solution. Their continuous network better mimics biological ion channels, facilitating efficient ion transport. MOG gelation arises from solvent encapsulation within nanoparticle networks, yielding a non-flowing gel state. Precise control over the nucleation-growth kinetic equilibrium enables the structural transition from MOFs to MOGs. Crucially, synchronizing MOF-to-MOG transformation with in-situ growth within polymers is key to constructing functional biomimetic channels.

Herein, we propose a confined molecular encapsulation (CME) strategy. Unlike conventional methods that physically blend pre-synthesized MOFs into polymers, our CME strategy enables in-situ MOF formation within the polymer matrix by regulating MOF nucleation. This process transforms MOF crystallites into a flexible gel-network-structured MOG through supramolecular interactions. This synergistic approach offers dual advantages: (i) streamlined fabrication, by eliminating the need for pre-synthesized MOF particles to allow size-tunable growth during in-situ crystallization, thereby shortening production and enhancing compatibility; and (ii) kinetic synchronization, achieved by matching the rapid MOG nucleation kinetics with the polymer solidification rates to ensure a uniform, continuous MOF distribution. The polymer matrix provides nanoconfinement for MOF growth while suppressing mesopore formation in MOGs during solvent evaporation. The resulting ordered MOF dispersion forms ion channels exhibiting rectification and F−-specific transport capabilities (Fig. 1a). This CME strategy unlocks the scalable construction of biomimetic ion channels within MMMs, holding potential for smart separation of critical ions with high similarity.

Results

In-situ fabrication of biomimetic ion channel membranes using MOG precursors

Conventional preparation of MOF-based MMMs typically involves dispersing pre-synthesized MOF particles into a polymer casting solution. However, preliminary trials incorporating synthesized UiO-66-NH₂ (U6N) powder into PVDF solution resulted in severe precipitation (Supplementary Fig. 1). Laser irradiation of this solution revealed distinct light scattering, confirming poor dispersion of the MOF crystals within the polymer matrix22. To overcome this limitation, we employed an in-situ synthesis approach by incorporating UiO-66-NH₂ precursors, specifically MOF precursors and MOG precursors, directly into the PVDF casting solution (Fig. 1b). The resulting membranes are denoted as PVDF-U6N and PVDF-U6N-gel, respectively. The key difference between the precursor solutions lies in the Zr salt, with ZrCl₄ used for the MOF precursor and ZrOCl₂·8H₂O for the MOG precursor. This substitution perturbs the MOF coordination environment and facilitates a sol-gel transition through dynamic reorganization of ligand-metal bonds23. Crucially, both precursor-PVDF solutions exhibited a Tyndall effect upon laser irradiation (Fig. 1c), which indicates molecular-level mixing and uniform dispersion. This observation stands in stark contrast to the outcome of the post-synthesis addition method.

Heating the casting solutions with two different UiO-66-NH₂ precursors at 120 °C for 30 min revealed distinct behaviors between them. The solution containing MOF precursors retained significant fluidity, while the MOG precursor solution transformed into a stable, non-flowing gel, as verified by solution inversion tests21,23. Notably, despite the polymer mass exceeding that of the precursors, the PVDF coexisted with and did not disrupt the formation of the MOG network. This observation points to strong supramolecular interactions and excellent compatibility between the MOG and PVDF24. X-ray diffraction (XRD, peak at 7.8°) and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (band at 1571 cm⁻¹) confirmed the in-situ growth of UiO-66-NH₂ from both types of precursors within the polymer matrix (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c)25,26.

Despite successful in-situ growth, the distribution and morphology of the resulting MOF/MOG within the polymer matrix differed markedly. SEM imaging (Fig. 1d) revealed surface defects and significant particle agglomeration in the cross-section of the PVDF-U6N membrane. These defects likely stem from poor interfacial compatibility between the MOF precursors and PVDF, potentially aggravated by the hygroscopic nature of ZrCl₄27. In contrast, the PVDF-U6N-gel membrane displays uniform nanoparticle distribution throughout both its surface and cross-section. Further corroborating these interfacial differences, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of the Zr 3d region (Fig. 1e) and stress-strain curves (Supplementary Fig. 2d) were performed. The PVDF-U6N-gel membrane demonstrated higher tensile strength than PVDF-U6N, likely attributable to enhanced interfacial bonding facilitated by the soft gel material. Notably, the Zr 3d binding energy in PVDF-U6N-gel showed a more pronounced shift relative to pure UiO-66-NH₂ powder compared to the shift observed in PVDF-U6N. These spectral shifts indicate polymer-induced alterations in Zr coordination and stronger interfacial interactions between PVDF and the MOG-derived material28. Collectively, these findings highlight distinct crystallization pathways and interfacial compatibilities for the MOF and MOG precursors within the PVDF matrix.

To probe the underlying intermolecular interactions, we conducted molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). The mobility of the precursors was characterized by their mean square displacement (MSD)29. The MOG precursor exhibited a progressively decelerating diffusion rate, indicative of sub-diffusive behavior (Fig. 1f). This sub-diffusive phenomenon signifies that the MOG precursor experiences restricted motion within the polymer network, reflecting a molecular-level confinement effect that precedes and directs the subsequent crystallization process. Conversely, the MOF precursor maintained a consistently high MSD growth rate, characteristic of free diffusion. This divergence in dynamic behavior arises from fundamental differences in precursor-polymer binding strength. A key factor underlying this divergence is the significantly lower (more negative) calculated interaction energy between the MOG precursor and PVDF than that for the MOF precursor (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 3c), signifying a stronger binding affinity for PVDF. This enhanced affinity originates predominantly from van der Waals (vdW) interactions, with electrostatic contributions playing a secondary role. Specifically, the positive vdW force component between the MOF precursor and PVDF denotes repulsion, while the strongly negative vdW component observed with the MOG precursor signifies a net attraction. Furthermore, a higher number of stable hydrogen bonds was observed between the MOG precursor and PVDF (Supplementary Fig. 3d), providing an additional mechanism for interfacial stabilization. These distinct interaction profiles profoundly influenced precursor distribution and spatial organization at the molecular level. The MOG precursor exhibited a shorter spatial movement range and a more restricted spatial path, confirming its confined diffusion state. Analysis of concentration profiles (Fig. 1h) revealed that the MOF precursor concentration fluctuated considerably with distance, whereas the MOG precursor concentration profile was markedly smoother and more uniform. Additionally, the higher average concentration and lower variance of the MOG precursor within the simulation box indicate a more homogeneous distribution15. These simulation results conclusively demonstrate that the CME, enabled by MOG-polymer supramolecular interactions, promotes superior dispersion homogeneity and interfacial compatibility within the polymer matrix. The stronger binding and restricted mobility collectively create a confinement effect that templates the subsequent formation of a continuous and well-dispersed MOF structure during the in-situ growth process.

Comparison of crystallization behavior between MOFs and MOGs

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying the distinct growth behaviors of MOF and MOG within the polymer matrix, we investigated their crystallization processes separately in polymer-free systems. As depicted in Fig. 2a, UiO-66-NH₂ crystals derived from ZrCl₄ (MOF precursor) exhibited slow nucleation and growth kinetics. No visible crystal formation occurred within the initial 30 min of heating. Even after 12 h, characteristic XRD peaks remained faint, consistent with literature reports indicating that ZrCl4-mediated UiO-66-NH2 synthesis typically requires ~24 h for complete crystallization (Supplementary Fig. 2b)30. In contrast, MOG precursors display markedly accelerated crystallization. Characteristic UiO-66-NH2 XRD peaks emerged within 30 min of heating, exhibiting broadened diffraction peaks (Fig. 2b). The crystal structure remained stable upon further heating, with no observable structural changes31. These results confirm that the MOG precursor enables rapid and stable MOF crystallization within 30 min25.

We further quantified the crystallization kinetics of both precursors under solvothermal conditions using turbidity measurements30. MOG precursors initiated rapid nucleation immediately upon heating, exhibiting a high kinetic rate of 3.8 × 10−3 min−1 within the first hour. Nucleation predominantly concluded within the first hour, as evidenced by the subsequent decline in nucleation rate (Supplementary Table 1). Conversely, MOF precursors exhibited a significant induction period, initiating nucleation only after ~2 h. Collectively, comprehensive physicochemical characterization confirms that despite the accelerated kinetics enabled by the MOG precursor, the resulting UiO-66-NH₂ crystals retain their inherent structure, thermal stability, and sub-nanometer channels. (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Fig. 2d).

Controllable MOG growth within MMMs and in-situ growth mechanism

Conventional MMMs fabrication relies on embedding pre-synthesized MOF particles as static fillers, resulting in fixed particle sizes. In contrast, our CME strategy enables simultaneous MOF growth and polymer membrane formation, allowing linear modulation of MOF particle size within the MMMs. Precursor concentration governs crystallization kinetics, wherein higher concentrations reduce growth rates to yield larger particles32. Increasing the MOG precursor loading from 20 to 40 wt% systematically enlarged MOF particles from ~200 nm to ~1600 nm (Fig. 3a–c and Supplementary Fig. 5a), a dimensional transition also corroborated by TEM (Supplementary Fig. 5b). At 10 wt% MOG loading, distinct MOF nanoparticles were absent. Conversely, at 50 wt%, particle agglomeration occurred due to inadequate polymer confinement of excessive precursors (Supplementary Fig. 6). Notably, MOF precursors consistently exhibited disordered agglomeration across all loadings (Supplementary Fig. 7), unlike their MOG counterparts. Physicochemical characterization of MMMs with varying MOG content confirmed that PVDF does not alter the crystal structure or chemical stability of in-situ-grown MOFs (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9 and Supplementary Table 2).

Energy-dispersive X-ray analysis further elucidated the growth mechanism by mapping F (PVDF polymer) and Zr (U6N-gel nanoparticles) distributions. Crucially, the signal intensities of these elements showed no inverse correlation (Supplementary Fig. 10)33. This observation is a deviation from literature reports on conventionally prepared MMMs. This distinct behavior arises from the CME approach, which first achieves uniform molecular-level precursor dispersion prior to thermally induced crystallization. TEM imaging (Fig. 3d) showed ~200 nm nanoparticles uniformly dispersed within the polymer matrix. Higher magnification identified uniformly distributed 2–5 nm particles, likely nascent metal clusters preceding MOF crystal formation. Based on these observations, we propose the following in-situ growth mechanism: (i) the high electronegativity of fluorine in PVDF facilitates strong bonding with Zr⁴⁺ ions28,34, stabilizing uniformly dispersed metal clusters. While such small clusters inherently tend to aggregate due to high surface energy, polymer confinement maintains their dispersion; (ii) subsequent heating provides energy for progressive crystallization, enabling controlled growth into MOFs within the PVDF-U6N-gel membrane. Higher precursor concentrations promote cluster aggregation during crystallization35,36, explaining the observed size increase with MOG loading. Digital photography confirmed the PVDF-U6N-gel membrane possesses uniform surface morphology (Fig. 3e). Combined with the method’s simplicity, this highlights its potential scalability.

Mechanistic basis for ordered assembly

MOG precursors achieve ordered assembly within the polymer matrix, while MOF precursors yield disordered structures. This disparity originates from two key factors. These factors are detailed as follows: (i) kinetic synchronization: MOG nucleation is rapid and aligns with the swift polymer membrane formation process, which is evidenced by mass loss primarily occurring within the first 30 min of heating (Supplementary Fig. 11). This synchronization provides abundant nucleation seeds for subsequent ordered growth within the consolidating polymer matrix. In contrast, MOF precursors exhibit slow nucleation kinetics, initiating only after ~2 h. By this time, complete polymer matrix consolidation and exhaustive DMF solvent evaporation create an unfavorable microenvironment for subsequent ordered MOF growth. (ii) Gel-network templating: The MOG structure formed upon heating comprises interconnected MOF nanoparticles via supramolecular interactions. This pre-formed, ordered nanoparticle array acts as a template, directing the alignment of subsequently growing MOF crystals and enhancing overall structural order (Fig. 3f)37,38. To further validate the in-situ growth mechanism and confinement effect of the CME strategy, real-time observation revealed distinct crystallization dynamics between the two precursor systems (Supplementary Movies 1 and 2 and Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). Consistent with the hypothesis, MOF precursors tend to agglomerate in polymer solutions with slow nucleation and growth, while MOG precursors disperse uniformly with rapid nucleation and growth. Temperature-dependent experiments demonstrated gradual activation of the confinement effect with elevated temperatures, directly corroborating the precise regulation of the CME strategy over in-situ MOF growth (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Ion transport in biomimetic ion channel membranes

Ion transport characteristics of the MMMs were evaluated using linear sweep voltammetry to obtain current-voltage (I–V) curves (Fig. 4a)28,39. The PVDF-U6N membrane exhibited negligible separation capability for F− and Cl− ions. In contrast, PVDF-U6N-gel demonstrated pronounced ion selectivity coupled with distinct rectification behavior (Fig. 4b). Control experiments using pure PVDF (0% MOG) confirmed its inability to separate F⁻ and Cl⁻ (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Table 3), indicating that the separation capability originates exclusively from the in-situ formed MOF gel-network within the polymer matrix. Increasing the MOG loading to 10 wt% elevated the F⁻/Cl⁻ selectivity to 3.90. This initial selectivity likely stems from selective F⁻ binding to Zr metal clusters and amino groups within UiO-66-NH₂, facilitating preferential F⁻ transport (Fig. 4e)3,8. At 20 wt% MOG loading, the F⁻/Cl⁻ selectivity reached 32.0, accompanied by clear ion rectification (Fig. 4c, d). This rectification arises from two critical conditions, namely sub-nanometer confinement and structural/charge asymmetry within the channels. However, further increasing the MOG loading to 30 wt% diminished the rectification effect and reduced the selectivity to 2.18. At 40 wt%, ion conductance increased rapidly while F−/Cl− selectivity became virtually undetectable.

The parabolic trend in separation performance originates from variations in nanoparticle-polymer interfacial compatibility. At low loadings (10 wt%), the inability of MOF to fully penetrate the membrane hinders continuous ion transport within the MOF pores. This inhibits ion rectification and reduces ion separation performance. Excessive MOG loadings (>30 wt%) overwhelm the polymer’s confining capacity, inducing interfacial defects that disrupt sub-nanometer channel integrity and impair ion separation. This is corroborated by the sharp rise in ionic conductance (Fig. 4e). In addition, incorporating MOFs significantly enhances the hydrophilicity of MMMs40. Pristine PVDF was hydrophobic with a contact angle of 109.3°, and increasing MOG loading progressively improved surface hydrophilicity (Supplementary Fig. 15). The enhanced ion separation and rectification of PVDF-U6N-gel over PVDF-U6N directly result from its uniform MOF distribution within the MOG network, contrasting sharply with the agglomeration and defects prevalent in PVDF-U6N.

Low-field NMR analysis further elucidated the structural basis for this performance difference (Fig. 4f). Larger T₂ relaxation times correspond to larger pore sizes. Peaks in the T₂ range of 10−2 to 10⁰ signify sub-nanometer channels, while those between 10⁰ and 10³ indicate nanometer-sized pores41,42. PVDF-U6N exhibited an additional peak in the 100–103 range absent in PVDF-U6N-gel, revealing the presence of extraneous nanometer-sized channels. These channels likely arise from defects caused by poor interfacial compatibility between the MOF precursor and PVDF. Although occupying minimal area, such defects critically compromise sub-nanometer ion separation.

COMSOL simulations were performed to model cation and anion transport through nanochannels featuring uniform versus non-uniform MOF distributions (Supplementary Fig. 16 and Supplementary Table 4), revealing that structural order is a prerequisite for generating effective ion transport (Fig. 5a). For the disordered PVDF-U6N membrane, concentration maps showed minimal changes in cation/anion concentration under varying voltages or electrolytes (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Figs. 17a, b)43. Despite the inherent positive surface charge of UiO-66-NH2 nanoparticles, their chaotic arrangement creates disordered nanopores and spatially inhomogeneous charge distributions. Within such a discontinuous network, the local surface charges cannot synergistically act to create a macroscopic, directionally dependent driving force for ions. Instead, their effects are localized and cancel each other out on a macroscopic scale, failing to establish a significant energy barrier difference for ion transport in opposite directions (Fig. 5d, e). Consequently, current density remained symmetric at ±2 V, confirming the absence of rectification. The lack of effective interaction limited the KF/KCl separation selectivity to around 2 (Fig. 5f).

In contrast, the ordered MOF array within PVDF-U6N-gel induced ion redistribution. Applying +2 V increased Cl− concentration within the nanochannels from 10 mM to a peak of 23 mM, while K⁺ concentration decreased from 10 to 4 mM (Fig. 5c). The distinct variation in cation/anion concentrations stems from the positively charged UiO-66-NH2 surface44,45. Under a positive bias, the aligned charges generate a directional field that electrostatically repels K⁺ ions (increasing their transport barrier) and attracts Cl− ions (decreasing their transport barrier). This resulted in K⁺ accumulation below and Cl− depletion above the membrane (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 17d). Reversing the voltage polarity inverted ion movement and concentration profiles, demonstrating anion-selective transport. The voltage-dependent asymmetry in ion concentration, rooted in the modulation of ion transport barriers by the continuous MOF network, constitutes the physical origin of the observed ion rectification. As the electrolyte solution switches from KCl to KF, the diffusion rate of F− becomes faster than that of Cl− due to the specific interaction between F− and UiO-66-NH2. The concentration of F− in the PVDF-U6N-gel nanochannels reaches the highest point of 133 mM, nearly six times higher than the peak concentration of Cl− (Supplementary Fig. 17c–f). This marked difference confirms the formation of a preferential transport pathway for F− within the MOF nanochannels, governed by its specific binding sites. Adsorption tests confirmed UiO-66-NH₂’s preferential adsorption for F− over Cl− or K⁺ (Supplementary Fig. 18), attributed to F− binding to μ₃-OH groups of Zr₆ clusters and Zr₆ metal site3,46. The broader F⁻ enrichment region created a steeper concentration gradient, thereby establishing a stronger driving force for net ion flux. This enhanced driving force under positive bias directly manifested as higher current densities. The rectification ratios in KCl and KF solutions reached 2.95 and 3.10, respectively. Due to the higher concentration of F− in the nanochannels, the current density in KF is 32.38 times that in KCl (Fig. 5f). This agreement between the simulated and experimental current-voltage characteristics confirms the consistency between the simulation and experimental observations (Fig. 4c). Further investigation confirmed that ion transport within the membrane is also influenced by electrolyte concentration and pH (Supplementary Fig. 19).

The pH-responsive behavior of MOF components within the MMMs, characteristic of biomimetic ion channels, enhances their functional versatility. Comparative analysis (Supplementary Table 5) demonstrates that the CME-fabricated MMMs achieve enhanced performance, as they balance high separation accuracy with fabrication simplicity. Overall, the uniform MOF distribution within the polymer matrix enables homogeneous surface charge distribution, essential for ion rectification and selective transport. MOF precursor-fabricated membranes exhibit poor separation due to MOF agglomeration and poor interfacial compatibility. These issues cause discontinuous channels and defects that guide ions to non-selective migration. CME-fabricated membranes with ordered MOFs achieve excellent separation performance through the synergistic effect of specific binding capability and continuous, uniform sub-nanometer channel distribution.

Discussion

This work develops MMMs with biomimetic ion channels through synchronized MOF-to-MOG transformation during polymer membrane formation, achieving a F−/Cl− separation factor of 32.0. Rapid MOG nucleation within the PVDF confinement facilitates linear modulation of MOF size (200–1600 nm) by simply adjusting MOG content (20–40 wt%). Real-time in-situ optical microscopy visually corroborates this rapid and homogeneous nucleation of the MOG precursor, in stark contrast to the sparse and aggregated growth observed from MOF precursors. MD simulations elucidate the confinement effect at the molecular level, where stronger van der Waals attractions and hydrogen bonding between MOG precursors and PVDF restrict precursor mobility and template a uniform distribution of nucleation sites. COMSOL simulations elucidate the transport behavior by showing that aligned MOF arrays create a directional driving force, which in turn drives the asymmetric ion redistribution responsible for ion-selective differentiation. Collectively, this study establishes a strategy for constructing bioinspired ion channels within MMMs, opening avenues for next-generation smart ion-selective membranes and nanofluidic devices.

Methods

Membrane preparation

The pure PVDF casting solution was prepared by dissolving 2 × g of PVDF in 10 × g of DMF, stirring until homogeneous, and degassing for 12 h. The MOG precursor was synthesized by dissolving ZrOCl₂·8H₂O and 2-NH₂-BDC in DMF at equimolar ratios. The MMMs casting solution was obtained by combining the MOG precursor with the PVDF casting solution. The MOF concentration within the polymer matrix was adjusted by varying the precursor content (see Supplementary Information for details). For membrane fabrication, the casting solution was spread onto a glass plate using a 200 µm scraper and heated at 120 °C. After cooling, the membrane was peeled off in deionized water, washed with ethanol and water, and stored for future use (PVDF-U6N-gel). The non-gel MOF version (PVDF-U6N) was prepared by substituting ZrOCl₂·8H₂O with ZrCl₄ in the precursor solution.

Ion transport test

The ion transport capability of the membrane for different ions was tested by measuring the current-voltage curve on an electrochemical workstation (CS2350M, Corrtest). The membrane was sealed between the two chambers of an H-type electrolytic cell and fixed with clips. The two chambers were each filled with 25 mL of test salt solution at different concentrations, and a transmembrane voltage was applied using Ag/AgCl electrodes.
