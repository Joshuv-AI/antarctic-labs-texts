The comparative evaluation for the efficacy of different remineralization agents on initial caries lesion
Authors: Eylul Ozgel, Cigdem Atalayin Ozkaya, Guliz Armagan, Huseyin Tezel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the effectiveness of different remineralization agents on artificially initial caries created teeth.MethodThe bovine teeth crowns were divided into four equal-parts, and initial measurements (microhardness, micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT), scanning electron microscopy-energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS)) were recorded from each specimen (n = 6). Then the samples were subjected to a demineralization procedure for four-days, with the daily refreshed solution. After the demineralization process, second measurements were performed. In the experimental group, the products containing fluoride (Colgate Sensation White-Colgate Palmolive), hydroxyapatite (HAP) (Kinder Karex-Dr. Wolff), and casein phosphopeptide-amorphous calcium phosphate (CPP-ACP) (Tooth Mousse-GC) were applied twice daily for 7 days, for 3 min each time. The control group specimens were only stored in artificial saliva. The final measurements were performed after the remineralization procedure.ResultsThe initial microhardness, total mineral density (BMD), atomic % Ca, and P values of the groups did not show any statistical significant difference. The demineralization process resulted in a decrease in microhardness, BMD, Ca and P values (p < 0.05). The remineralization process led to an increase in microhardness and BMD values in all groups (p < 0.05). No statistically significant difference in microhardness values was detected when comparing the initial and remineralization measurements. A significant increase in BMD values was observed only in the HAP group when comparing the initial and remineralization measurements (p < 0.05). The remineralization process resulted in varied patterns and shapes of closures and accumulations on the enamel surface, which differed among the groups.ConclusionThe activity of the agents was observed as variable according to the parameters examined in the analyses. Considering the in vitro design of the study, further studies and clinical observations were suggested as necessary to clarify the action mechanism and differences of these agents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08688-7.Keywords: Fluoride, Casein phosphopeptide-amorphous calcium phosphate, Hydroxyapatite, Remineralization, Demineralization

Introduction

The development of caries is a complex and dynamic process involving the interplay of multiple factors and it is characterized by a continuous balance between demineralization and remineralization [1]. The initial enamel caries lesions are considered reversible, as mineral loss occurs predominantly in the subsurface layers while the integrity of the enamel surface remains intact [2]. At this stage, the reversible lesion can be remineralized, restoring the tooth structure to a healthy state. This remineralization process requires the buffering capacity of saliva and the presence of remineralizing agents [3]. The prevention of dental caries necessitates a collaborative approach between the patient and the dental professional. Within this process, the dentist implements various preventive measures aimed at inhibiting caries initiation and reducing risk. However, these interventions alone are insufficient for complete caries prevention; the patient’s adherence to daily and/or regular supportive home care is equally critical. For this purpose, a range of oral care products, most commonly fluoride-containing toothpastes, are widely utilized [3]. Although fluoride has long been the primary preventive agent against caries, it seems as insufficient on its own in high-risk individuals and its use is limited in children. Therefore, there is a need for new safe strategies and agents that can support or serve as alternatives to fluoride [4].

The effectiveness of remineralization agents is typically assessed through the parameters such as mineral uptake, increases in surface microhardness, improvements in crystal structure, and modulation of the oral biofilm [5]. The comparative studies demonstrate that each agent possesses unique advantages and limitations. The remineralization-based strategies form the foundation of preventive dentistry, offering significant biological and economic benefits by reducing the need for invasive interventions. Accordingly, the research comparing the efficacy of different remineralization agents is increasingly important. In recent years, biomimetic approaches have gained attention, emphasizing the need for agents that closely mimic natural biological systems, provide optimal efficacy and suitable for use across all age groups [6–8]. Since calcium phosphate compounds constitute the primary mineral phase of teeth, calcium phosphate-based agents used in oral care are regarded as biomimetic materials or approaches. Casein phosphopeptide-amorphous calcium phosphate (CPP-ACP), a milk-derived protein complex, has been employed for remineralization and caries prevention, with numerous studies supporting its effectiveness in this context [9–11]. Another remineralization agent, hydroxyapatite, has main potential applications including the remineralization of early carious lesions and the repair of minor enamel defects [12]. Owing to its close resemblance to natural enamel and its ability to be synthesized in various particle sizes, hydroxyapatite is particularly emphasized for repairing initial caries lesions and providing protection against acid challenges. In toothpaste formulations, hydroxyapatite increases the concentration of calcium and phosphate ions on the tooth surface and in saliva, serving as a reservoir that elevates local ion saturation [13–15]. Hydroxyapatite also forms a strong bond with enamel and dentin surfaces, creating a protective layer against acid attacks and erosive lesions [16]. Microradiographic studies have confirmed the efficacy of hydroxyapatite-containing toothpastes in remineralizing deep enamel lesions, while also highlighting the need for further research in this area [17]. Several studies have compared various agents containing CPP-ACP or hydroxyapatite with fluoride-based agents, which are considered the gold standard for remineralization [17, 18]. The studies concurrently and comparatively examining CPP-ACP, hydroxyapatite, and fluoride remain limited and this study has been designed to contribute to the literature in this regard.

The aim of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of different oral care products containing the active ingredients as fluoride, CPP-ACP and hydroxyapatite in remineralization of initial caries lesions via different test methods. The null hypothesis of the study was that all three agents have the same effect on the remineralization of artificial initial caries lesions compared to the baseline values.

Materials and methods

Preparation of the specimens

This study was approved by the Ege University Experimental Animals Local Ethics Committee (dated 29.06.2022 and 2022-048 permission number). The bovine incisors obtained from the same breeding farm and slaughterhouse were utilized. Teeth were carefully examined for visible defects such as caries, fractures, cracks, discoloration, or hypomineralization, and only those without any defects were included in the study. The teeth were mechanically cleaned with hand instruments to remove residual soft tissues, without any additional polishing procedures, in order to preserve the enamel surface characteristics.

Six bovine incisors were used for each experimental procedure (n = 6). The sample size was determined based on previously published in vitro studies with similar experimental designs [19, 20]. The bovine incisors were sectioned mesio-distally and inciso-gingivally under water cooling using a diamond disc. Four segments (mesio-incisal, disto-incisal, mesio-cervical, disto-cervical) were obtained from each tooth crown. Accordingly, a total the specimens were prepared to be distributed among three experimental groups and one control group for each test. Each segment was randomly assigned to one of the groups. The specimens were stored in 0.1% thymol solution at + 4 °C until further experimental procedures. For standardized positioning during analysis, each specimen was embedded in a standard 15 × 15 mm C-type silicone mold (Zhermack, Italy) with the vestibular enamel surface exposed.

The enamel surface microhardness, micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT), scanning electron microscopy-energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) analysis were performed following the induction of initial carious lesions through demineralization and subsequent remineralization using three different oral care products containing fluoride, hydroxyapatite, and CPP-ACP as active components. Demineralization procedure

Following baseline measurements, all specimens were subjected to a demineralization procedure. The demineralization solution, consisting of 2.0 mmol/L calcium, 2.0 mmol/L phosphate, and 75 mmol/L acetate in 40 mL at pH 4.3, was applied to the samples for 4 days [2]. The solution was refreshed daily to maintain consistent demineralizing conditions.

The experimental groups received fluoride (Colgate Sensation White, Colgate Palmolive, Poland), hydroxyapatite (HAP) (Kinder Karex, Dr. Kurt Wolff, Germany), or CPP-ACP (Tooth Mousse, GC, Japan). Each agent was applied twice daily (at 10:00 AM and 10:00 PM) for 3 min per session over a period of 7 days. After each application, agents were removed from the enamel surface using cotton pellets, followed by gentle brushing under running water for 10 s with a soft toothbrush to ensure complete removal of residual agents. The specimens were then stored in artificial saliva until the next application, with the saliva solution being renewed after each treatment. The artificial saliva was prepared according to the following composition: 0.7 mmol/L CaCl₂, 0.2 mmol/L MgCl₂, 4 mmol/L KH₂PO₄, 30 mmol/L KCl, and 20 mmol/L HEPES buffer at pH 7.0 [21, 22]. For the control group (the fourth segment of each tooth), no agents were applied, and samples were maintained solely in artificial saliva, which was refreshed daily.

Microhardness analysis

The specimens, embedded in standard molds, were positioned on the stage of the microhardness tester with their upper and lower surfaces parallel to ensure proper alignment of the specimen surface. Measurements were performed at baseline, after demineralization, and following the remineralization (agent application) processes. For each specimen, three measurements were taken within a standardized 3 × 4 mm window area on the surface, and the mean value was recorded for the respective sample. The Vickers diamond indenter was applied to the specimen surface with a load of 100 g for 15 s (Shimadzu Mod HVM-2, Japan). The indentations produced by the diamond tip were measured on a computer using software integrated with a microhardness device and a digital camera (Kameram CMOS122 CU, Turkey).

Micro-CT analysis

The measurements were performed using a micro-CT device (Skyscan 1172, USA) at 88 kV and 112 µA, with 0.6° rotational steps, a random movement value of 20, and an average frame of 5. An Al + Cu filter was used, and the scans were recorded at 1 K resolution by dividing the images into 668 rows and 1000 columns. A pixel size of 25.90 μm was employed for the micro-CT imaging. The scans were conducted with the sample-to-X-ray source distance set to 256 mm and the camera-to-X-ray source distance set to 345.946 mm. The exposure time for the samples was set to 850 ms. Prior to scanning, all samples were mounted in a standardized orientation to ensure consistent positioning. The imaging was conducted using identical scanning parameters for all specimens, including voltage, current, voxel size, and exposure time, to maintain measurement reliability. The region of interest (ROI) was defined using reproducible anatomical reference points to ensure consistent localization across all samples. A standardized volumetric area extending from the outer enamel surface toward the subsurface lesion was selected to capture mineral density variations associated with demineralization and remineralization processes. ROI dimensions were kept constant for all specimens to minimize operator-dependent variability. To further enhance standardization, ROI selection and measurements were performed using the same analysis protocol for each specimen. The optimum care was taken to avoid edge artifacts and non-representative areas during segmentation. This methodological approach was adopted to improve reproducibility and enable accurate comparison of mineral density changes between groups.

The analyses of the specimens were performed using the CTAn software. Micro-CT scans were carried out in three stages, based on a before-and-after imaging principle: the initial state before any treatment, after demineralization and after the application of remineralization agents. For the calculation of the total mineral density (BMD) of the specimens, the device was calibrated with phantom references using the exact measurement parameters described above. Following the initial, post-demineralization, and post-remineralization scans, BMD analyses were performed on predefined regions, and the density values were expressed in g/cm³.

SEM-EDS analysis

For the SEM analysis of the specimens, critical point drying was employed to ensure proper dehydration of the samples, compatibility with the vacuum system of the device, and optimal surface imaging. The drying process was performed using an Automated Critical Point Dryer (Leica EM CPD300, Germany.) In this process, the water within the specimens was replaced with liquid CO₂ under pressure. An intermediate exchange fluid, such as ethanol or acetone, was used to facilitate this replacement. Subsequently, the liquid CO₂ was transitioned to its gaseous phase, completing the drying process.

The sputter coating was intentionally not performed, as the same specimens were evaluated longitudinally at baseline, after demineralization, and following remineralization. The application of a conductive coating could potentially cause irreversible alterations to the enamel surface and interfere with repeated measurements across different time points. To preserve the native surface morphology and enable reliable longitudinal assessment, imaging was conducted on non-coated specimens under conditions suitable for uncoated biological samples. This approach minimized coating-related surface artifacts and allowed repeated evaluation without introducing additional surface modifications [23].The dried specimens were then placed in the SEM (Thermo Scientific Apreo S, USA), and images were obtained at magnifications of 10,000×, 5,000×, and 1,000×, with an accelerating voltage of 20 kV and an average working distance of 17 mm. EDS was performed on the acquired images under a 20 kV accelerating voltage (Thermo Scientific Apreo S, USA). Using the EDS detector, the weight percentages of elements from specific regions of the enamel surface were calculated. The atomic percentage changes of Ca and P ions were determined for each specimen at three different time points: baseline, post-demineralization, and post-remineralization.

Statistical analyses

For statistical analysis, GraphPad Prism for Windows (version 5.05; GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) was used. All analyses were conducted using a 95% confidence level (p < 0.05). Each tooth crown was sectioned into four parts and each section was assigned to a different treatment group in order to reduce variability between teeth. In this design, tooth sections were considered the experimental units, while the use of sections derived from the same tooth served as a blocking strategy to control for potential inter-tooth variability. For comparisons based on the treatment variable, two-way ANOVA followed by the Bonferroni post hoc test was applied. For intergroup comparisons at each stage (baseline, demineralization, and remineralization), one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used.

A post hoc power analysis was performed based on the observed effect sizes obtained from the study data. Using a significance level of α = 0.05 and a sample size of n = 6 per group, the calculated statistical power ranged between 0.95 and 0.99, indicating a high ability of the study to detect statistically significant differences.

Discussion

The calcium phosphate compounds, as the principal constituents of dental hard tissues, are regarded as biomimetic agents. In this context, CPP-ACP and hydroxyapatite are notable for their roles in caries prevention and remineralization [10–12]. The literature predominantly compares these agents with fluoride, whereas studies simultaneously evaluating all three components remain limited [17, 18]. In light of these considerations, the present study was designed to evaluate the remineralization potential of oral care products with different active components as fluoride, hydroxyapatite and CPP-ACP on artificially induced carious lesions via microhardness, micro-CT, and SEM-EDS analyses. In the present study, no statistically significant differences were detected among the tested agents in the intergroup comparisons, indicating comparable overall remineralization efficacy. However, significant improvements and changed profiles observed within groups compared with the demineralized baseline confirm the variety for agents in the promotion of the mineral recovery. Additionally, SEM analysis revealed distinct morphological patterns of mineral deposition among the treatment modalities. Therefore, while the quantitative findings partially suggest similar remineralization effectiveness, the observed diversities and qualitative SEM observations indicate differences in the structural characteristics of the remineralization process, leading to a partial acceptance and partial rejection of the null hypothesis.

The enamel samples obtained from human teeth may contain numerous biological variables, such as genetic differences, nutritional conditions, and environmental factors. Therefore, the enamel samples obtained from bovine teeth are preferred and recommended for remineralization and caries activity studies [24, 25]. In this study, the bovine incisors obtained from the same farm and slaughterhouse were used to minimize potential variability and ensure similar nutritional and living conditions. The lack of differences among initial measurements indicates the standardization of the samples before group assignment and experimental procedures. The storage medium and duration between tooth extraction and testing can influence the results. The common storage media includes distilled water and saline, sometimes supplemented with antimicrobials such as thymol, formalin, or chloramine. The storage in thymol solution has been reported not to affect enamel micro- or nanohardness. Thus, after mechanical tissue removal, the specimens were stored in 0.1% thymol solution as recommended [26–28]. The specimens were embedded in custom silicone blocks to prevent movement during test procedures. Since dehydration alters enamel microhardness and properties [29]. The samples were stored in distilled water except during remineralization, when they were immersed in artificial saliva.

The microhardness testing is a preferred method for evaluating the physical properties of dental materials and tooth tissues, as it is rapid, practical, and requires only a small surface area [30]. The Vickers loading time (10–30 s) has been shown not to significantly influence enamel or dentin hardness at a given load [31]. İndicating that 10 s is adequate to produce a permanent indentation. The same study also reported no significant effect of different loads (100–300 g) on enamel hardness. Accordingly, a load of 100 g and a dwell time of 15 s were applied in this study. The initial surface hardness values correspond with those previously reported [32, 33]. As demineralization of unabraded enamel has been shown to better simulate natural white spot lesions [34]. The specimens were not abraded before testing. The white spot lesions are associated with reduced microhardness due to subsurface mineral loss and porosity [35]. Consistent with these reports, our study showed a significant decrease in microhardness after demineralization. Following remineralization, all groups exhibited significant increases in microhardness, consistent with earlier findings [33, 36]. Notably, remineralization occurred even in the control group, in agreement with evidence that artificial saliva can promote repair of early lesions [37]. The previous studies have reported complicated microhardness findings regarding differences among remineralization agents. Vyavhare et al. (2015) [36] reported that fluoride and hydroxyapatite produced higher recovery than CPP-ACP and control groups, but without significant differences between fluoride and hydroxyapatite. In our study, although no significant differences were detected between the test groups, the control group showed the smallest increase. A longer treatment duration might have altered these results. Similarly, Kasemkhun & Rirattanapong (2021) [38] reported significantly higher hardness in all test groups compared with controls after 7 days, with fluoride producing the highest values. The differences of this point with our findings may be due to the use of the samples without polishing in our study to mimic natural lesions.

The calibrated micro-CT measurements enables reliable comparison of mineral loss in demineralized enamel [39, 40]. The literature reports that demineralized enamel exhibits significantly lower Ca/P ratios and mineral density compared to sound enamel [41, 42]. Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrated a significant decrease in mineral density in all groups following demineralization. Various studies using micro-CT have supported the positive effects of toothpastes containing fluoride, CPP-ACP, and hydroxyapatite on remineralization [43–45]. Consistent with these, our study also demonstrated that all tested agents produced a significant increase in BMD values, which had decreased due to demineralization. In a study by Amaechi et al. (2022) [39], it was reported that 14-day applications of fluoride- and hydroxyapatite-containing toothpastes increased mineral density, with hydroxyapatite exhibiting a more pronounced remineralization effect. Similarly, the significant increases in BMD values were observed in all groups after demineralization; however, only the HAP group showed a significant increase compared to baseline values. This finding supports the notion that hydroxyapatite enhances mineral density to a greater extent than other agents. In another study using micro-CT to evaluate the remineralizing effects of three different toothpastes containing CPP-ACP, fluoride, and miswak, no significant differences were observed between groups after application, consistent with the findings of our study [43].

A healthy enamel surface is smooth and homogeneous, whereas following demineralization, its structure is disrupted, resulting in an irregular surface containing voids and numerous microporosities [46]. The studies have shown that demineralized enamel exhibits a rough morphology with numerous craters of varying depths due to prism damage and dissolution of enamel crystals [47, 48]. In this study, consistent with the literature, SEM images after demineralization revealed disruption of enamel prism integrity and the exposure of microporosities. In a previous study [49] the protective effect of a zinc-hydroxyapatite-containing toothpaste was evaluated using SEM analysis by examining enamel surfaces exposed to acidic challenge and comparing them with a standard fluoride toothpaste, a fluoride-free toothpaste, and an untreated control group. The SEM evaluations presented that the zinc-hydroxyapatite toothpaste was superior in protecting the enamel surface compared to the other two toothpastes [49]. Similarly, in this study, SEM analysis revealed that, following remineralization, fluoride and nano-hydroxyapatite treatments effectively and homogeneously sealed the porosities formed on the enamel surface after demineralization, demonstrating notable surface recovery patterns. Another in vitro study was performed to compare the remineralization effects of synthetic hydroxyapatite, ACP, and nano-hydroxyapatite and the SEM imaging showed that nanoparticle forms of hydroxyapatite were not present at baseline enamel surface, indicating that the observed particles originated solely from the added nano-hydroxyapatite [50]. Unlike other forms, nano-hydroxyapatite demonstrated superior adhesion to the enamel surface and remained largely intact even under ultrasonic conditions, suggesting a critical role in bonding. Based on these findings, it was considered that the HAP used in our study, being in nano-hydroxyapatite form, adequately integrated into the enamel structure and formed globular deposits on the surface. Moreover, the persistence of nano-hydroxyapatite particles on the enamel surface even under rigorous conditions underscores its potential for durable remineralization. The comparative in vitro studies evaluating various remineralizing agents reveal nuanced differences in their efficacy and mechanisms. For instance, Gümüştaş et al.(2024) [51] reported that while NaF-containing toothpastes and NovaMin deposit crystalline layers along demineralized enamel, nano-hydroxyapatite formulations may result in lower deposit density, though the functional implications remain to be fully elucidated. In contrast, our findings of more pronounced and brighter deposits in the HAP group may stem from differences in application protocols; our longer daily contact time (3 min versus 5 s) potentially enhances mineral uptake and agent efficacy. This suggests that the duration and frequency of remineralization agent exposure are critical parameters influencing clinical outcomes.

The effects of remineralization agents such as CPP-ACP-fluoride, tricalciumphosphate-fluoride, and nano-hydroxyapatite have been evaluated using EDS analysis, reporting significant decreases in the Ca/P ratio after demineralization [52, 53]. After remineralization, the highest increases in Ca/P ratios were observed in the CPP-ACP-fluoride and tricalciumphosphate-fluoride groups, followed by nano-hydroxyapatite Thimmaiah et al., 2019 [52]. In our study, EDS analysis examined the atomic presence of Ca and P ions, showing that demineralization caused a significant decrease in both ions, while all remineralization agents except CPP-ACP led to increases in Ca levels. But when compared to baseline, Ca levels reached to initial values only in the control and fluoride group. For P ions, the increases were observed in all groups except the CPP-ACP group, and when compared to baseline, only the fluoride group restored P levels to their original values. These differences may be attributed to the distinct mechanisms of action, surface interactions, and compound formulations of the agents. Further studies are needed to support these findings.

The present study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the remineralization potential of the investigated agents through a rigorously designed experimental framework. Several methodological strengths enhance the reliability and interpretative value of the findings. In particular, the use of a within-tooth experimental design allowed different experimental conditions to be evaluated on comparable enamel substrates, thereby minimizing inter-sample variability associated with intrinsic differences in enamel composition, structure, and baseline mineral content. This design strengthened the internal validity of the comparisons among the tested remineralization agents. Another notable strength of the study is the integration of multiple complementary analytical techniques to assess enamel remineralization. Surface microhardness testing provided quantitative insights into changes in mechanical properties associated with mineral recovery, while micro-CT enabled non-destructive three-dimensional evaluation of mineral density alterations within the enamel structure. In addition, SEM-EDS allowed detailed qualitative and semi-quantitative assessment of surface morphology and elemental composition. The combined use of these analytical approaches allowed remineralization processes to be evaluated from both structural and compositional perspectives, thereby strengthening the overall interpretative framework of the study. Furthermore, the strict methodological standardization was maintained throughout the experimental procedures. Demineralization conditions, agent application protocols, and analytical measurements were carefully controlled to ensure consistency across all experimental groups. Such standardization enhances the reproducibility of the experimental model and contributes to the robustness of the obtained results.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. The relatively limited number of teeth represents an inherent constraint of controlled in vitro experimental designs, which may restrict the broader generalizability of the results. In addition, the use of a single-phase demineralization model followed by remineralization in artificial saliva does not fully replicate the dynamic demineralization–remineralization cycles occurring in the oral environment. Moreover, the standardized post-application rinsing and gentle mechanical removal protocol may have reduced superficially deposited mineral layers, particularly in agents capable of forming surface precipitates such as hydroxyapatite-based materials. Therefore, the observed outcomes likely reflect intrinsic mineral integration within the enamel structure rather than retention of loosely bound surface residues. Additional methodological considerations include the potential for minimal surface alterations associated with repeated SEM-EDS measurements and vacuum exposure, as well as the sensitivity of EDS-derived elemental values to surface topography and instrumental conditions. Finally, although partial blinding was implemented for SEM-EDS and micro-CT analyses, the operator responsible for applying the remineralization agents was aware of group allocation, which should be acknowledged as a potential source of bias.

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, all tested remineralization agents demonstrated the ability to enhance enamel mineral recovery, with no statistically significant differences observed among them. These findings suggest comparable remineralization efficacy among the investigated materials rather than the superiority of a single agent. Although morphological differences in mineral deposition patterns were observed, these variations may reflect differences in remineralization mechanisms while ultimately leading to similar overall outcomes. Future studies incorporating dynamic experimental models, such as pH-cycling protocols, longer application periods, and clinical investigations under oral conditions, are warranted to further validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

Collectively, these findings underscore the complexity of enamel remineralization, where agent composition, particle size, application regimen, and physicochemical interactions collectively determine efficacy. While nano-hydroxyapatite shows promising mineral deposition properties, optimizing clinical protocols is essential to maximize its therapeutic potential. Future longitudinal and in vivo studies are necessary to validate these in vitro observations and elucidate the long-term benefits of different remineralization agents on enamel integrity and caries prevention. Additionally, examining the biological properties of materials through a biomimetic approach would contribute to the relevant literature by aiding in the identification of both effective and biocompatible remineralization agents.
