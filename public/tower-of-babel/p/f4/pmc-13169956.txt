Influence of sodium hypochlorite concentration and treatment time on enamel surface morphology and shear bond strength for orthodontic attachments: an in-vitro study
Authors: Danqing Zheng, Chuqi Li, Tong Wang, Qingbo Zhao, Qiong Liu, Rui Zou, Yijie Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClear aligner therapy is widely used because of its aesthetics and comfort; however, repeated debonding of resin attachments remains a clinical concern. This study evaluated the effects of sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) pretreatment applied prior to phosphoric acid etching on enamel surface morphology and shear bond strength (SBS).MethodsSixty extracted premolars were randomly assigned to ten groups (n = 6 per group) in a staged experimental design. The enamel surfaces were pretreated with NaOCl at concentrations of 0%, 3%, 5.25%, or 10% for 30 s or with 5.25% and 10% for 20, 40, or 60 s before acid etching. Surface morphology was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and surface characteristics were semi-quantitatively analyzed using the ImageJ software. Standardized resin attachments were bonded, and the SBS was measured using a universal testing machine (UTM). Data were analyzed using one-way and two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post-hoc test (α = 0.05).ResultsNaOCl pretreatment altered enamel surface morphology and increased SBS in a concentration- and time-dependent manner. SBS values were significantly higher in the 5.25% (226.60 ± 22.17 N; 113.30 ± 11.09 MPa) and 10% (239.21 ± 51.52 N; 119.61 ± 25.76 MPa) groups than in the control (P < 0.05). The highest SBS was observed with 10% NaOCl for 60 s (276.01 ± 63.67 N; 138.01 ± 31.84 MPa), which was significantly greater than shorter application times at the same concentration (P < 0.001). Two-way ANOVA demonstrated significant effects of both concentration and exposure time, as well as their interaction (P < 0.001).ConclusionNaOCl pretreatment applied prior to phosphoric acid etching significantly enhanced the SBS of resin attachments under controlled in vitro conditions. These findings suggest that NaOCl may influence enamel surface characteristics and bonding performance; however, further studies are warranted to clarify the underlying mechanisms and evaluate its clinical applicability.

Introduction

Direct bonding of orthodontic attachments to enamel has become an essential component of contemporary orthodontic treatment [1]. Composite resin attachments are critical for achieving effective tooth movement in clear aligner therapy [2, 3]. However, repeated attachment detachment remains a common clinical challenge [4] that can potentially prolong treatment duration and increase chairside time [5]. Therefore, optimizing enamel-bonding protocols is of considerable clinical importance [6].

Conventional etch-and-rinse systems rely on phosphoric acid etching to achieve micromechanical retention [7]. Phosphoric acid selectively dissolves the inorganic mineral phase of enamel, primarily hydroxyapatite, generating microporosities that facilitate resin infiltration [8, 9]. Although enamel is highly mineralized (approximately 96% inorganic), it contains a minor organic component within its superficial microstructure [10]. Phosphoric acid primarily acts on the mineral phase and does not specifically degrade organic constituents.

NaOCl is a well-established oxidizing and deproteinizing agent that is widely used in dentistry, particularly in endodontics [11–14]. In adhesive dentistry, NaOCl has primarily been investigated as a pretreatment step in self-etch adhesive systems [15], where its ability to remove organic components may enhance the efficacy of relatively mild acidic monomers [16]. However, evidence regarding its potential role as an adjunctive pretreatment prior to conventional phosphoric acid etching on enamel surfaces is limited [17, 18].

The influence of controlled NaOCl application before acid etching on enamel surface characteristics and subsequent bonding performance in etch-and-rinse systems remains unclear [19, 20]. Furthermore, the combined effects of NaOCl concentration and exposure time have not been systematically evaluated [21, 22].

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of different NaOCl concentrations and treatment durations applied prior to phosphoric acid etching on enamel surface morphology and SBS. The null hypotheses were as follows: (1) NaOCl pretreatment does not affect enamel surface characteristics or SBS compared with conventional etching alone, and (2) variations in NaOCl concentration and exposure time do not influence bonding performance.

Materials and methods

Study design

This in-vitro study was conducted using a sequential experimental design to evaluate the effects of NaOCl pretreatment on enamel surface characteristics and SBS [23].

The first phase assessed the effect of NaOCl concentration under a standardized exposure time of 30 s. Based on the results of the concentration screening, the second phase evaluated the influence of treatment duration using the two concentrations that demonstrated the most pronounced effects [24, 25].

This staged design was adopted to isolate the main effects while maintaining statistical robustness and feasibility, rather than employing a full factorial design, which requires a substantially larger sample size. A schematic of the procedure is shown in Fig. 1.

Sample size calculation and randomization

The sample size was determined using G*Power (version 3.1; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). With a significance level of α = 0.05, statistical power (1–β) = 0.90, and effect size f = 0.40 (large effect), the minimum required sample size per group was calculated as six specimens.

Each experimental group included six samples. Sixty extracted teeth were randomly assigned to the experimental groups using a computer-generated randomization sequence created in R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Randomization was performed to ensure unbiased allocation and minimize potential selection bias. Each tooth was coded numerically, and an allocation sequence was generated using the sample function in R. The resulting randomization list was applied to assign the teeth to the designated groups in a concealed manner, thereby ensuring that the grouping process was both objective and reproducible.

Resin attachment preparation

Each tooth was embedded in the center of a dental gypsum block (3 × 3 × 3 cm) (Shanghai Qing Pu Dental Materials Co. Ltd., China) was prepared according to the manufacturer’s recommended water–powder ratio and allowed to set completely. These blocks were stored in a water bath (Shanghai Hengyue Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., China) at 37 °C for at least 24 h, following the ISO/TS 11,405 recommendations for adhesion testing, which simulated an oral environment. The blocks were inspected after storage, and no deterioration, softening, or loosening of the teeth was observed. Specimens showing instability were excluded (none occurred).

Digital impressions of the teeth were obtained using a digital intraoral scanner (Align Technology, Inc., Warrenton, VA, USA), and the resin attachment was digitally designed. The resin attachment templates were produced using 3D printing (Shanghai UnionTech, Ltd., China) and biostar thermoplastic molding (Wuhan Yayuan Medical Technology Co., Ltd., China). Standardized resin attachments (1 × 2 × 1 mm; bonding area = 2 mm²) were fabricated using a 3D-printed template [26].

SBS testing

Each specimen was embedded in a dental gypsum block (3 × 3 × 3 cm) prepared in accordance with the ISO 6873 standards.

SBS testing was performed using a UTM (HY-5080 × 1; Heng Wing Precision Instrument Co., Shanghai, China) at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min [25]. The loading blade was aligned parallel to the long axis of the tooth.

The maximum load at the point of failure was the debonding force (F, N). The SBS was calculated in megapascals (MPa) by normalizing the debonding force to the bonding area (A, mm²) using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:SBS\left(MPa\right)=\frac{F\left(N\right)}{A\left(m{m}^{2}\right)}$$\end{document}

where F represents the maximum debonding force and A represents the standardized bonding area of the resin attachment, which was 2 mm². This standardization ensured comparability with other studies [30].

For each specimen, three representative non-overlapping fields were imaged.

The surface morphology was evaluated qualitatively and semi-quantitatively using ImageJ (version 1.53e, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Identical calibration parameters were applied to all samples.

The images were imported, converted to 8-bit grayscale, and spatially calibrated using an SEM scale bar. For each field, 3D surface plots and height-distribution (intensity) maps were generated to provide objective descriptors of topographic variation. Group comparisons of roughness were based on standardized imaging conditions supplemented by ImageJ-derived topographic readouts, enabling consistent evaluation of concentration-dependent changes in enamel surface features.

No biochemical or spectroscopic analyses were performed to directly quantify the organic content.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and as frequency for categorical variables.

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. One-way ANOVA was used for concentration comparison. Two-way ANOVA was used to evaluate the main effects and interaction between concentration and treatment duration. Tukey’s post hoc test was applied when homogeneity assumptions were satisfied. When variance homogeneity was violated, Welch ’s ANOVA with the Games–Howell post hoc test was applied. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

SEM and enamel surface roughness

Enamel surface roughness was quantified using the ImageJ software based on SEM images to provide an objective evaluation of morphological alterations (Fig. 2D). In the control group, the enamel surfaces appeared relatively smooth with minimal grooves, and 3D reconstruction confirmed a flat topography with low height variation (Fig. 2D). Treatment with 3% NaOCl produced a slight increase in surface irregularities, with 3D plots showing modest peaks and valleys compared to the control (Fig. 2D). The 5.25% NaOCl group exhibited the most pronounced changes, characterized by saturated, raised areas and a distinct “scale-like” morphology, with ImageJ analysis revealing the highest topographic variation (Fig. 2D). Interestingly, although 10% NaOCl treatment further altered the enamel surface, the overall roughness appeared less pronounced than that of the 5.25% group. The enamel exhibited a coarser but more eroded morphology, where extensive deproteinization and surface dissolution may have reduced the measurable surface irregularities in 3D reconstructions (Fig. 2D).

Overall, the enamel surface roughness increased with increasing NaOCl concentration up to 5.25%, where the most pronounced morphological alterations were observed. At a 10% concentration, although the enamel surface exhibited evident erosion, quantitative analysis revealed a reduction in measurable roughness compared to the 5.25% group. This suggests a nonlinear relationship in which moderate NaOCl concentrations enhance surface irregularity and bonding receptivity, whereas excessively high concentrations may lead to over-deproteinization and surface dissolution, thereby reducing topographic variation.

Effect of NaOCl concentration on SBS

SBS values differed significantly among the groups pretreated with varying NaOCl concentrations (Fig. 2C). The control group (0% NaOCl) exhibited the lowest SBS values (114.13 ± 28.98 N; 57.06 ± 14.49 MPa). Pretreatment with 3% NaOCl moderately increased the SBS to 154.43 ± 13.15 N (77.22 ± 6.58 MPa), although the improvement was less pronounced. A significant enhancement was observed in the 5.25% NaOCl group, with SBS reaching 226.60 ± 22.17 N (113.30 ± 11.09 MPa, P < 0.001 vs. control group). Similarly, 10% NaOCl pretreatment resulted in high SBS values of 239.21 ± 51.52 N (119.61 ± 25.76 MPa, P < 0.001 vs. control). Comparisons between the 5.25% and 10% NaOCl groups revealed no significant difference in SBS (P > 0.05), suggesting a plateau effect. Overall, NaOCl pretreatment significantly increased SBS in a concentration-dependent manner up to 5.25%, with no further increase at 10%.

Effect of NaOCl treatment time on SBS

SBS was significantly influenced by the duration of NaOCl pretreatment (Fig. 3). Subsequently, the SBS was assessed to identify the optimal treatment duration. Compared with the control group, all NaOCl-treated specimens exhibited significantly higher SBS values at each time point (P < 0.05). The lowest mean SBS (128.67 ± 5.65 N; 64.33 ± 2.83 MPa) was observed with 5.25% NaOCl treatment for 20 s. Within the 5.25% group, SBS values increased with treatment time, with 60 s (154.07 ± 8.93 N; 77.03 ± 4.46 MPa) significantly higher than 20 s (128.67 ± 5.65 N; 64.33 ± 2.83 MPa, P < 0.001) and 40 s (153.67 ± 11.86 N; 76.84 ± 5.93 MPa, P < 0.01). For the 10% NaOCl group, the highest mean SBS (276.01 ± 63.67 N; 138.01 ± 31.84 MPa) was observed at 60 s, which was significantly higher than that at 20 s (168.18 ± 27.50 N; 84.09 ± 13.75 MPa) and 40 s (180.03 ± 13.21 N; 90.02 ± 6.61 MPa), as well as for all treatments at 5.25% concentration. Two-way ANOVA indicated significant effects of both treatment duration and NaOCl concentration, as well as a significant interaction between these factors (P < 0.001). Tukey’s post-hoc analysis further confirmed that the SBS values were higher at 60 s than at 20 s (P < 0.01) and 40 s (P < 0.01). At 60 s, 10% NaOCl produced significantly higher SBS than 5.25% NaOCl did (P < 0.01). No significant differences between the two concentrations were detected at 20–40 s.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the effects of NaOCl pretreatment on enamel surface characteristics and SBS using a staged experimental design. The results demonstrated that NaOCl application prior to phosphoric acid etching influenced both enamel morphology and bonding performance in a concentration- and time-dependent manner.

It is important to clarify that NaOCl was applied before acid etching. The intention was not to replace phosphoric acid but to determine whether modification of the superficial enamel substrate prior to conventional etch-and-rinse bonding could influence subsequent adhesive performance [7, 9]. Although enamel is highly mineralized, it contains a minor organic component. Phosphoric acid primarily dissolves hydroxyapatite crystals and creates microporosities but does not specifically degrade organic constituents [7]. Therefore, adjunctive pretreatment may alter the surface characteristics that affect resin infiltration [33]. However, this study did not directly quantify protein removal or evaluate chemical surface alterations, and any mechanistic interpretation should be considered speculative.

In the concentration analysis, both 5.25% and 10% NaOCl demonstrated significantly higher SBS than the control group. Interestingly, surface roughness appeared to be most pronounced at 5.25%, whereas the highest SBS was observed at 10% under extended exposure. This suggests that measurable surface irregularity alone may not fully explain the observed bonding performance. Adhesion is likely governed by a dynamic balance among microstructural modification, surface energy changes, and preservation of enamel integrity [24]. Previous in vitro investigations of enamel surface pretreatment have also reported concentration-dependent improvements in bond strength, although differences in methodology and bonding area may influence absolute values [24, 30, 33].

A nonlinear trend was observed in the time-dependent analysis, particularly in the 10% NaOCl group [34]. The SBS did not increase monotonically across all exposure times. Such fluctuations may reflect the complex physicochemical interactions between oxidative treatment and the enamel microstructure [27]. Insufficient exposure may limit surface modification, whereas intermediate exposure may introduce localized heterogeneity without proportionally enhancing micromechanical retention. Prolonged exposure (60 s) may further enhance substrate receptivity and resin penetration. Statistical analysis confirmed a significant interaction between concentration and exposure time, indicating that the treatment effects were not purely linear.

Concerns regarding the magnitude of the SBS values should be interpreted in light of the standardized bonding area used in this study (2 mm²). Because stress values are calculated by dividing the force by the area, smaller bonding areas may yield numerically higher MPa values compared with studies using larger attachment bases. All specimens were tested under standardized conditions, and the values remained within the ranges reported in comparable in vitro orthodontic bonding studies [33, 35].

NaOCl is a strong oxidizing agent that may interfere with free-radical polymerization if residual oxidants remain on its surface [36]. In this study, thorough water rinsing was performed following NaOCl application prior to acid etching to minimize residual oxidative effects. The degree of conversion or polymerization kinetics of the resin adhesive were not directly assessed; therefore, no conclusions can be drawn regarding the chemical enhancement of polymerization. In addition, high concentrations of NaOCl are associated with increased organic tissue dissolution and potential cytotoxicity in endodontic applications [11, 13, 14]. Because the present study was conducted on extracted teeth, pulpal or periapical toxicity was not applicable. Nevertheless, the long-term effects of high-concentration NaOCl on enamel integrity remain unclear. Safer alternatives, such as papain or ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) [37, 38], have demonstrated deproteinization potential with potentially reduced cytotoxicity and warrant further comparative investigation [2, 23, 30].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study employed a staged experimental design rather than a full factorial matrix [13]. While this approach allowed for the efficient evaluation of primary effects and selected interactions, a complete factorial design could provide a more comprehensive interaction analysis [35, 39, 40]. Second, no artificial aging protocol was performed [41]; thermal cycling may better simulate intraoral conditions. Third, the adhesive interface morphology and failure modes were not evaluated, which limited mechanistic insights. Additionally, although SEM combined with ImageJ provided a standardized semi-quantitative assessment, non-contact profilometry or atomic force microscopy may offer more precise surface characterization [32, 34, 42].

Finally, the extracted teeth were stored in saline at 4 °C prior to experimentation [43]. Although this storage method is widely used, prolonged storage may subtly alter enamel properties [44]. Future investigations should compare storage media and incorporate surface chemical analyses to further elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

From a clinical perspective, optimizing pretreatment protocols may reduce the frequency of attachment debonding and improve treatment efficiency in clear aligner therapy [45]. Further long-term and clinical studies are necessary to determine whether the improvements observed in vitro translate into enhanced clinical durability [46].

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, NaOCl pretreatment applied prior to phosphoric acid etching influenced enamel surface characteristics and significantly increased SBS in a concentration- and time-dependent manner. The highest bond strength was observed after treatment with 10% NaOCl for 60 s. However, the relationship between the surface morphology and bonding performance appeared nonlinear, indicating that multiple physicochemical factors may contribute to adhesion.

Although the findings suggest that controlled NaOCl pretreatment may improve the bonding performance of resin attachments under standardized laboratory conditions, the underlying mechanisms have not been directly investigated. Further studies incorporating surface chemical analysis, aging protocols, and clinical evaluations are required to determine long-term durability and clinical applicability.
