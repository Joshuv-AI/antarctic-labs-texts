The effect of the restorative justice program implemented in schools on adolescents’ peer bullying behaviors
Authors: Saniye Selin Döğer Şenel, Asya Çetin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Peer bullying poses a serious risk to adolescents’ psychosocial development and school adjustment. However, culturally adapted and evidence-based intervention programs remain limited in non-Western societies. Restorative justice approaches provide an alternative to punitive discipline by emphasizing relationship repair, accountability, and empathy development. This study examined the effects of a 12-week multi-component Restorative Justice Program implemented with students aged 12–13 in Istanbul, Türkiye, on peer bullying behaviors and restorative justice orientations. A quasi-experimental design was used, including an experimental group of 25 students and a control group of 20 students. Participants completed pre-test and post-test assessments, while the experimental group also completed a four-week follow-up assessment. Findings indicated a significant increase in empathy levels within the experimental group. Strong improvements were also observed across all subdimensions of restorative justice orientation. Within-group analyses revealed significant reductions in verbal bullying, property damage behaviors, and total bullying perpetration. In between-group comparisons, the most robust post-intervention difference was found for empathy after statistical corrections. Follow-up results suggested that these gains were maintained in the short term. Overall, the findings provide preliminary evidence that a culturally adapted and structured restorative justice program may be effective in increasing empathy and reducing bullying behaviors at the classroom level. In particular, attitudinal change appears to be an accessible proximal target for restorative justice-based interventions. Nevertheless, the results should be interpreted cautiously due to the small sample size, lack of random assignment, and baseline group differences. Future research should replicate these findings using larger samples and randomized controlled designs.

Introduction

Peer bullying is a serious public health concern affecting children and adolescents worldwide. Defined as repeated, intentional harmful behavior characterized by a power imbalance between perpetrator and victim [1], bullying encompasses physical aggression, relational exclusion, verbal harassment, and property-related harm. The consequences are substantial and enduring: victims experience elevated rates of anxiety, depression, low academic achievement, and social withdrawal, while perpetrators face increased risks of later antisocial behavior and criminal involvement [2–4].

Global prevalence data underscore the urgency of the problem. Approximately one in three adolescents reports bullying involvement [5, 6], and OECD [7] data indicate that bullying remains among the most reported barriers to school safety and student wellbeing. In Türkiye, the situation is particularly pressing: TIMSS 2015 [8] data placed the country among those with the highest elementary school bullying rates internationally (32%), and Gökkaya and Sütçü ), [9] found that 34.9% of Ankara middle school students were victims, 2.8% perpetrators, and 27% bully-victims. Despite this elevated prevalence, culturally adapted, evidence-based interventions developed specifically for the Turkish school context remain limited. The present study directly addresses this gap.

Traditional punitive disciplinary responses—zero-tolerance policies, suspensions, exclusions—have shown limited effectiveness in reducing bullying and may exacerbate school disengagement and inequitable outcomes [10]. Restorative justice (RJ) has emerged as a theoretically distinct and practically promising alternative framework. Grounded in the principle that harmful behavior should be addressed through repair of relationships and community reintegration rather than punishment [11], RJ approaches have demonstrated positive outcomes across a range of school behavioral challenges [12–14].

A crucial conceptual distinction is necessary to situate the present study accurately. Restorative justice as a normative philosophical framework [11, 15] must be distinguished from restorative practices as specific implementation tools (e.g., restorative circles, peer conferencing, restorative conversations), and from constructs such as empathy, responsibility, and repair which function as hypothesized psychological mechanisms through which these tools operate. Morrison and Vaandering, [16] emphasize that authentic restorative justice requires not merely a set of techniques but a comprehensive shift in institutional culture. This distinction has important implications for interpreting the present findings: a 12-week class-level program can meaningfully develop individual restorative competencies and orientations, but cannot achieve the systemic institutional transformation required for whole-school RJ implementation.

The theoretical pathways linking RJ to bullying reduction operate at two levels. At the individual level, RJ practices strengthen empathic perspective-taking, accountability for harm, and constructive conflict resolution competencies—psychological capacities that directly undermine the motivational and cognitive foundations of bullying behavior [17, 18]. Empathy, in particular, represents a critical proximal target: the ability to recognize and respond to others’ emotional states is a well-established protective factor against aggressive behavior [19, 20]. At the relational and peer ecology level, restorative practices aim to shift classroom norms toward prosociality, creating an environment in which conciliatory and supportive gestures are normatively expected. Borinca et al., [21] demonstrated that ingroup norms shape whether prosocial behaviors are perceived as sincere—a determinant of whether restorative gestures actually transform interpersonal dynamics rather than being dismissed as performative. Borinca et al., [22] further establish that conflict reduction is deeply embedded in shared normative expectations at the group level. These insights suggest that effective RJ programming must attend not only to individual skill development but to the normative climate in which those skills are expressed.

A substantive gap in the RJ intervention literature concerns the measurement of restorative justice orientations as a proximal outcome. Most prior studies have assessed behavioral outcomes (suspension rates, observed incidents) or general school climate indices, without measuring the degree to which participants have internalized restorative dispositions. This omission limits our understanding of the psychological mechanisms through which RJ programs operate. The Restorative Justice Approaches Scale (RJAS; [23]), developed and validated specifically for adolescents aged 12–16, addresses this gap by operationalizing restorative justice orientation across four theoretically grounded dimensions: Empathy, Responsibility, Repair, and Harm and Needs awareness. In the present study, restorative justice orientation is conceptualized as a proximal, malleable program target—a set of relational and ethical dispositions that the RJP seeks to strengthen, and which may mediate downstream reductions in bullying behavior. This is consistent with the established theoretical position that attitudinal and motivational change precedes behavioral change [16, 17].

The ecological context of bullying further informs the present study. Peer-group and classroom ecology models [24, 25] establish that bullying is maintained not only by individual characteristics but by group dynamics, status hierarchies, and bystander norms. These models suggest that relational forms of bullying—social exclusion, rumor spreading—may be particularly responsive to norm-shifting interventions that address the peer climate in which such behaviors occur. The present RJP, designed to cultivate both individual empathic competencies and collective community norms around harm and repair, was theoretically positioned to target both perpetration behaviors and the relational dynamics that sustain them.

A further important context is the comparative evaluation of RJ approaches. Alonso-Rodríguez and colleagues’ [26] systematic review of restorative practices in reducing school violence across 21 international studies found consistent positive effects on emotional wellbeing, prosocial behavior, and conflict reduction—providing a robust evidence base that contextualizes the present findings. Similarly, Lodi et al.,‘s , [14] systematic review demonstrated that RJ practices at school produced significant reductions in disciplinary incidents and improvements in school climate. However, both reviews note that effect sizes are substantially larger for whole-school, sustained implementations than for class-level or time-limited programs. The present study contributes evidence specifically for the class-level dosage, filling a gap between theory and large-scale implementation research.

The present study therefore addresses the following needs in the literature: (a) evidence for class-level RJ intervention effectiveness in a Turkish middle-school context, extending predominantly Western research to an underrepresented setting; (b) evaluation of a multi-component RJP integrating adolescent, parent, and school staff components; (c) use of the RJAS as a validated proximal outcome measure, enabling assessment of attitudinal change as a pathway to behavioral change; and (d) methodologically rigorous data—including effect sizes and multiple-testing corrections—to inform power calculations and design refinement for future larger-scale evaluations.

Based on this theoretical framework, the following hypotheses were formulated:H1: Adolescents in the experimental group will demonstrate significantly greater improvements in restorative justice orientations (Empathy, Responsibility, Repair, Harm and Needs, and RJAS Total) from pre-test to post-test, compared to the control group.H2: Adolescents in the experimental group will show significantly greater reductions in peer bullying perpetration and victimization from pre-test to post-test, compared to the control group.H3 (Exploratory): Improvements in restorative justice orientations and bullying perpetration behaviors achieved at post-test will be maintained at the four-week retention assessment within the experimental group.

Method

Research model

A pre-test–post-test non-equivalent control group quasi-experimental design with a four-week retention test was employed [27]. This design was selected because random assignment of individual students was not administratively feasible within the participating school; intact classes were assigned to conditions by school administration. Quasi-experimental designs are well-established in applied intervention research when randomization is not feasible and represent the most rigorous design achievable in naturalistic school settings [28]. The current design provides preliminary effect size estimates and directional evidence that may inform the design of future randomized controlled trials. Primary methodological limitations, including non-random assignment and baseline group differences on several outcome variables, are acknowledged transparently throughout the study.

Study group

The study sample comprised 45 adolescents aged 12–13 attending a public middle school in Istanbul, Türkiye. The school was located in a middle-income urban district and was selected through purposive sampling on the basis of administrative willingness to participate, student population characteristics meeting the age criterion, and the absence of any ongoing anti-bullying program during the study period. All participants and their legal guardians provided written informed consent prior to study commencement. No participants withdrew during the 12-week program.

The experimental group (n = 25) comprised 22 twelve-year-olds and 3 thirteen-year-olds; 11 female and 13 male. The control group (n = 20) comprised 11 twelve-year-olds and 9 thirteen-year-olds; 10 female and 10 were male. Pre-test Mann–Whitney U analyses confirmed baseline equivalence on all RJAS subscales (all p > .05; Table 1). However, significant pre-test group differences were found on several PBS-AF subscales (Table 2): the experimental group scored significantly lower than the control group on Physical Victim (p = .001, r = .466) and Total Victim (p = .002, r = .456), both of which survived Bonferroni correction. This baseline nonequivalence is the principal internal validity limitation of the study and is addressed throughout the Findings and Discussion sections. The experimental group’s lower baseline victimization must be considered as an alternative explanation for observed post-test patterns.

The Restorative Justice Program (RJP)

The RJP was developed through a systematic, iterative process grounded in the restorative justice literature. Development stages comprised: (1) systematic literature review establishing the theoretical framework; (2) needs assessment administered to 15 school stakeholders (5 adolescents, 5 parents, 5 teachers) independent of the study sample, identifying empathy, emotional awareness, constructive communication, and harm repair as priority competency areas; (3) content development by the first author; (4) expert validity review by a panel of ten specialists (one assessment specialist, six child development experts, three psychological counselors), with inter-rater content validity agreement above 0.80 across all sessions; and (5) a small-scale pilot test with modifications implemented prior to the main study.

The program consisted of twelve weekly 50-minute sessions organized across five thematic modules: (1) Introduction to Restorative Justice and Harm (Sessions 1–2)—defining harm, exploring its impact, and introducing RJ principles; (2) Empathy and Perspective-Taking (Sessions 3–4)—developing emotional recognition skills, practicing perspective-taking exercises; (3) Conflict Resolution and Communication (Sessions 5–7)—nonviolent communication skills, de-escalation strategies, constructive dialogue; (4) Repair and Responsibility (Sessions 8–10)—accountability processes, restorative circle practices, community repair; and (5) Community Building and Consolidation (Sessions 11–12)—whole-class restorative commitments, relapse prevention, and program review. Each session combined brief didactic input (approximately 10 min), structured small-group discussion, role-play activities, and individual reflective exercises. Fidelity to the program manual was maintained throughout; no sessions were cancelled or substantially modified. Attendance data confirmed that all 25 experimental group participants attended at least 10 of 12 sessions (≥ 83% attendance).

In addition to the adolescent sessions, the RJP included two supplementary components. The parent component comprised one online information session (via Zoom) addressing the nature and consequences of peer bullying, warning signs, and recommended family-based strategies, followed by one individual consultation meeting per family. The school staff component (‘Truth Commissions’) involved two structured awareness sessions with homeroom teachers, guidance counselors, the school principal, and vice principals, presenting prevalence data and introducing restorative approaches to classroom management. As these staff sessions were conducted within the same school environment, they may have indirectly influenced the control group’s classroom climate—a potential source of contamination that is acknowledged as a study limitation.

Measures

Peer Bullying Scale Adolescent Form (PBS-AF)

The PBS-AF [29] is a 53-item self-report measure assessing both bullying perpetration and victimization across six behavioral dimensions: physical bullying, verbal bullying, isolation, rumoring, inflicting damage to property, and sexual bullying. Items are rated on a five-point frequency scale (0 = Never to 4 = Almost every day), yielding separate Bully and Victim subscale scores. In the original validation sample of Turkish middle school students, Cronbach’s α was 0.92 (Bully subscales) and 0.93 (Victim subscales), indicating excellent internal consistency. The PBS-AF was specifically developed and validated with the Turkish adolescent population, providing strong cultural and linguistic validity for the present context. The PBS-AF’s multidimensional structure enables differentiated analysis of specific bullying forms, a methodological strength over single-score instruments. Self-report limitations—social desirability bias, demand characteristics, and the possibility that psychoeducational programming alters participants’ conceptualization of what constitutes bullying—are acknowledged and discussed in the Limitations section.

Restorative Justice Approaches Scale (RJAS)

The RJAS [23] is a 28-item, four-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 4 = Strongly Agree) developed to assess restorative justice orientations among adolescents aged 12–16. The scale encompasses four theoretically grounded subdimensions: Empathy (capacity to recognize and share others’ feelings in conflict contexts), Responsibility (acknowledgment of one’s role in causing harm), Repair (motivation to make amends and restore relationships), and Harm and Needs (awareness of the harm caused to others and understanding of their needs). Total scores range from 28 to 112, with higher scores indicating stronger restorative justice orientations. Internal consistency in the validation study was excellent: Cronbach’s α = 0.882 (Empathy), 0.830 (Responsibility), 0.887 (Repair), 0.812 (Harm and Needs), and 0.947 (Total).

The RJAS represents a methodological contribution of the present study: to our knowledge, no validated instrument specifically measuring adolescents’ restorative justice orientations across multiple dimensions existed prior to this development. The scale fills an important gap between general empathy measures and the specific attitudinal and motivational constructs targeted by RJ programming. Psychometric caveats include: (a) the scale is newly developed with limited independent replication data; (b) item content alignment with RJP session content creates a potential instructional priming risk; and (c) confirmatory factor analysis and criterion-related validity evidence are established in the development study [23] but await independent replication. These caveats are addressed in the Limitations section.

Personal information form

A brief researcher-developed form collected demographic information including age and gender.

Procedure

Ethical approval was obtained from the Karabük University Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2024/05-E.334453) and the Republic of Türkiye Ministry of National Education prior to data collection. Written informed consent was obtained from all legal guardians and assent was obtained from all adolescent participants. The study was conducted between October 15 and November 15, 2024. Pre-test instruments were administered to both groups in the week prior to program commencement. Post-tests were completed in the final program week. The four-week retention test was administered to the experimental group only; the absence of a control group follow-up is acknowledged as a study limitation.

Data analysis

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test; significance values below 0.05 combined with skewness coefficients exceeding |2.0| and visual inspection of Q–Q plots indicated departures from normality for most variables. Given the small sample size and non-normal distributions, nonparametric methods were employed throughout: the Mann–Whitney U test for between-group comparisons and the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank test for within-group pre-post comparisons [30]. In response to reviewer concerns—and as a matter of methodological rigour appropriate for studies with multiple comparison sets—two correction procedures were applied. Bonferroni correction controls familywise error rate and provides conservative, highly specific inferences. The Benjamini-Hochberg (1995) [31] false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, which controls the expected proportion of false discoveries among significant results, was additionally applied as a less conservative alternative. Effect sizes were estimated using r = |z| / √N [32], where r < .10 indicates a negligible effect, 0.10–0.29 small, 0.30–0.49 medium, and ≥ 0.50 large. All analyses report uncorrected p, Bonferroni-corrected p, FDR-corrected p, r, and effect size classification. Results described as ‘significant’ in the text refer to effects surviving at least one correction procedure; where both corrections are survived, this is explicitly noted.

Post-test between-group comparisons: RJAS

Consistent with H1, the experimental group demonstrated a significant post-test advantage on the RJAS Empathy subscale (U = 385.00, z = 3.08, p = .002, r = .460, medium effect; Table 3). This is the study’s most robust finding: it survived both Bonferroni correction (p_bonf = 0.008) and FDR correction (p_fdr = 0.008). The experimental group’s mean Empathy score increased from 26.40 (SD = 3.42) at pre-test to 30.24 (SD = 2.05) at post-test, while the control group showed only a modest change from 25.80 (SD = 5.46) to 26.80 (SD = 4.23). This represents a clinically meaningful improvement in the experimental group’s capacity for empathic orientation in conflict contexts.

This pattern of results is consistent with the known challenges of detecting between-group behavioral effects in small-sample class-level intervention studies. The absence of corrected between-group significance does not imply absence of effect: with N = 45, statistical power for detecting medium effects (r ≈ .30) at the two-sided α = 0.05 level with Bonferroni correction across 14 comparisons is approximately 12%. Within-group pre-post changes: experimental group

Wilcoxon Signed-Rank analyses for the experimental group (Table 5) revealed large, consistently robust improvements across all RJAS subdimensions. All five RJAS measures showed statistically significant pre-to-post improvement, with large effect sizes (r = .815–0.874). Critically, all five survived both Bonferroni and FDR corrections (all p_bonf ≤ 0.040; all p_fdr ≤ 0.008). The pattern was consistent: zero or near-zero participants showed deterioration (0–3 negative ranks), while the large majority showed improvement (12–20 positive ranks). These large within-group RJAS effects represent the most consistent and compelling signal in the dataset and directly support the study’s core theoretical proposition that structured RJ programming can produce meaningful improvements in restorative justice orientations.

In contrast, no significant within-group reductions were observed on any victimization subscale in the experimental group (all p ≥ .180; Table 6). This pattern is theoretically coherent and important for interpretation: the RJP was designed primarily as a skills-building and attitudinal change program targeting perpetration behaviors and empathic orientation. Within-group pre-post changes: control group

Control group within-group analyses (Tables 7 and 8) revealed no significant changes on any RJAS subscale (all p > .05; all failed correction). For PBS-AF, three raw-p significant effects emerged (Physical Victim p = .045, Damage to Property Victim p = .042, Total Victim p = .036), but none survived Bonferroni correction (smallest p_bonf = 0.503) or FDR correction. These results indicate that apparent within-group changes in the control group are statistical artifacts of uncorrected multiple testing rather than genuine systematic change. After appropriate correction, the control group showed no robust evidence of spontaneous improvement on any outcome, which strengthens the interpretability of the experimental group’s large, correction-surviving within-group improvements.

Retention test results

Four weeks following post-test, experimental group RJAS scores remained elevated relative to pre-test levels: total RJAS mean at retention (M = 98.76) remained substantially above pre-test (M = 89.92) and close to post-test (M = 100.24), suggesting that attitudinal gains were maintained in the short term. PBS-AF bully perpetration scores similarly remained lower than pre-test levels for the majority of subscales. These patterns are consistent with H3 and provide preliminary support for short-term maintenance of program effects. Without a parallel control group follow-up, these patterns cannot be definitively attributed to the intervention; maturation and testing effects remain possible alternative explanations. A minimum 6-month follow-up with both groups is recommended for future evaluations to adequately assess sustainability.

As shown in Fig. 1, the mean scores of adolescents in the experimental group on the RJAS increased from pre-test following the implementation of the Restorative Justice Program (RJP), and remained elevated at the four-week retention assessment. These patterns are preliminary and should be interpreted with caution, given the single-group retention design and the absence of a parallel control group follow-up.

Discussion

This quasi-experimental study examined the effect of a structured, multi-component Restorative Justice Program on peer bullying behaviors and restorative justice orientations among early adolescents in a Turkish school context. Three findings emerge as the study’s most substantive contributions.

First, and most robustly, the RJP produced a significant, correction-surviving between-group improvement in RJAS Empathy (r = .460, medium effect; both Bonferroni- and FDR-corrected), providing the clearest evidence of a causal intervention effect in the dataset. This finding is theoretically important: empathy has been identified as a critical proximal mechanism through which restorative justice practices are expected to reduce bullying [17, 18], and the present result demonstrates that a structured 12-week class-level RJP is sufficient to produce a measurable, robust improvement in this mechanism. The program’s dedicated emphasis on perspective-taking and emotional recognition activities (Sessions 3–4) appears particularly well-targeted. This finding aligns with and extends evidence from Kehoe et al., [33], who found that HEART-framework restorative approaches improved social-emotional skills including empathy among students, and with Lustick et al.,‘s [20] finding that restorative practices can function as effective empowerment tools for social-emotional development.

Second, the experimental group demonstrated large, correction-surviving within-group improvements across all five RJAS subdimensions (r = .815–0.874), indicating that the program produced comprehensive improvement in restorative justice orientations—not limited to empathy alone. The consistency of this pattern (zero or near-zero negative ranks across all RJAS subscales) suggests that the program was effective in shifting adolescents’ attitudinal and motivational orientations toward restorative values. That the between-group advantage was significant only for Empathy—and not for Responsibility, Repair, or Harm and Needs—may reflect the fact that the control group also showed directional improvement on some dimensions (particularly Harm and Needs), consistent with possible indirect school-level effects of the teacher awareness sessions or testing effects. Alternatively, Empathy may be more rapidly acquired than the other constructs, which may require longer-term or more intensive programming to produce robust inter-group differences.

Third, the experimental group showed large, correction-surviving within-group reductions in verbal bullying perpetration (r = .810), damage to property perpetration (r = .861), and total bullying perpetration (r = .821). These effect sizes are strikingly large and clinically meaningful, indicating that the RJP was associated with substantial reductions in the frequency with which experimental group participants reported engaging in bullying behaviors. That these effects did not achieve corrected between-group significance is most plausibly attributable to the study’s limited statistical power: with N = 45 and Bonferroni correction across 14 comparisons, the study is severely underpowered to detect even medium-large between-group effects. This interpretation is supported by the consistency of the within-group effects: 16 of 25 experimental group participants showed reduction (vs. 4 increase) on Total Bully (p < .001), a pattern unlikely to occur by chance regardless of between-group comparisons. This underscores the importance of reporting within-group effect sizes alongside between-group tests, particularly in pilot studies where power constraints are inherent.

The absence of significant within-group victimization improvements in the experimental group requires careful interpretation. The experimental group entered the study with significantly lower victimization scores than the control group on Physical Victim and Total Victim (both Bonferroni-significant). Their post-test scores remained similarly low—reflecting stability rather than improvement. This pattern is most parsimoniously explained by regression to the mean having acted at baseline rather than any intervention-induced process. The uncorrected between-group differences on Isolation Victim and Damage to Property Victim, reported in earlier analyses, are reinterpreted here as artifacts of this baseline nonequivalence rather than evidence of intervention effectiveness on victimization outcomes.

These findings make theoretical sense within the study’s two-level mechanistic model. At the individual level, the RJP successfully targeted the attitudinal and motivational substrates of bullying perpetration—empathy, responsibility, harm awareness—producing large improvements in restorative justice orientations. These attitudinal changes appear to have translated into self-reported reductions in bullying perpetration behaviors. At the peer ecology level, the program’s community-building and norm-shifting activities (Sessions 11–12) may have contributed to the normative context in which empathic and restorative gestures were expressed, consistent with Borinca et al.,‘s [21] finding that ingroup norms shape the authenticity and impact of prosocial behaviors. The greater responsiveness of verbal and property-related bullying (compared to rumoring, which showed a non-significant trend) may reflect that these forms are more directly targeted by the program’s communication and accountability modules.

The present findings also contribute to the growing literature on restorative practices in schools. Alonso-Rodríguez et al., [26] systematic review found that restorative practices consistently improve emotional wellbeing and prosocial behavior across diverse contexts, with the largest effects observed for programs that attend to both individual competencies and relational ecology. Lodi et al., [14] systematic review similarly found positive effects of school-based RJ on disciplinary outcomes and school climate. The present study’s within-group bully perpetration effects (r = .810–0.861) are notably larger than typical effect sizes reported in anti-bullying intervention meta-analyses (e.g., Gaffney et al., [19] reported average d ≈ 0.20 for perpetration), which may reflect the large-effect-size amplification that within-group analyses can produce when pre-post changes are consistent and in the expected direction. Readers should therefore not directly compare these within-group effect sizes to between-group effect sizes from other studies.

The present findings situate the RJP as a promising class-level complement to whole-school approaches. Morrison and Vaandering, [16] emphasize that authentic restorative justice requires institutional culture transformation that a 12-week class-level program cannot fully achieve. However, the present results suggest that even within the more modest scope of a class-level program, meaningful attitudinal change and large within-group behavioral improvements are achievable. This has practical implications: class-level RJPs such as the present one could serve as an accessible entry point for schools seeking to build restorative competencies before embedding them within broader school-wide frameworks. Parker and Bickmore, [34] similarly found that classroom-level restorative dialogue, when supported by adequate teacher training, can produce genuine prosocial learning outcomes even without whole-school implementation.

The Turkish school context provides important specificity for these findings. The majority of RJ school intervention research has been conducted in Anglo-American and Western European settings. The cultural collectivism characterizing Turkish society, combined with institutional emphases on hierarchical authority relationships in schools, creates a distinct context for restorative practices. The positive response of Turkish adolescents to the RJP’s community-building and relational repair emphasis suggests that restorative values may resonate strongly in collectivist cultural contexts—a finding that warrants systematic comparative investigation. The RJAS’s development for and validation with Turkish adolescents [23] provides a culturally appropriate measurement tool that can support future comparative research.

Finally, the present study’s methodological contribution—introducing Bonferroni and FDR corrections alongside effect sizes for all analyses—has diagnostic value beyond the present findings. The comparison between corrected and uncorrected results reveals that several findings that would have been highlighted in a standard analysis (control group within-group changes, victimization between-group differences) are not statistically robust after correction. This illustrates the value of rigorous reporting practices in pilot intervention research and provides a methodological template for future RJ evaluation studies.

Limitations

The following limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings:

Non-random assignment and baseline nonequivalence. Intact-class assignment resulted in significant pre-test group differences on Physical Victim and Total Victim (both Bonferroni-significant, medium effects). Regression to the mean is a plausible explanation for the stable-low post-test victimization scores in the experimental group. Post-test between-group comparisons on victimization subscales should not be interpreted as evidence of intervention effectiveness.

Small sample and limited statistical power. With N = 45 and Bonferroni correction across 14 between-group PBS-AF comparisons, power to detect medium effects is approximately 12%. The absence of corrected between-group PBS-AF significance should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of ineffectiveness. Future studies should conduct a priori power analyses targeting N ≥ 100 per group.

Single school and potential contamination. Both groups attended the same school; teacher and parent components were conducted school-wide. Full condition isolation was not achievable, and indirect effects on the control group’s classroom environment cannot be excluded.

Self-report measurement. All outcome data were collected via adolescent self-report, which is subject to social desirability bias and demand characteristics. The RJP’s explicit focus on harm, empathy, and responsibility may have influenced participants’ self-reports in ways not reflecting behavioral change.

RJAS psychometric maturity. The RJAS is a newly developed instrument. Although internal consistency is excellent and the development study [23] establishes initial validity, independent replication and long-term test-retest stability data are not yet available. Item-content alignment with RJP sessions creates a risk of instructional priming.

Single-group retention assessment. The four-week follow-up was conducted only with the experimental group, precluding determination of whether retention patterns reflect intervention effects, maturation, or other processes.

Absence of control variables. Socioeconomic background, prior conflict resolution exposure, and teacher-student relationship quality—all known to moderate RJ program effectiveness [16]—were not measured.

Researcher-implementer overlap. The intervention was developed and delivered by the first author without independent fidelity monitoring, limiting assessment of delivery variability.

Conclusions

This study provides preliminary evidence that a structured, theory-based, multi-component Restorative Justice Program can produce meaningful improvements in early adolescents’ restorative justice orientations and bullying perpetration behaviors. The program’s most robust effect was a significant, correction-surviving between-group improvement in empathy—the primary attitudinal mechanism through which restorative practices are theorized to reduce bullying. Large within-group improvements in total and specific bully perpetration behaviors provide additional, though interpretively constrained, evidence of behavioral change.

These findings carry several practical implications. First, they suggest that class-level RJ programs are a feasible and potentially effective entry point for schools seeking to build restorative competencies in adolescents, even without whole-school institutional redesign. Second, empathy development emerges as an accessible and sensitive proximal target for class-level interventions, with measurable change achievable within a 12-week timeframe. Third, the RJAS demonstrates promise as a sensitive outcome measure for future RJ intervention research. Fourth, the present study’s Turkish-context implementation suggests that restorative justice programming may be culturally compatible with collectivist school environments, warranting comparative cross-cultural investigation.

The study’s limitations—particularly the small sample size, non-random assignment, and baseline nonequivalence—require these conclusions to be interpreted with caution and indicate the need for replication using more rigorous research designs. Future research should employ random assignment or matched-class designs, larger samples (N ≥ 100 per group), multi-informant assessment, independent fidelity monitoring, long-term follow-up with both groups, and whole-school embedding to test whether class-level gains can be sustained and amplified within a systemic institutional change context. The present study provides effect size estimates and methodological guidance to inform such designs.
