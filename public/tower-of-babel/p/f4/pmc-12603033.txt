Optimized loading effects on pressure steam sterilization of loaned surgical instruments using product family categorization
Authors: Zhan Meng, Yao Zhuoya, Geng Junhui, Li Manchun, Ding Lina
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWet packs of loaned surgical instruments have always been a challenge for the Central Sterile Supply Department. Compared to other factors affecting pressure steam sterilization, loading is full of variables in practice. Based on the Product Family Categorization Theory, this project scientifically evaluates the steam penetration resistance level of each kit of loaned surgical instruments according to their different attributes to guide the sterilization staff to standardize the loading. By optimizing the loading method, the sterilization staff can quickly determine the most difficult to sterilize the loaned surgical instruments package in each batch, and select the best sterilization procedure according to its level, reducing their subjective judgment, and making the selection of sterilization procedures more standardized. That is to reduce the generation of wet packs, but also to ensure the safety of the use of patient equipment. However, due to manpower and time constraints, this study was only conducted in loaned surgical instruments and was a single-center study, and the sample size can be further expanded in the future to provide a basis for further improvement of the theory.Keywords: Central supply room, hospital; Loaned surgical instruments; Pressure steam sterilization; Steam penetration resistanceSubject terms: Health services, Public health

Introduction

Loaned surgical instruments are reusable tools rented from device providers by various healthcare institutions. These instruments are employed in specialized procedures related to implant surgeries and can be circulated among multiple healthcare facilities1. According to relevant literature, implant infections constitute approximately 45% of hospital-acquired infections2. These instruments often possess complex structures, are composed of diverse materials, and tend to be oversized or overweight, with most being made of solid metal. This composition makes them susceptible to condensate formation during the sterilization process. If the condensate is not promptly removed, wet packs may develop, leading to sterilization failure. The incidence of wet packs in loaned surgical instruments ranges from 12.95% to 37.5%3–5, with factors such as sterilizer failure, rigid sterilization container systems, and improper loading procedures contributing to their occurrence6. Wet packs can compromise the sterile barrier, exposing sterile instruments to contamination and potentially delaying or canceling patient procedures. Furthermore, the reprocessing of these instruments is both time-consuming and labor-intensive, thereby increasing the workload and associated costs. Recent studies have identified that loaned surgical instruments, particularly those composed of specialized materials and intricate structures, were susceptible to temperature delay, during pressure steam sterilization processes7,8. Additionally, it has been observed that various loaned surgical instruments require specific sterilization parameters, including distinct sterilization and drying times9. Consequently, optimizing the sterilization conditions for loaned surgical instruments is essential to ensure effective sterilization outcomes.

A case study investigating the positioning of the phaco handpiece lumen head demonstrated that vertical placement of the head during sterilization results in optimal sterilization conditions10. The process of sterilization loading is subject to numerous variables, which can significantly impact the effectiveness of sterilization. The AAMI ST-79 comprehensive guidelines emphasize the necessity for institutions to establish written protocols or procedures for the loading of sterilizers, ensuring that items requiring identical sterilization cycle parameters are processed within the same batch11. Similarly, the Chinese industry standard WS 310 advises that instruments, appliances, and items composed of similar materials be grouped together for sterilization12. In clinical practice, the techniques used for loading sterilizers are predominantly based on empirical placement, provided they do not contravene these principles. However, these practices primarily consider the weight of the instruments when selecting sterilization procedures, often neglecting the material and structural characteristics of the instruments.

The Product Family Categorization Theory13 is derived from the standard “Product Families and Processing Category for Steam Sterilization of Medical Devices” (YY/T 1600–2018), which utilizes four attributes of medical instruments—design, weight, material, and sterile barrier system—as indicators of sterilization resistance. This theory articulates the challenges of sterilization in terms of steam penetration resistance levels, facilitating the classification and more efficient loading of sterilized items. This approach aims to guide the selection of sterilization procedures, offering novel insights for standardizing pressure steam sterilization operations in clinical settings. Despite its potential, there is a paucity of reports on the practical application of this theory. Consequently, this study proposed a load optimization project for sterilized items based on the Product Family Categorization Theory, using loaned surgical instruments as a pilot project. The objective was to validate the feasibility of this theory and to assess its impact on the efficacy of pressure steam sterilization.

Methodology

Study design

This quasi-experimental study employed a non-contemporaneous controlled experimental design. Surgical instruments that were sterilized between January and March 2023 served as the control group, while those sterilized from April to June 2023 constituted the test group.

Study setting and subjects

The research was conducted within the Central Sterile Supply Department (CSSD) of a large tertiary care hospital, which has a capacity of 5,500 beds and an average daily surgical volume ranging from 600 to 800 procedures. The CSSD encompasses a floor area of 3,200 m2 and is equipped with six pressure steam sterilizers (Beliman, MST 9618HS2). Based on a sample size calculation using G*Power software (3.1.9.7 Version), with a medium effect size of 0.3 and a two-sided test with 0.05, the required total sample size was determined to be 172 batches.

Ethical considerations

This study exclusively involved loaned surgical instruments and did not include human or animal subjects. All loaned surgical instruments utilized in this project were duly recorded by the hospital. The Institutional Review Board of our institution concluded that the study protocol was exempt from review.

Intervention program

Product family theory

Within the framework of product family theory, the attributes of surgical instruments pertinent to moist heat sterilization (Table 1) are systematically categorized into design (code a), material (code b), weight (code c), sterile barrier system (code d), and steam penetration resistance (code e). Each attribute is further stratified into various levels according to its potential impact on steam penetration resistance. A higher coded number signifies increased resistance to steam penetration. The ultimate class of steam penetration resistance for a kit of surgical instruments is determined by the highest-level attribute within that kit.

Determination of steam penetration resistance class

The research team conducted a meticulous evaluation of each instrument within each kit, adhering to the rating criteria established for general surgical instrument attributes as outlined in the Product Family Categorization Theory (Table 1). An independent assessment by two evaluators was employed to rate the attributes of design, material, weight, sterile barrier, and steam penetration resistance for each loaned surgical instrument. Higher ratings corresponded to increased challenges in achieving effective sterilization. In cases where the two evaluators disagreed on the results, a third evaluator was engaged to assist in the assessment process. After evaluating each kit, the instrument with the highest steam permeation resistance was assigned as the steam permeation class for that kit of loaned surgical instruments (Fig. 1). Initially, the project team conducted an inventory of all loaned surgical instrument kits registered with our hospital, amounting to a total of 98 kits.

Expert review

The research team convened a review meeting with a panel of 10 experts specializing in CSSD, hospital infection control, and medical devices. The panel comprised 4 senior-level and 6 associate senior-level professionals, all of whom possessed at least a bachelor’s degree. The mean age of the experts was 50 years (SD = 5.53), and their average professional experience spanned 19.5 years (SD = 4.96). During the meeting, the experts evaluated the steam penetration resistance class of various loaned surgical instrument kits using a 5-point importance scale. The authority coefficient for the experts involved in this project was calculated to be 0.854, signifying a high level of expertise in the domains of disinfection, sterilization, and infection control research. The coefficient of variation ranged from 0 to 0.24. The Kendall consistency coefficient was 0.352 (p < 0.001), reflecting a high degree of agreement among experts on the results of steam penetration resistance class of loaned surgical instruments and a credible result of expert opinion consistency.

Determination of sterilization procedures

The pressure steam sterilizer employed in our hospital was equipped with two pre-set sterilization programs for surgical instruments: the standard instrument program and the heavy-duty sterilization program. Based on prior research14, the project team found that surgical instruments made from specialized materials, such as wood, silicone, and high-polymer substances, tend to exhibit temperature delay. This delay could lead to the accumulation of condensation and an increased risk of wet packs. According to the theory of product series, the steam permeation resistance of instruments manufactured from these specialized materials typically ranges from e5 to e6. Consequently, the drying time required for these instruments was longer than that of the standard instrument procedure, aligning with the heavy-duty sterilization procedure in this sterilizer. Implementation

The team systematically recorded the steam permeation resistance classes of all loaned surgical instruments in a computerized format within the existing information traceability system. This was done to facilitate the ease of operation for the staff. Prior to the project’s implementation, all staff members received training to perform system maintenance. Consequently, personnel responsible for packaging could distribute the loaned surgical instruments based on their steam permeability resistance classes, as indicated on the traceability labels. Instruments were then placed in different loading racks according to their resistance classes to optimize the sterilization process. Following a unified loading process, the sterilization staff scanned the traceability bar code of each instrument kit individually. For the test group, the sterilization procedure was selected based on the highest steam penetration resistance class within the batch. In contrast, the control group based their selection on the oversized and overweight instrument markings on the items to be sterilized. Specifically, the heavy-duty instrument procedure was employed when oversized and overweight instruments were included in the batch, whereas the standard instrument procedure was utilized for standard weight. All loaned surgical instruments underwent the sterilization cycle according to the designated sterilization program. To minimize bias arising from variations in sterilizers, only pressure steam sterilizers No. 6 or 5 were employed for the project implementation period.

Outcome measurement

Selection of sterilization procedure

The selection of sterilization procedure was observed in two groups, both prior to and following the implementation of the optimized loading protocol. The procedures included both standard instrument procedure and heavy-duty instrument procedure.

Sterilization monitoring result

Sterilization monitoring encompasses physical, chemical, and biological assessments, as well as wet pack inspections. Physical monitoring is defined by maintaining a temperature fluctuation range within ± 3℃ during sterilization, a sterilization duration of no more than 5 min, and a sterilization pressure between 201.7 kPa and 229.3 kPa. Both chemical and biological monitoring employ the 3 M Attest™ Super Rapid 5 Steam-Plus Challenge Pack (41482 V), which includes an integrated Type 5 Chemical Integrator and a Super Rapid Readout Biological Indicator. Successful sterilization is indicated when the black reagent on the Type 5 Chemical Integrator advances to the “ACCEPT” window. The Super Rapid Readout Biological Indicator is incubated in a 3 M Rapid BioReader at 56℃ for 24 min, a negative result signifies a successful sterilization process. For wet pack inspection, after a cooling period of 30 min, both the interior and exterior of the instrument package, as reported by the operating room nurse or CSSD staff, must be free of visible water droplets or moisture. All these criteria must be simultaneously met to confirm successful sterilization.

Data collection

Following the completion of the sterilization cycle, an independent quality inspector, who is not involved in the project, systematically documents the procedures, as well as the results of physical, chemical, and biological monitoring, and assesses the wet pack conditions for each batch of sterilizers.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22.0 software. Categorical data were analyzed using the chi-square test. The significance level was set at α = 0.05, with differences considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Monitoring results

Prior to and following the implementation of the program, the pass rates for physical, chemical, and biological monitoring in both the control and test groups were consistently 100%. However, the number of batches in which wet packs were observed in the control group was 11 (3.74%) and 3 (0.99%) in the test group, with the difference being statistically significant (χ2 = 4.905, P = 0.027).

Discussion

Pressure steam sterilization remains the most prevalent method employed in hospitals, with sterilization personnel playing a crucial role in the process. Previous research has indicated that sterilization staff often possess limited professional knowledge regarding the primary structural characteristics of sterilizers, as well as their instruction manuals or guidebooks and operational principles, thereby posing significant operational safety risks15. The majority of current sterilization personnel in CSSD are skilled workers with non-medical backgrounds and lower educational levels. This, combined with the substantial workload in clinical practice, often leads to an overload, which is not conducive to establishing optimal sterilization conditions for the complex structures of loaned surgical instruments.

The current pressure steam sterilizers employed in medical institutions were typically equipped with various pre-set sterilization procedures to accommodate different combinations of surgical instruments. According to a survey conducted by Liu L. et al.16, only 9.2% of the manuals for large steam sterilizers in 98 hospital CSSD in Guangdong Province have been provided a comprehensive description of the sterilization procedures. This lack of detailed information hindered the scientific selection of appropriate sterilization procedures by the sterilizing staff. The study’s findings indicated a significant increase in the use of heavy-duty procedures following the program’s implementation. This shift may be attributed to the previous reliance on external identification cues for selecting sterilization procedures, overlooking the specific requirements of certain instruments composed of specialized materials. Following the implementation of the program, the traceability system automatically displayed the steam penetration resistance class alongside the name of each scanned kit of loaned surgical instruments. The visualization interface enhanced the capacity of sterilization personnel to promptly identify the most complex loaned surgical instrument kits for sterilization within each batch and to select the optimal sterilization procedure according to the steam penetration resistance class. This has reduced subjective judgment and standardizes the selection of sterilization procedures. Van et al.17 found that an increase in the weight of the sterilizer load prolongs the sterilization process. In terms of loading capacity, the WS310-2009 standard previously mandated that each batch in pre-vacuum pressure steam sterilization should not exceed 90% of the chamber’s volume. Although this requirement has been revised and is no longer rigorously enforced in contemporary standards, this project complies with standard requirements for each batch loading. Adequate steam permeation spaces are maintained between instrument kits to prevent overloading, thereby mitigating the effects of excessive loading.

The monitoring of pressure steam sterilization utilized conventional evaluation methods that integrate physical, chemical, and biological technologies to facilitate real-time tracking of each sterilization process in China18. The assessment and approval of quality were based on the outcomes of these three primary monitoring methods, as well as the occurrence of wet packs. The study indicated that all three monitoring approaches achieved a 100% pass rate. However, significant differences were noted in the incidence of wet packs. This discrepancy may arise from the similar resistance to steam permeability across batches following the implementation of the optimized loading program, which contributed to establishing favorable sterilization conditions and minimized inter-item interference13. It was important to note that the formation of wet packs was influenced by multiple factors, including steam quality and equipment performance, with loading procedures accounting for only 10% of occurrences19,20. Although wet packs as an indirect indicator of sterilization quality, prior research has indicated that variables such as the characteristics of surgical instruments, types of packaging, loading procedures, and positioning were all related to the loading process and may contribute to the formation of wet packs. This underscores the significance of wet packs as a crucial factor influencing sterilization quality. Future research should aim to identify more sensitive indicators to better elucidate the complexities associated with the management of sterilization loads.

The ISO standard was established in 2013. However, there is a paucity of research applying this theory, resulting in limited availability of research data. Although this study is of exploratory importance, it is constrained by certain limitations, including its single-center design and exclusive focus on empirical research involving surgical instruments with relatively complex structures, which may restrict the representativeness of the sample. Additionally, during the assessment of steam penetration resistance in loaned surgical instruments, the research team encountered difficulties in classifying certain instruments made from specialized materials or featuring intricate structures based solely on theoretical frameworks. This necessitated further deliberation to accurately determine their classification. The paucity of theoretical research in this domain introduced a degree of instability into the assessment process. Moreover, each instrument requires individual evaluation, which entails substantial preliminary work and demands advanced information technology capabilities. These factors may impede its widespread implementation. Future research could benefit from investigating the application of intelligent systems to acquire foundational data on the attributes of various instruments. With the integration of big data, this theoretical approach is likely to be more readily applicable in clinical practice.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that an optimal loading program for loaned surgical instruments significantly enhances the categorization of instruments with similar attributes for loading purposes. It also aids sterilization personnel in swiftly identifying the most challenging instrument kits to sterilize. Furthermore, the program contributes to the standardization of sterilization procedure selection for pressure steam sterilizers, reduces the incidence of wet packs, and ensures effective sterilization outcomes. However, this study was limited by manpower and time constraints, being conducted solely on loaned surgical instruments within a single-center setting. Future research should aim to expand the sample size to further refine and validate the theoretical framework established herein.
