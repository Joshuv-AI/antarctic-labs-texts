KBDR: document retrieval based on graph matching with knowledge enhancement
Authors: Jingxuan Liu, Yihan Huang, Jialuoyi Tan, Zhen Hua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Document retrieval refers to the identification of document contents that are semantically most relevant within a corpus. Existing matching algorithms may not recognize the synonymy of all documents for vocabulary, leading to retrieval inaccuracies. To address this limitation, this paper proposes Knowledge Based Document Retrieval (KBDR) that utilizes graph-based knowledge expansion. Firstly, named entity recognition and relation extraction are used to construct document graphs. Secondly, it expands entities using external knowledge bases such as HowNet and ConceptNet to enrich semantic connections. Thirdly, applies a Graph Attention Network (GAT) to model inter-entity dependencies for relevance ranking. Furthermore, we use five (i.e., CIPP, CNSE, CNSS, AAN, and OC) datasets to evaluate, KBDR outperforms baselines, achieving the highest Recall@1 of 25.88% on the CNSE. Overall, the KBDR can enhance efficiency and robustness in document retrieval, addressing conceptual deviations and advancing the field toward more complicated retrieval systems.

Introduction

Document retrieval helps users efficiently find relevant papers on search engines and guarantees the originality of technical solutions during patent applications1. However, a challenge in this field is dealing with synonymy in such documents. These synonyms blur deeper semantic relations, leading to conceptual deviations that make it difficult for the retrieval system to match results with the user’s intent.

Document retrieval requires a profound understanding of the synonymy and semantic relationships between documents. When documents are long paragraphs, the semantics of individual words and phrases may be hidden within complex document structures. Niu et al.2 combined the Siamese network structure with BiLSTM to extract deep features of sentences. Furthermore, Yang et al.3 applied the attention mechanism to the Chinese semantic matching task and improved the representation of feature vectors for sentences. Meanwhile, Xu et al.4 proposed Logiformer, a graph transformer network that integrates logical and syntactic branches to enhance logical reasoning by modeling long-distance dependencies between logical units. However, previous methods have achieved success in estimating the semantic distance between document pairs, they have not addressed synonymy between documents.

Existing methods face challenges in addressing conceptual deviations, particularly in the document retrieval task. Traditional neural networks often struggle to model long-range dependencies within paragraphs, which limits their ability to capture deeper synonymy relations and leads to a reliance on surface-level features. In addition, constructing graphs based on sentences or phrases with punctuation fails to capture global semantic relations in large paragraphs and results in complex and inefficient graph construction.

To address a set of documents that cover the same words but are written by different authors at different times and vary in depth, detail, clarity, and precision, we propose Knowledge Based Document Retrieval (KBDR), a novel document retrieval framework that integrates semantic knowledge expansion with graph-based reasoning and redefines the representation space on which graph learning operates. Specifically, KBDR jointly models semantic and structural information through a coordinated graph refinement and matching pipeline. Our method explicitly conditions the representation prior to graph propagation and reduces structural heterogeneity as well as lexical variability before similarity estimation. This representation conditioning establishes structurally comparable document graphs, enabling more reliable and semantically coherent cross-document matching. Accordingly, the technical contribution of this paper lies in the principled integration and coordination of refinement operations within a document-level graph representation paradigm. By emphasizing structural comparability and semantic normalization, the framework shifts the focus from model complexity to representation consistency, which remains a critical yet underexplored factor in document-to-document retrieval.

In this paper, we focus on a document–document retrieval setting, where both the query and the candidates are full-length documents rather than short keyword queries. This setting naturally arises in applications such as patent prior-art search, same-event news retrieval, and scholarly article recommendation, where an incoming document is used to retrieve semantically related documents from a large archive. It therefore differs from classical ad-hoc retrieval and calls for models that can directly compare the semantics and structure of long documents. The following steps outline the construction and processing pipeline of our proposed model. Firstly, the named entity recognition and relation extraction are used to extract the triplet set from the document and construct the document graphs. Then, the document graph is expanded by mapping entities to a knowledge base. After that, the document and entity features are obtained through RoBERTa. Next, the entity features are weighted by attention, the representations of the entities are updated using a Graph Attention Network (GAT), and finally, the matching scores are calculated using graph similarity. To sum up, our model can combine entity relations in the document, not just linear or neighborhood relations. Related work

Document retrieval

Document retrieval including document-level similarity and relevance matching, aims to enable the efficient identification of semantically related documents within a large corpus. Several researchers have proposed enhancing document retrieval performance. Karpukhin et al.5 introduced the Dense Passage Retriever (DPR), a bi-encoder architecture initialized with BERT. This model is trained discriminatively using pairs of queries and relevant documents, incorporating hard negatives sourced from BM25. Building upon this work, Xiong et al.6 extended the approach by dynamically mining hard negatives using the model itself, which improved retrieval precision on large-scale datasets such as MS MARCO. While both rely on dual-encoder structures, Xiong et al.’s method demonstrates that the dynamic selection of negatives can mitigate overfitting to fixed retrieval patterns observed in DPR. Guu et al.7 further advanced this direction by integrating the retrieval model into the pre-training of BERT, thus jointly optimizing retrieval and representation learning. In contrast, Ram et al.8 proposed an unsupervised alternative using pseudo-examples derived from document spans, demonstrating that retrieval can be effectively learned without explicit relevance labels. Bhopale et al.9 and Nguyen et al.10 instead focused on improving retrieval quality through query reformulation and attentive representation learning, respectively, addressing the challenge that long documents often contain only partially relevant sections.

Overall, these models collectively rely on dense vectorization and dual-encoder structures but differ in supervision strategies (supervised vs. unsupervised), negative sampling mechanisms (static vs. dynamic), and document granularity (passage- vs. document-level). Karpukhin et al.’s and Xiong et al.’s approach depends heavily on annotated relevance pairs, whereas Ram et al. eliminate this need through pseudo-labeling. Guu et al. emphasize end-to-end optimization, contrasting with the modular retriever design of Bhopale et al. and Nguyen et al. Despite their progress, these methods mainly emphasize surface-level semantic matching while neglecting document structure and logical coherence. Such limitations highlight a gap in bridging semantic retrieval with structural reasoning, motivating the need for retrieval models that can capture both semantic relevance and inter-concept relations.

Graph matching

Graph-based methods have been proposed to capture structural relationships within and across documents. Liu et al.11 introduced the Concept Interaction Graph (CIG), representing documents as weighted graphs whose nodes denote keywords or keyword sets. This design enables the comparison of concept-level relations across documents. Huang et al.12 took a step further with the Discourse-Aware Graph Network (DAGN), which integrates discourse relations with contextual token embeddings derived from pre-trained language models. While CIG models co-occurrence and content overlap, DAGN explicitly models discourse flow, offering a more comprehensive representation of textual structure. Building on this, Li et al.13 proposed AdaLoGN, which fuses symbolic reasoning with DAGN’s neural framework to combine logical inference with deep contextual understanding.

These approaches share the goal of leveraging graph structures for relational reasoning but differ in the type of relations they model. Specifically, CIG focuses on concept co-occurrence, DAGN on discourse flow, and AdaLoGN on symbolic logic. Nevertheless, each suffers from structural limitations: CIG overlooks logical order, DAGN’s chained network weakens long-range dependencies, and AdaLoGN’s symbolic extraction introduces noise due to low-quality relation detection. Hence, while they represent an important evolution toward structural document modeling, they remain insufficient for retrieval tasks requiring scalable and logically coherent document alignment.

Triplet extraction

Triplet extraction aims to extract structural information from unstructured text and is widely used in relation extraction. Xu et al.14 proposed a dual-channel span pruning mechanism that captures sentiment relations between target and opinion spans, while Chia et al.15 introduced RelationPrompt, which integrates language model prompting to generate synthetic relation samples tailored to specific relation types. Wang et al.16 extended this line of research by representing inter-word relations through a three-dimensional tensor, allowing fine-grained semantic correlation modeling.

Although all three studies share the objective of capturing relational information, they diverge in focus: Xu et al. emphasize explicit span interactions, Chia et al. focus on prompt-based relation synthesis, and Wang et al. explore multi-dimensional relation encoding. Yet, they all operate at the sentence level and do not extend these structured representations to multi-sentence or document-level contexts. Consequently, despite their success in relation extraction, their potential contribution to large-scale document retrieval, especially in linking entities and relations across documents, remains largely unexplored.

Semantic similarity

Semantic similarity anticipation is a process of identifying and linking similar meanings across different texts. Whalen et al.17 provided one of the earliest document-level similarity datasets using patent data, establishing a foundation for quantitative evaluation. Liu et al.18 refined similarity modeling by combining paragraph-level and sentence-level semantics, whereas Lee et al.19 and Bekamiri et al.20 further leveraged transformer-based encoders to improve contextual embedding quality. Lin et al.21 later combined dense lexical and semantic representations into hybrid embeddings (DHRs), demonstrating superior retrieval performance.

These studies converge on dense semantic representation but diverge in modeling granularity and fusion mechanisms. Whalen et al. emphasize lexical overlap at the document level, Liu et al. balance sentence and paragraph semantics, and Lee et al. and Bekamiri et al. shift toward contextualized embeddings with transformer backbones. Lin et al. unify these directions through hybrid embeddings that blend lexical precision with semantic abstraction. Despite their strong semantic alignment capabilities, these models struggle with long-range dependencies, complex logical relations, and explicit reasoning across entity connections. This limitation suggests that semantic similarity alone is insufficient for document retrieval tasks requiring structured, logic-aware understanding.

Comparative discussion

Across the four research lines reviewed, dense retrieval, graph matching, triplet extraction, and semantic similarity, clear patterns of methodological evolution and complementary strengths emerge. Document retrieval methods [5–10] achieve scalability and efficiency through vectorized matching but lack interpretability. Graph-based models [11–13] enrich structural representation but face scalability and noise issues. Triplet extraction methods [14–16] provide fine-grained relational insight but are mostly limited to sentence-level data. Semantic similarity models [17–21] excel in holistic semantic alignment yet fail to incorporate explicit logical or relational cues. The similarities among these studies lie in their shared pursuit of improved document understanding through representation learning, while their differences arise in the level of abstraction (semantic vs. structural), type of relations modeled (dense vs. graph vs. symbolic), and scalability to large corpora. Collectively, they expose a persistent research gap: no existing approach simultaneously captures document-level semantics, structural relations, and logical dependencies. Addressing this gap forms the central motivation and positioning of the present paper, which aims to integrate semantic similarity, relational graph modeling, and structural reasoning into a unified framework for document retrieval22,23.

Document graphs construction

Document-level triplet extraction studies have shown that extracting the relations of entities in a sentence in the form of triplet groups can effectively mine the intrinsic information of long sentences, including the explicit relation between two entities, and can also be easily fine-tuned for different domains and tasks with a high degree of adaptability25. For English text, open information extraction (OpenIE)26 tools are typically employed to automatically discover relation triples without requiring pre-specified relation types. In contrast, for Chinese text, the Language Technology Platform (LTP)27 is widely used to extract structured triplets by leveraging its comprehensive Chinese linguistic analysis capabilities.

The triplet extraction process typically relies on predefined relation types and domain-specific rules form OpenIE and LTP, which determine how entities and relations are recognized and paired. For example, in the sentence “The purpose of the present invention is to provide mechanical handling lines. These handling lines are designed to achieve high working efficiency.” The extracted triplet [[‘invention’, ‘provide’, ‘mechanical handling lines’], [‘handling lines’, ‘achieve’, ‘high working efficiency’]] is obtained28 because the model or method is configured to treat ‘invention’ and ‘handling lines’ as the primary subject performing the action ‘provide’ and ‘achieve’ while ‘purpose’ is considered part of the contextual framing rather than the core relational entity. The semantics-based document graph is constructed in two steps. First, the document is partitioned into triplets, with entities as nodes and relations as edges. The entities ‘invention’ and ‘mechanical handling lines’ are connected by the relation ‘provide’, while ‘handling lines’ and ‘high working efficiency’ are connected by the relation ‘achieve’. These triplets form the initial structure of the graph, where entities are nodes, and relations are edges. Next, for each pair of neighboring triplets in the graph (including the last node of the former triplet and the first node of the latter triplet), bidirectional arrows are used to connect them based on the contextual order relation, defined as “relevance.“ For instance, ‘mechanical handling lines’ from the first triplet is linked to ‘handling lines’ from the second triplet through the “relevance” relation. This connection represents the potential semantic relationship between the neighboring triplets in the document, ensuring that the document graph reflects both the entities’ interactions and their semantic relationships across the entire text.

In view of the fact that the document usually contains many domain-specific terms and complex sentence structures, it is necessary to conduct an in-depth extension on the set of triplets parsed by triplet extraction. By extracting each entity or relation from the content of the document and associating each relation with a certain number of concepts, the content of the document can be converted into a formally defined document graph. In our implementation, OpenIE is applied at the sentence level to extract triplets from each individual sentence. These sentence-level triples are then aggregated to form a document-level graph, where entities are connected by relevance edges. It is important to note that our approach does not extract triples that span multiple sentences. Additionally, we do not explicitly resolve pronouns (e.g., “it”, “they”) to their antecedents. Most of the entities in our document graphs are technical noun phrases, and any pronoun-only nodes are filtered out during entity normalization, ensuring the clarity and accuracy of the extracted graph.

Given two input documents, DocX and DocY, retrieved from the document library, named entity recognition and relation extraction are first applied to parse the documents and extract the triplets that form their initial representations. Knowledge expansion

Composite-entity Splitting

To enhance the granularity of triplet representations, we employ Composite-entity Splitting, a method that identifies and decomposes composite entities within extracted triplets. Composite-entities are those connected by specific discourse cues:

Conjunction: signaled by coordinating conjunctions such as “and” or “or”;

Juxtaposition: indicated by discourse markers like “meanwhile” or “yet”.

This splitting process relies on relation types (Conjunction and Juxtaposition) from the Penn Discourse TreeBank 2.0 (PDTB 2.0)29 to detect valid segmentation points. The algorithm processes a triplet containing a composite entity and splits it into its constituent sub-entities. Each resulting sub-entity is then paired with the original relation and subject/object to form new, atomic triplets. For instance, a triplet like [‘invention’, ‘comprises’, ‘motor and sensor’] would be decomposed into two distinct triplets: [‘invention’, ‘comprises’, ‘motor’] and [‘invention’, ‘comprises’, ‘sensor’].

Composite-entity Splitting focuses on breaking down complex entities into smaller, atomic components based on the relationships between them. These relationships are identified using a set of Conjunctions and Juxtaposition terms, which are derived from the PDTB 2.0, a well-established resource for discourse-level relations. These terms indicate how entities are semantically linked within a document and serve as cues for splitting the composite entities. Importantly, the Conjunctions and Juxtaposition we reference are not related to general coordinating conjunctions (e.g., “and”, “or”) or discourse markers (e.g., “however”). Instead, they refer to specific terms that highlight relationships between entities at the document level, based on PDTB 2.0’s semantic categories. This distinction ensures that our approach is focused on identifying meaningful entity relationships rather than simple sentence-level connections.

Composite-entity Splitting refines triplet representations by breaking down composite entities into their minimal, most meaningful parts using juxtaposition and conjunction cues. It then recombines these smallest elements with the original relation to create new triplets. This fine-grained approach ensures that each triplet captures the core semantic details, resulting in a more transparent and precise graph representation. Algorithm 1Composite-entity Splitting

This method systematically identifies and decomposes composite entities connected by discourse cues, using established linguistic patterns from the Penn Discourse TreeBank. Relation replacement

Relation Replacement is designed to correct potential misclassifications in the initial triplet extraction, specifically when a noun is incorrectly identified as a relation. This module processes a triplet only if its head entity is a composite phrase that can be segmented into a noun followed by a verb, and the original relation of the triplet is a noun.

When these conditions are met, the module performs a replacement: the original relation is substituted with the verb extracted from the head entity, while the head entity itself is replaced by the preceding noun. For example, given an initial triplet such as [‘system improve’, ‘basic’, ‘performance’], where ‘basic’ is a noun and the head entity ‘system improve’ can be split into the noun ‘system’ and the verb ‘improve’, the module would yield a corrected triplet: [‘system’, ‘improve’, ‘performance’]. Knowledge expansion

To address vocabulary heterogeneity across document graphs, this layer performs entity synonym unification using established knowledge bases, HowNet and ConceptNet30. The HowNet and ConceptNet as general-domain knowledge resources are designed to address the distinct linguistic characteristics of our multilingual evaluation datasets. For Chinese datasets, we leverage HowNet’s sophisticated lexical database, which offers comprehensive semantic networks and well-defined synonym sets tailored to Chinese linguistic structures, domain-specific terminology, and technical concepts commonly encountered in Chinese documents. For English datasets, we employ ConceptNet’s extensive English-centric knowledge graph, which covers a broad range of conceptual relationships and synonymy mappings within English lexical semantics. This careful selection of language-specific knowledge bases ensures optimal semantic coverage and terminological accuracy in each language, facilitating improved synonym matching and reducing term variability.

It is important to clarify that the term ‘knowledge expansion’ focuses on structured semantic normalization and conceptual consolidation within the existing document graph. By leveraging curated knowledge bases, the mechanism resolves lexical variability and aligns semantically equivalent entities under a unified representation. Therefore, ‘expansion’ should be interpreted at the semantic level rather than the structural level: the knowledge base is used to expand the semantic equivalence space of entities, enabling concept-level alignment across document graphs without altering the underlying graph topology. This design prioritizes contextual accuracy and structural stability over aggressive graph augmentation.

The core mechanism operates through synonymy-based entity consolidation, where entities within each document graph are mapped to their canonical representations using the respective knowledge base’s synonym networks. To ensure that synonym replacement remains contextually accurate, we adopt a context-constrained unification strategy. Specifically, synonym substitution is performed only when the candidate synonym appears within the entity set of the same retrieval instance. This unification process systematically reduces lexical variation within each linguistic domain while preserving semantic integrity, thereby minimizing vocabulary-induced heterogeneity in the resulting document graphs, enhancing retrieval robustness against terminological variations in both Chinese and English document collections. The process operates as follows:

First, all entities from both graphs are mapped in HowNet and ConceptNet. For each entity, the system retrieves its synonym set from the knowledge base. However, a synonym is considered valid only if it belongs to the same connected component defined in the knowledge base, thereby avoiding cross-sense substitution caused by polysemy. If any synonym from this set is also present in the entity set, all occurrences of synonymous entities from the entity set are replaced with this entity. When multiple synonymous forms co-occur, the canonical representation is deterministically selected as the first matched synonym returned by the knowledge base, ensuring consistent normalization across retrieval instances. If no unambiguous mapping can be established, the system abstains from replacement to preserve semantic integrity.

For example, consider an entity set containing ‘motor’, ‘actuator’, and ‘drive’. If ‘motor’ is linked in HowNet and ConceptNet to synonyms including ‘actuator’, and both terms appear in the entity set, then all instances of ‘actuator’ are replaced with ‘motor’. This ensures that different but semantically equivalent entities are consolidated into a single representation. Importantly, the synonym sets in HowNet and ConceptNet are structured to prevent ambiguity. Each word is linked to a specific connected component set, meaning that synonyms are only replaced within the same set, thus avoiding the introduction of ambiguity from multiple possible meanings of a word. When synonymous entities are identified across documents, they are consolidated into a unified representation, effectively reducing lexical variation while preserving semantic meaning. The module thus serves as a crucial semantic bridge in the KBDR, mitigating vocabulary mismatch problems and facilitating more robust identification of conceptually related documents across different linguistic expressions and terminological conventions.

Experiments

Datasets

To evaluate the performance of KBDR across diverse document retrieval scenarios, we employ five datasets spanning multiple domains, languages, and retrieval objectives. The selection criteria are designed to assess our model’s capabilities in handling domain-specific terminology, structural complexity, and semantic matching under varied contextual constraints.

CIPP: To the best of our knowledge, no publicly available document retrieval dataset on intellectual property. We have captured a logic relation dataset: Chinese Intellectual Property Patents (CIPP), which professional examiners label. The CIPP contains document-level Chinese patents obtained from the China National Intellectual Property Administration and covers five domains of intellectual property, such as mechanical engineering, chemistry, control engineering, intelligent electromechanical and microelectronics. The CIPP dataset contains 121 patent sets, each set includes 100 to 400 patents, which are labeled to indicate whether or not the content of the patents described in the sets of patents is similar. The average word count of all documents in the dataset is 1,463, and the maximum value is 11,709. These datasets are publicly available for research.

Chinese News Same Event (CNSE)11: This dataset contains 29,063 pairs of Chinese news documents, with each pair labeled according to whether they report on the same breaking news event. Focusing on timeliness and event consistency judgment, the dataset has an average document length of 734 words.

Chinese News Same Story (CNSS)11: Comprising 33,503 pairs of news documents, this dataset is annotated based on whether the documents belong to the same news story. Compared to CNSE, CNSS places greater emphasis on identifying semantic relationships across multiple articles under broader thematic contexts, thereby helping assess the model’s retrieval performance at a more generalized topic level.

ACL Anthology Network (AAN)35: This dataset includes computational linguistics papers published between 2001 and 2014. Open Citation (OC)35: Containing approximately 7.1 million papers in computer science and neuroscience, it serves as a scalability benchmark, testing our model’s efficiency in large-scale scholarly retrieval and its ability to leverage citation networks for enhanced semantic matching.

All five datasets consist of long documents with average lengths exceeding typical paragraph-level texts. This characteristic makes them effective for validating the semantic understanding and matching capabilities of our proposed method in real-world document retrieval scenarios. Besides, across all five datasets, each dataset is organized into 100–1000 retrieval instances. In each instance, there is one labeled query document and one labeled target document (the ground-truth most relevant document to the query), while all remaining documents in the same collection are treated as non-relevant candidates. Concretely, for each retrieval instance in the dataset, we rank all documents within the same instance (excluding the query document itself) with respect to the labeled query document. Under the benchmark annotations, only the provided target document is considered relevant to the query, while all remaining documents in that instance are treated as non-relevant. This evaluation protocol is applied consistently across the train, validation, and test splits. Table 1 shows the detailed breakdown of the CIPP, CNSE, CNSS, AAN and OC datasets. For all datasets, we use 60% of all samples as the train set, 20% as the validation set, and 20% as the test set. Evaluation metrics

The evaluation metrics are Recall@k, NDCG@k, Accuracy and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{1}$$\end{document} for test retrieval results. k is a positive integer representing the first k retrieval results. Recall@k measures the coverage of retrieval results in a retrieval system. It represents the proportion of how many relevant documents are included in the first k retrieval results. NDCG@k is achieved by comparing the first k results returned by the system with the ideal ranking (all relevant documents in descending order of relevance) to measure the performance of the system.

To provide a comprehensive evaluation, we also incorporate universally applicable evaluation metrics Accuracy and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{1}$$\end{document}. Accuracy evaluates the overall correctness of the retrieval outcomes, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{1}$$\end{document}, combining Precision and Recall, offers insight into the system’s efficiency in identifying relevant documents without overlooking significant results. This combination of metrics ensures a robust evaluation framework that not only emphasizes retrieval accuracy but also the system’s ability to effectively rank and retrieve relevant information.

Baselines

To position our approach with respect to previous studies on document–document retrieval, we compare KBDR against a set of representative baselines that have been widely applied in similar settings. Together they cover the main methodological paradigms that operate at the document level and thus serve as strong references for evaluating the effectiveness of KBDR. These include the representation-based models ARC-II36 and DSSM37, which represent classical neural matching architectures; the terminology-based model LDA38 as a strong non-neural probabilistic baseline; the pre-training-based model RoBERTa32 as the current de facto standard in transformer-based semantic understanding; and the graph-based model CIG11 as a representative of recent graph-structured approaches. Additionally, we include KBDR without Knowledge Expansion (KBDR-K) as an ablated variant to isolate and validate the contribution of our knowledge expansion module. This diverse baseline selection enables a systematic assessment of KBDR’s advantages across different architectural models and specifically highlights the value of integrating knowledge enhancement with graph-based reasoning.

Implementation details

All models are trained on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU.

For KBDR and KBDR, we use RoBERTa-based encoders to obtain initial document and entity representations. For Chinese datasets we adopt the chinese-roberta-wwm-ext checkpoint, and for English datasets we use roberta-base. The maximum sequence length is set to 512 tokens. We optimize the models with Adam, using a learning rate of 7e-6, weight decay of 0.01, and dropout rate of 0.1. The batch size is 16 and we train for 10 epochs on each dataset. The hidden dimension of the Graph Attention Network is 256, and we use 3 attention layers with 4 attention heads each.

For the LDA baseline, we use the Gensim implementation with 100 topics on Chinese datasets and 120 topics on English datasets. The Dirichlet priors are set to α = 0.1 and β = 0.01, and we run 1,000 Gibbs sampling iterations. The resulting topic distributions are used as document representations for similarity computation. ARC-II and DSSM are implemented following their original architectures. We use 300-dimensional word embeddings initialized from pretrained word2vec models, a hidden layer size of 256, and ReLU activations. Both models are trained with Adam, a learning rate of 1e-3, batch size 128, and 10 training epochs. Early stopping is applied based on validation loss.

For the RoBERTa baseline, we use the same pretrained checkpoints as in KBDR (chinese-roberta-wwm-ext for Chinese and roberta-base for English). We fine-tune RoBERTa for document–document similarity with a maximum sequence length of 512, learning rate 7e-6, batch size 16, and 10 epochs, using Adam with weight decay 0.01.

The CIG baseline is re-implemented according to the settings reported in the original work. We construct document graphs and apply 3 graph convolution layers with hidden size 128. The model is trained with Adam, using a learning rate of 1e-3, batch size 64, and 10 epochs. All baselines are tuned on the validation sets within these ranges, and the best-performing checkpoints are used for test evaluation.

The experiment results are given in Tables 2, 3 and 4. The experiment results shows KBDR outperforms all baselines on Chinese datasets. Specifically, KBDR demonstrates performance with a 2.43% increase in Recall@1 compared to CIG and a 4.52% enhancement over RoBERTa on the CIPP dataset. Moreover, in terms of NDCG@1, KBDR achieves a 6.60% improvement relative to ARC-II, and an 7.00% increase over LDA on the CNSE dataset. Additionally, KBDR exhibits a 5.74% enhancement in Recall@1 over DSSM, and a 2.53% increase in comparison to CIG on the CNSS dataset. From Tables 2, 3 and 4, we can observe that KBDR consistently achieves the best NDCG@k and Recall@k on CIPP, CNSE and CNSS. In particular, KBDR yields higher NDCG@1 and Recall@1 than baselines, so the observed improvements of KBDR in NDCG@k and Recall@k can be attributed to the proposed knowledge-based graph refinement rather than implementation differences. These results indicate that, on Chinese datasets, KBDR is more effective at placing truly relevant documents at the very top of the ranked list than existing document–document baselines.

In addition, the ablation variant KBDR-K (KBDR without the Knowledge Expansion) consistently performs worse than the full KBDR model on all Chinese datasets. This consistent performance degradation further demonstrates that the refinement components within Knowledge Expansion, including composite-entity splitting, relation correction, and knowledge base-based synonym unification, enhance the structural consistency between document graphs and consequently improve top-ranked retrieval accuracy.

These results can be attributed to KBDR’s capability to integrate semantic representation with structured relational reasoning. Unlike traditional embedding-based models that rely solely on word-level or contextual similarity, KBDR leverages knowledge-based entity expansion and graph reasoning to align semantically equivalent yet lexically diverse terms. The use of HowNet and ConceptNet enriches the semantic space, allowing the model to better capture conceptual equivalence and mitigate synonym, induced mismatches common in Chinese texts. Furthermore, by employing graph attention to weight critical entity relations, KBDR effectively identifies key conceptual dependencies within documents, especially beneficial for lengthy, information-dense patent and news texts.

Regarding the NDCG@3 and NDCG@5 metrics, the moderately lower values observed across Chinese datasets can be explained by the inherent characteristics of Chinese document retrieval. In real-world Chinese corpora, the number of genuinely relevant documents per query is limited due to high topic specificity and strong semantic concentration. This scarcity restricts performance gains beyond the Top-1 ranking. This decline in ranking performance indicates that Knowledge Expansion plays a critical role in enhancing semantic alignment between document representations. By refining entity structures and normalizing relational semantics, the module strengthens the structural coherence of document graphs, thereby facilitating more accurate relevance estimation and improving top-ranked retrieval effectiveness.

The performance improvement primarily stems from KBDR’s relational reasoning and cross-entity semantic expansion. Through the integration of knowledge bases such as ConceptNet, KBDR enhances its ability to connect semantically similar academic or technical concepts that are often treated as distinct entities in dense embedding models. Additionally, the graph-based attention mechanism captures the logical and causal dependencies between research statements, improving both retrieval accuracy and interpretability. In academic document retrieval, each query typically corresponds to a narrow and well-defined research topic, limiting the number of truly relevant documents. Furthermore, variations in academic writing style and terminology, especially across disciplines, contribute to vocabulary mismatches that hinder consistent mid-rank performance.

To sum up, KBDR transcends traditional models by incorporating expanded entities’ logical relations and leveraging the capacity to construct directed graphs from expanded entities. Relation replacement is integrated with composite-entity splitting to create a unified structural refinement stage preceding knowledge expansion, ensuring the extracted triplets maintain structural consistency. The improvements observed in both knowledge expansion and retrieval performance further substantiate the synergistic contribution of these refinements to the overall effectiveness of the model. In ablation studies, KBDR without the knowledge expansion module exhibits significant drops in retrieval accuracy, underscoring the effectiveness of our expansion approach. Particularly in document retrieval scenarios, KBDR excels by accurately capturing and depicting complex logical structures in documents, including causal, sequential, and conditional relations. This capability enhances the model’s comprehension of document meaning, facilitates inference of connections between entities, and supports in-depth semantic analyses.

Computational complexity analysis

Let n denote the number of tokens in a document, m is the number of extracted entities (nodes), E is the number of edges (relations), d is the hidden dimension, L is the number of RoBERTa layers and T is the number of GAT layers. The model consists of three main components: (1) RoBERTa-based encoding, (2) knowledge expansion, and (3) graph reasoning via GAT. The RoBERTa encoding costs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(L\cdot n \cdot d)$$\end{document}. Graph reasoning costs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(T \cdot E \cdot d)$$\end{document} due to neighbor aggregation in each GAT layer; for a sparse document graph where E=O(m), this becomes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(T \cdot m \cdot d)$$\end{document}. Knowledge expansion requires m knowledge base lookups; with a hashed/indexed knowledge base, this is O(m · s) (where s is average synonyms per entity) or O(mlog|K|) for indexed search. Therefore, the overall time complexity is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(L\cdot n\cdot d+T\cdot E\cdot d+KnowledgeBase\_cost)$$\end{document}.

This complexity remains comparable to standard transformer-based retrieval frameworks, but the use of relation pruning and sparse graph construction effectively reduces redundant message-passing operations. Empirical profiling indicates that the proposed model achieves approximately 20% - 25% faster inference than baselines in this paper while maintaining equivalent or competitive retrieval accuracy.

These characteristics confirm that the model is both computationally efficient and practically scalable for large document collections.

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose that the KBDR solve the synonymy problem of document retrieval. Firstly, consider the entities in a triplet extracted by dependencies as nodes and construct a semantic-based document graph using the relations of the triplet as edges. Then, the expansion document graph is constructed based on the knowledge expansion and iterates and strengthens the expansion document graph using the GAT. With the help of professional examiners from the China National Intellectual Property Administration, we captured and evaluated a new document retrieval dataset, which includes 121 patent sets. The experiment results show that KBDR can improve document retrieval.

This improvement primarily arises from KBDR’s ability to capture both semantic and relational dependencies among entities. By incorporating relation-aware graph expansion, KBDR can better distinguish semantically consistent entities, thereby reducing false matches commonly observed in dense embedding-based retrievers. Moreover, the attention mechanism dynamically emphasizes the most informative relations, enabling the model to retain long-range contextual cues and enhance both precision and recall. These findings indicate that the performance gain is not accidental but results from a deeper integration of semantic relevance and structured reasoning within the retrieval process. Besides, the method is general and has potential in various fields other than intellectual property, such as search scenarios of literary works.

In terms of computational efficiency, KBDR achieves a time complexity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(L\cdot n\cdot d+T\cdot E\cdot d+KnowledgeBase\_cost)$$\end{document}. This represents a notable improvement over graph-based baselines. Empirical evaluation further shows that KBDR reduces average inference time by approximately 20% - 25% while maintaining competitive retrieval accuracy, demonstrating its scalability and practical applicability in large-scale retrieval scenarios.

However, the proposed KBDR framework is subject to certain limitations. Its retrieval performance is contingent upon the accuracy of the underlying triplet extraction and the quality of the constructed document graphs, which currently capture primarily local entity-relation structures and may not fully represent broader discourse-level dependencies. Future research could explore integrating large language models to further enhance the semantic understanding and feature extraction capabilities of the KBDR, particularly for processing complex and lengthy documents such as literary works. Moreover, the current effectiveness of KBDR depends on the quality of the entities and edges in the document graph. Besides, employing more complicated natural language processing tools may improve the extraction of interrelations between entities in neighboring non-triplets within a document and create more affluent and accurate document graphs.
