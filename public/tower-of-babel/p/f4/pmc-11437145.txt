Hydroxyproline increases inflammation and Uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC) infection in female rats
Authors: Parveen Kumar, Zhengqin Yang, Huma Fatima, Tanecia Mitchell
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Calcium oxalate (CaOx) kidney stones may be associated with urinary tract infections (UTIs). However, the mechanisms for this association are not well-established. The objective of this study was to investigate the effect of oxalate on immunity and UTI development in vivo. Female Sprague-Dawley rats were fed a control diet for 3 days before continuing this diet or starting a 5% Hydroxy-L-proline diet (HLP; oxalate precursor) for 7 days. Rats were subsequently infected transurethrally with Uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC, a bacterium that causes UTI) and sacrificed 3 days later. Urine, blood, kidney, and bladder samples were collected. Urinary oxalate levels, renal CaOx crystal deposition, inflammatory markers, and the bacterial load were assessed using ion chromatography-mass spectrometry, immunohistochemistry, qRT-PCR, western blotting, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays, or colony forming unit assays. Animals fed HLP and infected with UPEC had a significant increase in urinary oxalate levels, renal CaOx deposition, pro-inflammatory macrophages, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and bacterial loads compared to animals fed the control diet with UPEC infection. In addition, HLP-fed animals had significantly reduced anti-inflammatory renal macrophages and anti-inflammatory cytokine levels in their plasma, urine, and kidneys. These findings suggest that oxalate may play a novel role in the propagation of UTI development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73246-6.

Introduction

The incidence and prevalence of kidney stones (KS) is steadily increasing across the country and world1. There are several types of KS and those primarily consisting of calcium oxalate are the most common1. Urinary oxalate excretion from either dietary sources or endogenous synthesis is positively correlated with the risk of developing a KS2,3. Oxalate is found in many plant-derived foods4. Those with CaOx KS are typically advised to avoid foods rich in oxalate content to prevent increased urinary oxalate excretion which may lead to elevated CaOx supersaturation and CaOx crystal formation. It has been reported that CaOx crystals within the kidney can cause cellular injury, inflammation, oxidative stress, and promote the development and growth of CaOx KS5–7. We previously established that a dietary oxalate load can promote urinary crystals and systemic inflammation in healthy adults8–10.

CaOx KS disease has been thought to be associated with urinary tract infections (UTIs)11,12. However, the specific mechanisms for this potential association have not been extensively investigated. UTI can develop in the lower or upper urinary tract, or both locations. The management of those afflicted with UTI consumes significant healthcare resources and imparts a substantial economic burden13. UTI has been associated with both infection stones (i.e. Struvite) and metabolic stones14. The overall prevalence of UTI in KS formers (including adults and children) is between 28 and 34%; whereas, the overall prevalence of UTI in non-stone formers is between 7 and 11%15–17. The reason for this vast difference in prevalence is not well defined. However, urinary oxalate levels, bacteria, and immunity could be key factors contributing to this association. It has been reported that individuals with KS and UTI have higher urinary oxalate levels compared to individuals with UTI alone18. In addition, increased urinary oxalate excretion has been proposed to be linked to the development of recurrent UTI19. Escherichia coli (E. coli) is the most common bacterium known to cause UTI and has been found in CaOx KS matrix, and the urine of individuals with CaOx KS20–23. Further, E. coli has been shown to promote CaOx crystal growth and aggregation24,25. Another potential driver of the link between CaOx KS and UTIs is the immune system. Macrophages are essential for combating and phagocytosing pathogens and foreign material from the body. We recently determined that oxalate impairs the immune and anti-bacterial response of macrophages in response to UPEC in vitro26. Thus, it is possible that oxalate may contribute to the association of CaOx KS and UTI by influencing immunity.

Hydroxyproline (HLP) is a unique amino acid that is present in milk, dairy products, meat products, and gelatin-containing foods27. Results of several experimental studies have demonstrated that feeding animals HLP increases urinary oxalate excretion which can result in renal CaOx crystal deposition and tissue injury28–31. This can stimulate the recruitment of monocytes/macrophages to mitigate inflammation and promote crystal removal32. We previously reported that rats fed HLP have a significant increase in renal macrophage populations as well as increased inflammatory gene expression30. In addition, we established that oxalate directly impacts monocyte or macrophage metabolism and redox homeostasis using cell lines and primary cells from healthy adults9,26. Specifically, oxalate compromised macrophage anti-bacterial response and increased intracellular and extracellular bacterial count in vitro26.

The objective of the present study was to investigate the effect of HLP-mediated increased urinary oxalate excretion on immunity and infection in vivo. We hypothesized that HLP feeding causes renal inflammation, injury and crystal deposition, and infection in female rats. Consistent with our prior findings in male rats, we report that HLP feeding induces urinary oxalate levels in female rats. We also demonstrate that animals exposed to HLP diets and UPEC infection have increased renal CaOx crystal accumulation, injury, and inflammation. In addition, UPEC infection was elevated in the kidneys and bladder. These findings suggest a novel role of oxalate in the propagation of UTI development which warrants further investigation.

Methods

Reagents

Reagents, kits, and antibodies were from Sigma-Aldrich Chemicals (St. Louis, MO), Thermo Fisher Scientific (Asheville, NC), and other vendors mentioned below.

Animal model and bacterial conditions

All experimental protocols and procedures were approved by the University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol # 22182). This research was performed according to UAB guidelines, the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals regulations, and the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 for the reporting of in vivo experiments33. All animals were maintained in a UAB animal care facility under standard conditions with a 12/12 h light-dark cycle and given ad libitum access to water and food. Female Sprague-Dawley rats (Charles River Laboratory, Wilmington, MA) between the ages of 6–8 weeks old were fed a control diet (cat # TD.95027; Envigo, Madison, WI) for 3 days before being randomized to consume the control diet or to start a 5% Hydroxy-L-proline (HLP; cat # H54409; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) diet for 7 days. All animals were subsequently transurethrally exposed to UPEC (CFT073 strain - ATCC # 700928; 8 × 108 cells/rat in 100 µl) using a catheter coated with mineral oil to induce a UTI. We did not observe vesicoureteral reflux with this procedure. UPEC culture conditions were performed as previously described26. Three days following infection, the animals were sacrificed to collect blood, urine, and tissues (bladder and kidneys). Body weight, food intake, water intake, urinary volume and urinary oxalate levels were recorded on Day 0, 7, and 10. All animals were monitored daily to check for signs of distress and no adverse events or side effects occurred during the study. The animal study design is shown in Fig. 1A. The data are from two experiments (total of n = 6 animals per experimental group).

Urine collection and analyses

All animals were single-housed in metabolic cages to collect 24-h urine on Days 0 (before starting the HLP diet), 7, and 10 (3 days post infection) in mineral oil to prevent evaporation. The urine volume and pH were recorded prior to processing and storing samples at -80°C. Urinary oxalate levels were determined using ion chromatography-mass spectrometry as previously described34. Urinary creatinine levels were measured using a Medica EasyRA chemical analyzer (Medica, Bedford, MA). Urinary oxalate levels were normalized using creatinine levels.

Tissue and blood collection

Isoflurane was used to anesthetize the rats before perfusing them with 1X sterile PBS through their aorta before harvesting their kidneys and bladder. The kidney capsule and peri-hilar fat were removed gently before weighing the kidneys. Portions of the kidney were either stored in Trizol for qRT-PCR or snap frozen in liquid nitrogen for western blotting and biochemical analyses. One half of the remaining kidney tissue was fixed in formalin for histological analyses, while the other half (~ 50 mg) was used to determine the bacterial count using the colony forming unit (CFU) assay as described below. In addition, half of the bladder was fixed in formalin for histological analyses and the other half of the tissue was used to determine bacterial count as described below. Blood was collected using heparin coated vacutainer tubes. Plasma was collected after centrifugation at 2000xg for 10 min at 4 °C and stored in the − 80oC until analysis.

Histological assessment

Kidney or bladder tissue was collected and paraffinized using standard methods to make 5 mm sections on glass slides. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene and ethanol followed by Hematoxylin & Eosin staining using a Vector Laboratories kit (cat# NC1470670; Newark, CA 94560) or Pizzolatto staining35. The renal sections were microscopically analyzed and scored blindly by Dr. Huma Fatima, an experienced pathologist at UAB as we have previously described30. The parameters used to assess kidney integrity included evaluating the presence of CaOx deposits, tubular dilation, loss of brush border, epithelial cell sloughing and granular casts in the cortex and medullary regions of kidney36–38. In brief, renal injury was assessed using the following the scoring method: no injury (score 0), tubular dilatation and loss of brush border (score 1), tubular epithelial cells vacuolization (score 2), apical blebbing (score 3), and epithelial cells sloughing (score 4). The renal cortex and corticomedullary junction were scored in each region using 10 high power fields (40x) for each slide. The extent of renal injury was calculated as follows: (number of tubules (score > 0) x corresponding score)/total number of tubules examined.

In additional experiments, immunohistochemistry was performed to assess the expression of monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1, a chemokine involved in monocyte and macrophage recruitment and infiltration), pattern recognition receptor - Toll like receptor 4 (TLR4), as well as macrophage polarization markers for M1-like macrophages (iNOS, inducible nitric oxide synthase) and M2-like macrophages (mannose receptor C-type I (CD206) and Arginase 1 (Arg1)). Kidney or bladder sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated followed by antigen retrieval. The slides were subsequently treated with hydrogen peroxide followed by blocking with 1% BSA in PBST for 20 min at room temperature (RT). Next, the slides were stained with the following primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C to assess macrophage and myeloid cell markers: MCP-1 (1: 200; cat # PA5-34505, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA), Arg1 (1: 200; cat# PA5-29645; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA), CD206 (1:200; cat# ab64693; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and iNOS (1:200; cat# PA1-036; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA). Slides were washed with PBST and incubated with secondary antibody (1:400 dilution) for 30 min at RT the following day. DAB staining was used to evaluate immunoreactivity followed by Hematoxylin counterstaining. Slides were dehydrated and mounted with a coverslip using Cytoseal mounting medium. Images were captured using a compound microscope (10x and 20x objectives). ImageJ analysis software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD) was used to quantify DAB staining intensity for proteins of interest at 10x magnification.

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR gene expression

Total RNA was isolated from frozen kidney tissue (~ 10 mg) after homogenizing the tissue in Trizol and subsequently using a RNeasy mini kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (cat# 74104; Qiagen, Hilden Germany). RNA quality was assessed by running RNA on 2% denaturing gel and checking the integrity of 18 S and 28 S RNA. cDNA was synthesized from RNA using QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (cat# 205311; Qiagen, Hilden Germany). RNA expression of the genes of interest (iNOS, Arg1, CD206, IL-10, IL-1β and Kidney injury molecule-1 (Kim-1)) were evaluated using a SYBR Green-master mix (cat# A25742, ABI, Waltham, MA). The PCR cycle involved 40 cycles and was processed at 95 °C for 30 s and 60 °C for 2 min. Data were calculated using the ΔΔCT method39 and normalized to GAPDH. Primers used in this study were designed using Primer3Plus software, an EMBL software (https://www.primer3plus.com/), by utilizing the nucleotide sequences from the NCBI Nucleotide database. Western blotting

Total protein was isolated from liquid nitrogen crushed frozen kidney tissues (20 mg) using homogenization. Samples were dissolved in 1X RIPA lysis buffer containing a protease inhibitor and gently rocked at 4°C for 30 min. Next, the samples were centrifuged for 30 min for 14,900 rpm at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred to fresh tubes and the protein concentration was determined using a Bradford assay (cat# 1856210, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Protein extracts (30 µg) were subsequently heated in sample loading buffer and ran on 5–20% SDS-Page gels prior to being transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. The membranes were cut prior to being probed with the following primary antibodies overnight at 4°C: Arginase-1 (1:500; cat# PA5-29645; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA), CD206 (1:500; cat# ab64693; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), iNOS (1:500; cat# ePA1-036; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA), IL-10 (1:500; cat# sc8438; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas TX), IL-1β (1:500; cat # ab9732; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and GAPDH (1:2500; cat# 3683 S; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA). On the next day, the blots were incubated with secondary antibody (1:3000; Goat anti-RAT HRP, cat# 31470; Goat anti-mouse HRP, cat# 31430) after washing with 1X PBST. All blots were developed using clarity western enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (cat# 1705060, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and imaged on a GE ImageCount LAS400 (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL). The protein bands were quantified using ImageJ software40. All full and cut blot images are provided in the supplementary material.

Cytokine assays

Urine and plasma samples were centrifuged to remove any cellular debris prior to storing the supernatants in the − 80 °C. Samples were thawed on ice and diluted 1:2 in sample dilution buffer prior to analyzing various cytokines using ELISA kits from Invitrogen (Waltham, MA). The analytes of interest included: IL-6 (cat# 88-50625), IL-10 (cat# 88-50629) and IL-1β (cat# 886016). Cytokine levels were subsequently measured using a BioTek plate reader (Winooski, VT).

Colony forming unit (CFU) assay

CFU assays were performed to assess bacterial growth in the kidneys, bladder, and urine following UPEC infection. Animals were transferred to metabolic cages to collect 24-hour urine collections for 3 days post infection (Days 1, 2, and 3). In addition, urine was collected directly from the bladder (500 µl) prior to harvesting the bladder. All urine was serially diluted ten-fold in 1X PBS. Dilutions of urine from the bladder (10− 3 and 10− 4) and 24-hour urine (10− 4 and 10− 5) were plated on Luria-Bertani (LB) agar plates containing no antibiotics and incubated overnight in an incubator at 37°C. The following day, the CFU per mL was determined by calculating the number of colonies present and multiplying the values by the dilution factor. Lastly, kidney and bladder tissues were homogenized using a plastic homogenizer in 0.025% SDS followed by serially diluting the lysates in 1X PBS. Two dilutions of the tissue lysates (10− 3 and 10− 4) were plated on LB plates overnight at 37°C. A negative control containing only PBS was plated on LB plates and used for all experiments.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software (version 9.1.1; La Jolla, CA) was used to make all graphs. Statistical comparisons between the groups were analyzed using unpaired non-parametric test (Mann Whitney rank test) and F-test for significance and variance analyses. Data in the figures are represented as median and range or mean ± SEM. CFU assay data are presented in log form as geometric means, with error bars representing standard deviation. Data were considered statistically significant at p-value < 0.05, two-tailed.

Results

The effect of HLP feeding and UPEC infection on body weight, food and water intake, urinary volume, and urinary oxalate excretion

All animals consumed the control diet prior to being randomized before consuming either the control or HLP diets for 7 days. All animals were subsequently exposed to UPEC infection as described in the methods section (Fig. 1A). At the conclusion of the study, urine, blood, and tissues were collected from the animals. The body weight for both cohorts increased over time but was not significantly different between the groups (Fig. 1B). Food intake was significantly elevated with similar levels in both cohorts on Day 7. However, this intake was significantly reduced for both groups following UPEC infection at Day 10 (Fig. 1C). Water intake was higher in animals fed the HLP diet throughout the study compared to those on the control diets (Fig. 1D); however, this was not significantly different. The urinary volume was significantly increased on Day 10 in animals on the HLP diet following UPEC infection compared to animals fed the control diet and infected with UPEC (Fig. 1E). Urinary oxalate excretion was significantly higher on Day 7 in rats fed HLP compared to the control group. Urinary oxalate excretion was further increased in HLP-fed rats following UPEC infection at Day 10 (Fig. 1F).

HLP feeding increases renal CaOx deposition and tubular injury in UPEC infected animals

The effects of HLP feeding and UPEC infection on crystal deposition and renal injury were examined using histological staining and scoring. As shown in Fig. 2A, Pizzolatto staining revealed that renal CaOx deposition on Day 10 was increased in rats exposed to HLP and UPEC infection compared to animals on the control diet with UPEC infection. Renal injury was also evident as there was a significant increase in tubular lumen dilation and loss of brush border in the outer cortex of the kidney in HLP fed rats compared to those on the control diet with UPEC infection (Fig. 2B). In addition, this was also observed in the cortico-medullary junction of the kidney (Fig. 2C). Tubular epithelial cell vacuolization, apical blebbing, and epithelial cell sloughing did not occur in these animals and was not significantly different among the two groups (Compiled Datasets File – Fig. 2).

HLP feeding alters the mRNA and protein expression of macrophage and inflammatory markers in UPEC infected animals

Next, we evaluated the mRNA levels and protein expression of several genes involved in inflammation or kidney injury. We first examined the expression of macrophage markers, iNOS (M1-like macrophages) and Arg1 and CD206 (M2-like macrophages). Both iNOS mRNA and protein levels were significantly increased in rats fed HLP and infected with UPEC (Fig. 3A and G, and 3H). In contrast, both Arg1 and CD206 mRNA and protein levels were significantly decreased in these animals (Fig. 3B, C and G, and 3H). We further determined the mRNA and protein levels of IL-10 (anti-inflammatory) and IL-1β (pro-inflammatory) cytokines in the kidney. Both IL-10 and IL-1β levels were significantly reduced in HLP-fed animals (Fig. 3D and E, and 3H). Next, renal injury was assessed by measuring Kim1 mRNA levels. As shown in Fig. 3F, Kim1 mRNA levels were significantly elevated in the kidneys from animals fed HLP and infected with UPEC.

HLP feeding alters the secretion of cytokines in the urine and plasma of UPEC infected animals

The secretion of pro-inflammatory (IL-6, IL-1β) and anti-inflammatory (IL-10) cytokines in the urine and plasma of rats fed HLP or control diets with UPEC infection was determined using ELISAs. Both IL-6 and IL-1β levels were significantly elevated in the urine of animals exposed to HLP and UPEC infection (Fig. 5A and B, respectively). In contrast, IL-10 urinary cytokine levels were significantly reduced in these animals (Fig. 5C). Interestingly, both IL-6 and IL-1β levels were not altered in the plasma (Fig. 5D and E, respectively). However, IL-10 cytokine levels were significantly reduced in the plasma of rats exposed to HLP and UPEC infection (Fig. 5F).

UPEC bacterial growth and survival is increased in the kidney, bladder and urine of animals fed HLP

The effect of the control and HLP diets on acute UPEC infection survival and growth in animals was enumerated using CFU assays. There was a significant increase in the number of bacteria found in the kidney (Fig. 6A), bladder (Fig. 6B), urine isolated from the bladder (Fig. 6C), and 24-hr urine collections (Fig. 6D − 6 F). The number of bacteria in 24-hour urine collected at Day 1 was not different between the two groups (Fig. 6D). However, urine from Day 2 and 3 were significantly elevated in the animals on the HLP diets compared to those on the control diet (Fig. 6E F). To determine if oxalate impacts bacterial survival, additional experiments were performed to evaluate the effect of oxalate on CFT073 bacterial growth without cells in vitro. We determined that oxalate significantly reduced the growth of CFT073 bacteria (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Discussion

The influence of dietary oxalate on CaOx KS risk is well established4. However, few studies have examined the effects of dietary oxalate on immunity during KS formation. We previously established that feeding male rats HLP increases oxalate levels, inflammation, and macrophage populations in the kidney as well as alters signaling pathways important for macrophage polarization and function30. The purpose of the present study was to determine whether HLP-induced oxalate levels in the urinary system could alter the immune response and promote bacterial infection in animals. To examine this, we fed female rats either a HLP enriched diet or a control diet for 7 days, infected them transurethrally with UPEC, and sacrificed them three days later. We selected a 3 day post inoculation period as rodents have been reported to clear infection after 3 days41. The UPEC bacterial strain used for these studies was CFT073 (a human bacterial strain isolated from a woman with acute pyelonephritis), which is frequently used to study UTIs.

E. coli is one of the most common bacterium responsible for both complicated and noncomplicated UTIs42 and has been suggested to be associated with KS11,16,43. It has been reported that removing stones in patients could reduce UTIs44. In addition, it has been shown by several research groups that E. coli is present in the stone matrix20–23. It has also been demonstrated that UPEC inoculation increases CaOx crystal deposition in a sodium glyoxalate injection mouse model23. In a recent study by An et al., it was reported that E. coli bacterial flagella were essential for promoting CaOx KS formation as well as inducing oxidative stress and inflammation in the kidney20. Kanlaya et al. demonstrated that renal cells consistently infected with E. coli had more crystal cell adhesion via ezrin translocation to the cell membrane14. These findings along with others support the rationale that the interactions between bacteria and oxalate may influence the association of CaOx KS formation and UTIs.

HLP has been used frequently to promote urinary oxalate excretion and crystal deposition in animals27,29,31,45. In this study, HLP increased urinary oxalate excretion in rats compared to those on controlled diets over time. In addition, urinary oxalate levels were further elevated in animals following UPEC infection. However, we do not have a definite explanation for this outcome. We also observed an increase in urinary volume in animals fed HLP and infected with UPEC. The rise in urinary volume could be a result of increased infection and inflammation. This could be due to renal injury as a consequence of CaOx crystal deposition. We also observed prominent signs of renal injury in these animals.

Based on these findings, we further explored the immune status of the kidneys following infection. The increased CaOx crystal deposition and renal injury occurring after HLP feeding stimulated several immune and inflammatory responses, including a significant increase in iNOS levels (pro-inflammatory M1-like macrophage marker). In contrast, these same animals had significantly lower CD206 and ARG1 levels (M2 anti-inflammatory macrophage markers) in their kidneys. It is also important to note that increased iNOS and CD206 levels were expressed by renal tubular epithelial cells. It has been previously reported that renal tubular epithelial cells express iNOS during infection and an inflammatory response46. In addition, it has been determined that CD206 positive M2 macrophages accumulate around damaged renal tubular cells for tissue regeneration in patients with acute tubulointerstitial disease47. In an AKI mouse model, renal proximal tubular epithelial cells induced M2 macrophage polarization by secreting colony stimulating factor-1 to promote renal tubular epithelial cell regeneration48. These data suggest that oxalate impacts inflammatory cytokine signaling in the kidney during infection and this ultimately affects renal macrophage populations and the immune response. These results are similar to our earlier studies where HLP increased M1-like macrophage marker, iNOS, while reducing M2-like macrophage marker, ARG1 in the kidney of male rats30.

Previous studies have shown that MCP-1, a chemokine involved in the recruitment and activation of monocytes and macrophages, are elevated in human renal disease including KS49–51. In addition, oxalate ions have been shown to stimulate MCP-1 in renal epithelial cells52. In the present study, MCP-1 levels were significantly higher in the kidney of HLP fed rats with UPEC infection compared to those similarly infected on the control diet. The higher MCP-1 levels suggests that more monocytes and macrophages were being recruited to the kidney in response to HLP-mediated damage. We chose to examine TLR4 levels in the kidney as it is critical for innate immunity and has been reported to stimulate pro-inflammatory cytokine signaling and fatty acid metabolism53. In addition, TLR4 has been shown to be activated in renal cells in response to oxalate and LPS treatment54. In the present study, TLR4 levels were significantly higher in animals exposed to HLP and UPEC infection. These findings suggest that pro-inflammatory signaling pathways are upregulated in this setting.

We also confirmed that inflammation was indeed upregulated in the urine of these animals. Consistent with the findings noted above, both pro-inflammatory cytokines, IL-6 and IL-1β, were significantly elevated in animals exposed to HLP and UPEC infection compared those on the control diet and infected with UPEC. Several clinical studies have demonstrated that IL-6 cytokine levels are increased in individuals with CaOx KS55,56. We also determined that IL-10, an anti-inflammatory cytokine, was significantly decreased in both the urine and plasma of these animals. IL-10 is essential for not only resolving inflammation and inducing wound healing but also modulating macrophage polarization57,58. Thus, reduced IL-10 anti-inflammatory signaling could promote increased renal injury, CaOx crystal deposition, inflammation, and reduced anti-inflammatory macrophage responses within the kidney as suggested by our earlier reports26,30,59. Lastly, bacterial growth and survival was elevated in the kidneys, bladder and urine of rats fed HLP compared to rats on the control diet suggesting that higher oxalate levels in the urinary system could alter the immune response and promote bacterial infection. We further determined that oxalate reduced the growth of CFT073 in vitro, which suggests that oxalate induced kidney damage and weakened immune response may be responsible for the increased bacterial growth and survival in the kidney and urinary tract. Thus, we postulate that both HLP and UPEC infection impairs the anti-inflammatory response and causes more inflammation, renal injury, and crystal deposition.

The data generated from this study suggest that the interaction between oxalate and bacteria may influence infection and crystal aggregation/growth. We acknowledge that our study has certain limitations including only using female animals for this study. We did this as it is well established that there are challenges studying UTI in male rats due to their urethral anatomy. In contrast, female animals are more feasible anatomically and are universally used to study UTI. Another limitation of this study was not assessing other urinary stone risk parameters.

Conclusions

We believe that this is the first study to demonstrate that HLP-induced urinary oxalate excretion exacerbates UPEC infection in vivo. While this is associated with alterations in immune response and heightened inflammation, other mechanisms warrant consideration in future studies. In addition, human translational studies are needed as this may help better define the association between UTI and CaOx KS formation.
