Research on the residual mechanical properties of hybrid fiber ECCs after high-temperature exposure
Authors: Jinyu Cui, Hao Jiang, Yunlong Zhang, Ao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this work, the mechanical properties of hybrid fiber-reinforced cementitious composites (CP-ECCs) composed of polypropylene fibers (PPs) with varying volume fractions and 1% volume fraction chopped carbon fibers (CFs), as well as the influence of different fly ash (FA) contents on material performance under both room temperature (20 °C) and high-temperature conditions, are systematically studied. Through experiments conducted at five target temperatures (20, 200, 400, 600, and 800 °C), the residual mechanical properties, fundamental mechanical properties, and microstructural evolution patterns of the CP-ECC were analyzed. The results indicated that the mechanical properties of the material generally tended to increase with increasing temperature, reaching peak strength at 600 °C, followed by a significant decrease at 800 °C. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis revealed that the incorporation of PP fibers effectively suppressed cracking and spalling in concrete at 600 °C. Moreover, the carbon fiber-like reinforcement structure formed by the CF fibers and molten PP fibers further enhanced the mechanical properties of the ECCs at elevated temperatures. This study innovatively designed a novel high-temperature resistant hybrid fiber ECC material. This material provides a new theoretical foundation for the application of ECCs in high-temperature environments, with significant theoretical value and practical significance.

Introduction

To overcome the inherent limitations of cementitious materials, Li et al.1–3 designed a class of high-performance fiber-reinforced engineered cementitious composites (ECCs) on the basis of the micromechanics of fiber‒matrix bonding. It is a new type of material that replaces coarse aggregates with chopped fibers. Its components include cement, fly ash, fine aggregates, and polymer fibers. This material exhibits ductility, damage tolerance, high durability, high compressive and tensile strengths, and excellent crack control capability, making it a high-toughness concrete4–6. However, cement-based composites exhibit significant drawbacks in high-temperature environments7, including a sharp decline in compressive strength, increased thermal deformation, a reduced elastic modulus, and widespread cracking and spalling phenomena, making the material highly susceptible to damage under high-temperature stress conditions8. These characteristics severely reduce its load-bearing capacity and durability. Therefore, increasing the high-temperature resistance of ECCs is both urgent and essential.

In recent years, the application of ECC has become increasingly widespread. For example, Han9 investigated the shear performance of steel-ECC composite slabs and reported that the ECC replacement ratio and steel beam web thickness significantly influence the shear capacity of steel-ECC composite beam‒slab systems. Pan et al.10 developed an ultralightweight ECC with a density below 1771.6 kg/m³, which maintained a compressive strength of 40 MPa after 28 days of curing. Gadhiya S et al.11 designed a sprayable ECC by integrating micromechanical and rheological principles. It exhibited strain hardening characteristics under tensile loading. Currently, fiber reinforcement technology is widely adopted in the enhancement of ECCs. For example, Tian et al.12 enhanced polyethylene engineering cementitious composites (PE-ECCs) by incorporating basalt fibers (BFs). The study revealed that BF fibers increased the first crack strength, ultimate tensile strength, equivalent flexural strength, and flexural toughness of the material. Zhang et al.13 investigated the energy dissipation and stress‒strain relationships of PVA fiber-reinforced ECCs under compression and tension. Their study demonstrated that incorporating different types of fibers into ECCs can significantly enhance their compressive strength, energy release rate and dissipation capacity, tensile performance, elastic strain energy, and fracture energy14. However, most existing studies focus on the mechanical properties of ECC materials at room temperature15–17, while the deterioration of ECCs in high-temperature environments remains an urgent issue to be addressed.

The internal components of ECCs vaporize in high-temperature environments, resulting in a pressure differential between the interior and exterior that causes cracking and speeling. The technology must simultaneously meet two key requirements: first, to mitigate the spalling of concrete under high-temperature conditions; second, to ensure that the material maintains reliable residual strength under high-temperature conditions. Zhang et al.18 investigated the effect of polypropylene (PP) fibers on polyethylene fiber-reinforced engineered cementitious composites (PE-ECCs) by partially substituting PE fibers with PP fibers in polyethylene-engineered cementitious composites. Their study revealed that PP fibers can effectively reduce explosive spalling in PE-ECCs. This is because the voids created by the PP fibers promote gas diffusion, rapidly equalizing the internal and external air pressures. This characteristic enables an ECC to maintain its structural integrity more stably in high-temperature environments. Liu et al.19 investigated the reinforcement of reinforced concrete beams with carbon fiber-reinforced polymers (CFRPs) and engineered cementitious composites (ECCs). The results indicated that the CFRP‒ECC composite strengthening technique significantly increased the cracking load, yield, and ultimate load of the beam. Although CFRPs and ECCs play different roles in reinforcement, the carbon fibers (CFs) themselves exhibit excellent mechanical applicability. Adding polypropylene fibers (PPs) and carbon fibers (CFs) as reinforcing fibers in ECCs can enhance the resistance of the matrix to explosive spalling and its high-temperature performance. Therefore, the hybrid design combining PP with CFs holds promise for preventing explosive spalling while maintaining satisfactory residual mechanical properties in ECCs under high-temperature environments.

This study develops a novel high-temperature-resistant CF-PP hybrid fiber-reinforced cementitious composite (CP-ECC) with enhanced mechanical performance at elevated temperatures. For this composite, manufactured sand was used as a fine aggregate to replace conventional river sand to increase the material strength while achieving environmental sustainability. To investigate the interactions and relationships between PP and CFs, we investigated the effect of fly ash (FA) on the overall material strength by replacing cement with different volume fractions. This experiment involved hybridizing PP fibers at volumes of 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5% with a fixed 1% CF. Fly ash (FA) was used at different replacement ratios (20%, 40%, and 60% by volume), and the influence of the FA content on the mechanical properties of the material was investigated. The material was subjected to high-temperature testing at five temperatures (23 °C, 200 °C, 400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C). Then, the mechanical properties of the CP-ECC after high-temperature exposure were investigated through three-point bending tests, compressive strength tests, pore characterization, mass loss tests, and residual mechanical property tests. Ultimately, the accuracy of the above analysis was verified at the microscopic level through scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

Materials and methods

Materials

The cementitious material used in this work was P.O. 42.5 grade ordinary Portland cement (OPC). The OPC used in this study was sourced from Yatai Dinglu Cement Co., Ltd., located in Jilin Province, China. A portion of the cement was replaced with Class I fly ash (FA). The FA used in this study was sourced from Borun Casting Materials Co., Ltd., located in China. The addition of an appropriate amount of FA is beneficial, as it enhances later-stage hydration, improves the workability of the paste, reduces material costs, and enhances the ductility of the ECC. Table 1 lists the primary oxide constituents of the cement and fly ash. Free silica and alumina constitute 76% of the total FA content. The primary minerals in the cement are calcium oxide and silicon dioxide, with calcium oxide accounting for 66.5% of the total. The particle size of FA is similar to that of OPC, and the FA particles are more compact than those of OPC20. Therefore, the incorporation of FA can alter the flowability, density, and rheological properties of cement mortar. This study employed a polycarboxylic acid high-performance water-reducing agent with a water-reduction rate of 27% and a density of 1.07 g/cm3. The particle size range of the manufactured sand is 0.1–5 mm, with a fineness modulus of 2.7. The manufactured sand used in this study was sourced from Lingshou County Haoqian Mineral Powder Processing Plant, located in China. Compared with traditional river sand, manufactured sand offers greater strength and environmental sustainability. Since the drying and cold shrinkage strains of ECCs during hardening are three times greater than those of ordinary concrete21, structures made with ECCs are prone to severe cracking. Adding an appropriate amount of expansive agent can reduce the shrinkage strain and increase the compressive strength of the material. A study by Hu, Zeng et al.22 investigated the effects of varying expansive agent contents on material properties. The results indicated that the incorporation of 4% and 8% expansive agent enhanced the compressive strength of fiber-reinforced concrete by 11% and 21%, respectively. Furthermore, the expansive agent demonstrated favorable compatibility with both steel fibers and the sand within the fiber-reinforced concrete matrix, collectively contributing to the improvement of the material’s compressive capacity.A study by Gao et al.23 indicated that a 9% volume replacement of cement with an expansive agent reduced certain mechanical properties of the material. It also reduced the material’s shrinkage strain by 31.9%. This effectively resolves the issue of shrinkage strain commonly found in ECC materials. Therefore, this test employed an expansive agent constituting 8% by volume of the CP-ECC to counteract the material’s shrinkage strain. Zhang et al.24 reported that adding methylcellulose as a fiber dispersant to concrete could reduce fiber agglomeration and increase the strength of the concrete. Given that the incorporation of PP and CFs in the test posed a high risk of fiber agglomeration, methylcellulose (MC) was added at a volume fraction of 1% as a fiber dispersant.

The properties of the PP and CFs are shown in Table 2. The density of the PP fibers is 910 kg/m³, which is lower than that of the CF fibers (1750 kg/m³). The PP fibers used in this study were sourced from Senxiang Building Materials Trading Company, located on Langli Street, Changsha County, China. The CF fibers used were sourced from Jiasheng New Materials Co., Ltd., located in Shenzhen, China. Compared with PP fibers, CFs are shorter in length and have a smaller diameter, but they exhibit greater strength and elastic modulus. The elongation at break of the PP fibers (15%) is approximately 7.5 times greater than that of the CF fibers (2.02%). The PP fibers appear white at 20 °C. At temperatures between 200 °C and 400 °C, they begin to melt and degrade, changing color from white to yellow, and ultimately transforming into a black powdery solid. In contrast, CFs exhibit exceptional high-temperature resistance, retaining certain mechanical properties even at 600 °C. At 800 °C, the color of the CFs darkens, and their mechanical properties deteriorate.

Mixing ratios and curing

To investigate the effects of CFs and PP fibers on CP-ECC performance under different temperature conditions, nine mix proportions were designed and tested, as shown in Table 3. The sand-to-binder ratio in the CP-ECC should not be excessively high, as an excessive amount of fine aggregate increases friction between the matrix and fibers, leading to fiber breakage and impairing the bridging effect. An excessively high water-to-binder ratio results in high flowability of the matrix, which weakens the adhesion between the fibers and the matrix and consequently reduces the tensile strength and ductility of the ECC. Therefore, a sand-to-binder ratio of 0.3 and a water-to-binder ratio of 0.26 were adopted in this experiment. The nine mix groups differ primarily in the PP fiber volume content (0.5%, 1.0%, and 1.5%) and the volume fraction of FA replacing cement (20%, 40%, and 60%). The samples were cured in strict accordance with the Chinese National Standard GB/T 17671-2021, “Test method of cement mortar strength (ISO Method).” The dimensions of the samples for both the flexural and compressive strength tests were 40 × 40 × 160 mm³.

Test method

High-temperature exposure

A ZH-252-12 multifunctional modular rectangular high-temperature furnace (Fig. 1) was used for the heating test. The test was conducted at five temperatures: 20 °C, 200 °C, 400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C. The furnace temperature was increased at a rate of 5 °C/min during the heating phase. To ensure the relative thermal stability between the resistance furnace and the test samples, the target temperature was maintained for 60 min before cooling to 20 °C. The specific heating process of the high-temperature furnace is shown in Fig. 2.

The sample was subsequently placed along the direction of the fixture, as shown in Fig. 3. The loading rate for the test was set at 50 N/s. SEM testing

The primary approach to investigating the decomposition of hydration products and the posthigh-temperature evolution of cementitious composites involves microscopic observation of internal damage mechanisms and material alterations following thermal exposure. Investigating the microstructural characteristics of the fiber‒matrix interface at different elevated temperatures, as well as the complex bridging effects of hybrid fibers within the matrix under varying fiber ratios, aims to validate the role of fibers in enhancing the overall performance of concrete. Square samples with a side length of approximately 1 cm were extracted from the fiber pull-out zone of the CP-ECC sample after flexural testing. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to analyze the ECC samples. The objective was to observe the microstructure of CP-ECC composite fibers with different mix proportions under microscopic conditions, analyze the mechanism of high-temperature effects on materials and fibers, and verify the conclusions drawn from the macroscopic analysis results.

Experimental results and discussion

This section primarily discusses the mechanical properties and SEM microstructure analysis of the CPP-ECC material under elevated temperature conditions.

Mechanical properties at high temperatures

Analysis of material color at high temperatures

Figure 5 illustrates the typical variations in surface color observed in specimens from Group 5 after exposure to high temperatures ranging from 20 °C to 800 °C. In this group, the contents of polypropylene (PP) fiber and carbon fiber (CF) were both 1%, while the fly ash (FA) content was 40%. As similar surface color changes were observed in other mix proportions, they are not discussed further herein. At 20 °C, the sample was uniformly dark, tending toward black. When the temperature reached 200 °C, the sample remained intact, and its surface color changed to pale gray. A dull sound was produced upon light tapping with a small hammer, which was attributed to the presence of a substantial amount of unhydrated OPC and FA. At 400 °C, partial degradation of the PP fibers occurred, releasing a distinct odor. The sample surface remained intact, grayish-white with localized yellowish scorch marks from decomposed PP fibers. The sound upon tapping became slightly clearer than that at 200 °C, yet overall, it remained relatively dull. At temperatures above 600 °C, the sample remained intact, with its surface exhibiting a uniform, pale orange‒yellow color. The formation of β-dicalcium silicate (β-2CaO·SiO2) and mullite (3Al2O3·2SiO2) within the matrix27 increased the brittleness and induced tiny pores, resulting in a relatively clear sound when tapped. At 800 °C, the sample remained intact with a uniform bright orange-yellow surface. CFs left localized black residues, and the surface was smooth yet more porous. As the chemical reactions proceeded to completion, the resulting sound upon tapping was markedly clear and metallic.

Analysis of cross-section pore characteristics

To evaluate the internal state and pore changes of the CP-ECC materials, 2 cm thick specimens were extracted from the central section of the cement mortar bars shown in Fig. 5 for pore structure analysis. The corresponding pore size distribution is presented in Fig. 6. The pores are marked with red circles, and the cracks are outlined with green lines. The numbers of pores and cracks at the material interface are presented in Table 4.At 20 °C, the material cross section exhibited a generally dense structure with small holes approximately 1 mm in diameter localized near some fibers. When the temperature increased to 200 °C, the number of pores in the cross section increased, and their color gradually increased. Notably, most of the pores were micropores with diameters of approximately 0.1 mm and were distributed more densely than those at 20 °C. Additionally, the distribution of pulled-out fibers decreased compared with that at 20 °C. This is because of the drying and cooling shrinkage strains generated during the hydration process of cement and fly ash (FA), which led to an increase in the volume of voids. As the temperature increased to 400 °C, a distinct hardening of the material matrix was observed, accompanied by the formation of finer and more densely distributed pores. Larger pores emerged in localized areas, and a substantial reduction in the fiber content was evident. This occurred because of the denaturation and melting of some PP fibers due to dry and cold shrinkage strains within the material, which subsequently formed a channel network. Some CFs remained within the matrix to continue carrying loads. The network pathways formed by the high-temperature melting and denaturation of the PP fibers effectively balanced the air pressure inside and outside the matrix while increasing the permeability of the material. This observation was consistent with the findings reported by Zhang et al.18. At 600 °C, the cross section exhibited a high density of pores, accompanied by localized microcracks. The interior of the cross-section remained light gray, whereas the exterior gradually transitioned to orange‒yellow. Only a small number of undamaged CFs remained inside the cross-section, working in conjunction with the material matrix. This occurred because of the formation of β-dicalcium silicate (β-2CaO·SiO2) and mullite (3Al2O3·2SiO2) within the material due to high-temperature reactions, leading to discoloration at the cut surfaces. The presence of CFs within the material enhances the high-temperature resistance of the CP-ECC, resulting in a lower core temperature than that at the surface. This temperature gradient caused a gradual color change and allowed some carbon fibers to be retained in the interior. This finding is consistent with the research conducted by Wang et al.28. After reaching 800 °C, the cross section exhibited numerous cracks and pores, with the overall color turning orange–yellow. The fibers inside the material were completely denatured, and the hydrated cement and FA underwent complete hydration, resulting in full dehydration of the cementitious network. Consequently, the material transitioned to a brittle state, accompanied by a significant reduction in mechanical properties.

Mass loss analysis

Figure 7 shows the mass loss curves of the samples with different mix ratios. As shown in Fig. 7, the CP-ECC materials undergo pyrolysis as the temperature increases. Overall, the specimen mass gradually decreased with increasing temperature. The mass loss generally accelerated significantly between 200 °C and 400 °C (the mass loss rate markedly increased). As the temperature reached 600 °C and 800 °C, the mass loss rate decreased. At temperatures of 200 °C, 400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C, the average mass loss for the samples with a PP fiber volume fraction of 0.5% was 4.41%, 12.8%, 17.94%, and 21.39%, respectively. For a PP fiber volume fraction of 1.0%, the average mass losses were 3.22%, 11.91%, 15.86%, and 19.67%, respectively. For a PP fiber volume fraction of 1.5%, the average mass losses were 2.01%, 10.57%, 15.11%, and 18.31%, respectively. These results suggest that increasing the PP fiber content helps reduce material mass loss and has a positive effect on the structural stability of the CP-ECC. At temperatures of 200 °C and below, the mass loss of the material is attributed primarily to the transformation of free water into bound water within the material matrix. Part of the water is expelled from the matrix through interfacial voids formed between the PP fibers and the matrix material, part combines with cement to undergo hydration reactions, and another portion participates in secondary hydration reactions with FA. When the temperature reached 400 °C, some bound water molecules in the CP-ECC broke free from chemical bonds. The C–S–H cementitious network, formed by the hydration of cement and FA29, undergoes chemical dehydration, and the hydrated products gradually transform into an anhydrous phase. The PP fibers begin to undergo partial denaturation, leading to a significant reduction in the mass of the materials. At temperatures between 400 °C and 600 °C, cement reaches its critical temperature and begins to decompose, generating free CaO. Some components form β-dicalcium silicate (β-2CaO·SiO2) and mullite (3Al2O3·2SiO2), accompanied by the release of water vapor. The PP fibers completely decompose, and partial carbonization of the CFs begins, resulting in a further reduction in weight. At 800 °C, the CFs undergo denaturation, and the PP fibers are completely destroyed. Cement, manufactured sand, and FA undergo complete denaturation. Owing to the combustion reaction, many pores are left within the material matrix, and the mass loss reaches its maximum.

Residual flexural strength

Flexural strength is one of the key mechanical properties of materials. The proposed method was applied to measure the flexural strength of the CP-ECC. This study systematically analyzed the effects of incorporating PP fibers and FA at different ratios on the flexural strength of CP-ECC composites. Furthermore, the formation mechanism of the flexural failure modes of the CP-ECC was elucidated.

Figure 8 shows the fracture morphology of the CP-ECC flexural sample under three-point bending loading. After flexural failure, a significant number of fiber pull-out traces were present on the material cross-sections. As the temperature increased, the content of pull-out fibers decreased. During the loading process, cracks gradually extended along the centerline of the sample, accompanied by significant deflection deformation. The primary reason for this is that during flexural loading, the failure mechanism shifts from intermolecular slip within the concrete (in ordinary concrete) to fiber pull-out (in the CP-ECC). The energy required for the failure of conventional concrete is primarily determined by the C–S–H gel. In contrast, in the CP-ECC under bending, the energy dissipation is primarily determined by the friction between the fibers and cementitious materials, as well as the inherent ductility of the fibers. Both the PP and the CFs exhibited high toughness, with the PP fibers additionally possessing excellent deformation capability, which significantly enhanced the flexural capacity of the CP-ECC. As the temperature increased, the residual fibers decreased significantly after flexural failure. The failure mode of the materials gradually shifted toward brittle fracture. At 800 °C, the material failed in a completely brittle manner, resulting in a substantial reduction in the flexural strength of the CP-ECC.

Figure 9 shows the flexural strength of the CP-ECC with different PP fibers and FA contents at 20 °C, 200 °C, 400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C. At 20 °C, the flexural strength of the material with 40% FA content was significantly greater than that of the 20% and 60% FA test groups. When the PP fiber content was approximately 1.0%, the flexural strength of this test group reached 12.38 MPa, which was higher than that of the other test groups. It can be inferred that when the PP fiber content reached 1.0%, both the PP fibers and the CFs provided the optimal enhancement effect on the flexural strength of the material, with a combined volume fraction of 2%. This finding was consistent with the research of Maninder et al.30. As the temperature increased, the flexural strength of the CP-ECC initially increased but then decreased. The average flexural strengths at temperatures of 200 °C, 400 °C, and 600 °C were 10.35 MPa, 10.76 MPa, and 10.43 MPa, respectively. At 800 °C, the average flexural strength was only 6.5 MPa, representing a decrease of approximately 38.19%. This occurred because during the initial heating phase, elevated temperatures promoted the reactions of compounds such as Ca3SiO5, 2CaO·SiO2, and 3CaO·Al2O3 in Portland cement with water, forming hydration products such as C–S–H gel and Ca(OH)2. Ca(OH)2 further underwent a secondary hydration reaction with FA to generate additional C–S–H gel, which enhanced the bonds between the fibers and the CP-ECC matrix. The distinction between CP-ECC and conventional concrete lies in the presence of a fiber network composed of PP and CF fibers within its structure. During flexural testing, the PP fibers and CFs within the CP-ECC matrix acted as bridges. The failure mechanism of the test samples shifted from direct shear failure to a combination of fiber pull-out and fiber fracture, which contributed to the improvement in the flexural strength of the CP-ECC. When the temperature reached 400 °C, the hydration of FA and Portland cement was complete, and the flexural strength of certain mix proportions (2C1P0.5F60 and 7C1P1.5F20) peaked. The flexural strengths of these two mixes increased by 51.58 and 13.07%, respectively. At 600 °C, the peak flexural strength was generally achieved for compound ratios with PP fiber volume fractions of 0.5% and 1.0%. Compared with the other mixtures, the 5C1P1.0F40 mixture presented greater flexural strength across the entire temperature gradient. This occurred because the PP fibers melted and decomposed at 600 °C, creating numerous pathways within the material that allowed gas to escape, effectively preventing explosive spalling of the material at high temperatures. However, the increased porosity also reduced the flexural capacity. When the FA content was 40%, it participated in hydration reactions along with the cement in the presence of water vapor and other substances. The resulting C–S–H gel reduced the pore volume, ultimately contributing to the excellent flexural performance of the 5C1P1.0F40 group. When the temperature reached 800 °C, it exceeded the critical temperature of the cement, causing the C–S–H gel network to harden and the hydration product Ca(OH)2 to decompose into CaO, which led to a significant reduction in the material strength.

Residual compressive strength

The compressive strength is also one of the key indicators for evaluating the mechanical behavior of materials. At 20 °C, compressive strength measurements were conducted on the CP-ECC via the proposed method. The effects of PP fibers and FA at different volume fractions on the compressive strength were analyzed, and the compressive failure mechanism of the CP-ECC was elucidated.

Figure 10 shows the mechanical failure morphology of the CP-ECC under the action of a compression fixture. At temperatures below 400 °C, the CP-ECC material first underwent slow elastic deformation and then suddenly lost its mechanical properties in the presence of loud noise. The primary reason was that both the CFs and PP fibers had high elastic moduli. In CP-ECC materials, the PP-CF fiber network is firmly embedded within the matrix. During the compression process, the cementitious material was squeezed and tended to extrude outward. The fibers within the material matrix retained their confinement ability. It would not cause the concrete to crack or break. As the temperature increased to 600 °C and above, the internal fibers of the material underwent denaturation and melting, destroying the fiber network. After compressive failure, owing to the loss of constraint from the fiber network, the material ultimately fractured, exhibiting brittle failure.

As shown in Fig. 11, at 20 °C, the compressive strength of the material decreased with increasing fly ash (FA) content. At high temperatures, compressive strength variation across mix ratios remained generally consistent across the five temperature gradients. The compressive strength of the samples initially increased but then decreased as the temperature increased. At 200 °C, 400 °C, and 600 °C, the average compressive strengths of the samples were 35.74 MPa, 39.52 MPa, and 39.82 MPa, respectively. At 800 °C, the compressive strength was 22.43 MPa. Thus, the compressive strength of the samples improved slightly between 200 °C and 600 °C. From 600 °C to 800 °C, the compressive strength decreased significantly but still retained a certain residual strength. This behavior was associated with the hydration of cementitious materials. During the melting and degradation of the PP fibers, elongated channels formed inside the material. These channels provide escape routes for gaseous substances generated by high-temperature reactions, reducing internal damage caused by pressure imbalances and thereby enhancing the stability of the material at high temperatures. Additionally, after the melting of the PP fibers, the network formed by the CFs could lock the aggregates and cementitious materials in multiple directions. Under compressive loading, this network helped convert part of the vertical load into distributed loads along the fiber arrangement. The high elastic modulus and high-temperature resistance of the CFs contributed partially to the compressive performance of the material. The interaction between PP and CFs, along with the hydration of cementitious materials, led to an increase in compressive strength after exposure to 200 °C to 400 °C. As the temperature further increased to 600 °C, the secondary hydration reactions of the cementitious materials were largely complete, and the compressive strength was no longer influenced by this factor. When the temperature reached 800 °C, the negative effects induced by high temperature became dominant. At this stage, the cement underwent chemical decomposition as it exceeded its critical temperature, and the loss of stability of the CF network led to a reduction in compressive strength. In particular, the melting of the PP fibers increased the internal porosity of the material, which became sites of stress concentration under loading. The complete failure of the CFs ultimately accelerated crack propagation within the CP-ECC matrix, leading to a decrease in compressive strength.

The two mix proportions corresponding to the peak compressive strength of the material under high-temperature conditions were 1C1P0.5F40 and 2C1P0.5F60. The corresponding peak compressive strengths from 20 °C to 800 °C were 50.76 MPa and 50.32 MPa, respectively. The compressive strength increased with increasing temperature and began to decrease above 600 °C. Notably, the PP fiber content in both mixes was only 0.5% by volume. These results indicated that incorporating an appropriate amount of PP fibers into the CP-ECC not only enhanced the structural stability but also improved the residual compressive performance after exposure to high temperatures. However, excessive PP fibers led to an increase in the structural porosity of the matrix, which affected the compressive performance of the CP-ECC at elevated temperatures. In summary, a PP fiber content of 0.5% had a relatively positive effect on the high-temperature resistance of the material.

Flexural-to-compressive strength ratio analysis

To verify the aforementioned degradation behavior, another brittleness index—the ratio of flexural strength to compressive strength (flexural-to-compressive ratio)—was employed as a criterion to analyze the toughness characteristics26. As shown in Fig. 12, the flexural-to-compressive ratio of conventional concrete was approximately 15%, whereas that of the CP-ECC was significantly greater, indicating its superior toughness. At temperatures of 20 °C, 200 °C, 400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C, the toughness of the CP-ECC decreased with increasing temperature. This was attributed to the fact that as the hydration reactions of the cementitious materials and FA proceeded to completion at high temperatures, the stiffness and brittleness of the material gradually increased, and the fibers progressively degraded and lost effectiveness, ultimately resulting in a reduction in material toughness. The flexural-to-compressive ratio of the 5C1P1.0F40 group decreased by 15.97% in high-temperature environments, yet it still maintained a ratio above 35% after exposure. This can be attributed to the synergistic effect of the CFs and PP fibers at an optimal volume fraction, which fully leverages the characteristics of both fiber types. The combined action of the cementitious network formed by hydration reactions between FA and cement, along with the fiber system, collectively enhanced the toughness of the CP-ECC.

SEM analysis

As shown in Fig. 13a, at 20 °C, the untreated samples exhibited mutual integration and adhesion between the CFs and PP fibers. A substantial amount of cementitious structures adhered to the surface of the fibers. The cementitious material featured interlocking, intertwining crystals that formed a compact, fabric-like structure. Poorly crystallized portlandite (Ca(OH)2) and numerous spherical unhydrated fly ash particles were also observed.

At 200 °C, as shown in Fig. 13b, after the hydration reaction of the cement, a secondary hydration reaction continued with FA. More hydration products, such as C–S–H gel and Ca(OH)2, were generated, leading to strong bonding between the fibers and the cementitious matrix. Chemical reactions occurring within the cement-based system consumed moisture, causing the concrete to enter a dry state. During the cooling process of the sample back to 20 °C, thermal contraction and drying shrinkage strains resulted in the formation of numerous microcracks within the matrix.

After exposure to 400 °C, as shown in Fig. 13c, the PP fibers underwent melting and degradation. The free water in the concrete matrix almost completely evaporated, and some of the chemically bound water dissociated from its molecular bonds, converting some substances into an anhydrous state. This led to the formation of numerous pores. As the temperature increased, the microstructure of the concrete gradually transitioned to a loose and porous structure.

After 600 °C (Fig. 13d), the PP fibers had completely melted. Some residual substances adhere to the CF fibers, forming compounds similar to those in CF reinforcement31. This structure enhanced the compressive strength of the sample, making it superior to that at 20 °C. However, the series of pores generated in the matrix after PP melting also served as channels for the escape of water vapor in the concrete, which was the primary factor preventing explosive spalling in the CP-ECC. By this stage, the secondary hydration reaction was essentially complete. Owing to the formation of the fiber-reinforced composite, the mechanical properties of the material became superior to those at 20 °C.

At 800 °C (Fig. 13e), the mechanical performance of the concrete material decreased significantly. The hydration and pozzolanic reactions were complete, generating substantial heat. This led to an increase in the internal temperature, resulting in thermal stress. The cement paste underwent volume shrinkage, producing microcracks that adversely affected its compressive strength and durability. Furthermore, numerous pores were formed due to the secondary hydration of the matrix and the melting and degradation of the fibers within the matrix. As the temperature increased, differences in the thermal expansion coefficients of the various components inside the concrete caused damage to the cementitious material and an increase in porosity. Consequently, the concrete structure was altered, leading to a reduction in its residual mechanical and durability performance.

Conclusion

To address the demand for ECC materials under high-temperature conditions, a novel high-temperature-resistant hybrid fiber ECC (CP-ECC) was developed. The CP-ECC samples were subjected to three-point bending tests and compressive strength tests after exposure to temperatures of 20 °C, 200 °C, 400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C. Following high-temperature exposure, the mass loss, pore characteristics, and residual mechanical properties were evaluated. The performance of the CP-ECC material after high-temperature exposure was analyzed, leading to the following conclusions.

The incorporation of CFs and PP fibers formed a hybrid fiber network within the CP-ECC matrix, affecting the mechanical properties of the CP-ECC at 20 °C. With the increase in PP fiber content, the overall flexural performance of the material increased by 17.6% and 5.91%, respectively, while the compressive performance decreased by 33.96% and 12.66%, respectively.The high-temperature resistance of the CFs contributed to their superior residual mechanical performance after high-temperature exposure. Moreover, the gradual melting of the PP fibers at elevated temperatures created microchannels within the matrix, which helped balance the internal and external vapor pressures, effectively mitigating the spalling and fragmentation of the material.Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis revealed that at high temperatures, CFs and melted PP residues collectively formed a reinforcement-like composite, altering the structure of the internal fiber network. This structural evolution allowed the CP-ECC to maintain high residual mechanical performance in high-temperature environments. After exposure to 600 °C, certain mix proportions achieved a residual flexural strength of 13.69 MPa and a residual compressive strength of 50.32 MPa.When both the CF and PP fiber volumes were set at 1%, with 40% FA replacing cement by volume, the CP-ECC exhibited optimal residual mechanical performance and toughness after high-temperature exposure. Even at temperatures up to 600 °C, the material retained significant mechanical functionality.

In summary, this study demonstrates that the hybrid use of short carbon fibers and polypropylene fibers enhances the mechanical properties of the material under high-temperature conditions. Short carbon fibers can form a bridging effect within the ECC matrix after exposure to elevated temperatures, thereby improving material performance. The melting characteristics of polypropylene fibers increase the internal pore channels within the CP-ECC, balancing the internal and external vapor pressure. This contributes to the enhanced stability of CP-ECC under high-temperature conditions. Such a complex fiber network is beneficial for improving the high-temperature resistance of ECC materials in practical applications. However, this study was conducted only on small-scale specimens. Furthermore, the effect of short carbon fibers on the overall material performance was not investigated beyond a dosage of 1%. Future research should explore the effects of varying short carbon fiber contents and evaluate the performance of CP-ECC materials when applied to structural components.
