CircFN1 promotes invasion of papillary thyroid carcinoma cells by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway via the miR-29a/TRIB2 axis
Authors: Ning Zhang, Jingyan Zhang, Xiaonuo Wang, Ming Tao, Gongqi Li, Yanjiao Li, Qingyang Bai, Qiuting Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCircular RNAs regulate cancer progression, but their mechanisms in papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) invasion remain incompletely defined. This study investigated circFN1 expression and function and examined whether circFN1 promotes PTC invasion through microRNA-29a (miR-29a)/tribbles pseudokinase 2 (TRIB2)-mediated PI3K/AKT activation.MethodsThe abundance of TRIB2 and p-AKT in paired PTC and adjacent non-cancerous tissues was first assessed by immunohistochemistry (IHC), and circFN1, miR-29a, TRIB2, p-PI3K and p-AKT were further evaluated by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and Western blotting. In TPC-1 cells, circFN1 overexpression/knockdown, miR-29a overexpression/inhibition and TRIB2 overexpression models were established. Cell proliferation, colony formation, apoptosis, migration and invasion were assessed. Dual-luciferase reporter and RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays were used to validate miR-29a/TRIB2 targeting and circFN1-miR-29a association. The phosphorylation status of crucial proteins within the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade (p-PI3K, p-AKT) was detected by qRT-PCR and Western Blot to confirm the activation status of this signaling axis.ResultsExamination of clinical specimens indicated that the abundance of TRIB2 and p-AKT showed significant upregulation when compared with adjacent normal tissues. Additional paired-tissue validation further showed increased circFN1 and TRIB2 expression, decreased miR-29a expression, and enhanced PI3K/AKT phosphorylation in PTC tissues. In vitro functional experiments demonstrated that elevated expression of circFN1 markedly enhanced the growth, migration, and invasion of PTC cells while concurrently suppressing their apoptosis, whereas knockdown of circFN1 produced contrary results. Mechanistic studies revealed that circFN1 could function as a competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) for miR-29a, negatively regulating its expression through a sponging effect. The newly added RIP-based assay evidence supported the direct association between circFN1 and miR-29a. The dual-luciferase reporter assay further confirmed that TRIB2 is a direct target of miR-29a. TRIB2 overexpression was sufficient to increase PTC cell migration and invasion and to enhance p-PI3K/PI3K and p-AKT/AKT ratios. Consequently, circFN1 competitively binds to miR-29a, thereby relieving the inhibition of its downstream target gene TRIB2, leading to an upregulation of TRIB2 protein expression. Ultimately, the upregulation of TRIB2 activated the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, as demonstrated by a substantial rise in the phosphorylation status of p-PI3K and p-AKT.ConclusionscircFN1 may promote PTC invasion through the miR-29a/TRIB2/PI3K/AKT axis. This axis represents a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for assessing invasion risk in PTC.

Highlight box

Key findings

• circFN1 promotes malignant biological behavior in papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) cells by suppressing miR-29a, upregulating TRIB2, and activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

What is known and what is new?

• Circular RNAs can function as competing endogenous RNAs in cancer, and miR-29a has tumor-suppressive activity in PTC.

• This study expands current knowledge by defining a circFN1/miR-29a/TRIB2/PI3K/AKT signaling cascade in PTC and by adding clinical tissue and mechanistic validation data.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The circFN1/miR-29a/TRIB2 axis may serve as a candidate biomarker and therapeutic vulnerability for aggressive PTC, although larger clinical cohorts and in vivo validation remain necessary before clinical application.

Introduction

Globally, thyroid cancer represents the most prevalent endocrine malignancy, and its incidence has been showing a continuous upward trend over the past few decades. Among all pathological types of thyroid cancer, papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) is the most prevalent, accounting for over 80% of cases (1-3). Although the majority of PTC patients have a favorable prognosis, local invasion and distant metastasis remain key factors leading to disease recurrence and mortality (4). Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms driving PTC invasion and metastasis and identifying novel biomarkers are of paramount importance for improving patient diagnosis, prognostic assessment, and therapeutic strategies.

In recent years, circular RNAs (circRNAs), a distinct category of non-coding RNA entities, have attracted considerable interest due to their crucial regulatory functions in the development and advancement of tumors (5). A large body of research has shown that circRNAs can exert powerful gene regulatory functions at the post-transcriptional level. Among these, their function as a “sponge” for competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) is the most classic mechanism: circRNAs, rich in microRNA (miRNA) binding sites, competitively adsorb and sequester specific miRNAs, thereby relieving the suppressive effect of miRNAs on their subsequent target genes, which ultimately results in the indirect modulation of target gene activity (6,7). This regulatory network, composed of circRNA-miRNA-mRNA, has been confirmed as one of the core mechanisms driving the progression of various human malignancies (8,9).

Among the many tumor-associated circRNAs, circFN1, derived from the fibronectin 1 (FN1) gene, has been identified as a key pro-cancerous molecule in various cancers (10,11). Previous studies have confirmed that circFN1 also acts as an oncogene in PTC, significantly facilitating the growth, infiltration, and spread of PTC cells (12), but the specific molecular mechanisms still require complete clarification. On the other hand, as a classic tumor suppressor molecule, miR-29a is downregulated in various tumors, including PTC, and inhibits the malignant biological behaviors of tumors by targeting different downstream genes (13,14). Our previous research further found that low expression of miR-29a within serum exosomes from PTC patients is significantly associated with adverse clinical features such as tumor size, extrathyroidal extension, TNM stage, and shorter survival. Furthermore, experiments have confirmed that miR-29a can potently suppress the growth, movement, infiltration, and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) of PTC cells (15,16).

Tribbles pseudokinase 2 (TRIB2) is an atypical protein kinase with weak intrinsic catalytic activity, but it can act as a key signaling scaffold protein to multiple internal cellular communication routes (17). Studies have reported that TRIB2 can physically interact with protein kinase B (AKT) and act as its upstream regulator, promoting the phosphorylation and activation of AKT (18,19). Functioning as a pivotal node for controlling cell growth, viability, and movement, the anomalous activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade is a common feature of almost all human cancers (20,21). Previous studies have demonstrated that miR-29a mediates its anti-tumor effects in PTC by acting on a crucial component of this cascade, AKT3 (22). Notably, our preliminary analysis of circRNA differential expression profiles showed that circFN1 is significantly upregulated in PTC tissues, indicating its promise as a biomarker for diagnosis and a goal for therapy in PTC. Further bioinformatics prediction revealed a stable binding potential between circFN1, hsa-miR-29a-5p, and TRIB2 mRNA (Appendix 1, Figures S1-S3 and Table S1), suggesting the possible existence of a circFN1/miR-29a/TRIB2 regulatory axis that could have a pivotal function in the disease process of thyroid cancer through its impact on the activation status of AKT and the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade.

Therefore, the present research will combine clinical specimen evaluation, cellular functional assays, and investigation of molecular pathways to comprehensively clarify the exact function and regulatory network of the circFN1/miR-29a/TRIB2/PI3K/AKT signaling axis in PTC invasion and metastasis, with the aim of providing innovative molecular targets and theoretical basis for the precise diagnosis, prognostic assessment, and targeted therapy of high-risk PTC patients. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1-0061/rc).

Methods

Clinical sample collection

PTC tumor tissues (n=30) and paired adjacent non-tumor tissues (n=30; at least 5 cm from the tumor margin) were harvested from 30 PTC patients, including both male and female patients, at Qiqihar Medical University. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Qiqihar Medical University (approval No. (Qi) Lunshen [2025] 61). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. None of these PTC patients had received radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or other anti-tumor treatment prior to surgical resection. Written informed consent was provided by each PTC patient before surgical resection. All tumor tissues and paired adjacent non-tumor tissues were stored at −80 ℃ before processing. All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all samples were anonymized before analysis.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) postoperative pathological confirmation of PTC; (II) age between 18 and 75 years, with both male and female patients eligible for inclusion; (III) availability of complete paired tumor tissue and matched adjacent non-tumor tissue samples; (IV) no preoperative radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or other anti-tumor treatment; (V) complete clinical information and follow-up records; and (VI) voluntary participation with signed written informed consent.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) coexistence of other malignant tumors or major underlying diseases, including severe cardiac or renal insufficiency or psychiatric disorders; (II) pregnancy or lactation; and (III) refusal to provide written informed consent or withdrawal from the study.

Cell culture

The human PTC cell line, TPC-1, was acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). This cell line was maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Grand Island, NY, USA) augmented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. The cultures were kept in a controlled, humidified environment at 37 ℃ containing 5% CO2.

Cell transfection and grouping

For circFN1 functional studies

siRNAs were sourced from Life Technologies. The entire cDNA sequence of human circFN1 was generated by Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA) and subsequently ligated into the pcDNA3.1 expression plasmid (Clontech Laboratories). The resultant construct’s sequence was confirmed through sequencing. Plasmid vectors for transfection were prepared using the E.Z.N.A Endo-Free Plasmid Mini Kit II, and transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). siRNAs were transfected into cells using RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were allocated into the following 5 groups: (I) control group: cells that received no intervention; (II) circFN1-OE group: cells into which the pcDNA3.1-circFN1 plasmid was introduced; (III) circFN1-OE-NC group: cells that received the empty pcDNA3.1 vector; (IV) circFN1-KD group: cells into which small interfering RNA targeting circFN1 (si-circFN1) was introduced; (V) circFN1-KD-NC group: cells transfected with an NC for si-circFN1.

For miR-29a functional studies

The introduction of miRNA and small interfering RNA (siRNA) inhibitors and mimics (produced by GenePharma, Shanghai, China) was accomplished using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Life Technologies), adhering to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Four experimental groups of cells were subsequently established: (I) a Control group, comprising cells not subjected to any intervention; (II) a miR-29a-OE group, where cells received miR-29a mimics; (III) a miR-29a-KD group, into which miR-29a inhibitors were introduced; and (IV) an NC group, which was treated with non-specific NC sequences for the mimics/inhibitors.

For TRIB2 functional validation

TPC-1 cells were transfected with a TRIB2 overexpression plasmid (TRIB2-OE) or its corresponding empty-vector NC (TRIB2-OE-NC) using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Non-transfected cells served as the Control group. Forty-eight hours after transfection, TRIB2 overexpression efficiency was verified by qRT-PCR and Western blotting, and the effects of TRIB2 on cell migration, invasion, and PI3K/AKT pathway activation were assessed by Transwell assays and Western blotting, respectively.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from both cultured TPC-1 cells and frozen paired clinical specimens with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). A NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was utilized to assess the concentration and purity of the extracted RNA. For the detection of circRNA and TRIB2, cDNA was generated from 1 µg of total RNA via reverse transcription using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Dalian, China). For the detection of miR-29a, reverse transcription was performed employing the Mir-X™ miRNA First-Strand Synthesis Kit (Takara, Dalian, China). QRT-PCR was conducted on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) with SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara, Dalian, China). The thermal cycling parameters were established as follows: an initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 10 minutes, succeeded by 40 cycles. For normalization, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) served as the endogenous reference for circRNA and TRIB2, while U6 small nucleolar RNA was employed for miR-29a. Gene expression levels were quantified relatively using the 2−ΔΔCt calculation. All necessary primers were produced by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) Co., Ltd., with their specific sequences detailed in Table 1.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from both cultured TPC-1 cells and frozen paired clinical specimens using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 hour at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with the indicated primary antibodies listed in Table 2. Protein signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) and captured with a ChemiDoc™ XRS+ system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cells from each experimental group were dispensed into 96-well plates at a concentration of 3×103 per well. After culturing for 24, 48, and 72 hours, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) was introduced into each well. After an additional incubation of 2 hours, the optical density in each well was quantified at a 450 nm wavelength, employing a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Plate colony formation assay

For each experimental group, 500 transfected cells were placed into each well of a 6-well plate and cultured routinely for about 2 weeks, with the culture medium being replenished every 3 days. Once discernible colonies had developed, the spent medium was removed, and the cells underwent two rinses with PBS. Then, the cells were preserved with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes and then stained with a 0.1% crystal violet solution for 20 minutes. After washing away the excess dye with clean water, the colonies were photographed and counted (a colony with ≥50 cells are counted as a valid colony).

Apoptosis assay

The assessment of cellular apoptosis was carried out with the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). At 48 hours post-transfection, cells from every group were harvested, subjected to two rinses with chilled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and then reconstituted in 1× Binding Buffer. To the resulting cell suspension, 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL of PI were introduced sequentially, followed by gentle mixing and a 15-minute incubation in darkness at ambient temperature. Subsequently, the cells were examined via flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), with the resultant data processed through FlowJo software.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Migration and invasion assays were performed using Transwell chambers with a pore size of 8 µm (Corning, NY, USA). For the invasion assay, the upper chamber bottom was pre-coated with Matrigel matrix gel (BD Biosciences). For these experiments, transfected cells were reconstituted in serum-free medium at a density of 3×104 cells/well (for migration) or 1×105 cells/well (for invasion) and subsequently placed into the upper compartment of the Transwell insert. To act as a chemoattractant, the lower compartment was filled with medium supplemented with 20% FBS. Following a 24-hour incubation period at 37 ℃, the inserts were removed, and any non-migratory cells on the upper surface of the membrane were carefully removed using a cotton swab. Cells that had successfully traversed the membrane were immobilized with 4% paraformaldehyde and then visualized by staining with 0.1% crystal violet. Five random fields were photographed and counted under a microscope.

Dual-luciferase reporter gene assay

To validate the direct interaction between miR-29a and the TRIB2 3'-UTR, dual-luciferase reporter constructs incorporating either the wild-type (WT) or a mutated (MUT) version of the TRIB2 3'-untranslated region (3'-UTR) (pmirGLO-TRIB2-WT/MUT) were generated. TPC-1 cells were then co-transfected with these reporter constructs along with either the miR-29a mimic or an NC mimic. At 48 hours post-transfection, the luminescence signals from both firefly and Renilla luciferases were measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The Renilla luciferase signal served as an internal reference to normalize the firefly luciferase activity.

RNA immunoprecipitation

To provide experimental evidence for the direct association between circFN1 and miR-29a, an RIP-based assay was performed in TPC-1 cells. Briefly, cell lysates were incubated with a miR-29a probe or the corresponding NC, and input and IgG controls were included to assess background enrichment. The retrieved RNA fraction was purified, and circFN1 enrichment was quantified by qRT-PCR. A significant enrichment of circFN1 in the miR-29a-associated fraction compared with IgG control was considered supportive evidence for direct circFN1-miR-29a association in PTC cells.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Sections of paraffin-embedded PTC and adjacent tissues underwent deparaffinization and rehydration. For antigen retrieval, the sections were treated with citrate buffer (pH 6.0). To quench endogenous peroxidase activity, the sections were immersed in 3% H2O2 for 15 minutes at ambient temperature, followed by a 30-minute blocking step with 5% BSA. Following this, the sections were probed overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies targeting TRIB2 (Abcam, ab101993, 1:100) and p-AKT (Ser473) (CST, #4060, 1:1,000). On the following day, after being washed with PBS, the sections were treated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 hour at room temperature. Visualization was achieved using a DAB chromogen kit, and the sections were subsequently counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, the sections were dehydrated, cleared, mounted, and observed under a microscope to capture images. Representative paired thyroid carcinoma and matched adjacent non-tumor tissues were selected for the presentation of IHC staining images. For quantitative analysis, three non-overlapping microscopic fields were randomly selected from each section under identical magnification, and the proportion of positively stained cells was calculated for each field before statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Unless otherwise stated, in vitro experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, whereas one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for comparisons among multiple groups. A P value less than 0.05 was deemed to indicate statistical significance.

Results

TRIB2, circFN1 and PI3K/AKT pathway activity are increased, whereas miR-29a is decreased, in PTC tissues

To elucidate the functions of TRIB2 and the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade in PTC, we initiated our study by assessing the protein abundance of TRIB2 and p-AKT in both cancerous tissues and their corresponding adjacent non-cancerous counterparts from PTC patients via IHC. Our findings indicated that, in comparison with the adjacent normal tissues, TRIB2 expression was markedly increased in PTC tissues (P<0.01) (Figure 1A,1B). Similarly, as a crucial indicator of PI3K/AKT pathway stimulation, the abundance of p-AKT was also found to be substantially greater in PTC tissues relative to the matched adjacent non-tumor tissues (P<0.05) (Figure 1C,1D). To strengthen the clinical evidence, we further performed qRT-PCR and Western blotting in paired tissue samples. These supplemental data showed that TRIB2, p-PI3K/PI3K and p-AKT/AKT levels were significantly higher in PTC tissues than in matched adjacent non-tumor tissues; moreover, circFN1 and TRIB2 mRNA were increased, whereas miR-29a was decreased, in PTC tissues (Appendix 1, Figure S4). This evidence implies that the elevated expression of circFN1 and TRIB2, coupled with suppression of miR-29a and the stimulation of the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade, might be intimately linked with the pathogenesis and progression of PTC.

CircFN1 promotes the malignant biological behaviors of PTC cells in vitro

To investigate the biological roles of circFN1 within PTC cells, we generated TPC-1 cell models with circFN1 overexpression (circFN1-OE) and knockdown (circFN1-KD). QRT-PCR findings revealed that, in comparison with the NC group (circFN1-OE-NC), a substantial elevation in circFN1 expression was observed in the circFN1-OE group (P<0.001); whereas relative to the corresponding NC (circFN1-KD-NC), the expression of circFN1 was markedly suppressed (P<0.05), confirming that the cellular models were successfully constructed (Figure 2A). CCK-8 assay findings revealed that, relative to the control group, ectopic expression of circFN1 significantly enhanced the proliferative capacity of TPC-1 cells (P<0.001), whereas depletion of circFN1 significantly suppressed cellular proliferation (P<0.001) (Figure 2B). In terms of apoptosis, flow cytometry results indicated that, relative to the control cohort, ectopic expression of circFN1 significantly diminished the apoptotic rate in TPC-1 cells, whereas in comparison with the circFN1-OE group, depletion of circFN1 promoted cell apoptosis (P<0.001) (Figure 2C,2D). Furthermore, Transwell assay findings demonstrated that, relative to the control group, the cell migration and invasion abilities of the circFN1-OE group were both significantly enhanced, while relative to the circFN1-OE group, the migration and invasion abilities of the circFN1-KD group were significantly inhibited (P<0.001) (Figure 2E-2H). The plate colony formation assay also confirmed similar results, with the colony count for the circFN1-OE group being markedly greater than the control group (P<0.01), and considerably diminished within the circFN1-KD group (P<0.001 versus circFN1-OE group) (Figure 2I,2J). Collectively, these in vitro experimental data robustly show that circFN1 has a pivotal pro-cancerous function in PTC, by promoting cell proliferation, migration, and invasion while inhibiting apoptosis.

CircFN1 activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway by regulating the miR-29a/TRIB2 axis

Next, we delved into the molecular underpinnings through which circFN1 exerts its pro-cancerous properties. Grounded in bioinformatic analyses and published findings, we hypothesized that circFN1 may operate as a ceRNA for miR-29a. QRT-PCR analysis revealed that, relative to the NC group, the expression of miR-29a was markedly decreased in cells with circFN1 overexpression; in contrast, within cells experiencing circFN1 knockdown, the expression of miR-29a was substantially elevated (P<0.001) (Figure 3A,3B), suggesting a negative regulatory relationship between the two. Subsequent analysis demonstrated that, relative to the control group, ectopic expression of circFN1 upregulated the mRNA level of TRIB2 (P<0.001), whereas depletion of circFN1 resulted in a reduction of the TRIB2 mRNA level (P<0.001 versus circFN1-OE group) (Figure 3C). Western blot analysis confirmed the regulatory relationship at the protein level. Relative to the control group, ectopic expression of circFN1 markedly elevated the protein expression of TRIB2, which was associated with a substantial rise in the phosphorylation status of PI3K and AKT (p-PI3K, p-AKT) (P<0.05, P<0.001, P<0.01, respectively); while compared to the circFN1-OE group, knockdown of circFN1 showed the opposite results (all P<0.001) (Figure 3D,3E). Importantly, the newly added RIP-based assay demonstrated significant enrichment of circFN1 in the miR-29a-associated fraction compared with the IgG control (Appendix 1, Figure S5), providing direct experimental support that circFN1 can bind miR-29a in PTC cells. This evidence suggests that circFN1 can enhance the expression of TRIB2 by suppressing miR-29a to alleviate its inhibition on the target gene TRIB2, and ultimately activate the downstream PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

miR-29a directly targets TRIB2 and inhibits the malignant phenotype of PTC cells

To confirm the pivotal function of miR-29a within the circFN1/TRIB2 regulatory axis, we first established cell models with miR-29a overexpression (miR-29a-OE) and inhibition (miR-29a-KD) (Figure 4A). Data from the dual-luciferase reporter assay indicated that the simultaneous introduction of miR-29a mimic and a plasmid containing the wild-type TRIB2 3’-UTR (TRIB2-WT) significantly inhibited luciferase activity (P<0.01); however, for the plasmid with the mutant TRIB2 3’-UTR (TRIB2-Mut), there was no significant effect (Figure 4B), confirming that TRIB2 is a direct target of miR-29a. Functional experiment results showed that, relative to the control group, ectopic expression of miR-29a markedly suppressed the proliferative potential (P<0.001) (Figure 4C) and promoted cell apoptosis (P<0.001) (Figure 4D,4E), while also suppressing the migration (P<0.05) (Figure 4F,4G) and invasion ability (P<0.01) (Figure 4H,4I) of PTC cells; while compared to the miR-29a-OE group, suppressing the expression of miR-29a led to the completely opposite effect of promoting the malignant phenotype. The plate colony formation assay also yielded consistent conclusions (Figure 4J,4K). This evidence strongly suggests that miR-29a exerts a tumor-suppressive function in PTC, with its function being exactly the opposite of that of circFN1.

miR-29a inhibits the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway by targeting TRIB2

Finally, we confirmed the influence of the miR-29a/TRIB2 axis upon the downstream PI3K/AKT signaling cascade. As depicted in Figure 5A-5D, qRT-PCR and Western blot data indicated that ectopic expression of miR-29a in PTC cells markedly downregulated the mRNA and protein expression of TRIB2; in contrast, relative to the miR-29a-OE group, suppression of miR-29a resulted in a substantial elevation of TRIB2 expression (P<0.001). Pathway protein analysis further demonstrated that, relative to the control group, ectopic expression of miR-29a markedly diminished the abundance of p-PI3K (P<0.01) and p-AKT (P<0.05), while suppression of miR-29a significantly elevated the phosphorylation status of both (P<0.001 versus miR-29a-OE group). These findings are in agreement with the outcomes of the preceding circFN1 regulation experiments, suggesting that circFN1 downregulates the level of the tumor suppressor gene miR-29a through a “sponging” mechanism, thereby alleviating the inhibitory effect of miR-29a on its target gene TRIB2, resulting in an increase of TRIB2 protein expression, and ultimately stimulating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway to enhance the invasion and progression of PTC. To further establish TRIB2 as a downstream functional node, we performed TRIB2 overexpression experiments. TRIB2-OE significantly increased TRIB2 mRNA expression, enhanced the number of migrated and invaded cells, and increased the p-PI3K/PI3K and p-AKT/AKT ratios compared with the control and vector-NC groups (Appendix 1, Figure S6). These findings indicate that TRIB2 is sufficient to promote PTC cell motility and PI3K/AKT pathway activation, thereby reinforcing its position as a key downstream effector of the circFN1/miR-29a axis.

Discussion

Globally, thyroid carcinoma stands as the most frequent endocrine neoplasm, and its occurrence has been showing a continuous upward trend over the past few decades. Among all pathological types of thyroid cancer, PTC represents the most widespread subtype, constituting more than 85% of all instances (23,24). Although the majority of PTC patients have a favorable prognosis, local invasion and distant metastasis remain key factors leading to disease recurrence and mortality (25). Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms driving PTC invasion and metastasis and identifying novel biomarkers are of paramount importance for improving patient diagnosis, prognostic assessment, and therapeutic strategies. The present investigation examined the function of the circFN1/miR-29a/TRIB2 regulatory network in PTC and uncovered the molecular basis through which it enhances PTC cell invasion and proliferation via the stimulation of the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade.

In recent years, circRNAs, a category of non-coding RNAs characterized by a covalently closed-loop configuration, have become a focus of tumor research due to their diverse functions in post-transcriptional regulation (26). One of the most classic functions of circRNAs is to function as ceRNAs, which, by “sponging” specific miRNAs, alleviate the suppressive effects of miRNAs on their corresponding target genes, a mechanism that is pivotal for the advancement of diverse cancers (27). One of the core findings of this study is that circFN1, derived from the FN1 gene, exhibits marked upregulation within PTC tissues, a finding initially suggested by prior bioinformatic analyses. Functional experiments demonstrated that ectopic expression of circFN1 markedly enhanced the proliferative, migratory, and invasive capacities of PTC cells. The FN1 gene itself encodes fibronectin 1, a well-known glycoprotein that promotes tumor metastasis and EMT (28), and our findings suggest that circFN1 may partially inherit the pro-cancerous properties of its parent gene. Furthermore, circFN1 has shown similar pro-cancerous functions in other tumors, such as mediating chemotherapy resistance in gastric cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma by sponging miR-182-5p and miR-1205, respectively (10,29). These studies corroborate our results, collectively pointing to circFN1 as a pro-cancerous molecule with important functions in various tumors.

This study further confirmed that circFN1 primarily exerts its biological functions in PTC by functioning as a molecular sponge for miR-29a. As a pivotal constituent of the miR-29 family, miR-29a has been extensively documented to function as a tumor suppressor across multiple cancer types (30). In thyroid cancer, published research indicates that the abundance of miR-29a is markedly diminished and inhibits the proliferation and invasion of tumor cells by targeting different downstream genes (such as AKT3 and DPP4) (13,22). By constructing cell models with miR-29a overexpression and inhibition, we confirmed the tumor-suppressing function of miR-29a within PTC cells, and our findings are highly consistent with these reports. More importantly, by constructing cell models with circFN1 overexpression and knockdown, we confirmed that circFN1 is an upstream negative regulator of miR-29a. The added RIP-based validation further strengthened this ceRNA model by demonstrating direct enrichment of circFN1 in the miR-29a-associated fraction. This finding not only introduces a novel molecular framework for understanding the diminished expression of miR-29a in PTC but also elucidates the signaling pathway through which circFN1 exerts its pro-cancerous functions.

The most important innovation of this study is the successful identification of TRIB2 as a key downstream effector molecule of the circFN1/miR-29a axis and its connection to the classic PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. TRIB2 is an atypical pseudokinase that, although lacking canonical catalytic activity, acts as an important scaffold or adaptor protein in cell signal transduction (18,19). Current evidence indicates that TRIB2 can regulate AKT-related signaling in a context-dependent manner, including through scaffold/adaptor interactions that influence AKT phosphorylation and downstream survival pathways (18,19,31). Dysregulated stimulation of the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade is a frequent characteristic in nearly all human malignancies and holds a critical function in the initiation and progression of thyroid carcinoma (32,33). This study found that TRIB2 and p-AKT are significantly upregulated in PTC tumor tissues compared with matched adjacent non-tumor tissues. The newly added paired-tissue Western blotting further demonstrated elevated TRIB2 expression and increased p-PI3K/PI3K and p-AKT/AKT ratios in cancer tissues. Utilizing a dual-luciferase reporter assay, we confirmed that TRIB2 is directly targeted by miR-29a. Mechanistically, circFN1 competitively binds to miR-29a, relieving the translational inhibition of TRIB2 mRNA by miR-29a, leading to an upregulation of TRIB2 protein expression, and ultimately causing an increase in PI3K/AKT phosphorylation levels. Furthermore, TRIB2 overexpression alone enhanced migration, invasion, and PI3K/AKT activation in TPC-1 cells, providing direct functional evidence that TRIB2 is not merely a downstream marker but a biologically active node in this axis. The elucidation of this complete “circFN1-miR-29a-TRIB2-PI3K/AKT” signaling chain provides a logically rigorous and data-supported new theoretical model for the molecular pathogenesis of PTC.

In summary, the present investigation not only confirmed the pro-cancerous role of circFN1 in PTC but also systematically elucidated the intricate molecular underpinnings through which it enhances PTC invasion and proliferation by functioning as a molecular sponge for miR-29a, upregulating TRIB2 expression, and thereby activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. This finding deepens our comprehension of the complex regulatory network of PTC invasion and metastasis and suggests translational value. Clinically, circFN1 may be explored as a biomarker for identifying invasive PTC, whereas therapeutic strategies aimed at inhibiting circFN1, restoring miR-29a, suppressing TRIB2, or blocking PI3K/AKT activation may provide rational approaches for molecularly guided intervention. However, this study also has certain limitations. First, although the added TRIB2 overexpression and RNA binding assays strengthen the mechanistic evidence, all experiments were conducted in in vitro cell lines, and further validation of the in vivo functions and clinical relevance of this signaling axis is needed in orthotopic transplantation or genetically engineered mouse models. Second, the clinical sample size of this study is relatively limited, and more extensive clinical cohort investigations are required to assess the correlations among circFN1, miR-29a, TRIB2, p-AKT, clinicopathological characteristics, lymph node metastasis, and prognosis. Despite these limitations, our research results clearly suggest that the circFN1/miR-29a/TRIB2 signaling axis has the potential to serve as a molecular indicator for evaluating the risk of PTC invasion and provides new candidate targets for creating tailored therapeutic approaches for PTC.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrates that circFN1 promotes PTC cell invasion and progression through the miR-29a/TRIB2/PI3K/AKT signaling axis. The added paired-tissue validation, direct circFN1-miR-29a binding evidence, and TRIB2 overexpression experiments strengthen the mechanistic chain and support the potential clinical utility of this axis as a biomarker and therapeutic target for aggressive PTC.
