Oral milk-derived exosomes loaded with tafatinib for anti-inflammatory therapy
Authors: Qiao Qiao, Caoganhong Wang, Zhen Mao, Weijun Chen, Weibo Kong, Lipeng Qiu, Huiming Tu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic idiopathic inflammatory bowel disease primarily affecting the colon and rectum, characterized by complex pathogenesis and clinical challenges, such as disease recurrence and potential malignant transformation. The pan-JAK inhibitor tofacitinib (TOF) has emerged as an effective therapeutic option for inducing and maintaining clinical remission in moderate-to-severe UC. Recent advances in nanomedicine have identified milk-derived exosomes (mEXOs) as promising natural drug delivery vehicles due to their favorable physicochemical properties and biocompatibility. Therefore, an oral TOF-loaded mEXOs system (mEXOs@TOF) was successfully developed, which exhibited favorable pharmaceutical characteristics, including stability, uniform size distribution, high drug-loading capacity, and efficient macrophage uptake. The therapeutic efficacy of mEXOs@TOF was mediated through multifaceted mechanisms including suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IFN-γ, NO), elevation of anti-inflammatory IL-10 levels, reduction of reactive oxygen species production, and inhibition of JAK-STAT3 signaling pathway activation. Comprehensive in vitro and in vivo assessments of mEXOs@TOF confirmed the enhanced anti-inflammatory treatment on UC, with no detectable adverse effects. Collectively, the mEXOs@TOF nanodelivery system represents a targeted therapeutic strategy for improving UC treatment.

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC), a chronic idiopathic inflammatory bowel disorder and one of the two principal subtypes of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) alongside Crohn's disease (CD), constitutes a significant global health burden with rising incidence and prevalence (Wangchuk et al., 2024). UC is characterized by a chronic relapsing-remitting course and complex multifactorial pathogenesis, leading to incurability and multidimensional complications that necessitate enhanced clinical management strategies (Zeng et al., 2023a). Despite therapeutic advances, current treatments remain suboptimal. The primary therapeutic goal is to induce and maintain clinical and endoscopic remission; however, only a significant proportion of patients (approximately 40%) achieve this endpoint with conventional therapies, while many experience inadequate responses (Kucharzik et al., 2020). Consequently, some patients may ultimately require colectomy due to treatment failure, imposing substantial physical and psychological burdens and severely compromising quality of life. Contemporary approaches primarily rely on pharmacological interventions, including conventional agents (e.g., 5-aminosalicylates, corticosteroids) and advanced biologics (e.g., infliximab, vedolizumab). Complementing these, emerging non-pharmacological modalities such as dietary modulation and microbiome-targeted therapies are under active investigation (Coqueiro et al., 2019). Furthermore, small molecule inhibitors targeting key inflammatory pathways (e.g., janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) pathway (Chen et al., 2024), sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P) (Kitsou et al., 2024), tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2 (Cui et al., 2024),) have significantly expanded the therapeutic arsenal by modulating intracellular signaling cascades. As the first small-molecule oral inhibitor for IBD, JAK inhibitors greatly improved the treatment of IBD and have demonstrated significant efficacy, with tofacitinib (TOF) and upadacitinib being approved for the treatment of UC (Chen et al., 2024).

TOF, an oral JAK inhibitor, preferentially inhibits JAK1 and JAK3 phosphorylation, thereby blocking the transduction of pro-inflammatory cytokine signals. Its oral administration avoids the need for parenteral delivery associated with biologics, offering advantages such as reduced immunogenicity risk and improved treatment adherence (Fernández-Clotet et al., 2018). Additionally, its relatively lower production costs make TOF a cost-effective strategy for moderate-to-severe UC (Lasa et al., 2022). Clinical trials demonstrate TOF's superiority over placebo in achieving clinical remission (Bonovas et al., 2018), mucosal healing, and endoscopic improvement, including in patients refractory to TNF-α inhibitors during induction therapy (Paschos et al., 2018). Health economic analyses indicate improved quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) with TOF treatment, reflecting enhanced patient well-being (Petryszyn et al., 2020). Nevertheless, long-term high-dose TOF is associated with serious adverse events, such as herpes zoster reactivation, thromboembolism, malignancy, major adverse cardiovascular events, and increased mortality risk (Sandborn et al., 2023). Systemic exposure concerns prompted the U.S. FDA to issue a black box warning in 2021 regarding serious opportunistic infections that may lead to hospitalization or death. This necessitates enhanced targeting strategies to improve TOF's safety profile while maintaining therapeutic efficacy (Kitsou et al., 2024).

Nano-delivery systems, with their smaller particle sizes, can effectively target damaged colon tissues by leveraging their inherent biological properties, such as the enhanced lymphatic vascular permeability and inflammatory cell uptake (ELVIS) effect (Qindeel et al., 2019). This approach reduces systemic drug exposure, minimizes toxicity, and enables sustained drug release. Exosomes (EXOs) are nano-scale extracellular vesicles (30–150 nm in diameter) secreted by virtually all cell types and ubiquitously present in physiological fluids (Koritzinsky et al., 2017). These membrane-bound vesicles serve as essential mediators of intercellular communication by transporting diverse bioactive molecules, including proteins, lipids, nucleic acids (DNA, mRNA, and microRNA), and functionally specialized proteins such as adhesion molecules, costimulatory molecules, and ligand receptors.

The advantage of EXOs compared with other nano-delivery carriers is that they combine the “evolutionarily optimized” natural delivery system with therapeutic drugs, and directly attack how the drug delivery system can deliver to intracellular targets efficiently, accurately and safely. Compared to synthetic carriers such as liposomes and nanoparticles, the endogeneity and heterogeneity of EXOs give them extensive and unique advantages in the field of disease diagnosis and treatment. EXOs exhibit significant advantages over liposomes in terms of long-term safety and in vivo retention time (Chen et al., 2025). A 30-day repeated-dose study demonstrated that HEK293F-EVs did not induce abnormalities in hematological parameters, organ damage, or elevation of inflammatory factors, and their immunogenicity was significantly lower than that of PEG-Liposomes. Histopathological analysis revealed inflammatory cell infiltration in the lungs, liver, and kidneys following administration of PEG-Liposomes, whereas no pathological changes were observed in the EVs group, further supporting their potential as a delivery vehicle for chronic disease therapy. In addition, HEK293F-EVs exhibited a slightly slower clearance rate from the bloodstream and longer organ retention time compared to liposomes, which may enhance drug delivery efficiency. Multi-omics analysis showed that EVs lack pro-inflammatory proteins and toxic metabolites, while liposomes triggered dose-dependent increases in IgG and cytokine storms, providing direct evidence for the replacement of synthetic nanoparticles with natural carriers.

Through uptake mechanisms including endocytosis, phagocytosis, and membrane fusion, EXOs facilitate the horizontal transfer of molecular cargo from donor to recipient cells, thereby modulating intracellular signaling pathways and influencing cellular functions. This includes immunoregulatory effects ranging from immunosuppression to immune activation, which are critical in disease contexts like inflammation (Zhao et al., 2020) and cancer (Zeng et al., 2023b).

Advances in EXOs isolation and purification technologies have systematically revealed the therapeutic potential of EXOs-based therapies for UC. Among various sources, milk-derived EXOs (mEXOs) have emerged as a promising class of natural nano-carriers for oral drug delivery, offering distinct advantages over conventionally cultured cell-derived EXOs (Marsh et al., 2025). mEXOs exhibit superior source stability, low immunogenicity, and excellent oral biocompatibility (Li et al., 2023). Their industrial-scale production is economically viable, providing higher yields and lower costs, which are critical for large-scale pharmaceutical applications. Notably, mEXOs demonstrate remarkable gastrointestinal stability, maintaining structural integrity under gastric acidic conditions and resisting enzymatic degradation in the intestinal tract. This property, coupled with their ability to cross the gastrointestinal barrier, enables efficient systemic delivery of therapeutic cargo (Timofeeva et al., 2023). Mechanistically, mEXOs modulate gut immunity by expanding intestinal macrophage populations and regulatory T cells (Tregs) while protecting intestinal barrier function (Ngu et al., 2022). Additionally, their bioactive cargo (e.g., proteins and microRNAs) contributes to intestinal homeostasis through microbiota regulation (Fabbri et al., 2012).

Herein, we presented a groundbreaking orally administered mEXOs drug delivery system encapsulating TOF (mEXOs@TOF), specifically designed to address the challenges in TOF therapy for UC (Fig. 1). Despite its potent anti-inflammatory properties as a JAK inhibitor, TOF has been limited in clinical application due to poor aqueous solubility and unpredictable gastrointestinal absorption. The engineered mEXOs@TOF demonstrated enhanced drug loading capacity and stability in gastrointestinal fluids, attributed to its unique lipid bilayer structure that protects the payload from degradation. Following oral ingestion, the vesicles' nano-scale architecture and specific protein-receptor recognition facilitate precise localization to colonic inflammation. Localized release of TOF within inflamed tissues significantly suppressed the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines production (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α). Furthermore, mEXOs@TOF demonstrated superior therapeutic efficacy compared to free TOF in murine colitis models, evidenced by marked reductions in disease activity index scores and histological damage. mEXOs@TOF preparation and characterization

mEXOs isolation and extraction

mEXOs were extracted by a combination of isoelectric precipitation and ultracentrifugation. First, fresh milk was centrifuged at 10,000g for 30 min at 4 °C to remove fat globules and large proteins. The supernatant was adjusted to pH 4.6 to precipitate casein, and the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove the precipitated proteins. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane and centrifuged at 13,500g for 60 min at 4 °C in an ultracentrifuge to obtain the exosome pellet. The pellet was rinsed three times with precooled sterile PBS and resuspended in PBS. The samples were stored at −80 °C.

mEXOs@TOF preparation

To evaluate the loading capacity of mEXOs for TOF using different drug loading methods, the ratio of TOF to mEXOs was first preliminarily screened using the co-incubation method. Based on the determined ratio through screening, three commonly reported drug loading techniques (co-incubation, ultrasound, and electrotransfer) were applied to optimize the preparation of mEXOs@TOF. The particle size, zeta potential, encapsulation efficiency (EE), and drug loading (DL) of the resulting nanoparticles were characterized for each method.(1)Incubation method: The mixed solution of TOF and mEXOs was incubated in a 37 °C water bath for 1 h.(2)Ultrasound method: The TOF and mEXOs mixture was sonicated using an ultrasound probe for 10 s, followed by a 50-s pause; this cycle was repeated 5 times. The sample was kept on ice throughout the process. After sonication, the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to restore the integrity of the exosomal membrane.(3)Electrotransfer method: The mixture of TOF and mEXOs was pre-cooled at 4 °C and transferred to the 4 mm electroporation cuvette. Electroporation was performed under conditions of 125 μF capacitance and 100 V voltage. Subsequently, the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to promote membrane recovery.

Following each loading procedure, the mixture was transferred to an ultracentrifuge tube and centrifuged at 135,000g for 1 h. The pellet was then resuspended in a small volume of PBS. The resulting drug-loaded exosomes were stored at 4 °C for further use.

Determination of DL and EE

The concentration of TOF loaded in mEXOs was quantified using an organic solvent-based membrane disruption method. Briefly, a volume of the prepared mEXOs@TOF solution was mixed with an equal volume of acetonitrile. The mixture was vortexed thoroughly and then sonicated for 10 min to achieve complete membrane lysis. Subsequently, it was centrifuged at 12,000g for 10 min to pellet the insoluble debris. The resulting supernatant was collected and appropriately diluted for HPLC analysis. The DL was calculated as follows: DL(%) = Mdrug/Mtotal × 100, where Mdrug represents the TOF that have been loaded in, and the Mtotal represents the total dosage of TOF. Similarly, the EE of mEXOs@TOF was determined by ultracentrifugation. The amount of the supernatant after ultracentrifugation was measured and recorded as Mfree, and the formula was used to calculate EE as follows: EE (%) = (Mdos - Mfree) / Mdos × 100, where Mdos is the total mass of mEXOs@TOF.

Particle size, potential and morphology

mEXOs and mEXOs@TOF solutions were diluted and transfer 1 mL of each into a sizing cuvette for particle size measurement using dynamic light scattering (DLS). Similarly, 1 mL of each solution was transferred into a zeta potential cuvette for zeta potential measurement using a zeta potential analyzer. 10 μL of the diluted mEXOs and mEXOs@TOF samples was stained with 2% tungsten trioxide solution onto a copper grid, and observed and captured images of the morphology using transmission electron microscopy (TEM).

Identification of marker proteins

To confirm the successful isolation of membrane-enriched exosomes (mEXOs) and evaluate the stability of drug-loaded exosomes (mEXOs@TOF), western blotting (WB) was performed to detect EXOs marker proteins, including the soluble protein TSG101 and the transmembrane proteins CD9 and CD81. Protein concentrations were standardized using the BCA assay prior to loading. Equal volumes of 5× SDS sample buffer were added, thoroughly mixed, and the samples were denatured by heating at 100 °C for 10 min in the metal bath before cooling to room temperature. Following standard WB procedures, target protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system and analyzed using a gel imaging system.

The cytotoxicity and hemolysis tests of mEXOs@TOF

The cytotoxic effects of TOF, mEXOs, and mEXOs@TOF were evaluated using the CCK-8 assay, a widely accepted method for quantifying viable cell populations. RAW264.7 macrophages and 3 T3 cells were seeded at densities of 1 × 104 cells/well and 7 × 103 cells/well, respectively, in 96-well plates. After overnight adherence, cells were co-cultured with test compounds (TOF, mEXOs, or mEXOs@TOF) for 24 h. Following incubation, the CCK-8 reagent was added, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader to determine cell viability.

To evaluate the hemolysis of mEXOs@TOF, a standardized hemolysis assay was conducted following established in vitro safety assessment protocols. Fresh whole blood was anticoagulated with 10% heparin sodium solution (1 mg/mL) and centrifuged at 3000g for 10 min at 4 °C to separate plasma. The supernatant was discarded, and the erythrocyte pellet was washed three times with sterile saline until the supernatant became optically transparent. The final washed erythrocytes were resuspended in saline to prepare a standardized 5% (v/v) erythrocyte suspension (500 μL in 10 mL saline). Serial dilutions of mEXOs@TOF and mEXOs were prepared at concentrations ranging from 50 to 500 μg/mL. Each experimental group was incubated with 500 μL of the 5% erythrocyte suspension at 37 °C for 3 h under gentle agitation. Positive (100% hemolysis, deionized water) and negative (0% hemolysis, saline) controls were included in triplicate. Post-incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 3000g for 5 min, and 200 μL of supernatant from each tube was analyzed for hemolysis rates (HR) calculation.

Stability study of mEXOs@TOF

To assess the stability of drug-loaded exosomes, the mEXOs@TOF solution was stored at 4 °C for 7 consecutive days, and its particle size was measured using DLS to evaluate short-term storage stability. The mEXOs@TOF was also stored at −80 °C for one month, after which it was thawed at 4 °C and the particle sizes were measured using DLS to assess long-term storage stability.

In vitro drug release from mEXOs@TOF

The in vitro drug release profile of mEXOs@TOF was evaluated using a dialysis membrane method (MWCO 1000 Da) under simulated gastrointestinal conditions. The release study was conducted in two phases to mimic the gastrointestinal transit: initially in artificial gastric fluid (pH 1.2) for 2 h, followed by transfer to artificial intestinal fluid (pH 6.8) to simulate the oral administration pathway. The experimental setup involved a 5 mL suspension of mEXOs@TOF in a dialysis bag, immersed in 10 mL of release medium, maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C with 100 rpm agitation in an orbital shaker. Sampling was performed at predetermined intervals (0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 h) with 1 mL of medium withdrawn and replaced with pre-warmed fresh medium to maintain sink conditions. TOF concentration was quantified by HPLC, and the cumulative drug release percentage was calculated using the standard pharmacokinetic equation:Er%=Ve∑1i−1Ci+V0CimD×100.

Where, Er represents the cumulative release amount of the drug, Ve represents the volume of the release medium extracted each time, Ci represents the concentration of the drug in the sample measured at the i-th sampling, V0 represents the total volume of the release medium, and mD represents the total mass of the drug.

Cell uptake of mEXOs@TOF

The cellular uptake of PKH26-labeled mEXOs@TOF in RAW264.7 cells was evaluated using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). RAW264.7 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well in confocal dishes and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h to allow cell adhesion. Cells were then incubated with fluorescently labeled mEXOs@TOF for 24 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, the cells underwent the following processing steps: washing three times with ice-cold PBS (pH 7.4), fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min at room temperature, additional washing with PBS, and nuclear counterstaining with DAPI (1 μg/mL) for 15 min in the dark. Finally, the cells were observed using CLSM to assess the uptake of mEXOs@TOF.

Nitric oxide (NO) production determination

The RAW264.7 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C to allow cell adhesion. After co-incubation with each group for 24 h, the culture medium was collected, centrifuged at 1500 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was used to determine nitrite (NO) levels using a Griess-based assay or a specific NO detection kit.

ELISA assay for the secretion of inflammatory factors

The RAW264.7 cells were uniformly seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. After overnight culture in the incubator, the cells adhered to the plate surface. Each group was added with corresponding culture medium and co-incubated for 24 h. The cell culture medium was collected, centrifuged at 1500 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was taken. The contents of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-10, and IFN-γ in the supernatant were determined using an ELISA assay kit.

Determination of intracellular ROS content

The RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. After overnight incubation in the incubator, the cells adhered to the plate. Each group was incubated with the corresponding culture medium for 24 h. The culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, 1 mL of 2′,7′-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) solution diluted in DEME high-glucose culture medium was added. The plate was incubated for 30 min.

JAK-STAT3 pathway-related proteins detection

RAW264.7 cells were seeded at 4 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ to allow cell adhesion. Cells were then treated with respective media for 24 h. Cells were washed three times with ice-cold PBS, and the cell pellet was collected by centrifugation at 1500 g for 5 min at 4 °C. Cell lysates were prepared using a pre-chilled lysis buffer containing PIPA and PMSF inhibitor (100:1:2 v/v) and incubated on ice for 30 min. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to collect supernatants. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay, and samples were denatured with 5× loading buffer at 100 °C for 10 min. Samples were cooled to room temperature and stored at −80 °C until subsequent WB analysis.

In vivo anti-inflammatory effect of mEXOs@TOF in UC mice

Dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) represents the predominant chemical agent for establishing UC models. Upon membrane fusion with colonic epithelial cells, DSS triggers a cascade of inflammatory responses characterized by elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine levels and disruption of gut microbiota homeostasis, thereby recapitulating key pathological hallmarks observed in human UC. In the present study, an acute UC murine model was successfully induced through ad libitum administration of 3% DSS in drinking water.

The health status of mice in each group was monitored daily, and their body weights were recorded. On the 9th day of the 3% DSS-induced UC model, mice were fasted for 6 h (with free access to water). The colon and spleen were then collected from each group. The colon was photographed and its length was measured. The spleen was weighed, and the spleen-to-body weight ratio was calculated. A portion of the colon tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological analysis, while the remaining tissue was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent molecular analysis.

The fecal occult blood qualitative kit (o-toluidine method) was used to detect fecal occult blood. The Disease Activity Index (DAI) was scored according to the body weight, fecal characters and fecal occult blood of the mice. The scoring details are shown in the Table 1. Serum levels of IL-6, IL-10, and IFN-γ in UC mice

The plasma was centrifuged at 3000 g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected as mouse serum. The supernatant was placed in a refrigerator at −80 °C for use. The levels of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-10 and IFN-γ in the serum were measured according to the relevant inflammatory factor kits.

H&E staining

The fixed colon tissues underwent a standardized histological protocol, including sequential dehydration in graded ethanol solutions, followed by clearing in xylene. The tissues were then embedded in paraffin blocks and incubated at controlled temperatures to ensure optimal paraffin solidification. Serial sections were cut to produce uniform slices. The tissue sections were gently flattened and air-dried under controlled conditions. Prior to staining, the sections underwent deparaffinization with xylene and rehydration in descending ethanol concentrations. Hematoxylin-eosin staining (H&E) was performed, with hematoxylin staining nuclear components and eosin counterstaining cytoplasmic and extracellular matrix structures. This standardized protocol ensures consistent and reproducible visualization of tissue morphology, enabling precise assessment of pathological features.

Immunofluorescence staining for ZO-1 protein detection

Following deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, tissue sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a diluted primary antibody targeting ZO-1. After thorough washing with PBS, the sections were incubated with a Cy3-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h under light-protected conditions. Subsequent PBS rinses were followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) for 5 min. Following final PBS washes, the slides were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium and imaged using fluorescence microscopy to assess ZO-1 protein localization.

Statistical analysis

Data statistical analysis and plotting were conducted using Origin software and GraphPad Prism software. One-way ANOVA analysis was used to determine the statistical significance of the differences between the groups, and the data were presented in the form of Mean ± SD. ⁎p < 0.05, ⁎⁎p < 0.01 and ⁎⁎⁎ p < 0.001 mean that there are significant differences between the groups, while p > 0.05 means that there is no significant difference between the groups.

In vitro safety study of mEXOs@TOF

In vitro cytotoxicity

Cytotoxicity profiles of TOF, mEXOs, and mEXOs@TOF were systematically evaluated in RAW264.7 cells and 3 T3 cells using CCK-8 viability assays. The concentration of 50 μg/mL TOF exhibited no significant cytotoxicity toward RAW264.7 cells, while exposure of 3 T3 cells to the same concentration reduced cell viability to 80%. Both mEXOs (at equivalent amounts to mEXOs@TOF) and mEXOs@TOF showed no toxicity in RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 3A) or 3T3 cells (Fig. 3B), demonstrating that mEXOs are a safe carrier capable of reducing TOF-induced cytotoxicity (Matic et al., 2020). Hemolytic results

Hemolysis rate quantifies drug-induced erythrocyte damage and serves as a critical biocompatibility metric. The supernatant of the positive control group (H₂O) exhibited a red colour with no erythrocyte sedimentation at the bottom, indicating complete erythrocyte rupture and significant hemolysis. Consequently, the hemolysis rate was defined as 100%. In contrast, the PBS group (negative control) showed no hemolysis, with a hemolysis rate defined as 0%. For all concentrations of mEXOs (Fig. 3C) and mEXOs@TOF (Fig. 3D), no hemolysis was observed. mEXOs (Fig. 3C) and mEXOs@TOF (Fig. 3D) did not cause hemolysis at any concentration range, and even at a concentration of 500 μg/mL, the hemolysis rate was still less than 5%, indicating that they did not cause hemolysis and exhibit favorable blood compatibility.

The stability of mEXOs@TOF

Particle size stability is a fundamental parameter in nano-material research, directly influencing physicochemical properties, functional performance, and biocompatibility. This parameter is critical for maintaining structural integrity during synthesis, storage, and application (Zu et al., 2021). As demonstrated in Fig. 4A, both mEXOs and mEXOs@TOF maintained consistent hydrodynamic diameters after 7 days of static storage at 4 °C, with no observable aggregation or structural disintegration. These findings underscore the system's potential for clinical translation, as its stable colloidal properties and uniform particle size distribution are maintained under standard storage conditions. Long-term particle size stability is a critical determinant of material preservation integrity (Jakubek et al., 2023). To evaluate the effects of cryogenic storage on structural integrity, mEXOs and mEXOs@TOF formulations were stored at −80 °C for 30 days and subsequently analyzed using DLS (Fig. 4B). Importantly, neither formulation exhibited structural compromise, including particle rupture or colloidal aggregation under these ultra-low temperature conditions. These findings support the stability of this nano-platform and its suitability for long-term biopharmaceutical storage, consistent with studies on the stability of nano-medicines and exosomes under similar conditions.

In vitro drug release behavior

To evaluate the gastrointestinal release profile of the mEXOs@TOF nano-delivery system, we conducted physiologically relevant dissolution testing simulating human digestive tract conditions. As shown from Fig. 4C, mEXOs@TOF exhibited relative gastric stability, with a small amount of drug release during the initial 2 h incubation in simulated gastric fluid (pH 1.2), confirming acid-resistant protection of the EXOs carrier. Upon transition to simulated intestinal fluid (pH 6.8), a sustained release pattern emerged, achieving about 40% cumulative release at 12 h. This biphasic release profile demonstrates: surface-associated TOF undergoes gradual dissolution, while the majority of encapsulated payload remains sequestered within the exosomal lumen. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the platform maintains structural integrity during gastrointestinal transit while facilitating site-specific drug delivery to inflammatory lesions via endogenous cellular uptake pathways.

In vitro anti-inflammatory assays

NO is generated in vivo through the activity of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), which is induced during inflammatory responses. The induction of iNOS leads to substantial production of NO, which can contribute to tissue damage at elevated concentrations (Goodman et al., 2004). To evaluate the anti-inflammatory efficacy of mEXOs@TOF in vitro, we assessed its effect on NO and cytokine production in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. 6). After stimulation with LPS, NO secretion was markedly unregulated, confirming the successful establishment of the inflammatory model. However, treatment with mEXOs@TOF significantly attenuated NO production, showing a superior inhibitory effect compared to equivalent doses of free TOF or mEXOs alone. This highlights the advantage of the mEXOs-based delivery system. Consistent with its effect on NO, mEXOs@TOF also significantly suppressed the LPS-induced secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 (Fig. 6B) and IFN-γ (Fig. 6C), while concurrently elevated the level of the anti-inflammatory mediator IL-10 (Fig. 6D). These multifaceted regulatory effects were significantly more pronounced in the mEXOs@TOF group than in the TOF or mEXOs groups. Detection of JAK-STAT pathway-related proteins in RAW264.7 cells

The JAK-STAT signaling pathway is a well-established regulator of inflammatory and neoplastic pathologies, and it plays a central role in the regulation of immune responses and cellular homeostasis. As a selective JAK inhibitor, TOF demonstrates potent inhibition of JAK1 and JAK3 activity, thereby disrupting the signal transduction cascade of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines and modulating immune responses. To preliminarily explore whether the anti-inflammatory effects of TOF and mEXOs@TOF involve modulation of the JAK-STAT pathway, we examined the phosphorylation status of JAK1 and STAT3 in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells using WB analysis. As shown in Fig. 7C, while total protein levels of JAK1 and STAT3 remained relatively stable in TOF group and mEXOs@TOF group, a reduction in the band intensity of phosphorylated JAK1 (p-JAK1) and phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) was visually observed in cells treated with either free TOF or mEXOs@TOF compared to the LPS model group. It is important to note that this analysis is qualitative in nature, as it is based on representative blot images. Nevertheless, these visual trends also suggest that the therapeutic effects of both TOF and mEXOs@TOF may be associated with suppression of the JAK-STAT3 signaling axis.

In vivo the anti-inflammatory effect of mEXOs@TOF in UC mice

Following 3% DSS induction, mice exhibited reduced mobility, frequently huddled in the corners of their cages, accompanied by dull, partially shed, and unkempt fur. Treatment with TOF, commercial mesalazine sustained-release granules, and mEXOs@TOF alleviated the weight loss. Compared with commercial drugs, TOF demonstrated superior efficacy in ameliorating weight loss in murine models of UC. This is attributed to the fact that TOF is commonly used for moderate-to-severe UC, while mesalazine is primarily effective for mild UC lesions. However, mEXOs@TOF treatment significantly improved weight loss, indicating enhanced drug delivery to the inflammatory site, improved targeting, and reduced toxic side effects. Immunofluorescence staining for ZO-1 protein detection

ZO-1 is a critical tight junction protein essential for maintaining intestinal epithelial barrier integrity. Disruption of ZO-1 expression is associated with increased epithelial permeability. Immunofluorescence analysis of ZO-1 revealed that, compared with the healthy control group, the level of ZO-1 protein in the colon tissue of DSS-induced mice was significantly reduced (Fig. 9B and C), with almost no detectable fluorescence, consistent with impaired barrier function. In contrast, administration of mEXOs@TOF markedly enhanced the fluorescence intensity of ZO-1 expression, indicating its therapeutic potential in preserving intestinal barrier integrity and slowing the progression of colitis, possibly through enhancement of immune defense mechanisms. These findings underscore the pivotal role of ZO-1 in maintaining mucosal homeostasis and highlight the potential of mEXOs@TOF as a promising agent for epithelial barrier repair.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the rational design and synthesis of mEXOs@TOF, a targeted drug-loaded exosomal system, exploiting the advantages of exosomal nano-carriers for UC therapy. The formulation was systematically optimized and subjected to comprehensive in vitro and in vivo evaluations to assess its anti-inflammatory efficacy. The nano-delivery system was engineered to integrate safety, efficiency, and site-specific targeting, with thorough characterization of its physicochemical properties and biological performance.

mEXOs were isolated and extracted from fresh raw milk. Subsequently, mEXOs@TOF was prepared using a co-incubation method, where TOF was loaded into the exosomal cavity through gentle drug loading to obtain drug-loaded exosomes. The obtained mEXOs@TOF showed suitable particle size and uniform dispersion, with relatively high EE (∼ 36%) and DL (∼59%). Compared with previous studies on loading small molecular drugs by co-incubation, the EE and DL of mEXOs loaded TOF are better than that of curcumin (∼28%) (Schifano et al., 2025) and anthocyanin (∼5%) (Jiang et al., 2024), respectively. This indicates that TOF can be effectively incorporated into the mEXOs system to exert its anti-inflammatory effects. The cup-bilayer membrane structure and exosomal marker proteins remained intact before and after drug loading.

The most notable aspect of this study is the demonstrated favorable safety profile of mEXOs@TOF. The mEXOs@TOF formulation demonstrated significantly reduced cytotoxicity, with no observed hemolytic activity even at elevated concentrations. In a simulated gastrointestinal environment, the formulation demonstrated sustained release characteristics. In the cell uptake experiment, the membrane of mEXOs@TOF exosomes was stained red with PKH26, and it was observed by CLSM that it was easily taken up by RAW264.7 cells in a time-dependent manner, thereby confirming the efficacy of the system in delivering its cargo to immune cells.

At the same time, some studies have also demonstrated that microRNAs carried by mEXOs can regulate the direction and intensity of immune responses by up-regulating T cells while down-regulating IL-4-producing helper T cells. Additionally, these microRNAs may influence immune responses by inducing key immunomodulatory factors required for the Treg lineage development in the thymus. mEXOs actively participate in regulating the host's immune processes by carrying and delivering bioactive substances, demonstrating an immune regulatory function that cannot be overlooked. The mEXOs@TOF formulation demonstrated potent anti-inflammatory effects through dual modulation of oxidative stress and cytokine networks. In LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells, this system significantly downregulated pro-inflammatory mediators including NO, ROS, IFN-γ, and IL-6, while concurrently upregulated the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10. Mechanistically, these effects were mediated through precise regulation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, as evidenced by modulated phosphorylation levels of both JAK1 and STAT3 proteins. At the same time, we should be aware that it remains unclear whether the regulation of signaling pathway is due primarily to improved cellular uptake by macrophages, altered intracellular trafficking, or differential biodistribution in vivo. Some studies have shown that mEXOs nanocarriers can significantly enhance cellular uptake (Yan et al., 2022; Vedpathak et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). Because they enter the cells through multiple pathways simultaneously: fusion with the cell membrane, interaction through the cell surface proteins, and endocytosis (Krylova and Feng, 2023). Furthermore, exosomes contain various components, such as proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, etc., which can not only synergistically exert anti-inflammatory functions, but also alter the cellular transport and distribution. These factors may have led to the decreased levels of pJAK1 and pSTAT3 in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells.

In DSS-induced UC mice model, administration of mEXOs@TOF effectively attenuated disease progression by reducing the severity of pathological manifestations, including body weight loss and colon shortening. The therapeutic efficacy was further supported by improvements in disease activity index scores and a significant reduction in systemic pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, confirming its anti-inflammatory activity in vivo. Although we have not yet conducted a comprehensive quantitative analysis of histological inflammation and ZO-1 fluorescence intensity at present, these findings demonstrated the formulation's ability to restore immune homeostasis through the coordinated modulation of oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling pathways.

The mEXOs oral delivery system exhibits structural integrity under gastrointestinal degradation conditions, maintaining intact vesicular morphology and protecting encapsulated labile therapeutic cargo (e.g., proteins and nucleic acids). This inherent stability positions mEXOs as a promising platform for overcoming enzymatic and pH-mediated degradation barriers in the gastrointestinal tract. We acknowledge that although oral mEXOs@TOF shows promising anti-inflammatory effects, the current research is limited to preliminary evaluations in cells and animals. We have not yet conducted in-depth evaluations of pharmacokinetics, tissue distribution, and safety in vivo. This is particularly important for preclinical research and is also something that needs to be studied in the future. Furthermore, future development strategies of exosome-based delivery systems should focus on surface engineering approaches to confer programmable functionality, including: ligand-mediated targeting moieties for site-specific delivery, and stimulus-responsive gatekeepers for controlled payload release. Such modifications hold significant potential to enhance therapeutic efficacy through improved bioavailability and precision targeting.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the mEXOs@TOF nano-delivery system effectively can encapsulate TOF and exhibit significant anti-inflammatory effects both in vitro and in vivo. This approach offers a novel therapeutic strategy for UC and expands the application of mEXOs as an oral delivery system. The mEXOs@TOF delivery system has dual advantages, including inherent biocompatibility and reduced toxicity compared to free TOF, thereby circumventing gastrointestinal degradation and enabling controlled drug release. Mechanistically, mEXOs@TOF delivery system acts on the JAK-STAT3 signaling pathway, reduces inflammatory factors, alleviates oxidative stress responses, and demonstrates efficacy in treating ulcerative colitis.
