Prevalence of Malaria and Associated Factors Among Adult Population in Borama Community, Somaliland: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Hani Mahamoud, Fitsum Weldegebreal, Mesay Arkew, Ephrem Tefera Solomon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsMalaria is a global public health problem and remains a significant public health challenge in Somaliland, with a high burden of morbidity and mortality, particularly among vulnerable populations. Moreover, malaria infection in adults is a serious public health concern in tropical and subtropical areas, posing a severe risk. There is a paucity of data about the frequency of malaria and its associated factors among adults in Borama. This study aimed at determining the prevalence and associated factors of malaria among adults in Borama, Somaliland.MethodsA community‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted. A systematic sampling technique was employed to select 280 study participants. Information on the various factors were collected using pre‐tested structured questionnaire. Blood samples were collected and examined by using microscopic examination. Data were coded, entered, and cleaned before analysis using SPSS version 27. Both bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with malaria, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe overall prevalence of malaria among adults was 37.9% (95% CI: 32.1–43.9). Regarding species distribution, Plasmodium falciparum was the most frequently detected, accounting for 28.6%, while Plasmodium vivax comprised 9.3%. Participants who reported participation in the removal of stagnant water were 72% (AOR = 0.28; 95% CI: 0.07–0.89, p = 0.02) less likely to be infected with malaria.ConclusionsApproximately two out of five participants were infected with malaria, indicating that malaria remains a public health concern in the area. Both P. falciparum and P. vivax were identified as the circulating species. The removal of stagnant water was the only factor that showed a statistically significant association with malaria infection. Effective reduction of malaria in Borama requires coordinated efforts that include improved access to healthcare services, strengthened community‐based prevention strategies, and active surveillance.

Introduction

Malaria is an acute febrile illness caused by Plasmodium parasites, transmitted to humans through the bites of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. The disease is preventable and curable, and it is not contagious between people. Among five Plasmodium species infecting humans, Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax pose the greatest threat [1].

According to the World Malaria report, an estimated 282 million malaria cases occurred globally in 2024, with 610,000 deaths reported. When compared with 2023, 2024 is 9 million higher [2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), malaria remains one of the most serious public health challenges in Africa, especially in sub‐Saharan Africa. The WHO African Region carries the highest burden of the disease globally. Africa accounted for approximately 95% of global malaria cases and 95% malaria deaths in 2023–2024 [3]. The countries with the highest malaria burden in Africa include Nigeria, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and Niger [4].

In Africa, transmission is influenced by demographic factors (age and gender), environmental factors (the presence or absence of bushes and forests), climatic factors (temperature, rainfall, humidity), and socioeconomic factors (education, occupation and income), which affect human exposure and treatment pattern [5]. Behavioral interventions, such as consistent bed net use, elimination of stagnant water, and public awareness campaigns, are critical to reducing malaria transmission [6, 7].

Globally, malaria continues to impose a heavy burden, with 247 million cases and approximately 619,000 deaths in 2021 across 84 endemic countries [8]. In Somaliland, the main vectors are Anopheles arabiensis, Anopheles stephensi, which can bite both indoors and outdoors, complicating control efforts [9]. Adults play a crucial role in malaria transmission, serving as reservoirs that sustain infections in the community and contribute to morbidity and mortality [10, 11]. Despite ongoing prevention measures, malaria remains a major public health problem in Somaliland, with Plasmodium falciparum accounting for more than 95% of cases and rising reports of P. vivax [12]. Coverage of insecticide‐treated nets (ITNs) and long‐lasting insecticide nets (LLINs) remains limited [12], and environmental factors, such as stagnant water and irrigation areas, further increase transmission risk.

In Somaliland, malaria transmission is seasonal, peaking during the rainy season, and is affected by poverty, limited access to healthcare services, population displacement, and environmental conditions [13]. Artemisinin‐based combination therapies (ACTs) are used for treatment, though drug resistance poses ongoing challenges. Despite this, limited evidence exists on malaria prevalence and associated risk factors among adults, particularly in Borama, making difficult to design targeted interventions. Hence, this study aimed to assess the prevalence and its associated factors among adults in Borama, Somaliland. The findings will provide valuable information to the community, health professionals, and policymakers, serve as baseline data for future research, and support the design of interventions to reduce malaria‐related morbidity and mortality.

Methods

Study Setting

The study was conducted in Borama City, located approximately 118 km from Hargeisa, the capital of Somaliland. Borama is the largest urban center in the northwestern Awdal region and is situated in a predominantly mountainous and hilly landscape near the Ethiopian border. The city has an estimated population of 300,000 and serves as the commercial hub of the region. There were 12 maternal and child health (MCH), 11 health posts, and two governmental hospitals at different locations within the district (Document review from the local government of Borama district). This study was conducted from May 13 to June 28, 2025.

Study Design, Population, Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

A community‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted. It is a community‐based study including both symptomatic and asymptomatic Plasmodium infections in adults. The source population comprised all adults who were living in Borama city, while the study population included adults living in the randomly selected zones during the study period. All adults aged 18 years and older who had lived in the selected zones for at least 6 months were included based on the sampling technique we employed. Also, any adult greater than or equal to 18 years, irrespective of the presence or absence of malaria symptoms, was included. Adults who were critically ill and unable to communicate, regardless of whether they did or did not have malaria, and individuals who had taken antimalarial drugs within 2 weeks prior to data collection were excluded from the study.

The sample size for prevalence was calculated using a single population proportion formula with the assumptions of 19.7% of prevalence of malaria among adults from a previous study [14], a 95% confidence interval, a margin of error of 5%, and 10% non‐response gave 267.

Sampling Procedure

For the sampling procedure, Borama city was divided into four zones, each of which was further subdivided into sub‐zones (Sheiks). Two zones; Sheik Osman and Sheik Ahmed Salan were randomly selected for the study. Each selected zone contains four sub‐zones, from which two sub‐zones were chosen using simple random sampling. Hayayabe and Kaama were selected from Sheik Osman, and Fardaha and Kuwait were selected from Sheik Ahmed Salan. The sample size was proportionally allocated based on the number of households in the selected areas, resulting in 151 households from Sheik Osman and 144 from Sheik Ahmed Salan. Households were selected using systematic sampling with a sampling interval of 39 households. Details of the sampling procedure are depicted in Figure 1.

Data Collection Methods

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire developed from previously published studies [14, 16, 17, 18, 19]. It was initially prepared in English, translated into the Somali language by a certified translator, and then back‐translated into English to ensure consistency. The questionnaire included items related to socio‐demographic, work‐related history, behavioral factors, and malaria prevention practices. Data collection was carried out through face‐to‐face interviews conducted by trained data collectors using the structured questionnaire. Supervisors closely monitored the data collection process, conducting regular checks to ensure the completeness and consistency of the gathered information. The principal investigator oversaw the entire data collection process in close communication with the supervisors.

Laboratory Investigation

One milliliter of venous blood was collected from each participant. Then thin and thick blood films were prepared on the same slide. For the thick film, 6 μL of blood was spread over a 10 mm diameter area using an adjustable micropipette, while 2 μL of blood was used for the thin film. The slides were prepared in duplicates and labeled appropriately. The thin film was fixed by dipping in absolute methanol for 1–2 s, and both films were air dried for 8–10 min. The slides were then stained using 3% Giemsa at pH 7.2 for 30 min and examined under the microscope using an oil immersion objective [20].

Data Quality Assurance

Data collectors and supervisors received a day‐long training, and the questionnaire was pretested on 5% of the calculated sample in a non‐study area. Supervisors and investigators reviewed the data daily to ensure completeness and clarity. Quality assurance was ensured by proper collection and labeling of samples, verifying participant information, calibrating equipment, using quality control samples, and strictly following the standard operating procedure (SOP) for Giemsa‐stained microscopic examinations.

Data Processing and Analysis

Collected data were coded, edited, and entered into SPSS (Statistical Product and Service Solutions) version 27 for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequency, tables and figures were used to summarize participants' characteristics such as socio‐demographic, ITNS use, attitude, and outdoor activity. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to examine associations between independent variables and malaria infection. Variables with p < 0.25 in bivariate analysis were included in the final model of multivariable analysis. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF). Statistical significance was set at p value less than 0.05 (p < 0.05).

Ethical Consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Health Research Ethical Review Committee (Ref. No. IHRERC/084/2025) of Haramaya University. Formal letters were obtained from the University and submitted to the selected Borama zones. Before data collection, all study participants were informed of the purpose of the study and their voluntary participation. The study subjects were informed clearly and in detail about the importance of the study. They were also informed that they have the right to refuse or withdraw from participating in the research without any explanation, and they have the right to ask any question at any time during the data collection period. Informed, volunteer, written and signed consent was obtained from all study participants. To keep the confidentiality of the study subjects, their names and any personal identifiers were not included in the collected data; instead, codes were used. Confidentiality and participants' rights were strictly upheld. All respondents were informed of their right to participate voluntarily or to decline participation at any stage. Respondents were assured that all information collected would be kept strictly confidential and used solely for the purpose of this study.

Results

Socio‐Demographic and Behavioral Characteristics

In this study, 280 participants of the 295 calculated sample size (94.9%) took part, and the remaining 15 were not willing to participate in the study. All the study participants were recruited from Hayayabe, Kaama, Fardaha and Kuwait areas of Borama in Somaliland. The mean age of the study participants was 32.91 (± 8.72). Among the study participants, 39.6% (111/280) were in the age range 30–39. Females accounted for about two‐thirds (66.1%) (185/280) of the participants, and 43.9% were single. More than half (52.5%) (147/280) of the participants had attained a college education or higher (Table 2).

Awareness of Malaria Infection

About 76.1% (213/280) of respondents were aware of malaria symptoms. Fever was the most commonly mentioned symptom, reported by 50% (140/280) of participants. Most participants (83.6%) (234/280) recognized mosquito bites as the primary mode of malaria transmission, while 39.3% (110/280) noted that temperature and precipitation could increase transmission (Table 4).

Malaria Prevention Methods and Environmental Factors

About 67.5% (189/280) of participants reported using insecticide‐treated bed nets (ITNs) sometimes, and around 64.3% (180/280) used indoor residual spraying (IRS) in their homes. Mosquito repellents were used by 54.6% (153/280) of participants, and more than half (51.1%) (143/280) wore protective clothing to prevent mosquito bites. Additionally, 51.4% (144/280) reported taking prophylactic medication against malaria. About 52.9% (148/280) of participants did not live near stagnant water bodies. However, more than half (52.1%) (146/280) lived near irrigation areas. Regarding household conditions, 52.5.% (147/280) had no broken clay pots, and about 51.4% (144/280) had no damaged or old car tyres in their compounds (Table 5).

Prevalence of Malaria

The overall prevalence of malaria among adults in this study was 37.9% (106/280) (95% CI: 32.1%–43.9%). Among the identified malaria cases, Plasmodium falciparum accounted for 28.6% (80/280), and Plasmodium vivax for 9.3% (26/280). Malaria was more common among adults aged 30–39 years (40.6%).

Distribution of Plasmodium Species Across Sociodemographic Characteristics

The age range 30–39 was the highest infected group with both P. falciparum and P. vivax, 41.1% (115/280) and 38.6% (108/280), respectively. The majority of the infected participants were single according to their marital status, with a prevalence of 51.1% (143/280) for P. falciparum and 53.9% (151/280) for P. vivax (Table 7).

In the multivariable analysis, only participation in eliminating standing water was significantly associated with malaria. Discussion

The aim of this study was to assess the prevalence of malaria and associated factors among adults living in the Borama community of Somaliland. The overall prevalence of malaria among adults was 37.9% (95% CI: 32.1%–43.9%). Among the identified malaria species, Plasmodium falciparum accounted for 28.6% of cases, while Plasmodium vivax comprised 9.3%. Eliminating standing water (AOR = 0.28; 95% CI: 0.07–0.89; p = 0.02) was the only factor significantly associated with malaria infection.

The 37.9% prevalence of malaria of this study is comparable to the findings from Mainland Tanzania (34.4%) [21]. However, the finding of this study was higher than studies conducted in Samre Woreda Tigray region of Ethiopia (14.0%) [22], Northwest Ethiopia (16.1%) [23], Dembiya district, Ethiopia (22.4%) [24], east Shewa zone, Oromia Regional State of Ethiopia (25.0%) [18], Mizan‐aman town, Southwestern Ethiopia (21.1%) [25], Tanzania (8.3%) [17], 13.6% result of systematic review and meta‐analysis in Ethiopia [26], Kisumu county, Kenya (28.0) [27], and rural areas southern, Mozambique (14%) [28].

This discrepancy may be due to differences in study period, design, participant characteristics, and geographical location. For instance, data collection in Samre district, Tigray region of Ethiopia, occurred in mid‐December, a period of declining malaria transmission [22], whereas the current study was conducted between May and June, coinciding with a peak transmission season. Additionally, the study by Setegn et al. used a comparative cross‐sectional design among febrile individuals from areas with differing malaria transmission intensities [23]. Altitude variation may also play a role, as Dembiya District lies between 1500 and 2600 m above sea level, while Borama city is situated below 1400 m, a condition more favorable for malaria transmission.

Conversely, the prevalence reported in this study is lower than figures documented in different parts of the world, such as in Eastern Ethiopia, Kalala health center (49.4%) [19], Southwestern Nigeria (63.3%) [29], and Ilorin, Kawara State, Nigeria (78.8%) [30]. These differences may reflect discrepancies in transmission intensity, climatic factors, vector distribution, diagnostic methods, and the effectiveness of local malaria control interventions. Variations in socioeconomic status, access to healthcare, and community use of preventive measures, such as insecticide‐treated nets and indoor residual spraying may also explain the relatively lower prevalence in Borama.

Literature indicates that there is a strong association between the elimination of standing water and the reduction of malaria transmission because malaria‐carrying mosquitoes breed in stagnant water. Removing or managing these water sources reduces mosquito populations and, therefore lowers the spread of malaria [31, 32, 33]. In this study, eliminating standing water was the only factor significantly associated with malaria among adults in Borama. Participants who practiced standing water elimination were 72% (AOR = 0.28; 95% CI: 0.07– 0.89, p = 0.02) less likely to acquire malaria compared with those who took no action to remove existing standing water. Moreover, this finding is supported by the raw data of the present study, in which 25.4% (71/280) of the participants identified “proximity to mosquito breeding sites such as standing water and temperature” as factors that increase the risk of malaria transmission when asked whether they could mention at least two malaria risk factors. The mosquito larvae develop in water before becoming adult mosquitoes capable of transmitting malaria [31], because standing water provides favorable breeding habitats for mosquitoes, thereby increasing the risk of mosquito‐borne diseases like malaria. Studies show that reducing standing water lowers the number of infective mosquitoes by decreasing available aquatic habitats [34]. Furthermore, mosquito larval source management can reduce the number of new cases of malaria (malaria incidence) by up to 74% and malaria prevalence by up to 89% [35].

Similar associations have been reported in studies conducted in South Gondar, Northwestern Ethiopia [36], Dembia district, Northwest Ethiopia [15], and Southwestern Nigeria [29]. This strong protective effect underscores the importance of community‐based environmental interventions as a complementary approach to conventional malaria control strategies. Artificial mosquito breeding sites, particularly those located near households, are often more persistent and widespread than natural habitats, leading to increased human‐vector contact. A recent study from Kinshasa, Democratic Republic of Congo, indicates that inadequate waste management is associated with higher availability of mosquito breeding sites and increased malaria incidence [37]. Furthermore, stagnant water combined with decomposing organic matter in residential areas further promotes mosquito proliferation and sustains malaria transmission [38, 39].

A key strength of this study is its community‐based approach, which enhances the relevance and applicability of the findings. The use of simple random sampling among adults further enhances the generalizability of the findings to the wider population within the study area. However, the study was not free of limitations; its cross‐sectional design limits the establishment of causal relationships between variables. Since we didn't gather information about clinical data from the participants, we could not correlate the laboratory findings with the clinical data. Due to technical faults, we were not able to do parasite density estimation. As per our exclusion criteria, some individuals with malaria may have been excluded from the study, particularly those who were critically ill and those who had already received malaria treatment without undergoing microscopy testing, which may have led to an underestimation of the prevalence could be another limitation of this study.

Conclusions

In all, the prevalence of malaria in the present study is high. The prevalence of malaria in the study area was higher than that reported in most previous studies, with approximately two out of five participants testing positive. Eliminating standing water was the only factor found to be associated with malaria infection. Based on the findings, it is recommended to strengthen malaria diagnosis and treatment services in Borama city by ensuring the availability of microscopy, and effective treatments such as ACTs, while also promoting multi‐sectoral collaboration among health authorities, local leaders, and relevant institutions. Finally, further research, including follow‐up and longitudinal studies, is needed to better understand the behavioral, environmental, and socioeconomic factors influencing malaria prevalence among adults.
