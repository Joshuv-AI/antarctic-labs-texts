Trends in the seroprevalence of hepatitis C viruses among patients received at the Centre Pasteur du Cameroun, with an indication of anti-HCV Ac serology from 2019 to 2024
Authors: Aristide Mounchili-Njifon, Pretty Rosereine Mbouyap, Laure Ngono, Elizabeth Berinyuy Nsai, Annie Epote, Suzanne Belinga, Richard Njouom
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatitis C virus (HCV) remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, especially in resource-limited countries. Hepatitis C is a major cause of cirrhosis, liver cancer, and death. This study aimed to determine trends in the seroprevalence of hepatitis C among patients at the Centre Pasteur du Cameroun with anti-HCV antibody serology from 2019 to 2024.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted on patients who underwent HCV antibody testing. Plasma was analyzed for this marker using an Alinity i immunoassay kit (Abbott GmbH, Germany) during the period from 2019 to 2024. Data were entered and cleaned with Excel 2021. Binary logistic regression tests were performed using SPSS version 27. A p-value < 0.05 at a 95% confidence interval was considered statistically significant.ResultsA total of 31,429 participants were included in this study. The overall prevalence of anti-HCV was 13.50% (4245/31429). Anti-HCV seroprevalence was 13.48% (1857/13771) in men and 13.52% (2388/17658) in women. In this study, being over fifty-six years old (AOR 6.21; 95% CI 5.50–7.01; p < 0.001), being female (AOR 1.12; 95% CI 1.04–1.2; p = 0.002), and diagnosis in 2019 or 2020 with respective ranges (AOR 38; 95% CI 1.23–1.55; p < 0.001) and (AOR 1.15; 95% CI 1.03–1.3; p = 0.03) were significantly associated with hepatitis C seropositivity. Our study detected a high seroprevalence of hepatitis C virus antibodies, especially in women, those over fifty-six, and patients diagnosed in 2019 and 2020, who were most at risk.ConclusionSerious public health measures are urgently needed to reduce the disease burden and transmission, through systematic screening of all HCV infections by confirming risk cases with PCR, and health education campaigns for the population on the dangers associated with HCV infection. A national HCV control program, backed by a sustainable funding plan, is therefore needed to accelerate the elimination of HCV in Cameroon.Trial registrationThe study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Regional Ethics Committee for Human Health Research of the Center (CRERSH/C) (Number: E00571/ CRERSH/C /2023) and adhered to the ethical guidelines outlined in the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki.

Introduction

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, especially in resource-limited countries. HCV infection is a major public health issue, causing conditions such as liver cirrhosis and cancer [1]. The latest estimates from the World Health Organization (WHO) indicate [2] that more than 50 million people worldwide are living with chronic hepatitis C. The WHO African region accounted for 9.2% of the 57.8 million people with chronic HCV infection worldwide in 2019 [3]. Cameroon, a lower-middle-income country in Central Africa with a population of 27 million, has a high prevalence of HCV, estimated at 190,000 people with HCV, but few receive a diagnosis and treatment due to resource constraints and inadequate healthcare infrastructure [4, 5].

HCV was identified in 1989. It is an enveloped virus with a genome that is a single-stranded, linear RNA of positive polarity. It belongs to the Flaviviridae family and the genus Hepacivirus [6]. The risk of HCV transmission is highest following contamination during transfusion, followed by drug use through injection or inhalation using shared equipment, exposure to non-sterile medical or non-medical instruments (e.g., during tattooing or piercing), pregnancy or childbirth, and sexual transmission [7]. The introduction of direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) has significantly reduced HCV incidence, prevalence, and mortality, especially in high-endemicity areas [3, 8, 9]. Sustained virological response after pan-genotypic DAA treatment exceeds 90% in the general population and among HIV-coinfected individuals [10–14]. However, low- and middle-income countries continue to face challenges in accessing screening and treatment, hindering progress in HCV diagnosis and therapeutic coverage [15]. A recent study in Cameroon showed genetic diversity, with genotype 4 (38.48%) and genotype 1 (37.11%) being predominant, followed by genotype 2, detected in 22.46%, with subtypes 4f (18.95%) and 1e (16.02%) being most common. and 1e (16.02%) [16].

There are effective treatments for hepatitis C; the goal of which is to cure the disease and prevent long-term liver damage. Antiviral drugs, primarily used to treat active hepatitis C, can sometimes enable the immune system to fight the infection without requiring treatment for new infections. However, treatment is still essential for cases of chronic hepatitis C.

In January 2016, the Cameroonian government brought-in DAAs through the Minister of Public Health, making these treatments available at a relatively affordable cost for genotypes 1, 2, and 4. Eventually, pan-genotypic treatments also became available. Despite these advances, further efforts are needed to strengthen screening strategies and improve the accessibility and availability of medications, which remain exorbitant in our context [17]. DAAs, while effective, are often expensive. In Africa, where many people live in difficult socioeconomic conditions, access to these treatments is limited, contributing to the spread of the virus. It is therefore crucial to conduct in-depth studies and collect more data to effectively guide HCV control policy in Cameroon.

In general, on our continent, different studies have shown a high infection rate among patients exhibiting signs of HCV infection. Little is known about the seroprevalence of hepatitis C viruses in underdeveloped countries like Cameroon. Caregivers are not sufficiently aware of the seriousness of this disease. To help our government achieve the WHO’s goal of eliminating hepatitis C by 2030, this study aimed to determine HCV seroprevalence in patients referred to the Centre Pasteur du Cameroun with anti-HCV antibody serology from 2019 to 2024.

Materials and methods

Study design, area and period

We conducted a retrospective study of patients referred by clinicians. The majority of these patients presented signs suggestive of hepatitis C virus infection and were referred for HCV antibody screening. This study was executed in the computer system of the Centre Pasteur du Cameroun (CPC) laboratory and covered the period from January 1, 2019 to November.

30, 2024, including only those referred for serology. The CPC is a technical establishment of the Cameroon Ministry of Public Health and serves as a reference laboratory for diagnosing and monitoring various infections in Cameroon, including hepatitis C virus infections. The CPC is medical analysis laboratories have a quality management system accredited to the international standard ISO 15,189. As such, the facility receives patients from across the country for routine screening and follow-up.

Serological testing

EDTA plasma samples were analyzed on six Alinity i (Abbott) machines in parallel, divided between two shifts (morning/evening). Internal quality control (IQC) adhered to Westgard rules. Anti-HCV antibodies were measured using CMIA (Alinity i Anti-HCV, Abbott), a qualitative test that detects anti-HCV with 99% specificity and 100% sensitivity. The method involves a two-step immunoassay, comparing the signal to a calibration curve. Results are reported as a signal-to-threshold ratio (S/S): S/S < 1.0 indicates non-reactive; S/S ≥ 1.0 indicates reactive. Procedures were performed under the manufacturer’s recommendations.

Data processing and analysis

Patient information, including date of birth, sex, and year of diagnosis of Anti-HCV antibody serology, was extracted from the database. Laboratory data were entered and analyzed using SPSS version 27 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Descriptive analysis describes and calculates the frequencies and percentages of variables. A chi-square test of proportions identified significant differences between variables, with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of study participants

Over the past six years (2019–2024), 31,429 study participants underwent testing for HCV antibody serology, and all provided blood samples to the CPC serology laboratory. Most of them, 17,658 (56.2%), were female, compared to 13,771 (43.8%) male participants. Patients’ ages ranged from one to one hundred and eight, with an average of 43.33 years. Most samples were tested in 2019 (20.22%), followed by 2021 (18.24%) and 2024 (16.41%). The majority of samples came from patients aged 19 to 40 (45.52%) (Table 1).

Sero-prevalence of anti-HCV antibodies

In this study, the overall anti-HCV seroprevalence was 13.50%, or (4245/31429). Among participants, anti-HCV prevalence was 13.48% (1857/13771) in men and 13.52% (2388/17658) in women. Hepatitis C virus prevalence was highest in the > 56 age group at 34.53% (2907/8419), followed by the 30–45 age group with 5.55% (575/10357). Additionally, the highest HCV prevalence was observed in 2019 at 15.60% (992/6356), which was the first year of the study, while a clear decrease was noted in the last two years, 2023 and 2024 (Table 2, Fig. 1).

Factors associated with HCV infection

Binary logistic regression was performed to account for any confounding variables, and all variables with a p-value < 0.05 in the analysis were considered significant. Accordingly, the adjusted odds ratio was calculated for different age groups, gender, year of diagnosis, and HCV infection. The regression results showed that being female, over fifty-six years of age, and diagnosed in 2019 or 2020 were associated with hepatitis C seropositivity; participants over 56 years of age were about six times more likely (AOR 6.22; 95% CI 5.51–7.02, p < 0.001) to be hepatitis C seropositive than men. Additionally, women were 1.12 times more likely to be seropositive for hepatitis C (AOR 1.13; 95% CI 1.04–1.22, p = 0.001). The years 2019 and 2020 also showed a significantly higher probability of hepatitis C seropositivity compared to other years, with respective AORs of 1.38 (95% CI 1.23–1.55, p < 0.001) and 1.15 (95% CI 1.03–1.3, p = 0.03) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, the aim was to determine the seroprevalence of anti-HCV antibodies among patients who visited the Centre Pasteur du Cameroun for serology. We found an overall HCV seroprevalence of 13.50%. According to the WHO classification, the prevalence of HCV infection in this study is considered “high” because Hepatitis C virus infection can also be classified as high, moderate, or low when the prevalence is > 3.5%, 1.5–3.5%, and < 0.1.5% [18].

This seroprevalence is higher than that found in other studies carried out in Cameroon by Bikoi et al. in 2022 in patients with non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma in a hospital, which was 2.2% [19] and another study carried out recently in HIV-infected individuals with a seroprevalence of 3.8% of anti-HCV antibodies [20]. It was also higher than that found in Nigeria among patients with suspected liver disease (4.4%) [21] and in Pakistan among patients presenting to surgical outpatients 10.8% [22]. This difference may be due to the fact that in our study these were patients referred by the clinician with a request for examination for the presence of anti-HCV antibodies and therefore highly suspicious and with signs suggestive of HCV infection. This difference may be linked to variations in sample size, geographical location, detection technique, cultural practices and behavioral differences. and in the study carried out at Guhalla primary hospital in northwest Ethiopia, among patients attending the hospital serology laboratory 19.9% [1].

In this study, the prevalence of hepatitis C virus was high in the > 56 age group. These results are consistent with studies carried out in African countries, including Cameroon, which have shown that the average age of the most HCV-infected patients varies between 50 and 62 years [23, 24]. Several studies have demonstrated that the spread of HCV in certain African countries is the result of a cohort effect linked to past iatrogenic exposures. In particular, mass treatment or vaccination campaigns using non-sterile equipment and repeated injections have been a major source of transmission in Egypt [25], Cameroon [16, 26, 27] and the Central African Republic [24]. These practices, often annual, coincide with the age distribution of infections. Similar trends have been observed in Turkey, Spain, Italy, China and Japan, where people over 50 have a 20-fold higher risk of infection than younger people [28]. These data support the hypothesis of high HCV seroprevalence in sub-Saharan Africa, disproportionately affecting the elderly (≥ 65 years), rural areas, West Africa and parts of Central and East Africa [29].

In the present study, among study participants, the rate of HCV positivity was higher among women (13.52%) than men (13.48%). However, these results contradict those of a study conducted in Ethiopia, where the HCV positivity rate was higher in men (14.4%) than in women (5.5%) [1], and in Pakistan, where the HCV positivity rate was higher in men (2.5%) than in women (0.9%) [30]. This inconsistency could be due to variations in the study population and study design. Our results, which confirm the predominance of women as observed in our previous study and in other works, can be explained by several factors. First, in Cameroon, women have higher hospital attendance than men [16], particularly due to prenatal consultations. Although systematic screening for hepatitis C during prenatal care is not standard practice in our context, these consultations could contribute to more frequent detection among women. While injecting drugs and smoking – risk behaviors for HCV transmission – are generally more common among men, Cameroonian women are also exposed to them, particularly in certain socio-economic contexts. Moreover, their vulnerability is increased by more common gender-specific practices such as the use of cosmetic piercings with unsterilized equipment. Finally, their more frequent use of health facilities (prenatal consultations, pediatric care, etc.) increases the risk of nosocomial transmission. In contrast, men are usually less inclined to consult, which could also play a role in this disparity.

Our results show a progressive decrease in anti-HCV seroprevalence between 2019 and 2024, with significantly higher rates in 2019–2020. This trend cannot be attributed to the impact of DAAs introduced according to the 2018 WHO recommendations [17]. Since our method only detects anti-HCV antibodies, markers that persist even after virological recovery. The observed decline therefore probably reflects an effective reduction in new infections. Several factors could explain this development: the delayed effect of DAAs on community transmission (the reduction of active carriers), improved preventive measures, or changes in screening strategies. The 2019–2020 period could correspond to the peak in detection of old infections before the optimization of therapeutic protocols.

However, to achieve the WHO’s goal of eliminating HCV by 2030, sub-Saharan Africa, particularly Cameroon, needs to prioritize efforts. An assessment of national HCV prevalence and modes of transmission must be completed to enable authorities to focus on prevention and utilize resources efficiently. More precise data on the costs and societal burden of HCV are urgently required. Twenty-one years after HCV’s discovery, the assessment remains incomplete, and little progress has been made in many countries over the past decade.

Limitations of this study

Since the study relied on secondary data from patients referred to the CPC by clinicians, it was limited by incomplete information and an inadequate record-keeping system due to missing parameters. Another issue is that, because of budget constraints given the over thirty-thousand-patient population, laboratory tests were restricted to anti-HCV ELISA detection only; therefore, RNA, which is used for genome searching as a reference HCV screening method, was not utilized for confirmation.

In conclusion

Hepatitis C infection remains a significant public health issue. Cameroon is also affected by this burden. All stakeholders must collaborate to raise awareness, implement screening for hepatitis C, and ensure access to affordable care. Public health measures are urgently needed to decrease the disease burden and transmission, especially through systematic screening of all HCV cases, with confirmation of high-risk cases using PCR. Governments should also launch health education campaigns to inform the population about the dangers of HCV infection, aiming to achieve the WHO’s global goal of eliminating HCV by 2030. Although there is still much work to do, the participation of all stakeholders is vital to reaching the WHO’s optimistic target.

Outlook

This study highlights the critical importance of conducting representative population-based surveys in Cameroon to determine the true burden of HCV, combining anti-HCV serology and viral RNA detection. Such data would help distinguish between active and passive infections, identify key populations, and adapt screening and treatment strategies. These elements are essential to optimize resource allocation, evaluate the impact of antivirals, and achieve the WHO elimination targets by 2030, requiring urgent multisectoral collaboration.
