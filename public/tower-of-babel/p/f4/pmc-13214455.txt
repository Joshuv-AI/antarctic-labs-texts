Immune landscape of gingivo-buccal oral cancer indicates high enrichment of immune-related gene sets in tumors of a large proportion of patients
Authors: Debodipta Das, Arindam Maitra, Chinmay K. Panda, Sandip Ghose, Bidyut Roy, Rajiv Sarin, Partha P. Majumder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOral squamous cell carcinoma of the gingivo-buccal region (OSCC-GB) is the most common cancer among males in India and the second most prevalent cancer overall. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop precision therapies based on an evaluation of molecular heterogeneity and molecular subtyping.MethodsWe performed bulk RNA-Seq on tumor and adjacent normal tissue samples from 72 OSCC-GB patients, as well as leukoplakia (precancerous) tissue from 25 patients with concurrent leukoplakia.ResultsAnalysis of our data revealed activation of epithelial-mesenchymal transition, angiogenesis, and cell-cycle pathways. Metabolic analysis revealed enhanced glycolysis and reduced oxidative phosphorylation, consistent with the Warburg effect. Gene set enrichment analysis identified two distinct molecular subtypes, one of which comprises 76.4% of the patients. This subtype is characterized by higher immune cell infiltration, suggesting potential responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Additionally, CD226, CD38, and KBTBD8 were identified as potential biomarkers for patient classification (average classification accuracy of 86.1%) and were validated in an independent cohort. The smaller number of patients (23.6%) who comprised the second subtype showed lower enrichment in immune-related gene sets. Pre-malignant leukoplakia tissues showed significantly higher M1 macrophages and CD4 + T-cells compared to normal tissues, indicating an activated host defense mechanism and potential for early intervention. TCGA-HNSC tumors also exhibited molecular heterogeneity; some subtypes were similar to OSCC-GB tumors and revealed shared enrichment of glycolysis and immune-related pathways.ConclusionsOur findings provide critical insights into the molecular and immune landscape of OSCC-GB, paving the way for improved therapeutic strategies and immunotherapy approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10020-026-01473-w.

Background

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is the most prevalent malignancy of the oral cavity, with the gingivo-buccal (GB) region as the prominent subsite in South Asia due to widespread use of smokeless tobacco and betel quid containing areca nut (Bray et al. 2024; Gupta et al. 2016). In India, OSCC-GB contributes substantially to the cancer burden among men but remains poorly characterized at the molecular level (Mummudi et al. 2019). OSCC-GB is often subsumed under the broad and heterogeneous category of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), which includes tumors from diverse anatomical and biological sites (Cancer Genome Atlas N 2015). A focused analysis of OSCC-GB-specific molecular alterations is essential to explore whether some subgroups of patients are likely to benefit from targeted or immunotherapy-based treatments.

Recent transcriptomic studies in HNSCC have revealed extensive heterogeneity in oncogenic signaling, immune cell infiltration, and metabolic rewiring (Puram et al. 2017; Thorsson et al. 2019; Chen and Mellman 2017). Hallmark features such as epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), angiogenesis, cell cycle dysregulation, and the Warburg effect are commonly observed in these tumors (Hanahan and Weinberg 2011; Pavlova and Thompson 2016; Vander Heiden et al. 2009). Immune evasion via checkpoint axes such as PD-1/PD-L1, CTLA4/CD80, and BTLA/HVEM has emerged as a central mechanism (Sharma and Allison 2020; Topalian et al. 2015; Sun et al. 2023). Given the considerable variability in immune checkpoint expression and immune cell infiltration across patients and tumor sites in oral cancer (Diao et al. 2021), site-specific immune profiling is essential, especially because mutation profiles across sites within the oral cavity show notable differences (India Project Team of the International Cancer Genome C 2013).

To address this gap, we performed transcriptomic profiling of tumor and matched adjacent normal tissues from 72 OSCC-GB patients, including leukoplakia tissues from a subset of 25 individuals. Although not all leukoplakia tissues transform to cancer, it is most likely that leukoplakia tissue in the oral cavity of a patient with oral cancer represents a precancerous tissue. Our previous work demonstrated that PD-L1 and CD80 upregulation in OSCC-GB (Das et al. 2021) is driven by promoter hypomethylation (Das et al. 2019). In the present study, patient-level gene set enrichment analysis revealed upregulation of immune signaling, angiogenesis, cell-cycle activity, and glycolysis, with concurrent suppression of oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid metabolism. We further identified two OSCC-GB subtypes—GB1 and GB2—with GB2 characterized by higher immune infiltration (CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T cells, M1 macrophages) and elevated expression of checkpoint ligands (PD-L1, CD80, HVEM), suggesting greater immunogenicity. Immune checkpoint ligand expression progressively increased from normal to leukoplakia to tumor in GB2 patients, indicating early immune modulation. While OSCC-GB and TCGA head and neck cancer (TCGA-HNSC) tumors share core transcriptional features, they exhibit distinct immune landscapes, highlighting regional and molecular heterogeneity.

Methods

Patient recruitment

Following approval from the Institutional Ethics Committees of all participating institutions and after obtaining written informed consent, 72 patients with gingivo-buccal oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC-GB) were enrolled. Patients were recruited from the Advanced Centre for Treatment, Research and Education in Cancer (ACTREC), Mumbai; Dr. R. Ahmed Dental College & Hospital (RADCH), Kolkata; and Chittaranjan National Cancer Institute (CNCI), Kolkata. Tumor and matched adjacent normal tissue samples were collected from each patient. The generation of transcriptomic data from these biospecimens was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committees of the BRIC-National Institute of Biomedical Genomics (BRIC-NIBMG), Kalyani, and the Indian Statistical Institute (ISI), Kolkata.

Only treatment-naïve patients with histopathologically confirmed oral squamous cell carcinoma of the gingivo-buccal region were included in the study. Patients who had received any prior therapy, including surgery, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy, were excluded. Tumor samples included in the analysis had ≥ 80% tumor cellularity, as confirmed by pathological review. In addition, histologically verified leukoplakia tissue samples were obtained from 25 of the 72 OSCC-GB patients who presented with associated oral leukoplakia. In most cases, leukoplakia lesions were spatially distinct from the primary tumor, occurring either at sites distant from the tumor or on the contralateral side of the cheek relative to the tumor location.

RNA sequencing and data preprocessing

Total RNA was extracted from all tissue samples of each OSCC-GB patient using AllPrep DNA/RNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). The concentration and quality of the isolated total RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with the RNA 6000 Nano Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). For each sample, the OD260/OD280 ratio was ≥ 2 and the RNA Integrity Number (RIN) was ≥ 7.0. We used the TrueSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, CA) to prepare sequencing cDNA libraries from the RNA samples following rRNA depletion. The quality and quantity of the cDNA libraries were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with a High Sensitivity DNA chip and a 7900HT Fast Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies) with the Kapa Library Quantification Kit (Kapa Biosystems). We conducted 100 bp paired-end sequencing for each triplex cDNA library pool using the HiSeq-2000 and HiSeq-2500 platforms (Illumina, CA). All assays were carried out following the manufacturer’s protocols.

The quality of the FASTQ files, generated by demultiplexing the raw sequencing data, was assessed using FastQC (v. 0.10.1) software (www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Over 50 million high-quality paired-end RNA sequence reads were generated from each tumor, leukoplakia, and adjacent normal tissue sample. The paired-end RNA-seq reads were aligned to the human reference transcript (ftp://ftp.illumina.com/Homo_sapiens/UCSC/hg19/) and reference genome (hg19) using the Tuxedo protocol (TopHat2 (v. 2.1.1) (Kim et al. 2013), Bowtie 2 (v. 2.3.4.1) (Langmead and Salzberg 2012)), and SAMtools (v. 0.1.19) (Li et al. 2009), with default settings. To reduce false positives, multi-mapped and non-concordant reads were discarded using SAMtools, and duplicate reads were identified and removed with PICARD's MarkDuplicates (v. 2.17.0) tool (https://github.com/broadinstitute/picard). Mapped transcripts were assembled with Cufflinks (v. 2.2.1) (Trapnell et al. 2012) using default settings. The normalized gene expression (FPKM) value for each sample was calculated using Cuffnorm (v. 2.2.1) (Trapnell et al. 2012) with default parameters.

TCGA data acquisition

The gene expression profiles of 520 HNSCC patients from TCGA Pan-Cancer Atlas datasets were downloaded from the UCSC Xena (https://xena.ucsc.edu/) in October 2023. The data had been uniformly processed using the UCSC Toil RNA-seq recompute pipeline, which performs standardized alignment, quantification, and normalization across tissues and donors to minimize technical variability (Goldman et al. 2020). Normalized expression data were provided as log₂-transformed FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped) values with an offset, which were back-transformed to FPKM for downstream analyses. For further analyses, we considered only those 471 TCGA-HNSC patients with no evidence of metastasis. Adjacent normal samples were available for 41 of 471 TCGA-HNSC. Detailed clinical information for all patients was obtained from the TCGA Pan-Cancer Atlas (https://gdc.cancer.gov/about-data/publications/pancanatlas).

We also retrieved normalized protein expression data generated using the reverse phase protein array (RPPA) platform for TCGA Pan-Cancer samples from UCSC Xena.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA)

Expressed genes with average FPKM > 1 across 72 OSCC-GB tumor–normal pairs (n = 13,413) were ranked by average log₂ fold-change in tumor compared to paired adjacent-normal samples. For TCGA-HNSC patients, expressed genes (average FPKM > 1 across 41 tumor–normal pairs) (n = 15,070) were similarly ranked by average log₂ fold-change. These ranked gene lists were used for gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using the clusterProfiler R package (v. 3.18.1) (Yu et al. 2012) to assess enrichment of hallmark gene set collections (n = 50) from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB v7.4) (Liberzon et al. 2015), with all parameters set to default.

For patient-level analyses, we used the same set of genes expressed in 72 OSCC-GB and 41 TCGA-HNSC patients described above. Genes for each OSCC-GB (n = 72) and TCGA-HNSC (n = 41) sample were ranked individually based on log2 fold-change between tumor and paired normal tissues. Subsequently, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed to assess enrichment of the hallmark gene sets (n = 50) using the ssGSEA2.0 R package (https://github.com/broadinstitute/ssGSEA2.0) with default settings.

Transcriptome-based classification of OSCC-GB and TCGA-HNSC

Unsupervised clustering of OSCC-GB (n = 72) and TCGA-HNSC (n = 41) patients was conducted using Ward’s agglomerative method on Euclidean distances calculated from ssGSEA-derived normalized enrichment scores (NES) of the 50 hallmark gene sets. The analysis was carried out in R using the pheatmap package (v. 1.0.12).

Characterization of the immune microenvironment

We applied the ESTIMATE algorithm using the R-based Estimate package (v. 1.0.13) (Yoshihara et al. 2013) to identify the infiltration level of immune and stromal cells in each tumor from OSCC-GB (n = 72) and TCGA-HNSC (n = 471). CIBERSORT deconvolution algorithm (Chen et al. 2018a) was used to characterize the immune cell compositions (n = 22) from the normalized gene expression (FPKM) profiles of all tumor (OSCC-GB, n = 72; TCGA-HNSC, n = 471), adjacent normal (OSCC-GB, n = 72), and leukoplakia (n = 25) tissue samples. CIBERSORT was run in “absolute mode” using 1000 permutations to generate the p-values. Immune infiltration was characterized by using transcriptome-based computational deconvolution approaches, and no additional immunohistochemistry (IHC)-based validation was performed in the present study.

Statistical analysis

Because tumor–normal paired samples are not always available in clinical settings, we sought to identify a minimal set of marker genes capable of classifying OSCC-GB tumors using tumor-only transcriptomic data. To achieve this, we applied partial least squares–discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), a supervised method that combines dimensionality reduction with classification to select discriminative features (Barker and Rayens 2003). PLS-DA was performed using FPKM values of the same set of genes used in the GSEA/ssGSEA analyses, derived exclusively from tumor samples of 72 OSCC-GB patients. Analyses were conducted in R using the ropls Bioconductor package (v. 1.22.0) (Thevenot et al. 2015) with Pareto-scaled gene expression values. The contribution of each gene to the classification model was assessed using variable importance in projection (VIP) scores, and genes with VIP > 1 were considered candidate discriminative features. To identify potential cluster-specific biomarkers, two sample t-tests were performed to compare tumor samples between the two molecular clusters identified in the OSCC-GB cohort.

A similar analytical strategy was applied to 41 TCGA-HNSC patients, in which t-tests were conducted between tumor samples within each pair of the three identified HNSCC clusters. Genes identified as potentially discriminative by the PLS-DA model (VIP > 1) and also significantly differentially expressed in the t-tests were considered candidate subtype classifiers.

The diagnostic performance of the selected gene classifiers (biomarkers) was evaluated using logistic regression with Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) (Friedman et al. 2001) to assess their ability to classify (i) OSCC-GB tumors into GB clusters (2 clusters) and (ii) TCGA-HNSC tumors into the corresponding HNSCC clusters (3 clusters).

Validation of OSCC-GB biomarkers using external data

Microarray data for independent validation of OSCC-GB biomarkers (Bhosale et al. 2017) were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/; accession GSE85195). Log₂-transformed, 75th percentile–normalized expression data were downloaded for 34 OSCC-GB tumors and one matched normal (control) sample. To evaluate the robustness of the identified biomarkers, we applied a logistic regression model to predict cluster membership for patients in the GSE85195 cohort (n = 34) using log2-transformed normalized tumor expression levels of genes comprising the OSCC-GB biomarker panel. The model was trained using log₂FPKM values derived from the 72 OSCC-GB tumor samples in our discovery cohort.

For ssGSEA, ~ 10.5 k protein-coding genes were ranked by log₂ fold-change in each tumor sample relative to the control. The immune cell compositions within the tumor samples (n = 34) were estimated using CIBERSORT based on normalized gene expression values, run in "absolute mode" with 1000 permutations.

Results

Patient cohorts and sample collection

We collected tumor and adjacent normal tissue samples from a cohort of 72 OSCC-GB patients. Additionally, leukoplakia tissue samples were collected from 25 of the 72 patients each of whom had concomitant leukoplakia in the oral cavity (Fig. 1a). As expected, the majority of patients were male (81.9%) and habitual users of tobacco, primarily chewers (87.5%), many of whom (33.3%) were also smokers. The proportion of patients who presented with low (T1 + T2) or high (T3 + T4) tumor stages were nearly equal; 51.4% and 48.6%, respectively (Table S1). None of the patients showed any evidence of HPV infection. Enriched hallmark gene sets identified two clusters of OSCC-GB patients

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) identified 33 hallmark gene sets significantly (adjusted p-value < 0.05) enriched in tumor samples compared to paired normal samples (Fig. 1b). Positive enrichment in 78.8% of these gene sets highlighted activation of immune functions (including interferon alpha and gamma responses; inflammatory response; IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 signaling), angiogenesis, cell-cycle-related functions (including E2F targets, G2M checkpoint, mitotic spindle), TGFβ signaling, p53 pathway and glycolysis in tumors. Conversely, negative enrichment indicated deactivation of metabolic pathways (e.g., bile-acid and fatty-acid metabolisms), adipogenesis, and oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 1b).

Unsupervised clustering of the OSCC-GB cohort, using enrichment scores from hallmark gene sets (n = 50), grouped the patients into two clusters – GB1 and GB2 (Fig. 1c). GB2 patients had higher positive enrichment of apoptosis (94.5% vs. 23.5%), apical junction (76.4% vs. 35.3%), and angiogenesis (76.4% vs. 35.3%) compared to GB1. In contrast, GB1 patients showed higher enrichment than GB2 of DNA repair (94.1% vs. 58.2%) and glycolysis (94.1% vs. 60%). IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling was significantly (FDR < 0.05) positively enriched in all GB2 patients but negatively enriched in ~ 53% of GB1 patients.

Cosine similarity analysis of ssGSEA-derived NES values revealed higher intra-cluster similarity compared to inter-cluster similarity, with only a few GB1 patients resembling patients in GB2 (Fig. S2). However, no significant differences were found (Fisher’s exact test, p > 0.05) between GB1 and GB2 patients for any of clinical and histological features presented in Fig. 1c. Owing to incomplete longitudinal follow-up and exposure-related metadata in the OSCC-GB cohort, we were unable to perform stratified multivariable analyses to assess the independent effects of tobacco chewing, smoking, or their combinations on molecular subtype assignment and immune-related outcomes.

Significant differences (Mann–Whitney U test, p < 0.0001) in immune-related hallmark gene sets between GB1 and GB2 patients (Fig. S3) lead us to explore their tumor immune microenvironment profiles. We found significantly (Mann–Whitney U test, p < 0.05) higher immune scores (p = 0.0004) and lower tumor purities (p = 0.006) in GB2 tumors than in GB1 (Fig. 1d). Stromal scores were roughly the same between the subtypes. Using CIBERSORT absolute deconvolution, we found that GB2 tumors had significantly higher abundance (t-test, p < 0.05) of CD4 + (activated/resting) and CD8 + T-cells, memory/naive B cells, M1 macrophages, and monocytes (Fig. 1e).

Significant positive (Pearson's correlation; p < 0.05, R > 0) association between expressions (log2FPKM) of immune checkpoint receptors (ICRs) – PDCD1 (PD-1), CTLA4, BTLA, CD28—and CD8 + T-cell abundances were observed in the tumor samples (Fig. 1f). Similar positive correlations were found with CD4 + memory T-cells (Fig. S4a, S4b). No significant differences were detected in Human Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) or Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) gene expression levels between GB1 and GB2 clusters (Fig. S5), with HLA-A/B/C genes expressed in both subtypes. However, GB2 tumors showed significantly higher (Mann–Whitney U test, p < 0.05) expression of immune checkpoint ligands (ICLs), such as CD274 (PD-L1), CD80, CD86, and TNFRSF14 (HVEM) compared to GB1 tumors (Fig. 1g). Expressions of PDCD1LG2 (PD-L2) and three other ICLs were also higher in GB2 than GB1 tumors, although not at statistically significant levels (Fig. 1g, S6). The significant (Pearson's correlation, p < 0.05) positive correlations observed between the expressions of ICRs and corresponding ICLs – PD-1 vs. PD-L1 (p = 3.2 × 10–6) or PD-L2 (p = 1.1 × 10–5), CTLA4 vs. CD80 (p < 2.2 × 10–16) or CD86 (p < 2.2 × 10–16), BTLA vs. HVEM (p = 5.6 × 10–14), and CD28 vs. CD80 (p = 3.6 × 10–10) or CD86 (p = 2.2 × 10–14) – in the OSCC-GB tumors (Fig. S7) suggest an active immune checkpoint blockade mechanism. Progressive upregulation of immune checkpoint ligands from normal through leukoplakia to tumor tissues

To investigate immunological changes during oral tumorigenesis, we analyzed immune cell infiltration patterns and immune checkpoint ligand (ICL) expression across matched normal, leukoplakia, and tumor tissues from OSCC-GB patients. Since only one patient with triad data belonged to the immunologically poor (GB1) cluster, we focused our analysis on the remaining 24 patients from the immunologically active GB2 cluster.

Histological examination confirmed progressive epithelial abnormalities across tissue types, from normal buccal mucosa (Fig. 3a) and leukoplakia with mild (Fig. 3b) or severe dysplasia (Fig. 3c), to well- and moderately differentiated OSCC-GB tumors (Fig. 3d–e). M1 macrophage infiltration was significantly (p < 0.05) higher in (a) tumors compared to leukoplakia, and (b) leukoplakia compared to normal tissues (Fig. 3f). Memory B-cells, and memory CD4 + T-cells (activated/resting) showed significantly higher infiltration in tumors and leukoplakia than in normal tissues, with no significant difference between tumors and leukoplakia. CD8 + T-cells, naïve B-cells, and monocytes showed no significant differences across tissue types (Fig. 3g). However, monotonic dysregulation of ICLs (PD-L1, PD-L2, CD80 and CD86) was observed from normal to leukoplakia to tumor tissues (Fig. 3h). Additionally, ICRs (PD-1, CTLA4, and BTLA) were significantly (Pearson's correlation, p < 0.05) positively correlated with CD8 +, and CD4 + T-cells (Fig. S8). These findings suggest that targeting PD-1 or CTLA4 may effectively treat oral cancer and leukoplakia in GB2 patients, and a combination of PD-L1 and CD80/CD86 (which interact with CTLA4) inhibitors may offer additional therapeutic potential. Unsupervised clustering of these patients based on ssGSEA-based NES scores grouped them into three clusters – HN1, HN2, and HN3 (Fig. 4b). Immune-related gene sets were most enriched in HN3 and least enriched in HN2 patients (Fig. S9). Myogenesis showed significant (pFDR < 0.05) positive enrichment in 61.5% of HN2 tumors, while significant positive EMT enrichment was highest in HN2 (92.3%) and lowest in HN1 (27.3%) patients. Despite distinct hallmark gene set enrichment patterns across subtypes (Fig. S10), no significant survival differences were observed (Fig. 4c, S11).

ESTIMATE analysis revealed significantly higher stromal (p = 0.012) and immune (p = 2.62 × 10–5) scores in HN3 compared to HN2 (Fig. 4d). Nine immune cell types, including CD8 +, CD4 +, and follicular helper T-cells, showed significant (t-test, p < 0.05) differences between the subtypes (Fig. 4e, S12). HLA gene expression was lowest in HN2 but significantly (Mann–Whitney U test, p < 0.05) higher in HN1 and HN3 (Fig. 4f, S13). The ICLs—CD274 (PD-L1), CD80, CD86, and TNFRSF14 (HVEM) – were significantly (Mann–Whitney U test, p < 0.05) upregulated in HN3 compared to HN2 (Fig. 4g). No significant difference in ICL expression was found between HN1 and HN3 tumors (Fig. 4g, S14). We observed significantly high positive correlations between the ICR-ICL pairs, including PD-1 vs. PD-L1 (p = 7.5 × 10–6) and PD-L2 (p = 8 × 10–4), CTLA4 vs. CD80 (p = 1.6 × 10–7) and CD86 (p = 3.5 × 10–7), BTLA vs. HVEM (p = 1.9 × 10–6) in the tumor samples of TCGA-HNSC patients (Fig. S15), indicating an active immune checkpoint blockade mechanism. Additionally, PDL1 protein was significantly more abundant (Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.03) in HN1 tumors than in HN2, and was also elevated in HN3 compared to HN2 (Fig. 4h). Thus, HN1 and HN3 subtypes appear as immunologically hot and HN2 as immunologically cold HNSCC patients. However, none of the 41 TCGA-HNSC patients had a tumor mutational burden (TMB) greater than 10 mutations/Mb, indicating that the potential benefit to be derived from immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy may be limited in this cohort (Aggarwal et al. 2023).

Identification of biomarkers specific to gingivo-buccal or head and neck cancers

We applied a partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) algorithm to distinguish OSCC-GB tumor subtypes (Fig. 6a). Among 13,413 genes, 4431 (~ 33%) had a variable importance in the projection (VIP) > 1 (Table S3). Discussion

Our analysis of transcriptome data of tumor and adjacent normal samples from 72 patients has provided a comprehensive characterization of genes and pathways significantly and consistently altered in OSCC-GB. Our gene set enrichment analysis of OSCC-GB has revealed, consistent with some earlier findings, the activation of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and angiogenesis (Ling et al. 2021; Shivamallappa et al. 2011), cell cycle-related functions (Tan et al. 2023) and significant positive enrichment of glycolysis and negative enrichment of oxidative phosphorylation in tumors compared to normal tissues, indicating the presence of the Warburg effect in the hypoxic tumor microenvironment (Bose et al. 2021). Further, our observation of altered fatty acid metabolism is indicative of sustained ATP production and macromolecular requirements for the growth of cancer cells (Koundouros and Poulogiannis 2020). Our findings also suggest that the synthesis, decomposition, and transport of fatty acids are involved in various immune cell functions – destruction, activation, and differentiation – in the immune microenvironment of tumor cells (Zhang et al. 2024). We observed significant positive enrichments of major immune function-related gene sets in tumors compared to adjacent normal samples of OSCC-GB patients. The expression of immune-related genes was upregulated as cells transformed to moderate dysplastic stages in a mouse OSCC model (Lee et al. 2023).

A methodological limitation of this study is the RNA-seq analysis framework used for transcript quantification. The TopHat2–Cufflinks pipeline, widely used when HiSeq2000/2500 short-read data were generated, has since been superseded by newer aligners such as STAR and HISAT2, which offer improved splice-junction detection and computational efficiency. Although consistent use of this pipeline across all samples minimizes technical bias, future analyses using updated workflows – especially if longer read data are generated by, for example, the Illumina NovaSeq platform – may improve transcript-level quantification.

Immune landscape-based oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) studies have identified immune-hot and immune-cold patient subtypes (Diao et al. 2021). Despite overall positive enrichment, significantly high enrichment of immune-related gene sets and higher immune scores in a subset of OSCC-GB patients (GB2 compared to GB1 patients; patient subsets identified using enrichment scores from hallmark gene sets) similarly indicates non-uniform immune involvement among OSCC-GB patients. Considerable enrichment of anti-tumor immune cells, including B-cells, M1 macrophages, CD4 +, and CD8 + T-cells in the tumor microenvironments of GB2 patients suggests that those tumors were likely suppressed by host immune surveillance (Lei et al. 2020). Significantly high M1 macrophages and cytotoxic T-cell infiltrations in tumor microenvironments signify elevated pro-inflammatory and tumor-inhibitory functions among GB2 compared to GB1 patients. Classical major MHC I molecules were equally expressed in both GB1 and GB2. However, significant upregulation of ICLs, including PD-L1, CD80, CD86, and HVEM in GB2 compared to GB1 tumors, and a strong positive correlation with corresponding ICRs suggests that the GB2 subtype of patients is likely to be more responsive to immune checkpoint inhibition therapy compared to the other patients. BTLA/HVEM may be a potential target for treating GB2 tumors with checkpoint inhibition (Qin et al. 2019).

However, because none of the OSCC-GB patients were treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors and no longitudinal treatment-response data were available, these findings should be interpreted strictly as transcriptomic correlates of immunogenicity, rather than evidence of therapeutic benefit that needs to be ascertained in future experimental and clinical studies. Another limitation is that immune infiltration and tumor microenvironment features were inferred solely using computational methods (ESTIMATE and CIBERSORT) on transcriptome data. While widely used, these approaches cannot fully substitute experimental validation using methods such as immunohistochemistry, spatial transcriptomics, or single-cell profiling, and clinical validation, which will be important in future studies.

Our additional analysis of the transcriptome profiles of leukoplakia (precancer) tissue samples collected from a subset of 25 OSCC-GB patients showed a higher density of M1 macrophages and CD4 + T-cells in microenvironments of leukoplakia than of adjacent normal samples, indicating that host defense mechanisms are activated even in precancer lesions. Unlike higher abundance of CD8 + T-cells in proliferative leukoplakia compared to localized leukoplakia (Hanna et al. 2021), we were unable to find significant changes in expression of CD8 + T-cells in immune-enriched GB2 subtype of OSCC-GB tumors and leukoplakia compared with adjacent normal tissues. Progressively high expression of ICLs, including PD-L1, PD-L2, CD80, and CD86, in leukoplakia than normal tissues indicate that precancer tissues may be responsive to anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 combination therapy. We, however, note that an earlier report (Hanna et al. 2024) indicated that proliferative verrucous leukoplakia tissues harboring 9p21.3 somatic copy-number loss developed OSCC even after treatment with anti-PD-1 drug nivolumab.

We note that our interpretation of leukoplakia-associated immune signals should taken cautiously, as triad samples (tumor–leukoplakia–normal) were available only for a subset of patients and were underrepresented in GB1 tumors. Thus, these observations mainly reflect features of the immune-active GB2 subtype and require validation in larger, balanced cohorts.

The TCGA-HNSC data indicate that HNSCC tumors exhibit significant enrichment of gene sets related to epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) (Chung et al. 2006) and gene alterations regulating the cell cycle (Cancer Genome Atlas N 2015). Similar to OSCC-GB, there are positive enrichments of hallmark glycolysis, indicating the presence of Warburg effect in tumors of TCGA-HNSC patients. A previous study (Takahashi et al. 2021) on expression profiles of 520 TCGA-HNSC tumor samples revealed increased hallmark interferon-alpha response, myc targets, unfolded protein response, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling, and interleukin 6-Janus kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (IL-6/JAK/STAT3) signaling pathways in patients with upregulated glycolysis. Immune infiltration in TCGA-HNSC tumors, irrespective of HPV infections, suggests a complex interaction between the tumor and the immune system, particularly in HN1 and HN3 subtypes (Mandal et al. 2016). Additionally, potential immune evasion mechanisms were observed at the single-cell level of HNSCC tumors during early metastasis (Quah et al. 2023). These findings underscore the critical role of understanding these pathways in cancer biology and immunology, as they are crucial in tumor progression, immune evasion, and potential therapeutic targeting for future treatment of HNSCC patients.

Since GB1 and GB2 are predicted to respond differently to immunotherapy, it was of interest for practical purposes to identify a small number of marker genes that could differentiate GB1 from GB2 tumors; we identified that three genes could differentiate between these two subtypes of patients. Studies have shown optimal activation or re-activation of CD226 — expressed on the surface of natural killer (NK) cells and CD8 + T-cells — may be helpful as an adjunct to current PD-1/PD-L1 blockade treatments (Chiang and Mellman 2022). CD38 inhibits CD8 + T-cell function via adenosine receptor signaling, and their blockade could be considered an effective strategy for overcoming resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade (Chen et al. 2018b). KBTBD8 controls the stability of proteins that regulate the cell cycle and cellular differentiation, playing a role in the proliferation and differentiation of various cell types (Chen et al. 2021). Together, these genes play significant roles in immune regulation, cellular signaling, and protein homeostasis, with implications for immune responses and cancer biology. Experimental validation at the protein and functional levels will be essential before these genes can be considered for clinical translation.

Over half of HNSCC patients experience recurrence or metastasis despite current treatments, including immunotherapy, underscoring the need for new targeted therapies (Li et al. 2023). The poor performance of CD226, CD38, and KBTBD8 as markers in differentiating the three HNSCC clusters in the TCGA cohort highlights the need for new marker genes to improve classification. This discrepancy between OSCC-GB and HNSCC could be due to the unique profile of a subset of TCGA-HNSC patients known as HN2 subtypes. Markers specific to TCGA-HNSC have oncogenic roles in various cancers. For example, leupaxin (LPXN), involved in focal adhesion, promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion of bladder cancer cells (Hou et al. 2018). The transcription factor ETV7 was upregulated in breast and colorectal cancers (Meskyte et al. 2023; Chai et al. 2024). It stimulates the proliferation, migration, and cell cycle progression and suppresses the apoptosis in colorectal cancer cells (Chai et al. 2024).

Based on CD226, CD38, and KBTBD8 expression, GSE85195 patients were confidently classified within the GB1 patient cluster. Principal Components Analysis further confirmed that GSE85195 samples aligned closely with GB1 OSCC-GB patients. Additionally, these patients showed lower enrichment in immune-related gene sets and reduced levels of naive B cells, M1 macrophages, and CD4 + and CD8 + T-cells compared to the GB2 group. These findings underscore shared immunological and gene expression characteristics, suggesting potential avenues for targeted therapies in OSCC-GB patients.

Independent transcriptomic datasets specific to gingivo-buccal OSCC are limited. The external cohort (GSE85195) lacked tumor–normal pairs and immune-enriched tumors, resulting in uniform GB1 classification and preventing robust validation of the GB2 subtype. Larger, well-annotated cohorts with balanced immune states will be needed to fully validate the classifier.

In the TCGA-HNSC dataset, some predicted subtypes show immune profiles consistent with those observed in 41 patients with paired tumor-normal samples. HN3 tumors exhibit the highest immune cell infiltration, whereas HN2 tumors show the lowest. Patients with HN1 and HN3 subtypes have better progression-free survival than those with HN2. HPV-positive patients tend to have lower progression risk, likely due to higher immune cell scores (Mito et al. 2021). Similar to OSCC-GB, HN3 tumors exhibited elevated M1 macrophage and cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, along with higher MHC I expression, compared to HN2. Across tumor sites, immune checkpoint pairs such as PD-1/PD-L1(2), CTLA4/CD80(86), and BTLA/HVEM may serve as promising targets for immunologically hot tumors in the GB2, HN1, and HN3 subtypes. Differences in immune landscapes between OSCC-GB and TCGA-HNSC likely reflect a combination of anatomical site–specific biology, etiological exposures (e.g., smokeless tobacco versus HPV), and tumor–microenvironment interactions. Given the limited representation of gingivo-buccal tumors and heterogeneous HPV status in TCGA-HNSC, mechanistic inferences are constrained and warrant future subsite-matched and single-cell–based studies.

Conclusions

Comprehensive transcriptomic analysis of transcriptome data of OSCC-GB patients revealed activation of epithelial-mesenchymal transition, angiogenesis, and cell cycle functions, and significant involvement of the Warburg effect and altered fatty acid metabolism in tumors. Immune analyses identified two molecular states—immune-active (GB2) and immune-cold (GB1)—distinguished by differences in immune gene signatures, immune cell infiltration, and checkpoint ligand expression, highlighting substantial immune heterogeneity in OSCC-GB.

Several limitations should be considered: older RNA-seq alignment methods, computational inference of immune infiltration without orthogonal validation, analysis of leukoplakia in a patient subset, and limited external validation due to the scarcity of datasets for OSCC-GB.

These findings provide a framework for understanding immune diversity in OSCC-GB but remain hypothesis-generating. Future studies should incorporate larger cohorts, use multimodal profiling approaches such as spatial transcriptomics or single-cell analysis, and adopt longitudinal designs to validate these subtypes and directly assess their clinical relevance.
