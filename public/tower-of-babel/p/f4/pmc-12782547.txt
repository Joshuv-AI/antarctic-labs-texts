Comparison of angiographically derived coronary radial wall strain and superficial wall stress for the characterization of plaque vulnerability
Authors: Zhiqing Wang, Jiayue Huang, Chunming Li, Tianxiao Xu, Huihong Hong, Xinkai Qu, Lianglong Chen, Shengxian Tu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAngiography-derived radial wall strain (RWS) estimates the radial coronary wall deformation caused by pulsatile blood pressure, whereas superficial wall stress (SWS) summarizes the comprehensive wall deformation caused by both blood pressure and cardiac motion. This study sought to investigate the difference between RWS and SWS for the association with plaque vulnerability and the impact of cardiac motion on RWS.MethodsConcurrent RWS, cardiac motion-induced bending angle change (ΔCBA), SWS, and optical coherence tomography image analyses were retrospectively performed in 49 eligible intermediate coronary lesions. Correlation and multivariate linear regression analyses were applied to investigate the difference in the correlations of RWS and SWS with plaque characteristics and the impact of ΔCBA on RWS assessment.ResultsLipid-to-cap ratio (LCR), a novel vulnerable plaque indicator, was found to be correlated with both maximum RWS (RWSmax) (r = 0.58, p < 0.001) and peak SWS (r = 0.29, p = 0.041). RWSmax tended to be more relevant to LCR in comparison with peak SWS, albeit statistically nonsignificant (z = 1.75, p = 0.080). With multivariate linear regression, LCR was independently associated with both RWSmax (normalized β = 0.49, p = 0.001) and peak SWS (normalized β = 0.34, p = 0.012), whereas ΔCBA could only affect peak SWS (normalized β = 0.29, p = 0.035). Peak time-averaged SWS showed similar results to peak SWS.ConclusionsRWS tended to be more relevant to plaque vulnerability and less affected by cyclic bending as compared with SWS.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease remains the leading cause of death globally, mostly ascribed to the onset of acute coronary events [1], of which disruption of an atherosclerotic plaque with a vulnerable phenotype, generally characterized by a large lipid core, thin fibrous cap, as well as active inflammation, represents the major underlying mechanism [2, 3]. Despite substantial advances in plaque interpretation by various imaging modalities, accurate prediction of an acute coronary event remains challenging [4, 5]. Moreover, the uptake of adjunctive intracoronary imaging to standard angiography is not straightforward and is generally underutilized in the cardiac catheter laboratory [6]. A better strategy would be to concurrently derive plaque vulnerability information beyond stenotic severity simply from angiograms.

Recently, a novel approach of angiography-derived radial wall strain (RWS) analysis for plaque interpretation was proposed [7]. By delineating lumen diameter change over an entire cardiac cycle from a single angiographic view, RWS showed favorable correlations with morphologically vulnerable plaque features on optical coherence tomography (OCT) [7]. The use of RWS has also been extensively validated for risk prediction of deferred coronary vessels/lesions in multiple clinical datasets [8–11]. Compelling evidence clearly indicates a role of RWS in coronary lesion risk stratification with sufficient feasibility and accuracy.

Notably, besides the radial strain dependent on pulsatile blood pressure, coronary arteries undergo bending, twisting, and stretching deformation during cyclic cardiac motion as well. Accordingly, Wu et al. [12] previously proposed a unique approach that allows dynamic superficial wall stress (SWS) computation from 3-dimensional angiographic reconstruction at several representative phases of the cardiac cycle, by integrating the comprehensive deformation data of all four biomechanical vectors. In silico simulation indicated that softer plaques and positive remodeling were more likely to be associated with increased SWS [13]. Although both methods are angiographically based, SWS analysis requires higher image quality and finite element modeling; in this regard, RWS is obviously more feasible and user-friendly for widespread clinical adoption. However, in terms of plaque interpretation, whether RWS is noninferior or even superior to SWS remains largely unknown. The focus of this study was primarily on the comparison of the correlations with plaque vulnerability between RWS and SWS.

In addition, we also need to determine whether cyclic cardiac motion has an impact on RWS measurement, to prescribe the appropriate scenarios for RWS application. Since cyclic bending represents the most studied and major contributor to comprehensive stress/strain in coronary plaques [14], the impact of bending deformation as assessed by cardiac motion-induced bending angle change (ΔCBA) on the correlation between RWS and plaque vulnerability was investigated [15].

Methods

Patient population

This study was performed using datasets from the CardHemo core laboratory (Med-X Research Institute, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Shanghai, China). The local Ethics Committees of the hospitals where the patients were originally enrolled approved the retrospective analysis of these data. All imaging data were fully anonymized before being transferred to the core laboratory. We screened 277 patients with coronary angiography and OCT imaging performed in de novo intermediate lesions (reference lumen diameter ≥ 2.0 mm) prior to balloon pre-dilatation. RWS and OCT image analyses were performed by a certified analyst (H. Hong) and ΔCBA and SWS analyses by a second analyst (Z. Wang). Angiographic exclusion criteria for RWS assessment were as follows: 1) the presence of tandem lesion, diffuse stenosis, in-stent restenosis, myocardial bridge, or coronary dilatation/aneurysm within the interrogated segment; and 2) poor image quality including blurry lumen contours, severe overlap or tortuosity, and image acquisition lasting < 1 entire cardiac cycle after complete contrast filling. Lesions meeting the following criteria would be further excluded from ΔCBA and SWS measurements: 1) the lack of two optimal angiographic projections > 25° apart; 2) angiograms acquired at a frame rate of less than 15 frames/s; 3) insufficient image quality for 3-dimensional angiographic construction at any of the five representative cardiac phases. Exclusion criteria for OCT pullback analysis included the following: 1) incomplete lesion coverage; 2) vessel spasm or injury during OCT imaging; 3) incomplete blood clearance or substantial thrombosis; and 4) insufficient image quality or significant imaging artifacts. From the datasets, 290 vessels were excluded: inadequate for RWS and OCT analysis (n = 215), suboptimal image quality or lack of two angiographic projections with ≥ 25° separation for ΔCBA (n = 6) or SWS (n = 69) analysis. The final analysis consisted of 49 vessels in 48 patients (Fig. 1).

Angiographic and OCT image acquisition

After routine intracoronary administration of nitroglycerin, coronary angiography was performed according to the local practice standards. Angiograms were acquired by using a monoplane or biplane system (Allura Xper FD 10, Philips Healthcare, Best, Netherlands) at 15 frames/s. Then, OCT examination was carried out using a frequency-domain OCT imaging system (ILUMIEN™ or OPTIS™; Abbott, St. Paul, MN, USA). After complete blood clearance, an automatic pullback device was employed at a frame rate of 100 or 180 frames/s.

Angiographic analysis

Angiograms were analyzed at the core laboratory (CardHemo, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Shanghai, China) using the AngioPlus Core software package (version V3, Pulse Medical, Shanghai, China) and Abaqus CAE (Dassault Systemes, France). The assessment of RWS takes about 1 minute [8], while the computations of ΔCBA and SWS for each individual target lesion usually take 3–5 minutes and 15–20 minutes, respectively.

Detailed protocol of RWS analysis has been provided elsewhere [8, 9]. Briefly, a frame with clear lumen contours and minimum overlap and foreshortening was designated as the key frame for determination of the region of interest (ROI). The software then automatically selected three other frames, with the final four frames corresponding to four representative cardiac phases (i.e., end-diastole, early-systole, end-systole, and mid-diastole). After automatic lumen contour delineation and coregistration, the software calculated the RWS value at each longitudinal site of the ROI segment and presented the maximum RWS (RWSmax) and its location (Fig. 2A).

Cyclic bending (i.e., ΔCBA) was calculated based on a 3-dimensional quantitative coronary angiography-based bending angle (BA) assessment method [15]. Two optimal angiographic projections with ≥ 25° separation were selected. The ROI segment identical to that in RWS analysis was defined in the key frame (at end-diastole) of the first projection, and automatic lumen contour detection was performed. Then a frame at the same cardiac phase of the second projection was added for 3-dimensional angiographic reconstruction at the ROI segment. Manual correction was allowed, to ensure accurate coregistration between the two projections. The BA was finally calculated according to a specific algorithm using normalized proximal and distal vectors [15]. Likewise, BA at end-systole was assessed following the aforementioned steps. ΔCBA was defined as the absolute difference of the BA between end-diastole and end-systole (Fig. 2B).

As described previously [13], SWS was analyzed by acquiring superficial coronary wall deformation data and finite element modeling. First, 3-dimensional angiographic reconstruction of the ROI segment was performed at five phases of an entire cardiac cycle, being end-diastole, early-systole, end-systole, end-rapid inflow, and end-diastasis. Next, the reconstruction files were exported and meshed using ICEM version 15.0 (ANSYS Inc., Canonsburg, Pennsylvania). Superficial coronary wall was assumed to be an isotropic, nearly incompressible, Mooney-Rivlin material [16]. The material properties were incorporated into the finite element software (Abaqus CAE; Dassault Systemes, France) for stress simulation under prespecified loading conditions. The von Mises stress, a composite of three principal stresses, was used for characterizing SWS. Time-averaged SWS was defined as the sum of the stress at each site over a full cardiac cycle divided by the cardiac cycle duration [17]. Both peak SWS and peak time-averaged SWS were calculated and recorded (Fig. 2C).

OCT image analysis and definitions

The identical ROI for OCT analysis was determined by angiography-OCT coregistration analysis using AngioPlus Core software (version V3, Pulse Medical, Shanghai, China) [18]. OCT images were analyzed separately by another certified analyst from the same core laboratory using OctPlus, version V2 (Pulse Medical, Shanghai, China). The software enables automatic lumen contour and internal elastic lamina delineation and plaque characterization using artificial intelligence algorithms to derive quantitative parameters [19]. Asymmetry index was calculated as [1 – (minimum lumen diameter/maximum lumen diameter)], and lesions with an asymmetry index greater than 0.3 were considered as asymmetric [20]. Lipid-to-cap ratio (LCR), a novel vulnerable plaque indicator, was calculated as lipid burden divided by fibrous cap thickness of each image frame, and its maximum value within the ROI segment was recorded for per-lesion analysis [21]. A prespecified threshold of > 0.33 was used for LCR to identify vulnerable plaque [21].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed on a per lesion basis using SPSS version 23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation, otherwise as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables are presented as percentages. Correlation between two continuous variables was quantified using Spearman correlation analysis. Comparison of two correlation coefficients based on the same group was conducted using the method proposed by Meng et al. [22]. Multivariate linear regression models were applied to investigate the factors independently associated with RWS and SWS. ΔCBA and OCT-derived variables with p < 0.10 on correlation analysis were included in the multivariate model. The predictive performances of RWS and SWS for vulnerable plaque identified as LCR > 0.33 were evaluated by using the area under the curve (AUC) from receiver operating characteristic plots and compared with the DeLong test. The associations of RWS and SWS with LCR > 0.33 were estimated using logistic regression analysis. A two-tailed p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient and lesion characteristics

Patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. The mean age of the patients was 68.9 ± 9.9 years and 32 (66.7%) were male. Prior history of myocardial infarction and percutaneous coronary intervention were found in 18 (37.5%) and 22 (45.8%) cases, respectively. Most patients (75.0%) were diagnosed with silent myocardial ischemia or stable angina.

Angiographic and OCT data are presented in Table 2. Target lesions were mostly located in the left anterior descending coronary artery (42.8%). In general, quantitative analysis disclosed a median minimum lumen area of 2.02 (1.16–2.42) mm2 and a mean LCR of 0.25 ± 0.13. The median ΔCBA for cyclic bending assessment was 6.1 (2.7–8.2)°. RWSmax had a median value of 11.1 (10.0–13.9)% with 87.7% located in the proximal or throat region of the lesion segment. The median value was 36.8 (28.4–54.5) kPa and 26.4 (22.7–34.9) kPa for peak and peak time-averaged SWS, respectively.

Correlations of ΔCBA with RWS and SWS

For an identical lesion segment, ΔCBA was significantly correlated with both peak SWS [rho = 0.30, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.01–0.55, p = 0.034] and peak time-averaged SWS (rho = = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.03–0.59, p = 0.017) (Fig. 3A–C). By contrast, no significant correlation of ΔCBA with RWSmax was observed.

Comparison of RWS and SWS for the correlations with OCT characteristics

In the setting of intermediate stenosis, RWSmax was positively correlated with plaque burden (rho = 0.30), lipid burden (rho = 0.40), as well as LCR (rho = 0.58), and it was negatively correlated with fibrous cap thickness (rho = −0.35) (all p-values < 0.05; Tab. 3, Fig. 3D). There was also a trend toward a positive correlation of RWSmax with asymmetric index (rho = 0.25, p = 0.082) and percent area stenosis (rho = 0.27, p = 0.060). Peak SWS was predominantly correlated with fibrous cap thickness (rho = −0.35) and LCR (rho = 0.29) (both p-values < 0.05; Fig. E–F), and similar results were found for peak time-averaged SWS. In terms of the correlation strength, RWSmax tended to be more relevant to LCR in comparison with peak SWS (z = 1.75, p = 0.080) and peak time-averaged SWS (z = 1.64, p = 0.102), although it was statistically nonsignificant. Meanwhile, RWSmax also demonstrated the highest discriminative ability in predicting vulnerable plaque identified as LCR >0.33 (AUC = 0.833, 95% CI: 0.696–0.969; Fig. 4), which was numerically higher than that of peak SWS (AUC = 0.634, 95% CI: 0.439–0.839; DeLong test p = = 0.130) and peak time-averaged SWS (AUC = 0.672, 95% CI: 0.485–0.860; DeLong test p = 0.205). A 1% increase in RWSmax was significantly associated with an 87% higher risk of LCR >0.33 (odds ratio = = 1.87, 95% CI: 1.25–2.81; p = 0.003). A 1-kPa increase in peak time-averaged SWS (odds ratio = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.00–1.12; p = 0.047) but not peak SWS (odds ratio = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00–1.05; p = 0.083) was associated with a higher risk of LCR > 0.33.

Multivariable associations with RWS and SWS

In the multivariate linear regression models containing ΔCBA and OCT-derived parameters with p < 0.10 in the correlation analysis, both LCR and ΔCBA were found to be independently associated with peak SWS and peak time-averaged SWS (all p-values < 0.05; Tab. 4). Notably, only LCR was identified as an independent factor associated with RWSmax (standardized β = 0.49, p = 0.001).

Discussion

The findings of our study, comparing angiography-derived RWS and SWS in relation to OCT-derived lesion characteristics, revealed that while both parameters exhibited modest associations with plaque vulnerability, RWS demonstrated a somewhat stronger correlation than SWS. In addition, we found no substantial impact of cyclic bending on RWS assessment.

Radial strain was initially proposed for plaque interpretation using intravascular ultrasound-based elastography and palpography [23, 24]. By detecting tissue compression with the action of intracoronary pressure, the strain rate was found to be reflective of plaque composition, as a softer plaque with a large lipid core and a thin fibrous cap appeared to be more mechanically deformable [23, 25]. However, the diagnostic accuracy of these approaches for the identification of vulnerable plaque might be frequently affected by the out-of-plane motion of the imaging catheter, resulting in relatively low penetration in clinical practice [26]. The concept of RWS was inspired by intravascular elastography, since the tissue deformation was supposed to be theoretically correlated with the variation of lumen area and diameter. The utility of RWS for plaque characterization and prognostic associations has been extensively validated [7–11, 27]. Our finding of a significant association between RWSmax and LCR was consistent with previous observations [7, 27]. More importantly, we observed that RWS seemed to be superior to SWS with respect to plaque characterization. This can be explained by the fact that SWS is also affected by bending, stretching, and twisting deformation caused by cardiac motion, and accordingly, the radial strain property may be partially diminished. The preferable performance of RWS in plaque characterization and the straightforward nature should have clinical implications. Despite the modest correlations of RWS with vulnerable plaque features, a high strain amplitude may also have a direct impact on the progression of atherosclerosis by provoking plaque fatigue and rupture [28], resulting in independent prognostic significance of RWS beyond plaque vulnerability [10]. It should be reasonable to incorporate this adjunctive technique into the coronary lesion decision-making process, and it warrants further investigation.

It is well recognized that radial strain is primarily dependent on pulsatile blood pressure and local plaque composition, and it represents a surrogate for circumferential stress [24]. However, whether cardiac motion-induced complex deformation may also have an impact on radial strain remains undetermined. Studies based on fluid-structure interaction models have highlighted cyclic bending as an important contributor to stress/strain conditions in coronary plaques [14, 29]. As reported, although the impact of bending deformation on hemodynamic status is modest, the incorporation of bending together with anisotropic properties significantly increased maximum principal stress by 300% to 800% and maximum principal strain by 80% to 185% [14]. However, in the present study, we observed no substantial impact of cyclic bending on RWS. Of note, in vivo physiological conditions should be more sophisticated as compared with those prespecified in finite element simulation. In particular, studies’ conditions regarding the definition and range of cyclic bending might also vary significantly between the present and previous studies [14, 29]. Additionally, our study simply investigated the impact of cyclic bending on radial strain rather than the comprehensive vessel wall strain behavior in biomechanical simulation studies, which might also account for the difference to some extent.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, to ensure accurate RWS and SWS measurements, a substantial number of vessels were excluded from final analysis. Although a sample size of 49 vessels seemed considerable as compared with prior SWS studies, the relatively small sample size might be incapable of generating conclusive findings. Second, limited by the methodological availability, stretching and twisting had not been investigated. Their impacts on radial strain thus remain undetermined. Third, due to the relatively low prevalence of thin-cap fibroatheroma [3 (6.1%)], we did not investigate the associations of RWS and SWS with this commonly used dichotomous variable of plaque vulnerability. Finally, nonsignificant impact of cyclic bending on RWS was found in interrogated lesion segments with a median ΔCBA of 6.1°. Whether this finding is applicable to those vessel segments with larger bending deformation is uncertain.

Conclusions

The relatively high relevance to plaque vulnerability and straightforward nature enable angiography-derived RWS analysis as a useful tool for coronary lesion risk stratification. RWS seemed to be less affected by cyclic bending as compared with SWS.
