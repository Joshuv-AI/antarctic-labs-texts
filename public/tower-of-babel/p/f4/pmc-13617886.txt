Differences in fetal soft tissue thickness between GDM and non-GDM pregnancies and an exploratory nomogram for LGA prediction
Authors: Qiaoli Zeng, Chunyan Hu, Yuqiao Liang, Jiaxin Liu, Yixing Chen, Dan Zhao, Yue Wei, Runmin Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to compare fetal soft tissue thickness, particularly anterior abdominal wall thickness (AAWT), between GDM and non-GDM pregnancies, and to preliminarily explore its association with LGA as a secondary analysis.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 250 pregnant women who underwent regular prenatal examinations at our hospital from January 2021 to February 2022, consisting of 125 cases in the GDM group and 125 in the non-GDM group. Ultrasound measurements of conventional fetal biometric parameters (biparietal diameter [BPD], head circumference [HC], abdominal circumference [AC], and femur length [FL]) and AAWT, fetal thigh soft tissue thickness [FTSTT], thigh muscle thickness [TM], and thigh fat thickness [TF]) were performed at 24–26, 30–32, and 36–38 weeks of gestation. Differences between the two groups were compared. Correlations between each indicator and birth weight were analyzed, along with their individual predictive efficacy for LGA. Predictor variables were selected using univariate and multivariate logistic regression as well as LASSO regression to construct an LGA risk prediction model. Its performance was compared with a estimated fetal weight (EFW) prediction model. Finally, a nomogram was developed for visual prediction.ResultsMaternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, and neonatal birth weight were significantly higher in the GDM group than in the non-GDM group (P < 0.05). Fetal AAWT was also significantly greater in the GDM group across all measured gestational periods (P < 0.05). In LGA fetuses, AAWT and AC, and other parameters were significantly larger than in non-LGA fetuses at all gestational weeks (P < 0.05). Among all indicators, AAWT exhibited the strongest correlation with birth weight, and this correlation strengthened with advancing gestation (r = 0.666 at 36–38 weeks). ROC curve analysis showed that AAWT at 36–38 weeks had the highest predictive efficacy for LGA (AUC = 0.940). The multivariate logistic regression model identified AAWT at 30–32 weeks, AAWT at 36–38 weeks, and AC as independent predictors of LGA. The logistic regression model constructed based on these factors demonstrated good predictive performance (AUC = 0.966) and outperformed the EFW prediction model. Based on this model, a clinically practical nomogram was subsequently established.ConclusionsFetal AAWT is significantly greater in GDM pregnancies than in non-GDM pregnancies and is strongly associated with LGA. A preliminary exploratory model incorporating AAWT and AC demonstrated potential utility in identifying LGA, but these findings require validation in larger, LGA-enriched cohorts. The nomogram should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than ready for clinical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09665-2.

Background

In recent years, there has been a noticeable upward trend in neonatal birth weight, accompanied by a significant increase in the incidence of large-for-gestational-age (LGA) infants born to mothers with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). Statistical data indicate that the incidence of LGA reached 7%–8% at the beginning of this century in some populations, and was notably higher, exceeding 10%, in certain regions, including the southeastern coastal areas of China [1, 2]. Studies have shown that GDM pregnancies are associated with a higher risk of delivering LGA infants. This significantly increases the probability of complications such as shoulder dystocia, operative vaginal delivery, cesarean section, and perinatal asphyxia, elevates the risks of maternal birth canal injury and postpartum hemorrhage, and also markedly raises the risks of diabetes and microvascular disease in these offspring during adulthood compared to the normal pregnancy population [3]. Therefore, the effective clinical identification of LGA can enhance the detection of abnormal fetal growth, optimize prenatal diagnosis and management strategies, and improve maternal and neonatal outcomes [4, 5].

Currently, the Hadlock formula, established four decades ago, is widely used in clinical practice [6]. It plays an important role in the timely detection of abnormal fetal growth [7]. However, some studies have found that the accuracy of this formula for estimating fetal weight is only around 70% [8, 9]. Its accuracy further decreases when applied to LGA fetuses, with measurement errors as high as 15%, often leading to an underestimation of LGA infant weight [10, 11]. This limitation may be because three of the formula’s parameters are related to bone (skull, femur), with only AC related to fetal soft tissue. Fetal soft tissue generally includes skin, adipose tissue, muscle, and fascial tissue. Research has found that fetal fat content is directly related to energy storage, and fat and muscle are commonly used to assess fetal nutritional status [12]. Total body fat in the fetus accounts for approximately 12–14% of its birth weight, and about 46% of the variation in birth weight (BW) may be associated with fetal fat content. Therefore, emphasizing the ultrasonographic measurement of fetal soft tissue thickness may contribute to a more accurate assessment of fetal BW.

Lertvutivivat et al. found that fetal anterior abdominal wall thickness (AAWT) was significantly increased in the mid-to-late trimester in women with GDM, and that fetal AAWT was more sensitive than AC in identifying LGA [13]. Another study discovered that fetal AAWT and fetal thigh soft tissue thickness (FTSTT) showed strong positive correlations with neonatal skinfold thickness measured at the anterior superior iliac spine, subscapular, and above the quadriceps femoris sites, respectively [14]. It was proposed that ultrasonographic measurement of fetal subcutaneous soft tissue thickness is a non-invasive and reliable method for predicting neonatal skinfold thickness, thereby further reflecting fetal nutritional status. Other researchers further studied FTSTT by separately assessing thigh muscle thickness (TM) and thigh subcutaneous tissue thickness (TF) [15]. Their findings suggested that incorporating fetal thigh fat measurements into traditional fetal weight prediction formulas could improve prediction accuracy. Maruotti et al. concluded that ultrasonographic measurement of fetal soft tissue parameters (including abdominal and thigh regions) in the third trimester showed significant predictive efficacy for macrosomia, with an AUC of 0.92 and sensitivity and specificity of 80% and 95%, respectively [16]. This indicates that this method aids in the accurate detection of macrosomic infants.

Some domestic and international studies have measured AAWT, TM, and TF in fetuses of healthy pregnant women. However, research focusing on soft tissue thickness in fetuses of women with GDM is scarce. Given that adipose tissue is the component most susceptible to changes influenced by external factors during intrauterine growth and development [17], assessing fetal soft tissue status, which reflects adipose tissue, holds significant clinical value for guiding timely and appropriate management decisions in GDM pregnancies.

This study primarily aims to compare fetal soft tissue thickness (AAWT, FTSTT, TM, TF) between GDM and non-GDM pregnancies across three gestational windows, in order to characterize the differential pattern of soft tissue deposition associated with maternal glucose metabolism. As a secondary exploratory analysis, we also examined the association between these ultrasound parameters and LGA, and constructed a preliminary nomogram to visualize this relationship.

Methods

Study population

General information

Pregnant women who underwent regular antenatal checkups and routine prenatal ultrasound examinations at Shunde Women and Children’s Hospital of Guangdong Medical University between January 2021 and February 2022 were selected for this study. A total of 250 women were included, comprising 125 in the normal pregnancy group (non-GDM group) and 125 in the GDM pregnancy group (GDM group). All participants provided written informed consent for the use of their routine prenatal examination data for research purposes. Complete clinical data for both mothers and neonates were collected, including maternal age, height, pre-pregnancy BMI, gestational age at delivery, neonatal birth weight, and fetal sex.

Selection criteria

Inclusion criteria (for both GDM and non-GDM groups)

① Singleton pregnancy. ② Women who underwent ultrasound examinations at 24–26, 30–32, and 36–38 weeks of gestation. ③ Confirmed absence of fetal structural anomalies and fetal growth restriction (FGR) on prenatal ultrasound. ④ No other pregnancy-related complications (e.g., preeclampsia, pregnancy-related anemia, heart disease). ⑤ No chronic systemic diseases affecting embryonic development (e.g., viral hepatitis, thyroid dysfunction, cardiac insufficiency). ⑥ No history of teratogen exposure (including teratogenic medications, ionizing radiation, or occupational toxicants). ⑦ For women with irregular menstrual cycles, gestational age was confirmed by first-trimester ultrasound.

Exclusion criteria (for both GDM and non-GDM groups)

①Type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus. ② History of GDM in previous pregnancies. ③ Other endocrine diseases. ④ Severe pregnancy-related complications. ⑤ Multiple pregnancy (all types). ⑥ Fetal developmental abnormalities confirmed by imaging or prenatal diagnostic techniques. ⑦ Fetal growth restriction.

Note: The only difference between the two groups is the presence (GDM group) or absence (non-GDM group) of a GDM diagnosis. GDM was diagnosed based on the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) criteria: a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was performed after fasting, with diagnostic thresholds of fasting plasma glucose ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1-hour glucose ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, or 2-hour glucose ≥ 8.5 mmol/L.

Instruments and methods

Instruments

GE Voluson E8 or E10 color ultrasound diagnostic systems were used, equipped with convex array abdominal probes (frequency range: 3.5-5.0 MHz) to ensure clear and accurate image acquisition and review.

Measurement protocol

The three ultrasound time points (24–26, 30–32, and 36–38 weeks) represent the standard prenatal ultrasound examination schedule for routine antenatal care at our institution, in accordance with Chinese clinical practice guidelines.

In our institution, standard routine prenatal ultrasound reports include conventional fetal biometric parameters (BPD, HC, AC, and FL) but do not routinely document fetal soft tissue thickness measurements (AAWT, FTSTT, TM, or TF). For the purpose of this retrospective study, all stored ultrasound images were retrieved from the hospital’s picture archiving and communication system (PACS). A single senior physician with extensive experience in fetal ultrasonography, who was blinded to the pregnancy outcomes and group assignments, retrospectively measured the fetal soft tissue thickness parameters on the stored images according to the standardized protocols described below. Each parameter was measured at least three times, and the average value was recorded for analysis.

BPD, HC, AC, and FL were measured according to internationally recognized standards [18]. In brief: BPD was measured on the transverse plane of the fetal head at the level of the thalami, from the outer edge to the inner edge of the skull; HC was measured on the same plane along the outer edge of the skull; AC was measured on a transverse section of the fetal abdomen at the level of the liver, including the skin layer (and was performed using the ellipse tracing tool on the ultrasound system); FL was measured along the calcified portion of the femoral shaft (Supplementary Fig. 1A-C).

AAWT measurement

On the standard plane used for AC measurement, AAWT was measured within an echoic area approximately 2–3 cm lateral to the umbilical cord insertion site. The measurement encompassed the fetal skin and subcutaneous tissue (Supplementary Fig. 1D).

TF and TM measurement

On the standard plane used for FL measurement, the distance from the outer surface of the skin to the surface of the muscle at the mid-thigh level was measured as TF. The distance from the surface of the muscle to the midpoint of the femoral shaft was measured as TM (Supplementary Fig. 1E). FTSTT was calculated as the sum of TF and TM.

Outcome definition

LGA was defined as a neonatal birth weight above the 90th percentile for the corresponding gestational age, based on diagnostic criteria referenced from the “Growth reference standards and curves for birth weight, length, and head circumference of newborns of different gestational ages in China (2020)” [19]. Macrosomia was defined as a neonatal birth weight ≥ 4000 g, regardless of gestational age [20]. Birth weight was measured by healthcare professionals within 4 h after delivery. Estimated fetal weight (EFW) was calculated using the Hadlock 3 formula [21]. Although BPD was measured as part of routine fetal biometry, it was not included in the EFW calculation because Hadlock 3 (which uses HC, AC, and FL) has been validated as a reliable standard in late pregnancy and is widely adopted in clinical practice, whereas BPD measurement can be influenced by fetal head shape and positioning in the third trimester [21]. For the purpose of model comparison, the EFW-based prediction model was defined as a logistic regression model using continuous EFW values as the independent variable to predict LGA status. We did not dichotomize EFW based on external growth chart percentiles (e.g., > 90th percentile) for this comparison. Instead, the continuous EFW values were entered directly into the logistic regression, which generated a predicted probability of LGA for each case. This approach ensures methodological consistency between the EFW model and the nomogram, as both output a probabilistic prediction, allowing for direct comparison of AUC, sensitivity, and specificity.

It should be noted that BPD, HC, AC, and FL are all standard components of routine fetal biometry at our institution. However, for the estimation of fetal weight, we adopted the Hadlock 3 formula, which uses HC, AC, and FL but excludes BPD. This decision was based on clinical evidence suggesting that Hadlock 3 performs reliably across various populations and is the default algorithm in our ultrasound system. BPD was retained in the biometry protocol as it remains an essential parameter for fetal head growth assessment, gestational age estimation, and cranial morphology evaluation, but it was intentionally not incorporated into the EFW calculation to maintain consistency with the Hadlock 3 formula.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.4.2). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution were analyzed using independent samples t-tests and are presented as mean ± standard deviation; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test was used. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test.

Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess the correlation between continuous ultrasound indices and neonatal birth weight. To evaluate the predictive efficacy of individual ultrasound indices for LGA, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted, and the area under the curve (AUC), optimal cut-off value, sensitivity, and specificity were calculated. To identify independent predictors of LGA, univariate logistic regression analysis was first performed on all candidate variables (including demographic characteristics, clinical indicators, and ultrasound indices across different gestational weeks). Subsequently, based on clinical relevance, candidate variables were included in a multivariate logistic regression model. Forward stepwise regression (based on the Akaike Information Criterion, AIC) was employed for variable selection to establish the final predictive model. Adjusted odds ratios (OR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for the included variables. To optimize the model and prevent overfitting, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was also applied for variable selection. Variables identified by LASSO regression were incorporated into the final multivariate logistic regression model.

Based on the results of the final multivariate logistic regression model, a nomogram was constructed using the rms package. The regression coefficients of each predictor were converted into a 0-100 point scoring system, with the total score corresponding to the predicted probability of LGA. The ggplot2 package and basic plotting functions were used for chart visualization.

The EFW-based model was constructed using univariate and multivariate logistic regression, with continuous EFW values at each gestational window as predictors. Model performance (AUC, sensitivity, specificity, Positive Predictive Value [PPV], Negative Predictive Value [NPV]) was evaluated using the predicted probabilities from this logistic model, and compared with the nomogram using DeLong’s test, Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI), and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI).

Results

Maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, and neonatal birth weight were significantly higher in the GDM group than in the non-GDM group (P < 0.05). Gestational age at delivery were significantly lower in the GDM group (P < 0.05). The incidence of LGA was higher in the GDM group (16.0%) compared to the non-GDM group (8.0%), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.080). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in the rate of macrosomia or fetal sex distribution (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Comparison of fetal ultrasound indices between LGA and non-LGA groups

Fetuses in the LGA group exhibited significantly larger values in multiple growth indices (e.g., AC, FL, AAWT) at all gestational weeks compared to the non-LGA group (P < 0.05). The magnitude of these differences increased markedly with advancing gestation. This was most pronounced at 36–38 weeks, where the effect size for AAWT was as high as 1.707 and for AC was 1.359, highlighting the important role of these late-gestation indicators in distinguishing LGA (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 1).

Correlation between ultrasound indices and birth weight

Analysis revealed that anterior AAWT at all gestational weeks had the strongest and most stable correlation with birth weight, with the correlation coefficient increasing with advancing gestation (24–26 weeks: r = 0.433; 30–32 weeks: r = 0.578; 36–38 weeks: r = 0.666; all P < 0.001). Abdominal circumference (AC) in late gestation (36–38 weeks) also showed a strong correlation with birth weight (r = 0.495, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Predictive performance of single ultrasound indices for LGA

The ability of individual ultrasound indices to predict LGA was assessed using ROC curve analysis. Among all ultrasound indices, AAWT at 36–38 weeks demonstrated the highest predictive performance, with an AUC of 0.940 (95% CI: 0.906–0.974). At the optimal cut-off value of 5.65 mm, sensitivity was 93.3% and specificity was 83.6%. AAWT at 30–32 weeks (AUC = 0.866) and AC at 36–38 weeks (AUC = 0.872) also showed good predictive value (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 2).

Construction of the LGA risk prediction model

Univariate logistic regression analysis

Univariate analysis identified several strong predictors significantly associated with LGA risk (OR > 5, P < 0.001), primarily including AAWT at 24–26 weeks (OR = 7.033), AAWT at 30–32 weeks (OR = 19.871), and AAWT at 36–38 weeks (OR = 9.313). Additionally, maternal age (OR = 1.092, P = 0.046) and pre-pregnancy BMI (OR = 1.285, P = 0.001) were also significant associated factors (Supplementary Table 3).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis

Candidate variables were included in a multivariate logistic regression model. Forward stepwise regression (based on AIC) was used for variable selection to establish the predictive model. Adjusted ORs and their 95% CIs were calculated. The multivariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated a good model fit (McFadden’s R² = 0.699, AIC = 77.26). The analysis revealed that AAWT at 36–38 weeks (OR = 26.44, P < 0.001) and AAWT at 30–32 weeks (OR = 30.18, P = 0.007) were the strongest independent predictors of LGA (Fig. 3), consistent with previous studies indicating that fetal abdominal circumference and abdominal wall thickness are important indicators of fetal growth and nutritional status.

Forest plot of multivariate logistic regression analysis for LGA predictors. Note:1 BPD, 2 HC, 3 AC, 4 FL, 5 AAWT , 6 FTSTT, 8 TF

Variable selection based on LASSO regression and construction of the final multivariate model

To build a concise and robust prediction model, LASSO regression was employed to screen the candidate variables. The variables retained after screening, combined with key clinical variables, were used to construct the final multivariate logistic regression model. The overall fit of this model was good (McFadden’s R² = 0.589, AIC = 85.49, BIC = 103.10). After adjusting for other variables, AAWT at 30–32 weeks, AAWT at 36–38 weeks, and AC were significant predictors of LGA (P < 0.05). Among these, AAWT at 36–38 weeks had the highest OR (OR = 5.606), suggesting a significantly positive association with the risk of LGA (Fig. 4).

Construction and visualization of the nomogram

The nomogram constructed based on the final multivariate logistic regression model is shown in Fig. 5. The nomogram includes three predictor axes (AAWT at 30–32 weeks, AAWT at 36–38 weeks, and AC at 36–38 weeks), a total points axis, and a bottom axis displaying the corresponding predicted probability of LGA.

Usage Example: If AAWT at 30–32 weeks = 4.0 mm (assigning approximately 28.5 points), AAWT at 36–38 weeks = 6.0 mm (38 points), and AC at 36–38 weeks = 340 mm (28 points), the total score is 94.5 points, corresponding to an estimated LGA risk probability of approximately 20% (Fig. 5).

Exploratory analysis: comparison of the nomogram with EFW for LGA prediction

To compare the predictive performance, we constructed an EFW-based logistic regression model using continuous EFW values (rather than percentile-based categorization) to predict LGA probability. This allows a direct performance comparison with the nomogram, which also outputs a probability.

After calculating EFW, univariate logistic regression analysis revealed that EFW at 24–26 weeks (OR = 1.006, P = 0.004) and EFW at 36–38 weeks (OR = 1.005, P < 0.001) were associated with LGA, whereas EFW at 30–32 weeks (OR = 1.001, P = 0.160) was not associated with LGA. When EFW at 24–26 weeks and EFW at 36–38 weeks were included in multivariable logistic regression, EFW at 36–38 weeks was identified as an independent predictor (OR = 4.50, P < 0.001) (Fig. 6A).

Comparison between the EFW model and the nomogram model showed that the nomogram model (AUC = 0.966) outperformed the EFW model (AUC = 0.857, DeLong test P < 0.001) (Fig. 6B). The nomogram exhibited higher sensitivity (63.3% vs. 26.7%), accuracy (88.0% vs. 76.0%), PPV (86.4% vs. 72.7%), and NPV (88.5% vs. 76.7%) (Fig. 6C). NRI was 0.0758 and IDI was 0.2804 (Fig. 6D).

In this exploratory analysis, the nomogram showed promising discrimination (AUC = 0.966) compared with the EFW-based model (AUC = 0.857). However, given the limited number of LGA events (n = 30), these results are preliminary and require confirmation in larger independent cohorts.

Discussion

Alterations in fetal soft tissue thickness significantly impact both intrauterine and postnatal development. Such changes can induce metabolic and inflammatory adaptations, potentially rendering the fetus more susceptible to higher morbidity and exerting long-term effects [22]. Compared to traditional methods for assessing fetal growth, evaluating fetal body composition offers distinct advantages. In recent years, ultrasound technology has advanced notably. Improved resolution allows for more precise analysis of tissue characteristics and enables accurate quantification of fetal fat accumulation.

Traditional ultrasound techniques for assessing fetal growth primarily involve measuring fetal biometric parameters, most commonly BPD, FL, AC, and EFW. AAWT is a simple measurement obtainable using the standard AC plane. This retrospective study systematically investigated the dynamic changes and clinical value of fetal AAWT in the management of GDM and the prediction of LGA infants.

The first study evaluating fetal abdominal fat layer thickness in GDM pregnancies was conducted in 2003. Greco et al. studied fetuses from 15 GDM patients and 16 normal pregnancies, finding increased fat tissue deposition in the fetuses of GDM patients [23]. In that prospective case-control study, the mean AAWT at 31 weeks was 4.4 ± 0.1 mm in the GDM group versus 3.7 ± 0.1 mm in the non-GDM group (P < 0.05). These findings are similar to ours. We observed a significantly higher mean AAWT at 30–32 weeks in the GDM group (3.87 ± 0.503 mm) compared to the control group (3.57 ± 0.486 mm; P < 0.001). The lower mean AAWT values in our study compared to Greco et al. may be attributed to racial differences and variations in the exact gestational weeks of measurement. Aksoy et al. found significantly increased fetal AAWT in women with abnormal 50g oral glucose challenge test (OGCT) results at 26–28 weeks, who also had higher maternal BMI, but no significant changes in fetal weight or AC [24]. This suggests that even mild glucose intolerance not meeting GDM diagnostic criteria may affect fetal fat deposition, warranting further research into intervention measures. Lertvutivivat et al. observed significantly increased fetal AAWT at 28–30 and 32–34 weeks in GDM pregnancies [13]. Our study measured AAWT and other traditional fetal biometric parameters at 24–26, 30–32, and 36–38 weeks in GDM patients and compared the results with the non-GDM group. We found no significant differences between the two groups in standard biometric parameters (BPD, HC, FL, AC), whereas the mean AAWT was significantly higher in the GDM group across all three gestational periods. Consistent with our findings, Chang Zhe et al. studied 60 pregnant women at 22–30 weeks and found a significant difference in AAWT between GDM and non-GDM groups (P < 0.01) [25]. Another study in a Chinese population comparing AAWT in 40 GDM pregnancies and 40 healthy controls found that AAWT in the GDM group was 3.95 ± 0.67 mm, significantly different from 3.11 ± 0.29 mm in the control group (P < 0.05) [26]. That study also reported that among the 40 GDM cases, ultrasound diagnosed anterior abdominal wall malformations (omphalocele and gastroschisis) in 2 fetuses, which were confirmed postnatally. This demonstrates that ultrasound measurement of fetal AAWT can effectively assess fetal growth in GDM pregnancies.

In a study investigating fetal AAWT as a predictor of fetal growth, Petrikovsky et al. performed ultrasound measurements of AAWT in 133 term fetuses to predict macrosomia, finding that this indicator helped rule out macrosomia and LGA [27]. Gardeil et al. showed that measuring abdominal subcutaneous tissue thickness is a simple and effective method for predicting fetal growth restriction [28]. In that prospective cohort study, researchers performed serial ultrasound scans in the second and third trimesters on 137 women with singleton pregnancies. Both studies indicated that this measurement can predict fetal growth restriction and macrosomia/LGA in a general, unselected pregnant population. Our study differs in design and population, as we focused on differences between LGA and non-LGA groups within both GDM and non-GDM pregnancies. We observed that AAWT was significantly different at all three measurement time points across all pregnant women, while FTSTT and TM were only significantly different at 30–32 weeks. Langer et al. also found that fetal soft tissue thickness in GDM pregnancies had superior sensitivity for predicting LGA compared to EFW [29]. In another prospective clinical trial evaluating the clinical utility of early third-trimester routine fetal abdominal fat layer measurement in managing GDM pregnancies, Bethune et al. reported that a fetal AAWT ≥ 5 mm was the most effective indicator for predicting term macrosomia [30]. Higgins et al. enrolled 125 diabetic pregnant women and investigated the predictive value of third-trimester fetal AAWT for macrosomia [31]. They found that third-trimester AAWT was significantly higher in the macrosomic group and showed a regular increase with advancing gestation, consistent with our findings.

In our study, AAWT at 36–38 weeks demonstrated the best performance for predicting LGA across all pregnant women, with an AUC of 0.940, a cut-off value of 5.65 mm, and sensitivity and specificity of 93.3% and 83.6%, respectively. This result suggests that AAWT can serve as a stable indicator for predicting LGA across populations. The likely reason is that AAWT directly characterizes subcutaneous fat layer thickness, and its growth is continuously driven by anabolic hormones such as insulin, allowing it to reliably distinguish LGA from non-LGA fetuses from the mid-trimester onward.

In our exploratory analysis, AAWT at 30–32 weeks showed a strong unadjusted association with LGA (OR = 19.87), while AC at 36–38 weeks also demonstrated a notable effect (OR = 9.31). These observations suggest that different growth parameters at different gestational windows may provide distinct signals regarding fetal overgrowth, though these estimates should be interpreted with caution given the small number of LGA cases. Abnormal AAWT in the late mid-trimester may signal premature activation of the fetal fat accumulation program, whereas abnormal AC in late gestation more often reflects excessive growth of overall abdominal contents, including viscera and fat.

The final multivariate model, constructed after variable selection via LASSO regression, integrated AAWT at 30–32 weeks, AAWT at 36–38 weeks, and AC.

This model achieved an AUC of 0.966 on the test set, demonstrating superior predictive performance over the EFW model, as evidenced by a significant increase in the area under the curve (ΔAUC = 0.1089, P < 0.001), along with a NRI of 0.0758 and an IDI of 0.2468. These indices suggest that the addition of serial AAWT measurements and AC not only improves overall discrimination but also enhances reclassification of LGA cases, reflecting a clinically meaningful gain in risk prediction. Integrating abdominal soft-tissue markers with standard biometry may thus refine late-gestation screening strategies and aid in more personalized obstetric management.

A nomogram was constructed based on the exploratory model to visualize the relationship between the selected predictors and LGA probability. While such tools can facilitate individualized risk communication, it is important to emphasize that this nomogram is preliminary and has not undergone external validation. Its current utility is restricted to hypothesis generation; prospective studies in LGA-enriched populations are needed before any clinical application can be considered.

This study has several limitations. First and foremost, the primary grouping criterion of this study was GDM status rather than LGA status, resulting in only 30 LGA cases (12%) in the entire cohort. This limited number of events restricts the statistical power of the multivariable models and increases the risk of overfitting. Consequently, all nomogram-related findings should be regarded as exploratory, not as validated clinical prediction rules. Future studies should adopt an LGA-enriched case-control design to adequately power predictive modeling. Second, as a single-center study, although internal validation showed good performance, external validation across different populations, ultrasound devices, and operators is necessary to confirm its generalizability. Third, the standardization of AAWT measurement is crucial. Although we followed a strict protocol (e.g., fetus in a quiet state, measurement perpendicular to the abdominal skin, 2–3 cm lateral to the umbilical cord insertion site as per established methodology), the reproducibility of its measurement across different operators requires large-scale training and formal interobserver agreement studies to ensure consistency. Future research could explore AI-assisted automatic measurement to improve efficiency and consistency. Fourth, the fetal soft tissue thickness parameters were measured retrospectively from stored ultrasound images rather than being obtained prospectively during routine clinical examinations. Although all measurements were performed by a single experienced physician who was blinded to outcomes, the retrospective nature of this measurement protocol may introduce information bias, and the inter-observer reproducibility of these measurements could not be assessed. Future prospective studies with pre-specified soft tissue measurement protocols are needed to confirm our findings. Fifth, the nomogram incorporates ultrasound measurements obtained at 36–38 weeks of gestation, which is relatively late for implementing interventions. Future more studies should evaluate whether integrating serial AAWT measurements starting from the second trimester could enable earlier risk stratification and intervention.Sixth, detailed data on glycemic control in the GDM group (e.g., HbA1c levels, fasting and postprandial glucose trajectories, and the proportion of patients requiring pharmacotherapy) were not systematically collected. Consequently, the relationship between the degree of glycemic control and fetal soft tissue thickness could not be analyzed. Future studies should investigate whether improved glycemic control can mitigate the increased AAWT observed in GDM pregnancies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that fetal AAWT is significantly greater in GDM pregnancies than in non-GDM pregnancies and correlates strongly with LGA. A preliminary exploratory model combining AAWT and AC showed potential for identifying LGA, but these findings are constrained by the small number of LGA cases and the lack of external validation. Future large-scale, LGA-focused studies are warranted before clinical implementation of this nomogram can be recommended.
