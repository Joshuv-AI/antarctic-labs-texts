Using the socioecological model to guide service delivery improvements to the prison needle exchange program in Canada: insights from multi-level stakeholders
Authors: Nadine Kronfli, Lise Lafferty, Frederic Leone, Mark Stoové, Behzad Hajarizadeh, Andrew R Lloyd, Frederick L Altice
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn 2018–2019, Canada introduced a Prison Needle Exchange Program (PNEP) across nine federal facilities to mitigate the harms associated with drug injection among incarcerated people. However, program uptake has been limited. We explored the barriers and facilitators to improving PNEP services among key stakeholders in prison.MethodsStakeholders in nine federal prisons with active PNEP participated in focus groups using nominal group technique to achieve rapid consensus. Responses were generated, rank-ordered, and prioritized by each stakeholder group (correctional officers, healthcare workers, and people in prison). We identified the highest-ranking responses to questions about barriers and solutions to PNEP uptake and described them using the five levels of the Socioecological Model: individual, interpersonal, organizational, system, and structural/policy.ResultsBetween September 2023 and February 2024, 34 focus groups were conducted with 215 participants (n = 51 correctional officers (24%); n = 67 healthcare workers (31%); n = 97 people in prison (45%)). Key barriers identified were lack of confidentiality and privacy across all levels and fear of repercussions from drug use and fear of being targeted at the individual-interpersonal levels. Preferred solutions included comprehensive education across all levels, and establishment of supervised/safe injection sites and external program management, potentially involving peers, at the structural level.ConclusionsSeveral multi-level modifiable barriers to improving PNEP uptake in Canadian federal prisons were shared among key stakeholders. Structural changes to PNEP delivery, including supervised/safe injecting sites and peer-led programs, were proposed as solution-driven enablers to increasing PNEP uptake among incarcerated people who inject drugs. These data will inform Canadian efforts to expand PNEP provision.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24935-y.Keywords: Prison, Needle exchange program, Socioecological model, Barrier, Solution, Implementation science

Introduction

Prison-based needle and syringe programs are recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime as an essential element of an effective HIV/hepatitis C virus (HCV) prevention strategy for people who inject drugs (PWID) in prisons [1, 2]. The potential health benefits from the provision of prison-based needle and syringe programs include decreased needle and syringe sharing and reutilisation among PWID, increased health-seeking behaviours (including increased blood borne virus testing and treatment and referrals to drug treatment programs), and decreased overdose-related deaths [1–3]. In 2024, however, only eleven countries offered these programs globally, including Canada [4]. The number of countries providing prison-based needle and syringe programs has changed little over 20 years and, in some settings, they have been discontinued, primarily due to the failure to protect participant confidentiality [5]. Both modelling [6, 7] and real-world [8] studies confirm that HCV treatment scale-up is insufficient for the elimination of HCV in prisons, underscoring the importance of high coverage prison-based harm reduction services.

In 2018–2019, Correctional Service Canada (CSC) initiated a Prison Needle Exchange Program (PNEP) in nine of its 43 federal prisons, where individuals housed are sentenced for at least two years [9–12]. In Canada, an estimated 30% of women and 21% of men in federal prison report ever injecting drugs [13], and most recent data from CSC show that 32% and 21% of women and men in federal custody, respectively, have been exposed to HCV [14]. With this in mind, the Government of Canada’s Sexually Transmitted and Blood-borne Infections 2024–2030 Action Plan outlined a goal of expanding the PNEP to all remaining federal prisons by 2030 [15]. Recent modelling suggests that this expansion could substantially improve the cost-effectiveness of the PNEP if high service uptake was achieved [16].

At the end of 2024, the PNEP was expanded to three additional federal prisons [17]. However, while these programs should be accessed by all PWID in prison, less than 10% are currently utilising these services [18]. Furthermore, from June 2018–2022, 23% of PNEP applications were declined due to safety concerns [19]. These data highlight important barriers to the provision of harm reduction services in Canadian federal prisons, compromising blood-borne virus elimination efforts set by the WHO for 2030 [20, 21] and undermining the effectiveness and investment in these programs.

Several studies have now been undertaken across Canada to explore barriers to improving PNEP uptake among correctional officers and people with lived or living experience of incarceration [22–26]. Confidentiality breaches, fear of being targeted by correctional officers, and long and complex application processes were consistently reported barriers to PNEP enrolment. However, much of this research was conducted with previously incarcerated people, or with correctional officers working in prisons without the PNEP. More importantly, few studies have explored solutions to PNEP uptake [27, 28].

Despite the mounting evidence that service delivery improvements are needed to ensure the sustainability of Canada’s PNEP, a synthesis of the barriers and solutions across all key stakeholders and across all sites providing PNEP is lacking. A more thorough understanding of both the barriers and solutions identified by all key stakeholders is needed to comprehend the implementation landscape and guide improvements to service delivery. Understanding the perspectives of stakeholders who deliver the PNEP (healthcare workers), ensure security in custodial settings (correctional officers), or participate in the PNEP (PWID in prison) is essential to improving overall reach and coverage. The aim of this study was to explore and synthesize the perceived barriers and solutions to improving uptake of the PNEP by PWID in prison among multi-level stakeholders.

Methods

Setting

In September 2023, nine of 43 federal prisons were implementing the PNEP, all of which served as the study sites. These represent the nine prisons where the PNEP was originally introduced in 2018–2019. Three of these nine had not yet enrolled a PNEP participant by December 31, 2023. From west to east, these included Fraser Valley Institution for Women and Mission Medium Institution in British Columbia, Edmonton Institution for Women in Alberta, Grand Valley Institution for Women and Joyceville Institution in Ontario, Joliette Institution for Women in Quebec, Atlantic Institution and Dorchester Medium Institution in New Brunswick, and Nova Institution for Women in Nova Scotia.

Prison healthcare workers deliver the PNEP in Canadian federal prisons. As a first step to enrolment, a nurse submits a “Threat and Risk Assessment” (TRA) to the Assistant Warden of Operations following an initial needs assessment with the potential participant [17]. The TRA seeks to juxtapose risks to oneself or others with the benefits of participation [29]. The TRA is first reviewed by the Assistant Warden of Operations, with input from the Security Intelligence Officer, followed by a final decision by the Warden. This decision is expected within 10 business days of the initial submission. Approved participants are then given one PNEP kit, consisting of one needle/syringe and other sterile supplies, by a nurse and are asked to sign a contract. During PNEP enrolment, used needles/syringes are exchanged 1:1 at Health Services during operating hours only. Correctional officers perform daily “visual inspections” of the kits during morning and afternoon head counts. According to CSC, PNEP participation should not affect parole nor should it result in “increased searches, observation, or disciplinary action” (Correctional Service Canada, “Information for Inmates: Prison Needle Exchange Program”). PNEP participation can be terminated by prison leadership for those who are not able to show their kit during the “visual inspections” or who are observed either injecting drugs or sharing PNEP kit equipment.

Study design

Nominal group technique was chosen to identify the barriers and solutions to improving PNEP uptake and ranking their importance [30]. This mixed-methods strategy generates a rank-ordering (quantitative) combined with a qualitative approach to weigh priorities [31, 32]. Nominal group technique ensures equal participation despite group power imbalances and involves four steps, culminating in independent voting [33]. The ideal size for nominal group technique focus groups is 8–10 participants [33].

Participants

Focus groups were conducted with four key stakeholder groups at each prison: correctional officers, healthcare workers, peer advocates, and people who are (or have been) enrolled in the PNEP or/and receiving opioid agonist therapy (OAT). We use the term ‘peer advocates’ to describe people who are incarcerated, irrespective of drug use history, who have an influential and trusted status among people in prison, as demonstrated through their involvement/leadership in designated committees within prison.

Participant recruitment

The recruitment of correctional officers and healthcare workers relied on prison leadership [27], while the recruitment of peer advocates and PNEP/OAT participants depended primarily on healthcare workers [28]. The Assistant Warden of Operations sought correctional officer participation through email inquiry while the Chiefs of Health Services solicited participation from their healthcare workers [27]. Members of the healthcare teams at each prison supported recruitment by approaching PNEP participants (current or previous) and people currently receiving OAT, as well as members of their prison’s peer-led committees (e.g., “Inmate Committee”), providing information about the study, and soliciting preliminary interest/participation [28].

Participants were provided with details of the study by a member of the research team. Before participation, written and witnessed consent was required from all participants. The focus groups were conducted in either English or French. Correctional officers or prison healthcare workers were not present during focus group discussions with people in prison. Participants were not financially compensated [34]. As per Commissioner’s directive 009 [35], people in federal prison cannot be compensated for participation in research. Correctional employees (officers and healthcare workers) were not compensated as focus groups took place during working hours. Where permitted, refreshments were provided during participation.

Study procedures

During each focus group, two questions, designed to identify barriers and solutions to improving PNEP uptake for people who inject drugs in prison, were asked:

Question 1 (Q1): “What do you think is getting in the way of people at this prison from enrolling in the prison needle exchange program?”

Question 1 (Q1): “What do you think is getting in the way of people at this prison from enrolling in the prison needle exchange program?”

Question 2 (Q2): “Based on the top responses (to Q1), what should or could be done to get more people to use the prison needle exchange program at this prison?”

Question 2 (Q2): “Based on the top responses (to Q1), what should or could be done to get more people to use the prison needle exchange program at this prison?”

Three research team interviewers (I; NK, FLA or FL) conducted the focus groups at each prison. After each question, participants were first given 3–5 min to silently generate their responses. Thereafter, each participant was asked to provide one response until all responses were saturated. Participants then had the opportunity to clarify and combine responses where there was overlap. During the final step, each participant was given three votes in any combination for weighting responses. Votes were then counted, and responses were ranked by the interviewer in front of all participants, allowing for one last discussion for endorsement of the highest-ranking responses. After identifying the top-ranked barriers in Q1, the process was repeated for Q2. An identical process was repeated for all stakeholder groups at each prison.

To contextualize and rank-order the responses, all focus groups were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. The transcriber simultaneously translated the focus groups conducted in French (n = 4). Transcripts were proofed for accuracy and de-identified.

Guiding framework: the socioecological model

We adapted the Socioecological Model (SEM) of McLeroy et al. [36] to understand perceived barriers and solutions to PNEP uptake by PWID in prison. Socioecological systems theory demonstrates the nested and interdependent nature of human development and interactions at the personal, relational, and collective levels. As such, the SEM was developed recognizing that individuals affect and are affected by a complex range of social influences and environmental interactions. The SEM involves five levels of influence: individual, interpersonal, organizational, community, and policy [37]. For this research, we adapted these levels of influence to reflect both the PNEP and the existing hierarchy in carceral settings: individual, interpersonal (i.e., relationships with peers, healthcare workers, and correctional officers), organizational (related to PNEP), system (i.e., prison leadership), and structural/policy. Each level represents a distinct sphere of influence, although they are all interconnected and reflect how changes or interventions at one level can impact the others. This comprehensive framework helps in understanding and addressing the multifaceted determinants (i.e., barriers and solutions) of PNEP participation, facilitating targeted and effective changes across various contexts.

Analysis

A full description of the analysis can be found elsewhere [27, 28]. In this study, we added all votes for each barrier and each solution for each stakeholder group across all nine prisons and ranked them in descending order, with respect to the total number of votes across stakeholder groups. Barriers and solutions were categorized according to their respective SEM levels, and the analysis focused on understanding how these barriers and solutions interacted to influence outcomes. We present findings of only the highest-ranking responses (i.e., those that received at least 5% of the overall votes per question when combined across stakeholder groups). Given the similarities in responses among the peer advocates and PNEP/OAT participants, we combined their responses into ‘People in prison’.

After this study was approved by the McGill University Health Centre Research Ethics Board (#2023–9201), CSC reviewed the approval and sought no further ethical oversight.

Results

Participants

From September 23, 2023, to February 1, 2024, 34 focus groups were conducted across nine prisons with a total of 215 participants (51 correctional officers, 67 healthcare workers, and 97 people in prison, representing 24%, 31%, and 45% of participants, respectively). Across the nine prisons, two groups of correctional officers declined participation. Focus group sizes ranged from 4–15 for correctional officers, 4–11 for healthcare workers, and 2–10 for people in prison. No demographic information was collected on participants to minimize the risk for re-identification. Only one participant (a healthcare worker) declined participation during the consent process.

Highest ranking barriers to PNEP uptake among all stakeholders

Individual to interpersonal

Most barriers were experienced at the individual level but were primarily the result of interactions at the interpersonal level, i.e., with correctional officers, peers, and healthcare workers (Fig. 1). These included fear of repercussions from drug use, fear of being targeted, bullying from peers to share equipment/drugs, stigma/judgement for participation, and lack of awareness/knowledge. Each of these barriers are organized below by the primary stakeholder group enacting the behaviour.

A. Correctional officers

Fear of repercussions due to drug use

Fear of repercussions due to drug use was the second-highest ranking barrier to PNEP uptake overall, the top barrier among people in prison (20.3% of votes), and similarly, received ≥ 10% of votes among correctional officers. This barrier received 3–10 votes in each prison among people in prison and did not receive any votes among correctional officers in three of the seven participating prisons (Supplementary Table 1). Implicit in how people in prison prioritized this finding more than other stakeholders is that they have lived experience of being sanctioned for their behaviours in prison. Correctional officers acknowledged the potential consequences of PNEP participation for people in prison, including that participation could ‘be used against them’ during their incarceration, and in particular, by correctional employees in their correctional plans towards release. PNEP participation alone could thus negatively impact relationships between correctional officers and people in prison, with consequences felt at the individual level.

P1: Yeah. So, I guess they don’t want to like deal with the consequences because it might have a negative impact on their correctional plan.

P2: […] I don’t believe that the inmates feel that the program… I don’t feel like they don’t believe that there won’t be any consequences for being on the program, that it won’t be used against them at one point or another. (Correctional officers, Prison 9)

Healthcare workers went so far as to say that repercussions from failed drug screens could negatively impact the quality of health care services received, a consequence of PNEP participation. Similarly, PNEP participation could jeopardize established and healthy relationships between healthcare providers and people in prison, with dramatic repercussions felt at the individual level.

P1: Fear of punitive measures from other health services members. So, for example, if someone has a urine drug screen that has a positive result, the psychiatrist may discontinue medications or restrict medications for the person.

P2: He has access to the chart which kind of pisses me off. (Healthcare workers, Prison 1)

People in prison often reported their participation in the PNEP could result in ‘punishment’ by various correctional employees, contributing to increased and unnecessary stress and leading to poorer mental health. People in prison also reported that participation in the PNEP and in other harm reduction programs were rarely viewed positively. In fact, the overwhelming belief among people in prison was that PNEP participation could negatively influence parole and/or release.

P: Inmates fear being punished by their CMT, so their Case Management Team, that includes your CX [correctional officer] too, your parole officer, the MAI, what not.

I: “MAI” is…?

P: The MAI is the Manager of Assessment in Interventions, or something like that. They go with the parole officers. It’s a strange title. It’s basically the parole officer’s boss. But, I mean, you know, like I’ll give you an example. My PO [parole officer] asked me, a while back, she’s like, “Oh, how long have you been on suboxone for?” I’m like … so, I didn’t answer right away. How the hell did she find out? Like, I never told her. And I’m like, “Why are you asking me that?” Or maybe I asked her, “How do you know that I’m on it? I never told you that I’m on it.” Or something like that. And she’s like, “Well, aren’t you?” And I’m like, “Fuck!” Now, I’m in this spot where I don’t want to lie to her, but I didn’t want her to know either, so I’m like, “Well, this is kind of private. It’s a private issue. If I was on it or if I wasn’t on it, I wouldn’t really want you to know either way. It’s a personal issue and that’s something that I would choose to keep to myself.” So, I kind of left it at that, but now, it’s on my mind, you know, “Is she gonna use that against me? What is she thinking about me?” It’s like, “All the progress I’ve made, does that not count for anything now?” And that’s where … it goes back to—how he said—mental health. It does affect your mental health because it’s added stress, it’s added … you know … stress is just immense, man, like, people don’t understand that. (People in prison, Prison 9)

Fear of being targeted

Fear of being targeted was the third highest ranking barrier to PNEP uptake overall, received 14.4% of votes among all stakeholder groups, and was ranked the second most important barrier to PNEP uptake among correctional officers and healthcare workers. Although it was a highly ranked barrier, it did not receive at least one vote among correctional officers and healthcare workers in several prisons (Supplementary Table 1). However, it received at least one vote among incarcerated individuals in each prison. The fear of being targeted at the individual level (i.e., by people in prison) for accessing the PNEP was driven largely by interpersonal interactions with correctional officers. All three stakeholder groups described PNEP participation as creating a “target,” often attracting increased searches and targeted urinalyses by correctional officers, to which correctional officers acknowledged contributing. While the searches and urinalyses are meant to be random, healthcare workers and people in prison reported that this was unlikely the case.

P1: Becoming a target for CSC.

I: What kind of target?

P1: Basically, searches, strip searches, cells being torn apart, everything is … you’re basically being looked from under the microscope. It doesn’t really matter where you are, whether it’s here, or elsewhere, it’s the same, because the moment CSC … like, it has to be … that CSC is a part of the process [I: Yeah]. In order to get the needle, you have to tell CSC staff that, first of all, you need one. Then, it has to go through the process. Then, you have to tell them where it’s in your cell and so on.

P2: Once they know that you want a needle, they know you have drugs or something along that line, tattoo equipment, and that’s all contraband to them. So, you’ll be more targeted for searches, targeted for everything. [P1: Anything] They’re gonna now watch you like a hawk because they think you have something. (People in prison, Prison 2)

P1: Fear that Operations will do more searches on them.

I: And by Operations, ok. ‘Fear of … Is it a fear or does it actually happen?

P1: It actually happens …

P2: They can’t be biased, off the record.

P1: Or obviously …

P2: And we don’t really know though because we’re not Operations. So, we don’t know how often they search individuals based on whether or not they’re on the PNEP program or not, right?

P1: As per the program’s guidelines, they’re not supposed to, but…

P2: They say that they don’t, but like …

P3: As per the Guidelines, they shouldn’t be searching more or targeting the PNEP users.

P2: But like when your random drug searches happen, is it really random? Is it? (Healthcare workers, Prison 9)

Stigma/judgement for participation

Stigma, sometimes termed judgement, received ≥ 10% of votes among people in prison, but received significantly less votes among correctional officers and healthcare workers. While over half of the votes received among people in prison were from Prison 4, three prisons did not cast any votes for this as a barrier to PNEP uptake. Correctional authorities were widely regarded as the ones enacting stigma towards people known to be injecting drugs, with PNEP participation being indicative of drug use. Participation was thus regarded as drawing negative attention rather than viewed as indicative of making healthier decisions. Stigma thus had a negative impact on individual-level participation, driven largely by the judgment enacted by correctional officers.

Stigmatization of being involved with the program and being a drug user. [I: Yeah] I know recently somebody that was caught with a needle on his desk. He was alone in his cell. He was getting prepared to do his drugs and staff came … or he just finished doing his drugs, and staff came in because they saw the needle on his desk, but he’s involved with the program. He is doing what he thought he was doing safely. [I: Right] He wasn’t influencing anybody else. He was satisfying his own needs alone and, all of a sudden, because the staff saw the needle on the desk, they decided that that was reason enough to turn his cell inside out and upside down, give him a charge or two, and basically, you know, make his life miserable for the coming weeks and months. (People in prison, Prison 9)

B. Peers

Bullying to share equipment/drugs

Bullying from peers to share drug injection equipment or drugs was the top-ranked barrier to PNEP uptake among correctional officers, receiving ~ 22% of total votes among this stakeholder group. It received at least two votes among correctional officers in all participating prisons except for Prison 4. Importantly, it received substantially fewer votes (2.7%) among people in prison. Individuals perceived to be enacting this barrier (i.e., peers) ranked it as an unlikely barrier to PNEP participation while correctional officers distal to the interpersonal relationship between peers and PNEP participants ranked it as their top barrier. While peers have privileged lived experience insights into this as a potential barrier, correctional officers perceived this through a highly subjective lens rooted in negative judgment of peers. Correctional officers perceived participation in the program would put participants at risk of bullying or “muscling” from peers because being the person who had a kit may also be perceived as being the person with access to drugs and clean needles.

Because some people will muscle them or make them … You know, right. “Because I know you have a needle and you’re not supposed to lend it to me, but I still want to use it, right. So, I’m going to do something to make you do that”. But some people, rather they will just take the needle … the inmates, right, they will just take it. (Correctional officer, Prison 9)

Healthcare workers reported that ‘fake’ applications submitted by individuals who were more likely to be approved but were unlikely to participate often resulted from individuals being bullied or muscled by others who were more likely to fail the application process. Healthcare workers perceived that bullying from peers regarding PNEP enrolment thus began prior to possession of a PNEP kit.

P1: Because we’ve had people do fake applications, get people to do applications when they don’t even use drugs, just so they can use their kit and it’s not in their name.

P2: It’s someone else’s.

P1: Right.

P2: If they aren’t approved, if they can’t get approved themselves, they’ll put it through somebody else. (Healthcare workers, Prison 4).

C. Healthcare workers

Lack of awareness

A lack of PNEP awareness among people in prison received 8.5% of votes among healthcare workers, ranking among the top five barriers among this stakeholder group. Healthcare workers reported that they failed to receive any formal education or training on PNEP, and many only came to learn of the program’s existence through their interactions with incarcerated individuals. This lack of knowledge (among healthcare workers) then translated into a lack of awareness of the program’s existence among people in prison.

P1: I’d be surprised if more than three people know about it.

I: Interesting.

P1: Right? So, but everybody in this institution should know about it. It should be everybody’s business. It’s just like CPR. It should be like CPR, basically. Right? But it’s not.

P2: I can’t say much about it. I’ve seen people walk with boxes in the hall. But I haven’t had a formal education about it. I know about it. Because the institution that I worked in before… That was before but not here.

P3: I think he made a really good point. No offense but I didn’t even know we had bleach here. I worked here two and a half years before I even knew they worked with bleach.

P4: I was a staff, I don’t know. Like I didn’t know it was there.

P5: Well, the inmates told me. (Healthcare workers, Prison 7)

People in prison reported that information regarding the PNEP was available to some of them at the time of program inception within their respective prison, but not regularly thereafter despite new admissions. Those who missed out on this opportunity were then only exposed to PNEP through ‘hearsay’ or the occasional poster. Healthcare workers noted similar observations.

P: So, when the program was first implemented, there was like big education sessions and stuff for the inmates about the programs. And there were like pamphlets handed over and stuff. So, most of the inmates know about the program, but we have like our changing population so we’re getting new inmates from other institutions who doesn’t have the program.

I: And that info session that you’re talking about happened once?

P1: Yes, at the beginning. And then never since then. (Healthcare workers, Prison 9)

P: And there’s no knowledge about it anywhere. Like, people just hear from it from people having it [PNEP kits], like, “Oh, how do you get on that?” There’s not anything around, where you could see, “Oh, okay, look, I could sign up for this. It’s a healthy way to get something in here, you know?” And there’s no knowledge about it. It’s just hearsay. (People in prison, Prison 7)

P1: Some people don’t know how to get on it. Because it’s not advertised. There’s one poster and that’s inside healthcare and the only way you get to read that poster is if you go inside to healthcare.

I: How did you guys find out about the program?

P2: I heard it as a rumour when I was in provincial.

P3: I seen it when I first got here. I’d seen a poster inside healthcare. (People in prison, Prison 6).

Individual to system

Lack of confidentiality/privacy

A lack of confidentiality and privacy vis-à-vis PNEP participation was the highest ranking barrier to PNEP uptake overall, receiving 15% of all votes across all stakeholder groups and spanned all SEM levels from individual to system. Confidentiality concerns vis-à-vis program participation were among the top barriers cited for both healthcare workers and people in prison, having received at least one vote in each participating prison. A lack of confidentiality was the highest ranking barrier for healthcare workers, having received 15.9% of their votes and the second highest ranking barrier for people in prison, having received 17.5% of their votes (Table 1). Correctional officers are made aware of who is enrolled in the program for their daily visual inspections by prison leadership and healthcare workers, which then results in other peers being made aware, some of whom were never cognizant. All levels along the SEM contribute to confidentiality concerns, which then impact individuals’ decision to apply for the PNEP or continue to participate.

Well, when you have a kit, the guards are in your room or they’re at your door at 12 o’clock count, 10 o’clock count, and they’re like, “We want to see what’s in your kit,” while girls are standing at their door, looking out, wondering, like, “Hey, what the hell is going on there?” And they’re standing right in the hallway, with your kit, looking at your stuff. [I: Right] And then, there’s a list in the … it was supposed to be… I thought it was confidential for us and the nurses, but there’s a list in the guards’ staff room of women on the PNEP program. (People in prison, Prison 7)

Confidentiality concerns ranked as the fourth highest barrier among correctional officers, having received 8.8% of their total votes. Many correctional officers acknowledged that the numerous individuals involved in the application process hindered participants’ confidentiality, thereby affecting interpersonal relationships and leading to a decreased appetite for PNEP participation among people in prison.

So, it starts in Healthcare. They will send the name to someone in Operations. They’ll send the paperwork to Operations. The paperwork goes back to the SIO, Security Intelligence Officer. They will review it. So, they know, you might be a drug user in the past or present. And then, it has hierarchies, if you’re an active user or if you’re involved in the drug subculture. So, then, they’ll put their recommendation on in. It comes back to me. So, I have to make an informed decision. Are we gonna approve this or not? Depending on how deep you’re in the program. Then, I’ll say “yay” or “nay.” I send it back to Healthcare. It’s supposed to be confidential, but it’s not because it goes out to other staff. So, then, the email will come out, “This person has a kit.” Now, I know you have a kit. (Correctional officer, Prison 1)

Long and complex application process

The application process was ranked as an important barrier only among healthcare workers, having received ≥ 10% of votes and ranking third overall among this stakeholder group. While the program is theoretically available to anyone who injects drugs in prison, participants may be denied access to the program by prison leadership, who control the fate of interested participants.

So, my understanding is that anyone who applies for the program has to have a Threat and Risk Assessment conducted by security and they can deny them access to the kit. So, whether or not … Like the patient may not qualify for it because their threat is of having possession of a needle or that it could be used as a weapon [I.: Yeah], so they may not quality for it. (Healthcare worker, Prison 1)

The time to approval was also reported as an important barrier to enrolment. Several healthcare workers and participants reported prolonged delays in the TRA approval process, hindering many from even applying.

I’ve had them say it to me before—they don’t apply because it takes … like, the actual length of time to be approved. (Healthcare worker, Prison 4)

I: And so, you’ve been waiting for how long now?

P1: Three months. Yeah. I applied when I first got here and it took me about a month to see somebody, and that was, like, two months ago.

I: And how long did it take for you to get approved?

P2: Probably about the same time. They call you up, they interview you, they get you to sign something, then you wait again just to get called up again, like a month later, to see if you got approved or if you got denied. (People in prison, Prison 4)

Structural/policy

There were no barriers at this level that received ≥ 5% of total votes.

Individual to system

Education

The provision of comprehensive education was the top-ranked solution overall among all stakeholder groups and spanned all SEM levels from individual to system (i.e., prison leadership). Education received the highest proportion of votes among healthcare workers (28.7%) and was ranked as the top solution for this stakeholder group, having received at least one vote in all prisons but one (Supplementary Table 2). Education was also highly ranked among people in prison, receiving the second highest proportion of votes among this stakeholder group, and at least one vote in all prisons but one. Similarly, correctional officers recognized the importance of education, accounting for ≥ 10% of votes, and at least one vote in all but two participating prisons. While correctional officers were identified by all stakeholder groups (including their own) as requiring targeted education in harm reduction and addiction to promote program buy-in, all prison stakeholders, including peers, healthcare personnel, and prison leadership were also felt to benefit from an improved understanding of the purpose of the PNEP, including its role in facilitating community re-integration. This, in turn, would foster program promotion and increased access to drug treatment and other health care services for people who inject drugs in prison.

The way the program is presented, and I’ll speak for the institution as a whole, it’s viewed as though it’s just a harm reduction strategy with the specific intent to prevent communicable diseases. There’s not a lot of understanding as to the synergy between PNEP and then, what’s to happen after. I think if the inmates knew and the staff knew that the PNEP was sort of the gateway into more effective treatment, it would help to a) reduce stigma, and b) it would also increase demand regardless of stigma, if they saw the intent of it was to actually help them with their reintegration, versus the stigma of, “Oh, my PO [parole officer] knows, my officer knows, now I’ve got to go up against the board and convince them I am not a drug addict.” (Correctional officer, Prison 1)

Yeah, for me knowledge is a big one. Once there’s knowledge, then it sort of clarifies expectations for what I think … I got expectations. Knowledge increases expectations because you actually … It’s the person that works here that will know what they need to do, what role they need to play. Because sometimes it’s because people don’t know what role they play. It sort of like confuses things. And if they don’t know anything like the harm reduction, the benefits of harm reduction … (Healthcare worker, Prison 7)

Program participants felt that an opportune time for the provision of education to people in prison was following admission to prison. This systematic approach could serve as an opportunity to provide ‘information sessions’ dedicated to evidence-based interventions to reduce the harms from within-prison drug use.

For newcomers into the institution, there should be info sessions on the program and part of the info session, there should be to let them know … like, give them the knowledge that they’re doing it for a good cause in not spreading diseases. Because if they do info sessions on people that want to do the program, they still know, like … I don’t know, maybe … that they know that there’s something that they could use, instead of, like … or letting them know that there is a safer way to use instead of sharing. (People in prison, Prison 6)

Organizational

There were several proposed solutions at the organizational level (i.e., related to the PNEP). In addition to integrating education into program implementation, stakeholders advocated for improved confidentiality, concurrent wrap-around services for PNEP participants, and automatic approval into the PNEP. These are each discussed separately.

Improved confidentiality

Strategies to increase confidentiality received the third highest number of votes among people in prison, having received at least one vote in all but two prisons (Supplementary Table 2). It received < 10% of votes among correctional officers and healthcare workers, having received votes in only two and three prisons, respectively. It is important to note that while increasing confidentiality was a top ranked solution among people in prison, solutions to increase confidentiality were suggested in other ways which underpinned many of the proposed solutions (e.g., supervised/safe injection sites, external program delivery by peers). Here we focus on suggestions to address confidentiality concerns, ranging from small changes (e.g., changes to the location where the kit is stored) to larger programmatic solutions, such as providing increased supports to correctional employees through destigmatization training.

P: Having like a lock-up that inmates have access to, like, freely, so if they want to participate, like, they don’t have to have the kit in their units where they’re being, like, intruded upon to show the kit. So, also providing a safe lock-up for their kits.

I: So, it doesn’t have to be in the room?

P: Yeah, if they want. I mean, I guess they can have it if they don’t mind, but I mean, I’ve seen staff like basically kick at doors to wake somebody up just to see their kit and I’ve seen them hold up counts just to see a kit, and it shames them, it dehumanizes them. (People in prison, Prison 1)

Increasing supports similar to, I guess, what I said before. Because I think it would assess the de-stigmatization of it because I feel that people are more likely to be empathetic to say people that realize and acknowledge like, “I have a problem”. And they are looking for ways to participate in harm reduction initiatives or get proper healthcare education. (People in prison, Prison 8)

Wrap-around services

Given previously articulated fears that the PNEP fails to promote recovery and rehabilitation, correctional officers identified wrap-around services as necessary and complementary to program implementation. While this solution received ≥ 10% of votes only among correctional officers, Prison 9 accounted for the majority (19/24; 79%) of votes, with only two additional prisons voting for this as a solution. Wraparound services were endorsed by few people in prison and even fewer healthcare workers but were a particularly popular solution among these stakeholders in Prisons 1 and 8. A PNEP model with enhanced services, including counselling and/or rehabilitation, would provide more holistic care and could potentially become more acceptable to correctional officers, who in turn, would begin promoting the program recognizing its potential to provide additional support beyond ‘facilitating drug use.’ Others viewed wrap-around services as a critical component of any drug use-focused program, with provision of enhanced services as ultimately supporting people in prison to reintegrate into society.

So, since we now as a society have come to view addiction as a mental health issue, it kind of calls attention to the lack of follow-up, in terms of like additional resources provided to the inmates on this program. There aren’t any, like in terms of like additional counselling, things like that. (Correctional officer, Prison 9)

Automatic enrolment

Reducing the multiple barriers of the application process was a top-ranked solution only among healthcare workers, having received at least one vote in five prisons, and ranking third overall among this stakeholder group. This solution was not suggested by any correctional officer stakeholder group in any prison and was voted upon by people incarcerated in only three prisons. People incarcerated in Prison 7 were responsible for almost half of the overall votes among all people in prison. Recommendations for revising the application process focused primarily on removal of the application and providing automatic approval either at the time of request or for everyone on admission. Several healthcare workers also pointed to the program’s inherent requirement for PNEP participants to re-apply if they had previously ‘given back their kits’ or were cascading down to lower security prisons, an area that could be subject to process improvement.

P1: It’s bullshit. I’m asking for a needle. Give me a needle.

P2: So, reduce the wait time, and we don’t have to say “10 days,” we don’t have to say “1 day,” we don’t have to say “4 days,” just quick!

P3: It has to go through so many people before you get it and then, back down.

P4: And then, you have to wait for the Warden’s signature, which is ridiculous. (People in prison, Prison 1)

We have a client that’s joined the program three times. Every time she’s joined, she still doesn’t seem to understand the process. Even though we explained it to her at length. And every time she gets less and less patient. Having to wait … Because there’s a certain amount of days from the time I get it. I have 72 h to review it, process it, hand it off to the Warden of operations who then reviews and decides. From there, they send it up to the Warden to approve. Once the Warden approves and do their checks, which is up to 14 working days, then it comes back to us, and we can issue it. And this person’s already chopping at the bit. “You got it. Did you send it up?” Then they come back and check, and check and check. And it’s gotten more intense … and now this is her third time coming on and off the program. (Healthcare worker, Prison 1)

Structural/policy

Several top-ranked solutions provided by the stakeholder groups were primarily focused on making large structural changes to the program itself. The proposed solutions entailed replacing the current service delivery model with supervised/safe injection sites or peers. These are each discussed separately.

Supervised/safe injection sites

Supervised injection sites were the second-highest ranking solution among all stakeholder groups and were the top ranked solution among correctional officers. It failed to receive any votes, however, in two of the participating prisons (Supplementary Table 2). Although safe injection sites accounted for ≥ 10% of votes among healthcare workers and people in prison, they ranked only fourth among both stakeholder groups as a solution to the current program (Table 2). From correctional officers’ perspectives, supervised/safe injection sites were preferable to the current program because they could decrease the number of circulating needles (thereby potentially decreasing bullying from peers and the risk for needlestick injuries to everyone in prison), increase participant confidentiality, and alleviate the pressure for correctional officers to enforce CSC’s rules around injection drug use in prison (thereby reducing their motivation to perform targeted searches among PNEP participants).

P1: The thing is, it’s like as peace officers, everyone in this room has an obligation to do their job when it comes to drug use. So, the thing is, it’s like, it’s against the law to do drugs. So, by going to the safe injection portion of it, a) it’s safer for staff because the needles are in a controlled area; b) security staff are not involved with it. Then it relieves us of our duties as peace officers to complete our jobs.

P2: Right. Because, and it gives them their anonymity and it also means that because that is actually a structured part of our job, you are supposed to do a certain … “Here is your job description as a peace officer. This is what you’re supposed to do.” Inmate does it. You’re supposed to do something to stop it. You have to do something, even if it’s just paperwork. So, the concept is the safe injection site does, it eliminates the safety portion of it for staff members. And then it also, kind of like, gets rid of that obligation as a peace officer to enforce the law. (Correctional officers, Prison 9)

In addition, people in prison recognized that safe injection sites could offer increased safety (i.e., fewer overdose deaths) to people who inject drugs in prison but were only part of the solution as they were unlikely to reduce the stigma they experienced from correctional officers.

P1: See, last week, they opened the safe injection site at Collins Bay, which is essentially an extension of this [the PNEP], but there’s medical supervision while you inject. You just bring your dope in, they give you a clean needle, you shoot up.

P2: And they take your needle …

P1: Twenty minutes later to make sure that you’re not gonna die, and you get to go. (People in prison, Prison 2)

P1: […] Make safe injection sites, but I don’t think that would get rid of the stigma from staff.

P2: Well, it could, because then, people wouldn’t see. (People in prison, Prison 4)

External program delivery

Many individual-level barriers to participation were the result of correctional officers’ behaviours (e.g., increased searches, targeting, stigma/judgment) towards PNEP participants. It was thus proposed that removal of correctional employees from program implementation would ease some of the barriers, a solution that was the highest ranking among people in prison, surpassing ‘supervised/safe injection sites’ by only two votes, and receiving 22.7% of the overall votes in this stakeholder group and at least one vote in every prison but one (Supplementary Table 2). This was an equally favoured solution among healthcare workers, ranking two overall among this stakeholder group. This solution was not suggested by any correctional officer stakeholder group in any prison. While many healthcare workers and people in prison suggested task-shifting delivery of the program to others, including peers, some recognized that the ideal program should be fully independent of all correctional employees, including healthcare workers, if the ultimate goal was a low-threshold program.

P: I went to an international conference a couple of years back and some countries have inmates in a position of trust who have the supplies. So, for example, you know, I work with Jane Doe and I give Jane 20 syringes, and she needs to return 20 syringes.

I: Peer programs?

P: Yeah, pretty much. (Healthcare worker, Prison 1)

P1: In another prison I’ve been to, they created a job for somebody to be a representative of us. He has knowledge about health care stuff and that’s his job. You know what I mean? So, he’s the one that everybody contacts. If somebody’s got a problem, like the con goes and sees him, and then, through him, it can all be fucking taken care of.

P2: The program can be delegated through a health representative.

P3: That’s an excellent idea, yeah! (People in prison, Prison 2)

P: If we want this needle exchange with needles, why are we making it a process?

I: So, what is the solution?

P: It’s everything. It’s even to remove Healthcare from the exchange. (Healthcare worker, Prison 4)

Discussion

To guide implementation opportunities, the SEM provides a framework for identifying barriers and prioritizing solutions. The traditional SEM was adapted to the carceral context and the unique Canadian PNEP model, providing a comprehensive framework to understand and address the multifaceted determinants of PNEP participation. It is noteworthy that we found significant overlap in responses between correctional officers, healthcare workers, and people in prison, particularly for potential solutions and program modifications to improve PNEP uptake. While most barriers to PNEP uptake were clustered at the individual-interpersonal levels, no standalone solution was proposed at these levels, suggesting that prison-based interpersonal relationships are likely challenging to address. Conversely, proposed solutions focused primarily on organizational and/or structural modifications to the delivery of the PNEP, levels of the SEM that can likely be more easily influenced, with the advantage of having secondary benefits on interpersonal relationships. By synthesizing the top-ranked barriers and solutions across many stakeholder groups, and organizing them across distinct spheres of influence, targeted interventions at each unique site can potentially be facilitated to improve reach and overall coverage, ensuring program sustainability.

Several top-ranked barriers to PNEP uptake among incarcerated PWID were the result of the behaviours of correctional officers towards participants (i.e., at the individual-interpersonal levels of the SEM). Correctional officers described the complexities of PNEP oversight, whereby obtaining and possessing injecting equipment is allowed, but using ‘contraband’ remains an offence. Here, the entrenched ethical dilemma concerns the dual loyalty [38] faced when correctional officers are tasked to uphold their professional duties in preventing illicit drug use and ensuring security (i.e., CSC’s mandates) with their professional duties to espouse the contract signed by PNEP participants stipulating that participation should not result in increased searches, observation, or disciplinary action [39]. Our findings point to potentially harmful and stigmatizing implementation practices by correctional officers (e.g., targeted searches and drug tests, increased charges for drug use) towards PNEP participants, practices that favour their responsibilities to defend CSC’s mandates and are misaligned with program guidelines. One potential consequence of these behaviours is the reinforcement of enacted stigma (defined as experiences of discrimination or prejudice related to drug use felt by incarcerated PWID) leading to increased internalized stigma, or the acceptance of negative views and self-devaluation as a result of drug use, both of which have been shown to contribute to negative health care-seeking attitudes [40]. These have the potential to compromise PNEP uptake. In fact, stigma/judgment by correctional officers was perceived as a top-ranked barrier among people in prison, likely underpinned by correctional officers’ struggles to balance their dual loyalty. One possible solution would entail altering the delivery of the PNEP to remove correctional officers from the process, a solution that was highly favoured by healthcare workers and particularly, by people in prison.

While most prison-based needle and syringe programs globally rely on prison healthcare staff for program delivery, several programs are delivered without the involvement of correctional employees [5]. For example, in Germany and in parts of Switzerland, dispensing machines provide needles/syringes, a delivery model that is centred on preserving patient confidentiality while offering 24-h accessibility. In Moldova, and recently in the Ukraine, PNSP delivery is operated by peers, a delivery model that is rooted in trust in addition to confidentiality and accessibility. A lack of confidentiality was perceived as the most important barrier to PNEP enrolment in our study. In fact, the most common reason for the discontinuation of prison-based needle and syringe programs worldwide has been the failure to protect participant confidentiality [5], underscoring the importance of addressing this potentially catastrophic barrier to ensure PNEP sustainability. If the current delivery model was replaced by peers, a structural/policy change proposed by healthcare workers and people in prison, participants would not be publicly ‘outed’ during visual inspections, stigmatized by correctional officers, and targeted for increased searches – solving many of the top-ranked barriers and leading to improved interpersonal relationships. Prison-based interventions involving peers have demonstrated increased viral hepatitis testing and subsequent engagement in the care cascades [41–43]. An important consideration, however, is that prison subcultures can greatly influence uptake of evidence-based practices like enrolment in maintenance programs in carceral settings [44–49]. Prison subculture (sometimes referred to as gangs in North America) refers to the distinctive set of norms, values, beliefs, and behaviours that develop among people in prison, shaping their interactions and social hierarchies. This subculture often emerges as a response to the unique environment of incarceration, where people in prison adapt to the constraints and challenges of prison life by establishing informal codes of conduct and social structures. These adaptations can influence inmates' identities and behaviours both during incarceration and after release. In the case of PNEPs, the actual delivery of the program may be influenced by how it is set up. Moreover, it is important to recognize that an external delivery model was not proposed as a solution by correctional officers in any of the nine prisons, and without buy-in from all stakeholder groups, these types of policy-level program modifications will unlikely occur. Perhaps a more feasible alternative to the current model is one that is replaced by safe injection sites.

Safe injection sites received broad support from correctional officers but were met with less enthusiasm among healthcare workers and people in prison. Correctional officers articulated that the rationale behind their enthusiasm for safe injection sites was to keep needles/syringes in contained spaces for their own perceived safety. While this may be true; however, safe injection sites could also address bullying from peers as the drugs consumed and equipment used would be constrained to the sites, thereby potentially improving interpersonal relationships between PNEP participants and both correctional officers and peers. As of January 31, 2025, safe injection sites, or “overdose prevention sites” (OPS), operate in five federal prisons [14]. Despite being the top-ranked solution among correctional officers, enthusiasm for safe injection sites was unevenly distributed across prisons, with only four correctional officer groups endorsing these as a solution. These findings may be explained by a recent study that found that OPS were perceived to potentially hinder behavioural rehabilitation, recovery from drug dependence, and effective reintegration post-release among Canadian correctional officers [50], and should be further explored as a viable, long-term solution before any structural modifications are made to the current PNEP.

An important barrier to PNEP uptake at both the organizational and system levels was the lengthy application process, with approval at the discretion of prison leadership. This was reported to take up to three months among people in prison despite current program guidelines that suggest that a TRA decision is expected within 10 working days. These misaligned implementation processes may be due to a lack of knowledge of PNEP guidelines and expectations among prison leadership (a result of personnel turnover), lack of time due to competing priorities, and/or simply a lack of stakeholder buy-in to support PNEP activities. While automatic enrolment would alleviate many barriers, it too, was not proposed as a solution by correctional officers in any of the nine prisons, and was only recognized as a barrier in one prison. Although Canada is the only country without an automatic approval process for PNEP participation [5], abolishing the TRA is unlikely to occur if endorsement from correctional officers and their Union is not obtained. The TRA was introduced to maximize security and to allay correctional officers’ concerns that needles could be used as weapons, particularly by individuals with a history of violent offenses. Unfortunately, data regarding needlestick injuries or the use of post-exposure prophylaxis, have not been made available to CSC employees, even those who work in PNEP prisons. Accurate data provision to key stakeholders is a keystone to better implementation [5]. Data regarding the overall safety of the PNEP could potentially have a positive impact on its perception among correctional officers and may be a necessary step towards automatic enrolment.

Education was the top-ranked solution among healthcare workers and similarly received considerable support from people in prison and correctional officers. Education was felt to be needed across all SEM levels, spanning from the individual level to as high up as prison leadership (i.e., system). There are several potential knowledge gaps which could be addressed through targeted education. As a starting point, all correctional employees (prison leadership, correctional officers, and healthcare staff) working in PNEP prisons should receive training on current PNEP guidelines, emphasizing the TRA approval process and the content of the PNEP contract signed by participants. This would improve alignment between policies and implementation practices. Similarly, employees and people in prison should receive education on the principles of harm reduction, the biological basis of addiction, and the primary and secondary benefits of needle and syringe programs, such as referral to ancillary programs [51–56], likely improving knowledge and attitudes towards and among PWID in prison [57]. Education is considered an essential part of a bundle of implementation strategies to promote quality improvement [58], and consideration could even be given to the delivery of education by peers. Given their ability to create trustworthy and non-stigmatizing relationships, peers are an effective [41, 59] and cost-effective strategy for education delivery and information sharing in prisons [60].

One important observation is that there is significant granular heterogeneity across prisons vis-à-vis top-ranked barriers and solutions. This likely reflects important contextual differences and emphasizes the need to tailor responses to site-specific implementation challenges. Nonetheless, the findings suggest important shared challenges and solutions.

Our study has limitations. First, recruitment relied entirely on prison personnel and, as with all qualitative research, social desirability and sampling biases may have been introduced. Second, correctional officer stakeholders declined participation in two prisons, influenced by Union representatives opposing participation in research aimed at supporting PNEP uptake. Furthermore, we included data for groups with as few as two participants, despite 8–10 participants being the preferred size for nominal group technique [33]. While our findings may not be fully representative of all stakeholders at each prison, we did reach data saturation. Finally, given the heterogeneity across prisons, our findings are unlikely setting agnostic; country- and site-specific research should be undertaken to understand the unique barriers and enablers that may exist. [61]

Conclusions

Several multi-level modifiable barriers to improving PNEP uptake in Canadian federal prisons were shared among all key stakeholders. Structural changes to PNEP delivery including supervised/safe injecting sites and a peer-led program were proposed as solution-driven enablers to increasing PNEP uptake among PWID in prison. Education should be prioritized to help align PNEP guidelines with implementation practices. These data will inform efforts to improve service delivery to ensure the sustainability of Canada’s PNEP.
