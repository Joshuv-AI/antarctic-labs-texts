Efficacy of Biologics for Hidradenitis Suppurativa: A Network Meta Analysis and Meta Regression for Baseline Disease Severity
Authors: James Pham, Tara Sholji, Marra Aghajani, Ericka Maye, Kate Burrell, Cindy Kok, John W. Frew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHidradenitis Suppurativa is a challenging, heterogeneous inflammatory skin disease, with three approved biologic therapies. No head‐to‐head studies exist comparing these therapeutic modalities, and previous work has identified the influence of baseline disease severity upon clinical response. We aimed to perform a network meta‐analysis of approved biologic therapies and meta‐regression of baseline disease severity in moderate to severe HS. Pubmed, Embase, and Web of Science were searched from their inception up until 31 July 2025. Randomised Phase 3 clinical trials of biologic therapies approved for use in moderate to severe Hidradenitis Suppurativa. Network meta‐analysis was conducted via both frequentist and Bayesian frameworks. Subgroup analysis was performed using a priori identified covariates (baseline AN count, tunnel count, BMI, and smoking), and random effects meta regression was performed with the final model selected using restricted maximum likelihood, I 2, and Cochran's Q statistic. Adjusted odds ratios were then calculated. Efficacy outcomes analysed were the Hidradenitis Suppurativa Clinical Response (HiSCR‐50) score, compared using odds ratios and 95% CI for frequentist analysis and odds ratio and 95% CrI for Bayesian methods. Adalimumab (OR = 2.81 95% CI 2.01–3.92), followed by Bimekizumab q2weekly (OR 2.24, 95% CI 1.52–3.29) had the highest odds of HiSCR50 clinical response compared to placebo. Baseline tunnel count was the only significant covariate influencing odds of response (OR = 0.225, 95% CI = 0.013–0.436, p = 0.0375), with meta‐regression and adjusted odds ratios accounting for baseline tunnels demonstrating an increase in the odds of clinical response to Adalimumab and Bimekizumab, whilst the odds of achieving clinical response to Secukinumab became non‐significant relative to placebo.

Introduction

Hidradenitis Suppurativa (HS) is a chronic inflammatory disease manifesting in painful swollen nodules, abscesses and malodorous draining tunnels with a predisposition for flexural areas of the skin [1]. The disease is relatively heterogeneous with this heterogeneity influencing clinical response to therapy [1, 2, 3, 4]. Biologic therapies are the mainstay of therapy with three agents FDA approved for the management of moderate to severe HS across international published guidelines [2, 3, 4]. These include Adalimumab [5], Secukinumab [6] and Bimekizumab [7], with all agents having been validated in placebo‐controlled Phase 3 randomised clinical trials (RCTs).

The relative efficacy of each agent is difficult to interpret given the absence of head‐to‐head clinical trials. Hidradenitis Suppurativa Clinical Response (HiSCR) 50 rates range from 42% with Secukinumab to 58% of respondents with Bimekizumab. Placebo response rates are known to vary widely with HS clinical trials [8], with rates ranging from 25% in Adalimumab to 32% with Bimekizumab phase 3 studies, reflecting the dynamic nature of HS lesions and the imprecision of objective disease activity scoring.

Network meta‐analyses (NMA) provide a robust methodological framework to synthesise evidence from direct and indirect comparisons of different therapies, allowing for a comprehensive assessment of the relative efficacy of biologic agents in various inflammatory skin diseases [9, 10] including HS [11, 12]. NMA requires that included studies are comparable in terms of patient and disease characteristics which are known to influence the response of the outcome measure analysed. Existing NMAs do not account for this disease heterogeneity and hence may not accurately represent the indirect comparisons between therapies.

Emerging evidence suggests that patient level variables such as baseline abscess and nodule (AN) count [13, 14], draining tunnel count [13, 14], BMI [13, 14] and smoking [13, 14] may influence the relative efficacy of both Adalimumab and Secukinumab as well as placebo response rates of these studies. Therefore, a robust NMA requires examination of the impact of these a priori identified variables to ensure that the comparison of agents is valid. If any significant influence of these characteristics is identified, then meta‐regression will allow for adjustment for these characteristics with respect to treatment effect.

We aimed to perform a NMA of Phase 3 clinical data of approved agents in HS, with testing and adjustment for baseline disease severity using meta regression.

Methods

Study Design

Eligibility Criteria

All approved agents for moderate to severe HS were considered eligible for inclusion in this NMA. Currently (August 2025) Adalimumab, Secukinumab, and Bimekizumab are the three FDA and EMA approved agents for HS. Studies investigating the above medications were included if they were Phase 3, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled RCTs. Studies were required to report clinical efficacy according to the HiSCR50 outcome measure [15] and include details regarding baseline disease severity (AN count and tunnel count), smoking status and BMI for inclusion in meta regression.

Studies were excluded if they did not include a placebo control arm, were not phase 3 studies, were not conducted in moderate to severe HS, included less than 50 participants, or did not include adequate patient characteristic data to enable meta regression analyses.

Data Sources and Search Strategy

We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science up to July 31st 2025. The search terms included “hidradenitis suppurativa,” “acne inversa”, “phase 3,” “randomized controlled trial,” and terms specific to treatment options (e.g., “adalimumab,” “secukinumab” “bimekizumab”). References from eligible studies and relevant reviews were also manually searched.

Data Extraction and Management

Two independent reviewers (J.P. and J.F.) screened titles and abstracts for eligibility, followed by full‐text assessment. Disagreements were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer (T.S.). Data extracted included study primary author, year, design; summary statistics of patient demographics including age, sex, baseline disease severity as measured by baseline AN count and baseline tunnel count; interventions including drug names, dosage, and administration method, and rates of the HiSCR‐50 outcome.

Statistical Analysis

A network meta‐analysis was conducted using both a frequentist and a Bayesian framework. The network structure was visualised to ensure all treatments were connected either directly or indirectly through common comparators.

The frequentist network meta‐analysis was conducted using the netmeta package in R Studio (version 2022.07.1.). The Bayesian network meta‐analysis was conducted using the gemtc package in R Studio (version 2022.07.1.). Analyses for both methods were based on a random‐effects model, estimating odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) (CrI or credible intervals for Bayesian analysis) between all treatments in the network. For the frequentist method the relative treatment effects were estimated by fitting a pairwise comparison model and adjusting for the correlation between multiple treatments within each study [16]. For the Bayesien method, consistency between direct and indirect evidence was assessed using the node‐splitting method, and model convergence was evaluated using trace plots and the Gelman‐Rubin statistic [17, 18].

Sensitivity Analysis, Heterogeneity and Bias Assessment

Sensitivity analysis included assessment of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool. The overall risk of bias for each study was categorised as low, unclear, or high. We also evaluated the quality of the evidence for each treatment comparison using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [18].

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I 2 statistic, where I 2 values of 0%–30%, 30%–60%, and 60%–100% represent low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Inconsistency was evaluated through the node‐splitting method, which compares the results from direct evidence with those obtained from the network model. Publication bias was assessed visually by inspecting funnel plots for each outcome and performing Egger's test when appropriate.

Subgroup Analyses and Meta Regression

We performed subgroup analyses to investigate potential treatment effect modifiers as identified a priori based on data from previous publications. These included: Baseline AN count, Baseline Tunnel Count, BMI and Smoking Status [13, 14].

Meta‐regression analysis was conducted using the metafor package in R Studio (version 4.4.2). The rma() function was used to fit the random‐effects meta‐regression model using significant associations identified in subgroup analysis. The Restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method was used to estimate the model parameters, with AIC model selection and the I 2 statistic used to assess model fit. Cochran's Q statistic was measured to assess residual heterogeneity after accounting for each individual covariate, with p < 0.05 indicating lack of homogeneity.

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

Results

A systematic review of the literature was undertaken for all publications up until January 31st 2025. The PRISMA diagram is included in Figure S1. A total of six articles were identified. Three articles were excluded as they reported open‐label extension data from an included placebo‐controlled clinical trial and also did not include patient‐level data. The remaining three articles reporting six RCTs were used as the basis for the systematic review.

Studies included in the NMA are as follows: two RCTs of adalimumab 40 mg weekly versus placebo (PIONEER 1 and 2; n = 307 and 326) [5]; secukinumab at 300 mg two‐weekly (q2weekly) or q4weekly dosing versus placebo (SUNSHINE and SUNRISE; n = 541 and 543) [6]; and two RCTs of bimekizumab 320 mg at q2weekly or q4weekly dosing versus placebo (BE HEARD 1 and 2; n = 505 and 509) [7]. The primary endpoint of all studies was HiSCR50 response at week 16, defined as a 50% reduction in abscess and inflammatory nodule count with no increase in abscess or draining tunnels relative to baseline [15]. All studies report efficacy outcomes in the intention to treat populations, applying non‐responder imputation as appropriate [19].

Frequentist and Bayesian Metanalysis

The network map of the included studies and treatment arms is presented in Figure S2. Study level results of the frequentist NMA are presented in Figure 1.

Adalimumab had the highest odds ratio of achieving HiSCR50 compared to placebo (OR = 2.81 95% CI 2.01–3.92), followed by Bimekizumab q2weekly (OR 2.24, 95% CI 1.52–3.29), Bimekizumab q4weekly (OR 2.19, 95% CI 1.44–3.34), Secukinumab q4weekly (OR 1.62, 95% CI 1.20–2.20), followed by secukinumab q2weekly (OR 1.62, 95% CI 1.19–2.19). Sensitivity, Heterogeneity and Bias

Sensitivity analysis identified no significant differences between the frequentist and the Bayesian methodologies for treatment groups in NMA, with no change in the overall ranking of treatments (Figure 3).

Adjusted odds ratios after meta‐regression for Baseline draining tunnel count.

Quantifying heterogeneity revealed a relatively low I 2 value of 22.3%, suggesting modest variability among study outcomes that could not be attributed to chance alone. The tau2 value was estimated at 0.0280, indicating small but present between‐study variance. The Q‐test for heterogeneity was not significant (p = 0.224), further supporting the relative homogeneity of study results included in the analysis.

Funnel plot identified potential publication bias with PIONEER 2 being a specific outlier (Figure S4). Baujat plot also identified PIONEER 2 having significant contribution to heterogeneity (Figure S4). The exclusion of PIONEER 2 data from the meta‐analysis resulted in a significantly reduced OR for Adalimumab (OR = 2.05, 95% CI 1.26–3.32) with ranking placing Bimekizumab q2weekly (OR = 2.24, 95% CI 1.52–3.29) and q4weekly dosing (OR = 2.19, 95% CI 1.44–3.34) with a higher OR of HISCR50 response compared to placebo (Figure S5).

The fail‐safe N calculation suggests that a substantial number of studies (100) reporting no effect would be needed to nullify the observed significance, indicating the robustness of the findings against potential unpublished negative results. The GRADE assessment for the included studies was high, and a low risk of bias was identified across all included studies.

Subgroup Analysis and Meta‐Regression

Subgroup analysis identified baseline tunnel count as the only significant covariate (Table 2) with an odds ratio of 0.225 in likelihood of achieving HiSCR50 for each unit increase in mean baseline tunnel count (OR = 0.225, 95% CI = 0.013–0.436, p = 0.0375). Baseline tunnel counts remained significant after adjustment for all other covariates (Table 2). In selection of the variables for the meta‐regression model, the lowest Akaike Information Criterion corrected (AICc), as a measure of prediction error, with the least number of variables was used to avoid overfitting. Hence meta‐regression with adjustment only for baseline tunnel count was undertaken [20].

The adjusted odds ratios after adjustment for baseline tunnel count are presented in Figure 3, where baseline tunnel count was set to the mean of 3.4. Increases in the odds of achieving HiSCR50 clinical response were seen with Adalimumab 40 mg weekly (OR = 3.83, 95% CrI = 1.76, 8.00), Bimekizumab q2weekly (OR = 2.65, 95% CrI = 1.42–4.81) and Bimekizumab q4weekly (OR = 2.59 95% CrI = 1.38–4.79), and a decrease in the odds were seen with both doses of Secukinumab. The overall ranking of therapies remains unchanged, however the credible interval for both secukinumab doses now crosses OR = 1 compared with placebo.

Discussion

This network meta‐analysis and meta‐regression evaluating the efficacy of approved biologic therapeutics for HS identifies a small but significant between study variance (Tau2 = 0.028) although the Q‐test for heterogeneity was not significant for the HiSCR‐50 outcome examined. The PIONEER 2 study was identified as having the greatest contribution to between‐study heterogeneity, but not significant enough to justify removal from analysis. It has been noted in previous meta‐analyses that PIONEER 1 assigned a non‐responder imputation to all individuals receiving antibiotics; however, in PIONEER 2, the SUNRISE, SUNSHINE, BE‐HEARD 1, and BE‐HEARD 2 trials, tetracycline class antibiotics were allowed in stable dosages from screening [19]. PIONEER 2 did have lower baseline AN counts than PIONEER 1, but not as low as those seen in the SUNSHINE and SUNRISE trials.

Only Baseline draining tunnel count was identified as a significant treatment effect modifier (Table 2) and adjustment via meta regression led to significant alterations to the Odds Ratios of Adalimumab, Secukinumab and Bimekizumab (Figure 3). The adjusted Odds Ratios used a comparator average baseline tunnel count of 3.4, leading to a non‐significant CrI of Secukinumab at both dosing frequencies versus placebo. This needs to be interpreted in the context of the generally lower baseline tunnel counts of participants in the SUNRISE and SUNSHINE trials compared to other Phase 3 RCTs (Figures S5 and S6). Rather than suggesting a lack of efficacy of Secukinumab in HS overall, it instead suggests that the odds of achieving HiSCR‐50 are reduced with every unit increase in baseline tunnel count, and above a certain threshold (3 in our weighted analysis) may not meet significance over placebo. Therefore, Secukinumab may be of more benefit in individuals with lower baseline tunnel counts (Figure S7), while alternative agents such as Bimekizumab and Adalimumab may have clinically meaningful activity in such cases.

Conclusion

Network meta‐analysis of efficacy of approved therapies in Hidradenitis Suppurativa identifies Adalimumab 40 mg weekly as the most efficacious therapy over placebo in moderate to severe HS, followed by Bimekizumab q2weekly, Bimekizumab q4weekly, Secukinumab q2weekly, and Secukinumab q4weekly. Baseline tunnel count was a significant a priori covariate influencing the odds of clinical response as measured by HiSCR50 compared to placebo. Meta‐regression and adjusted odds ratios accounting for baseline draining tunnels resulted in an increase in the odds of clinical response for Adalimumab 40 mg weekly, Bimekizumab q2weekly, and Bimekizumab q4weekly, and a reduction in the odds of clinical response for both Secukinumab doses. This highlights the importance of the consideration of baseline disease severity in the interpretation of clinical trial results and may indicate more appropriate patient severities or populations for targeting with specific therapeutic modalities. In addition, these findings support the use of baseline draining tunnel counts as randomisation strata for future RCTs in order to ensure balanced interpretation of treatment effect sizes for novel therapeutics in HS.
