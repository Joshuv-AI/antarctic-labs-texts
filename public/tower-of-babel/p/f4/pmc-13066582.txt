Role of CTGF-LRP1 in impaired healing of cesarean section incisions
Authors: Chuqing He, Shunna Ge, Wei Xia, Yang Wang, Li Yan, Junyan Sun, Lirong Yan, Qian Zhu, Xiaoya Zhao, Jian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Niche, also known as cesarean scar defect, is a long-term complication of cesarean sections (CS), and its pathogenesis remains incompletely understood. Here, we profile 135,793 individual cells from adjacent myometrium tissue of niche (Adjacent, n = 48,587), well-healed cesarean scar tissue (Control, n = 47,653), and niche tissue (Niche, n = 39,553) using single-cell RNA sequencing. We identify a deficiency in LRP1 within a specific subgroup of fibroblasts in niche tissue, which correlate with a reduced ability to synthesize extracellular matrix (ECM). Through in vitro experiments, we demonstrate that LRP1 deficiency inhibited CTGF from effectively activating both the ERK and WNT signaling pathways in fibroblasts, thereby impairing their function. Tissue staining (30 non-healing vs 30 healing groups) validates our findings, showing decreased expression of LRP1 in non-healing groups. In a rat uterine scar model, treatment with recombinant human CTGF (rhCTGF) promoted myometrial regeneration and enhanced collagen production in uterine fibroblasts. Our analysis provides deep insights into the defective healing microenvironment of CS incisions and identifies critical cell types and signaling pathways involved in the formation of the niche. These results may inform novel prognostic approaches for predicting and treating niches.

Introduction

Niche, also known as cesarean scar defect, resulting from inadequate healing of a cesarean section (CS) incision, is characterized by an indentation at the site of the CS scar in the myometrium of ≥2 mm1–3. The reported incidence of niche ranges from 50% to 65%. The advancement of diagnostic techniques, such as ultrasound, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and hysteroscopy, may contribute to a higher clinical prevalence4. Niche can lead to postmenstrual spotting, chronic pelvic pain, and secondary infertility. Additionally, it increases the risk of complications in future pregnancies, including placenta previa, postpartum hemorrhage, and uterine rupture5–7. The mechanisms underlying the impact of improper healing of CS incisions and the formation of niche remain unclear. Relevant factors may include the techniques used for CS closure, the location of the CS incision, and the number of CS performed8,9. Furthermore, perioperative infection and hypercoagulability should be considered as predictors of niche formation10.

The niche cannot be repaired in a normal, orderly, and timely manner11,12. Extensive research has been conducted on the roles of fibroblasts and endothelial cells in wound healing and scar formation13,14. However, their involvement in wound healing within CS incisions remains poorly understood. A previous study15 identified fibroid proliferation in CS incisions, accompanied by thick-walled arterial blood vessels and signs of vascular degeneration. Another study reported macrophage infiltration and increased expression of inflammatory markers, such as CD16 and TNF-α, within the niche. The authors suggested that the expression of these inflammatory factors is positively correlated with fibrosis and angiogenesis16. To date, no reports have elucidated the mechanisms underlying these pathological changes.

There is currently no consensus on the optimal treatment for a niche17. Surgical interventions, such as hysteroscopic niche resection, laparoscopic repair, and transvaginal repair, are the primary treatment options18. However, it is important to note that in some cases, the niche may not demonstrate significant improvement following surgical treatment19. Therefore, it is essential to develop new therapeutic or prognostic approaches for managing niches.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) is a powerful tool for investigating cellular function and the pathophysiology of diseases. It has been employed to assess the microenvironment of chronic non-healing wounds, such as diabetic ulcers, burns, and pressure ulcers20–22. However, to our knowledge, the cellular heterogeneity and regulatory changes within the niche have not yet been systematically investigated at a single-cell resolution.

In this work, we profile 135,793 individual cells from adjacent myometrium tissue of niche (Adjacent, n = 48,587), well-healed cesarean scar tissue (Control, n = 47,653), and niche tissue (Niche, n = 39,553) using single-cell RNA sequencing. We identify distinct subgroups of fibroblasts and endothelial cells involved in CS incision healing. We find that LRP1 deficiency in the FB3 fibroblast subgroup impairs extracellular matrix synthesis and blocks CTGF-mediated activation of ERK and WNT signaling pathways. In vivo experiments in a rat uterine scar model demonstrate the therapeutic potential of rhCTGF. Additionally, the combined expression of CTGF and LRP1 predicts the healing degree of uterine incisions after transvaginal repair. These findings clarify the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying impaired uterine incision healing and highlight CTGF and LRP1 as potential therapeutic targets.

scRNA-seq reveals cellular heterogeneity in niche tissue

ScRNA-seq was performed to analyze cellular heterogeneity and investigate the regulatory changes among the Adjacent, Control, and Niche groups. Prior to the scRNA-seq analysis, fresh tissue specimens from 15 cases were dissociated through enzymatic digestion, followed by the preparation of single-cell transcriptome libraries and subsequent sequencing (Fig. 2a). Following quality control, a total of 135,793 cells were acquired for further analysis, comprising 48,587 cells from the Adjacent group, 47,653 cells from the Control group, and 39,553 cells from the Niche group. The Harmony24 package was utilized to integrate all samples and mitigate batch effects. The integrated cells were subsequently clustered using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) (Fig. 2b) and identified based on established classical cell markers (Fig. 2c–d)25. In total, we identified eight major cell types, which included fibroblasts (FB, n = 60,148), endothelial cells (Endo, n = 23,165), epithelial cells (Epi, n = 3934), T cells (T, n = 8681), natural killer cells (NK, n = 1156), macrophages (Macro, n = 4709), and two distinct types of smooth muscle cells (SMC1, n = 26,165; SMC2, n = 7847). The two types of smooth muscle cells are situated in the lower uterine segment, with one type deriving from the uterine body and the other from the cervix26. The specific localization of the niche at the junction of the uterine body and cervix facilitated the identification of these two smooth muscle cell types. Both SMC types exhibited elevated expression levels of TAGLN and ACTA2. Notably, SMC1 cells displayed increased MYH11 expression, whereas SMC2 cells showed reduced MYH11 expression levels.

Subsequently, we analyzed the variations in cellular composition across the different groups and samples (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 1b, Supplementary Data 2). A significant increase in the proportion of fibroblasts was observed in the Niche group compared to the Adjacent and Control groups (Fig. 2f). Additionally, a reduced proportion of endothelial cells was noted in the Niche group relative to the Adjacent and Control groups (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Researches have indicated that scar formation is influenced by the complex interactions between fibroblasts and endothelial cells27,28.As a result, we directed our focus towards a comprehensive analysis of these two predominant cell types.

Niche fibroblasts exhibit impaired extracellular matrix synthesis

To further investigate fibroblast heterogeneity, we conducted secondary clustering and identified three FB subgroups (Fig. 3a–b), each exhibiting distinct molecular signatures (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Data 3). The FB subgroups were characterized by specific markers: FB1-PDGFRA+CD10+C7-, FB2-PDGFRA+CD10-C7-, and FB3-PDGFRA+CD10-C7+ (Fig. 3d). Visualization of cellular density and distribution revealed that FB1 was predominant in the Niche group, while FB2 and FB3 were predominant in the Adjacent and Control groups (Fig. 3b). We observed that FB3 exhibited high expression levels of mesenchymal cell markers, including C729,30 and C331,32. Additionally, genes associated with cell-matrix interaction, matrix assembly, and the WNT signaling pathway, such as DPT33 and SFRP234, were also enriched in FB3. Immunofluorescence (IF) staining results confirmed the presence of these three FB subgroups (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Fig. 2a–c). Consistent with the transcriptomic findings, FB3 abundance was significantly reduced in the Niche group (Fig. 3f). Gene Ontology35 (GO) analysis provided insights into the biological functions of the FB subgroups based on their characteristic genes. The prominent genes within the FB1 were found to be associated with processes related to methylation, oxidative phosphorylation, lipid oxidation, and the cellular response to endoplasmic reticulum stress (Fig. 3g). In contrast, the characteristic genes of the FB2 were enriched in terms associated with estradiol/estrogen stimuli and NOTCH signaling pathway (Fig. 3g). Conversely, the feature genes of the FB3 demonstrated enrichment in terms related to collagen biosynthesis and wound healing (Fig. 3g). Endothelial cell activation in niche tissue

We categeorized Endo into three subgroups(Fig. 4a–b), each exhibiting unique expression profiles(Fig. 4c, Supplementary Data 5). Endo subgroups were labeled based on specific markers: Endo1-PECAM1+CXCL12+EGR1-, Endo2-PECAM1+CXCL12-EGR1-, and Endo3- PECAM1+CXCL12-EGR1+ (Fig. 4d). Notably, Endo3 exhibited high expression levels of SELE and IL6. Compared to the Adjacent group, whereas Endo3 represented a greater proportion in both the Control group and the Niche group (Fig. 4b). The existence of these three Endo subgroups was confirmed using multiplex IF staining on paraffin sections (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 3a–c). GO enrichment analysis revealed that Endo1 was implicated in the standard functions of endothelial cells, encompassing differentiation and development (Fig. 4f). In contrast, Endo2 was linked to inflammatory processes, particularly antigen presentation (Fig. 4g). The characteristic genes associated with Endo3 exhibited enrichment in terms related to ECM organization, cellular response to interleukin −1, as well as responses to transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) and hypoxia (Fig. 4h). Previous research indicates that the pathways enriched by Endo3, such as responses to TGF-β37, responses to hypoxia38, and ECM organization39, are associated with tissue remodeling and wound healing. Monocle2 analysis40 was used to further clarify the interrelationships among the Endo subgroups. The results indicated that Endo1 was primarily located at one end, Endo3 at the opposite end, and Endo2 in the middle (Supplementary Fig. 4a–b). According to the pseudotime sequence, it was observed that Endo3 exhibited high expression of typical markers associated with endothelial cell activation, such as CCL2, CEBPD, SELE, and THBD41 (Supplementary Fig. 4c). It is hypothesized that the activation of endothelial cells prompted Endo1 to transition to Endo2, ultimately evolving into Endo3. Endothelial cells secrete cytokines that indirectly stimulate fibroblasts and enhance the ECM synthesis42. We hypothesize that this function of endothelial cells may play a role in the CS incision healing. These findings are consistent with our previous histological results (Fig. 1b).

To explore intercellular interactions, we conducted both CellChat43 (Supplementary Data 6) and iTALK44 (Supplementary Data 7) analyses. The ligand-receptor plot generated from the iTALK analysis, along with the heatmap produced by CellChat, indicated that the most significant interactions occurred between FB3 and Endo3 (Fig. 5a–b). Based on the CTGF-LRP1 observed in iTALK analysis (Fig. 5a), we further explored the Connective Tissue Growth Factor (CTGF) signaling pathway in the Cellchat analysis. The results indicate that Endo3 acted as the principal signal sender and FB3 as the key receiver (Fig. 5c). Numerous studies have indicated that CTGF stimulates the proliferation of fibroblasts, promotes their transformation into myofibroblasts,mediates cell adhesion,thus plays a crucial role in the progression of wound healing and fibrotic diseases45,46. The expression levels of CTGF were observed to be consistent across Endo subgroups (Supplementary Fig. 5a).

A bubble plot analysis identified three dominant CTGF receptor candidates—LRP1, LRP6, and ITGA5 (Fig. 5d). Among fibroblast subgroups, FB3 expressed the highest levels of LRP1, whereas LRP6 and ITGA5 were low across all subgroups (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Tissue-level analyses confirmed low expression of LRP6 and ITGA5 in Adjacent, Control, and Niche tissue, while Niche FB3 showed reduced LRP1 expression (Fig. 5e). Fibroblasts were isolated from patient samples (Adjacent, Control, Niche) and confirmed via Vimentin immunofluorescence (Supplementary Fig. 6b). RT-qPCR identified decreased LRP6 and ITGA5 expression across all fibroblasts, while Western blot assays confirmed that LRP1 was downregulated in Niche fibroblasts compared to Adjacent and Control cells. (Supplementary Fig. 6c–e). These findings guided our focus on CTGF–LRP1 in cesarean incision healing.

LRP1 binds CTGF, stimulating fibroblast collagen secretion and ECM synthesis, promoting wound repair47. Conversely, the LRP1 antagonist RAP inhibits these effects48. IF co-staining of C7 and LRP1 demonstrated LRP1 expression in FB3 (Fig. 5f), while co-staining of EGR1 and CTGF indicated CTGF expression in Endo3 (Supplementary Fig. 5b). This localization aligns with prior reports showing CTGF is primarily secreted by uterine vascular endothelial cells49. Immunohistochemical (IHC) revealed a significantly lower proportion of LRP1-positive areas in Niche tissue (16.1% ± 3.2%) compared to Adjacent (29.7% ± 3.1%) and Control (29.8% ± 3.5%) groups (Fig. 5g–h), whereas CTGF expression remained similar across groups (Supplementary Fig. 5c).

LRP1 inhibition attenuates CTGF-induced collagen synthesis

We hypothesized that reduced LRP1 in Niche FB3 impairs fibroblast responsiveness to Endo3-derived CTGF, contributing to ECM deficiency and defective healing. Fibroblasts from Adjacent, Control, and Niche tissues were treated with recombinant human CTGF (rhCTGF) (Fig. 6a). Collagen I and III production increased dose-dependently in Control and Adjacent fibroblasts but was blunted in Niche fibroblasts (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Fig. 7a). siRNA-mediated LRP1 knockdown in human uterine fibroblasts (HUF) followed by rhCTGF treatment revealed that silencing LRP1 diminished CTGF-induced collagen synthesis, confirming LRP1 as a key mediator in ECM remodeling (Fig. 6c–d, Supplementary Fig. 7b–c).

LRP1 inhibition blocks CTGF-induced ERK/WNT signaling

RNA sequencing of HUF transfected with LRP1-siRNA or control siRNA and treated with rhCTGF showed that LRP1 knockdown significantly reduced ERK and WNT pathway activities (Fig. 6e–f). RT-qPCR confirmed downregulation of canonical WNT pathway markers (Supplementary Fig. 7d). rhCTGF treatment increased ERK phosphorylation in HUF within 15–30 minutes, whereas LRP1 silencing abolished this response (Fig. 6g).

Inhibition of MEK1 with PD98059 prior to rhCTGF treatment blocked CTGF-induced Collagen I and III expression, whereas activation of ERK1/2 with C16-PAF in LRP1-silenced HUF restored ECM production and enhanced β-catenin phosphorylation (Fig. 6h). Niche fibroblasts displayed impaired ERK/WNT activation in response to rhCTGF, mirroring LRP1 knockdown phenotypes (Fig. 6i–j). These results support a model in which LRP1 deficiency in Niche FB3 limits CTGF-mediated pro-regenerative signaling, compromising ECM synthesis.

CTGF-LRP1 could be used to predict the degree of healing of uterine incisions

In order to further investigate the predictive value of LRP1-CTGF in relation to the efficacy of transvaginal niche repair, we collected samples of niches during the transvaginal surgical procedure and conducted IHC staining for further analysis.

Based on MRI findings, clinical symptoms, and criteria established by Huirne, Judith1, Jian Zhang2,3, and Xipeng Wang50,51, we defined the healing group as cases with a myometrial indentation at the uterine incision site <2 mm and a postoperative menstrual duration ≤7 days. Cases not meeting these criteria were assigned to the non-healing group. Clinical characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Data 9. No significant differences were observed between groups in baseline variables, including age, gravidity, parity, cesarean history, preoperative symptoms, and MRI findings. Postoperatively, the healing group demonstrated marked improvement in symptoms and myometrial thickness.

Primary rat uterine fibroblasts (RUFs) treated with CTGF also exhibited enhanced collagen production, consistent with the in vivo findings (Fig. 8f–g). These results align with previous studies on CTGF in wound healing. Twigg et al. reported that CTGF accelerates diabetic wound repair by increasing type IV collagen, improving wound architecture53. Similarly, Leep et al. demonstrated that CTGF enhances meniscal healing by recruiting mesenchymal stem cells and promoting their differentiation into fibrocartilage-producing cells54.

Together, our study integrates clinical patient samples, single-cell transcriptomic analysis, in vitro functional assays, and an in vivo rat model, establishing a comprehensive framework for understanding CTGF-LRP1 signaling in the impaired healing of cesarean section incisions. These findings highlight CTGF and LRP1 as potential therapeutic targets warranting further translational investigation.

Discussion

We established a single-cell atlas of the niche and explored the respective roles of fibroblasts and endothelial cells in the healing of CS incisions. It was observed that fibroblasts in the Niche group exhibited a relatively weaker ability to synthesize ECM. Additionally, there was a lower proportion of endothelial cells in the Niche group compared to the Adjacent and Control groups. We found that the expression of LRP1 in FB3 from the Niche group was reduced. In vitro experiments confirmed that CTGF was unable to promote ECM synthesis by uterine fibroblasts after silencing LRP1. Additionally, inhibition of LRP1 hindered the activation of the ERK and WNT signaling pathways by CTGF. Furthermore, the expression of CTGF and LRP1 was positively correlated with the degree of healing of CS incisions following transvaginal niche repair. This study represents a systematic investigation into the mechanisms underlying the poor healing of CS incisions (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Blood vessel disorders were observed in the CS incision55. Donnez et al. emphasized a significant deposition of collagen fibers at the niche site compared to the adjacent myometrium56, which were consistent with our research. Additionally, it was noted that existing pathological studies had overlooked the importance of well-healed CS scars as a crucial control group. Furthermore, we investigated the role of endothelial cells and fibroblasts beneath the serosa.

Several studies have elucidated the various subtypes of fibroblasts and endothelial cells involved in wound healing and scar formation27,28. A distinct subgroup of fibroblasts, characterized by high expression levels of MMP1 and MMP3, was identified in patients with diabetic foot ulcers20. This subgroup also demonstrated significant capabilities for secreting ECM components. Deng et al. reported a notable increase in the proportion of mesenchymal fibroblast subtypes in hypertrophic scars compared to normal scars57. Additionally, Song et al. observed the activation of a unique cell fate in endothelial cells within severely fibrotic myocardium, with representative markers including CEBPD, VCAM1, SELP, and SELE58. Integrating the findings from these studies, we identified that fibroblasts in the niche can be categorized into three subgroups. FB3 exhibited a diminished capacity for ECM synthesis and was found to be less prevalent in the Niche group. Furthermore, it was noted that endothelial cells can also be classified into three subgroups, with those in the Control and Niche groups displaying activated characteristics.

As one of the receptors of CTGF, the disruption of LRP1 led to a loss of fibroblast involvement in cytoskeletal remodeling and impacted ECM synthesis59. CTGF played an important role in the early repair of burn wounds60. Schultz et al.61 found that after corneal injury, the epithelial cells located at the edge of the wound secrete increased levels of CTGF, while a deficiency of CTGF interferes with the re-epithelialization process of corneal wounds.

Research has demonstrated that in renal fibrosis, the interaction between CTGF and LRP1 triggers the phosphorylation of intracellular domains, thereby activating the ERK1/2 signaling pathway in myofibroblasts47,62. Baron found that ERK phosphorylation can enhance WNT signaling and promote bone formation in the appendicular skeleton63. Both Cellchat and iTALK analysis suggested that CTGF+ Endo3 could interact with LRP1+ FB3. Our experimental findings support this interaction, indicating that after silencing LRP1, CTGF may be unable to activate the ERK1/2 and WNT pathways, thereby inhibiting the synthesis of collagen I and collagen III in primary uterine fibroblasts. A rat uterine injury model was employed to dynamically evaluate the role of rhctgf in the healing of uterine incisions. These results suggest that the ctgf-lrp1 interaction may play a significant role in the healing process of CS incisions.

In hysteroscopic niche repair, the use of electrosurgical instruments may introduce thermal injury to tissue margins, compromising structural integrity. In contrast, transvaginal surgery relies on mechanical tissue resection, preserving histoarchitectural features critical for pathological evaluation1. Accordingly, our clinical study, which included 60 patients undergoing transvaginal niche repair, demonstrated that CTGF and LRP1 levels more accurately predicted postoperative healing quality of CS incisions. These findings suggest that CTGF and LRP1 not only contribute to impaired CS healing but may also serve as prognostic biomarkers.

Our functional data emphasize the central role of LRP1 in CTGF-mediated niche formation. Nevertheless, the low yet detectable expression of ITGA5 and LRP6 suggests that their involvement cannot be excluded. ITGA5 may contribute to cell adhesion processes relevant to fibrosis64,65, while LRP6 may regulate Wnt signaling pathways66,67. These findings indicate a potential role for ITGA5 in fibrotic mechanisms and for LRP6 in modulating Wnt signaling. Future studies employing combined approaches are required to clarify these interactions and elucidate the mechanistic basis of niche formation.

In the future, intraoperative assessment of CTGF and LRP1 expression during cesarean sections or niche repair may provide a theoretical framework for real-time prognostic evaluation of incision healing. However, this application requires preclinical validation. In cases with substantial CTGF or LRP1 deficiencies, early postpartum interventions—such as localized CTGF administration or pharmacological activation of ERK/WNT signaling—may enhance tissue regeneration and reduce niche formation risk. Rigorous preclinical studies are necessary before clinical translation, including optimization of dosing, comparison of delivery methods, safety evaluation, and extended follow-up to monitor adverse effects. Furthermore, systemic measurement of CTGF in maternal blood or in patients with niche formation represents a promising noninvasive biomarker candidate. However, its diagnostic accuracy and clinical utility require confirmation in larger, well-controlled studies.

We acknowledge the limitation that laparoscopic repair was not assessed due to its infrequent use in our patient cohort. Further studies are warranted to validate the predictive relevance of CTGF and LRP1 in laparoscopic and other surgical contexts.

Our study has several limitations. While our findings establish CTGF–LRP1 signaling as a key driver of ECM dysregulation in niche formation, the upstream regulation of LRP1 remains to be elucidated. Notably, hypoxia has been shown to enhance the transcriptional activity of the wild-type LRP1 promoter68, consistent with reports that HIF-1 deficiency impairs chronic wound healing, whereas HIF-1 overexpression promotes fibrotic scar formation69. Moreover, TGF-β stimulation significantly increases LRP1 mRNA levels in human retinal pigment epithelial (hRPE) cells70. Our scRNA-seq data indicate activation of both TGF-β and HIF-1 signaling pathways in Endo3, suggesting that these upstream signals may converge to modulate LRP1 expression. Concurrently, our current RNA-seq analysis of LRP1 knockdown (siLRP1) samples was specifically designed to dissect the downstream signaling cascades influenced by the CTGF–LRP1 axis. Future studies employing temporal multi-omics approaches will be essential for mapping the hierarchical regulation of this pathway. Secondly, we did not investigate the upstream pathways that regulate fibroblasts in relation to CTGF and LRP1. This study did not investigate potential risks of CTGF-based interventions, underscoring the importance of animal models to address knowledge gaps and optimize therapeutic strategies, including dosage, duration, and safety profiles for CTGF or ERK/WNT activators.

In summary, we have presented a single-cell atlas of the niche and reported the potential influence of CTGF-LRP1 on the healing of CS incision. This research provides a theoretical foundation for preventing the formation of niches and promoting the healing of CS incision.

Methods

Sample collection

This study complies with human research protection guidelines and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the International Peace Maternity and Child Health Hospital (GKLW 2017-126). This study included only female participants (sex assigned at birth, based on self-report during clinical registration), as the research focuses on uterine niche. All participants provided written informed consent for study participation and de-identified data publication. Supplementary Data 8 contains no direct identifiers, and indirect data are presented in aggregated form to protect privacy.

A Niche (an indentation at the site of the CS scar in the myometrium of ≥2 mm) and well-healed cesarean section scar tissue (an indentation at the site of the CS scar in the myometrium of <2 mm) were diagnosed using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)1–3. No female patients received hormone treatment prior to surgery. Adjacent myometrium tissue from the niche group (Adjacent, n = 38), well-healed cesarean scar tissue (Control, n = 8), and niche tissue (Niche, n = 68) were harvested. Most patients with uterine niches present clinically with prolonged menstrual bleeding, prompting surgical intervention during the secretory phase to reduce procedural risk and optimize postoperative recovery. Consequently, the clinical setting limited the acquisition of proliferative-phase niche specimens. To control for this potential bias, all control samples used for comparison were strictly matched to the secretory phase.

All tissue samples were collected under direct visualization using sterile surgical scissors. To preserve tissue architecture and avoid thermal or mechanical artifacts, no grasping forceps or electrocautery devices were used during dissection (Supplementary Fig. 10).Written informed consent for the publication of these surgical images was obtained from all participants prior to the study.

The freshly excised tissue was washed three times in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), immersed in physiological saline, and then placed on ice for transportation back to the laboratory. Based on the intended experimental purposes, the tissue was divided into three segments: (1) immersed in tissue storage solution (130-100-008, Miltenyi) for scRNA-seq within 24 hours; (2) immersed in 10% neutral formalin for histological and immunological analysis; and (3) stored in liquid nitrogen for experiments (western blot and RT-qPCR).

Clinical data

Preoperative baseline data, including age, gravidity, parity, cesarean history, symptoms, and MRI findings, were collected. All patients underwent follow-up at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months postoperatively, focusing on menstrual patterns and imaging characteristics related to uterine incision healing (Supplementary Data 9).

Histology

The clinical specimens were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for a minimum of 48 hours, dehydrated using an ethanol gradient, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned to a thickness of 4 μm. Subsequently, H&E staining, Masson staining, and Picro Sirius Red staining were performed according to the manufacturer’s guidelines (60524ES60, 60532ES74, Yeason, G1462, Solarbio, China).

Single cell suspension preparation

The tissue was cut into small pieces using scissors and then incubated in DMEM medium containing 0.1% collagenase I (Gibco, 17100017) and 0.1% collagenase II (Gibco, 17101015) at 37 °C with gentle shaking for 40 to 60 minutes. The digested suspension was filtered through a 100-µm cell strainer (CSS-013-100, BIOFIL). The enzymatic reaction was then halted by adding 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 350 × g for 5 minutes at 4 °C. Red blood cell lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotech, C3702) was employed to remove red blood cells. After removing the supernatant, the cells were resuspended in 100 μL of DMEM medium. Cell analysis was performed using a Trypan blue cell counter (cell viability ≥ 80%). The cell concentration utilized for loading onto the 10x Genomics Chromium™ system varied between 700 cells/μL and 1200 cells/μL.

Single cell cDNA library preparation and sequencing

The procedure was conducted by Neo Bio Technology Corporation in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Cells were counted and loaded onto the Chromium Controller (10X Genomics) to recover a target of 8000 single cells. The process involved the generation of gel beads in emulsion (GEMs), barcoding, GEM-RT clean-up, complementary DNA amplification, and library construction. Library quantification was performed using Qubit prior to pooling. The final library pool was sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using 150-base-pair paired-end reads.

ScRNA-seq data analysis

Aligning to the GRCh38 human genome, Cell Ranger software (version 6.0.3) was utilized for quality control and read counting of Ensemble genes. The raw and processed data were uploaded to the GEO datasets (GSE261147, GSE297012).

Single-cell analysis was performed using Seurat (version 5.1.0). Potential doublets in the single-cell data were identified and removed using DoubleFinder (version 2.0) (available from https://github.com/chris-mcginnis-ucsf/DoubletFinder). Genes included in the analysis were required to be expressed in a minimum of three cells, with the total number of expressed cells ranging from more than 200 to fewer than 5000 genes. Cells with a high percentage of mitochondrial genes to total genes ( > 20%) and hemoglobin genes ( > 2%) were filtered out. A total of 135,793 qualified cells were used for scRNA-seq analysis, including 48,587 qualified cells from Adjacent tissues (n = 5), 47,653 qualified cells from Control tissues (n = 5), and 39,553 qualified cells from Niche tissues (n = 5). Using Seurat, the top 2000 most variable genes were identified as highly variable genes (HVGs) based on their average expression and dispersion. The dimensionality of the data was reduced using principal component analysis (PCA) on HVGs. The Louvain modularity optimization algorithm was employed to cluster the cells in the graph, and the clustering results were visualized using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP).

Differential expression and enrichment analysis

The Seurat functions FindMarkers and FindAllMarkers conducted marker gene expression analysis for each cluster using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and a fold-change threshold of >1.2. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis35 and Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA)36 were performed based on differential expressed genes (DEGs). Significantly enriched GO terms and GSVA terms were identified using an adjusted p-value (<0.05). The results were presented through bubble plots, bar plots, violin plots, and heatmaps.

Trajectory analysis in the endothelial cells

In the process of wound healing, endothelial cells transited from a normal state to an activated state37. Therefore, scRNA-seq data that captures various cell states can be utilized to analyze the connections between these cells. Trajectory analysis was conducted for the endothelial cell subgroups using Monocle 2 (version 4.6.0)40. Based on scRNA-seq analysis, Endo1 exhibited the typical functions of normal endothelial cells, while Endo3 expressed markers associated with activated endothelial cells. Consequently, we manually established the pseudotime trajectory from Endo1 to Endo3.

Cell–cell communication analysis

CellChat (version 2.2.0)43 was performed to characterize and illustrate cell-cell communication across cell types. Only cell clusters containing > 10 cells were used for analysis. The “trimean” method was used for calculating the average gene expression in a “computeCommunProb” function. Pairwise comparison and visualization are performed by CellChat build-in function, only interactions with P-value < 0.05 are considered to significant.

ITALK (version 1.0)44 was employed to further elucidate growth factor-associated ligand-receptor pairs mediating cell-cell communication across cell types.

Immunofluorescence staining (IF)

Immunofluorescence staining was performed utilizing the Fluorescence Labeling Kit (RC0085, RC0086-23RM, RecordBio Biological Technology). The paraffin sections underwent a series of steps, including deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval. Following these procedures, the sections were washed with PBS and subsequently treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for a duration of 30 minutes to block endogenous peroxidase activity. This was followed by incubation with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for one hour to reduce nonspecific binding. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. Next, the sections were exposed to secondary antibodies at room temperature for one hour. Subsequently, the sections were treated with a TYR-520/TYR-570 or TYR-520/TYR-570/TYR-CY5 solution for a period of 10-15 minutes. The primary antibodies employed included rabbit monoclonal anti-PDGFRA (1:200, ab203491, Abcam), rabbit monoclonal anti-CD10 (1:200, E5P7S, Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit polyclonal anti-C7 (1:75, HPA001465, Atlas Antibodies AB), mouse monoclonal anti-PECAM1 (1:400, ab182981, Abcam), rabbit polyclonal anti-CXCL12 (1:200, 17402-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit polyclonal anti-EGR1 (1:200, 22008-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit polyclonal anti-LRP1 (1:100, A1439, Abclonal), and rabbit polyclonal anti-CTGF (1:100, A11456, Abclonal). DAPI (Beyotime, China) was employed for the visualization of cell nuclei.

Immunochemical staining (IHC)

The sections were immunolabeled using the following primary antibodies: rabbit polyclonal anti-LRP1 (1:200, A1439, Abclonal), rabbit polyclonal anti-C7 (1:100, HPA001465, Atlas Antibodies AB), rabbit polyclonal anti-CTGF (1:100, A11456, Abclonal), rabbit polyclonal anti-αSMA (1:3000, 14395-1-AP, Proteintech), and rabbit polyclonal anti-EGR1 (1:200, 22008-1-AP, Proteintech). The primary antibodies were diluted in 1% goat serum in PBS. The sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, subjected to antigen retrieval, and then incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide to inhibit endogenous peroxidase activity. Subsequently, the sections were blocked with 3% BSA for 25 minutes at room temperature and then incubated with the primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Immunoreactivity was visualized using a Rabbit Specific HRP/DAB Detection IHC kit (RC0080R, RecordBio Biological Technology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The levels of LRP1 and CTGF were measured by calculating the percentage of cells with positive signals from five randomly selected fields per section under a magnification of ˟400.

Cell culture and siRNA transfection

Adjacent, Control, and Niche fibroblasts were isolated from corresponding tissue specimens through enzymatic digestion as described in the single-cell sequencing protocol, followed by purification via differential adhesion and validation through Vimentin immunofluorescence staining. Cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. For functional assays, cells were treated for 48 hours with recombinant human CTGF (rhCTGF; HY-P70106, MCE), PD98059 (MEK/ERK inhibitor, HY-12028, MCE), or C16-PAF (MEK/ERK agonist, HY-108635, MCE).

The human uterine fibroblast (HUF) and rat uterine fibroblast (RUF) were obtained from Procell Biological Technology in China. Human LRP1 siRNA, human negative control (NC) siRNA, rat lrp1 siRNA and rat negative control (nc) siRNA were obtained from Tsingke Biological Technology in China. The siRNA was prepared in a stock solution at a concentration of 20 μM. JetPRIME (101000046, Polyplus) was employed as an effective transfection reagent. The transfection complex, consisting of 5.5 μl of siRNA stock solution, 200 μl of JetPRIME Buffer, and 4 μl of JetPRIME reagent, was mixed gently by vortexing and incubated for 10 minutes at room temperature. This transfection complex was then added to a 6-well plate containing 1.5 ˟10^5 cells and gently mixed to achieve a final siRNA concentration of 50 nM in each well. Following transfection with LRP1 siRNA, NC siRNA, lrp1 siRNA or nc siRNA for 48 hours, the cells were subsequently incubated with rhCTGF (HY-P70106, MCE), PD98059 (HY-12028, MCE), or C16-PAF (HY-108635, MCE) for 48 hours. RNA and protein were then extracted for further analysis. Three independent replicate experiments were conducted. Sequences of siRNAs were listed(Supplementary Data 10).

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

In summary, total RNA was extracted using the RNA Isolator Total RNA Extraction Reagent (R401-01, Vazyme). cDNA was generated using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix (R223-01, Vazyme). Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711-02, Vazyme) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequences of primers were obtained from Tsingke Biological Technology in China and were listed(Supplementary Data 11). Three independent replicate experiments were conducted.

Western blot

Fibroblasts were obtained and lysed in cold RIPA buffer (PC101, Epizyme). Protein concentrations were determined using a Bradford Protein Assay Kit (P0006, Beyotime). The proteins were separated using a 10% polyacrylamide gel and subsequently transferred onto 0.45 μm PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-HCl buffer containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following antibodies: rabbit polyclonal anti-Collagen Type I (1:1000, 14695-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit polyclonal anti-Collagen Type III (1:1000, 22734-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit monoclonal anti-LRP1 (1:1000, A0633, Abclonal), rabbit polyclonal anti-phospho-ERK1/2 (1:1000, 28733-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit polyclonal anti-ERK1/2 (1:1000, 11257-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit monoclonal anti-phospho-β Catenin(1:5000, 80067-1-RR, Proteintech), purified mouse anti-β Catenin (1:1000, 610154, BD biosciences) and mouse anti-GAPDH (1:10,000, 60004-1-Ig, Proteintech). The level of GAPDH was used as the internal standard. The blots were visualized using an Enhanced Chemiluminescence Kit (SQ201, Epizyme), and the intensities of the immunoreactive bands were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, MD, USA). Three independent replicate experiments were conducted.

Animal experiment

All animal procedures were authorized by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai and were conducted in compliance with the National Research Council’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (GKDW-A-2023-08).

The rat uterine scar model was established as follows:Female wild-type Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats weighing between 250 and 300 grams were utilized. The rats were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions and were given at least one week to adapt to their new environment. Sixty uterine horns from 30 rats were randomly allocated to three groups: Sham group (n = 20 uterine horns), PBS group (n = 20 uterine horns) and rhctgf group (n = 20 uterine horns). The rats were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of tribromoethanol (300 mg/kg), and an incision was made in the abdominal area to expose the uterine horns. A full-thickness defect was created by excising a segment approximately 1.0 cm in length and half of the total circumference from each uterine horn, while preserving the mesometrium. The four edges of the uterine horn defect were delineated with a 4-0 nylon suture. In the Sham group, the uterine horn was left intact in the abdominal cavity without removal52. All rats were administered antibiotics (Polymyxin B at 40 mg/L and Neomycin trisulfate at 1 g/L) in their drinking water for 14 days. For the PBS group, 1 ml of PBS was injected intraperitoneally every other day after surgery. For the rhctgf group, 0.1 µg/kg of rhctgf in 1 ml of PBS was injected intraperitoneally every other day following surgery. At days 14, and 30 post-treatment, the rats were euthanized, and the injured uterine horns were examined. The structural changes in the uterine horns were assessed using H&E staining, Masson staining and IHC. Fibrosis deposition was quantitatively assessed by analyzing three randomly selected fields per section under a magnification of ˟400.

Construction of RNA sequencing libraries and sequencing

Primary uterine fibroblasts were transfected with either NC-siRNA or LRP1-siRNA for 48 hours, followed by treatment with 10 ng/ml rhCTGF for an additional 48 hours. Total RNA was extracted utilizing the RNA Isolator Total RNA Extraction Reagent (R401-01, Vazyme). The integrity and quality of the RNA were evaluated using the Agilent 2200 system. RNA samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) exceeding 10.0 were considered suitable for the construction of complementary DNA (cDNA) libraries. cDNA libraries were constructed for each RNA sample utilizing the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NR604-01, Vazyme) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The protocol involved several key steps: Poly-A containing mRNA was isolated from 1 µg of total RNA using oligo (dT) magnetic beads and was subsequently fragmented into 200-600 base pairs through the application of divalent cations at 85 °C for 6 minutes. The resulting cleaved RNA fragments were utilized for the synthesis of both first- and second-strand cDNA. A dUTP mix was incorporated during the second-strand cDNA synthesis to facilitate the removal of the second strand. The cDNA fragments underwent end repair, A-tailing, and ligation with indexed adapters. The ligated cDNA products were purified and treated with uracil DNA glycosylase to eliminate the second-strand cDNA. The purified first-strand cDNA was then amplified via PCR to generate the cDNA libraries. Quality control was conducted using the Agilent 2200 system. Sequencing was performed on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform utilizing a 150 bp paired-end run.

RNA sequencing mapping

Prior to read mapping, clean reads were generated from the raw reads by eliminating adaptor sequences and low-quality reads. The clean reads were then aligned to human genome (GRCh38, Ensembl104) using the Star (available from https://github.com/alexdobin/STAR). Bioinformatics techniques were used to obtain gene counts, and the Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) method was applied to assess gene expression. The data were uploaded to the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets (GSE271372). A volcano plot was utilized to visualize DEGs, and GO analysis was conducted based on the identified DEGs.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were independently repeated a minimum of three times with consistent results. Statistical analysis was performed using R version 4.3.1 and GraphPad Prism 9.0. For normally distributed data, Student’s t-test was utilized for comparing two groups, otherwise Mann-Whitney U test was used. Multiple group comparisons for normally distributed data were performed using one-way ANOVA. Correlation analysis was done using either Spearman or Pearson methods. Results were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05.
