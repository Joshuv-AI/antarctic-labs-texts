Upcycling Spent Lithium Iron Phosphate Battery Into Fe‐CN3P Single Atom Catalyst for Environmental Remediation
Authors: Xianhui Ke, Kun Qian, Xiaoning Li, Xinyu Zheng, Zhongyong Yuan, Tianyi Ma, Wei Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIncreasing environmental pollution and shortage of critical resources call for technologies capable of achieving total resource recovery from waste materials. In this study, high leaching rates of 99.16% for lithium and 97.37% for iron were obtained from spent lithium iron phosphate (LFP) batteries through electrochemical (EC) advanced oxidation processes (EAOPs), and associated leaching mechanism was subsequently investigated. Moreover, residual C, Fe, and P elements in leach residue were directly converted into a P, N‐doped asymmetric single‐atom Fe catalyst (Fe‐CN3P), enabled by well‐dispersion of Fe elements and removal of surface passivation layers during leaching. When activated with peroxymonosulfate (PMS), Fe‐CN3P catalyst exhibited a pseudo‐first‐order kinetic rate of 10.768 min−1 for bisphenol A (BPA) degradation, which is approximately 2–10 times higher than those of conventional single‐atom catalysts. Based upon experimental and theoretical investigation, the presence of P in the local coordination environment was found to substantially enhance catalytic activity of Fe sites. P incorporation alters the adsorption mode of HSO5 − on Fe active centres and increases Bader charges in the FeIV═O reactive intermediate, thereby improving the capability of Fe to withdraw electrons from the BPA molecules. This study offers new perspectives for synergistically advancing “comprehensive resources recovery” and “waste to treat waste.”Keywords: coordination environment, peroxymonosulfate activation, spent lithium‐ion batteries, single‐atom catalysts, upcycling

Introduction

Endocrine‐disrupting chemicals (EDCs), which interfere with the biosynthesis, metabolism, or interactions of endogenous hormones, can exert severe detrimental effects on the human reproductive system and developmental processes [1, 2, 3, 4]. Continuous exposure to EDCs poses potential threats to human health and aquatic species, even at very low ng·L−1 to µg·L−1 concentrations [5]. Bisphenol A (BPA), a key material in the plastics industry, is one of the most prevalent EDCs in aquatic environments [6, 7]. Considerable technologies for removing BPA from water have been proposed and involve primarily physicochemical and biological approaches. The main advantages of physicochemical methods (e.g., Fenton oxidation, ultrasonic oxidation, and photocatalytic oxidation) are rapid reaction kinetics, chemical‐free operation, and low operational costs [8, 9, 10]. However, these methods also have critical drawbacks, such as stringent pH requirements (pH 2.0–4.0), limited scalability, and incomplete mineralization of the pollutant. On the other hand, biological strategies that involve microbial and enzymatic degradation exhibit superior environmental compatibility [11, 12]. Nevertheless, their industrial application is hindered by poor process stability and sluggish degradation kinetics. Thus, the development of cost‐effective and highly efficient catalytic oxidation systems is an urgent priority.

It has been identified that peroxymonosulfate (PMS)‐based advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have garnered significant attention in the environmental contaminant remediation owing to their superior reactivity, broad operational pH range, and cost‐effectiveness [13]. PMS activation systems that utilize single‐atom catalysts featuring metal‐N4 (M‐N4) coordination sites are particularly noteworthy. For instance, Zeng et al. developed an atomically dispersed iron (Fe)‐N4 catalyst with a remarkable specific activity of 5.16 × 103 L·min−1·g−1 for BPA degradation. Their Fe‐N4/PMS system exhibited superior selective oxidation activity across a broad pH range (3.0–11.0) [14]. However, conventional M‐N4‐coordinated single‐atom catalysts always suffer from their symmetric electron distribution, which restricts efficient electron transfer and results in weak polarity [15, 16]. As a result, the reactant adsorption and activation processes are significantly frustrated [17]. Recent studies have demonstrated that modifying the metal coordination environment through heteroatom doping (particularly with non‐metallic elements) can disrupt this symmetric electronic structure of single‐atom catalysts [18, 19]. For example, an oxygen‐doped cobalt (Co)‐N3O1 asymmetric configuration exhibited significantly enhanced ciprofloxacin degradation kinetics (0.287 min−1) compared with the conventional symmetric Co‐N4 structure (0.0289 min−1) while maintaining excellent selectivity [20]. Furthermore, representative studies using boron regulated Fe1 centres on graphene‐like carbon nitride show that constructing asymmetric coordination microenvironments redistributes electron density at Fe sites and steers HSO5 – adsorption geometry, thereby directing PMS activation toward selective generation of singlet oxygen (1O2) [21]. However, conventional protocols for preparing heteroatom‐doped single‐atom catalysts typically involve complex procedures, generate substantial amounts of wastewater, and consume excessive materials, leading to inefficient resource utilization.

Currently, the rapid proliferation of portable electronics, electric vehicles, and grid‐scale energy storage systems has driven an unprecedented surge in demand for lithium‐ion batteries (LIBs), which in turn will increase the quantity of waste batteries once they are spent [22, 23, 24, 25]. It has been demonstrated that worldwide waste LIBs will reach 11 million metric tons by 2030 [26]. Of these, 313,300 tons will come from lithium iron phosphate (LFP) batteries, which are widely used in LIBs owing to their low cost, enhanced safety, and superior thermal stability [27, 28]. The spent LFP (S‐LFP) batteries contain substantial amounts of valuable lithium (Li) and Fe metals, as well as non‐metallic carbon (C) and phosphorus (P) elements. Thus, their improper disposal not only wastes resources but can also lead to environmental pollution [29, 30]. Current recycling technologies (e.g., pyrometallurgy, hydrometallurgy, bioleaching, and electrochemical (EC) methods) predominantly focus on the leaching and recovery of Li and Fe from S‐LFP [31, 32]. Notably, a direct EC oxidation method developed by Yang et al. achieved Li leaching efficiency of 95.02% from spent LIBs within 3 h at 2.5 V. This approach not only ensures the recovery of highly pure Li but also minimizes secondary pollution risks [33]. Additionally, an EC solid‐state electrolysis method proposed by Wang et al. succeeded in leaching Li and Fe into a phosphate electrolyte with remarkable efficiencies of 98.23% and 96.06%, respectively, with no wastewater generated throughout the process [34]. However, these state‐of‐the‐art methods generally neglect the treatment of the residues leached from S‐LFPs. Residual Li, Fe, P, and C from unreacted components can infiltrate soil via rainwater, posing risks to human health, and disrupting microbial communities.

Based upon this background, this study develops an innovative method for the short‐loop recovery of Li and Fe from S‐LFPs using EC advanced oxidation processes (EAOPs), while simultaneously utilizing the resulting leach residue as C, Fe, and P sources. The mechanism of metal leaching from S‐LFP via the EAOP was investigated using x‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and x‐ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) analyses. Subsequently, this residue was combined with an in situ N‐rich precursor (dicyandiamide) to construct a single‐atom Fe catalyst via a one‐step calcination process. The as‐fabricated catalyst was evaluated for BPA degradation to assess its catalytic performance and PMS activation behavior. Additionally, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to elucidate the P doping mechanism which enhances the catalytic activity of the Fe‐N3P sites. This study not only establishes a pathway for the application of high‐value leach residues from S‐LFPs but also introduces a cost‐effective and highly efficient catalytic system for aqueous BPA remediation, fulfilling the circular economy principles of “comprehensive resources recovery” and “waste to treat waste.”

Results and Discussion

Short‐Loop Electrochemical Metal Recovery From S‐LFP

The material characterization of S‐LFP (mixed LiFePO4 cathode and graphite anode materials after disassemble) is shown in Figure S1, confirming that the aggregation of nonuniform LiFePO4 particles after use. In conventional hydrometallurgical approaches, H2O2 is externally introduced to oxidize Fe2+ and disrupt the LiFePO4 crystal structure, facilitating Fe and Li metals leaching. However, such direct chemical oxidation often leads to inefficient Li/Fe extraction, as well as environmental handling issues due to the instability and storage limitations of commercial H2O2. In contrast, EC methods offer a sustainable alternative by in situ generating H2O2 via the 2‐electron oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) at the cathode surface (Figure 1a) [35]. The H2O2 generation under varying H2SO4 concentrations was evaluated during the EC process to identify the optimal leaching conditions. Notably, after the continuously O2 bubbling and the stable electrolysis for 2 h, 6.85 mM H2O2 was generated in the 0.1 M H2SO4 (Figure S2a,b), significantly surpassing the yields obtained in 0.5 and 1 M H2SO4 conditions (Figure S2c). Importantly, when the carbon felt cathode was reused for up to 40 consecutive cycles, the H2O2 concentration remained stable at around 6.23 mM (Figure S2d), indicating that the excellent acid resistance and operational durability of the electrode [36]. Post‐cycling SEM characterization of the carbon felt (Figure S2e,f) revealed slight particulate deposition on the fibre surfaces while preserving the original texture and roughness.

A clear correlation between H2SO4 concentration and the leaching kinetics of the EC leaching kinetics of Li and Fe from S‐LFP was observed (Figure S3). Remarkably, near‐complete leaching efficiencies of 99.16% for Li and 97.37% for Fe were achieved in 0.1 M H2SO4 (0.1 M H2SO4 + EC), substantially outperforming those obtained at 0.5 M (83.59%/85.27%) and 1 M (73.01%/73.05%) at higher acid concentrations (Figure 1b,c). By contrast, the direct addition of 6.85 mM H2O2 under the same acid concentration (0.1 M H2SO4 + H2O2) resulted in substantially lower Li and Fe extraction rates (63.45% and 47.72%, respectively). While the blank condition without the oxidant (0.1 M H2SO4) showed the poorest performance, achieving merely 39.33% Li‐ and 39.03% Fe‐leaching efficiencies. These results clearly demonstrate that EC advanced oxidation with 0.1 M H2SO4 enables near‐quantitative efficiency in leaching Li and Fe from S‐LFP, outperforming both direct chemical oxidation and acid‐only treatments.

After five consecutive electrolytic cycles conducted in the same cell, followed by pH‐adjusted filtration, amorphous ferrous phosphate dihydrate (FePO4·2H2O) was obtained, as confirmed by XRD analysis (Figure S4). This crystalline‐phase determination validates the successful isolation of the target Fe species which is suitable for the subsequent catalytic applications. To obtain battery‐grade FePO4, the FePO4·2H2O precursor material was subjected to calcination at 500°C for 2 h in a muffle furnace. On the other hand, trisodium phosphate solution was added to the leachate for precipitation, and Li3PO4 was generated after thermal treatment as shown in Figure S5. XRD analysis (Figure 1d,e) revealed well‐defined crystalline diffraction peaks for the FePO4 and Li3PO4 products, exhibiting excellent correspondence with the standard JCPDF cards (No. 00‐29‐0715 for FePO4 and No. 25–1030 for Li3PO4, respectively). These results indicate the successful synthesis of high‐purity, well‐crystallized FePO4 and Li3PO4 materials that meet battery‐grade quality standards. To further evaluate the economic feasibility of recycling S‐LFP, particle size distribution of the recovered FePO4 and Li3PO4 products was analyzed using laser granulometry in Figure S6. Approximately 90% (by sample volume) of the FePO4 product consisted of particles smaller than 51.9 µm, and 90% (by sample volume) of the Li3PO4 particles were smaller than 53.5 µm, indicating uniform morphology and narrow size distribution. Based on these parameters, the economic benefits of recovering 1 kg of S‐LFP through the designed laboratory‐scale EC reaction were estimated to $21.29 in China and $216.42 in Germany (Figure S7). And as compared with conventional production routes, this recovery process exhibits lower environmental impacts in nearly all impact categories from life cycle assessment (LCA) (Figure S8). These findings highlight the techno‐economic viability of this short‐loop EC recovery strategy for large‐scale industrial applications.

To investigate the influence of free radicals on the Li/Fe‐leaching efficiencies, tert‐butanol (TBA) was used to quench the hydroxyl radicals (•OH). The addition of TBA to the system without H2O2 showed negligible impact on the metal‐leaching efficiencies (Figure 1f). Conversely, in the system with direct H2O2 addition, the leaching efficiencies decreased substantially by 24.66% (Li) and 18.21% (Fe). By comparison, the EC in situ H2O2 generation system demonstrated moderate decreases in leaching efficiency of 11.60% (Li) and 13.61% (Fe). These differences suggest that direct H2O2 addition results in a higher radical‐driven contribution relative to the EC in situ H2O2 generation route. To validate the participation of •OH, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) test was performed on the sample after 20 min of EC reaction in 0.1 M H2SO4, and a clear •OH signal was detected in the spectrum (Figure S9), further confirming its active oxidative role of this radical during the leaching process.

SEM‐EDS images after 30 min of treatment by EC method and direct H2O2 addition method were presented in Figure S10a–c and Figure S10d–f, respectively. It can be clearly observed that considerable leaching holes formed on the material surface of electrochemically in situ generated H2O2 condition. This rapid Li deintercalation induced localized volumetric contraction, generating nanoscale voids throughout the particle matrix, thereby creating diffusion channels that enhanced the subsequent leaching kinetics. In contrast, SEM‐EDS analysis of the S‐LFP subjected to direct H2O2 addition revealed only superficial corrosion, while the bulk structure remained largely intact, suggesting passivation layer formation on the S‐LFP surface that hindered Li and Fe extraction. Figure 1g shows the valence states of Fe on the surface of pristine S‐LFP and S‐LFP treated with different methods determined by XPS. The untreated S‐LFP sample has two main binding energy peaks at 712.3 and 709.9 eV, which correspond to Fe (III) and Fe (II) of Fe 2p3/2. Notably, metallic Fe0 was observed exclusively in the H2O2‐treated sample (S‐LFP + H2O2) and was absent in the electrochemically treated powder (S‐LFP + EC), suggesting particle agglomeration and incomplete lattice decomposition under direct H2O2 conditions.

The chemical states and coordination environments of the Fe sites were further elucidated using XAFS spectroscopy. Fe K‐edge XAFS analysis (Figure 1h) revealed that S‐LFP contains Fe species in a state more reduced than the standard Fe2+ in fresh LFP. The presence of these over‐reduced iron species significantly raises the energy barrier for the Fe2+/Fe3+ oxidation–delithiation step, thereby hindering the liberation of Li+ from the FePO4 framework [37, 38]. As mentioned above, direct H2O2 addition induces a high radical contribution, which oxidizes only the LFP surface and promotes the formation of a passivation layer. Consistent with this behavior, the near‐edge spectra revealed that, compared with untreated S‐LFP, the H2O2‐treated S‐LFP exhibited an inefficient, layer‐by‐layer surface decomposition process. In contrast, the EC, characterized by a lower yet sustained radical contribution, effectively avoids surface passivation. Moreover, the in situ‐generated H2O2 molecules can diffuse more deeply into the particle interior, enabling oxidation throughout the bulk phase. The XAFS results therefore confirm that EAOPs accomplish bulk‐level Fe oxidation, which is crucial for the efficient separation of Li and FePO4.

Extended XAFS (EXAFS) analysis (Figure 1i) provided further insights into the local coordination environment of Fe. The R‐space profiles show nearly identical Fe–O bond lengths in the untreated S‐LFP, H2O2‐treated S‐LFP, and the fresh LFP reference. This indicates that direct H2O2 addition failed to significantly alter the local atomic structure of the cathode, leading to the largely intact LiFePO4 phase which is consistent with its limited Li/Fe separation efficiency. In contrast, the electrochemically treated S‐LFP exhibited a pronounced shift, with its Fe–O bond length shortening substantially and aligning closely with that of the FePO4 reference. This bond‐length shortening provides direct local structural evidence for the formation of FePO4‐like transitional phases within the bulk material, which is a key structural prerequisite for the efficient and rapid separation of Li and FePO4 during the regeneration process.

Fabrication of Fe‐N3P Single‐Atom Catalyst Using Leach Residues

Having established the effective EC recovery of Li and Fe from S‐LFPs, attention was next directed toward the high‐value utilization of the leach residue for fabricating a Fe–N3P single‐atom catalyst. The procedure for synthesizing Fe‐CN3P is shown in Figure 2a. It can be seen that the Fe and P were uniformly distributed in the leaching residue by SEM (Figure S11) and XRD (Figure S12), and no new phase was generated in the ball milling step. During the calcination, the Fe was trapped by the C–N network to form Fe–NX peak, and isolated phosphate (P–O) was also conversed to P─N/P─C bonds (Figure S13). Furthermore, as shown in the Figure S14 of in situ high‐temperature XAFS, no Fe─Fe bond was detected in the leaching residue, further indicating the well‐distribution and no aggregation of Fe. And with the temperature increased, Fe‐N3P coordination was formed on the carbon substrate (mainly previous graphite in the S‐LFP) due to the C2H4N4 and PO4 3− disassociation. SEM (Figure S15a) revealed that Fe‐CN3P product had a porous, multilayered, oval morphology with distinct stacked structures that can enhanced the contact area between the active sites and reactants [39]. In the high resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM) images of Fe‐CNP‐H2O2 from the leach residue with direct H2O2 addition and Fe‐CN3P with electrochemically in situ generated H2O2 (Figure 2b,c, respectively), it can be observed that Fe‐CNP‐H2O2 exhibits granular aggregation, while no significant bulk aggregates are observed for Fe‐CN3P. High‐angle annular dark‐field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF‐STEM, Figure 2d) and EDS analyses of the Fe‐CN3P sample further confirmed the homogeneous distribution of Fe and non‐metallic elements (C, N, and P) on the material surface. Particularly, the inherent P atoms in the leach residue served as effective precursor for the preparation of iron single‐atom catalysts with asymmetric coordination structures. The XRD patterns (Figure S16) showed a broad peak corresponding to the layered stacking structure of graphitic C, with no characteristic metal oxide peaks observed for Fe‐CN3P. By contrast, the XRD spectra of Fe‐CNP‐H2O2, Fe‐C‐EC (electrochemically leached residue directly calcined without dicyandiamide), and Fe‐C‐H2O2 (direct addition of H2O2 method for leaching residue without adding dicyandiamide and direct calcination) exhibited distinct Fe0 signals, indicating that the EC method provides better dispersed Fe species than the direct H2O2 addition technique. XPS analysis (Figure S17b) revealed an Fe–N x peak at 398.6 eV in the N 1s spectrum, confirming successful N doping into the leach residue of S‐LFP. Four distinct peaks at 397.8, 399.9, 401.0, and 404.3 eV corresponded to pyridinic N, pyrrolic N, graphitic N, and oxidized N species, respectively [40]. The Fe 2p spectra of the Fe‐CN3P and Fe‐CNP‐H2O2 samples exhibited characteristic peaks of Fe(II), Fe(III), and satellite species, whereas metallic Fe0 was detected only in Fe‐CNP‐H2O2 (Figure S17c). Additionally, in the P 2p XPS spectra (Figure S17d), both samples exhibited a main peak deconvoluted into two sub‐peaks at 133.1 and 134.0 eV, attributed to P─C and P─N bonds, respectively, confirming the successful incorporation of P atoms into the graphitic carbon matrix [19]. The Fe‐CNP‐H2O2 sample displayed two additional deconvoluted peaks at 129.7 eV, which correlated with the Fe0 peak in its Fe 2p spectrum, suggesting the presence of iron phosphide species.

Furthermore, XAFS spectra were collected to determine the chemical state and coordination environment of single Fe atoms in Fe‐CN3P and Fe‐CNP‐H2O2. The absorption edge position of Fe‐CN3P was located between those of FeO and Fe2O3, suggesting that the valence state of Fe in Fe‐CN3P is between +2 and +3 (Figure 2e). The Fourier‐transform k3‐weighted Fe K‐edge EXAFS spectrum of Fe‐CN3P exhibited two prominent peaks at 1.5 and 1.8 Å corresponding to Fe–N and Fe–P scattering paths, respectively, whereas the spectral pattern of Fe‐CNP‐H2O2 was remarkably similar to that of Fe foil, suggesting the presence of metallic Fe aggregates (Figure 2f) [41]. To further verify these local coordination configurations, quantitative least‐squares curve‐fitting analysis of the first Fe coordination shell was conducted, and the detailed fitting parameters are summarized in Table S1. The optimal fitting results revealed that the Fe active centres in Fe‐CN3P simultaneously possessed Fe–N and Fe–P coordination, confirming the formation of a dual‐ligand asymmetric single‐atom environment (Figure 2g). In combination with STEM‐EDS, XPS, and XAFS analyses, these results confirm Fe‐CN3P hosts atomically dispersed Fe sites coordinated by both N and P (Figure 2g inset), whereas Fe‐CNP‐H2O2 contained aggregated metallic centres that formed nanoparticles. Consequently, as shown in Table S2, this novel recovery approach not only overcome the bottlenecks of high energy/reagents requirement, long process flow and sluggish kinetics in traditional methods, but also upcycled the leaching residues to value‐added Fe‐CN3P catalysts.

Fe‐CN3P Activated PMS for Ultra‐Fast BPA Degradation

To evaluate the PMS activation capabilities of the Fe‐CN3P, Fe‐CNP‐H2O2, Fe‐C‐EC, and Fe‐C‐H2O2 catalysts, their performances in degrading BPA were tested [42]. It should be noted that PMS alone exhibited a weak ability in directly oxidizing BPA, contributing negligibly to the overall reaction. The optimal experimental conditions were determined to be a catalyst dosage of 60 mg/L and a PMS concentration of 0.3 mM (Figure S18). Under these conditions, Fe‐CN3P demonstrated outstanding adsorption capability and completely degraded BPA within 60 s, surpassing those of the other three catalysts (Figures 3a and S19). Specifically, the Fe‐CN3P/PMS system presented a substantially higher pseudo‐first‐order rate constant (10.768 min−1) than that achieved using Fe‐CNP‐H2O2 (5.481 min−1). Given that natural water bodies contain diverse compositions, background substances may affect the pollutant degradation efficiency to varying degrees. Therefore, the effects of common inorganic cations (K+, Na+, Ca2+, and Mg2+) and anions (Cl−, NO3 −, CO3 2−, and HCO3 −) in natural water, and a range of initial solution pH values on the BPA degradation efficiency of the Fe‐CN3P system were also investigated (Figure 3b). Neither the presence of ions nor variations in pH significantly affected BPA degradation due to the stable coordination environment and reactive species of this single atom system [43, 44]. Finally, to explore the long‐term stability of Fe‐CN3P in practical applications, four cycles of experiments (Figure 3c) were conducted, revealing no significant deterioration in the performance of the material over these cycles. Moreover, Fe‐CN3P exhibited extremely low Fe leaching rates during long‐term operation (Table S3). The microstructure and coordination environment also well‐maintained, without any fracture, reconstruction, or new phase generation, as illustrated by XRD (Figure S20) and XPS (Figure S21). These results indicate the suitability of Fe‐CN3P for application in real aquatic environments.

In the Fe‐CN3P/PMS system, the removed BPA was partially mineralized, and thus 60.79% of the total organic carbon (TOC) was eliminated within 1.0 min (Figure S22). The main transformation products of BPA in this system were identified using ultrahigh‐performance liquid chromatography‐coupled quadrupole time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry (Figure S23 and Table S4). An illustration of the possible BPA degradation pathway in the Fe‐CN3P/PMS system was created and is shown in Figure S24 [45]. Various ring‐opening products were detected, which are expected to ultimately mineralize into CO2 and H2O. Additionally, the Ecological Structure–Activity Relationship program was used to predict the acute (short‐term) and chronic (long‐term or delayed) toxicity of BPA and its transformation products (Table S5) [46]. BPA and its transformation products were classified into four toxicity levels (Figure S25) according to the Globally Harmonized System of Classification and Labelling of Chemicals (Table S6). Overall, the toxicity of BPA was substantially reduced after treatment with the Fe‐CN3P/PMS system.

To understand the pollutant degradation capability of Fe‐CN3P after PMS activation, specific ROS generation and contribution toward the system were evaluated using several quenching agents of methanol, TBA, furfuryl alcohol, and L‐histidine [21]. The quenching experiments revealed that BPA was rapidly eliminated within 60 s, even after the introduction of those agents (Figure 3d), indicating the minimal involvement of •OH, SO4 •−, and 1O2 in the oxidation process. However, the addition of 200 mM furfuryl alcohol to the Fe‐CN3P/PMS system completely inhibited BPA degradation, due to the fact that the high concentration of furfuryl alcohol molecules directly reacted with PMS [47]. Besides, the reaction mechanism was investigated using electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy and 5,5‐dimethyl‐1‐pyrroline N‐oxide (DMPO) as a spin‐trapping agent. A typical DMPO–•OH quartet signal was observed, indicating the presence of •OH in this system (Figure S26a), suggesting that although •OH exists, its contribution to pollutant degradation is negligible. The effect of 1O2 on BPA degradation was also tested using 2,2,6,6‐tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP) as the trapping reagent, and weak TEMP‐1O2 triplet signal was detected (Figure S26b). Together, these results suggest that non‐radical pathways predominantly govern the catalytic degradation mechanism, as discussed below.

Since the initial valence states of Fe sites (Fe2+ and Fe3+) determine the characteristics of high‐valent iron–oxo species (e.g., FeIV═O), it is reasonable to propose that PMS can generate FeIV═O species from the Fe2+ coordinated with N and P atoms in the carbon matrix [40]. To further analyze the catalytic activity and surface chemical evolution of Fe‐CN3P during the PMS activation process, in situ Raman spectroscopy was conducted. As shown in Figure 3e, characteristic peaks of the PMS solution emerged at 878 and 1060 cm−1, while the peak at 982 cm−1 is associated with the symmetric stretching vibration mode of S═O bonds in SO4 2− [41]. Therefore, the change in the ratio of the intensity of the peak at 1060 cm−1 to that at 982 cm−1 (I 1060/I 982) can be employed to evaluate the PMS decomposition rate. It can be seen that in the presence of Fe‐CN3P, the I 1060/I 982 ratio decreased from 1.15 to 0.05 within 5 min of reaction. Moreover, a new peak was observed at 843 cm−1 after reaction, which is attributed to the surface‐active Fe species of FeN4 = O [48]. The addition of 10 mM sodium thiocyanate (NaSCN) completely inhibited the degradation of BPA (Figure S27). This is attributed to the strong complexation of SCN− with surface Fe species, which prevents the formation of FeIV═O. As FeIV═O can selectively oxidize PMSO to PMSO2 via an oxygen‐transfer reaction, this reaction served as a probe for detecting the iron–oxo species [49]. Fe‐CN3P exhibited high efficiency in catalyzing the selective oxidation of PMSO to PMSO2, achieving up to 88.73% selectivity, confirming the generation of the reactive FeIV═O species (Figure 3f). However, when 50 µM PMSO was added to the BPA solution, the selectivity of PMSO conversion to PMSO2 decreased significantly to 52.37%. Nevertheless, the BPA degradation efficiency remained high at 79.12%, indicating that FeIV═O primarily contributes to the degradation reaction.

Practical Implication of Upcycled Fe‐CN3P Single Atom

Given the excellent catalytic performance of the Fe‐CN3P/PMS system, it was further tested in practical scenarios using membrane‐immobilized catalysts [20, 50]. The Fe‐CN3P catalyst was deposited onto a commercial polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane and operated in a flow‐through catalytic mode. PMS is activated when flowing through the Fe‐CN3P membrane, achieving rapid degradation of BPA (Figure 4a). The detailed experimental setup is illustrated in Figure S28. During prolonged continuous reaction, the PVDF/Fe‐CN3P system exhibited nearly 100% degradation efficiency for BPA as shown in Figure 4b, whereas the degradation efficiency of the PVDF/Fe‐CNP‐H2O2 system started to decline at 180 min. This decrease was attributed to the aggregation of Fe‐CNP‐H2O2 and Fe° formation, which reduced the number of accessible active sites. Besides, throughout the PVDF/Fe‐CN3P experiments, the removal efficiency of TOC in the effluent consistently remained above 54.61%. As shown in the SEM images of PVDF/Fe‐CN3P before and after the continuous reaction (Figure S29), the PVDF/Fe‐CN3P membrane appeared slightly more compact following reaction. Furthermore, the EDS mapping indicates Fe, N, P, and C well‐retained in the membrane structure (Figure 4c,d). As shown in Figure S30 (HRTEM and XRD) and Figure S31 (XPS) after stability test, the phase, microstructure and coordination environment all well‐maintained, demonstrating significant potential for practical applications in environmental remediation.

Recent studies have shown that Fe‐CN4 single atom catalysts can achieves a pseudo‐first‐order reaction rate of 7.44 min−1 for BPA degradation [51], surpassing other single atom and metal oxide catalysts reported to date (Figure 5a and Table S7). However, the Fe‐CN3P prepared using the P element from S‐LFP itself in this study achieves a much higher reaction rate of 10.681 min−1. It can be attributed to the pseudo‐homogeneous feature in atomic metal‐carbon substrate catalyst system and electronic modulation effect of coordinated N and P [21]. Besides, Fe‐bearing catalyst exhibited better performance as compared to Co and Cu‐bearing ones, due to the appropriate energy for PMS adsorption and products desorption [52]. Consequently, to gain atomic‐level insights into the origin of the enhanced catalytic activity of the S‐LFP derived Fe‐CN3P catalyst, DFT calculations were performed with two representative models: a symmetric Fe‐CN4 structure configuration as the benchmark and a P‐doped Fe‐CN3P configuration to mimic the actual active site [53]. In both models, the O atom adjacent to hydrogen (H) in the peroxide bond of PMS coordinates with Fe to form N4Fe‐OOSO3H complexes. Electron transfer from FeN4 to PMS cleaves the O─O bond, generating the reactive FeIVN4═O intermediate [54]. The PMS adsorption energies (E ads) of the two single‐atom Fe catalysts were calculated, and PMS exhibited stronger binding to the Fe‐CN3P surface (E ads = −3.84 eV) than to Fe‐CN4 (E ads = −2.05 eV), indicating preferential adsorption onto the former catalyst (Figure 5b,c). Charge‐density difference analysis revealed significant electron transfer between the active sites of both catalysts and PMS, with transferred electron amounts (Q) of 0.756 e (Fe‐CN4) and 0.769 e (Fe‐CN3P). This confirms that Fe‐CN3P facilitates more efficient electron transfer to PMS than Fe‐CN4 does, thereby accelerating PMS decomposition into the highly reactive FeIV═O intermediate. It can be identified that the presence of P atoms significantly enhances the catalytic capability of the FeN4 sites through the electronic effects toward the active centres, thereby strengthening the coordination between PMS and Fe‐CN3P, promoting electron transfer from Fe‐CN3P to the adsorbed PMS and further facilitating the formation of more reactive FeIV═O intermediates. The surface electrostatic potentials of the FeIV═O intermediates with adsorbed O on the two types of single‐atom Fe catalysts and the corresponding Bader charges of the reactive O atoms are shown. The Bader charges of the Fe and O atoms in Fe‐CN3P were –1.072 and 0.660 e, respectively, which were higher than those in Fe‐CN4 (Fe: –1.297 e; O: 0.639 e). The Bader charges of both the Fe and O atoms exhibited a positive correlation with the BPA degradation rate constant (k obs) of the corresponding single‐atom Fe/PMS system (Figure 5d). This indicates that P incorporation reduces the electron density of Fe in FeIV═O, thereby enhancing its ability to attract electrons from organic pollutants (i.e., oxidation capability).

Conclusion

In summary, this study develops an effective and integrated pathway for the high‐value recovery of all components from spent batteries (S‐LFPs), while simultaneously offering new insights into the rational design of high‐performance asymmetric coordination single‐atom catalysts. The EC advanced oxidation process efficiently leaches lithium and iron from S‐LFP, and the recovered products FePO4 and Li3PO4 products exhibit high economic potential. The feasibility of this short‐loop strategy at laboratory scale was verified through techno‐economic analysis, highlighting its promise for industrial applications. Importantly, the remaining leach residue was precisely upcycled into a valuable precursor for constructing a P‐modified N‐doped asymmetric Fe single‐atom catalyst (Fe‐CN3P). This synthetic approach is notably simpler and faster than conventional methods for preparing single‐atom catalysts yet achieves exceptional catalytic performance. Both experimental investigations and DFT calculations indicate that the incorporation of P into the coordination environment enhances the catalytic activity of Fe sites by modifying the adsorption mode of HSO5 – on the Fe active centres and increasing the Bader charges of both Fe and active O atoms in the FeIV═O reactive intermediate, thereby strengthening its electron‐withdrawal capability toward BPA molecules. Overall, this dual functionality strategy for metal recovery and high‐value upcycling offers new perspectives for advancing low‐carbon “zero‐waste city” initiatives.
