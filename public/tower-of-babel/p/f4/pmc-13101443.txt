Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease and Kidney Transplant Outcomes
Authors: Seo Yeon Yoon, Samuel El Bouzaidi Tiali, Sara Wing, William Davalan, Sukhdeep Jatana, Lawrence Slapcoff, Roy Nitulescu, Elham Rahme, Meranda Nakhla, Suzanne N. Morin, Giada Sebastiani, Ruth Sapir-Pichhadze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionWe describe the epidemiology of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and evidence of advanced liver fibrosis by readily available noninvasive diagnostics and their implications to kidney transplant recipients (KTRs).MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study of first-time KTRs (2008–2020), we assessed the prevalence and incidence of MASLD (hepatic steatosis on abdominal ultrasound alongside ≥ 1 cardiometabolic risk factors) and advanced hepatic fibrosis (Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index > 2.67). We fitted multivariable time-dependent Cox regression models to assess associations between MASLD and a composite of graft dysfunction (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] < 30 mL/min per 1.73 m2), death-censored graft failure (DCGF), death with graft function (DWGF), and each distinct end point.ResultsIn 650 eligible KTRs (median age 57.2 [interquartile range {IQR}: 45.0–66.0] years, 34% female), prevalence per 100 KTR (95% confidence interval [CI]) at transplantation and 5-years post-transplant, was 21.7 (18.6–25.1) and 42.3 (38.5–46.2), respectively, for MASLD and 42.9 (39.1–46.8) and 69.7 (66.0–73.2), respectively, for advanced fibrosis. Incidence rate per 100 person-years was 5.5 (4.7–6.4) for MASLD and 2.2 (1.6–2.9) for advanced fibrosis in KTRs without MASLD and advanced fibrosis at transplantation, respectively. Adjusted hazard ratios (HR) for the composite, graft dysfunction, DCGF, and DWGF were 1.27 (0.98–1.65), 1.24 (0.86–1.78), 1.14 (0.75–1.73), and 1.56 (1.04–2.33) with versus without MASLD and 1.76 (1.14–2.71), 1.47 (0.71–3.01), 3.28 (1.78–6.06), and 1.68 (0.91–3.13) with FIB-4 > 2.67 versus FIB-4 < 1.3, respectively.ConclusionKTRs with MASLD and advanced hepatic fibrosis demonstrate lower patient and graft survival, which could be amenable to timely interventions for cardiometabolic risk reduction.

Kidney transplantation has markedly enhanced the survival and quality of life of patients with end-stage renal disease.1 Although important developments in techniques and immunosuppressive regimens post-transplantation have improved short- and long-term outcomes,2,3 cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in KTRs.3, 4, 5 Cardiometabolic risk factors like hypertension, obesity, dyslipidemia, and diabetes mellitus are highly prevalent in KTRs,6,7 and long-term immunosuppression (e.g., steroids and calcineurin inhibitors) further increase the risk for metabolic syndrome.8,9

In recent years there has been a growing appreciation of interconnectedness between cardiometabolic risk factors and hepatic steatosis. The identification of shared pathways, including systemic inflammation and insulin resistance gave rise to the diagnosis of MASLD10 (Figure 1). MASLD affects 38% of the global population11 and is defined as steatotic liver disease (≥ 5% hepatic steatosis) with at least 1 cardiometabolic risk factor (i.e., high body mass index, hyperglycemia, hypertension, or dyslipidemia), in the absence of harmful alcohol intake (i.e., amounts < 20 g/day for females and < 30 g/day for males).12, 13, 14 Hepatic steatosis is associated with cardiovascular disease, and regardless of traditional risk factors, such as obesity and diabetes, is also an independent predictor of progressive decline of native kidney function.15, 16, 17Figure 1Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome in transplantation. Commencing with simple steatosis, MASLD may progress to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, represented by inflammation and hepatocellular ballooning.18 The initial stages of MASLD are potentially reversible with lifestyle modification. Yet, MASLD may progress to liver fibrosis, decompensated cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma.18 There is a paucity of knowledge on the epidemiology of MASLD in KTRs and its association with kidney transplant outcomes.19 Given the implications of MASLD to kidney function, cardiovascular morbidity, and mortality in the general population, and the prevalence of cardiometabolic risk factors in KTRs, we sought to assess the burden of MASLD and advanced liver fibrosis by readily available noninvasive biomarkers, and to ascertain their implications for allograft function, graft, and patient survival.

Methods

Study Design and Population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study in first-time kidney-only transplant recipients transplanted at the McGill University Health Centre (MUHC, Montreal, Canada) from January 1st, 2008 to December 31st, 2020. Recipients with multiorgan transplants, liver diseases from other etiologies, including harmful alcohol intake, hepatitis B or C, primary kidney nonfunction, or without pretransplant abdominal ultrasound reports, were excluded. The clinical and research activities being reported are consistent with the Principles of the Declaration of Istanbul as outlined in the Declaration of Istanbul on Organ Trafficking and Transplant Tourism. Ethics approval for retrospective data analysis, waiving the need for informed consent, was obtained from the MUHC research ethics board (2019-5121). This study adheres to the STROBE guidelines.20

Data Sources

Baseline recipient, donor, and transplant characteristics, including comorbidities, habits (e.g., history of pretransplant alcohol intake, distinguishing between alcohol dependency, infrequent social drinking, or none), longitudinal immunosuppression and other medication data, laboratory tests, and abdominal ultrasound reports, as well as outcomes were obtained from the MUHC transplant database and electronic medical records.

Main Exposures

MASLD diagnoses were assigned based on presence of hepatic steatosis on abdominal ultrasounds, and meeting ≥ 1 cardiometabolic adult criteria as follows: body mass index ≥ 25 kg/m2 (or body mass index ≥ 23 kg/m2 in Asian individuals); fasting serum glucose ≥ 5.6 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥ 5.7%, history of type 2 diabetes or treatment for type 2 diabetes; history of hypertension or antihypertensive drug treatment; plasma triglycerides ≥ 1.70 mmol/L; plasma HDL cholesterol ≤ 1.0 mmol/L (males) and ≤ 1.3 mmol/L (females); and history of dyslipidemia or use of lipid lowering treatment.12, 13, 14

Baseline hepatic steatosis was determined from the abdominal ultrasounds routinely conducted in consecutive transplant candidates as part of the pretransplant assessments. These ultrasounds were reported by radiologists from the MUHC as well as other referring dialysis centers. When multiple pretransplant abdominal ultrasound reports were available, ultrasounds performed closest to the date of transplant verified the presence of hepatic steatosis. Post-transplant abdominal ultrasounds were conducted as part of annual screening procedures and reported by radiologists at the MUHC. Hepatic steatosis was defined as the presence of sonographic findings of a bright liver pattern, liver-kidney contrast, deep hepatic attenuation, and/or vascular blurring.21 Severity of hepatic steatosis was classified based on liver echogenicity as follows: mild, indicating a slight and diffuse increase; moderate, indicating a moderate increase; and severe, indicating a marked increase.22 When none of these features were described, hepatic steatosis was deemed absent. When hepatic steatosis was reported to be present but not quantified, severity was deemed indeterminate.

To estimate the risk of advanced liver fibrosis, we used the Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index. FIB-4 is a noninvasive index that considers patient’s age, platelet count, alanine aminotransferase, and aspartate aminotransferase and is the most recommended first-line screening test for estimating MASLD patients’ risk of advanced fibrosis (F3-F4),21 with international societies considering FIB-4 < 1.30, 1.30–2.67, and > 2.67 to exclude, deem indeterminate, and endorse presence of advanced fibrosis, respectively.12,14

Outcome Definitions

In alignment with our hypothetical construct (Figure 1) and the implications of MASLD in the general population, the primary outcome was a composite of advanced chronic graft dysfunction (defined as eGFR by the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation23,24 of <30 mL/min per 1.73m2 sustained for a period of ≥ 3 months), DCGF, defined as return to dialysis or re-transplantation and DWGF. Secondary outcomes were graft dysfunction, DCGF, DWGF and all-cause graft failure (including DCGF and DWGF).

Covariates

We considered recipient, donor, and transplant characteristics as covariates. Recipient characteristics included age, sex, self-reported race and ethnicity, cause of end-stage renal disease, dialysis type, and duration. As body mass index, hyperglycemia, hypertension, and dyslipidemia were already included in the MASLD diagnosis,12,14 these variables were not considered as covariates. Donor characteristics included age, sex, self-reported race and ethnicity, and type (living or deceased). Transplant characteristics included cold-ischemia time, era (by transplant years), human leukocyte antigen mismatch, induction immunosuppression (i.e., lymphocyte depleting [antithymocyte globulin or alemtuzumab] or nonlymphocyte depleting [basiliximab]), and maintenance immunosuppression, which typically included a calcineurin inhibitor (i.e., tacrolimus), antimetabolite (i.e., mycophenolate) with or without prednisone.

Statistical Analysis

Distributions of baseline recipient, donor, and transplant characteristics at transplantation were assessed among KTRs with and without MASLD. Categorical and continuous variables were summarized using proportions and medians with IQRs, respectively. We estimated the prevalence (per 100 KTRs) of MASLD and advanced fibrosis, defined as FIB-4 > 2.67, at transplantation and at 1-, 3-, and 5-years post-transplant. The incidence rate (number of new cases per 100 person-years) of MASLD and advanced fibrosis were assessed among KTRs, who did not have MASLD and those with FIB-4 ≤ 2.67 at transplantation, respectively. We also estimated the cumulative incidence of MASLD and advanced fibrosis at 1-, 3- and 5-years while considering graft failure and death as competing risks. KTRs who did not experience the study end points were censored when lost to follow up or administratively on December 31st, 2021.

To assess associations between the main exposures and the composite end point, we fitted Cox regression models, including the primary exposure (time-dependent MASLD, unadjusted Model 1), MASLD and recipient characteristics (age and sex [Model 2]), Model 2 variables and donor characteristics (sex and type [Model 3]), and Model 3 variables and transplant characteristics (era, cold ischemia time, antigen mismatch, induction and maintenance immunosuppression, including use of prednisone or not [Model 4]). Recipient and donor age were highly correlated. Consequently, only recipient age was included in multivariable models. Since donor sex was missing for 1 KTRs, we did not perform imputation. KTRs were followed until the first event date, including either advanced graft dysfunction, graft failure, or death, with censoring occurring on the date of loss to follow up or December 31, 2021.

We also fitted Cox regression models to assess the association of time-dependent MASLD and advanced fibrosis and each distinct end points of advanced graft dysfunction (censored at graft failure or death), DCGF, and DWGF (censored at graft failure). We similarly assessed associations between the presence versus absence of time-fixed MASLD and advanced fibrosis at transplantation with the primary and secondary end points.

Sensitivity Analyses

We conducted sensitivity analyses to ensure robustness of our observations. First, we fit Cox regression models to assess the association of the composite and distinct end points with the severity of hepatic steatosis at transplantation classified as none (reference), mild, moderate, or severe, and indeterminate. Second, we evaluated the association of transplant outcomes with the presence (vs. absence) of hepatic steatosis on abdominal ultrasounds, independent of cardiometabolic risk factors. Third, we studied the effect of MASLD on transplant outcomes in the subgroup of KTRs who reported abstinence from alcohol pretransplant. Fourth, we studied the effect of MASLD and advanced fibrosis on study end points in the subset of KTRs without history of cystic kidney disease. Finally, we reported on longitudinal models predicting eGFR values by 12-month periods starting from 6 months post-transplantation by MASLD status and FIB-4 categories, capacity to rank participants based on their risk of experiencing study end points earlier by hepatic steatosis and increasing cardiometabolic burden as well as new-onset diabetes post-transplant.25 All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio (version 4.0.2, Posit software, PBC.).

Results

Of the 1066 participants in the cohort, 650 KTRs met the study eligibility criteria (Figure 2, study flow diagram). Baseline characteristics of the study cohort and KTRs not meeting the eligibility criteria are presented in Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1, respectively. The median age of KTRs with MASLD and without MASLD was similar (58.8 years IQR [49.3–67.0] vs. 56.5 IQR [44.3–65.9], respectively). The proportion of male KTRs with MASLD was 72.3% and 64.2% KTRs without MASLD. Diabetes and dyslipidemia at transplantation were present in 63.8% and 68.1% of KTRs with MASLD, respectively, in comparison to 38.7% and 49.3% KTRs without MASLD, respectively. As expected, a higher proportion of KTRs with MASLD were classified as obese (60.3%) in comparison to KTRs without MASLD (27.9%). The intersectionality between MASLD status and cardiometabolic risk factors is presented in Supplementary Figure S1. Most KTRs in the analytic cohort (75.4%) reported no alcohol intake pretransplant. Of those acknowledging alcohol intake, most consumed it socially, and only 3.1% reported history of alcohol dependence (without chronic liver disease diagnosis) at baseline. Nearly all eligible KTRs received a lymphocyte depleting induction immunosuppression (90.2%) alongside baseline maintenance immunosuppression regimens including tacrolimus (99.1%) and mycophenolate (98.8%). Prevalence and Incidence of MASLD and Advanced Fibrosis Risk in Primary KTRs

The prevalence of MASLD at transplantation was 21.7 per 100 KTRs (95% CI: 18.6–25.1). Among those with MASLD at transplantation 40.4%, 26.2%, and 33.3% had mild, moderate to severe, and indeterminate hepatic steatosis, respectively. The prevalence of MASLD per 100 KTRs at 1-, 3-, and 5-years post-transplant was 28.2 (95% CI: 24.7–31.8), 38.8 (95% CI: 35.0–42.6), and 42.3 (95% CI: 38.5–46.2), respectively. The prevalence of advanced hepatic fibrosis per 100 KTRs by FIB-4 index > 2.67 at transplantation, 1-, 3-, and 5-years post-transplant was 42.9 (95% CI: 39.1–46.8), 46.3 (95% CI: 42.4–50.2), 58.0 (95% CI: 54.1–61.8), and 69.7 (95% CI: 66.0–73.2), respectively.

In KTRs without MASLD at baseline, the incidence rate for MASLD was 5.5 (95% CI: 4.7–6.4) per 100 person-years and the cumulative incidence of MASLD by 5 years post-transplant was 29.3 (95% CI: 25.1–33.6). In KTRs without advanced hepatic fibrosis, the incidence rate of FIB-4 > 2.67 per 100 person-years was 2.2 (95% CI: 1.6–2.9) and the cumulative incidence of advanced hepatic fibrosis by 5 years post-transplant was 11.7 (95% CI: 8.3–15). Sensitivity Analyses

Of the KTRs noted to have MASLD at transplantation, 57 (40.4%), 37 (26.2%), and 47 (33.3%) were reported to have mild, moderate to severe, and indeterminate severity of hepatic steatosis on abdominal ultrasound, respectively. Of the KTRs found to have hepatic steatosis on abdominal ultrasound, 3 did not have at least 1 cardiometabolic risk factor and therefore did not meet the criteria for MASLD at transplantation. Of these 3, 1 KTR (0.2%) had mild hepatic steatosis, 1 (0.2%) had moderate to severe hepatic steatosis, and 1 (0.2%) had hepatic steatosis of indeterminate severity. The unadjusted model (Model 1) evaluating the association of MASLD with mild and moderate to severe hepatic steatosis in comparison to no MASLD at baseline demonstrated HRs of 1.38 (95% CI: 0.91–2.10) and 1.55 (95% CI: 0.93–2.58) for the composite end point and HRs of 1.28 (95% CI: 0.66–2.48) and 1.83 (95% CI: 0.84–3.99) for DWGF, respectively. The fully adjusted model (Model 4) demonstrated HRs of 1.23 (95% CI: 0.80–1.89) and 1.15 (95% CI: 0.68–1.93) for the composite end point and HRs of 1.16 (95% CI: 0.58–2.32) and 1.18 (95% CI: 0.53–2.61) for DWGF, respectively. HRs associated with time-fixed severity of hepatic steatosis categories, including indeterminate severity, are presented in Supplementary Table S2. When assessing the effect of hepatic steatosis (independent of presence or burden of cardiometabolic risk factors) on transplant outcomes, we similarly observed an increased risk for the composite end point and DWGF in unadjusted (HRs of 1.45 [95% CI: 1.11–1.88] and 1.42 [95% CI: 0.95–2.13] respectively) and multivariable (HRs of 1.29 [95% CI: 1.00–1.67] and 1.59 [95% CI: 1.06–2.38]) time-dependent regression models. Supplementary Table S3 presents analyses across the study end points, for time-dependent and time-fixed (at transplantation) Cox regression models considering the presence or absence of hepatic steatosis. Time-dependent Cox regression models in subgroup of KTRs, who reported abstinence from alcohol pretransplant, yielded HRs of 1.42 (95% CI: 1.06–1.90) and 1.45 (95% CI: 0.93–2.27) for the composite end point and DWGF end points, respectively, whereas adjusted models estimated HRs of 1.20 (95% CI: 0.90–1.60), and 1.48 (95% CI: 0.94–2.31) for the same end points, respectively (Supplementary Table S4). Similar observations were made in relation to these end points when considering MASLD at transplantation in this subgroup. An analysis excluding KTRs with history of cystic kidney disease confirmed an increased risk of composite end point, DCGF and all-cause graft failure in the presence of FIB-4 index > 2.67 versus < 1.3 (Supplementary Table S5). Models predicting annual eGFR slopes demonstrated that individuals with MASLD at transplantation experienced a more dynamic and ultimately less favorable pattern of kidney function over time compared with those without MASLD. KTRs with FIB-4 < 1.3 demonstrated nonlinear association with kidney function, maintaining stable predicted eGFR, albeit with greater variance in later years, whereas those with FIB-4 of 1.3 to 2.67 demonstrated intermediate decline in the predicted eGFR, and those with FIB-4 > 2.67 exhibited the greatest variability between 3.5 and 5.5 years post-transplantation (Supplementary Figure S2). When assessing the predictive value of cardiometabolic risk factors in addition to hepatic steatosis in informing KTRs’ risk of experiencing the outcomes of interest earlier, we found that the burden of cardiometabolic risk factors allowed better model discrimination (Supplementary Table S6). Finally, new-onset diabetes post-transplant was an important predictor of graft and patient survival (Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study assessing the epidemiology of MASLD in a contemporary Canadian cohort of KTRs, we observed that MASLD, including hepatic steatosis and often multiple simultaneous cardiometabolic risk factors, was present in a quarter of primary KTRs at transplantation, with prevalence exceeding 40% by 5 years post-transplant. Using FIB-4 as the recommended first-line screening test for advanced hepatic fibrosis, we found that over 40% of KTRs were at risk for advanced fibrosis at the time of transplantation with prevalence exceeding 60% by 5 years post-transplant. MASLD was associated with a 56% increase in the relative hazard of DWGF and risk of advanced liver fibrosis was associated with a 76% increase in the relative hazard of experiencing the composite end point and a 3-fold higher risk for graft failure. Given its prognostic significance, efforts to prevent, detect, and reverse MASLD among transplant candidates and recipients are of foremost importance.

We found the prevalence of MASLD surpassed the estimated burden in the general population globally (38%).11 In a small case series of KTRs from Croatia, Mikolasevic et al.26 reported that the prevalence of MASLD exceeded 50% of KTRs when using transient elastography to detect and quantify hepatic steatosis. More recently, in a small case series of Turkish KTRs, using the same diagnostic modality, Yesil et al.27 reported that the prevalence of MASLD exceeded 40%. Abdominal ultrasounds are particularly adept at detecting moderate or severe hepatic steatosis (fat infiltration exceeding 30%).28 Although abdominal ultrasounds are routinely preformed for pretransplant evaluation and post-transplant monitoring (e.g., for vascular assessment and malignancy screening), transient elastography with controlled attenuation parameter is considered to have better diagnostic performance for mild hepatic steatosis, with reported sensitivity and specificity when compared with the gold standard, liver biopsy, of 87% and 91% for transient elastography,29 and approximately 82% and 80%, respectively, for abdominal ultrasound.30 The burden of MASLD among KTRs may be, thus, more pronounced than detectable by abdominal ultrasounds. Interestingly, the prevalence of MASLD associated with FIB-4 > 2.67 was higher, suggesting that FIB-4, which is used in the general population to justify evaluation by transient elastography in consultation with hepatology,31 could be readily incorporated into transplant care pathways.

To our knowledge, this is the first report on the epidemiology of MASLD in primary KTRs, using readily available noninvasive biomarkers and imaging, and their association with long-term outcomes. Our study demonstrated that among KTRs, MASLD was associated with mortality and the burden of cardiometabolic risk factors, in addition to hepatic steatosis, which was prognostically important. These findings align with previous reports demonstrating increased all-cause mortality risk in the presence of metabolic abnormalities32 and, particularly, type 2 diabetes mellitus,33,34 which was also observed in our sensitivity analyses. Our study demonstrated that FIB-4 > 2.67 was associated with an increased risk for the composite end point and graft failure. In the general population, hepatic fibrosis is the most important predictor of mortality in MASLD.35 For example, in a multicenter cohort of patients demonstrating the full histologic spectrum of MASLD, Sanyal et al.36 observed that advanced fibrosis was associated with an increased risk of death. Similarly, a systematic review and meta-analysis including more than 4000 MASLD patients, demonstrated 3-fold increase in the unadjusted relative risk of all-cause mortality, an 11-fold increase in the relative risk of liver-related mortality, and increased risk for liver-related morbidity, as a function of the severity of biopsy-confirmed fibrosis.37 In our study, MASLD-associated advanced fibrosis was also associated with an increased risk for graft failure. In the general population, MASLD has been linked both to the development and progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD). A meta-analysis by Musso et al.,38 including approximately 30,000 participants, showed that MASLD was associated with a 2-fold increase in the prevalence of CKD, and MASLD severity was correlated with CKD severity. Furthermore, CKD prevalence and incidence were higher among patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis in comparison to those with simple steatosis.38 When predicting eGFR trajectories, we observed more dynamic and ultimately less favorable pattern of kidney function among KTRs with MASLD and higher FIB-4. As creatinine based GFR estimations in the context of evolving liver disease may be inaccurate,39,40 future studies should rely on cystatin C-based GFR estimation.41 Importantly, these findings suggest that, other than immune-mediated injury, MASLD is an important determinant of graft dysfunction and failure in KTRs.42,43

The rising prevalence of MASLD over the post-transplant course suggests that, in addition to cardiometabolic risk factors, nontraditional factors, specific to KTRs, may inform the incidence and progression of an additional comorbidity—chronic liver disease. As steroids and calcineurin inhibitors are known to increase the risk of metabolic syndrome,8,9 modification of immunosuppression (e.g., tacrolimus minimization and steroid-sparing regimens) in KTRs with high FIB-4 indices, may be considered. Screening by FIB-4, followed by transient elastography, may help identify patients who could benefit from lifestyle changes (e.g., weight loss, dietary modification, physical exercise, and diminished alcohol consumption), incretin-based therapies (for diabetes or obesity), and/or bariatric surgery (for individuals with MASLD and obesity). Future studies may also assess the role of resmetirom,12 a thyroid hormone receptor-beta agonist, for the management of KTRs with MASLD and noncirrhotic fibrosis.

This is the largest contemporary cohort of primary KTRs with extended follow-up, allowing the study of the epidemiology and outcomes of MASLD using noninvasive diagnostic modalities routinely available pre- and post-transplant, which were conducted for reasons other than MASLD diagnosis. Despite these advantages, the limitations should also be noted. First, our findings represent observations from a single tertiary transplant center serving a diverse North American patient population and operating within a publicly funded healthcare system. Consequently, our observations require external validation. Second, although most ultrasounds were conducted at a single tertiary center with expertise in MASLD, given multiple radiologists read the ultrasounds, detection of hepatic steatosis may be subject to interobserver variability.44,45 Importantly, we find that FIB-4, an alternative noninvasive tool, which is less vulnerable to interobserver variability, and is recommended by international societies for screening for MASLD-associated liver fibrosis, was also associated with less favorable transplant outcomes. Third, the quantity of alcohol intake had not been quantified in a detailed fashion. Importantly, our subgroup analysis excluding KTRs with any history of alcohol intake suggests that it is MASLD rather than metabolic dysfunction-associated and alcohol-associated liver disease or alcohol-associated liver disease that informs the outcomes observed. HR estimates suggest an increased risk for the study end points as a function of MASLD and FIB-4. Yet our analyses may be underpowered to ascertain associations across the end points studied. This can be examined prospectively in larger multicenter cohorts carefully monitoring the alcohol intake, noninvasive biomarkers (e.g., FIB-4) of KTRs, followed by transient elastography while considering longitudinal changes to immunosuppression (informing risks of opportunistic infections and/or rejection), and interventions for cardiometabolic risk reduction.46 Finally, as with any observational study, our analyses may be vulnerable to residual confounding.

In conclusion, our study provides insights into the epidemiology and prognostic implications of MASLD in a contemporary cohort of primary KTRs, demonstrating increased all-cause mortality risk and increased graft failure risk with FIB-4 > 2.67. Because of its reversibility, we do not believe that MASLD should preclude candidates from accessing transplantation. Rather, screening using the FIB-4 index and transient elastography, and timely interventions to address traditional cardiometabolic risk factors, should be promoted to prevent progression of liver fibrosis, and its implications to the renal allograft, liver disease, and overall health of KTRs. Further studies in multicenter cohorts and clinical trials are warranted to determine how modified immunosuppression regimens may mitigate these risks.
