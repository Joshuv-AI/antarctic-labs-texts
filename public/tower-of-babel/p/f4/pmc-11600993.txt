Education program for caregivers in congenital central hypoventilation syndrome: Initiation and experience families
Authors: Delphine Micaelli, Zina Ghelab, Christophe Delclaux, Caroline Artu‐Dumont, Maxime Patout, Fleur Le Bourgeois, Stéphane Dauger, Plamen Bokov, Benjamin Dudoignon
Published: 2024

ETHICS STATEMENT

Ethical approval was obtained from our local Ethical Committee for collecting data from this cohort (CEER‐629‐2022) and consent obtained from parents.

To the editor,

Congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (CCHS) is a rare disease caused by PHOX2B mutation characterized by a severe hypoventilation necessitating lifelong home mechanical ventilation (MV) as invasive ventilation by tracheostomy or noninvasive ventilation (NIV). 1 To ensure a safe transition from hospital to home, parents and caregivers must be trained to use home ventilators. Therapeutic education programs (TEP) exist for tracheostomy and most program are taught by nurses as the principal educators. 2 Recommendations have recently emerged for the type of equipment to use and for the management of these patients. Parental education is critical for children to live safely, and in 2016 the American Thoracic Society released a guideline for pediatric chronic invasive ventilation that includes educational objectives and professional caregivers. 3 This guideline describes the knowledge and skills required to act in an emergency situation and how to use monitors and ventilators properly. TEP are well established and regulated in France with set criteria, and each program is submitted to the regional health agency. Rare programs are available for NIV as mentioned in the last ERS statement. 4 Some teams used the informational booklet for pediatric long‐term ventilation support as a resource to improve patient and family knowledge 5 and others teams suggested that simulation and training programs should become increasingly widespread in medical education. 6 CCHS is a complex disorder with multiple presentations and symptoms. Parents need to be ready to deal with different emergency situations to take care of their children. Thus, parents must understand the disease and be able to use the ventilator and the interface perfectly. They must be able to contact the pediatric pulmonologist or intensivist if needed. A comprehensive TEP is therefore essential to acquire these skils. The aim of this preliminary study is to describe how the TEP was set up for CCHS, to have the satisfaction and perception of families with the implementation of the TEP. The secondary objectives are to identify barriers and levers to the implementation of this TEP.

In France, TEP is a healthcare practice that is an integral part of the care provided to patients with chronic diseases, and it benefits from a precise framework enshrined in law. TEP is a process of improving patients' skills (or the skills of those around them) to take care of themselves, manage their chronic diseases and maintain their quality of life. This practice is complementary to the medical treatment and care provided to patients. The process of implementing therapeutic education takes into account the specific needs of each individual. It is implemented in four steps: assessment of the patient's needs (diagnosis); co‐construction of educational objectives (action plan); implementation of the program with individual sessions or group workshops; and an assessment of the patient's skills.

In 2018, our team began thinking about designing a TEP for patients with CCHS and their families. In 2020, we began by analyzing patients' needs through semi‐structured interviews with six families. These interviews covered a number of aspects, including the families' perceptions of TEP, the initial period in hospital, the return home and the families' day‐to‐day lives. After several months of collegial work and meetings, an initial TEP examining the competences and skills (Table 1) to develop during training sessions was set up at Robert Debré Hospital in June 2022. Sessions included pathophysiology, video, simulation training and role play.

A questionnaire was designed with the team of researchers and program participants. The survey was distributed from 10 May, 2023 to 01 July 2024 by e‐mail with an information note for parents. Parents of children diagnosed with CCHS who had engaged in the patient TEP either during their initial phase in the intensive care unit or upon returning home were eligible to participate.

With regard to the perception of the experience of the program and the feedback, the use of questions with answers on a Likert‐type scale from 1 to 5, with 1 representing “not at all” and 5 “completely,” was chosen to gather the opinions and the levels of satisfaction of the parents. A “comment” item was included at the end of the questionnaire so that participants could make any comments or suggestions they wished.

Patients' demographic and clinical characteristics were gathered from electronic medical records. Ethical approval was obtained from our local Ethical Committee for collecting data from this cohort (CEER‐629‐2022).

The qualitative variables were shown using numerical values and percentages while the quantitative variables were represented by their median and interquartile range [Min; Max].

CHILDREN AND FAMILY CHARACTERISTICS

The median age of CCHS diagnosis was 1.8 [0.7; 65.9] months. The median duration of hospital stay was 3.2 [1.6; 7.6] months. Five out of seven (71%) of the children had NIV at home, while 2/7 (29%) had invasive ventilation via a tracheostomy. One patient was tracheostomized at 5 months after a first return home with NIV. Six out of seven (85%) children lived at home and only one child lived in a center for young children (Table 2). Parents' median age was 36, with a minimum to maximum range of 21 and 47, respectively. Eight out of nine (89%) were in a couple and one out of nine (11%) was separated. The median number of education sessions was 7 [5; 26] for a median training time of 6.5 [5; 34.5] hours.

DATA QUESTIONNAIRE

Seven patients were diagnosed with CCHS, followed by the national reference center and included in the study. Among 14 parents eligible for the survey, 9/14 (64%) responded. The TEP was carried out entirely by the program's creative team, and the nine parents felt that it met their needs and those of their child. One parent said that “the program was also tailored to the problems identified.” Six out of nine (67%) parents found that the educational sessions offered were sufficiently close together, while 7/9 (78%) said that the length of these sessions was very appropriate, with only one parent finding the length of the educational sessions “too long” and another “too short.” In the end, 8/9 (89%) of the parents felt that the program was appropriate and progressive, with 4/9 (44%) of the sessions taking place face‐to‐face, 4/9 (44%) face‐to‐face combined with distance learning and one parent receiving the entire program via distance learning. Six out of nine (67%) parents felt very comfortable with the management of ventilatory support at home. All the parents felt comfortable when they returned home, including 5/9 (56%) who felt very comfortable. Three out of nine (33%) parents felt that the program met their expectations, providing them with knowledge and skills, help, a listening ear and support in preparing for the return home, and 2/9 (22%) said they felt “reassured” and “confident.” From the parents' point of view, 8/9 (89%) of the program's levers were the instructors, with one parent stating: “Everything was consistent in terms of learning, whether it was the length of the sessions, the locations of the sessions, the instructors, it was perfect. The timetable was very well organized. The equipment used was totally appropriate for the situation.” For parents who have benefited from the therapeutic education program, it is a lever for the return home: “a springboard towards discharge,” “excellent preparation for the return home.” It also helps parents to understand the syndrome and acquire care skills: “This program has helped to improve our understanding of CCHS, because our lives will be organized around our son, to ensure his development and well‐being.”

One parent told us: “The program is nothing but happiness, because thanks to it I've been able to learn to understand and also to train myself about my son's pathology and his tracheotomy. Without this program, I think I'd still be in hospital.”

The main barriers shared by the participants were the availability of the teaching team (n = 2), with one parent expressing the view that the team was “overwhelmed,” and a lack of coordination between the teaching team and the care team in the field: “More coordination between the teaching team and the child's healthcare workers, such as what parents can do, and what practices they need support with. Perhaps put in place operational educational referents among the healthcare workers, i.e., those who assess the parents.” One parent told us that taking part in the program was a reflection of their child's difficult medical situation: “It reminds us of what we're going to go through and that scares us.”

Our TEP is based on the needs of CCHS families and they were satisfied with the program. Caregiver education plays a pivotal role in ensuring safe and effective home care. Parents of children who required tracheostomies had more sessions (vs. NIV) due to the complexity of the care involved in our preliminary study. One of the most significant limitations is the small number of trainees and this TEP aims to be shared with more families and implemented more broadly. TEP to support families are emerging and allow them to return home with peace of mind. It is possible to set up and deploy a TEP focused on a rare disease and home MV, but the time needed to deploy it is long and it requires an experienced team.
