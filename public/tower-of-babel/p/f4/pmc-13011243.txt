WDEIA case series: Do unique clinical presentations suggest mechanistic differences?
Authors: Srividya Sridhara, Omar Diab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Wheat-dependent exercise-induced anaphylaxis (WDEIA), is a rare but potentially life-threatening entity, presenting with anaphylaxis when wheat ingestion is accompanied by an augmenting cofactor, most commonly exercise. WDEIA is the most common variant of food-dependent exercise-induced anaphylaxis (FDEIA) and is often delayed in diagnosis. It is seen primarily among adults and adolescents. Omega-5 gliadin (rTri a 19) is reported as the key allergen component. The pathophysiology of WDEIA is not fully understood. The goal of this study was to take a closer look into the clinical presentation, diagnosis, management of WDEIA and propose potential mechanistic insights.

A retrospective chart review of patients diagnosed with WDEIA between 2017 and 2024 was performed in our outpatient allergy-immunology practice in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Its clinical presentation, diagnosis, management, and clinical course were analyzed. The cohort consisted of 7 patients, aged 21 to 46 years (6 male and 1 female). Patients with less than 12 months of follow-up were excluded from the study.

Results and discussion

As expected with WDEIA, all patients tolerated exercise or other cofactors and wheat independently, although they all reported random episodes of intermittent urticaria that were correlated with wheat ingestion and resolved with oral antihistamines. During the anaphylactic episode, the most common symptom was systemic urticaria (100%), followed by severe cardiovascular symptoms.

All patients had multiple (2-5) episodes of severe/Brown grade III anaphylaxis before presentation. Predominant cardiovascular symptoms (including loss of consciousness) with minimal respiratory involvement was noted in all. Loss of consciousness was noted in all 7 patients and observed in almost all of the anaphylactic events, except in 1 patient in whom it was noted in 2 of the 3 total episodes. Two patients endorsed having mild respiratory symptoms limited to chest tightness and mild dyspnea. Of these 2 patients, the patient with asthma had respiratory involvement during 3 of 5 anaphylaxis episodes, and the patient without asthma had respiratory involvement during only 1 of 3 anaphylaxis episodes. None of the patients had laryngeal edema.

The severity of the anaphylactic shock appeared consistent across all patients regardless of the intensity of exercise and the different cofactors (alcohol, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and menstruation). Exercise was the most common cofactor in 7 of 7 patients. Across all patients, when wheat was ingested within 6 hours prior, symptom onset was delayed by another 4 to 6 hours after cofactors had been encountered, except in the case of exercise, when it was within 30 minutes after exercising. Severe anaphylaxis 2 hours after ingestion of 125 grams of wheat (by a patient tolerant of 25 grams) in the absence of a cofactor was noted in 1 of 7 patients (after the patient had inadvertently consumed a food product mistaken for a wheat-free option).

All 7 patients were atopic; 7 of 7 had allergic rhinitis and/or conjunctivitis, 1 of 7 had asthma, and 2 of 7 also had immediate-onset food allergies (shellfish and peanut). None had pollen food syndrome or other relevant comorbidities, and none was taking β-blockers or angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors. Baseline serum tryptase and thyroid-stimulating hormone levels were within normal limits in all 7 patients. Serum tryptase was not drawn during or soon after any episode of anaphylaxis. No correlation between omega-5 gliadin, wheat, or gluten IgE levels and the severity of anaphylaxis was noted (Table I). Wheat/gluten–specific IgG testing was not performed at the time of evaluation, as it was not part of standard food allergy diagnostics.

Summary of baseline characteristics, clinical presentation, and outcomes (n = 7)

LOC, Loss of consciousness.

Multiple episodes of isolated urticaria were defined as a minimum of 5 episodes. These were especially hard for the patients to quantify, given their mild nature and longer duration of occurrence.

For patients 5, 6, 7 at the time of their evaluation, the omega-5 gliadin IgE test was not readily available at commercial laboratories in the United States, and they had chosen not to test when it became available.

All patients were prescribed epinephrine autoinjectors. In all patients, a wheat-free diet prevented both intermittent urticaria and anaphylaxis, whereas wheat separation from exercise and cofactors by 6 hours prevented only anaphylaxis and not intermittent urticaria. Although chronic spontaneous urticaria has been reported with FDEIA, in our study intermittent urticaria seemed to be fully related to wheat ingestion, as strict avoidance of wheat helped prevent the urticarial episodes in addition to anaphylaxis.

This is the first study highlighting the differential effectiveness of the 2 preventive strategies, with complete wheat avoidance helping to avoid intermittent urticaria and anaphylaxis. This finding needs further validation in larger studies.

In classic immediate-onset food allergy, the smaller dose of allergen binding to the high-affinity FCεR1 of IgE can trigger the allergic reaction, ranging from a milder reaction to severe anaphylaxis. Although classic IgE-mediated reactions can be potentiated by cofactors, they exhibit no dependency on cofactors or dose of the food allergen in the occurrence of the allergic reaction. In contrast, in FDEIA and WDEIA, the onset of severe allergic reaction is predominantly delayed in nature until a cofactor is encountered. Patients with FDEIA and WDEIA rarely experience anaphylaxis in the absence of a cofactor, and when severe reactions occur without cofactors, dose dependency is significantly evident.

Our current understanding is that food-directed IgG, especially IgG4, is associated with acquisition of tolerance (natural or oral immunotherapy–induced) inhibiting the IgE-mediated reaction.1

In WDEIA, the most common outcome of wheat ingestion in the absence of cofactors is no allergic reaction at all, with IgG playing a dominant inhibitory role. The occasional urticaria, which was likely wheat induced (ie, fully prevented with strict wheat avoidance in our study patients), in the absence of noticeable cofactors can be explained solely by IgE-mediated reactions. If there were any potential cofactors present in these situations at all, they were not obvious to patients or the physician, and the only commonality was wheat ingestion in amounts that did not seem different from the patients’ usual tolerated doses.

Once a cofactor is encountered, the reaction tends to be severe. There is evidence to support the idea that cofactors help increase the levels of food allergens in circulation2,3 (Fig 14,5). It is proposed that with an increase in the level of circulating allergen, there is increased binding and cross-linking of IgE, leading to more mast cell and basophil degranulation. However, a high level of circulating allergen is not needed for occurrence of the allergic reaction if the reaction is entirely IgE mediated owing to the high affinity of FCεR1, as is clearly the case in classic food allergy. Can this potentially be explained with IgG cross-linking and activation of other effector cells in the presence of excess antigen levels to cause or trigger a more severe reaction in FDEIA and WDEIA? Key immunology literature explores murine IgG-mediated anaphylaxis to draw connections and theorize the role of human IgG in hypersensitivity responses, particularly in addressing the gaps in which IgE alone does not fully explain the clinical presentation.4,6,7 IgG could have both activating and inhibitory roles depending on the dose of antigen and allergen-specific IgG.4,7 In IgG-mediated anaphylaxis, a higher antigen level is required, given the low affinity of the FCγRs.4,6,7 IgG-based hypersensitivity has been proposed in the context of parenteral medications and infusions, including intravenous immunoglobulin. The sudden and severe allergic reactions in this situation are theorized to be related to the sudden increase in the levels of circulating allergen triggering the immune complex formation with IgG and activating macrophages, monocytes, and neutrophils, leading to the production of platelet-activating factor, which is deemed to be the primary mediator in IgG hypersensitivity reactions.6,7 The need for a higher antigen load to cause the allergic reaction in WDEIA and FDEIA makes the case for a potential IgG-induced hypersensitivity. In addition, sudden and predominant cardiovascular involvement in WDEIA also supports preferential production of platelet-activating factor over histamine, likely supporting role of IgG (Fig 2).8,9 In a WDEIA provocation study,10 a significant elevation in serum tryptase above baseline was observed in only 13.6 % of challenges involving gluten combined with exercise, suggesting that mast cell and/or basophil activation might play only a partial role in WDEIA. In contrast, immediate-onset food-induced anaphylaxis caused by peanut challenge in a comparable study setting resulted in a much higher level of tryptase elevation over baseline in 62.5% of patients.11

Exercise, alcohol, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) increase gastric permeability, leading to larger intact allergens in circulation. Specifically, during exercise, increases in the production of cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and reactive oxygen species (ROSs) have been linked with the activation of tissue transglutaminase (tTg). Severe or prolonged exercise might cause decreased splanchnic blood flow, intestinal ischemia, and cellular damage, leading to increased gut permeability. tTg activation cross-links gliadins together, forming larger complexes, and it can embed itself within the complex, leading to increased circulatory intact allergen. Exercise also increases IL-1 production in endothelial cells and IL-6, which are shown to be a platelet-activating factor–acetylhydrolase (PAF-AH) inhibitor and stimulate PAF production in neutrophils, respectively. Furthermore, gliadin contains epitopes that are highly resistant to gastric, pancreatic, and intestinal proteolytic digestion in the gastrointestinal tract, escaping degradation in the human gut; it possesses cytotoxic properties that can compromise the integrity of tight junctions.4 In addition, Caputo et al5 showed that the gliadin peptides penetrate the cells and increase the calcium release, leading to activation of tTG.

Summary of the proposed mechanism for WDEIA in a classic IgE-mediated reaction. The allergen bound to specific IgE antibodies generates cross-linking of the high-affinity IgE receptor FcεRI on mast cells (MCs)/basophils (BASO), triggering an activating protein tyrosine kinase signaling cascade starting with the receptor proximal kinase Syk. This results in the immediate release of preformed or synthesized granules. A, In the absence of cofactors during wheat ingestion, IgG could be inhibiting MC degranulation via 2 mechanisms: epitope masking and receptor-mediated inhibition. Epitope masking occurs when IgG binds to the antigen covering immunodominant epitopes. Receptor-mediated inhibition involves the concurrent engagement of an allergen by FcεRI-bound IgE and FcγRIIB/CD32B-bound IgG, activating SHIP1/SHIP2, which in turn inhibits the cascade of MC degranulation. B, When the reaction is limited to urticaria, IgG inhibition is unable to fully prevent the antigen from binding to IgE FcεRI, leading to some MC degranulation (histamine). C, In the presence of a cofactor along with wheat ingestion, IgG takes up an activating role, leading to anaphylaxis or compounding the IgE-mediated reaction via platelet-activating factor (PAF) production from cells expressing FcγRs via FcγRI, FcγRIIA, FcγRIIC, FcγRIIIA, and FcγRIIIB, including macrophages (Mp), monocytes (Mono), Neutrophils (Neut), and eosinophils (Eos).

We urge further research to potentially understand the combined role of the food IgG- and IgE-mediated pathophysiologic mechanisms in delayed-onset food allergy reactions, including FDEIA, alpha-gal, and lipid transfer protein anaphylaxis.
