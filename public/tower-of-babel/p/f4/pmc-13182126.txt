Disseminated histoplasmosis presenting as primary adrenal insufficiency in an immunocompetent host in the United States: a case report
Authors: Gaurav Kumar, Ibrahim Zahid, Corey Lager, Benjamin S Avner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHistoplasma capsulatum is a dimorphic fungus that is mostly seen in immunocompromised individuals. Its presentation ranges from acute pulmonary infections to disseminated disease that can affect multiple organ systems, including the adrenal glands. Dissemination is uncommon in immunocompetent hosts, but involvement of the adrenal glands is largely reported in those with a competent immune system. Subsequent primary adrenal insufficiency is a rare finding, especially in the United States with only two such cases reported before.Case presentationWe report the case of 62-year-old immunocompetent Caucasian man with a history of exposure to farm animals and bats who was referred to the emergency department for weight loss, fatigue, decreased appetite, and hyponatremia. Computed tomography (CT) imaging demonstrated bilateral adrenal thickening with no focal nodules. Low serum cortisol, elevated adrenocorticotropic hormone, and an absent cortisol response to cosyntropin confirmed primary adrenal insufficiency. Biopsy of the adrenal gland revealed caseating granulomas and fungal elements consistent with Histoplasma species. Multiple scattered pulmonary nodules were also observed on chest CT, though there were no accompanying pulmonary symptoms. The patient was initiated on isavuconazole for one year with the treatment currently underway.ConclusionClinicians should consider histoplasmosis in immunocompetent individuals with primary adrenal insufficiency and negative 21-hydroxylase antibodies. Immunosuppression and pulmonary symptoms should not be considered necessary prerequisites for it to be a differential diagnosis.Keywords: Disseminated histoplasmosis, Histoplasma, Fungal infection, Mycosis, Immunocompetent host, Addison’s disease

Background

Histoplasma capsulatum, a dimorphic fungus, is endemic to North America and Central America, particularly the Ohio and Mississippi River valleys [1], as well as parts of Asia and Africa [2]. This organism thrives in soil enriched with nitrogen, often from birds and bats excrement. Inhalation of Histoplasma capsulatum spores produces a yeast form in the human body that is taken up by alveolar macrophages to produce an acute infection whose manifestations are most often purely pulmonary, but may also disseminate, with widely varying symptoms in either case [2].

Histoplasma capsulatum is predominantly seen in individuals with underlying immune suppression, including those with untreated human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, autoimmune disorders, cancer, prior transplant history, or using immunomodulatory drugs [3]. Cases in immunocompetent hosts are not commonly encountered in routine practice, unless there is significant environmental source exposure [4]. Acute infections are typically asymptomatic and self-limiting. However, immunosuppressed individuals and those at the extremes of age [1, 4] are susceptible to disseminated disease, which can affect almost any organ system such as the lungs, reticuloendothelial system, central nervous system, gastrointestinal tract, kidneys, and adrenal glands [5].

Interestingly, dissemination of Histoplasma to the adrenal glands is notably reported in immunocompetent individuals [6, 7]. It does not always cause primary adrenal insufficiency (PAI), which is a rare complication occurring in less than 10% of cases [8] and is the most common cause of death in patients with disseminated Histoplasma infection [9]. Of prior cases of bilateral adrenal histoplasmosis in immunocompetent hosts in the United States (US) [10–14], only two had concomitant PAI to the best of our knowledge. Of these, one [13] had recent travel to Bangladesh, while the other patient did not survive [14]. Nevertheless, when it comes to adrenal abscesses, Histoplasma capsulatum is the most common cause, according to a systematic review published in 2023 [15]. Majority of adrenal abscesses occur through secondary dissemination of an infectious focus, and adrenal insufficiency develops in approximately 46% of patients with adrenal abscesses [15].

This case is an uncommon presentation of PAI due to disseminated histoplasmosis in the US, emphasizing the importance of considering histoplasmosis as a differential in those presenting with primary adrenal insufficiency.

Case presentation

A 62-year-old Caucasian male retired machinist from rural Michigan was referred to the emergency department (ED) by his primary care physician due to complaints of fatigue, decreased appetite, and 30-pound weight loss over the past year, and a serum sodium level of 124 mEq/L (reference level: 135–145 mEq/L). He reported worsening energy levels and appetite over the last few weeks, accompanied by activity-induced shortness of breath, an intermittent throat-clearing cough, early satiety, and nausea. He did not report any abdominal pain, fever, chills, night sweats, or significant smoking history.

His medical history included coronary artery disease (status post-coronary artery bypass surgery 1.5 years prior), hyperlipidemia, Raynaud’s disease, and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) without any workup during a prior hospitalization. He lived on a farm with various animals (dogs, cats, horses, goats) and had ongoing exposure to a bat-infested barn that had been difficult to clear.

Physical examination was remarkable for orthostatic hypotension, pallor of the eyelid mucosa, prolonged capillary refill, and right lower quadrant abdominal discomfort. Blood work showed normal hemoglobin of 14.2 g/dL, elevated serum thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) of 9.04 µU/mL (reference level: 0.4-4.0 µU/mL) for which he had started levothyroxine 50 mcg two days prior to his admission, elevated liver transaminases (aspartate aminotransferase 129 U/L, alanine aminotransferase 141 U/L alkaline phosphatase 203 U/L, and leukopenia (white cell count of 2.4 × 109/L). Results of Hepatitis C and HIV serology were negative. Serum vitamin D level was 30 ng/mL, erythrocyte sedimentation rate was 3 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein was 15.8 mg/dL. A computerized tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen & pelvis revealed splenomegaly with a small lateral hypo-enhancing lesion, colonic diverticulosis, and severe bilateral adrenal thickening without any focal nodules. The right and left adrenal glands measured up to 14 mm and 9 mm in thickness respectively (Figs. 1 and 2). Suspicion of PAI was confirmed with low serum cortisol, elevated serum adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) of > 600 pg/mL (reference: 10–60 pg/mL), and absent cortisol response (5.7 to 6.2 mcg/dL) on ACTH stimulation test. Treatment with intravenous fluids, hydrocortisone 30 mg daily and fludrocortisone 0.1 mg daily raised his sodium levels to around 135 mEq/L and completely resolved his symptoms, permitting discharge from the hospital.

Endocrinology workup of 21-hydroxylase (21-OH) levels was unremarkable but repeat CT imaging of abdomen/pelvis two months later redemonstrated prominent bilateral adrenal thickening with an increase in left adrenal size to 13 mm. To distinguish between an immune etiology (Addison’s disease), infectious condition, or malignancy (lymphoma), a CT-guided biopsy of the left adrenal gland was performed (after ruling out pheochromocytoma biochemically) which revealed caseating granulomatous inflammation. Kinyoun stain for acid-fast organisms and CK OSCAR stain for malignancy were negative, but Grocott-Gomori’s methenamine silver (GMS) stain yielded several fungal elements morphologically consistent with Histoplasma species (Fig. 3). Serum antibodies against Histoplasma were positive, with a positive M-band and a complement fixation titer of 1:256. Furthermore, CT chest was positive for multiple scattered bilateral pulmonary nodules, measuring up to 4 mm on the right and 8 mm on the left, with the latter being partially calcified (Fig. 4). He did not have any history of being prone to infections in the past, but according to the patient his wife also had a ‘spot’ on her lung at some point, the biopsy of which revealed fungal elements. We concluded that histoplasmosis was the most likely diagnosis, an assessment that would not have been affected by whether we could demonstrate Histoplasma antigen in the patient’s urine or serum, so those tests were deferred.

The patient was initiated on isavuconazole 200 mg (every 8 h for two days as a loading dose followed by daily dosing) for at least one year, with follow-up monthly LFTs and a repeat chest CT scan at 12 months. Due to loss of access to isavuconazole after 7 months of treatment, he was treated with itraconazole for the remainder time after clearance from the Cardiology team to hold atorvastatin. At his one-year follow-up, the patient continues to do well with no reported symptoms or adverse effects, and he remains on glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids, with plans for clinical evaluation in 6 months after the completion of antifungal therapy.

Discussion and conclusions

Histoplasmosis has a wide range of presentations, from asymptomatic infections to a life-threatening crisis, depending on the level of exposure and the patient’s immune status. The lungs are the primary site of infection in histoplasmosis, with dissemination occurring via hematogenous or lymphatic spread. A self-limiting pneumonitis is the most common clinical manifestation of Histoplasma infection in 90–95% of cases [2]. Chest X-rays usually reveal cavitary lesions, focal infiltrates, and mediastinal or hilar lymphadenopathy, but these findings are notably absent in 40–70% of cases [5]. For most patients, an intact immune system can prevent acute pulmonary histoplasmosis from spreading [16], but if it does spread, disseminated histoplasmosis can involve almost any organ system, such as the reticuloendothelial system (liver, spleen, bone marrow, and lymph nodes), gastrointestinal tract, central nervous system, and the adrenal glands. The mortality rate of systemic disease ranges from 6 to 15% [17].

Our patient did not have any remarkable respiratory symptoms or initial chest imaging findings. However, his contact with farm animals and bats was concerning. Patients diagnosed with histoplasmosis reportedly have identifiable exposure to the pathogen, such as hiking or spelunking caves in endemic regions, close contact with bats or birds, farming, cleaning chicken coops, or gardening [5].

While disseminated disease is often attributed to immunosuppression, adrenal gland involvement is largely seen in immunocompetent hosts. It is the most common organ involved outside the lungs in immunocompetent hosts [18]. For example, Gupta et al.’s retrospective analysis and literature review demonstrated immunocompetence in all 49 cases of adrenal histoplasmosis [6]. Similarly, in Deodhar D et al., adrenal involvement occurred in 50% of immunocompetent patients, as opposed to 9% of patients in the immunocompromised group [7]. This may be explained by poor granuloma formation due to a weak immune response in an immunocompromised host [19]. Adrenal involvement has been reported in 30–40% of clinical studies worldwide, with rates as high as 80% in autopsy series [8, 19]. Their rich vascular supply, presence of extremely high local concentrations of steroids (20–40 times higher than systemic circulation), which suppresses local cell-mediated immunity, and a scarcity of reticuloendothelial cells within the glands make them a viable hub of infection [20]. While most systemic fungal infections target the immunocompromised (e.g., HIV, organ transplantation, corticosteroid use), endemic mycoses like Histoplasma, Coccidioides, and Blastomyces can cause a spectrum of disease, from asymptomatic to severe, even in healthy individuals [24]. Inhalation of infectious environmental spores initiates pulmonary infection, which is typically asymptomatic or self-limited in immunocompetent hosts due to robust immune responses [25]. Disease severity depends on the balance between host cell-mediated immunity and the volume of inhaled spores, as a high pathogen burden or subtle immune gaps can lead to extrapulmonary spread even in otherwise healthy individuals.

As a dimorphic fungus, Histoplasma capsulatum transitions from environmental mold to an intracellular yeast upon inhalation, where it survives within alveolar macrophages by evading lysosomal destruction. While innate defenses provide the initial response, definitive control requires a robust Th1-mediated immune response—specifically the production of IFN-γ and TNF-α—to activate macrophages and sequester the pathogen within calcified granulomas. However, if this cell-mediated response is overwhelmed by a high fungal inoculum or subtle immune deficiencies, the yeast can bypass local containment and disseminate hematogenously via infected macrophages to systemic sites such as the liver, spleen, bone marrow, and adrenal glands [26]. The dissemination of Histoplasma in immunocompetent hosts likely results from an intense environmental inoculum [26] that overwhelms host defences, or from subclinical immune variations that allow the pathogen to persist despite an active response. This failure of local containment enables the yeast to survive within macrophages and utilize the lymphohematogenous system to seed secondary organs [27], where localized inflammation and granulomatous infiltration—such as the destruction of adrenal tissue by parasitized macrophages—can lead to significant organ dysfunction like adrenal insufficiency.

Adrenal involvement is most strongly associated with disseminated Histoplasma capsulatum infection compared with other endemic mycoses. Histoplasma exhibits a distinct tropism for the reticuloendothelial system and survives within macrophages, enabling lymphohematogenous dissemination to organs such as the liver, spleen, bone marrow, and adrenal glands [5]. Autopsy and clinical series have demonstrated adrenal involvement in a substantial proportion of disseminated histoplasmosis cases, often presenting as bilateral adrenal enlargement, with progression to primary adrenal insufficiency resulting from granulomatous inflammation and cortical destruction [5, 28]. In contrast, adrenal involvement in other endemic fungal infections is considerably less frequent.

Disseminated coccidioidomycosis and blastomycosis may rarely involve the adrenal glands, typically in the setting of advanced multisystem disease, and adrenal insufficiency is uncommon in these infections [29, 30]. Paracoccidioidomycosis, an endemic mycosis geographically restricted to Latin America, demonstrates a higher propensity for adrenal involvement, with adrenal insufficiency recognized as a known complication; however, its epidemiology and exposure risk differ markedly from histoplasmosis in North America [31]. Opportunistic fungi such as Cryptococcus and Aspergillus may involve the adrenal glands, predominantly in immunocompromised hosts, but adrenal dysfunction in these cases usually reflects invasive or necrotizing infection rather than granulomatous replacement [32]. Collectively, these distinctions highlight Histoplasma capsulatum as the endemic mycosis most strongly associated with clinically significant adrenal disease, reinforcing the importance of considering histoplasmosis in immunocompetent patients presenting with bilateral adrenal lesions or unexplained primary adrenal insufficiency in endemic areas.

Adrenal gland involvement presents with many months of constitutional symptoms such as fever, malaise, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, and weight loss, along with bilateral findings on imaging. Although not as common, unilateral adrenal enlargement has also been described, especially in the early disease course [33, 34]. Similar to this case, the usual CT features of adrenal histoplasmosis are bilateral symmetrical enlargement with preserved normal contour [35]. This is usually seen in the acute phase of infection, whereas central hypodense areas with peripheral enhancement suggestive of caseous necrosis may be representative of subacute or chronic infection.

The differential diagnoses for bilateral adrenal masses/enlargement also include tuberculosis, adrenal adenomas, adrenal metastasis, lymphoma, adrenal hemorrhage, and, as noted above, other fungal infections. Since CT findings for these are indistinguishable, the gold standard diagnostic test for histoplasmosis is the growth of the organism on fungal tissue culture from an adrenal biopsy [36]. As it can take up to four weeks to isolate the organisms, rapid onsite cytopathological evaluation after percutaneous biopsy or fine needle aspiration using CT or ultrasound guidance is routinely used instead [34, 37]. This intracellular dimorphic fungus is commonly identified as a narrow-based budding yeast within macrophages, better visualized using special stains such as methenamine silver or periodic acid-Schiff [5].

Not all Histoplasma infections that spread to the adrenal glands result in PAI. This rare complication may result from large fungal cells embolizing and blocking the adrenal blood vessels, leading to extensive damage to the adrenal cortex [38]. Approximately, 80% of disseminated histoplasmosis in patients with disseminated disease had adrenal involvement, but fewer than 10% of cases had resultant adrenal insufficiency [8]. While disseminated infection can become life-threatening, a high degree of suspicion and early diagnosis can yield a quick recovery.

Current treatment guidelines recommend itraconazole for a minimum of 12 months, along with a one-to-two-week course of amphotericin B in critically ill patients with disseminated histoplasmosis [39]. Ketoconazole may be used in milder presentations [18]. Since recurrence has been described as long as nine years after treatment discontinuation, this prolonged treatment minimizes the risk of relapse. However, despite only being FDA-approved for mucormycosis and aspergillosis, isavuconazole was chosen off-label. Posaconazole is contraindicated when used together with atorvastatin [40], and isavuconazole has a safer profile over itraconazole when used in cardiac patients, as itraconazole demonstrates negative inotropic effects and has been associated with worsening heart failure [41, 42].

Isavuconazole exhibits potent in vitro activity against Histoplasma capsulatum, with median MICs of ≤ 0.007 µg/mL [43]. Notably, isolates from patients who relapsed on fluconazole therapy—which showed elevated MICs to fluconazole and voriconazole—maintained low MICs to isavuconazole, suggesting a higher barrier to resistance compared to structurally similar triazoles [43]. This activity is comparable to itraconazole and posaconazole [43, 44]. In the VITAL study, 63% of all patients with cryptococcosis or dimorphic mycoses (including histoplasmosis) demonstrated successful overall response, with a median treatment duration of 180 days [45]. The available evidence suggests isavuconazole may serve as an alternative agent for histoplasmosis when standard therapies are contraindicated, though further clinical data are needed to establish its routine use for this indication.

In conclusion, histoplasmosis is an important differential diagnosis in patients with PAI and negative 21-hydroxylase antibodies, even if the patient does not reside in an endemic area, has no pulmonary involvement, and is immunocompetent. This disease should no longer be assumed exclusive to those with immune suppression and while its presence should raise suspicion of underlying immunological disease, literature is now replete with evidence of widespread findings in immunocompetent hosts. PAI due to Histoplasma infection is a rare presentation in the US but should be considered before life-threatening sequelae develop.
