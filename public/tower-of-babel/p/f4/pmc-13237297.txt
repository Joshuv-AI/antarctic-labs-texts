CB1 receptor signaling at the cingulate-striatal circuit is anxiogenic
Authors: Thomas J. Kelly, Xiaojie Liu, Yao Huang, Ying Hu, Vladislav Friedman, Cecilia J. Hillard, Qing-song Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cannabinoids, such as Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), produce anxiety at high doses, yet the underlying neural mechanisms remain poorly understood. The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) sends dense monosynaptic projections to the dorsomedial striatum (DMS). Optogenetic excitation of the ACC → DMS pathway promotes anxiolytic-like behavior, suggesting that its suppression could induce anxiogenic states. We hypothesized that CB1 receptor-mediated suppression of this circuit contributes to cannabinoid-induced anxiogenic effects. We found that endocannabinoid (eCB) system components are expressed in ACC → DMS neurons and that cannabinoids depress excitatory transmission in this circuit in mice. Additionally, in vivo fiber photometry revealed that anxiogenic and aversive stimuli trigger eCB release from the DMS, which activates CB1 receptors on ACC axon terminals. Notably, pathway-specific deletion of CB1 receptors in the ACC → DMS circuit reduced both innate anxiety-like behaviors and conditioned place aversion to THC in mice. Collectively, these findings identify a circuit-level mechanism whereby cannabinoid signaling promotes anxiety and aversion.

Introduction

Endocannabinoids (eCBs) are lipid-derived neurotransmitters that retrogradely depress synaptic transmission through activation of presynaptic cannabinoid receptor 1 (CB1)1–3. CB1 receptors are key regulators of affective states, influencing stress responsivity, fear learning, and fear extinction4–8. Pharmacological elevation of the eCB 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG) by inhibiting its primary degradative enzyme, monoacylglycerol lipase (MAGL), produces anxiolytic and antidepressant-like effects in stress models9–12. Conversely, global knockout of diacylglycerol lipase (DAGLα), the principal enzyme responsible for 2-AG synthesis, induced anxiety-like behavior in mice13. Together, these findings demonstrate that emotional states are bidirectionally modulated by alterations in eCB signaling.

Population studies indicate that relaxation and euphoria are the primary motivations for cannabis use14. Δ9-Tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the principal psychoactive constituent of cannabis, acts as a non-selective partial agonist at cannabinoid receptors. THC mediates most of its central effects through CB1 receptor activation15, although CB2 receptors can also contribute16,17. CB1 signaling in key regions, including the amygdala, nucleus accumbens, ventral tegmental area, and bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST), is well established to regulate anxiety and reward processing6,16,18–20. It is likely that coordinated CB1 signaling across interconnected cortical and subcortical circuits is a critical determinant of the anxiolytic and rewarding effects of low dose THC.

Paradoxically, THC produces dose-dependent anxiogenic effects in both rodents and humans. At higher doses, THC can trigger panic, paranoia, and anxiety in humans21 and induces conditioned place aversion (CPA) in rodents22. Cannabis products with high levels of THC have been linked to anxiety, contributing to the growing proportion of cannabis-related emergency department visits as legalization has expanded23. Despite the clinical and public health relevance, the neural circuits mediating the anxiogenic and aversive effects of high-dose THC remain poorly defined.

One candidate locus is the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), a dorsomedial prefrontal structure that integrates limbic, cognitive, and motivational signals to guide adaptive behavior under conditions of uncertainty24–28. In decision-making tasks, ACC activity is associated with cognitive control, error detection, and behavioral flexibility28–30. Optogenetic silencing of the ACC impairs attention28,29, reduces error monitoring24, and promotes avoidance behavior30, which are commonly reported in cannabis users31,32. The ACC sends dense projections to the dorsomedial striatum (DMS)33. CB1 receptors are abundantly expressed in the striatum34,35 and are localized to glutamatergic terminals36, where their activation inhibits cortical glutamate release35,37. Given that optogenetic stimulation of ACC terminals in the DMS produces anxiolytic-like effects30, we hypothesized that suppression of the ACC → DMS transmission through CB1 receptor activation by either THC or eCBs could promote anxiogenic or aversive states. To test this hypothesis, we employed a multi-level experimental approach. First, RNAscope fluorescent in situ hybridization was used to quantify expression of key eCB system components in the ACC and DMS. Second, using optogenetic electrophysiology, we demonstrated that 2-AG released from DMS neurons retrogradely suppresses glutamatergic input from the ACC. Third, we used in vivo fiber photometry with the GPCR-based sensor GRABeCB2.0 to measure eCB signaling at ACC terminals in the DMS during approach and avoidance behavior. Finally, we determined how pathway-specific deletion of CB1 receptors from ACC neurons projecting to the DMS alters anxiety-like behavior and THC-induced conditioned place aversion. Together, these findings identify the ACC → DMS pathway as a critical locus at which cannabinoid signaling promotes anxiogenic affective states.

Results

The excitatory ACC → DMS projection is monosynaptic and is regulated by eCBs

We first performed brain-wide anterograde mapping of ACC excitatory neurons by injecting AAV9-CaMKII-hChR2(E123T/T159C)-mCherry into the ACC, which revealed widespread projections to cortical and subcortical regions, including dense innervation of the DMS (Supplementary Fig. 1). To test whether these projections form functional synapses with medium spiny neurons (MSNs) in the DMS, we next carried out electrophysiological recordings in striatal slices two weeks after viral injection (Fig. 1a). Blue light stimulation (470 nm, 2 ms) of ACC axon terminals through the 40x objective evoked excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) that scaled linearly with light power (Fig. 1b) and were blocked by the AMPAR antagonist CNQX (10 µM; Fig. 1c). Optogenetic EPSCs (oEPSCs) could arise from a monosynaptic connection between the ACC and DMS MSNs, or alternatively, are mediated via intermediary excitatory neurons in a polysynaptic circuit. We distinguished these possibilities by applying the Na+ channel blocker tetrodotoxin (TTX) and the K+ channel blocker 4-aminopyridine (4-AP), which block polysynaptic but not monosynaptic currents38. Bath application of TTX (1 µM) blocked opto-EPSCs, which was reversed by the addition of 4-AP (1 mM; Fig. 1d). Because rescue of light-evoked currents by 4-AP occurred in the presence of TTX, the oEPSCs must be monosynaptic, since no intermediary neuron would have been capable of firing an action potential. Blockade of K+ channels with 4-AP greatly enhances the ChR2-induced depolarization of axons and activation of Ca2+ channels, which are sufficient to rescue transmitter release38. Depolarization-induced suppression of excitation (DSE) is a form of short-term synaptic depression triggered by Ca2+ influx, which leads to the synthesis and release of 2-AG from postsynaptic neurons and retrograde activation of presynaptic CB1 receptors3,39. After establishing a stable baseline of oEPSCs, a brief depolarization (from −80 to 0 mV, 5 s) elicited DSE, which was blocked by the CB1 antagonist AM251 (Fig. 1e). Similarly, bath application of the CB1 receptor agonist WIN55212-2 (2 µM) depressed opto-EPSCs in DMS MSNs (Fig. 1f). Thus, CB1 receptor activation by both exogenous and endogenous cannabinoids leads to suppression of excitatory transmission at the ACC → DMS pathway.

The expression of the eCB system in the ACC → DMS pathway

We used RNAscope in situ hybridization to characterize the expression of genes involved in the signaling and degradation of the eCB ligand 2-AG in the ACC. 2-AG mediates DSE and is primarily degraded by MAGL in or near the presynaptic terminal, where it activates CB1 receptors40. We investigated the expression of Cnr1 (encoding CB1) and Mgll (encoding MAGL) in the two major neuron types in the ACC. Excitatory ACC neurons expressed mRNA for the vesicular glutamate transporter 1 (Slc17a7;Vglut1), while inhibitory ACC neurons expressed mRNA for the vesicular GABA transporter (Slc32a1;Vgat, Fig. 1g). Cnr1 was expressed on ~80% of excitatory and inhibitory ACC neurons (Fig. 1h). Mgll was expressed in all somata of excitatory and inhibitory neurons; Mgll puncta density was not significantly different between glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons (Supplementary Fig. 2). Overall, these data demonstrate that the presynaptic components of the eCB system are present in ACC neurons.

2-AG is synthesized from diacylglycerol in dendrites by diacylglycerol lipase α (DAGLα)41. The striatum contains two major neuronal populations: MSNs expressing D1 (Drd1) or D2 (Drd2) receptors. RNAscope showed that Drd1 or Drd2-expressing neurons were present in approximately equal proportions. DAGLα mRNA (Dagla) was ubiquitously expressed in both Drd1 and Drd2 MSNs, with no significant differences in expression density between the two neuronal populations (Supplementary Fig. 3). Thus, the DMS expresses Daglα and can synthesize the eCB 2-AG, as shown by the presence of DSE (Fig. 1e).

eCBs are released by DMS neurons in vivo and activate CB1 receptors on ACC terminals

We used GRABeCB2.0, a GPCR-based fluorescent sensor with low micromolar sensitivity to eCBs42, to investigate eCB dynamics at the ACC → DMS synapse in freely moving mice. AAV9-hSyn-eCB2.0 was injected into the ACC, and an optical fiber was implanted into the DMS to record eCB transients on ACC terminals (Fig. 2a, b). We first validated the efficacy of GRABeCB2.0 to detect CB1 receptor activation using WIN 55212-2 (5 mg/kg i.p.). WIN 55212-2 produced a robust increase in GRABeCB2.0 fluorescence within minutes of administration which was blocked by pretreatment with the CB1 receptor antagonist AM251 (10 mg/kg i.p, Supplementary Fig. 4a–d). JZL-184 is an irreversible and highly selective MAGL inhibitor that enhances extracellular concentrations of 2-AG43. Spontaneous eCB transients were relatively sparse at baseline, administration of JZL-184 (20 mg/kg, i.p.) led to an increase in the frequency of eCB transients at ACC terminals (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). Injection of AM251 (10 mg/kg, i.p.) 20 minutes prior to JZL-184 blocked the eCB transients (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). eCB signaling at the ACC → DMS synapse changes dynamically during approach and avoidance behavior

The DMS is involved in organizing locomotor activity44, action sequences45, and behavioral avoidance46. To identify behavioral conditions that trigger eCB release at ACC → DMS synapses, we determined whether GRABeCB2.0 signals change as mice transition into anxiogenic areas. Mice are naturally averse to open spaces and bright lights, such as the center of an open field. GRABeCB2.0 fluorescence increased as mice entered the center zone and was accompanied by locomotor acceleration (Fig. 2c, d). Mice reached an average speed of ~100 mm/sec as they entered the center zone. However, the general onset of comparable speed (100 mm/sec) in the open field was not associated with a change in GRABeCB2.0 fluorescence (Fig. 2e). Therefore, the elevation in GRABeCB2.0 signal was likely an effect of entering the center zone rather than locomotor initiation.

The elevated plus maze contains discrete transitions between open and closed arms. We next investigated eCB changes during key transitions in the elevated plus maze. GRABeCB2.0 signal increased as mice approached and exited the closed arms, peaked during entry into the open arms, and declined rapidly upon re-entry into closed arms (Fig. 2f–j). If mice chose to enter the open arm rather than the closed arm, GRABeCB2.0 fluorescence briefly remained elevated above baseline levels (Fig. 2h). In contrast, transitions from the open arm to a closed arm were associated with a reduction in GRABeCB2.0 fluorescence (Fig. 2i, j). These findings indicate that eCB signaling increases at ACC terminals in the DMS as mice approach anxiogenic areas and decreases as they leave them.

eCB signal increases at ACC terminals in the DMS after exposure to aversive stimuli but not conditioned cues

GRABeCB2.0 fluorescence was recorded from ACC terminals in the DMS during fear conditioning (tone and shock pairing) and during recall (tone alone) one day later (Fig. 3a). During conditioning, mice were presented with four 20 s tones (80 dB), each co-terminating with a 1 s foot shock (0.5 mA) and separated by variable inter-trial intervals (60-80 s). ACC terminals exhibited a robust increase in GRABeCB2.0 fluorescence following foot shock delivery (Fig. 3b), but not at tone onset (Fig. 3c). Next, we determined whether exposure to a predator odor alters GRABeCB2.0 fluorescence at ACC terminals in the DMS. The synthetic predator odor 2-methyl-2-thiazoline (2-MT) elicits freezing and avoidance behaviors in mice47. Mice were first acclimated to a novel home cage and then exposed to a control water odor, followed 20 minutes later by 2-MT (Fig. 3f). Introduction of 2-MT, but not water, produced a significant increase in GRABeCB2.0 fluorescence at ACC terminals in the DMS (Fig. 3g, h). Together, these results indicate that unconditioned aversive stimuli, including foot shock and predator odor, elevate eCB signaling at the ACC → DMS synapse.

Genetic deletion of the CB1 receptor in ACC → DMS neurons induces anxiolytic-like behavior

The above results suggest that eCB signaling is elevated at the ACC → DMS synapse as mice transition from areas perceived as safe to those perceived as dangerous. The elevation of eCBs at ACC terminals may serve either as an anxiogenic signal that promotes avoidance or as a mechanism that allows the mouse to cope with perceived threat. To examine the valence of CB1/eCB signaling at the ACC → DMS synapse, we investigated how selective genetic deletion of the CB1 receptor in ACC neurons that project to the DMS altered anxiety-like behavior. Knockout of the CB1 receptor in ACC → DMS projection neurons was accomplished by injecting AAVretro-EF1a-mCherry-IRES-flpo into the DMS and a mixture of AAV9-EF1a-fDIO-Cre and either AAV9-FLEX-EGFP (for behavioral studies) or AAV9-DIO-ChR2-EYFP (for validation of CB1 receptor deletion) into the ACC of either Cnr1LoxP/LoxP or Cnr1WT/WT mice (Fig. 4a–d). In principle, Flpo is expressed in all DMS afferents, but Cre expression is limited to ACC → DMS neurons, enabling CB1 receptor deletion and eGFP labeling specifically in projection-defined ACC neurons. Six weeks after mice received the viral injections, we performed slice electrophysiological recordings of DMS neurons evoking opto-EPSCs in ACC axons that project to the DMS. Bath application of WIN 55212-2 (4 µM) suppressed opto-EPSCs in Cnr1WT/WT mice, but not Cnr1LoxP/LoxP mice, suggesting that the CB1 receptor was deleted in ACC neurons that project to the DMS (Fig. 4b). Moreover, we found that AAV9-FLEX-EGFP was expressed in ACC neurons when AAVretro-EF1a-mCherry-IRES-flpo was injected into the DMS and AAV9-fDIO-Cre was injected into the ACC (Fig. 4d). In contrast, EGFP was not expressed when flpo was omitted, regardless of whether fDIO-Cre was present or not. After ~6 weeks of AAV expression, mice underwent a battery of behavioral tests to assess anxiety- and depression-like behavior (Fig. 4c). In the open field test, mice with ACC → DMS pathway-specific deletion of CB1 receptor (ACC → DMSCB1cKO) and controls (ACC → DMSWT) spent little time in the center during the first 2 minutes of the test. However, ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice rapidly increased the time spent in the center of the open field, an effect was sustained throughout the remainder of the test (Fig. 4e–g). Although ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice exhibited reduced total distance traveled during the open field test, their initial locomotor activity (Fig. 4h, i) and maximal speed (Supplementary Fig. 5) were not significantly different from controls. The subsequent reduction in locomotion is therefore unlikely to reflect motor impairment and is more consistent with faster habituation to the novel environment. In the elevated plus maze (EPM), ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice spent significantly more time in the open arms compared to ACC → DMSWT mice (Fig. 4j, k). There was no significant genotype difference in the number of open arm entries, center entries or time spent in the center zone (Fig. 4l–n). ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice had reduced overall distance traveled and speed compared to ACC → DMSWT mice (Supplementary Fig. 6). This was primarily due to reduced speed of ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice in the closed arms, rather than the open arms (Supplementary Fig. 6).

The novelty-suppressed feeding test assesses avoidance behavior in a conflict. The fasting mouse faces a dilemma between feeding and avoiding open space within the novel environment. A reduction in the latency to feed indicates that hunger is weighted more heavily than avoidance48. ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice had a reduced latency to feed compared to ACC → DMSWT mice (Fig. 4o). Mice defensively bury neutral objects, such as marbles, in response to anxiogenic environmental stimuli to reduce stress49. Stressed mice bury more marbles, while anxiolytic drugs reduce the tendency to bury marbles49,50. In the marble bury test, ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice buried fewer marbles compared to ACC → DMSWT mice (Fig. 4p). Together, these results show that loss of CB1 from ACC neurons that project to the DMS produced anxiolytic-like effects.

Interestingly, we did not observe any difference between genotypes in the sucrose preference test (Supplementary Fig. 7) or forced swim test (Fig. 4q). Moreover, conditioned freezing responses to tone did not differ between genotypes during fear conditioning and extinction (Supplementary Fig. 8a–c). Overall, these results indicate that ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice had an anxiolytic phenotype, did not display anhedonia or behavioral despair, and retained the ability to form associations between environmental cues and aversive stimuli.

CB1 receptor activation constrains the appetitive and anxiolytic effects of optogenetic stimulation of the ACC → DMS pathway

Real-time place preference (RTPP) or aversion (RTPA) is commonly used to assess reinforcing or aversive effects of optogenetic stimulation of specific circuits51. We tested whether in vivo optogenetic stimulation of the ACC → DMS pathway was reinforcing or aversive in ACC → DMSWT or ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice with an RTPP/RTPA paradigm (Fig. 5a–d). Compared to baseline, optogenetic stimulation of the ACC axon terminals with blue light (20 Hz, 465 nm) produced no significant change in preference or distance traveled in ACC → DMSWT mice but produced RTPP in ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice without altering the distance traveled (Fig. 5d–f). These findings reveal a CB1-dependent constraint on the appetitive impact of ACC → DMS activation. Next, we assessed whether in vivo optogenetic stimulation of the ACC axon terminals in the DMS could alter anxiety-like behavior in ACC → DMSWT or ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice using the open field and elevated plus maze. In the open field test, there were no significant differences in distance traveled or time spent in the center between genotypes during the optogenetic stimulation period (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). In the elevated plus maze, ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice spent significantly more time in the open arms and center zone compared to ACC → DMSWT mice (Fig. 5g, i). The number of open arm and center zone entries during the stimulation period were also greater in ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice compared to ACC → DMSWT mice (Fig. 5h, j). Aversion to THC requires CB1 receptor expression on ACC neurons that project to the DMS

High doses of THC reliably produce feelings of anxiety in humans32,52,53 and avoidance behavior in rodents54,55. Our data thus far suggest that eCB signaling at the ACC terminals in the DMS precipitates avoidant behavior in response to aversive stimuli and may therefore be a locus where activation of the CB1 receptor by exogenous agonists, such as THC, promotes avoidance. High-dose THC conditioned place aversion (CPA)54,55 was examined in ACC → DMSWT and ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice. Mice were first tested for basal place preference in a two-chamber apparatus (pre-test). For the next five days, mice were given an injection of high-dose THC (25 mg/kg i.p.) in the preferred chamber and a vehicle injection in the less preferred chamber. The final test was conducted drug-free, which minimized potential confounds from THC-induced motor suppression on CPA expression (Fig. 6a, b). In the CPA trial, ACC → DMSWT showed a marked reduction in the time spent in the THC-paired chamber and a corresponding increase in time spent in the vehicle-paired chamber on the test day compared with the pretest day, suggesting that THC-pairing induces CPA. In contrast, ACC → DMSCB1cKO showed no significant difference in the time spent in either the THC-paired chamber or vehicle-paired side between pretest and test days (Fig. 6c, d; Supplementary Fig. 10a). Discussion

Our study identifies the ACC → DMS pathway as a critical locus where cannabinoid signaling regulates anxiety-like behavior in response to averse stimuli. We demonstrate that both the ACC and DMS express key components of the eCB system, and that activation of presynaptic CB1 receptors on ACC axon terminals, either by endogenously released 2-AG from MSNs or by exogenous CB1 agonists, suppresses glutamatergic input from ACC to DMS MSNs. Pathway-specific deletion of CB1 receptors from ACC → DMS neurons (ACC → DMSCB1cKO) produced an anxiolytic-like phenotype and abolished THC-induced CPA. Together, these findings suggest that CB1 activation at ACC terminals in the DMS is engaged during aversive or anxiogenic conditions and contributes to anxiety-related behavioral responses.

Mice perceive open and brightly lit environments as threatening due to increased predation risk. Using fiber photometry with the GRABeCB2.0 sensor, we found that eCB tone at ACC terminals in the DMS increases during exposure to anxiogenic environments. Specifically, fluorescence increased during transitions from closed to open arms of EPM and from the periphery to the center of the open field and decreased upon re-entry into closed arms of the EPM. Similar increases in GRABeCB2.0 fluorescence were observed following foot shocks or exposure to predator odor, but not in response to conditioned tones that were previously paired with footshock. These data suggest that ACC → DMS eCB signaling is preferentially engaged by innate or unpredictable threats rather than learned cues. This pattern parallels prior calcium imaging studies that showed increased ACC neuronal activity during entry into anxiogenic environments30. Together, these observations support a model in which excitatory ACC input to the DMS triggers local eCB release from MSNs, followed by CB1-mediated negative feedback at ACC terminals that limits cortical drive and biases behavior toward avoidance. In support of this idea, ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice exhibited anxiolytic-like phenotypes as they spent more time in the center of the open field and the open arm of the EPM and displayed a reduced latency to feed in the novelty-suppressed feeding test. Thus, CB1 activation at ACC terminals in the DMS appears to promote anxiety-like behavior under threatening conditions.

These findings highlight the importance of pathway-specific manipulations compared with broader cell-type–specific knockouts. Previous work showed that conditional deletion of CB1 from GABAergic, but not glutamatergic, neurons prevents the anxiogenic effects of CB1 agonists in the EPM56. However, CB1 receptors are expressed across multiple GABAergic neuron classes in the cortex57, and activation of distinct interneuron populations can exert opposing effects on behavioral responses to stress58,59. In contrast, eCBs act locally at synapses, and targeted deletion of CB1 in specific projections, as in ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice, enables a more precise dissection of circuit-level mechanisms that contribute to behavior.

The ACC → DMS pathway has been implicated in risk assessment and decision-making27. Animals avoid bright, open spaces due to predation risk, inhibition of ACC → DMS projections enhances avoidance of such environments27, whereas excitation suppresses innate aversion to bright and open spaces30. Our results suggest that eCB/CB1 signaling provides a brake on ACC output to the DMS, biasing behavior toward avoidance in threatening contexts. Beyond approach-avoidance behavior, the ACC also plays a critical role in the top-down modulation of itch60, an aversive internal state that elicits rapid behavioral responses such as scratching61. For example, optogenetic activation of ACC → DMS neurons induces scratching, whereas inhibition suppresses histamine-evoked scratching62, supporting a broader role for this circuit in coordinating behavioral responses to aversive internal states such as itch or pain.

High doses of THC provoke anxiety in humans and produce CPA and anxiety-like behaviors in rodents32,52–55. Our findings identify CB1 signaling in the ACC → DMS pathway as a critical contributor to these behavioral effects. Specifically, high-dose THC produced robust CPA in ACC → DMSWT mice but not in ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice. Although THC can reduce locomotor activity, this is unlikely to account for the observed CPA, as conditioning occurred in a single chamber and final testing was conducted drug-free, minimizing confounds from acute locomotor suppression. Moreover, fear learning remained intact in ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice, indicating that the absence of CPA could not be attributed to impaired learning or motor deficits. Instead, the lack of THC-induced aversion in ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice is consistent with a reduced anxiogenic effect of high-dose THC. In support of this interpretation, optogenetic activation of the ACC → DMS pathway induced RTPP and enhanced anxiolytic-like behavior in the EPM in ACC → DMSCB1cKO mice, but not in ACC → DMSWT mice. These results indicate that place preference and anxiolysis are similarly unmasked by pathway-specific CB1 deletion at the ACC → DMS pathway, suggesting that THC-induced CPA in ACC → DMSWT mice was likely due to anxiogenesis produced by CB1-mediated suppression of the ACC → DMS pathway.

A limitation of this study is that we did not examine the dissociable roles of eCB-mediated inhibition of ACC inputs onto D1 versus D2 MSNs. ACC neurons preferentially innervate D1 over D2 MSNs30. While eCB-mediated long-term depression is more robust at D2 MSNs, both D1 and D2 MSNs express CB1 receptors and exhibit similar levels of DSE63. Interestingly, optogenetic stimulation of D2 MSNs enhances avoidance behavior64. It is possible that suppression of ACC → D1-MSN input through either eCBs or CB1 agonists, such as THC, bias excitatory drive toward D2 MSNs, which in turn promotes avoidance or anxiety-like behavior. It is likely that some ACC neurons collateralize to multiple downstream targets, and CB1 signaling may coordinate ACC output across multiple circuits. Additionally, recent studies indicate that stress-induced eCB signaling in striatal interneurons reshapes the integration of excitatory inputs. For example, acute stress enhances the intrinsic excitability of somatostatin-expressing interneurons and reorganizes their glutamatergic inputs in an eCB-dependent manner65. Future studies will be needed to resolve how eCB signaling differentially regulates ACC inputs across MSN subtypes and striatal interneuron populations.

In conclusion, our findings reveal a circuit mechanism whereby eCB release from MSNs engages CB1 on ACC terminals to regulate anxiety-like behavior and aversive responses to THC. This pathway functions as a synaptic brake on ACC output, biasing behavior toward avoidance when animals encounter anxiogenic or threatening environments. At this locus, eCB tone waxes with exposure to threat and wanes as threat dissipates. Persistent CB1 activation by THC may disrupt this dynamic regulation, impairing the ability to appropriately evaluate environmental risk. By identifying CB1 signaling within the ACC → DMS pathway as a key contributor to THC-induced aversion, this work helps explain how high doses of THC enhance threat perception and anxiety.

Methods

Animals

Animal use and maintenance was in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of The Medical College of Wisconsin. Mice were given ad libitum access to food and water, unless stated otherwise, and housed four to five per cage in a temperature (23 ± 1 °C) and humidity-controlled room (40–60%) with a 14 hr light, 10 hr dark cycle. All experiments were performed on adult male and female mice (8–16 weeks old at the start of experiments). Roughly equal numbers of male and female mice were included in all experiments. Both sexes exhibited similar responses; data from both sexes were pooled. Further disaggregation of data by sex was not statistically powered for detecting sex differences due to the sample sizes. C57BL/6 J (Jax stock#: 000664) were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine). Cnr1loxP/loxP mice (Cnr1tm1.1Zxx, MGI: 6119514) were generated66 and maintained on a C57BL/6 J background.

Stereotaxic surgery and AAV injection

Mice were anesthetized by isoflurane inhalation prior to surgery. One hole was drilled above each injection site. For all AAV injections into the ACC, 150 nl of total volume was injected at AP: +0.98, ML: ± 0.28, DV: 1.45-1.5. For all AAV injections into the DMS, 300 nl of total volume was injected at AP: +0.98, ML: ± 1.35, DV: 2.65-2.7. For electrophysiological experiments, AAV5-CaMKIIa-hChR2(H134R)-mCherry (Addgene #26975) was injected into the ACC. Mice were allowed about 2 weeks to recover before electrophysiology experiments. For pathway-specific knockout behavioral experiments, AAVrg-EF1a-mCherry-IRES-Flpo (Addgene # 55634) was bilaterally injected into the DMS. After DMS injections, a 1:1 cocktail of AAV9-EF1a-fDIO-Cre (Addgene #121675) and AAV9-EF1a-FLEX-EGFP (Addgene #98747) or AAV9-DIO-Chr2-EYFP (Addgene #35509) was injected into the ACC. Animals were housed individually following surgery. Experiments were performed 6 weeks after these injections to allow for genetic knockout.

For fiber photometry experiments, AAV9-hSyn-eCB2.0 (WZ Biosciences, Columbia, MD) was injected into the ACC. Next, a low autofluorescence fiber-optic cannula (OD = 200 µm, NA = 0.57; Doric Inc, Québec, Canada) was implanted into the DMS 2.6 mm below the surface of the skull and 1.35 mm to the right or left of the bregma. For in vivo optogenetic experiments two low autofluorescence cannulae were implanted into the DMS at a 10-degree angle 2.6 mm below the skull surface and 1.6 mm to the right and left of the bregma. Two screws (00–96 × 1/16; Invivo1) were screwed into the skull and held in place with C&B Metabond quick adhesive cement to secure the cannula.

Behavior

Sample sizes for behavioral tests were calculated using a statistical power analysis (α = 5%, Power = 0.8), with consideration of prior experience and the sample sizes generally used in the field. For tests that required manual scoring (e.g., forced swim test and marble burying test), the experimenters were blinded to groups. The least stressful behavioral tests were performed before high stress tests. No more than one behavioral test was performed in a single day.

Open field test (OFT)

Mice were placed in one corner of an open field (50 cm × 45 cm × 30 cm) and were allowed to explore for 20 min. Locomotor activity was recorded with ANY-maze (Stoelting, Wood Dale, IL). The total distance traveled, number of center entries, and time spent in the center was calculated. The center zone was defined as the central 25 cm × 22.5 cm area of the box. For fiber photometry experiments, Danse (Doric Lenses Inc.) was used in place of ANY-maze.

Elevated plus maze (EPM)

The EPM was elevated 40 cm above the floor. Mice were placed in the center zone and allowed to explore the maze for 5 minutes. The mice were tracked with ANY-maze. For fiber photometry experiments utilizing the EPM, mice were allowed to explore longer (15 min). The time spent in the open arms and the number of entries was quantified. For fiber photometry experiments, Danse was used to track animals.

Novelty-suppressed feeding (NSF)

Mice were deprived of food for 24 hours before being placed in a novel environment (a plastic box 50 cm long × 35 cm wide × 30 cm deep). One pellet of regular chow was placed in the center. A mouse was placed in the corner of the box and the latency to feed was determined. Feeding was counted only if mice bit with their forepaws, rather than sniffing or bumping into the food. Immediately following the test, the latency to feed in the home cage was measured.

Marble burying test (MBT)

Mice were placed in a cage filled with 7–8 cm of bedding. Twenty-four dark blue marbles (1.35 cm diameter) were on the top of the bedding. Mice were allowed to roam freely in the cage for 20 min. At the end of the 20 min session, the marbles buried (2/3 covered or more) were counted.

Sucrose preference (SP)

Mice were habituated to drinking from a single bottle for at least 48 hours before testing. Eight hours prior to the SP test, food and water were removed. During testing, two bottles were placed in the home cage: one containing 1% sucrose solution and the other containing plain tap water. After 16 hours, sucrose preference (%) was calculated as the mass of sucrose solution consumed divided by the total mass of fluid consumed.

Forced swim test (FST)

Mice were placed into glass cylinders (13 cm diameter, 25 cm tall) filled with ~18 cm of water (30 ± 1 °C). The mice were left in the cylinders for 6 min and the time spent immobile during the last 4 min of the test was scored. Immobility was defined as the absence of all movements except those required to keep the mouse’s head above the water.

Fear conditioning and extinction

Fear conditioning and extinction training were performed in light and sound-attenuating boxes (Actimetrics, Wilmette, IL, USA). FreezeFrame 5.0 software was used to measure freezing behavior (Actimetrics). During fear conditioning, mice were exposed to 5 auditory tones (75–85 dB, 20 s) that co-terminated with a 1 second foot shock (0.5 mA). Intertrial intervals were 60–80 s between the cues. Fear extinction training began one day later. Mice were exposed to ten 20 s tones in a novel environment with 60–80 s pseudorandom intervals without foot shocks. This procedure was repeated every 24 hours for 6 days.

Conditioned place avoidance (CPA)

During the pre-test, mice were placed in a two-chamber conditioned place preference apparatus for 30 min. One chamber featured vertical stripes and the other dots, with illumination maintained at 5–10 lux on each side. Time spent in each chamber was recorded to determine initial preference. For the subsequent five days, mice underwent place conditioning. On each day, mice received a vehicle injection in their initially less-preferred chamber and, four hours later, received THC (25 mg/kg, i.p.) in their preferred chamber. Immediately after each injection, mice were confined to the corresponding chamber for 45 minutes. On the test day, mice were allowed to freely explore all chambers for 30 min without injections. THC was dissolved in ethanol, and both THC and vehicle solutions were prepared at a 1:1:18 ratio of ethanol:kolliphor:saline.

Slice preparation and electrophysiology

Artificial cerebral spinal fluid (ACSF) contained the following (in mM): 119 NaCl, 3 KCl, 2 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 25 NaHCO3, and 10 glucose. Choline-based solution contained the following (in mM): 110 choline chloride, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 0.5 CaCl2, 7 MgSO4, 26 NaHCO3, 25 glucose, 11.6 sodium ascorbate, and 3.1 sodium pyruvate (pH to 7.4). Prior to slice cutting, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and perfused transcardially with a 10 ml mixture of artificial cerebrospinal fluid (40%) and choline-based cutting solution (60%). We found that transcardial perfusion with this solution improved DSE responses in the striatum. Following transcardial perfusion mice were decapitated and immediately embedded in 3% low-gelling temperature agarose at 40°C in a cylindrical steel mold. The mold was immediately transferred to ice and left to incubate for 2 min. After the agarose solidified, the mold was trimmed to reveal the prefrontal cortex. Brain slices (250 µm) containing the ACC and DMS were cut on a vibratome (Leica VT1200s) in choline-based solution before being transferred to a separate beaker containing choline cutting solution. ACSF was gradually introduced into the beaker before the slices were transferred to ACSF. Slices were incubated in ACSF for 30 minutes before recording. Cutting steps were performed at room temperature. All solutions were saturated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2.

Whole-cell patch clamp recordings were made from DMS neurons using patch-clamp amplifiers (Multiclamp 700B) under an infrared differential interference contrast microscope67,68. Data acquisition was performed using DigiData 1440 A and 1550B digitizers, and analysis was performed using pClamp 10.7 (Molecular Devices). Signals were filtered at 2 kHz and sampled at 10 kHz. Neurons were voltage-clamped at −80 mV unless stated otherwise. Optical stimulation was delivered through a 40x objective lens via Lumencor Spectra X light engine. For whole-cell recordings of opto-EPSCs, glass pipettes (4–6 MΩ) were filled with an internal solution containing the following (in mM): 140 K-gluconate, 8 NaCl, 10 HEPES, 0.4 EGTA-Na, 2 MgCl2, 2 Mg-ATP, 0.3 Na2GTP, pH 7.2 with KOH. With this slicing procedure, there was a substantial run-up of opto-EPSCs, especially during the first 10 min of whole-cell recording. DSE was not recorded until neurons reached a stable baseline for opto-EPSCs, which was ~10–15 minutes after breaking in. Series resistance (10–30 MΩ) was monitored throughout all whole-cell recordings, and data were discarded if the resistance changed by > 20%. All recordings were performed at room temperature.

RNAscope

The RNAscope protocol used can be found elsewhere66. Briefly, mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and transcardially perfused with 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffered saline (PBS) followed by 4% paraformaldehyde in 4% sucrose-PBS (pH 7.4). After perfusion, the brain was removed and post-fixed in the same fixative for 2 h at 4 °C and was then dehydrated in increasing concentrations of sucrose (20% and 30%) in 0.1 M PBS at 4 °C and frozen on dry ice. Coronal sections (14 μm) were cut with a Leica cryostat (Leica CM1860) and mounted on Superfrost Plus slides (Fisher Scientific). Probes targeting the mRNA transcripts of Daglα (target region: base pairs 396–1259 of Mus musculus diacylglycerol lipase alpha, NM_198114.2), Drd1 (target region: base pairs 444–1358 of Mus musculus dopamine receptor D1, NM_010076.3), Drd2 (target region: base pairs 69–1175 of Mus musculus dopamine receptor D2 NM_010077.2), Cnr1 (target region: base pairs 530–1458 of Mus musculus cannabinoid receptor 1, NM_007726.3), Slc17a7 (target region: base pairs 498–1009 of Mus musculus solute carrier family 17 (vesicular glutamate transporter) member 7 (Slc17a7), NM_182993.2), and Slc32a1 (target region: base pairs 894–2037 of Mus musculus solute carrier family 32 (GABA vesicular transporter) member 1 (Slc32a1), NM_009508.2), were designed by and purchased from Advanced Cell Diagnostics Inc. (Hayward, CA). The manufacturer’s instructions for the RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent V2 Assay were followed. Slides were counterstained with DAPI and mounted with Vectashield Antifade Mounting Medium (Vector Laboratories) and imaged on a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope with a 25x/0.95 NA/2.4 mm WD HC FLUOTAR water immersion objective. Imaris (Bitplane) was used for quantifying the imaging data. For analysis, cell-specific markers (Drd1 vs. Drd2; Slc17a7 vs. Slc32a1) were reconstructed as surfaces in Imaris. The Spots function was then applied to detect Dagla and Mgll puncta using a threshold of 1 μm diameter. The number of fluorescent puncta was quantified using the Spots tool.

In vivo fiber photometry of GRABeCB2.0

A fiber photometry system (Doric Lenses, Inc) was used to detect eCB-dependent changes in fluorescence from GRABeCB2.0 in the DMS of freely moving mice69. In brief, a computer-controlled fiber photometry console and low-power LED drivers (LEDD_4) generated 405 and 465 nm light from LEDs. LED intensities were sinusoidally modulated by a lock-in amplifier at nonsynchronous frequencies (208.616 and 572.21 Hz for 465 and 405 nm, respectively) to filter out the fluorescence arising from excitation spectra. A fluorescence MiniCube (Doric; FMC6_IE(400-410)_E(460-490)_F(500-550)_ E(555-570)_F(580-680)_S) directed and combined the excitation lights through a fiber-optic patch cord (Doric; MFP_200/230/900–0.57_1.0_FCM_ZF1.25_LAF) and rotary joint (Doric; FRJ_200/230/LWMJ-0.57_1m_FCM_0.15_FCM). The implanted fiber-optic cannula was connected to the patch cord by a 1.25 mm mating sleeve (Doric; 1.25 mm with black cover; Sleeve_ZR_1.25-BK). Fluorescence emissions returned through the same patch cord and were separated in the MiniCube before being redirected to photoreceivers (Model 2151, DC low setting; Newport Corporation, Irvine, CA). Time-locked video was captured by an overhead camera and recorded through Doric Neuroscience Studio during fiber photometry recordings.

Prior to experiments, the patch cord was photobleached with 405 and 465 nm light for at least one hour to reduce autofluorescence from the fiber-optic cable. The power output was adjusted to ~45–55 µW at the tip of the patch cord before each recording. Data were acquired using Doric Neuroscience Studio software at a rate of 12.0 ksps, then decimated by a factor of 100. After the patch cord was attached, mice were allowed to acclimate in their home cage for 15 min to allow environmental and patch cord habituation before the start of the experiment. Behavioral tests were performed at one-week intervals prior to pharmacological tests. All animals received an injection of WIN 55212-2 after completion of all behavior experiments to validate the signal. The open field test was 10 minutes. The elevated plus maze test was 15 min.

For open field and elevated plus maze tests, mouse movement and zone transitions were automatically detected using Danse. For the elevated plus maze, a baseline period of −20 to −15 s was selected because mice were typically positioned away from the center or open arms at this point. This baseline ( − 20 to −15 s) was compared with the 5 s preceding a compartment transition (pre-event: −5 to 0 s) and the periods directly after a compartment transition (post-event 1: 0 to 5 s; post-event 2: 5 to 10 s).

For fear conditioning and recall, mice were trained in a Med Associates (St. Albans, VT) operant chamber. After a 2-minute habituation period, mice were presented with a 20-second tone (4 kHz) that co-terminated with a 1-second, 0.5 mA foot shock. This tone–shock pairing was repeated four times with a randomized inter-trial interval of 60–80 s. Twenty-four hours later, fear recall was assessed in a novel context in which mice received tone presentations without shock. A Med Associates console sent TTL signals for tone and shock to Doric console that were used for peri-event analysis.

For 2-MT exposure experiments mice were placed into a new home cage without bedding and allowed to acclimate for 20 minutes. Then, a small glass container, which held a piece of filter paper with 20 μl of ddH2O was placed in the home cage. After 20 min, an identical container that held a piece of filter paper with 20 μl of 2-MT (diluted 1:50 times in ddH2O) was replaced by the container holding only ddH2O.

Following the completion of the experiments, AAV expression in the ACC, and the optic fiber implants in the DMS were verified by post hoc imaging on a VS200 slide scanner (Evident Scientific).

Analysis of fiber photometry results

The fiber photometry data were analyzed using Doric Neuroscience Studio and Danse software (Doric Inc, Québec, Canada). For Z-score generation, a running average of 2 seconds was used to smooth the raw 465 nm and 405 nm traces. The z-scored ΔF/F values were calculated by applying an adaptive iteratively reweighted Penalized Least Squares (airPLS) fit to the data70, the fitted 405 nm signal was subtracted from the fitted 465 GRABeCB2.0 signal and then divided by the fitted 405 nm signal. Location of the mouse, speed, and zone entries and exits were tracked in Danse. Peri-event analysis of z-scored values and area under the curve was performed with the “peri-event” function in Danse. For GRABeCB2.0 transient frequency analysis, data were exported to MiniAnalysis.

In vivo optogenetics during behavior

Following AAV injections and fiber optic cannula placement (200 μm core, 0.57 NA), mice were allowed to recover for 6 weeks for viral gene expression and genetic knockout to occur. Mice were acclimated to the optical patch cord for 10 minutes for two days (20 min total) before the start of behavioral experiments. Behavioral measurement and control of in vivo optogenetic stimulation was coordinated through ANY-Maze. For optogenetic experiments, mice received 20 Hz pulses (5 ms on 45 ms off) of 473 nm laser stimulation from the ANY-maze controlled computer which were converted to TTL signals via and AMI-2 optogenetic interface (Wood Dale, Illinois) and sent to a DPSSL laser system (Laserglow, Toronto, Ontario). Laser intensity was measured at ~4 mW at the fiber tip. For RTPP, mice were placed in a 27 cm×51 cm apparatus bisected into two chambers with equal area connected by a 10 cm wide opening. Mice were given a 10-minute baseline to acclimate to the chamber prior to testing. Then, mice underwent a 10-minute period where optical stimulation occurred in one side of the apparatus, but not the other. The percent time spent on the stimulation side and the distance traveled were compared during the stimulation phase to the baseline. For the open field test, mice were placed in the chamber for a 2-min baseline that was followed by a 2-minute stimulation period and a 2-min off period. In the elevated plus maze, mice underwent a 2-min baseline, followed by a 2-min stimulation period and a 2-min stimulation-off period.

Chemical reagents

AM251 and WIN 55212-2 were purchased from Tocris Bioscience (Ellisville, MO). JZL-184 was purchased from Cayman Chemical. Δ9-Tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) was provided by the NIDA Drug Supply Program. All other common chemicals were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO).

Statistics

Data are presented as the mean ± s.e.m. Animal numbers and sample sizes are calculated based on statistical power analysis (α = 5%, Power = 0.8). For behavioral tests, variance and normality were assessed prior to statistical testing with SigmaPlot. Data sets were compared with either Student’s t-test, Paired t-test, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, one-way ANOVA, or two-way repeated measures (RM) ANOVA followed by Tukey’s, Bonferroni, or Holm-Sidak post hoc analysis. Post hoc analyses were performed only when ANOVA yielded a significant main effect or a significant interaction between the two factors. Results were significant at p < 0.05.
