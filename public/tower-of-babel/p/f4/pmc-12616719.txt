LINC00114 promotes colorectal cancer metastasis by targeting HNRNPA1 to regulate glutamine metabolism reprogramming and angiogenesis
Authors: Shiyu Tang, Meng Liu, Yangyang Zhu, Lifa Li, Chen Qing, Yuehua Guan, Tong Zhou, Xuegui Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a common type of gastrointestinal malignancy, and it has a close connection with long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs). This study aims to examine the involvement of long noncoding RNA LINC00114, which targets heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein A1 (HNRNPA1) in regulating glutamine metabolism and angiogenesis in the metastasis of colorectal cancer (CRC). LINC00114 and HNRNPA1 levels are measured in CRC tissues and cells to determine their expression levels. Then, siRNA targeting LINC00114 (si-LINC00114) is used to transfect CRC cells, and cell proliferation and metastasis are detected. The influence of exogenous glucose and glutamine supplementation on angiogenesis induced by LINC00114 in CRC is investigated in HUVECs. Glutamine metabolism in CRC cells is also detected. Furthermore, the role of LINC00114 in CRC xenograft tumors is studied in vivo. LINC00114 and HNRNPA1 are highly expressed in CRC and positively correlate with CD31. si-LINC00114 significantly inhibits proliferation, metastasis and HNRNPA1 expression in CRC cells. An RNA-binding-protein immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay confirms that LINC00114 can bind to HNRNPA1 and positively regulate its expression. Further experiments confirm that si-LINC00114 significantly inhibits cell proliferation and tubule formation in HUVECs. Exogenous glucose and glutamine supplementation significantly promotes the levels of LINC00114 and HNRNPA1 in CRC cells and promotes tubule formation in HUVECs. In addition, transfection of CRC cells with si-LINC00114 and/or oe-HNRNPA1 regulates glutamine metabolism in CRC cells. Animal studies confirm that intervention with LINC00114 represses the progression and vascular normalization of CRC and regulates glutamine metabolism. In conclusion, LINC00114 promotes CRC metastasis by targeting HNRNPA1 to regulate glutamine metabolic reprogramming and angiogenesis.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a prevalent type of gastrointestinal malignancy and is the third most frequently occurring cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related death [1]. The invasion and migration of CRC are the main causes of death [2]. Therefore, understanding the molecular mechanism underlying CRC metastasis is crucial for discovering more efficient treatment approaches. Long-chain noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) are a category of RNA molecules whose length exceeds 200 nt and do not produce proteins, which are evolutionarily conserved [3]. LncRNA, as the largest class of noncoding transcripts, has a wide range of functions, affecting gene expression by regulating chromatin modification, protein synthesis, RNA maturation, and transport [4]. Studies have verified that alterations in the expressions of several lncRNAs occur in a variety of diseases, such as malignant tumors, affecting many biological functions and playing essential roles in the process of tumor formation [5]. The relationships between lncRNAs and CRC development and progression have been extensively discussed [ 6, 7] . The ceRNA network associated with CRC has been identified through a comprehensive gene expression database, in which lncRNA 00114 (LINC00114) is highly expressed in CRC and is thought to be associated with overall survival [8]. Another study confirmed that LINC00114 effectively predicts overall survival and plays an important role in colon adenocarcinoma [9]. LINC00114 has been shown to be one of the lncRNA markers with the greatest prognostic value in CRC [10]. LINC00114 can promote the development of CRC by inhibiting EZH2/DNMT1-induced miR-133b expression [11]. Our bioinformatics analysis also revealed that LINC00114 is significantly overexpressed in CRC. In addition, studies have shown that LINC00114 can promote the progression of esophageal [12] and nasopharyngeal cancers [13]. However, whether LINC00114 participates in CRC metastasis and its mechanism are unclear.

Heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (HNRNPs) are a group of RNA-binding proteins involved in various biological processes [14]. HNRNPA1, a highly prevalent and widely expressed constituent of this protein family, is involved in malignant transformation pathways [15]. In addition to promoting the expression of specific protein variants through selective mRNA splicing, HNRNPA1 is also responsible for controlling the levels and translation of crucial genes implicated in the progression of cancer [ 16, 17] . Significant levels of HNRNPA1 have been observed in different cancer types [ 18, 19] . Furthermore, HNRNPA1 is highly expressed in CRC and promotes aerobic glycolysis through pyruvate kinase M (PKM), thus promoting the growth of CRC [20]. HNRNPA1 also inhibits the maturation process of messenger RNA for angiopoietin-like protein 4 (ANGPTL4), resulting in compromised tumor angiogenesis and heightened metastasis in CRC [21]. Our bioinformatics analysis revealed that HNRNPA1 is a downstream target of LINC00114, suggesting that targeting HNRNPA1 with LINC00114 may be a key pathway for regulating CRC metastasis.

It has been reported that tumor cells need to reprogram metabolism to meet energy requirements, and glutamine metabolism is an important pathway [22]. Glutamine metabolism is considered one of the crucial distinctions between carcinoma cells and other normal cells, as carcinoma cells rely on it [23]. Tumor cells can obtain energy and nutrients through glutamine metabolism to support their proliferation [24]. In addition, glutamine metabolism is also influenced by some oncogenes or anticancer genes. LncRNAs are believed to modulate glutamine metabolism through diverse mechanisms, at either the transcriptional or the posttranscriptional level [25]. The intricate regulation of glutamine metabolism is influenced by various characteristics of lncRNAs. In addition, the importance of HNRNPA1 in the metabolic recognition of CRC has been demonstrated [ 26, 27] .

In the present study, we aimed to elucidate the mechanism by which LINC00114, which targets HNRNPA1, regulates glutamine metabolism to promote the metastasis of CRC to provide experimental evidence for further understanding the pathogenesis of CRC.

Materials and Methods

Online database analysis

The expression levels of LINC00114 in CRC was analyzed through the ENCORI line database ( https://rnasysu.com/encori/panGeneDiffExp.php).

Tissue sample collection

In this study, 15 pairs of CRC and corresponding noncancer tissues were obtained from the Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College. After diagnosis and confirmation by two pathologists, these tissues were used for RT-PCR and immunohistochemical analysis. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical University (No. 2022ER439-1), and all the subjects were aware of the sample collection.

Immunohistochemical staining

The expressions of CD31 and HNRNPA1 in cancer tissues and Ki67, GLUT1, HK2, GLS1, and GS in mouse CRC xenograft tumor tissues were detected via immunohistochemical staining. After fixation, the tissue samples were paraffin-embedded and sectioned. The paraffin sections were dewaxed with water, and antigen retrieval was performed with citrate buffer (pH 6.0; ZLI-9065; Zsbio, Beijing, China). After incubation with 3% H 2O 2 to block endogenous peroxidase, the goat serum was used to block the sections for 20 min. Then, primary antibodies against CD31 (1:100, ab182981; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), HNRNPA1 (1:200, Bs-17330r; Bioss, Beijing, China), Ki67 (1:100, GB121141; Servicebio, Wuhan, China), GLUT1 (1:200, ab115730; Abcam), HK2 (1:100, bs-9455R; Bioss), GLS1 (1:100, bs-10341R; Bioss) and GS (1:300, ab176562; Abcam) were used to incubate with the sections overnight at 4°C. Then, the sections were incubated with secondary antibody (1:100, GB23303; Servicebio) at 37°C for 30 min, followed by DAB (ZLI-9018; ZSbio) color development and hematoxylin (LM10N13; J&K Scientific, Guangzhou, China) re-staining. The sections were dehydrated and sealed, and image collection was carried out via a microcamera system (BA400Digital; MOTIC, Xiamen, China), and the Halo 101-WL-HALO-1 system (Indica labs, Albuquerque, USA) was used to calculate the DAB-positive tissue. In addition, the stained sections were scored as follows: cells with <10% staining were scored as 1; cells with 10%–49% staining were scored as 2; those with 50%–74% staining were scored as 3; and those with 75%–100% staining were scored as 4. The staining color scores were as follows: light yellow particles 1, brown yellow particles 2, and brown particles 3. The final score was defined as the staining number score multiplied by the staining color score. The scores of negative expressions were between 0 and 5, and scores that exceeded 5 were identified as positive expression [28].

Cell culture

Human normal colon epithelial NCM460 cells (SNL-519) were procured from SUNNCELL (Wuhan, China); the CRC cell lines HT-29 (CL-0118), SW480 (CL-0223B), Caco-2 (CL-0050) and COLO 205 (CL-0066) were purchased from Procell (Wuhan, China); and HUVECs (iCell-h110) were purchased from iCell (Shanghai, China). NCM460 and COLO 205 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (PM150110; Procell), and HT-29 cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5A medium (PM150710; Procell). SW480 and Caco-2 cells were cultured in DMEM (PM150210; Procell). All media contained 10% FBS (C04001; Viva Cell, Denzlingen, Germany) and penicillin-streptomycin solution (BL505A; Biosharp, Guangzhou, China). HUVECs were cultured in HUVEC-specific medium (iCell-h110-001b; iCell).

Cell transfection

Three small interfering RNAs targeting LINC00114 (si-LINC00114-1, si-LINC00114-2, and si-LINC00114-3) and short hairpin RNAs targeting LINC00114 (sh-LINC00114-1 and sh-LINC00114-2) were generated by RiboBio (Guangzhou, China) to interfere with the level of LINC00114 in CRC cells, in which siRNA was used in cell experiments and shRNA stable cell lines were used in subsequent animal experiments. The specific sequence information is shown in Supplementary Table S1. In addition, the HNRNPA1 overexpression plasmid was constructed via the pcDNA vector (oe-HNRNPA1), which was also obtained from RiboBio. HT-29 and SW480 cells were transfected with si-NC and si-LINC00114 with a RiboFect CP Transfection Kit (C10511-05; RiboBio). First, to prepare the transfection complex, the siRNA was diluted with RiboFect CP Buffer, incubated with RiboFect CP Reagent for 15 min, and then added to the cell medium to transfect the cells. For plasmid transfection, Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) was used, and the cell density reached 80% during transfection. The transfection reagent and DNA were diluted in Opti-MEM and then co-incubated to prepare DNA-lipid complex. The prepared transfection complex was subsequently used for transfection. After 6 h, all the media used for the transfections were replaced by fresh complete media, and a follow-up experiment was carried out after continuous culture for 42 h.

Cell proliferation activity analysis

The analysis of cell proliferative activity was performed via a cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay and a colony formation assay. For the CCK-8 assay, 4 × 10 4 cells were seeded into each well of 96-well plates and cultured at 37°C in 5% CO 2. Following various treatments according to the experimental design, the cells were incubated with CCK-8 reagent (BS350A; Biosharp, Guangzhou, China) for 2 h. The absorption value was then measured at a wavelength of 450 nm. For the colony formation experiment, 700 cells/well from different treatment groups were inoculated into 6-well plates and incubated at 37°C and 5% CO 2. The culture was terminated when visible clones appeared. After fixation with pure ethanol for 30 min, 0.1% crystal violet dye solution (JC3913; BOMEI, Shenzhen, China) was used to stain for 15–20 min, and the number of clones greater than 50 cells was counted under a DMI1 microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Cell invasion analysis

Transwell invasion wells were prepared with BD Matrigel (97011; Corning, New York, USA), and 4 × 10 4 cells/well were inoculated into the transwell chamber after different treatments. The lower chamber was subsequently supplemented with 600 μL of medium containing 20% FBS and incubated for 12–48 h. After fixation with precooled ethanol for 30 min, 0.1% crystal violet dye solution (JC3913; BOMEI) was used to stain the cells for 30 min. The number of invasive cells was counted via the DMI1 microscope.

Cell migration analysis

Even lines were drawn behind the 6-well plates, with at least five lines passing through each well. Approximately 2 × 10 5 cells/well of different types of transfected cells were seeded into 6-well plates. When the cells grew into a single layer, the cells were scratched perpendicular to the back surface, washed with PBS and placed in an incubator for further culture. At 0 and 24 h, the scratch status of each well was photographed under the DMI1 microscope.

Tubule formation test

The cell suspensions of different treatments were adjusted to 2 × 10 5 cells/mL, and 500 μL was added to each well of 24-well plates treated with Matrigel (97011; Corning) and cultured for 5 h. Then, the samples were observed and photographed under the DMI1 microscope.

RNA-binding protein immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

The cells were collected and resuspended in PBS. Then, RIP lysis buffer was added for lysis. Specific antibody against HNRNPA1 (A7491; ABclonal, Wuhan, China) was added and incubated at 4°C overnight to form RNA-protein complexes. Then, protein A/G magnetic beads were added to further enrich the complexes. After washing away the unbound substances, RNA purification was performed. The extracted RNA was used for subsequent RT-PCR analysis to detect the expression level of LINC00114.

Animal experimental design

Twenty-four SPF BALB/c male nude mice, aged 4–6 weeks, were obtained from Chengdu Dossy Experimental Animals Co., Ltd (Chengdu, China) with a license number: SCXK (Chuan) 2020-030. The experimental samples were divided into model group, sh-NC group, sh-LINC00114-1 group, and sh-LINC00114-2 group ( n = 6). The nude mice were subcutaneously injected in the groin of their right hind leg with a cell suspension containing different treatments in each group at a volume of 0.1 mL and a concentration of 1 × 10 7 cells/mL. The model group was inoculated with normal HT-29 cells, and the sh-NC, LINC00114-1 and sh-LINC00114-2 groups were inoculated with sh-NC, sh-LINC00114-1 or sh-LINC00114-2 stably transfected HT-29 cells, respectively. The mice were observed continuously for 5 weeks from the day of inoculation, and the weight and volume of the xenograft tumors were measured weekly. Five weeks later, the animals were sacrificed under anesthesia. This study received ethical approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of North Sichuan Medical University.

Histopathological staining

The tumor samples were collected and fixed with 10% formaldehyde (Chengdu Chron Chemicals, Chengdu, China). Then, the samples were dehydrated, embedded and sliced. Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) and Masson pathological staining were performed with the corresponding staining kits (BOMEI). After being dyed, the sections were dehydrated before being cleaned with xylene, sealed with neutral gum, and examined and photographed under a microscope (Panthera; MOTIC, Xiamen, China).

Immunofluorescence staining

Mouse tumor sections were dewaxed in water, and antigen retrieval was performed with citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After the samples were blocked with goat serum for 20 min, they were incubated with an anti-CD31 (1:200, ab12981; Abcam) primary antibody overnight at 4°C and with a FITC-conjugated secondary antibody (1:100, GB22303; Servicebio) at 37°C for 30 min. Then, they were incubated with DAPI (ZLI-9557; Zsbio) for 10 min to stain the nuclei. An anti-fluorescence attenuating tablet (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used to seal the sections, which were then observed via a fluorescence microscope (OlyVIA; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Metabolic index detection

Glucose uptake, adenosine-triphosphate (ATP) content, lactate level, and pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) activity in CRC cells and xenograft tumor tissues were evaluated via a glucose uptake assay kit (36503; AAT Bioquest, Pleasanton, USA), ATP assay kit (A095-1-1; Jiancheng, Nanjing, China), lactic acid assay kit (A019-2-1; Jiancheng) and PDH assay kit (ZC-S0391; ZCIBIO, Shanghai, China) respectively. In addition, serum was extracted from the mice in each group via centrifugation, and the levels of ALT, AST, BUN, Cr, LDH, CK and GLU in the serum were measured using the alanine aminotransferase (ALT) assay kit (C009-2-1; Jiancheng), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) assay kit (C010-2-1; Jiancheng), urea (BUN) assay kit (C013-2-1; Jiancheng), creatinine (Cr) assay kit (C011-2-1; Jiancheng), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay kit (A020-2-2; Jiancheng), creatine kinase (CK) assay kit (A032-1-1; Jiancheng), and glucose (GLU) kit (A154-1-1; Jiancheng) respectively. All operations strictly followed the instructions provided with the kits.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection

The ROS levels in the cells and xenograft tumor tissues were determined via an ROS assay kit (S0033S; Beyotime). Cell precipitates were obtained from the tissue samples after grinding and cracking. The cells subjected to different treatments were supplemented with 10 μM DCFH-DA and incubated at 37°C for 20 min. After the cells were washed with serum-free cell culture medium, they were resuspended in PBS, and flow cytometry was used for detection and analysis (Cytoflex; Beckman, Franklin Lakes, USA).

Western blot analysis

The cells and xenograft tumor tissues from the different treatment groups were collected and lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (G2002; Servicebio), and quantitative analysis was performed with a BCA kit (P0009; Beyotime). After the proteins were denatured and separated via SDS‒PAGE, they were subsequently transferred to PVDF membranes (ISEQ00010; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA). Then, the PVDF membranes were blocked for 2 h with 5% skim milk powder, and incubated with primary antibodies against HNRNPA1 (1:2000, A7491; ABclonal), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF, 1:2000, bs-0279R; Bioss), hypoxia-inducible factor 1-α (HIF-1α, 1:2000, A22041; ABclonal), CD31 (1:1000, A20228; ABclonal), PDK1 (1:1000, A0834; ABclonal), PKM2 (1:1000, A18799; ABclonal), GLUT1 (1:2000, AF5462; Affbiotech, Shanghai, China), HK2 (1:500, A9337; ABclonal), GLS1 (1:1000, A3885; ABclonal), GS (1:2000, DF7607; Affbiotech) or β-actin (1:50000, AC026; ABclonal). The membranes were then incubated with a goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:5000, S0001; Affinity, Liyang, China) for 2 h at room temperature. The final results were visualized via enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (17046; Zen-bio, Suzhou, China) and analyzed with a Tanon 5200 chemiluminescent instrument (Tanon, Shanghai, China).

Reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from tissue and cell samples using a total RNA isolation kit (RE-03014; FOREGENE, Chengdu, China). For lncRNA detection, a riboSCRIPT mRNA/lncRNA qRT-PCR Starter Kit (C11030-1; RiboBio) was used for reverse transcription and real-time fluorescence quantification. For mRNA detection, a PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (RR047A; TaKaRa, Dalian, China) was used for reverse transcription, and the obtained cDNA was subjected to real-time PCR with TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (RR820A; TaKaRa). The CT values of each sample were analyzed with Thermo Fisher Scientific PikoReal software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA), and when ACTIN was used as the internal reference, the relative mRNA level was obtained via the 2 –∆∆CT method. Statistical analysis

Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS) 22.0 software (IBM, Armonk, USA) was used to perform the statistical analysis of the experimental data. The results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. The expression of genes and proteins in cancer tissues was analyzed via paired t tests, while Pearson correlation analysis was used for correlation analysis. Comparisons between two groups were carried out via the independent sample t test, and comparisons among multiple groups were conducted via one-way analysis of variance. P < 0.05 indicates a statistically significant difference.

Results

LINC00114 and HNRNPA1 are highly expressed in CRC

As shown in Figure 1A, the ENCORI online database analysis results revealed that, compared with that in normal tissues, the expression of LINC00114 in CRC tissues was significantly increased ( P < 0.001). To further clarify the role of LINC00114 and HNRNPA1 in CRC, 15 pairs of CRC and paracancerous samples were collected, and the mRNA expression levels of LINC00114 and HNRNPA1 were detected. These expression levels were found to be significantly increased in CRC tissues ( P < 0.001, Figure 1B). Western blot analysis results revealed that the protein expression levels of HNRNPA1 and CD31 in CRC tissues were significantly greater than those in noncancer tissues ( P < 0.05, Figure 1C). The immunohistochemical results were consistent with the western blot analysis results, and the percentage of HNRNPA1- and CD31-positive tissue and the IHC staining score in CRC tissues were markedly greater than those in noncancer tissues ( P < 0.001, Figure 1D–F), suggesting high expression of HNRNPA1 and increased angiogenesis ability in CRC. The results of the correlation analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between HNRNPA1 and CD31, as well as between LINC00114 and CD31 ( P < 0.01, Figure 1G,H), suggesting that HNRNPA1 and LINC00114 are related to angiogenesis in CRC. RT-PCR results in CRC cell lines also revealed that, compared with those in NCM460 cells, the mRNA levels of LINC00114 in HT-29, SW480, Caco-2, and COLO 205 CRC cells were markedly increased ( P < 0.001, Figure 1I), among which HT-29 and SW480 cells presented the highest expression and were screened for subsequent in vitro studies. LINC00114 promotes CRC cell proliferation and metastasis by targeting HNRNPA1

A plasmid overexpressing LINC00114 (oe-LINC00114) was used to transfect HT-29 and SW480 CRC cells, and the transfection efficiency was detected by RT-PCR. As shown in Figure 2A, compared with that in the oe-NC group, the expression of LINC00114 in cells transfected with oe-LINC00114 was significantly increased ( P < 0.001). Compared with that of the oe-NC group, the cell proliferation activity of the oe-LINC00114 group was significantly greater ( P < 0.001, Figure 2B), suggesting that the overexpression of LINC00114 promoted the proliferation of CRC cells. Three siRNA interference sequences targeting LINC00114 (si-LINC00114) were subsequently used to transfect HT-29 and SW480 CRC cells. The RT-PCR results revealed that the level of LINC00114 in the three si-LINC00114 groups was markedly reduced ( P < 0.001, Figure 2C), which confirmed that the transfection effect was good. Subsequent experiments were conducted using si-LINC00114-3, and both CCK-8 ( Figure 2D) and colony formation experiments ( Figure 2E,F) revealed that the proliferative activity of the two CRC cell lines in the si-LINC00114 group was significantly decreased ( P < 0.001). In addition, transwell ( Figure 2G,H) and scratch experiments ( Figure 2I,J) revealed that the abilities of invasion and migration were markedly reduced in the si-LINC00114 group ( P < 0.001). Further detection revealed that the level of HNRNPA1 in the oe-LINC00114 group was significantly increased in CRC cells ( P < 0.001, Figure 3A,B), and the level of HNRNPA1 in the si-LINC00114 group was strongly reduced in CRC cells ( P < 0.001, Figure 3C,D). These findings suggest that LINC00114 can positively regulate the expression of HNRNPA1 in CRC. In addition, an RIP assay followed by RT-PCR analysis of LINC00114 confirmed the binding relationship between LINC00114 and HNRNPA1 ( Figure 3E). These results suggest that LINC00114 can promote the viability and metastasis of CRC cells by targeting and positively modifying HNRNPA1 level. Exogenous supplementation of glutamine and glucose in CRC promotes angiogenesis by promoting the expression of LINC00114 and HNRNPA1

Angiogenesis is generally associated with cancers, and previous correlation analyses showed that LINC00114 and HNRNPA1 are associated with angiogenesis (Figure 1 G,H). To further elucidate the effect of LINC00114 on angiogenesis, HUVECs were transfected with si-LINC00114 to determine the effect of LINC00114 on angiogenesis. The results revealed that the proliferative activity of HUVECs in the si-LINC00114 group was decreased ( P < 0.001, Figure 4A), and the amount of tubule formation was also strongly reduced ( P < 0.001, Figure 4B,C). These findings suggest that inhibiting LINC00114 can inhibit angiogenesis. In addition, glucose is a source of energy and biomass energy production that is consumed by cancer cells to support their growth. High glutamine concentrations in the blood can also be used to facilitate cancer cell biosynthesis, energy metabolism, and cellular homeostasis while fostering tumor growth. Exogenous supplementation of glutamine (Gln) and glucose (Glu) was performed in the culture medium of the cells. The cell activity measured by CCK-8 assay revealed that the cell proliferation activities in the Glu, Gln, and Glu + Gln groups were meaningfully improved ( P < 0.001, Figure 4D), and the results also indicated that CRC cell lines may prefer Gln to Glu. In addition, the RT-PCR results revealed that the levels of LINC00114 and HNRNPA1 in the Glu, Gln, and Glu + Gln groups were markedly increased ( P < 0.001, Figure 4E,F). Furthermore, CRC cells supplemented with exogenous glutamine and glucose were co-cultured with HUVECs via the transwell method at a ratio of 1:2 to analyze the effects on the angiogenesis of HUVECs. The results showed that the proliferative activity of HUVECs and the number of tubule formation were significantly greater in the Glu, Gln, and Glu + Gln groups ( P < 0.001, Figure 4G–I). These results suggest that exogenous supplementation of glutamine and glucose in CRC cells may promote angiogenesis by increasing the expressions of LINC00114 and HNRNPA1. LINC00114/HNRNPA1 regulates glutamine metabolism in CRC cells

HT29 and SW480 cells were transfected with si-LINC00114 and/or oe-HNRNPA1, respectively, to further explore the effects of LINC00114/HNRNPA1 on the metabolism of glutamine in CRC. The results revealed that the glucose uptake, ATP content, and lactic acid level in the si-LINC00114 group were strongly decreased, whereas the PDH content was significantly increased ( P < 0.001, Figure 5A–D). In addition, the glucose uptake, ATP content, and lactic acid content in the oe-HNRNPA1 group were markedly increased, whereas the PDH content was markedly reduced ( P < 0.001, Figure 5A–D). After co-transfection with si-LINC00114 and oe-HNRNPA1, the decreases in glucose uptake, ATP content, and lactic acid content caused by si-LINC00114 and the increase caused by oe-HNRNPA1 were reversed ( P < 0.001, Figure 5A–D). The ROS content in the cells of the si-LINC00114 group was decreased, whereas the ROS content in the cells of the oe-HNRNPA1 group was increased ( P < 0.001, Figure 5E). After co-transfection with si-LINC00114 and oe-HNRNPA1, the regulation of ROS by si-LINC00114 or oe-HNRNPA1 was reversed ( P < 0.001, Figure 5E). Moreover, the protein and mRNA expression levels of glucose transporter type 1 ( GLUT1), pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 ( PDK1), hexokinase 2 ( HK2), pyruvate kinase isozyme type M2 ( PKM2), glutaminase-1 ( GLS1) and glutamine synthetase ( GS) in the si-LINC00114 group were markedly lower than those in the control group and were significantly greater in the oe-HNRNPA1 group ( P < 0.001, Figure 6). After co-transfection with si-LINC00114 and oe-HNRNPA1, the decrease in expression caused by si-LINC00114 was increased, whereas the increase in gene expression caused by oe-HNRNPA1 was decreased ( P < 0.001, Figure 6). These results revealed that LINC00114/HNRNPA1 participated in guiding glutamine metabolism reprogramming in CRC cells. Interference with LINC00114 inhibits the growth and angiogenesis of CRC xenograft tumors

To further investigate the effects of LINC00114 intervention on CRC in vivo, xenograft tumor-bearing mice were used. Figure 7A shows images of xenograft tumors collected after the experiment. The results revealed that the weights and volumes of the xenograft tumors in the sh-LINC00114-1 and sh-LINC00114-2 groups were both markedly lower ( P < 0.001, Figure 7B,C). The mRNA levels of LINC00114 and HNRNPA1 in the xenograft tumors of the mice in the sh-LINC00114-1 and sh-LINC00114-2 groups were strongly reduced ( P < 0.001, Figure 7D), and the protein expression level of HNRNPA1 was also significantly decreased in the sh-LINC00114-1 and sh-LINC00114-2 groups ( P < 0.05, Figure 7L,M). Further histopathological examination of the xenograft tumors revealed that in the model group, the tumor cells were irregularly arranged, with different sizes, active pathological mitosis, more fibrous connective tissue and rich vascular distribution in the interstitium. The sh-NC had no significant effect on the model group. The tumor tissues of the sh-LINC00114-1 and sh-LINC00114-2 groups presented focal necrosis, irregular arrangement, and pathological mitosis of tumor cells in the tumor growth area ( Figure 7E). Immunohistochemical and Masson staining revealed that the percentage of Ki-67-positive tissue ( Figure 7F,G) and the percentage of fibrotic tissue ( Figure 7H,I) in both the sh-LINC00114-1 and sh-LINC00114-2 groups were strongly reduced ( P < 0.001). Immunofluorescence staining also revealed that the model group and the sh-NC group had noticeable vascular structures, the level of CD31 in the sh-LINC00114-1 and sh-LINC00114-2 groups was markedly reduced, and the vascular structure was not obvious ( P < 0.001, Figure 7J,K). Western blot analysis results also revealed that the protein levels of HIF-1α and VEGF in the sh-LINC00114-1 and sh-LINC00114-2 groups were strongly reduced ( P < 0.001, Figure 7L,N). These results indicated that intervention with LINC00114 in vivo inhibited the growth and angiogenesis of CRC xenograft tumors. Discussion

The occurrence of CRC is increasing with changes in dietary habits and structure, and CRC is characterized by a young age of onset, low degree of differentiation, and high degree of malignancy, which seriously endangers life safety [29]. LncRNAs are widely expressed in eukaryotes, and their abnormal expression is strongly associated with CRC progression, making them valuable molecular targets for CRC treatment [6]. The expression of LINC00114 appeared to be increased in CRC during this study, whereas reducing the level of LINC00114 hindered the growth and metastasis of CRC. Additionally, LINC00114 directly binds to HNRNPA1 to positively regulate its expression. Exogenous supplementation of glutamine and glucose in CRC promoted cell proliferation and angiogenesis by increasing the levels of LINC00114 and HNRNPA1. Further study confirmed that the regulatory network of LINC00114/HNRNPA1 is involved in glutamine metabolism in CRC. This study confirmed that LINC00114, which targets HNRNPA1, regulates glutamine metabolic reprogramming and angiogenesis to promote the metastasis of CRC.

LncRNAs are a particular type of noncoding RNA involved in various stages of tumorigenesis and progression, including cell proliferation, apoptosis, metastasis, and angiogenesis [30]. Studies have demonstrated that lncRNAs not only function as oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes in tumorigenesis but also have broad application prospects in the diagnosis and treatment of cancer due to their tissue and cell specificity [31]. Several lncRNAs have been identified as regulators of CRC cell proliferation and metastasis [ 32- 34] . Research has demonstrated a strong correlation between LINC00114 and the incidence and progression of malignancies [9]. Esophageal carcinomas have increased level of LINC00114 expression, and inhibiting LINC00114 prevents the glycolysis, invasion, migration, and proliferation of cells [12]. LINC00114 is upregulated in nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and LINC00114 knockdown inhibits proliferation, migration, and radioresistance [13]. The elevated expression of LINC00114 in CRC tissues and cells was also verified by our investigation, and the inhibition of LINC00114 inhibited CRC metastasis, suggesting that LINC00114 acts as a cancer-promoting factor in CRC. In addition, this study demonstrated that LINC00114 binds to HNRNPA1 and positively regulates its expression. HNRNPA1, a constituent of the HNRNP family, is expressed at elevated levels in lung cancer [35], gastric cancer [36], and CRC [37]. HNRNPA1 can participate in telomere length maintenance by activating telomerase, which triggers malignant transformation, thereby promoting cell cycle progression [38] and aerobic glycolysis [39] to support cancer growth. Numerous studies have verified that HNRNPA1 is a promising cancer biomarker that controls carcinogenesis via a number of methods that affect certain cancer characteristics [40]. This study revealed that HNRNPA1 overexpression significantly promoted glucose uptake, ATP content, lactic acid level, and PDH activity in CRC and significantly promoted the gene expression of GLUT1, PDK1, HK2, PKM2, GLS1, and GS. In addition, HNRNPA1 significantly inhibited the inhibitory effect of LINC00114 intervention on the above indicators, suggesting that LINC00114, which targets HNRNPA1, significantly promotes metabolic reprogramming in CRC.

The entire cancer process is accompanied by metabolic changes, and metabolic pathways are believed to be key to cancer cell metastasis [41]. Metabolic reprogramming enables cancer cells to maintain proliferation and overcome metabolic challenges associated with oxygen and nutrient limitations [42]. Current studies have revealed the role of these pathways in promoting metastasis in cells, including ROS-mediated signaling, epigenetic regulation, and interactions with the extracellular matrix [43]. Amino acid metabolism is thought to play a significant role in cancer metastasis, and glutamine metabolism is the most common amino acid metabolic pathway [22]. Glutamine, the most prevalent amino acid in the blood, can supply nutrients and energy for cell development and proliferation and is an important metabolic substance in cells [44]. Research has demonstrated that glutamine metabolism is a key and important feature of cancer cells [45]. Despite being nonessential for cells, glutamine plays a vital role in the growth of cancer cells and is the second most important nutrient, following glucose, for promoting the multiplication of tumor cells [46]. Studies have shown that the absorption of glutamine by cancer cells is significantly increased, exceeding the requirement of essential amino acids [47]. Glutamine metabolism is involved in the production of ATP and the biosynthesis of nucleotides, lipids, and proteins in cancer cells to provide a source of energy and material for the growth, diffusion, and metastasis of cancer cells [48]. Recently, cancer cells have been shown to use glutamine and other sources of nutrients to survive, and some cancer cells even prefer “additive” glutamine to glucose [49]. For most cancer cells, increased glutamine is essential for energy production and cell biosynthesis [50]. This study also revealed that exogenous supplementation of glutamine and glucose in CRC cells promoted proliferation and increased the levels of LINC00114 and HNRNPA1 in CRC cells, among which the effect of glutamine was greater than that of glucose. These findings also suggest that CRC cells are not entirely dependent on glucose for energy and may even prefer glutamine. Notably, exogenous glutamine and glucose supplementation in CRC cells promoted HUVEC proliferation and tubule formation, suggesting that glutamine promotes angiogenesis by promoting the expressions of LINC00114 and HNRNPA1 in CRC.

Tumor blood vessels act as the transport medium of substances in the metabolic activities of cancer cells, maintaining the supply of substances in metabolism and completing metabolite cleaning [51]. Studies have confirmed that cancers can lead to abnormal proliferation of blood vessels, and the proliferation and metastasis of cancers also require abundant blood to provide nutrients [52]. Angiogenesis is a necessary condition for the growth and metastasis of invasive cancers [53]. Disrupting the metabolic homeostasis of cancer cells by blocking the supply of substances to cancer cells through the inhibition of blood vessel formation may be an effective anticancer approach [54]. This study confirmed that LINC00114 intervention significantly inhibited vascular endothelial cell tubule formation in HUVECs, and in vivo studies demonstrated that the inhibition of LINC00114 markedly reduced angiogenesis and HIF-1α and VEGF expression in xenograft tumors and restrained the growth of CRC. These findings suggest that LINC00114 promotes the growth and metastasis of CRC by promoting angiogenesis. We also acknowledge certain limitations; for example, many key lncRNAs, miRNAs, mRNAs, and other RNAs are also involved in the process of cancer. It is also undeniable that other genes may be involved in the regulation of glucose and glutamine by LINC00114. This study is only based on a single lncRNA/mRNA regulatory network and can only clarify the role of this network in the process of CRC. Furthermore, this study only preliminarily identified the possible involvement of LINC00114 in the CRC process through the regulation of glutamine metabolic reprogramming, and further analysis of the specific mechanisms and regulatory pathways involved is needed. In addition, in in vivo experiments, the effects of LINC00114 on the proliferation and metastasis of CRC were preliminarily analyzed only in a CRC model. Furthermore, siRNA-mediated knockdown and overexpression of LINC00114 in metastatic mouse models should be used to fully understand its function in CRC metastasis. To effectively implement the findings in a clinical setting, many additional scientific experiments need to be conducted.

In conclusion, LINC00114 and HNRNPA1 are significantly overexpressed in CRC tissues and may be linked to angiogenesis. In vitro experiments confirmed that LINC00114 can bind to HNRNPA1 and positively regulate its expression. Interference with LINC00114 significantly inhibited the growth and metastasis of CRC, as well as the angiogenesis of vascular endothelial cells. Exogenous supplementation with glutamine and glucose in CRC promoted angiogenesis and cell proliferation by promoting the expressions of LINC00114 and HNRNPA1. Further studies confirmed that LINC00114/HNRNPA1 modulated glutamine metabolism in CRC. This study revealed the role of LINC00114 in CRC, suggesting that it could promote CRC metastasis by regulating glutamine metabolic reprogramming and angiogenesis through the targeting of HNRNPA1.
