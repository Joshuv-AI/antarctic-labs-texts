Management Approach to Obstructive Jaundice in Pregnancy due to Gallstones at the Milton Cato Memorial Hospital, St Vincent and the Grenadines: A Case Study
Authors: Afia Boko Ampe Asare, Francis Dwomoh, Barry Aussi, Immanuel Olarinde, Divine Amakhu Dele, Franké Joseph, Stephanie Providence, Kwame Adu Okyere Boadu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObstructive jaundice in pregnancy, though rare, can result from hormone‐induced biliary stasis and gallstone formation. Prompt recognition and timely surgical intervention, such as cholecystectomy and bile duct exploration, are critical to preventing maternal and fetal complications. This case highlights the need for vigilance in managing biliary disorders during pregnancy.

Introduction

Jaundice occurring in pregnancy confers a challenge due to the high risk of materno‐fetal morbidity and mortality [1]. It complicates about 3%–5% of pregnancies worldwide, with developing countries having a higher incidence. In South Asia and Sub‐Saharan Africa, about 75% of neonatal mortality is attributed to complications of jaundice [2]. Jaundice in pregnancy has a multifactorial etiology, including preexisting chronic liver disease (Wilson's disease, gallstone disease, viral hepatitis, Budd‐Chiari syndrome). It could also be a manifestation of certain pregnancy‐induced diseases such as HELLP syndrome, cholestatic liver of pregnancy, acute fatty liver of pregnancy, hyperemesis gravidarum, etc.

Gallstones are seen as a major cause of obstructive jaundice. According to literature [3], 40% of extra hepatic causes of jaundice are gall stone formation. Women tend to have a higher risk of gallstone formation due to higher levels of estrogen. Subsequently, pregnant women, who have even higher levels of estrogen, possess a very increased risk of gallstone disease [4]. In a 2015 study, symptomatic gallstone disease was the second most common abdominal emergency in pregnant women [5]. This incidence does not improve in developing countries where there is a comparatively delayed patient report to the hospital, inadequate logistics, among other things. During pregnancy, there is an increase in the metabolic, synthesizing, and excretory functions of the liver, which are influenced mostly by estrogen and progesterone. Elevation of estrogen during pregnancy can result in cholesterol crystal aggregation, hence increasing the viscosity of bile and the risk of cholelithiasis. Progesterone, on the other hand, causes the smooth muscle relaxation of the gallbladder, leading to bile stasis and hence an increased risk of gallstones and obstructive jaundice.

Most cases of obstructive jaundice can be treated with IV fluids, antibiotics, and in severe cases, surgical removal of the obstruction. The appropriate treatment plan is dependent on the exact cause of the jaundice and its severity. Jaundice in pregnancy is a common complication and can be treated routinely. For the safety of the fetus and avoiding the possibility of severe perinatal complications, a surgical approach during pregnancy is generally not recommended [6]. Alternatively, certain techniques such as endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography and laparoscopy have been widely useful. As reported, when necessary, laparoscopic cholecystectomy is well tolerated [7].

This study aimed to identify an alternative approach in the management of cholelithiasis during pregnancy in developing countries. Also, the study highlighted the need for appropriate medical decisions, adapting unconventional surgical techniques as well as careful monitoring of materno‐fetal health in a relatively poorer clinical setting.

Case History

A 35‐year‐old G3P2 woman at 23 weeks of gestation with a previous history of cholelithiasis presented to the Milton Cato Memorial Hospital (MCMH), St Vincent and the Grenadines, with complaints of epigastric pain. She was subsequently diagnosed with cholecystitis and conservative management with Augmentin 625 mg, was initiated. She was stabilized and discharged.

Four (4) days later, she returned to the hospital with severe sharp, persistent right epigastric pain radiating to the back. She complained of nausea with multiple episodes of vomiting a yellow‐greenish substance. On admission, a gravid conscious woman in severe discomfort was seen. The following vital signs were recorded: Temperature: 96°F, BP: 125/80 mmHg, Pulse: 75 bpm, Respiration: 26 bpm, SPo2: 98%. During the HEENT examination, an icteric sclera was the only pertinent finding. An abdominal exam, revealed hypoactive bowel sounds, a gravid abdomen tender on palpation to the right upper quadrant, a positive Murphy's sign and a palpable gall bladder of size averaging 3–4 cm. There is no significant medical history. The patient is a cook by profession and a mother of 2. She neither drinks alcohol nor smokes.

Differential Diagnosis

Obstructive jaundice secondary to gallstones.

Investigations

Initial serum analysis showed: WBC 16.51 103/μL, Total Bilirubin 301.9 μmol/L, Direct Bilirubin 131.9 μmol/L, Indirect Bilirubin 170.0 μmol/L, AST 455.0 U/L, ALT 544.1 U/L, GGT 154 U/L (Figure 1). The labs were run again 24 h after surgery (Figure 2) and 7 days after surgery (Figure 3).

Treatment

Upon consultation by a team of surgeons and obstetricians, an elective cholecystectomy was recommended after conservative therapy proved ineffective. The potential risks of the procedure including possible loss of pregnancy were vividly explained to the patient to which she obliged and consented.

During the laparotomic cholecystectomy, a midline incision was made under general anesthesia. A cholangiogram using contrast and a C‐arm X‐ray was done. The gall bladder was excised and 12 gallstones were removed. The average size of 10 gallstones was 8 mm. Two of the 12 gallstones measured 4 mm and were found in the common bile duct. A T tube was placed in the common bile duct with a Jackson Pratt drain next to it. Post‐surgery, the patient was referred to the post‐operative care unit where close monitoring, early ambulation and periodic repeats of laboratory tests were done. The patient initiated treatment with Augmentin 1.2 g IV TID, Clexane 40 mg SC OD, Flagyl 500 mg IV TID, Omeprazole 40 mg IV OD, Zantac 50 mg IV TID. The patient recovered and her laboratory values returned to within normal limits.

She was subsequently discharged after 16 days. Constant monitoring continued until the final delivery of a healthy baby at 37 weeks via Cesarean section.

Conclusion and Result

Obstetric jaundice, secondary to gall stones among pregnant women requires a careful approach in management. Generally an expectant management is recommended especially in asymptomatic cases. However certain cases require interventions as seen.

In this present study, a rare surgical approach, laparotomic cholecystectomy was performed. A procedure which was largely without complication culminated in the successful delivery of a healthy fetus at 37 weeks—11 weeks after the laparotomic surgery. This study highlights the importance of making decisions in context with the time of delivery, proper selection of surgical technique and close monitoring of the fetus.

Exploratory laparotomy remains a viable surgical alternative in very complicated cases of cholecystitis during pregnancy and also in settings where laparoscopy and ERCP remain unavailable.

Discussion

Jaundice remains a common complication associated with pregnancy. It involves multiple etiologies including gallstone formation. Cholelithiasis is a leading cause of obstructive jaundice. An icteric sclera with mild elevation of bilirubin associated with severe right epigastric pain, positive Murphy's sign, and yellow‐greenish vomitus largely suggests jaundice secondary to gallstone formation. An ultrasound provides a definitive diagnosis.

A higher risk of incidence occurs among pregnant women largely due to the elevation of plasma estrogen levels. A conservative approach to management is generally suggested for pregnant women due to the increased possibility of fetal mortality associated with surgical intervention. In severe obstetric cases of gall stones, laparoscopic cholecystectomy is considered the most proposed surgical approach. There are lower perinatal complications associated with laparoscopic than with open cholecystectomy [8]. Conservative management may suffice in uncomplicated cases, but it often fails in severe gallstone disease, as seen in our patient. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy, increasingly considered safe in pregnancy, is associated with fewer maternal and perinatal complications than open surgery [8, 9]. However, in resource‐limited settings where laparoscopy and ERCP are unavailable, open cholecystectomy remains a lifesaving alternative, despite its relatively higher risk profile. Open cholecystectomy also presents a higher rate of fetal loss [9]. However in situations where laparoscopy is not suitable (such as patients with multiple histories of abdominal surgeries, extensive inflammation, adhesions, uncontrolled bleeding, bile duct injury, anatomical variance), laparotomy offers a lifesaving alternative [10].

Open cholecystectomy, formally the gold standard of treatment for gallstones [11] still remains relevant today especially in surgical circumstances where the use of laparoscopic surgery is not suitable. As in this case, a laparotomic cholecystectomy remained the most viable option due to the relatively large number and size of stones. In developing countries, the availability of endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is not well advanced, limiting surgeons against its use. Also studies strongly associate ERCP with an increased risk of exposure to ionized radiation and electro cautery [12], making its use in pregnancy contraindicated. Careful and coordinated assessment of the patient, principally, her obstetric details, remains imperative in making this procedure successful. As noted by [10], an experienced surgeon who is comfortable performing an open cholecystectomy especially in rare but delicate cases such as this study, is of key importance.

Following the surgery in this study, the patient was kept under intense obstetric monitoring to avert any complications. Part of the final assessment was to allow the patient to progress to labor at 37 weeks preventing any possibility of rupturing suture lines during vaginal delivery. It should be noted that the surgery was performed without any complications.
