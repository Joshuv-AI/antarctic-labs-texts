For every generation, vaccines must reach everyone: closing the access gap in WHO South-East Asia
Authors: Catharina C Boehme
Published: 2026

The theme of World Immunization Week 2026 is “For every generation, vaccines work”. This reminds us of a powerful truth—vaccines have long been one of the most cost-effective tools in public health. In the last 50 years, vaccines have saved more than 150 million lives and prevented more than 30 life-threatening diseases and infections.1,2

Across the WHO South-East Asia Region, vaccines have saved 38 million lives and have accounted for a 22% reduction in the infant mortality rate.1 Further, countries in the Region have eliminated maternal and neonatal tetanus since 2016, made remarkable progress toward measles and rubella elimination, and sustained polio-free status despite persistent global threats.3,4 According to the WUENIC data released by WHO and UNICEF in July 2025, South Asia reached its highest-ever immunization coverage for children. Additionally, the zero-dose children decreased from 2.5 million in 2023 to 1.8 million in 2024.5 Despite all this progress, there are several subnational pockets of low immunization coverage, particularly in remote hill districts, urban informal settlements, border communities, and conflict-affected areas, revealing a different picture from national averages. An outcome of these missed children is the growing immunity gap, which is slowly accumulating over time. Between 2020 and 2024, an estimated 14–15 million zero-dose children accumulated in the Region.5 Number of big catch-up activities were conducted to provide protection to these missed children; however, these efforts have not yet fully closed the accumulated immunity gap. These zero-dose and under immunized children continue to drive the resurgence of vaccine-preventable diseases in the Region.

Countries in the WHO Southeast Asia region had committed to measles and rubella elimination by 2026; however, progress remains constrained.6 Sri Lanka reported measles outbreak in 2024 and now Bangladesh, India and Nepal have also reported outbreaks. The recent 2026 measles outbreak in Bangladesh further highlights the growing risks associated with immunity gaps. As of 11th May 2026, a total of 6937 confirmed and 50,500 suspected cases have been reported in Bangladesh. In addition, 65 confirmed and 350 suspected deaths have been reported, underscoring the severity of the outbreak.7 As of early 2026, India is actively fighting localized measles outbreaks, particularly in regions like Madhya Pradesh and north-east, due to pockets of low vaccination, migratory populations, and vaccine hesitancy.

Several factors have contributed to the growing immunity gap, including policy gaps such as the need to expand vaccination to older age groups in context to changing disease epidemiology, persistent subnational inequities and missed communities, and weak outbreak preparedness—particularly the limited availability of real-time, high-quality data to support timely policy decisions.

Any setback in immunization coverage will not only increase the risk of outbreaks, strain the health care system, increase out-of-pocket expenses but will also fuel vaccine hesitancy and misinformation. This weakens public confidence at a time when the Region is required to prepare for introduction of new critical vaccines like dengue, RSV and in future TB.

Adequate and sustained financing is another barrier in reaching these vulnerable children. Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance, a critical partner in helping lower-income countries sustain and expand their immunization programmes, faces a funding gap of around US$ 3 billion under its new 6.0 strategy.8 Globally, donor landscape financing for immunization is shrinking, and the consequences will be felt most sharply in countries that do not have domestic financing to support immunization. Several of the member states fall into this category. When funding tightens, vaccines do not vanish overnight—but first the outreach sessions become fewer, cold chain maintenance is affected, surveillance and lab services weaken. It is the most vulnerable communities who feel the effects first. The evolving geopolitical landscape is likely to further reduce the limited financing available, while ongoing conflicts continue to disrupt access—together worsening immunization gaps and limiting access to essential vaccines. The hard-won gains of immunization are additionally risked due to the complacency of high coverage, growing misinformation and traditional programme management.

Immunization science is an advantage for having efficient and tested tools; knowledge; and learnings from the past. What is needed now is greater efficiency—through investing in generation of evidence, prioritizing most vulnerable and using subnational real time data to guide mid-course corrections. Countries and partners must work together to conduct independent monitoring and evaluation and identify hidden data gaps to guide immunization programmes. Innovative approaches such as strengthening vaccine stockpiling mechanisms and acceleration of the long-overdue transition toward life-course immunization would be critical in coming time. Strengthening civil society’s engagement, working with communities to build trust, and enabling community-led and technology driven micro-planning and outreach will support in reaching missed and underserved populations. Ultimately, a resilient and efficient immunization programme will guide in closing the immunization access gap and ensure that, for every generation, vaccines truly reach everyone.
