RAD18‐ and BRCA1‐dependent pathways promote cellular tolerance to the nucleoside analog ganciclovir
Authors: Tasnim Ahmad, Ryotaro Kawasumi, Kouji Hirota
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGanciclovir (GCV) is a clinically important drug as it is used to treat viral infections. GCV is incorporated into the DNA during replication, where it interferes with subsequent replication on GCV‐incorporated templates. However, the effects of GCV on the host genome and the mechanisms underlying cellular tolerance to GCV remain unclear. In this study, we explored these mechanisms using a collection of mutant DT40 cells. We identified RAD17 /−, BRCA1 −/−, and RAD18 −/− cells as highly GCV‐sensitive. RAD17, a component of the alternative checkpoint‐clamp loader RAD17‐RFC, was required for the activation of the intra‐S checkpoint following GCV treatment. BRCA1, a critical factor for promoting homologous recombination (HR), was required for suppressing DNA double‐strand breaks (DSBs). Moreover, RAD18, an E3‐ligase involved in DNA repair, was critical in suppressing the aberrant ligation of broken chromosomes caused by GCV. We found that BRCA1 suppresses DSBs through HR‐mediated repair and template switching (TS)‐mediated damage bypass. Moreover, the strong GCV sensitivity of BRCA1 −/− cells was rescued by the loss of 53BP1, despite the only partial restoration in the sister chromatid exchange events which are hallmarks of HR. These results indicate that BRCA1 promotes cellular tolerance to GCV through two mechanisms, TS and HR‐mediated repair.

INTRODUCTION

Nucleoside analogs are chemical compounds with structures similar to those of nucleosides. They have been used as antiviral drugs (Witkowski et al., 1972) as these compounds interfere with DNA replication, which restricts the proliferation of viruses (Tada et al., 2015; Tsuda et al., 2017; Washif et al., 2023). This effect is exerted through certain distinct mechanisms. The nucleoside analog is incorporated at the 3′ end of nascent DNA, which inhibits the subsequent DNA polymerization reaction. This inhibitory effect is referred to as the chain‐terminating effect. When replication proceeds despite the incorporation of the nucleoside analogs into the genomic DNA, the next round of DNA replication is arrested at the sites where the nucleoside analogs have been incorporated into the template strands. Ganciclovir (GCV) is a clinically important guanine nucleoside analog used for treating cytomegalovirus infections (Zhang et al., 2021). Once this drug is incorporated into virally infected cells, it is phosphorylated by HSV‐TK, followed by further phosphorylation by endogenous kinases and conversion to GCV 5′‐triphosphate, resulting in marginal competitive inhibition of dGTP (Fillat et al., 2003; Kim et al., 2000). However, the mechanisms underlying cellular tolerance to GCV remain unclear.

Several mechanisms involved in cellular tolerance to nucleoside analogs have been reported. For example, the exonuclease activity of polymerase epsilon and tyrosyl‐DNA‐phosphodiesterase 1 (TDP1) remove inserted nucleoside analogs from the 3′ end of nascent DNA, mitigating the chain‐terminating effect (Tada et al., 2015; Tsuda et al., 2017). In another instance, two distinct mechanisms release stalled replication at the site of nucleoside analog‐incorporated damage on the template (Figure S1Aa). One is translesion DNA synthesis (TLS), which promotes bypass replication across the damaged template (Figure S1Ab), and the other is template switch (TS) which mediates DNA replication by switching the template strand from the damaged template to a damage‐free nascent template, where the progression of the replication fork is reversed converting the fork structure into a four‐way DNA junction called the chicken foot intermediate (Abe et al., 2018; Berti et al., 2020; Hirota et al., 2010; Hirota et al., 2015; Quinet et al., 2017; Ray Chaudhuri et al., 2012) (Figure S1Ac). BRCA1 plays a critical role in stabilizing these intermediates by suppressing nucleolytic degradation and promoting TS (Lemaçon et al., 2017; Schlacher et al., 2011). Replication fork collapse due to prolonged replication arrest at the damaged template leads to DNA double‐strand breaks (DSBs). These DSBs are repaired exclusively through homology‐directed repair (HDR) (Figure S1Ad). RAD18 and RNF8 collaboratively suppress the toxic action of non‐homologous end‐joining (NHEJ) at the site of replication‐associated DSBs (Figure S1B), preventing the occurrence of radial chromosomes caused by the aberrant ligation of chromosomes (Bunting et al., 2010; Kobayashi et al., 2015). Additionally, BRCA1 plays a critical role in HDR (Murai & Pommier, 2023). Consequently, defects in BRCA1 lead to defects in both HDR and TS. Recent studies have shown that the role of BRCA1 is to counteract the inhibitory role of 53BP1 in end resection and that the loss of 53BP1 in BRCA1‐deficient cells rescues the defects in HDR and TS (Figure S1C) (Bunting et al., 2010; Chen et al., 2022). Some HDR products show a crossover between the donor and recipient DNA, and such products are visualized as sister chromatin exchanges (SCEs) in mitotic spreads (Bunting et al., 2010).

Finally, DNA damage checkpoint also plays a crucial role in the tolerance of nucleoside analogs. This mechanism is activated by replication arrest‐mediated gapped DNA, which exposes single‐stranded DNA. RAD17 is a component of the alternative clamp loader RAD17‐RFC, which loads the 9‐1‐1 checkpoint clamp and stimulates the DNA damage checkpoint by inducing Chk1 phosphorylation (Day et al., 2022; Sancar et al., 2004; Smith et al., 2010).

In this study, we explored the mechanisms underlying cellular tolerance to GCV using a collection of mutant chicken DT40 cell lines (Buerstedde & Takeda, 1991) and found that RAD17 /−, BRCA1 −/−, and RAD18 −/− cells exhibit hypersensitivity to GCV. We show that RAD17 induces activation of intra‐S‐phase checkpoint and mitigates genome instability after GCV is incorporated at the end of nascent DNA. BRCA1 and RAD18 independently contribute to GCV tolerance; BRCA1 suppresses DSBs and RAD18 suppresses aberrant NHEJ, preventing toxic chromosome fusions. Moreover, we aimed to elucidate the two distinct mechanisms underlying the BRCA1‐mediated suppression of DSBs; in the mechanism with crossover, BRCA1 induces HDR, and in the mechanism without crossover, BRCA1 induces TS.

RESULTS

BRCA1, RAD17, and RAD18 play vital roles in cellular tolerance to GCV

To determine the mechanisms underlying cellular tolerance to GCV, we screened for mutant cells that showed hypersensitivity to GCV. In this screening, we used DT40 mutant collection deficient in HDR ( BRCA1 −/−, BRCA2 −/−) (Martin et al., 2007; Takizawa et al., 2010), NHEJ (KU70 −/, POLQ −/−−) (Takata et al., 1998; Yoshimura et al., 2006), HDR and NHEJ (RAD54 −/−/KU70 −/−) (Fujita et al., 2013; Takata et al., 1998), base excision repair (BER; POLB −/−, PARP1 −/−, FEN1 −/−) (Demin et al., 2021; Kikuchi et al., 2005; Ooka et al., 2018), nucleotide excision repair (NER; XPA /−) (Hirota et al., 2010), the Fanconi anemia pathway (FANCC /−, FANCJ −/−) (Yamamoto et al., 2011; Yoshikiyo et al., 2010), TLS (RAD18 −/−, SPRTN −/−, REV3 −/−, POLH −/−) (Edmunds et al., 2008; Hirota et al., 2010; Nakazato et al., 2018), repriming (PRIMPOL −/−) (Kobayashi et al., 2016), proofreading (POLE1 D269A/−) (Tsuda et al., 2017), removal of topoisomerase I‐DNA cleavage complexes (Top1‐cc repair; TDP1 −/−/TDP2 −/−) (Pommier et al., 2014), checkpoints (ATM −/−, RAD17 /−) (Morrison et al., 2000; Saberi et al., 2008), or sister chromatid cohesion (SCC; CTF18 −/−, DDX11 −/−, SA2 −/−) (Abe et al., 2016; Kawasumi et al., 2021; Rahman et al., 2024). We used these 24 cell lines and investigated their sensitivity by calculating the relative sensitivity using the formula: log2 (LD50 in the indicated mutant cells)/(LD50 in the wild‐type cells), where LD50 indicates the drug concentration that reduced cellular survival by 50%. We found that BRCA1 −/−, RAD17 /−, and RAD18 −/− cells exhibited relative sensitivity values that were <−2.5, −2, and −1.5, respectively. HDR‐deficient BRCA2 −/− cells also showed considerable sensitivity to GCV (Figure 1a). Cells deficient in the proofreading, repriming, Top1‐cc repair, NHEJ, BER, NER, and Fanconi pathways exhibited only marginally higher sensitivity to GCV than wild‐type cells. We confirmed that BRCA1 −/− cells showed the highest sensitivity to GCV, whereas RAD17 /− and RAD18 −/− cells exhibited lower sensitivity than did BRCA1 −/− cells (Figure 1b). These results indicate the vital roles of BRCA1, RAD17, and RAD18 in cellular tolerance to GCV.

RAD17 activates intra‐S phase checkpoint to prevent genome instability upon the incorporation of GCV

To assess the acute effects of GCV treatment on the cell cycle, we analyzed the DNA content of the cells at 0 and 6 h after GCV treatment. Wild‐type, BRCA1 −/−, and RAD18 −/− cells exhibited an accumulation of cells in the S phase, whereas RAD17 /− cells exhibited little change in cell cycle distribution (Figure 2a). These data suggest a role for RAD17 in the activation of intra‐S phase checkpoint after GCV is incorporated into the genomic DNA. To examine whether the accumulation of S‐phase cells is mediated through Chk1 phosphorylation, we investigated the status of Chk1 using α‐Chk1 and α‐phospho‐Chk1(S345) antibodies. We found that Chk1 phosphorylation was induced by GCV in wild‐type, BRCA1 −/−, and RAD18 −/− cells but not in RAD17 /− cells (Figure 2b). Taken together, these data indicate that RAD17 mediates Chk1 phosphorylation and induces the accumulation of S‐phase cells. To assess the role of RAD17‐mediated intra‐S phase checkpoint activation, we analyzed chromosome aberrations (CAs) in wild‐type and RAD17 /− cells. CAs were classified as isochromatid breaks (breakage of two sister chromatids at the same location; Figure 2c; blue arrow), chromatid breaks (breakage of one of the two sister chromatids; Figure 2c; black arrow), or radial chromosomes (aberrant chromosome fusion; Figure 2c; red arrow). We found that RAD17 /− cells exhibited more CAs than wild‐type cells (Figure 2c), indicating that RAD17‐mediated intra‐S phase checkpoint activation prevents genome instability upon GCV treatment.

Next, we examined the replication fork speed before and after GCV treatment by performing a DNA fiber assay to determine if the RAD17‐mediated DNA damage checkpoint restricts the incorporation of GCV at the 3′ end of nascent DNA, mitigating the chain‐terminating effect. The nascent DNA was labeled before (CldU, red) and after (IdU, green) GCV treatment (Figure 2d). We calculated the ratio (CldU/IdU) of unperturbed DNA fiber length relative to that observed in the presence of GCV, and a value >1.0 indicates that the fork slows down at the after GCV incorporation. We observed a similar CldU/IdU ratio in all tested cells, indicating that RAD17‐mediated checkpoint activation did not restrict GCV incorporation at the end of nascent DNA. Considering the role of DNA damage checkpoints in the suppression of origin firing (Iyer & Rhind, 2017), checkpoint activation may suppress new origin firing to avoid genome instability. The accumulation of cells in the mid‐S phase was observed in BRCA1 −/−, and RAD18 −/− cells (Figure 2a), without exhibiting fork progression defects (Figure 2d). This might be attributable to the active checkpoint‐mediated suppression of origin firing.

BRCA1 and RAD18 play independent roles in cellular tolerance to GCV

Having established the role of RAD17 in DNA damage checkpoint activation, we next analyzed the roles of BRCA1 and RAD18. To analyze the functional relationship between BRCA1 and RAD18, we generated BRCA1 −/−/RAD18 −/− cells. This double mutant showed higher GCV sensitivity than did each single mutant (Figure 3a). To determine if BRCA1 and RAD18 exert their roles immediately after GCV incorporation or during replication of the GCV‐incorporated template, we analyzed the number of GCV‐induced CAs in the cells after 6 and 16 h of GCV treatment (the cell cycle length for DT40 cells is approximately 8 h). We found that the number of CAs was not significantly increased in BRCA1 −/− and RAD18 −/− cells after 6 h of GCV treatment, indicating that these factors are not pivotal for avoiding genome instability immediately after GCV incorporation into nascent DNA (Figures 3b and S2). In contrast, BRCA1 −/− and RAD18 −/− cells exhibited a significantly greater number of CAs than did wild‐type cells at 16 h after GCV treatment (Figures 3c and S2). Moreover, BRCA1 −/−/RAD18 −/− cells exhibited significantly more CAs than did each single mutant. These results suggest that BRCA1 and RAD18 act independently in preventing genome instability when replication forks encounter GCV‐incorporated damaged sites on template strands. Notably, BRCA1 −/−/RAD18 −/− cells exhibited significantly more radial chromosomes but fewer isochromatic breaks than did BRCA1 −/− cells. These results suggest that DSBs are induced upon replication fork arrest at the GCV‐incorporated damaged site in BRCA1 −/− cells and that RAD18 plays a role in suppressing aberrant chromosome fusion by restricting toxic NHEJ.

Replication across GCV‐incorporated template produces DSBs

To test the hypothesis that DSBs arise during the replication of the GCV‐incorporated template, we analyzed the number of DSBs generated by GCV using a neutral comet assay. To identify the effect of GCV on acute replication and the next round of replication at the GCV‐incorporated damaged sites in the template strand, we cultured the cells for 6 and 24 h. The number of DSBs was not significantly augmented in any of the tested mutants at 6 h after GCV treatment (Figure 4a,b). In contrast, DSB augmentation was significantly high in BRCA1 −/− cells 24 h after GCV treatment, indicating that BRCA1 suppresses DSBs when the replication fork is stalled on the GCV‐incorporated template (Figure 4a,c). Furthermore, BRCA1 −/−/RAD18 −/− cells exhibited fewer DSBs than did BRCA1 −/− cells (Figure 4a,c). These data suggest that RAD18 prevents NHEJ and thus RAD18 loss results in NHEJ‐mediated DSB repair; however, this repair mediates the formation of radial chromosomes (Figure 3c), leading to cell death.

RAD18 suppresses toxic NHEJ

To test whether RAD18 suppresses toxic NHEJ to prevent the formation of radial chromosomes, we examined the role of NHEJ in radial chromosome formation in BRCA1 −/−/RAD18 −/− cells. We knocked out the KU70 gene, which encodes a critical NHEJ factor, Ku70 (Takata et al., 1998). Loss of Ku70 significantly mitigated the GCV sensitivity of RAD18 −/− cells, indicating that the toxic effect resulting from the loss of RAD18 is mediated through NHEJ (Figure 5a). Moreover, the loss of KU70 in BRCA1 −/−/RAD18 −/− cells significantly reduced the number of radial chromosomes (Figure 5b) but increased the number of DSBs induced by GCV (Figure 5c,d). Taken together, our data demonstrate the role of RAD18 in repressing toxic NHEJ, which results in aberrant chromosome fusions.

BRCA1 plays a dual role in GCV tolerance

After establishing the critical role of BRCA in preventing DSBs upon GCV treatment, we explored the mechanisms underlying BRCA1‐mediated DSB suppression. First, we analyzed the role of BRCA1 in the promotion of TS and HDR. We knocked out the 53BP1 gene (Nakamura et al., 2006) because the loss of 53BP1 in BRCA1 −/− cells rescues TS‐ and HDR deficiency in BRCA1 −/− cells (Bunting et al., 2010; Chen et al., 2022). We compared GCV sensitivity among BRCA1 −/−, 53BP1 −/−, and BRCA1 −/−/53BP1 −/− cells. 53BP1 loss restored the strong GCV sensitivity of BRCA1 −/−cells to approximately the same levels as in the wild‐type cells (Figure 6a). These results suggest that both the TS and HDR functions of BRCA1 contribute to cellular tolerance to GCV. To evaluate the functionality of HDR, we analyzed the number of GCV‐induced SCEs, which represent repair products of HDR with crossover (Figure S1d). Compared with that in the wild‐type cells, the SCE level was significantly reduced in BRCA1 −/− cells (Figure 6b). Notably, 53BP1 loss only partially restored SCE deficiency in BRCA1 −/− cells, and BRCA1 −/−/53BP1 −/− cells exhibited <50% SCE compared with that of wild‐type cells (Figure 6b,c). These data suggest that BRCA1‐mediated HDR exerts limited activity in BRCA1‐mediated GCV tolerance. This inference could be verified if lower sensitivity to GCV was exhibited in other mutants with defective HDR. In fact, this was found to be the case in HDR‐defective XRCC2 −/−and XRCC3 −/− cells that showed little or no hypersensitivity to GCV, validating the hypothesis; however, XRCC3 −/− cells showed a reduced level of GCV‐induced SCE (Figure 6b,c). Taken together, these results suggest that BRCA1 promotes cellular tolerance to GCV through two mechanisms with crossover (HDR) and without crossover (TS) functions.

To further test this hypothesis, we analyzed the replication fork speed on the GCV‐incorporated template, which is maintained by TS at the fork. The effect of DNA replication on GCV‐incorporated damage to the template strand was analyzed by culturing cells in the presence of GCV for 16 h and releasing them into a drug‐free medium for 1 h while sequentially pulse‐labeling the cells with CldU and IdU (Figures 7a and S3). We found that BRCA1 −/− cells exhibited significantly slower fork progression than did wild‐type cells, and defective fork progression in BRCA1 −/− cells was restored by the loss of 53BP1. We further analyzed the extent of strand gaps that arose as a result of replication fork arrest by performing an alkaline comet assay (Figure 7b). Augmented gap levels were observed in BRCA1 −/− cells and these augmented gaps were restored by the loss of 53BP1 (Figure 7b,c). These results show that BRCA1 mediates continuous replication on the GCV‐incorporated damaged template and that this role is regulated by 53BP1.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we elucidated the involvement of BRCA1, RAD18, and RAD17 in cellular tolerance to GCV (Figure 1). We found that RAD17 activates the DNA damage checkpoint upon GCV incorporation to prevent genomic instability (Figure 2). Moreover, BRCA1 and RAD18 independently suppress genome instability when replication forks encounter GCV‐incorporated damage on the template—BRCA1 suppresses DSB formation and RAD18 suppresses aberrant chromosome fusion via toxic NHEJ (Figures 3, 4, 5).

A previous analysis in yeast reported a less important role for RAD18 in cellular tolerance to GCV (O'Konek et al., 2009). However, the current study identified RAD18 as a critical factor in the cellular tolerance to GCV. RAD18 plays a role in the suppression of toxic NHEJ and subsequently preventing aberrant chromosomal fusion upon GCV treatment. These observations were consistent with the role of RAD18 in the suppression of radial chromosome formation upon camptothecin treatment (Kobayashi et al., 2015). This may be a new role gained by the vertebrate RAD18 protein during evolution in addition to its critical role in TLS (Edmunds et al., 2008; Kanao & Masutani, 2017). Recent studies reported that RAD18 competes with 53BP1 to bind histone H2A ubiquitinated by RNF8 and RNF168 at K13 and K15 (Nambiar et al., 2019; Palek et al., 2024). Thus, a possible rationale is that RAD18 prevents 53BP1 recruitment to replication‐born DSBs caused by GCV, which occur more frequently in the absence of BRCA1, preventing the toxic NHEJ.

We discovered that BRCA1 plays critical roles in the suppression of DSBs caused by GCV through two distinct mechanisms. BRCA1 aids in protecting reversed forks from nucleolytic degradation by nucleases (Lemaçon et al., 2017; Schlacher et al., 2011), and thereby promotes TS to avoid DSBs (Figure S1B). BRCA1 also promotes HDR of GCV‐induced DSBs and unreplicated gaps. Previous studies have suggested a role for HDR in mitigating the cytotoxic effects of GCV, as evidenced by the fact that SCEs are induced by GCV treatment in Chinese hamster cells (Thust et al., 2000). However, we found that HDR‐deficient mutant cells, other than BRCA1 −/− cells, such as XRCC2 −/−and XRCC3 −/− cells, showed little or no hypersensitivity to GCV, whereas they displayed a critical reduction in GCV‐induced SCE (Figure 6). This suggests that the effect of BRCA1‐associated HDR function in response to GCV tolerance may be limited, if any. Additionally, we identified BRCA2 as an important factor in cellular resistance to GCV (Figure 1). This result is consistent with the role of BRCA2, which plays a similar role as BRCA1 in both HDR and protection of reversed forks (Lemaçon et al., 2017; Schlacher et al., 2011).

The deficiency in SCE formation in BRCA1 −/− cells was not fully restored; however, cell survival in response to GCV was significantly restored by the loss of 53BP1 (Figure 6). This may be a consequence of the occurrence of fewer HDR events than TS‐mediated suppression of DSBs because only the HDR of DSBs or unreplicated gaps, rather than TS at stalled forks, causes double Holliday junctions, which could be resolved in crossover products (Giannattasio et al., 2014; Prado, 2021). Since 53BP1 antagonizes the role of BRCA1 in promoting HDR and TS (Bunting et al., 2010; Chen et al., 2022), the restoration of cellular survival after GCV treatment in BRCA1 −/−/53BP1 −/− cells could possibly be attributable to the combined antagonistic effect of 53BP1 on BRCA1 in HDR and TS events. Consistently, the fork progression defects on the GCV‐incorporated template in BRCA1 −/− cells were rescued by the loss of 53BP1 (Figure 7), indicating that TS is restored in BRCA1 −/−/53BP1 −/− cells.

Recently, we demonstrated that BRCA1 plays a dominant role in the cellular tolerance to the other nucleoside analog, alovudine (Hosen et al., 2024). In this case, the loss of 53BP1 completely rescued alovudine‐induced SCE formation in BRCA1 −/− cells (Hosen et al., 2024). Thus, the effect of the loss of 53BP1 in BRCA1 −/− cells is distinctly different when cells are exposed to GCV or alovudine. This difference might be due to the different importance of the HDR function of BRCA1 (the HDR of DSBs or unreplicated gaps) for cellular tolerance to GCV or alovudine. As GCV has 3′‐OH while alovudine does not, the possible rationale is that the continuity of the template strands determines the importance of the distinct mechanisms with crossover (HDR) and without crossover (TS) of BRCA1. Further studies might be required to clarify how such distinct mechanisms are selected for the cellular tolerance to each nucleoside analog.

Over the past 40 years, GCV has been widely used to treat cytomegalovirus infections (Zhang et al., 2021). Collectively, these findings demonstrate the pivotal role of BRCA1 in GCV tolerance. The clinical application of GCV in BRCA1‐mutated cancers with a combination of RAD17 and RAD18 mutations may exert an effective anticancer effect.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

DT40 cell cultures

The cell lines used in this study are listed in Table 1. DT40 cells were cultured as previously described (Hirota et al., 2015; Hirota et al., 2016) with slight modification. Briefly, DT40 cells were cultured at 39.5°C in D‐MEM/Ham's F‐12 medium supplemented with 10 μM β‐mercaptoethanol, penicillin (100 U/mL), streptomycin (100 μg/mL), L‐glutamine (2 mM), 10% fetal calf serum, and 1% chicken serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA).

Measurement of cellular sensitivity to DNA‐damaging agents

Cellular sensitivity analysis was performed according to the previously described method (ATP assay) (Abe et al., 2018). We performed at least two biological repeats, and p‐values were calculated using the Student's two‐tailed t‐test based on the averages of each data set. The raw data of each data set were presented in Supplementary S5_raw data, Data S1.

Colony survival assay

Cell survival was calculated as the percentage of surviving colonies. The colony formation assay was performed as described previously with slight modifications (Hirota et al., 2016). Briefly, to analyze sensitivity to GCV, the cells were plated in a medium containing 1% methylcellulose in D‐MEM/Ham's F12 (GE, USA) supplemented with 20% heat‐inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (AusgeneX, lot No. QLD 4210), 1% chicken serum (GIBCO‐BRL, Grand Island, NY, USA), 50 μM mercaptoethanol (Invitrogen, CA, USA), L‐glutamin (Nacalai Tesque, Japan), 50 U/mL penicillin, 50 μg/mL streptomycin (Nacalai Tesque, Japan), and the indicated concentration of GCV. The cells were maintained for 10 days at 39.5°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Then, the number of surviving colonies was counted.

Chromosomal aberration analysis

Mitotic chromosome spreads were prepared and analyzed as described previously (Kobayashi et al., 2016). The slides were observed under ECLIPSE‐Ni microscope (NIKON, Tokyo, Japan) at 1000× magnification, and the chromosomes in each mitotic cell were scored. Briefly, DT40 cells were arrested in the M‐phase by treatment with colcemid (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA; 0.1 μg/mL) for 2 h. The cells were pelleted by centrifugation (1200 rpm for 5 min), resuspended in 75 mM KCl (10 mL) for 13 min at room temperature, and fixed in a freshly prepared 3:1 mixture of methanol and acetic acid (Carnoy's solution; 2 mL). The pelleted cells were resuspended in Carnoy's solution (7 mL), dropped onto cold glass slides (approximately 106 cells/slide), and air‐dried. The slides were stained in 5% HARLECO Giemsa Stain solution (Nacalai Tesque) for 10 min, rinsed with water and acetone, and dried at 20°C. We measured blindly. At least 100 mitotic nuclei were counted for each data set. We performed at least two biological repeats, and p‐values were calculated by using the Student's two‐tailed t‐test based on the averages of each data set. The raw data of each data set were presented in Supplementary S5_raw data, Data S1.

Western blotting

Whole‐cell extracts were prepared by lysed cells directly with 1× Laemmli buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 2% SDS, 10% glycerol, 100 mM dithiothreitol, and 2.5 mg/mL bromophenol blue) and boiled for 10 min. The protein samples were separated using electrophoresis on 5%–15% gradient precast gel (Fuji film, Tokyo, Japan) and transferred onto PVDF membrane (GE, MA, USA) using 1 × transfer buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, 192 mM glycine, 20% methanol, and 0.01% SDS) at 30 V for 16 h in a wet transfer tank (Bio‐Rad, CA, USA). Then, the membranes were soaked in PBS‐T (0.05% Tween 20) containing 5% skim milk (Morinaga, Tokyo, Japan) for 30 min. The membranes were sequentially incubated with primary antibodies for 1 h and the secondary antibodies for 40 min at 25°C, and then detected using ImmunoStarR LD (Fuji Film, Tokyo, Japan). Primary antibodies; anti‐CHK1 (1:500; sc‐8408, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, SCBT, CA, USA), anti‐CHK1‐pS345 (1:1000; #2341, Cell Signaling Technology, CST, MA, USA). The raw images of the western blotting analyses are shown in Figure S4.

Measurement of sister chromatid exchange

Sister chromatid exchanges (SCEs) were measured as previously described (Ahmad et al., 2023). Briefly, DT40 cells were incubated in a medium containing GCV (10 μM) and 5‐bromo‐2′‐deoxy‐uridine (BrdU) (10 μM) at 39.5°C for 8 and 16 h at the one‐ and two‐cell cycle periods, respectively. Then, the cells were pelleted by centrifugation (1200 rpm for 5 min), resuspended in 75 mM KCl (0.2 mL) for 13 min at 20°C, and fixed in freshly prepared Carnoy's solution (0.4 mL). The pelleted cells were resuspended in Carnoy's solution (0.4 mL), placed onto clean glass slides (Matsunami Glass, Osaka, Japan), and air‐dried. The dried slides were incubated with Hoechst 33258 nuclei acid stain (10 μg/mL) in phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) for 20 min and rinsed with McIlvaine buffer (164 mM Na2HPO4 and 16 mM citric acid pH 7.0). Next, the slides were irradiated using black light (λ = 352 nm) for 25 min and incubated in saline‐sodium citrate (0.15 M NaCl plus 0.015 M sodium citrate) solution at 58°C for 20 min. This was followed by staining with 5% HARLECO Giemsa Stain Solution (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) for 10 min. The slides were examined under a microscope (ECLIPSE‐Ni; NIKON, Tokyo, Japan) at 1000× magnification, and all Giemsa‐stained metaphase cells in each test were scored. We measured blindly. At least 100 mitotic nuclei were assessed for each data set. We performed at least two biological repeats, and p‐values were calculated by using the Student's two‐tailed t‐test based on the averages of each data set. The raw data of each data set were presented in Supplementary S5_raw data, Data S1.

Neutral comet assay

DT40 cells (3 × 104) were suspended in 0.75% low melting point agarose (prepared in PBS) and dropped onto slides pre‐coated with 1% agarose. The mounted cells were lysed by incubating in lysis buffer (2.5 M NaCl, 100 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 10), 0.5% Triton‐X, and 1.0% N‐Lauroylsarcosine sodium salt) for 2 h. Electrophoresis was performed in a buffer (0.3 M sodium acetate and 100 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.3)) at 25 V for 1 h at 4°C. After electrophoresis, the slides were washed with 1 × PBS and dehydrated in 100% ethanol for 10 min, followed by drying at 37°C for 30 min and staining with 1× SybrGold solution (Invitrogen, CA, USA). Images were captured using a BZ‐X810 fluorescence microscope (Keyence, Tokyo, Japan). The percentage of tail DNA, which reflects the number of DSBs, was measured in the GCV‐exposed cells at different incubation times. OpenComet (https://cometbio.org/) was used to quantify the percentage of tail DNA, and 150 cells were scored per sample. We performed at least two biological repeats, and p‐values were calculated by using the Student's two‐tailed t‐test based on the medians of each data set. The raw data of each data set were presented in Supplementary S5_raw data, Data S1.

Alkaline comet assay

The slides were prepared in the same manner described in the methods for Neutral comet assay. The slides were placed in a tank containing alkaline electrophoresis buffer (0.3 M NaOH and 1 mM EDTA (pH >13)) for 40 min and subjected to electrophoresis at 25 V for 1 h at 4°C. Then, the slides were washed with 0.4 M Tris–HCl (pH 7.4) and dehydrated in 100% ethanol for 10 min. The slides were dried at 37°C for 30 min and stained with 1× SybrGold solution (Invitrogen, CA, USA). Images were captured and analyzed as described in the methods for the Neutral comet assay. We performed at least two biological repeats, and p‐values were calculated by using the Student's two‐tailed t‐test based on the medians of each data set. The raw data of each data set were presented in Supplementary S5_raw data, Data S1.

DNA fiber assay

DNA fiber assays were performed as previously described (Ahmad et al., 2023), with slight modifications. Briefly, cells were sequentially labeled for 15 min with 25 μM of 5‐chloro‐2′‐deoxyuridine (CldU) and 250 μM of 5‐iodo‐2′‐deoxyuridine (IdU). Fiber length was measured using ImageJ software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/docs/faqs.html). Measurements were recorded from areas of the slides with untangled DNA fibers to prevent the possibility of recording labeled patches from tangled fiber bundles. We performed at least two biological repeats, and p‐values were calculated by t‐test from the median of each data set. The raw data of each data set were presented in Supplementary S5_raw data, Data S1.

Statistical analysis

The Student's t‐tests were used to test for significant differences in cellular survival, DNA fiber length, and number of chromosomal breaks. The Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon test was used for statistical analysis of neutral comet assay and DNA fiber symmetricity analysis. In all experiments, we performed at least two independent biological repeats. When the data rely on only two biological repeats, average and individual values obtained from independent experiments were shown. When the data rely on three independent experiments, average, standard deviation (SD), and individual values obtained from independent experiments were shown.
