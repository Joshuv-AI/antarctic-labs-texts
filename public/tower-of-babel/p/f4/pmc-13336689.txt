Integrating DNA Mutations and RNA Expression of Cancer Driver Genes in Asian Rare Cancers: A Pan-Cancer Analysis
Authors: Kathleen Yasmin de Almeida, Marcelo Severino B Imasa, Pei Jye Voon, Hwoei Fen Soo Hoo, Tom Wei-Wu Chen, Suhana Yusak, Rangasamy Ramachandran, Najihah Abu Bakar, Ming-Huang Chen, Yasushi Yatabe, Kenichi Nakamura, Hitomi Sumiyoshi Okuma, Kan Yonemori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSERare cancers are molecularly heterogeneous and lack robust clinical evidence to guide treatment. Whether cancer driver gene expression is governed by tumor lineage or specific somatic alterations—and whether this distinction carries clinical relevance—remains poorly characterized, particularly in Asian populations. This study aimed to determine the relative contributions of tumor lineage and DNA mutations to RNA expression of cancer driver genes and to evaluate the clinical implications of genomic alteration status in Asian patients with rare cancers.METHODSDNA and RNA were extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tumor samples obtained through the MASTER KEY Asia research network. Next-generation sequencing was performed to evaluate genomic alterations and gene expression profiles. RNA expression of cancer driver genes was quantified as TPM Z scores. Elastic net regression with 10-fold cross-validation assessed the relative contributions of tumor lineage and DNA mutations to RNA expression, and findings were independently replicated in another cohort.RESULTSIntegrated DNA and RNA data were available for 128 patients. Tumor lineage was the predominant determinant of RNA expression in 17 of 27 cancer driver genes (63%), whereas ERBB2 and MDM2 demonstrated genomic-predominant regulation, replicated across both cohorts. Alterations in ERBB2 or ARID1A were associated with a lower frequency of disease progression compared with patients without either alteration (P = .01), independently of ERBB2 RNA expression. Functional subclassification of TP53 revealed that gain-of-function mutations were associated with higher rates of disease progression (P = .007) and shorter progression-free survival than loss-of-function variants (hazard ratio, 2.355 [95% CI, 1.086 to 5.105]; P = .029). Tumor mutational burden was not associated with treatment response.CONCLUSIONThese findings demonstrate that genomic alteration status, including TP53 functional subclassification, provides clinically meaningful information beyond transcriptional profiling alone, supporting integrative genomics and transcriptomics analysis for precision oncology in rare cancers.

INTRODUCTION

Rare cancers represent a challenging and often disregarded category in oncology.1,2 Although significant strides have been made in the treatment of more prevalent cancers, the diagnosis and treatment of rare cancers continue to be a complex task. Defined as malignant tumors occurring at a rate of fewer than six cases per 100,000 individuals per year, according to the definition of RARECARE in Europe, adopted in Japan,1,3 the rarity of these diseases often results in a lack of robust clinical data, hindering the development of targeted and personalized therapies.3,4 Notably, although each of these diseases represents < 1% of all cancer cases, collectively they contribute to 15% of all cancer diagnoses in Japan1 and 22% in Europe.3

Multi-omics approaches enable integrated analysis of multiple layers of biologic information, providing a more comprehensive view of molecular features.5–7 Pan-cancer analyses have further revealed that mutation processes may be consistent across tumor types, regardless of tissue origin,8,9 providing a biologic rationale for tumor-agnostic therapies, such as the use of TRK inhibitors for tumors with TRK fusions10 and BRAF inhibitors as a standalone treatment targeting BRAF V600 mutations.9

Whether this same principle extends to the transcriptional level—that is, expression of key cancer driver genes is governed by specific somatic alterations or by the tumor's tissue of origin—remains an open question in cancer genomics.11,12 Understanding these contributions has important implications for interpreting expression-based biomarkers in clinical settings. Furthermore, tumor mutational burden (TMB) reflects genomic instability and informs immunotherapy response.13–15 Understanding the DNA signatures underlying TMB can provide insight into carcinogenesis and inform patient stratification.

Genetic diversity across populations influences the incidence, progression, and treatment response of rare cancers. Asian patients face unique challenges because of differences in genetic background and epidemiology, emphasizing the need for population-specific precision medicine approaches.16–18 To address this, the MASTER KEY Asia (MKA) registry study was established within the Asian Clinical Trials Network for Cancers framework to enable collaborative investigation of rare cancers across the region.19

This study analyzed genomic and transcriptomic data from Asian patients in MKA to determine whether tumor lineage or DNA mutations drive the RNA expression of cancer driver genes and to evaluate the relationship between genomic alterations and treatment response. We hypothesized that for a subset of driver genes, RNA expression would be more tightly coupled to specific somatic alterations than to tumor lineage and that this genomic regulation would be reflected in clinical outcomes, offering a molecular basis for precision oncology in a population with limited clinical evidence.

METHODS

Study Design

MKA is a registry study spanning Malaysia, the Philippines, Taiwan, Korea, Thailand, Vietnam, and Indonesia that collects clinical data and performs targeted DNA and RNA sequencing on tissue specimens from patients diagnosed with solid rare cancers.

The study protocol was approved by the National Cancer Center's Institutional Review Board (No. 2021-105) and in each institution's ethics committee. All research procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards and with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians before inclusion in the study.

Inclusion and Exclusion

Patients aged 18 years or older at registration, with histologic diagnosis of cancer defined as rare cancer (Data Supplement, Table S1) or cancer of unknown primary origin, and incurable progressive (metastatic and/or unresectable) lesions, and who are able to provide written consent to participate in the study (legal representative may sign in case of physical disability), were eligible.

However, patients with complications of psychiatric disorders/symptoms that interfere with daily life were ineligible for participation in the study.

Patient Background and Treatment Outcomes

The Data Supplement (Fig S1) illustrates the study workflow. After obtaining the informed consents, the patients' characteristics and all clinical data were added to an Electronic Data Capturing system by the participating sites in Asia. Treatment outcomes for chemotherapy or adjuvant chemoradiotherapy were classified as complete response, partial response, stable disease, and progressive disease. When the measurement was not feasible, or a response cannot be determined for any reason, not evaluated was entered.

Molecular Analysis and Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS)

FFPE tissue specimens and blood samples were sent from each participating site to the research coordination center, the National Cancer Center Hospital Japan for concurrent DNA and RNA extraction (QIAcube; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany).

DNA quality was assessed, and 100 ng underwent target enrichment using Twist Custom Panels (Twist Bioscience, South San Francisco, CA). Total RNA extraction from FFPE specimens used a RNeasy FFPE Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and quality was evaluated using a High Sensitivity RNA Screen Tape system.

DNA quality thresholds required ΔΔCt ≤8; RNA required DV200 >40%. Sequencing was performed using the TOP2 Panel, a targeted NGS panel developed through collaborative research and based on the Todai OncoPanel.

The TMB was determined by counting all the synonymous and nonsynonymous variants, reporting as mutations per targeted exon megabase unit (Mut/Mb); TMB high was considered as ≥10 Mut/Mb.

Statistical Analysis

The data were analyzed by SPSS statistical package version 25.0 for Mac (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL) and R (version 4.1.3), packages glmnet, biomaRt, ComplexHeatmap, and circlize. Group differences were analyzed using ANOVA, t test, and χ2 test. Negative-binomial and beta-binomial distributions were used for the identification of significantly mutated genes. RNA expression was quantified using z scores derived from normalized TPM values. For visualization and descriptive analyses, expression levels were categorized as underexpressed (z < −1), normal (−1 ≤ z ≤ 1), or overexpressed (z > 1). Elastic net regression was used to perform penalized variable selection in high-dimensional settings, incorporating tumor type and genomic alterations as candidate predictors. The penalty parameter was selected using 10-fold cross-validation. For penalized regression analyses, RNA overexpression was defined as z score ≥ +1.5 to enrich for robust expression outliers. The Kaplan-Meier method was employed to estimate progression-free survival (PFS), with log-rank test. Additionally, Cox proportional hazard models were used to assess risk factors. Enrichment analysis for significantly mutated genes was performed using Reactome.20

Elastic net modeling was independently performed in a replication cohort of 116 patients (77 women and 39 men; mean age 51 ± 16 years; range, 19-82) enrolled in the MKA registry after the discovery cohort cutoff through August 2024, from Malaysia (n = 88), Vietnam (n = 15), Thailand (n = 8), the Philippines (n = 4), and Taiwan (n = 1), spanning 25 primary tumor sites, using the same predictor structure and classification criteria. Concordance in predominant driver category between the discovery and replication cohorts was used as the primary metric of validation. Model fit was assessed using McFadden pseudo-R2 and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calculated at λmin using 10-fold cross-validated predicted probabilities.

RESULTS

From November 2021 to June of 2023, 342 eligible patients were enrolled for MKA. From those, tissue samples were available for 280 cases. NGS analysis was completed for 173 patients, with both DNA and RNA analysis obtained for 128 patients (50 ± 15 years; Fig 1A).

Characteristics of the cohort. (A) Overall flowchart of the study cohort and population size for each analysis. (B) Cancer primary sites (organ of origin) in each cohort. (C) Population size of low TMB (<10) and high TMB (≥10) for each cancer type. TMB, tumor mutational burden.

The discovery cohort included 75 women and 53 men from Malaysia (n = 97), the Philippines (n = 21), and Taiwan (n = 10). A total of 25 primary sites (ie, organ or tissue of origin, within which multiple tumor types may occur) of rare cancer were identified, with soft tissue (n = 25), head and neck (n = 19), ovary/fallopian tube (n = 13), other (n = 9), and uterus (n = 8; Fig 1B) being the most frequent. The category other corresponds to tumors with non-otherwise specified (NOS) histology, such as neuroendocrine carcinoma and adenocarcinoma NOS.

Of the 173 patients with TMB information, most presented low TMB (90.2%; n = 156) and a mean of 3.2 ± 2.3 Muts/Mb, whereas 17 patients (9.8% of the sample) presented high-TMB values (>10 Muts/Mb), with a mean of 21 ± 16 Muts/Mb. The most frequent primary sites among the high-TMB patients were bladder/urinary tract, lung, and ovary/fallopian tube, with three cases each (42.8%, 23%, and 18.7%, respectively; Fig 1C).

Association of the RNA Expression and TMB

Nine of 27 cancer driver genes showed significant TMB associations. TMB was higher in patients with overexpression of BRCA1, BRCA2, CD274, MSH2, and MDM2 (P < .001 for BRCA1, BRCA2, MDM2; MSH2 P = .04; CD274 P = .05). EGFR and ERBB2 showed increasing TMB across underexpressed < normal < overexpressed (P = .05). NTRK3 and CDK4 showed opposite patterns, with normal NTRK3 and underexpressed CDK4 having higher TMB (P = .02 and P < .001; Fig 3).

DNA Mutations and RNA Expression

A total of 1,144 DNA alterations were identified (79% single-nucleotide variants, 9% amplifications, 9% deletions, 3% fusions/insertions) across 461 genes. The most frequently altered genes were TP53, KMT2D, TERT, LRP1B, ERBB2, ERBB3, ARID1A, PIK3CA, PTPRD, and RB1, which together accounted for 15% of total alterations identified. Overall, 47% of patients harbored at least one alteration in the top 10 genes (Fig 4A). Details of these alterations are shown in Figure 4B. Fifty-six significantly altered genes were analyzed for pathway enrichment. Most of the genes were enriched in the pathways of signal transduction, diseases of signal transduction by growth factor receptors and second messengers, and transcription process, among others (Data Supplement, Table 2).

Elastic net regression models were applied for each of the 27 cancer driver genes using a pan-cancer framework. Genomic predictors were restricted to the 10 most frequently altered cancer driver genes in the cohort (TP53, KMT2D, TERT, LRP1B, ERBB2, ERBB3, ARID1A, PIK3CA, PTPRD, and RB1) to reduce dimensionality and mitigate overfitting. In the discovery cohort, TP53 alterations were further subclassified into gain of function (GOF) and loss of function (LOF) based on functional annotation, while all other genes were modeled as binary variables. Variables retained are summarized in Table 1. Model fit statistics for all 26 gene models, including McFadden pseudo-R2 and AUC, are provided in Data Supplement (Table S3). Across tumor type–predominant genes, model fit ranged from modest (R2 = 0.081 for MSH6) to strong (R2 = 0.467 for ESR1; AUC = 0.972).

Tumor lineage emerged as the dominant determinant of RNA expression in 17 of 27 genes (63%), defined by a ≥20% greater maximum absolute coefficient. These included canonical lineage-associated drivers such as BRCA1, BRCA2, ESR1, FGFR family members, KIT, MET, and multiple mismatch repair genes.

In contrast, ERBB2 and MDM2 demonstrated genomic-predominant regulation, with RNA expression more tightly coupled to genomic alterations than to tumor lineage. For ERBB2, amplification-related genomic features exhibited the largest effect sizes, and classification was supported by an AUC of 0.809 in the discovery cohort.

A smaller subset of genes (MYC and EGFR; 7%) exhibited comparable contributions from tumor type and genomic alterations, meeting criteria for mixed regulation. Five genes did not retain predictors.

Modeling was independently performed in a replication cohort using the same predictor structure and predominant driver classification criteria (Data Supplement, Table S4). ERBB2 and MDM2 remained genomic-predominant, and EGFR retained a mixed pattern in both data sets. Category shifts were observed for a minority of genes, including BRCA1 and ALK. No genes classified as genomic predominant in the discovery cohort shifted to tumor type predominance in replication.

Molecular Features and Treatment Response

Patients harboring alterations in either ARID1A or ERBB2 had a significantly lower frequency of PD compared with those without these alterations (55.6% v 76%, P = .01; Data Supplement, Fig S2A). Notably, while modeling identified both ERBB2 and ARID1A as genomic determinants of ERBB2 expression (although in opposite directions), the results were independent of ERBB2 RNA expression levels (P = .6). Although a numerically longer PFS was observed in the mutated group, this was not statistically significant (median PFS: absence of alterations 195 days; presence of alterations 334 days, P = .3; Fig 5A). Functional subclassification of TP53 alterations revealed that patients with TP53 GOF presented a higher percentage of progression of disease than LOF (90% v 60%, P = .007; Data Supplement, Fig S2B). All patients with TP53 GOF had overexpression of MDM2 and a trend toward higher mean z score of MDM2 expression compared with patients who had a loss of TP53 function (TP53 GOF: 3.12 ± 1.07; TP53 LOF: 2.57 ± 2.57, P = .08). Additionally, there was a significant difference in PFS (hazard ratio TP53 GOF, 2.355 [95% CI, 1.086 to 5.105]) between TP53 GOF, normal (ie, wildtype), and LOF (median PFS: GOF 107 days; normal 134 days; LOF 401 days, P = .029; Fig 5B).

Kaplan-Meier survival curves for PFS. (A) PFS stratified by the presence or absence of alterations in either ARID1A or ERBB2. (B) PFS stratified by TP53 status: LOF, wild-type (normal), and GOF mutations. GOF, gain-of-function; LOF, loss-of-function; PFS, progression-free survival; SNV, single-nucleotide variant.

Finally, there was no significant association between the TMB and the treatment outcomes (P = .1).

DISCUSSION

Integrating DNA alterations, RNA expression, TMB, and treatment response across 25 primary tumor sites, this pan-cancer analysis reveals that tumor lineage is the dominant determinant of RNA expression for most driver genes, while ERBB2 and MDM2 are influenced predominantly by genomic alterations. Despite opposing effects on ERBB2 expression, ERBB2 and ARID1A alterations converge on favorable treatment outcomes, while TP53 GOF mutations confer markedly worse prognosis than LOF variants.

Tumor lineage predominated for most driver genes, consistent with prior pan-cancer transcriptomic studies, showing that molecular clustering is driven primarily by histology and tissue of origin rather than specific genomic alterations,11 suggesting that expression-based biomarkers for these genes should be interpreted accordingly in pan-cancer settings.

In contrast, ERBB2 and MDM2 demonstrated genomic-predominant regulation, with RNA expression more strongly explained by specific genomic alterations than by tumor lineage. For ERBB2, expression was most strongly associated with alterations in ERBB2 itself, consistent with a dominant cis-regulatory effect and extensive evidence showing that copy number gain is the main driver of HER2 overexpression across cancers such as breast and gastric tumors.21,22 Notably, this genomic-predominant pattern was replicated in the validation cohort, making ERBB2 the most robust example in our analysis. The relative independence of ERBB2 expression from tumor type aligns with the growing tumor-agnostic therapeutic paradigm, exemplified by the approval of trastuzumab deruxtecan for HER2-positive solid tumors irrespective of histology.23 In practical terms, this implies that expression-based biomarkers for lineage-driven genes require histologic context for meaningful interpretation, whereas ERBB2 genomic status may serve as a histology-independent biomarker candidate for therapeutic selection.

For MDM2, overall model fit was modest (R2 = 0.015; AUC = 0.625), although the selective retention of TP53 LOF as the sole predictor is mechanistically coherent with the canonical p53–MDM2 feedback loop.24 This suggests that MDM2 RNA expression reflects the functional status of the p53–MDM2 axis more than tissue identity, rather than representing an artifact of model complexity.

EGFR and MYC exhibited mixed regulation, with comparable contributions from tumor lineage and genomic alterations. EGFR expression is shaped by both tissue-specific transcriptional programs and copy number amplification.25–27 MYC, in contrast, is regulated downstream of multiple oncogenic inputs, including WNT, RAS/MAPK, and PI3K/AKT signaling, whose relative dominance varies by tumor context.28

The analysis identified TP53 LOF as a negative predictor of MDM2 expression, while all TP53 GOF patients exhibited MDM2 overexpression with a trend toward higher mean z score compared with LOF patients. Although MDM2 amplification occurs in approximately one third of human sarcomas with preserved wild-type p53,28 elevated expression is also observed without amplification, indicating alternative regulatory mechanisms.29–31 This pattern suggests that GOF variants retain or aberrantly enhance transcriptional activity at the MDM2 locus, creating a state where p53 tumor-suppressive function is simultaneously lost and actively degraded.

Functional subclassification of TP53 revealed a clinically meaningful prognostic gradient: Patients with TP53 GOF mutations experienced significantly worse outcomes than those harboring wild-type or TP53 LOF, with a higher frequency of disease progression. While LOF mutations abrogate tumor suppression, GOF mutations confer active oncogenic properties, promoting tumor progression,32,33 contributing to chemoresistance through activation of pro-survival pathways including EFNB2 and AXL,34,35 and impairing proapoptotic calcium signaling and autophagy under therapeutic stress.36–38 These findings argue for the clinical adoption of TP53 functional annotation in molecular profiling, as binary mutation reporting obscures prognostically meaningful biologic differences.

The results showed ERBB2, PIK3CA, and ARID1A alterations as the primary genomic determinants of ERBB2 RNA expression. ERBB2 alterations—predominantly gene amplification (36%) and GOF mutations (46%)—were positively associated with expression, consistent with copy number-driven transcriptional activation. ARID1A alterations, by contrast, were negatively associated with ERBB2 expression, consistent with the tumor suppressor role of ARID1A as a core subunit of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex.39

Although loss of ARID1A has been associated with dysregulated oncogenic signaling, increased TMB, and poor prognosis across multiple cancer types,40–44 patients with either ERBB2 or ARID1A alterations showed a lower frequency of progression after treatment in our study. ERBB2 amplification and GOF mutations establish oncogenic dependency, potentially sensitizing tumors to disruption of ERBB2-driven signaling,21,22 while ARID1A loss impairs homologous recombination repair and remodels the tumor immune microenvironment, sensitizing tumors to DNA-damaging agents and immune checkpoint blockade.44,45 That both alterations associate with better outcomes despite opposing expression effects suggests that genomic alteration status carries clinical information not captured by transcriptional profiling alone: Tumors lacking both alterations may represent a harder-to-target group without these specific vulnerabilities. Although PFS did not reach statistical significance (334 v 195 days, P = .3), the directional consistency with response data suggests a biologically meaningful signal warranting prospective validation.

Seven cancer driver genes showed significant TMB associations, functionally linked to proliferation, metastasis, genomic instability, and therapeutic resistance. BRCA1 and BRCA2 overexpression correlates with heightened cell proliferation and DNA replication errors,46,47 whereas MSH2 overexpression induces genomic instability through impaired mismatch repair.48–52 CD274 (PD-L1) overexpression contributes to immune evasion through PD-1/PD-L1 signaling, linking both PD-L1 expression and TMB to immunotherapy response.15,53,54 NTRK3 overexpression was associated with lower TMB in our data set, agreeing with Zhang et al,55 who reported a direct association of low TMB levels with high NTRK3 expression in bladder cancer. Overall, although the observed associations are biologically plausible, their clinical implications require further investigation in larger, prospectively designed studies.

Signal transduction was the most affected pathway by the DNA alterations, consistent with its central role in driving aberrant cell growth, impaired apoptosis, angiogenesis and immune evasion.56,57 Pathway-specific alterations have been reported across rare cancer histologies, including Notch pathway mutations in adenoid cystic carcinoma58 and PI3K/Akt/mTOR in uveal melanoma, highlighting potential therapeutic targets,59–61 while inhibition of the MAPK/ERK pathway has shown efficacy in BRAF-mutant anaplastic thyroid cancer,62 supporting pathway-targeted therapies in rare cancers.

The lack of TMB-treatment response correlation in this study likely reflects the limited number of high-TMB tumors and predominant use of conventional chemotherapy. Immunotherapy access remains a significant challenge across many Asian countries because of cost and limited availability, reinforcing the need for collaborative regional efforts to expand clinical trial access and precision medicine infrastructure.

The study's retrospective design and relatively small, regionally limited cohort may restrict generalizability. Replication cohort validation confirmed some clinically important findings; however, overall concordance across all 27 genes was limited by cohort size and, therefore, statistical power, warranting cautious interpretation pending validation in larger data sets. Additionally, this study is limited to DNA and RNA profiling; the absence of proteomic or functional protein activation data means that the observed DNA-RNA associations cannot be confirmed at the protein level, and mechanistic inferences should be interpreted accordingly. Experimental validation will also be essential to establish causal links and elucidate the biologic mechanisms underlying these associations.

In conclusion, tumor lineage governs RNA expression for most driver genes, whereas ERBB2 and MDM2 are regulated predominantly by genomic alterations. TP53 functional subclassification emerged as a strong prognostic discriminator, with GOF mutations conferring markedly worse outcomes than LOF variants, a distinction that has direct implications for therapeutic stratification. Together, these findings support integrative DNA-RNA profiling and functional genomic annotation for precision oncology in rare cancers and suggest that TP53 functional subtype and ERBB2 genomic status warrant prospective evaluation as stratification factors in future histology-agnostic trials.
