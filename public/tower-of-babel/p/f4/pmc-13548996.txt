A Wagner–Meerwein‐Like Rearrangement Generates a Vinyl Group in the Biosynthesis of the Polychlorinated Lipopeptides Fischerazoles
Authors: Sandra A. C. Figueiredo, Kathleen Abt, Konrad Viehrig, Peter J. Jervis, Teresa P. Martins, Iñaki Lacomba, Chuhan Li, Daniele Castagnolo, Pedro N. Leão
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe discovery of structurally unusual natural products is a major inspiration in the search for novel enzymatic transformations. In this study, we employ stable isotope‐labeled precursor feeding and metabolomics to reveal highly unusual cyanobacterial lipopeptides—fischerazoles A‐C. These metabolites contain a polychlorinated fatty acyl‐derived moiety with a vinyl branch and an N‐methylated carboxamide‐thiazole terminus. Most strikingly, stable‐isotope‐labeled precursor supplementation showed that during fischerazole biosynthesis, a linear, hexadecanoic acid‐derived intermediate is processed to yield the ultimately branched alkyl moiety. The branched carbon becomes part of the vinyl group together with an S‐adenosyl methionine (SAM)‐derived carbon. A candidate polyketide synthase/non‐ribosomal peptide synthetase (PKS/NRPS) fischerazoles biosynthetic gene cluster—fsh—was identified in the genome of the producing strain, Fischerella sp. PCC 9431. In vitro assays experimentally connected fsh genes to fischerazole biosynthesis and revealed that the SAM‐dependent methyltransferase FshF catalyzes a Wagner–Meerwein‐like rearrangement to generate the vinyl group from an ACP‐tethered unsaturated fatty acid. The discovery of FshF, which is related to the well‐characterized cyclopropane fatty acid synthases (CFASs), brings to light a new biocatalytic strategy for alkyl chain functionalization.

Introduction

The discovery of a new natural product is often the starting point for revealing a pharmacologically relevant biological activity [1], uncovering chemically mediated biological interactions [2], or discovering uncharted biochemical transformations and associated enzymes [3, 4]. Among natural products‐rich organisms, cyanobacteria display a range of metabolites occupying a unique chemical space as illustrated by the intricate structures of the hapalindole class of indole alkaloids [5] or the lipidic curacin [6] and cylindrocyclophanes [7]. Fatty acid‐derived moieties with diverse modifications are common in a range of cyanobacterial natural products, implying that rich enzymatic chemistry acting on alkyl chains is involved in their biosynthesis [8]. Notable examples include the dimetal carboxylate halogenase CylC and its partner Friedel‐Crafts alkylating enzyme CylK [9], or terminal alkene‐generating enzymes Aar/Ado [10]. The discovery of additional modifications in fatty acid‐derived portions of natural products can thus lead to the uncovering of new enzymatic functions. We have recently developed a metabolomics method aimed at discovering fatty acid‐incorporating natural products [11]. The method employs supplementation of cyanobacterial cultures with perdeuterated hexanoic acid. Because cyanobacteria do not appreciably degrade fatty acids and instead elongate and integrate them into various metabolites [11, 12], the deuterium label is incorporated into the fatty acid‐derived lipidome. This strategy exploits this peculiarity to detect mass features that are shifted in extracts from cultures receiving perdeuterated fatty acid supplementation compared to unlabeled controls. In our previous study [11], we applied this method to the cyanobacterium Fischerella sp. PCC 9431 and discovered a series of new hapalosins. Here, we explore additional mass features incorporating the deuterium label and identify, isolate and elucidate the structures of a series of polychlorinated lipopeptides, which we named fischerazoles A‐C (1–3, Figure 1). We identify the fsh biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC), which we connect through in vitro assays to the biosynthesis of 1–3. We further demonstrate that fischerazoles biosynthesis involves a Wagner–Meerwein‐like mid‐chain rearrangement of an unsaturated fatty acyl precursor, through the action of the SAM‐dependent methyltransferase FshF, generating the pendant vinyl group in 1–3.

Results and Discussion

Discovery of the Fischerazoles

In our previous study [11], we supplemented Fischerella sp. PCC 9431 with pulses of d 11‐hexanoic acid (d 11‐C6) and carried out LC‐HRESIMS comparative analysis with a non‐supplemented culture. After filtering out mass features presumably corresponding to primary metabolites, this analysis led to the identification of 124 labeled positive‐mode MS features resulting from incorporation of deuterium atoms [11]. Here, we looked into a few which exhibited d 7 and d 6 incorporation (Figure 1A) and isotopic patterns consistent with multiple halogenations. A more detailed inspection clarified that these corresponded to four major compounds, for which in general [M+Na]+ ions were more abundant and showed m/z values of 537.067, 503.106, 521.073, and 487.111 for the non‐deuterated isotopologues. Those that were labeled with d 7 (m/z 503 and 487) and those labeled with d 6 (m/z 537 and 521) had isotope patterns consistent with trichlorination and tetrachlorination, respectively. Dereplication of the corresponding high‐resolution masses against several databases (see experimental procedures) [11, 13] returned no hits, prompting their isolation. Cyanobacterial cells resulting from large scale (80 L) cultivation of Fischerella sp. PCC 9431 were subjected to organic extraction, followed by a series of normal‐ and reversed‐phase chromatographic steps. LC‐(+)‐HRESIMS analysis of the resulting fractions guided the isolation of spectroscopically pure 1 (3.6 mg), 2 (2.4 mg), and 3 (1.0 mg) (Figure 1B). A small amount (0.2 mg) of the partially purified metabolite with m/z 487.111, [M+Na]+ was also obtained, but additional purification was not pursued.

Compound 1, which was isolated in higher amounts, was selected for initial structure elucidation based on combined analysis of (+)‐HRESIMS data (Figure S1) and NMR (1D and 2D) data in DMSO‐d 6 (Table S1 and Figure S2–S8). This metabolite showed a monoisotopic [M+H]+ ion of m/z 515.0852 (calcd. 515.0855), and an isotope pattern indicating the presence of four chlorines, which allowed us to deduce the molecular formula C21H30O2N2Cl4S. Analysis of the combined 1D and 2D NMR data for 1, in particular those from HSQC and COSY experiments, established several spin systems, namely a terminal olefin, an N‐methyl group, two chlorinated aliphatic moieties (Figure 1C) and an isolated aromatic proton. HMBC and HSQC‐TOCSY experiments were used to establish the connectivity between the spin systems (Figure 1C). The NMR‐based structure elucidation of 2 and 3 used the proposed structure for 1 as reference. Comprehensive details on the structure elucidation of 1–3 are available in Texts S1 and S2; Tables S1 and S2; Figures S1–S20). Absolute configuration determination of the stereogenic centers in 1–3 (i.e., the two monochlorinated and the vinyl‐substituted position) is not trivial, due to the inherent flexibility of the alkyl chain, the distance between these centers and from them to chromophores. This will require total synthesis or x‐ray crystallography and will be the subject of future efforts.

The fischerazoles bear several unique structural features: in particular, we were unable to find another reported natural product with a mid‐chain vinyl group. The gem‐dichlorovinylidene aliphatic chain termini are rare in nature [14] and found in two other cyanobacterial natural products—caracolamide A [14] and the taveuniamides [15] (Figure 1B). The peptidic portion of 1–3 has some resemblance to the aranazoles [16] (Figure 1B), which are also extensively chlorinated cyanobacterial lipopeptides. While there are a small number of natural products containing a primary thiazole carboxamide [17, 18, 19], the terminal, N‐methylated thiazole carboxamide motif seems to be unique to the fischerazoles.

Identification of the fsh Biosynthetic Gene Cluster

The unusual structures of the fischerazoles motivated our efforts to explore their biosynthesis. Considering the tetrachlorination of compounds 1 and 3, we expected the presence of several halogenases in the corresponding BGC. We therefore mined the genome of Fischerella sp. PCC 9431 for different characterized halogenases known to act on alkyl moieties, namely, dimetal‐carboxylate halogenases such as CylC [20] and iron(II)/α‐ketoglutarate‐dependent halogenases [21]. We detected two BGCs that contained more than one halogenase (Figure S21), so we examined these BGCs further, particularly for functions that would explain the thiazole moiety. This resulted in a single candidate genomic region featuring three putative halogenases and a non‐ribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) that contained the expected domains for thiazole formation, which we named as the fsh BGC (Figure 2A and Table S3). FshA is a putative membrane‐associated fatty acyl‐AMP ligase (FAAL) responsible for activating and loading a fatty acid onto an acyl carrier protein (ACP). FshE and FshM are two free‐standing ACPs. Analysis of their N‐ and C‐terminal docking domains suggested FAAL‐ACP‐PKS interfaces for both [22], making both plausible acceptors of the activated fatty acid and involved in downstream transfer to a PKS (Figure S22). The fsh‐encoded halogenases comprise two CylC homologs (FshC with 45% and FshN with 47% identity) and a homolog of the Fe(II)/α‐ketoglutarate‐dependent halogenase ArzI from the aranazoles BGC (FshK, 70% identity), which together should install up to four chlorine atoms. FshD is annotated as a membrane fatty acid desaturase. FshF is predicted to be a SAM‐methyltransferase of the cyclopropane fatty acid synthase‐/mycolic acid cyclopropane synthetase (CFAS/CMAS) family. These enzymes are known to generate cyclopropane moieties, O‐Me and C‐Me groups in unsaturated fatty acid‐derived substrates [23]. The PKS FshG is composed of a ketosynthase (KS) and an acyltransferase (AT) domain but lacks a thiolation (T) domain. A second PKS, FshH, contains all three minimal PKS domains. The AT domains of both FshG and FshH and the KS domain of FshH are predicted by antiSMASH to be inactive. This prediction is supported by sequence alignments with known active PKS domains (e.g., lack of conserved active site serine in AT domains and of one of the conserved active site histidines in the KS domain, Figure S23). Analysis of the FshH docking domains indicated upstream connectivity to the FAAL‐ACPs and downstream connectivity to an NRPS, likely FshI whose N‐terminal docking domain matches PKS connectivity (Figure S21) [22]. This suggests that although inactive in their canonical function, the PKSs are still taking part in fischerazole biosynthesis. The NRPS FshI is predicted (antiSMASH) to incorporate cysteine and presents the previously described features capable of forming the thiazole moiety [e.g [24], namely a condensation/cyclization (C/Cy) domain and an interrupted A domain containing an oxidase (Ox) (Figure 2B)]. The second NRPSs FshJ features a rare type of A domain that contains an insertion of a monooxygenase (MOx) domain [25] and is followed by an N‐methyltransferase (nMT), T and thioesterase (TE) domains. The architecture of this module is similar to those in myxothiazol and melithiazol biosynthesis [25, 26, 27]. The predicted amino acid specificity for FshJ could not be determined by antiSMASH. However, after in silico excision of the MOx domain, antiSMASH recognized the A domain as glycine‐specific (Figure S24). Loading and N‐methylation of glycine would be consistent with a putative final step following the logic of myxothiazol/melithiazol biosynthesis [25]. In this hypothesized chain termination mechanism, FshJ would incorporate, N‐methylate and hydroxylate glycine, leading to the release of fischerazole as a terminal amide while most of the glycine backbone would be cleaved off as a glyoxylyl‐FshJ thioester (likely further broken down by the TE domain) (Figure 2B) [27]. This proposed sequence of biosynthetic steps associated with FshJ requires experimental validation and remains purely speculative. We propose that the cytochrome P450 FshL performs the C‐8 hydroxylation found in 1 and 2. Finally, FshB has no significant homology to any characterized protein. The fsh BGC is flanked by transposases on both sides.

Fischerazoles Biosynthesis Involves Carbon Rearrangement of a Hexadecenoic Acid‐Precursor

To learn more about the biosynthesis of the fischerazoles, in particular the origin of the unusual alkyl chain with a pendant vinyl group, we performed stable isotope incorporation experiments. We used a sequence of perdeuterated even‐chain fatty acids from octanoic to octadecanoic acid for supplementation of Fischerella sp. PCC 9431 cultures. We detected label incorporation into the fischerazoles from all fatty acids up to hexadecanoic acid (d 31‐C16) (Figure 3A; Figure S25 and S26). Compounds 1–3 feature an acyclic alkyl group in which the longest linear chain is composed of 15 carbons, and a carbonyl functionality is required for cysteine cyclization. As such, the observed activation of a C16 fatty acid for the biosynthesis of these compounds was surprising, but—at the same time—consistent with non‐elongating PKS modules, as predicted bioinformatically. We considered that this label incorporation could be explained only through a rearrangement of the carbon skeleton into a branched chain. To our knowledge, a biological fatty acyl chain contraction generating a pendant vinyl group has not previously been described. The pattern of deuterium incorporation into the fischerazoles (data for 1 and 3 are presented), from d 15‐C8 to d 31‐C16, was consistent with the structures of 1 and 3 derived from NMR (Figures 3A and S25). Additional losses of deuterons (i.e., other than the five deuteron losses from the chain terminus observed for all supplemented fatty acids) occurred only for d 19‐C10 and d 31‐C16, corresponding to the positions of the hydroxylation on C‐8, dehydrogenation on C‐16 and chlorination on C‐3. Notably, the incorporation of only 11 deuterons from d 19‐C10 into 1 (Figure 3A) and 12 deuterons into 3 (Figure S25) further supported the fatty acyl‐origin of the branched vinylic C‐16 atom. The stand‐alone methyltransferase FshF and the NRPS nMT domain of FshJ suggested SAM‐dependent methylation in two positions of 1–3. In particular, we considered that one of the vinyl carbons should originate from SAM, since the other would originate from hexadecanoic acid. To validate this, we used methyl‐13C,d 3‐l‐methionine for precursor feeding. Indeed, we observed a mass shift corresponding to the incorporation of two methyl groups from methionine into 1 and 3, one of which had lost one deuteron (Figure 3B). This was in accordance with one methylation giving rise to the C‐17 position in the vinylic double bond and the other being part of the N‐methyl carboxamide moiety (C‐21 position). Similarly, we sought to confirm hexadecanoic acid incorporation into 1 and 3 by supplementation with [U‐13C]‐hexadecanoic acid. We detected masses corresponding to 13C16‐labeled 1 and 3 (Figures 3C and S25). To assess the position of the labels from the different feeding experiments in the fischerazoles, we resorted to HRESIMS/MS analysis of 3 which allowed the best [M+H]+ detection. Fragmentation of 3 resulted in characteristic loss of HCl but also the N‐methyl group, which, for the 13C2,d 5‐3 isotopologue, indicated label incorporation in this position from 13C,d 3‐l‐methionine (Figure 3D). Additional fragments supported the proposed SAM‐derived methylation on both the aliphatic chain and the terminal carboxamide. For 13C16‐labeled 3, several diagnostic fragments confirmed that the labels were restricted to the alkyl part (Figure 3D). Hence, our hypothesis of alkyl chain rearrangement during fischerazole biosynthesis was validated.

FshA Activates Long‐Chain Fatty Acids

To gain biochemical evidence for the involvement of the fsh BGC in the biosynthesis of the fischerazoles, we separately cloned and expressed selected genes from this cluster in E. coli, namely FshA providing the FAAL for initial fatty acid activation and FshF predicted to encode a SAM‐methyltransferase. The two genes encoding ACPs in the BGC (FshE and FshM) were also heterologously expressed. First, to enquire which fatty acid chain lengths would be accepted as a substrate by FshA (Figure S27), the affinity‐purified enzyme was incubated with FshE and FshM, commercially‐available fatty acids of different lengths, recombinant phosphopantetheinyl transferase (Sfp) and CoA overnight. Following trypsinization, the samples were analyzed by LC‐HRESIMS and LC‐HRESIMS/MS for the presence of fatty acyl‐FshE peptide ions (Figure 4A). FshE peptides loaded with hexadecanoic acid, cis‐9‐hexadecenoic acid (FA‐4) or tetradecanoic acid were detected at similar levels, and slightly less abundant with dodecanoic acid (Figure 4B). Octadecanoic acid and cis‐9,10‐methylenehexadecanoic acid were also supplied but loading was not observed in this competition assay (Figure S28). Fatty acyl‐FshM peptides or even Ppant‐bound FshM peptides could not be detected (Figure S29). While FAALs are known to be promiscuous to some extent [28], they usually display a substrate preference [29]. Thus, with these experiments, we confirmed that FshA could activate and load saturated and unsaturated C16 fatty acids onto FshE, in line with our biosynthetic hypothesis. The inability of FshA to convert most of the holo‐FshE even in an overnight reaction (Figure 4B), suggested that it did not reach native activity in vitro. This, together with the decreasing substrate solubility with increasing fatty acid chain length, and with the absence of downstream enzymes in the assay, may account for the low selectivity of FshA toward the C16 fatty acids, one of which is expected to be the actual substrate of FshA. Fatty acyl‐loaded FshE was also detected in control samples where no fatty acid had been added to the assay, but not in samples where FshA had been heat‐inactivated (Figure 4B). The former might result from the use of detergent in the FshA purification as it may have solubilized membrane components. LC‐HRESIMS/MS analysis confirmed that the detected m/z values were derived from the acyl‐loaded FshE peptides as the spectra showed the respective acyl‐containing, rearranged phosphopantetheine (PPant) ejection ion as the main peak (Figure S30).

Because the broad substrate specificity of FshA did not provide an unequivocal link between the fsh BGC and the fischerazoles, and to obtain additional evidence for the involvement of Fsh proteins in the biosynthesis of 1–3, we interrogated the role of the FshF methyltransferase. CMAS/CFAS enzymes typically act on cis‐unsaturated fatty acids [30]. We thus tested FshF initially for its ability to methylate the commercially available cis‐9‐hexadecenoic acid (FA‐4) tethered to FshE, using a coupled assay with FshA and SAM as the methyl source (Figure 4C). As expected, a C17 product was formed and, as observed in the supplementation assays, two deuterium atoms were incorporated when methyl‐d 3 SAM was used in the assay (Figures 4D,E and S31). This strongly implicated the fsh BGC in the biosynthesis of the fischerazoles. Still, the nature of the C17 product remained unclear at this stage.

FshF Generates a Vinyl Group Through Methylation and Rearrangement of cis‐8‐Hexadecanoic Acid

The isolation of 3, a non‐hydroxylated version of 1, suggested that alkyl chain rearrangement should be independent of the action of the cytochrome P450 FshL. We thus hypothesized that FshF alone would modify the alkyl chain through rearrangement and vinyl group formation. Based on the previous FshF assay with cis‐9‐hexadecenoic acid, and given the observed pattern of deuterium incorporation into the alkyl chain, this could only be reconciled if the actual substrate of FshF bears an unsaturation at position 8. We thus synthesized cis‐8‐hexadecenoic acid (FA‐5) and tested FshF methylation activity using this substrate through in vitro enzymatic assays with His‐tagged FshF, FshA and FshE. To facilitate detection of free reaction products, we coupled the assay with recombinant truncated E. coli thioesterase ‘tesA (Figure S32) [31], which hydrolyzes the FshE‐acyl thioester and releases free fatty acids. The fatty acid standard corresponding to the expected vinyl branched product (8‐vinylpentadecanoic acid, FA‐6) was synthesized along with the standard corresponding to the cyclopropanated fatty acid product (cis‐8,9‐methylenehexadecanoic acid, FA‐7) (see SI for details). LC‐HRESIMS free fatty acid analysis revealed that a peak of m/z value consistent with the two isobaric C17 products was observed, with roughly the same conversion rate as observed previously for FA‐4 (< 10%, Figure S33), but separation between the standards was poor, even after optimization of the chromatographic conditions (Figure S33). We thus considered converting the TesA‐released fatty acids into fatty acyl methyl esters (FAMEs) for GC–MS analysis (Figure 5A). The GC conditions used allowed for good separation of the standards FAME‐6 and FAME‐7, corresponding to the methyl esters of the previously synthesized vinyl branched and cyclopropanated fatty acids FA‐6 and FA‐7, respectively. Satisfyingly, analysis of the FshF in vitro derivatized reaction products showed that the vinyl branched FAME‐6 could be detected (Figures 5B and S34). Taking into account these results and the deuterium incorporation data into 1–3, we posit (Figure 5C) that vinyl group formation and acyl chain rearrangement involves—as proposed for CMAS and CFAS [32, 33, 34]—the formation of a carbocation intermediate following SAM transfer to an olefinic carbon. The base‐catalyzed mechanism would proceed through a Wagner–Meerwein‐like 1,2 alkyl shift, with concomitant C─C bond breakage and C─C bond formation possible from either position adjacent to the olefin. Strikingly, full‐assay derivatized products also contained the cyclopropanated FAME‐7 in abundance (Figures 5B and S34). which can be reconciled with the common carbocation intermediate (Figure 5C). The in vitro conditions, including the nature of the substrate (i.e., the natural substrate might already bear other functionalizations), likely do not lead to full selectivity toward the vinyl product. Reinspection of the NMR data for 1–3 excluded the presence of cyclopropanated versions of the fischerazoles as minor co‐eluting constituents of the collected peaks. Interestingly, a previous theoretical study on the mechanism of cyclopropanation by CMAS, reported by Liao et al. [32] underlines that rearrangements through a 1,2‐hydride shift would benefit from a lower activation barrier, and it was later found that CFAS active site mutants could generate such rearranged products [33]. Still, to the best of our knowledge, 1,2‐alkyl shifts had not been proposed for these enzymes. In any case, a concerted mechanism, which has not been fully ruled out for CMAS/CFAS enzymes [35], could also be envisaged for the action of FshF—therefore further studies will be necessary to better understand the exact mechanism of FshF and CMAS/CFAS enzymes. A phylogenetic analysis (Figure S35) places FshF with other cyanobacterial SAM‐dependent methyltransferases of yet undescribed functions, separately from characterized CMAS/CFAS enzymes, and closer to a large number of uncharacterized homologs from multiple bacterial phyla, which are interesting targets for future characterization efforts. Taken together, our results show that the action of FshF is necessary and sufficient to generate the unusual alkyl chain rearrangement with concomitant vinyl group formation observed in the fischerazoles. Curiously, and while mechanistically unrelated, a SAM‐dependent methyltranferase has recently been implicated in initating carbon extrusion in sodorifen biosynthesis [36].

Conclusion

We have used a combined perdeuterated fatty acid feeding and metabolomics approach [11] to uncover several cyanobacterial polychlorinated lipopeptides (1–3). These metabolites contain a number of unusual structural features, such as a vinyl‐branched fatty acyl moiety, an N‐methylated thiazole carboxamide moiety and a gem‐dichlorovinylidene aliphatic chain terminus. By identifying and validating a candidate fischerazole BGC (fsh), we clarified several aspects of their biosynthesis. The biosynthetic events leading to the rare gem‐dichlorovinylidene moiety are unclear at this stage, but the identification of the fsh BGC provides the groundwork for their dissection. Most strikingly, we found that the alkyl chain scaffold of 1–3 derives from hexadecanoic acid, which is modified and rearranged to eventually generate the unique vinyl branch. The CMAS/CFAS‐related SAM‐dependent methyltransferase FshF was found to be responsible for generating this unusual moiety from an olefinic C16 precursor, in all likelihood through a mechanism that is expected to involve the same intermediate postulated for typical cyclopropane synthases. Mechanistic studies of the generation of vinyl and cyclopropane groups by FshF may be important in clarifying also the action of the well‐studied cyclopropane‐generating CMAS/CFAS enzymes. Unfortunately, we were unable to clarify whether the Δ8 unsaturated fatty acyl intermediate in fischerazoles biosynthesis is generated by the putative fatty acid desaturase FshD, or if this enzyme is instead involved in the generation of the gem‐dichlorovinylidene moiety. Hence, the timing and exact nature of the unsaturated fatty acyl substrate of FshF remains unclear, which could explain the low conversions observed in the in vitro assays. Rearrangements such as the one reported here, enable functionalization of remote carbon centers and could be explored for biocatalytic strategies [3]. Our study illustrates the usefulness of a supplementation strategy to reveal natural products with unusually modified fatty acyl chains. In turn, such compounds and their BGCs become ideal subjects for the discovery and characterization of unique biosynthetic events leading to alkyl chain modifications.
