Therapeutic effects of virtual reality technology on chronic low back pain
Authors: Gengbiao Huang, Wenjie Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:This study aimed to systematically evaluate the clinical efficacy of virtual reality (VR) technology in patients with chronic low back pain (CLBP), focusing on its effects on pain, disability, kinesiophobia, and inflammatory markers.Methods:A systematic search was conducted in 6 Chinese and English databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Central, Embase, Chinese Biomedical Literature Database, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure) to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published before January 2025 that compared VR interventions with conventional treatments for CLBP. Meta-analysis was performed using RevMan 5.4 and Stata software. Primary outcomes included Visual Analog Scale (VAS), Numeric Rating Scale, Oswestry Disability Index, Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia, and biomarkers such as C-reactive protein, adrenocorticotropic hormone, and the cross-sectional area of the multifidus muscle. Heterogeneity analysis, subgroup analysis, sensitivity analysis, and publication bias assessment were also conducted.Results:A total of 15 RCTs involving 711 patients were included. Meta-analysis revealed that VR intervention significantly reduced VAS (MD = −1.69, 95% confidence intervals [CI]: −2.47 to −0.88, P < .0001), Numeric Rating Scale (MD = -0.91, 95% CI: −0.97 to −0.85, P < .00001), Oswestry Disability Index (MD = −5.92, 95% CI: −7.97 to −3.86, P < .00001), and Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia scores (MD = −9.04, 95% CI: −14.08 to −4.00, P = .0004) compared with control groups. VR also significantly decreased C-reactive protein and adrenocorticotropic hormone levels and improved the cross-sectional area of the multifidus muscle. Subgroup analysis indicated that short-term, high-frequency, and immersive VR interventions were more effective. Moderate to high heterogeneity was observed in some outcomes, and potential publication bias was noted for VAS scores.Conclusion:VR interventions demonstrate positive clinical value in the treatment of CLBP by effectively alleviating pain, improving function, and reducing fear of movement, with favorable safety and feasibility. Future high-quality RCTs with long-term follow-up are warranted to explore standardized and personalized intervention protocols to further enhance clinical application and dissemination.

1. Introduction

According to the Global Burden of Disease study, approximately 577 million people worldwide are affected by low back pain, and the number continues to grow with global population expansion. The disability burden of low back pain is particularly severe among individuals aged 50 to 54 years,[1] with 11% to 12% of the population experiencing disability due to back pain.[2] Multiple risk factors are associated with the development of chronic low back pain (CLBP), including prolonged physical labor, sedentary lifestyle, smoking, and mental health issues such as depression. Chronic low back pain not only severely impairs patients’ quality of life, work capacity, and daily functioning but also imposes a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems globally.[2,3]

The application of virtual reality (VR) technology in the treatment of CLBP has attracted increasing attention. Studies have shown that VR, through its immersive experience, can effectively reduce pain perception, improve functional status, and enhance patients’ quality of life.[4] As a noninvasive therapeutic approach, VR can divert patients’ attention from pain, reduce pain intensity, and increase pain tolerance. Moreover, VR has been found to improve emotional well-being and enhance coping strategies, thereby complementing conventional treatments.[5,6] Notably, VR not only relieves pain but also enhances physical function, particularly in rehabilitation settings, helping patients better manage the challenges associated with CLBP.[7]

Although preliminary clinical studies suggest that VR may be effective in managing CLBP, its efficacy has not been widely recognized due to a lack of large-scale, high-quality evidence-based research. To date, comprehensive literature analyses on the application of VR in CLBP treatment remain limited. Therefore, this study conducted a meta-analysis to systematically collect and evaluate randomized controlled trial (RCTs) on the use of VR in CLBP treatment, aiming to provide more robust evidence and theoretical support for its clinical application.

2.3. Literature search strategy

Two reviewers (the 1st author and corresponding author) independently searched 6 medical databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Embase, Chinese Biomedical Literature Database, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure. Search terms included: “virtual reality,” “chronic low back pain,” “treatment,” and “exercise.” The search covered all literature up to January 2025.

2.4. Study selection and data extraction

Two independent reviewers screened studies, extracted data, and cross-verified the results. Disagreements were resolved by consulting a 3rd senior researcher.

Duplicate records were removed 1st. Next, non-RCTs and non-clinical studies such as reviews and case reports were excluded. Studies not related to VR intervention for CLBP were removed based on titles and abstracts. Full-text articles were then reviewed to exclude those without relevant outcome data (VAS, NRS, ODI, TSK, CRP, ACTH, CSA) or incomplete/missing data. If necessary, authors were contacted to obtain missing information.

A comprehensive data extraction table was created, including author, year, sample size, intervention details, control conditions, treatment duration, and outcome indicators.

2.5. Quality assessment

Two independent reviewers assessed the risk of bias using the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (Version 5.1.0). Criteria included: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, completeness of outcome data, selective reporting, and other potential sources of bias.

Each item was rated as “Yes” (low risk), “Unclear” (uncertain risk), or “No” (high risk). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or adjudication by a senior investigator if consensus could not be reached.

2.6. Statistical analysis

Meta-analyses were conducted using RevMan 5.4 (Cochrane Collaboration, London, United Kingdom) and Stata software (StataCorp LLC, College Station). Standardized mean difference (SMD) was used for continuous variables, while relative risk was calculated for categorical variables. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran Q test and the I² statistic. An I² > 50% indicated substantial heterogeneity, in which case a random-effects model was applied; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was used.

Subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, such as intervention type, duration, and VR format. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the results. Risk of bias was evaluated using the Cochrane tool, and publication bias was assessed with funnel plots and Egger regression test. All statistical tests were 2-tailed, with a significance level of P < .05. Forest plots were used to visually present effect estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

2.7. Ethical considerations

This study is a meta-analysis and does not involve individual patient data or direct patient intervention. All included studies were publicly available peer-reviewed articles. Therefore, ethical approval and informed consent were not required.

3. Results

3.1. Literature search results

In the Identification phase, a total of 1018 articles were retrieved from 6 medical databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Embase, Chinese Biomedical Literature Database, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure.

After removing 423 duplicates, 595 articles remained for the Screening phase.

Among these, 298 articles were excluded for being non-clinical studies (e.g., meta-analyses, reviews, case reports) or non-randomized designs (e.g., quasi-RCTs), leaving 297 articles for further screening.

Following a thorough review of titles and abstracts, 250 articles were excluded because they were unrelated to the use of VR in CLBP treatment or the intervention did not involve VR technology.

The remaining 47 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility.

After excluding studies without relevant outcome indicators (VAS, NRS, ODI, TSK, CRP, ACTH, or multifidus CSA), incomplete data, or inaccessible full texts (despite attempts to contact the authors), a total of 15 studies were included in the final Included phase.

3.4. Meta-analysis results (see Table 2 for summary)

3.4.1. VAS score

A total of 9 studies reported VAS scores, comprising 356 participants (179 in the experimental group and 177 in the control group). A test for heterogeneity revealed significant variability among studies (I² = 97%, P < .00001); therefore, a random-effects model was applied. The results indicated that the VAS scores in the VR intervention group were significantly lower than those in the control group [MD = −1.69, 95% CI (−2.47 to −0.88), P < .0001] (see Fig. 4 and Table 2 for details).

A sensitivity analysis was conducted by sequentially removing each study and reanalyzing the remaining data. The exclusion of “Nambi (2022)” resulted in the greatest change in heterogeneity (I² = 95%, P = .00003), though the overall effect size remained stable [MD = −0.48, 95% CI (−2.28 to −0.67), P < .0001], indicating the robustness of the results (see Fig. 5).

Forest plot of VAS scores (after sensitivity analysis). VAS = Visual Analogue Scale.

To explore the source of the high heterogeneity in VAS scores (I² = 97%), subgroup analyses were performed based on intervention duration, frequency, and device type. Studies with an intervention duration shorter than 4 weeks (MD = −2.45, 95% CI: −2.59 to −2.31) showed greater effectiveness compared to those longer than 4 weeks (MD = −0.87, 95% CI: −1.10 to −0.65). This may be attributed to heightened initial responsiveness, where short-term, high-intensity interventions more effectively activate neuroplasticity. In contrast, longer durations might reduce compliance or increase tolerance, diminishing effectiveness.

Intervention frequency of ≥ 3 times per week (MD = −2.83) was more effective than < 3 times per week (MD = −1.92), suggesting that frequency plays a key role in activating the “attention-shift/reward-regulation” pathway. Frequent immersive experiences may continuously engage the prefrontal–reward network, enhancing the combined desensitization and distraction effects. Regarding device type, immersive systems (MD = −2.04) were slightly more effective than non-immersive ones (MD = −1.92), possibly due to superior multimodal sensory integration (visual, auditory, kinesthetic), which enhances attentional engagement and facilitates deeper neurocognitive modulation of pain (see Fig. 6).

Five studies reported NRS scores with a total of 333 participants (168 in the experimental group, 165 in the control group). The heterogeneity test showed moderate variability (I² = 48%, P = .10), so a fixed-effects model was applied. The results demonstrated that the NRS scores in the VR group were significantly lower than those in the control group [MD = −0.91, 95% CI (−0.97 to −0.85), P < .00001] (see Fig. 7 and Table 2 for details).

Sensitivity analysis showed that exclusion of “Afzal (2022)” resulted in the greatest reduction in heterogeneity (I² = 0%, P = .58), though the pooled effect remained largely unchanged [MD = −0.90, 95% CI (−0.96 to −0.84), P < .00001], confirming the robustness of the findings (see Fig. 8).

Forest plot of NRS scores (after sensitivity analysis). NRS = Numerical Rating Scale.

3.4.3. ODI

Five studies reported ODI outcomes, with a total sample of 184 participants (93 experimental, 91 control). No significant heterogeneity was detected (I² = 0%, P = .54), thus a fixed-effects model was used. The VR group showed significantly lower ODI scores compared to the control group [MD = −5.92, 95% CI (−7.97 to −3.86), P < .00001] (see Fig. 9 and Table 2 for details).

Forest plot of Oswestry Disability Index (ODI).

Upon sensitivity analysis, the exclusion of “Lichun (2019)” slightly increased heterogeneity (I² = 3%, P = .38), but the overall result remained consistent [MD = −5.67, 95% CI (−9.13 to −2.21), P = .001], indicating result stability (see Fig. 10).

Forest plot of Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) (after sensitivity analysis).

3.4.4. TSK

Seven studies reported TSK scores, totaling 333 participants (168 experimental, 165 control). A significant level of heterogeneity was observed (I² = 95%, P < .00001), and thus a random-effects model was applied. The VR group showed significantly lower TSK scores than the control group [MD = −9.04, 95% CI (−14.08 to −4.00), P = .0004] (see Fig. 11 and Table 2 for details).

Forest plot of TSK. TSK = Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia.

Sensitivity analysis revealed that excluding “Nambi (2021)” reduced heterogeneity most substantially (I² = 91%, P < .00001), while the pooled effect size remained stable [MD = −7.24, 95% CI (−11.50 to −2.98), P = .0009], confirming the robustness of the conclusion (see Fig. 12).

Forest plot of TSK (after sensitivity analysis). TSK = Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia.

Subgroup analysis was conducted to examine the heterogeneity in TSK results (I² = 95%). Interventions with ≥ 3 sessions per week (MD = −14.61) were significantly more effective than those with < 3 sessions per week (MD = −5.45), likely due to repeated exposure leading to desensitization effects. High-frequency interventions may reinforce cognitive restructuring related to safe movement, disrupting the maladaptive link between movement and pain. Shorter intervention durations (<4 weeks, MD = −14.21) were also more effective than longer ones (>4 weeks, MD = −8.92), possibly due to stronger behavioral correction during concentrated training periods. Immersive VR systems (MD = −11.00) outperformed non-immersive systems (MD = −12.66), potentially because immersive VR allows better control over attentional focus and engagement of executive functions, modulating catastrophizing thoughts through the default mode network and enhancing cognitive-behavioral transformation (see Fig. 13).

Forest plot of subgroup analysis for TSK. TSK = Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia.

3.4.5. CRP

Two studies reported CRP levels, with 102 participants (51 experimental, 51 control). A high degree of heterogeneity was observed (I² = 98%, P < .00001), and a random-effects model was applied. The VR group showed significantly lower CRP levels than the control group [MD = −0.65, 95% CI (−1.14 to −0.16), P = .009] (see Fig. 14 and Table 2 for details).

Forest plot of CRP levels. CRP = C-reactive protein.

Two studies reported ACTH levels with 102 participants in total. Significant heterogeneity was present (I² = 99%, P < .00001), so a random-effects model was used. Results showed that the ACTH levels in the VR group were significantly lower than those in the control group [MD = −3.89, 95% CI (−6.06 to −1.71), P = .0005] (see Fig. 15 and Table 2 for details).

Forest plot of ACTH levels. ACTH = adrenocorticotropic hormone.

Two studies reported CSA measurements of the multifidus muscle, totaling 102 participants. Substantial heterogeneity was detected (I² = 88%, P = .03), and a random-effects model was applied. The CSA of the multifidus muscle was significantly larger in the VR group than in the control group [MD = 1.06, 95% CI (0.58–1.54), P < .0001] (see Fig. 16 and Table 2 for details).

3.4.8. Risk of bias assessment: Funnel plots and Egger test

For the VAS score, Egger test revealed a bias coefficient of −7.896632 with a P-value of 0.031, indicating statistically significant publication bias. The 95% CI [−14.85 to −0.95] excluded 0, further supporting this conclusion. Although the slope P-value was .184 (not statistically significant), the funnel plot displayed asymmetrical distribution. Studies were clustered on the left-hand side (negative SMD values), with fewer studies on the right, suggesting potential publication bias. Notably, the studies by Nambi (2021, 2022) and Yilmaz (2017) deviated substantially from the funnel plot center, with extremely low SMD values, and may have exerted considerable influence on the meta-analysis results. Possible sources of bias include publication bias (studies with larger VAS effect sizes are more likely to be published) along with small-sample bias and methodological heterogeneity. Collectively, these factors suggest that the meta-analysis of VAS scores may have overestimated the intervention effect (see Fig. 17 and Table 3 for details).

4. Discussion

This meta-analysis evaluated the efficacy of VR interventions in the management of CLBP. Based on 15 RCTs involving a total of 711 participants, the findings indicate that VR interventions outperform conventional treatments across multiple clinical outcomes. Specifically, VR significantly reduced patients’ VAS scores (MD = −2.12, 95% CI [−3.09 to −1.14]), NRS scores (MD = −1.86, 95% CI [−2.66 to −1.07]), and ODI scores (MD = −13.48, 95% CI [−19.67 to −7.28]). In addition, VR showed advantages in psychological parameters such as the TSK (MD = −4.13, 95% CI [−6.78 to −1.49]). Moreover, VR interventions were associated with improvements in physiological and morphological outcomes, including reductions in CRP levels (MD = −3.21, 95% CI [−4.72 to −1.70]), ACTH levels (MD = −8.16, 95% CI [−12.98 to −3.34]), and increases in the CSA of the multifidus muscle (MD = 3.94, 95% CI [1.08–6.79]). These findings suggest that VR may confer multifaceted benefits in alleviating CLBP and its associated symptoms. Despite the overall positive effects observed in this meta-analysis, considerable heterogeneity was detected in several outcomes, particularly in studies reporting VAS (I² = 97%) and TSK scores (I² = 95%). Subgroup analyses revealed that this heterogeneity could be largely attributed to variations in intervention duration, frequency, and device type. Interventions with shorter durations, higher frequencies, and immersive VR systems generally demonstrated greater efficacy, indicating a dose-form-dependent effect. Sensitivity analyses further confirmed that the removal of individual studies had minimal impact on the overall results, highlighting the robustness of the findings. These results reinforce the therapeutic potential of VR as a non-pharmacological modality for CLBP management.

The effectiveness of VR in pain management is thought to derive from its interactive and immersive features, which enable users to divert attention away from pain stimuli and focus on engaging virtual environments. For instance, studies have demonstrated that VR environments with rich sensory stimuli can effectively capture and redirect users’ attention, thereby reducing the perception of pain.[23] The interactivity and feedback mechanisms of VR games are considered core features that enhance immersion and attenuate pain experiences.[24] Additionally, auditory stimuli (especially well-designed sound effects) within VR can guide user attention and reduce subjective pain perception.[25] These multisensory distractions make VR an effective pain management tool by diverting cognitive resources away from nociceptive processing.

Recent functional magnetic resonance imaging studies have further elucidated the neural mechanisms underlying VR’s analgesic effects. Immersive VR experiences have been shown to enhance activation in the prefrontal cortex and anterior cingulate cortex, regions involved in emotional regulation and attentional modulation of pain.[26] Simultaneously, the insular cortex (an area crucial for the perception of pain and interoceptive awareness) is also activated during VR intervention.[27] On the other hand, VR has been reported to downregulate the activity of the primary somatosensory cortex (S1), reducing cortical representation of peripheral nociceptive input. More importantly, VR training has been found to enhance functional connectivity with midbrain reward circuits, such as the ventral tegmental area and nucleus accumbens. This activation of dopaminergic pathways may help disrupt the maladaptive cycle linking pain, emotion, and attention, thereby facilitating overall pain relief. These neural mechanisms provide a biological rationale for the effectiveness of VR in chronic pain treatment.[28,29]

Previous studies have demonstrated that VR therapy can significantly alleviate pain in patients with CLBP and improve emotional well-being, yielding favorable clinical outcomes.[30] Similarly, Wong et al[31] reported that VR interventions effectively reduce the intensity of various types of chronic pain, including CLBP, and show potential in alleviating emotional disturbances such as anxiety and depression. These findings are consistent with the results of the present study and further support the therapeutic value of VR in managing CLBP. Moreover, some researchers have suggested that VR may serve as a viable alternative or adjunct to opioid medications, particularly in the context of chronic pain reduction.[32,33] Additionally, VR interventions have been associated with reductions in biomarkers such as CRP and ACTH, implying potential anti-inflammatory and stress-relieving effects.[34] However, it is important to note that CRP and ACTH, as indicators of systemic inflammation and stress response, primarily reflect overall physiological status and cannot be directly linked to changes in pain scores such as VAS.[35] CRP levels may be influenced by factors including infection, emotional stress, and sleep quality, whereas ACTH reflects hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis activation and is closely related to anxiety and fear.[36,37] Therefore, while reductions in these biomarkers may suggest alleviation of systemic stress and inflammation, they should not be interpreted in isolation as evidence of pain relief. Future studies should incorporate multimodal analyses combining neural activity, electrophysiological signals, and emotional factors to explore their integrated predictive value in pain modulation.

Previous research has established a significant positive correlation between pain intensity and kinesiophobia in patients with CLBP. Specifically, kinesiophobia leads to fear-avoidance behaviors, resulting in reduced physical activity and progressive weakening of core stabilizing muscles. Diminished strength in these core muscles compromises spinal stability, exacerbating pain and potentially contributing to its chronicity, thereby forming a vicious cycle.[38] Moreover, the presence of kinesiophobia restricts functional mobility and adversely impacts overall quality of life and rehabilitation outcomes. Consequently, kinesiophobia is recognized as a critical factor in the management of CLBP and warrants targeted intervention.[39] VR technology has also been shown to modulate brain functional connectivity, particularly by enhancing integration between the central executive network (CEN) and the default mode network (DMN), thereby contributing to pain perception regulation and reducing the occurrence of kinesiophobia.[40] A separate review highlighted the efficacy of VR exposure therapy in treating anxiety disorders and specific phobias, demonstrating its ability to significantly reduce anxiety symptoms.[41] Thus, the current study’s finding that VR helps relieve kinesiophobia is consistent with previous evidence. From a neurobiological perspective, the development of kinesiophobia may be closely related to imbalances between the CEN and DMN. The CEN is primarily involved in goal-directed behavior and attentional control, while the DMN is associated with catastrophizing thoughts, introspection, and pain anticipation.[42,43] In individuals with chronic pain, this imbalance typically manifests as hyperactivity of the DMN and underactivation of the CEN, leading to impaired cognitive regulation and heightened fear of movement. Immersive VR interventions, by engaging multiple sensory modalities, may redirect attentional focus away from introspective pain-related processing toward task-oriented behavior, potentially suppressing DMN activity while enhancing CEN function. Functional MRI studies have shown that VR interventions can increase activation in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and anterior cingulate cortex (key regions involved in cognitive control and emotional regulation) while reducing activity in DMN-related areas such as the posterior cingulate cortex and medial prefrontal cortex. This rebalancing of brain network dynamics may help reduce catastrophizing, enhance motor control, and ultimately mitigate kinesiophobia symptoms.[44]

This study confirmed the overall efficacy of VR interventions in the management of CLBP. However, variability in individual responses suggests the need to identify patient subgroups that are more likely to benefit from this approach. Both existing literature and clinical observations indicate that patients with nonspecific CLBP (such as those with myofascial pain syndrome or posture-related chronic pain) tend to respond more favorably to VR interventions, possibly due to their reliance on cognitive-emotional mechanisms for pain regulation.[45] In contrast, patients with clearly defined organic pathology (e.g., severe disc herniation or nerve root compression) may experience limited benefits due to functional impairments. Age also appears to be a significant determinant of treatment response. Individuals aged 20 to 60 years typically exhibit better operational ability, perceptual receptivity, and interactive engagement with VR devices, resulting in more pronounced therapeutic effects. For older adults or those with comorbid neuropathic pain, anxiety, or depression, a pre-intervention assessment of individual capacities (using tools such as the Montreal Cognitive Assessment, Frontal Assessment Battery, or Patient Health Questionnaire-9) is recommended to determine whether modifications to content, pacing, or supplementary offline training are needed.[45] Future studies should incorporate more detailed subgroup analyses in RCTs, aiming to develop personalized VR intervention strategies based on variables such as age, pain mechanisms, and psychological profiles, thereby improving treatment efficacy and applicability.

Despite systematically evaluating the efficacy of VR in CLBP management, this meta-analysis has several limitations. The funnel plot for VAS scores revealed some asymmetry, suggesting potential publication bias from small-sample positive studies. However, Egger test did not reach statistical significance (P > .05), indicating that such bias may remain within an acceptable range statistically. Given the tendency for positive results to be more frequently published in interventional research, this potential bias should still be considered when interpreting the estimated overall effects. Future research should prioritize pre-registration and emphasize the reporting of neutral or negative outcomes to reduce systematic overestimation and enhance the robustness of meta-analytic conclusions. Another limitation lies in the relatively short follow-up durations of the included RCTs. Most studies had follow-up periods of less than 3 months, with only a few extending to 3 to 6 months. These limited data suggest that some effects of VR interventions may persist over time.[46,47] However, due to small-sample sizes and methodological heterogeneity, it remains difficult to draw definitive conclusions regarding the long-term efficacy of VR in CLBP treatment. From a mechanistic standpoint, VR may induce rapid responses by activating reward systems and emotional regulation networks in the brain, thereby facilitating the “attention-shifting-pain modulation” process. Yet, whether these effects are sustained after discontinuation of the intervention remains unclear, as supporting evidence from neuroimaging or behavioral studies is lacking.[48] To address this gap, future research should incorporate longer follow-up durations (particularly beyond 6 months) to assess trajectories of pain relief, functional recovery, and psychological adaptation. This would help clarify the durability and long-term value of VR interventions in the treatment of CLBP.

As technology advances and VR devices become increasingly accessible, the cost-effectiveness of VR interventions has significantly improved. With the continuous decline in hardware prices and the widespread adoption of portable, all-in-one devices (e.g., mobile-based head-mounted displays), patients can now undergo intervention training without requiring high-performance computing systems. This has substantially reduced both the operational and equipment-related costs per session. Preliminary studies have shown that, over comparable intervention periods, the average per-session cost of VR (after initial device investment) is lower than that of conventional physical therapy. Moreover, the reusability and remote applicability of VR systems make them especially suitable for long-term pain management and rehabilitation follow-up.[49] In some low- and middle-income countries, there have been promising attempts to integrate culturally adapted VR content, demonstrating high acceptability and preliminary clinical efficacy.[50] Therefore, future research should include region-specific cost–benefit analyses using health economic evaluation models to assess the feasibility and sustainability of VR implementation in countries with varying levels of economic development. Currently, there is no standardized guideline for VR intervention parameters in the literature. Variability in intervention duration, frequency, and total length has likely contributed to the observed differences in therapeutic outcomes and the heterogeneity among studies. Most trials have used interventions lasting 2 to 6 weeks, with frequencies ranging from 1 to 5 sessions per week and session durations typically between 15 and 30 minutes. Based on subgroup analysis in this study, shorter-duration (<4 weeks), high-frequency (≥3 sessions/week), and immersive VR interventions generally yielded more substantial clinical improvements. In light of these findings, we recommend adopting a standardized intervention protocol consisting of 20 to 30 minutes per session, 3 sessions per week, for a minimum of 4 weeks. Additionally, future studies should explore dose–response relationships to determine the marginal effects and optimal dose ranges of various intervention intensities for pain reduction and kinesiophobia alleviation.

5. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that VR technology provides favorable clinical outcomes in the treatment of CLBP, effectively reducing pain, improving functional impairment, and alleviating kinesiophobia. VR interventions also exert positive effects on inflammatory markers and muscle morphology. Short-duration, high-frequency, and immersive VR interventions tend to produce superior therapeutic results. Although certain heterogeneity and potential publication bias exist, the overall findings are robust, supporting the use of VR as an effective adjunctive tool in CLBP rehabilitation. Future research should focus on optimizing intervention parameters, extending follow-up durations, and developing personalized treatment strategies to maximize the clinical utility of VR.
