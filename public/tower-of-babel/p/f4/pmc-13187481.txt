An efficient uncertainty integrated aggregation scheme for water quality detection and longitudinal carbon offset estimation in the Yamuna River
Authors: M Sandhiya, H A Bhavithra, S Leoni Sharmila, S Poongothai, M Haritha, S Sindu Devi, K Kannan, A Menaga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRiver water quality deterioration caused by industrialization and urbanization not only threatens public health but also weakens ecosystem services such as carbon assimilation. Conventional water quality indices and deterministic assessment models inadequately represent the heterogeneous and uncertain nature of hydrochemical and microbial monitoring data, particularly in complex river systems like the Yamuna. This study aims to develop an uncertainty-aware framework for (i) reliable detection of water quality degradation hotspots and (ii) quantitative estimation of longitudinal carbon offset capacity along the Yamuna River by explicitly modeling measurement indeterminacy and nonlinear pollution effects. An ellipsoidal single-valued neutrosophic modeling structure is introduced to represent directional and anisotropic uncertainty in physicochemical and microbial parameters. Two trigonometric aggregation operators—ellipsoidal single-valued neutrosophic trigonometric weighted averaging (E-SvNVTWA) and weighted geometric (E-SvNVTWG)—are formulated to capture overall ecological condition and extreme pollution sensitivity, respectively. Neutrosophic efficiency indices derived from 25 CPCB monitoring stations are further linked to a normalized carbon offset estimation model to evaluate ecosystem service potential. The dual-operator framework reveals a pronounced upstream–downstream gradient in ecological condition. Upstream stations (e.g., Dehradun, Hanumanchatti) exhibit high neutrosophic efficiency (> 0.9) and higher relative ecological processing efficiency, while downstream industrial stretches (e.g., Hasanpur, Mohena Palwal) show sharp efficiency decline and near-zero TWG values, indicating ecological functional stress. The TWG operator demonstrates enhanced sensitivity to severe organic and microbial pollution, effectively identifying critical degradation hotspots. The proposed ellipsoidal neutrosophic dual-aggregation model provides a structured uncertainty-aware decision-support framework that integrates uncertainty modeling, nonlinear pollution response, and ecosystem service evaluation. By linking water quality diagnostics with carbon offset potential, the framework advances climate-relevant river basin assessment and supports sustainability-oriented management of heavily stressed river systems.Keywords: Hydrochemical parameters, Carbon offset, Water quality, Safe drinking water, Sanitation & hygiene (WASH), Wastewater treatment, Ecosystem protection, Sustainable managementSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Hydrology

Introduction and related works

Water quality science of the river system is the emerging topic in the industries, agriculture, and public health sectors. It is basically depending on the features (biological, physical, and geological) associated with the chemical processes of the water system on earth. The Yamuna River is the second-largest tributary river of the Ganges and the longest tributary in India and it has faced serious consequences of urbanisation, untreated industrial waste, and population pressure. Patel et al.1 have discussed the pollutant load across Delhi during the pandemic period and then they have showed the significant impacts of lockdown due to COVID-19 on the water quality of the Yamuna within its Delhi NCT stretch. Joshi et al.2 carried out the investigations for the physiochemical and biological conditions of the river Yamuna based on measurements acquired at Palla station, Delhi during 2009–19. In the Haryana stretch, Singh et al.3 have used the water quality from the river to react to changes during the pre- and post-monsoon. They indicated that their study has found a strong significant positive correlation between COD with BOD and total suspended solids (TSS) during the lockdown period. Kumar et al.4 have reported that the Yamuna River continuously undergoes pollution and algae are still survived in highly contaminated Yamuna water. Sharma et al.5 have presented the critical review for the level of pollution, especially in urban and agricultural areas and appeal for the ecological balance of the Yamuna River.

Groundwater is also one of the water resources used to preserve natural water sources for drinking, irrigation, and several other purposes, especially in industrial applications6–12. Ali et al.13 have presented a novel integration of the Water Ratio Index (WRI), Normalized Difference Chlorophyll Index (NDCI), Land Use/Land Cover (LULC) mapping, and Cellular Automata–Markov (CA–Markov) modeling with temperature fluctuations to monitor irrigated land dynamics using high-resolution (30 m) satellite imagery in South Africa’s North West Province between 2016 and 2023. Baloch et al.14–16 have investigated groundwater quality in confined and unconfined Quaternary aquifers in Western Jilin, focusing on key parameters: fluoride15 and nitrate16. The integration of AI technologies with groundwater pollution risk assessment methods to optimize the computational processes and evaluation workflows was explored and applied in the case study of Shenyang City in Northeast China recently17. Due to recent anthropogenic activities and environmental changes in Sakrand, Sindh, Pakistan, the groundwater is extremely vulnerable. To provide safe drinking and agricultural water, hydrogeochemical analysis is required18.

Baloch et al. have conducted a study to investigate the hydrogeochemical mechanism of Sargodha, one of Pakistan’s fastest-growing cities, during 2015 and 2021 under two different LULC indices named normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) and normalized difference built-up index (NDBI)19. Groundwater quality is influenced by urbanization and land use land cover (LULC) changes20. Iqbal et al.21 have investigated their impact on groundwater quality in Quetta City, Pakistan, from 2015 to 2021. Chai et al.22 have reported that small-sized bacteria exhibited a stronger migration potential and a higher biological risk. Due to the surface–subsurface interactions, the bacterial communities in river water were more similar to that in groundwater after rain.

Environmental monitoring data include uncertainty due to sampling, analytical errors, and natural fluctuations in river systems. Neutrosophic theory provides a flexible mathematical framework for handling uncertainty by modelling truth, indeterminacy, and falsity components of information. A simplified neutrosophic indeterminate set (SNIS) had been presented to describe the uncertainty degrees of the truth, falsity, indeterminacy by Du et al.23 and they have introduced a Q-indeterminate correlation method for multi-criteria decision-making conditions and then DUS-Weibull transformation model to improve the environment was proposed by Nayana et al.24. The recent works by Das, Gupta, Tripathy et al.25 have applied the interval-valued frameworks for sensitive detection of changes in water quality. Verma et al.26 used one-dimensional computer model, called QUAL2Kw, to simulate BOD, COD, DO, and pH and reported that wastewater qualities of point sources might vary depending on collection time and sampling procedure. Tiwari et al.27 have presented the improvement in DO levels and reduction of microbial conditions in the upper Ganga during the pandemic. Further, they stated that the rivers can self-rejuvenate if issues of human impact and anthropogenic events are adequately resolved and underline our duty for purifying the ecosystem.

Several techniques based on neutrosophic sets have been proposed, for example a decision-making method based on Pythagorean language28, a multi-criterion decision making (MCDM) method29, exponential aggregation operators-based method30, a new ranking methodology of single-valued neutrosophic (SVN) numbers31 and bioretention32. Das evaluated water quality of the Mahanadi River33,34, Baitarani River35, Brahmani River36,37 and he used multiple machine learning tools38 in few researches. In this consequence, the existing research on the Yamuna River has failed to adopt the approach of determining the connection between Hydrochemical status and ecosystem service. The present study addresses these gaps by introducing a dual-operator ellipsoidal single-valued neutrosophic aggregation framework (E-SvNVTWA and E-SvNVTWG) that model’s uncertainty through geometrically anisotropic ellipsoidal structures and incorporates trigonometric operators to enhance nonlinear sensitivity to pollution extremes. The novelty lies in (i) extending neutrosophic theory to an ellipsoidal, direction-dependent uncertainty representation suited to environmental monitoring, (ii) employing a complementary averaging–geometric operator pair to distinguish moderate degradation from severe multi-parameter ecological stress conditions, and (iii) integrating the resulting neutrosophic efficiency indices with a structured carbon offset estimation scheme, thereby creating a unified decision-support approach that connects uncertain water quality assessment with longitudinal carbon assimilation potential in a major river system.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the proposed methodological framework, including the theoretical foundations of circular intuitionistic fuzzy sets, single-valued neutrosophic sets, and the development of the Ellipsoidal Single-Valued Neutrosophic Trigonometric Weighted Averaging (E-SvNVTWA) and Weighted Geometric (E-SvNVTWG) operators. Section 3 provides the results and discussion, including spatial analysis of Yamuna River water quality, neutrosophic efficiency evaluation across monitoring stations, and the linkage between water quality degradation and carbon offset capacity. Finally, the concluding section summarizes the key findings, methodological contributions, and implication.

Materials and methods

In practical river monitoring, uncertainty is not uniform across all dimensions of information. Physicochemical parameters such as pH or temperature are typically measured with relatively small and symmetric experimental error, whereas biological and microbial indicators (e.g., BOD, fecal coliform) exhibit high variability due to sampling timing, laboratory procedures, and environmental fluctuations. Spherical neutrosophic models assume that uncertainty spreads equally in all directions of truth, indeterminacy, and falsity, which implies that every parameter has the same level and structure of imprecision. This is rarely realistic in environmental systems. Interval-based approaches, while allowing ranges, treat uncertainty independently along axes without representing how uncertainty intensity differs directionally or how it clusters around the central value. In contrast, ellipsoidal uncertainty modeling assigns different semi-axes to the truth, indeterminacy, and falsity components, enabling anisotropic representation of uncertainty. Intuitively, this means the model can stretch more in directions where measurement ambiguity is high (e.g., microbial data) and remain compact where confidence is stronger (e.g., temperature or pH). Such geometry better reflects the real structure of river water quality data, where uncertainty is parameter-dependent and direction-sensitive. Therefore, we have used ellipsoidal neutrosophic framework which provides a more faithful and physically interpretable representation of environmental monitoring uncertainty than spherical or interval-based models.

Proposed methodology

The rationale behind the selection of the framework for the ellipsoidal single-valued Neutrosophic set (E-SvNS) lies in the requirement to model the anisotropic and asymmetric uncertainty present in the data used for environmental monitoring. In riverine systems, the parameters of water quality have different levels of uncertainty based on the sampling rate, laboratory analysis accuracy, periodicity, and human impact. The spherical Neutrosophic models have a uniform uncertainty radius for all dimensions, whereas the ellipsoidal models allow for independent semi-axes for the truth, indeterminacy, and falsity components. This is especially significant for microbial indicators and organic pollution parameters, which have been found to have higher uncertainty levels than physicochemical parameters like pH and temperature.

This data modeled by applied mathematical equations an ellipsoidal Neutrosophic structural evaluation tool within this type of domain allows for Trigonometric Weighted Averaging (TWA) and Trigonometric Weighted Geometry (TWG) where a nonlinear mechanism between the water quality indicators. This effectively supports a particular type of decision-making mechanism that considers a relationship between uncertainty-based offsets and river assessment.

Proposed methodology.

Trigonometric aggregation operators are used in this research to add nonlinear sensitivity to the intensity of pollution, which is a critical requirement for ecological diagnostics. Linear aggregation operators can suppress extreme pollution information by averaging it with moderate information, resulting in the underestimation of ecological stress. The E-SvNVTWA operator can identify overall water quality information by reflecting average system behaviour, while the E-SvNVTWG operator focuses on the worst-case pollution scenario. This two-operator approach allows complementary interpretation, which can distinguish degradation from severe multi-parameter ecological stress conditions in highly polluted river sections.

Uncertainty impact is not balanced towards the various parameters of the water quality present in the process of environmental observation. This means that the standard deviation in the case of biological parameters, might be considerably large compared to the pH level or the temperature present in the water. Ellipsoidal uncertainty modeling is situations, in which the uncertainty would be independent in the truth, and the falseness axes. However, the spherical neuteosophic model would need the value to be the same in all the axes, thereby suggesting that the uncertainty present might be the same in all the parameters thereby oversimplifying real world systems. Models present based on the interval concept might be flexible but would, once again assume the uncertainty as being independent in the various parameters. This model takes into considerations the uncertainty in the shape of the ellipsoidal rather than the spherical or interval concepts thereby making it a more realistic approach towards the real-world systems present in the process of river water quality observation.

River water quality parameters show significant seasonal variability in response to rainfall, dilution effects especially in tropical regions. Monsoon period water quality parameters are affected mainly in terms of water flow, whereas in non-monsoon periods, water flow reduction and pollutant accumulation are significant. The water quality parameters in this study are represented terms of spatial comparisons across various water quality monitoring stations.

The Yamuna River has taken as a case study to evaluate the applicability of the model, the basic methodology followed does not require river constants, for the adaptation and application of the model to other river basins or areas, the only adaptation that needs to be made consists of the selection of the water quality parameters or the information.

Carbon -offset estimation

The carbon offsetting process is significantly influenced by a variety of critical Hydrochemical parameters, including dissolved oxygen, biochemical demand and microbial load, controlling the relative carbon-processing proxy derived from water quality efficiency indices. The decreased water quality status reduces the concentration of dissolved oxygen in the river. The functional connection between water quality and the implications for carbon offsetting may lead to a decline in Hydrochemical parameters on the natural carbon offsetting capacity of the river. The framework for carbon offset estimation is developed to provide a clear and understandable relationship between the status of water quality and the potential for ecosystem services. A linear scaling function is used to transform the normalized Neutrosophic water quality indices into carbon offset capacity. Although the ecological response to pollution might be nonlinear, the linear scaling function is used as a basic representation for comparison purposes. The model is developed in such a way that it can be extended to a nonlinear or threshold model based on additional empirical information in the future.

Results and discussion

The results clearly show that the water quality and carbon offset potential in the Yamuna River vary significantly between the upstream and downstream urban industrial parts33–38. The efficiency values obtained for certain stations, such as Yamunotri, Hanumanchatti, Lakhwar Dam, Dak pather, and Dehradun, are high, reflecting areas with better ecological conditions, high dissolved oxygen, and low organic pollutants. The carbon offset capacity is strong in these areas with less human activity. The downstream areas, such as Hasanpur, Mohena Palwal, and Shergarh, are highly degraded with low TWA and close to zero TWG, reflecting that these are highly polluted with high organic loading and microbial presence indicating conditions beyond the range associated with higher neutrosophic efficiency scores. The middle and downstream areas are moderately improved. A prominent observation in the TWA and TWG models, which is clearly reflects the pollution effect, particularly in highly stressed areas. The spatial variations of the efficiency values of the Neutrosophic models with pollution variations in the Yamuna River indicate that demonstrates consistent spatial diagnostic capability for the determination of ecologically stressed areas that can assist in regulating the management of the river.

Mid value water quality of the Yamuna River

The eight parameters selected in this study—temperature, dissolved oxygen (DO), pH, electrical conductivity (EC), biochemical oxygen demand (BOD), nitrate, fecal coliform, and total coliform—were chosen to represent the major physical, chemical, and biological processes governing river ecosystem health and carbon assimilation capacity. Temperature and pH regulate biochemical reaction rates and microbial metabolism; DO and BOD jointly characterize oxygen balance and organic pollution stress; EC reflects dissolved ionic and anthropogenic load; nitrate indicates nutrient enrichment and eutrophication risk; and coliform indicators capture microbial contamination and sewage influence. Together, these parameters provide a balanced representation of hydrochemical condition, ecological stress, and public health relevance, and they are consistently monitored across all CPCB stations, ensuring spatial comparability. Certain additional indicators such as chemical oxygen demand (COD), heavy metals, and toxic organics are also important for comprehensive pollution assessment; however, these variables were not uniformly available for all 25 monitoring locations and periods in the CPCB 2023 dataset. Including them would have reduced spatial consistency and comparability of the neutrosophic aggregation. Therefore, the study prioritizes parameters with complete and standardized records, while the framework itself remains extensible and can incorporate heavy metals, COD, or other pollutants in future applications when consistent datasets become available.

Table 1 shows a clear upstream-to-downstream decline in the Yamuna River’s water quality, where headwater stations such as Yamunotri, Hanumanchatti, and Dehradun display high dissolved oxygen, very low BOD, and negligible coliform levels, reflecting near-natural ecological conditions, consistent with observations of better water quality in upper stretches of the basin1,2. In contrast, downstream urban and industrial locations like Hasanpur and Mohena Palwal exhibit low DO, very high BOD, elevated conductivity, and extreme microbial contamination, indicating severe organic and sewage pollution widely reported for middle and lower Yamuna segments3–5. The strong rise in conductivity and coliform counts in middle and lower reaches highlights the impact of anthropogenic discharges, whereas the relative stability of pH across sites shows that single-parameter indicators can mask serious degradation1,4. The inverse DO–BOD relationship confirms oxygen depletion as a key ecological stress mechanism, and occasional midstream improvements suggest limited self-purification through dilution and reaeration, phenomena noted in previous Yamuna assessments2,5,33,34,36,37. The large variability in microbial parameters compared to physicochemical ones demonstrates the heterogeneous nature of river data, supporting the need for advanced uncertainty-aware modeling and linking degraded water quality to reduced ecological functioning and carbon assimilation potential1–5.

Neutrosophic efficiency scores (E-SvNVTWA) and (E-SvNVTWG)

Internal consistency assessment was performed using comparative behavior of TWA and TWG operators by means of E-SvNVTWA and E-SvNVTWG formulations. Trend analyses of the two operators show similar spatial degradation along the Yamuna River but the TWG operator is much more sensitive to extreme conditions of pollution and therefore reaches near zero values at most contaminated sites. This does not manifest a numerical instability but a severe multi-parameter ecological stress conditions and therefore confirms the capability of the model to make a distinction between moderate degradation and highly degraded hydro chemical state. Neutrosophic Efficiency Indexes make the industrial effect more quantifiable. The values of the E-SvNVTWA reduce dramatically from 0.91 to 0.93 in the upstream areas to 0.23–0.63 in the downstream industrial areas, while the values of the E-SvNVTWG tend towards zero in the areas with severe organic and microbial loads. The nonlinear reduction in the former indexes presents quantitative evidence of severe hydro chemical degradation caused not only by water quality but also by industrial pollution.

The TWA and TWG operators represent two complementary perspectives of river condition. The TWA operator behaves as an averaging measure, integrating all parameter contributions and therefore reflecting the overall ecological state; even when some parameters are severely degraded, moderate values of others can prevent the index from collapsing. In contrast, the TWG operator is multiplicative in structure and highly sensitive to extreme pollution inputs. When one or more critical parameters (such as BOD or coliform counts) reach severe levels, the geometric aggregation suppresses the overall score, causing TWG values to approach zero. This does not indicate numerical instability but represents severe multi-parameter ecological stress conditions, where excessive organic load and oxygen depletion disrupt normal metabolic and biogeochemical processes. Hence, TWA identifies general degradation trends, while TWG specifically signals locations where pollution intensity exceeds the river’s ecological tolerance threshold.

Table 3 reveals a strong spatial pattern in neutrosophic efficiency along the Yamuna River, where upstream stations such as Dehradun, Hanumanchatti, and Lakhwar Dam rank highest under both TWA and TWG, indicating stable ecological conditions and balanced water quality, consistent with reports of comparatively better upstream conditions in the Yamuna basin1,2. In contrast, downstream industrial–urban sites like Hasanpur, Mohena Palwal, and Shergarh show sharp declines, especially in TWG values approaching zero, signaling ecological functional stress rather than just moderate pollution, in agreement with documented severe organic and sewage pollution in lower stretches3–5. The TWA scores change more gradually, reflecting overall system condition33,36,37, whereas the TWG operator reacts more sharply to extreme organic and microbial loads, aligning with findings that downstream segments experience episodic and high-intensity pollution events1,4. Midstream stations display mixed rankings, suggesting transitional zones where partial recovery or dilution occurs, a phenomenon previously observed in certain Yamuna reaches due to hydrodynamic and tributary influences2,5. The contrast between the two operators highlights how some locations that appear moderately impacted under averaging still exhibit severe stress under geometric aggregation, demonstrating the importance of using both measures to distinguish general degradation from near-collapse conditions and to better prioritize management interventions, consistent with earlier basin-scale water quality assessments1–5.

Carbon offset estimation

The carbon offset scaling function in this study is not intended as a purely mathematical normalization, but as a simplified ecological representation of the river’s capacity to assimilate and process carbon through natural biogeochemical mechanisms. In flowing water systems, carbon offset potential is closely linked to dissolved oxygen availability, microbial respiration, organic matter decomposition, and primary productivity, all of which are influenced by water quality conditions. High neutrosophic efficiency values correspond to environments with stable oxygen balance, lower organic loading, and healthier microbial–aquatic interactions, where aerobic decomposition and carbon cycling processes function effectively. Conversely, low efficiency values indicate oxygen depletion, excessive organic load, and ecological stress, where carbon processing shifts toward anaerobic pathways, suggesting reduced ecological processing efficiency under high organic load and potentially increasing greenhouse gas emissions such as methane. The linear scaling function therefore acts as a first-order ecological proxy that translates overall water quality integrity into relative carbon processing capacity along the river continuum. While real carbon dynamics are nonlinear and affected by hydrology, sediment interaction, and temperature, the adopted formulation provides a physically interpretable baseline linking pollution stress to ecosystem service decline. The model structure remains extendable to nonlinear or threshold-based functions as empirical carbon flux data become available.

Table 4 shows that carbon offset capacity varies markedly along the Yamuna River and closely follows observed water quality conditions, consistent with documented pollution gradients along the basin1–5. Upstream stations such as Dehradun, Hanumanchatti, and Lakhwar Dam exhibit lower CO values under both TWA and TWG, indicating healthier ecological functioning, stronger oxygen balance, and more stable carbon processing, which aligns with reports of comparatively better water quality in upper stretches1,2. In contrast, downstream urban–industrial locations like Hasanpur, Mohena Palwal, and Shergarh show very high CO-TWG values approaching saturation, reflecting ecological stress where the river’s natural carbon assimilation capacity is exceeded due to heavy organic loading and microbial contamination, conditions widely reported for middle and lower Yamuna segments affected by sewage and industrial discharge3–5. The difference between CO-TWA and CO-TWG at several sites highlights that while average conditions may suggest moderate capacity, extreme pollution effects substantially reduce functional carbon processing33,36, supporting findings that severe organic pollution alters river metabolic balance and ecological performance1,4. Overall, the table demonstrates a clear link between deteriorating water quality and declining ecosystem service performance along the river continuum1–5.

The proposed neutrosophic efficiency (TWA, TWG) and carbon offset indices can serve as practical decision-support tools for river authorities by classifying river stretches into stable, stressed, and critical zones33–38. TWA reflects overall ecological condition for routine monitoring, while TWG acts as an early-warning indicator for severe pollution hotspots requiring urgent intervention. Limitations of the study

Despite the methodological advancements, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analysis relies on CPCB monitoring data for a single reference year (2023), and some potentially relevant parameters such as COD, heavy metals, and toxic organics were excluded due to incomplete spatial coverage, which may underrepresent certain pollution dimensions. Second, uncertainty is modeled through ellipsoidal neutrosophic representation at the parameter level; however, uncertainty propagation through aggregation and scaling is conceptual rather than empirically validated with measurement-error statistics. Third, the carbon offset estimation uses a linear scaling function as a first-order ecological proxy linking water quality to carbon assimilation capacity, while actual carbon flux dynamics in rivers are nonlinear and influenced by hydrology, sediment interactions, and temperature. Fourth, seasonal variability is simplified through mid-value representation, which may smooth short-term pollution peaks. Finally, the model identifies ecological stress patterns but does not explicitly simulate pollutant transport or biogeochemical process kinetics. These limitations do not affect the comparative and diagnostic value of the framework but indicate directions for future refinement using multi-season datasets, expanded parameter sets, and empirical carbon metabolism measurements. Further research should include comparative performance evaluation against established water quality assessment models, sensitivity and structural stability analysis of the ranking framework, probabilistic uncertainty propagation, and extension of the carbon offset formulation beyond a relative efficiency proxy. Additionally, incorporation of temporal dynamics and explicit modeling of inter-seasonal variability would enhance the robustness and generalizability of the proposed framework.

Conclusion

This study delivers a decision-support framework that translates uncertain, multi-parameter river monitoring data into operational insights for river basin management and climate-linked ecosystem planning. By introducing an ellipsoidal single-valued neutrosophic structure with dual trigonometric aggregation (TWA–TWG), the work provides a practical tool for distinguishing moderate water quality degradation from zones of severe multi-parameter ecological stress conditions, particularly under high organic and microbial pollution. The integration of neutrosophic efficiency indices with a carbon offset estimation model establishes a structured analytical linkage between water quality indices and ecosystem service proxies, enabling managers to interpret pollution not only as a chemical problem but also as a loss of natural carbon assimilation capacity.

From an implementation perspective, the framework supports (i) hotspot prioritization, by clearly identifying critical downstream sectors where TWG values approach critical degradation conditions; (ii) targeted pollution control, by revealing parameter groups (organic load, microbial contamination) that most strongly drive efficiency decline; (iii) restoration planning, through spatial comparison of recovery potential between upstream reference zones and stressed urban–industrial reaches; and (iv) climate-aligned water governance, by embedding carbon offset capacity as an ecosystem performance indicator in routine water quality assessment. Because the model is parameter-driven rather than river-specific, it can be transferred to other basins using standard monitoring datasets, offering a scalable methodology for uncertainty-aware environmental diagnostics.

Collectively, the study moves beyond descriptive assessment and provides a structured analytical basis for evidence-based intervention, sustainable river restoration, and integration of water quality management with carbon budgeting strategies.
