PRMT5-Cacna1d axis maintains calcium homeostasis to regulate postnatal motor development in mice
Authors: Jianbo Cao, Zixuan He, Weitai Chai, Meng Tian, Tianyan Wei, Xiu Liu, Yue Wang, Shuang Liu, Ruitong Liu, Yue Yin, Yajun Wang, Tong Liu, Zheng Liang, Songyan Liu, Shilai Bao, Huali Yu, Xiaoxiao He, Xiaojuan Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpigenetic regulation of neuronal calcium signaling during postnatal cortical development is critical for voluntary movement. Protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) acts as an epigenetic regulator that is involved in movement disorders, yet its underlying mechanism remains poorly understood. Here, we report that conditional knockout of Prmt5 in excitatory neurons (cKONex) during motor cortex development leads to severe hyperactivity and shortened lifespan in mice. Transcriptomics analysis reveals that PRMT5 deficiency upregulated Cacna1d expression without affecting its alternative splicing. We demonstrate that PRMT5 directly binds to the Cacna1d promoter, and represses its expression via catalyzing H4R3me2s histone methylation. Electrophysiological studies showed deletion of Prmt5 increased calcium influx and neuronal excitability, resulting from elevated L-type voltage-gated calcium channel (Cav1.3) activity. The enhanced calcium level further leads to oxidative phosphorylation defects in neurons. Importantly, administration of the L-type channel blocker, nimodipine, rescues hyperactivity and neuronal hyper-excitability in vivo, as well as markedly extends the lifespan of cKONex mice. Our findings establish a novel epigenetic mechanism wherein PRMT5-Cacna1d axis regulates neuronal excitability and motor development. Introduction

Voluntary movement is a goal-directed action initiated by intention and conscious choice [1], which is regulated by a complex neural network composed of motor cortex, basal ganglia, cerebellum, and spinal cord [2]. Pyramidal neurons in the primary motor cortex (M1) integrate inputs from diverse brain regions and project long axons to the spinal cord [3]. Voluntary movement control, ranging from simple locomotion to skilled execution, requires precisely coordinated neuronal activity in the motor cortex [4–6]. The initiation and refinement of these movements rely on dynamic intracellular signaling events in the brain.

Among these signals, calcium influx serves as a pivotal second messenger in coupling neuronal excitation to transcriptional regulation [7, 8]. Calcium (Ca²⁺) signal generated through several mechanisms are involved in movement processes. Firstly, glutamate-gated calcium channels open upon glutamate binding during neurotransmission, which triggers action potentials [9–11]. Secondly, voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCCs) open in response to membrane depolarization, which amplify the ion currents and propagate action potentials [12]. In contrast, intracellular Ca²⁺ release from the endoplasmic reticulum, which is normally activated by G protein-coupled receptors, generates widespread or oscillatory Ca²⁺ signals to regulate gene expression [13]. Dysregulation of calcium signaling or its downstream effectors would disrupt calcium homeostasis and impair neuronal activity.

Protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) is a well-characterized methylation enzyme that catalyzes symmetric dimethylation of arginine (R) residues on histones and non-histone proteins [14–17]. In the developing brain, PRMT5 is highly expressed in neural stem cells (NSCs) and is essential for NSCs proliferation and differentiation by methylating histones H4R3me2s and H3R2me2s [18, 19]. PRMT5 also plays a role in methylating non-histone proteins, such as spliceosomal Sm proteins, which are critical for accurate spliceosome assembly [16]. Cumulative evidence has confirmed the critical roles of PRMT5 in the proliferation and differentiation of astrocyte and oligodendrocytes [20–22]. Dysregulation of PRMT5 is also implicated in the pathogenesis of neurological and neurodegenerative diseases. PRMT5 is known to mediate the dimethylation of R1810 of RNA polymerase II, which is required for its interaction with survival of motor neuron (SMN) protein [23]. In addition, PRMT5 mediated dimethylation is critical for the clearance of stress granules, which is implicated in pathogenesis of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [24, 25]. Moreover, enhancing PRMT5 expression has been shown to be beneficial for multiple diseases, including Huntington’s disease, ALS and frontotemporal dementia [24, 26]. All these diseases exhibit converged phenotypic changes in motor function. However, a knowledge gap remains regarding the role of PRMT5 in regulating motor behavior.

Previous work from our lab revealed a critical role of PRMT5 in maintaining self-renewal of NSCs. Deletion of PRMT5 led to microcephaly and hyperlocomotion in mice [19]. While the function of PRMT5 in neurogenesis is resolved, its role in immature neurons remains unexplored. In the current study, we employed Nex-Cre mice to specifically knockout Prmt5 in post-mitotic excitatory neurons of the dorsal forebrain. It enables us to dissect the function of PRMT5 in established cortical circuits without confounding from microcephaly. The constructed mice showed serial motor behavior defects and sharply shortened lifespan. Molecular study showed PRMT5 repress Cacna1d expression via catalyzing H4R3me2s during postnatal development. PRMT5 deficiency disrupted calcium homeostasis, which in turn drives a cascade of calcium-dependent hyper-excitability and oxidative stress in neurons that underlies the observed neurological phenotypes.

Materials and methods

Animals

Prmt5flox/flox mice strain was obtained from the European Conditional Mouse Mutagenesis Program (EUCOMM; Prmt5tm2a(EUCOMM)Wtsi) [27]. The inducible conditional Prmt5 knockout mice were generated by crossing Nex-Cre [28], Rosa26-stop‐tdTomato reporter mice (Ai14) [29] and CamkⅡα-Cre (No.005359, Jackson Laboratory). They were subsequently bred and maintained in a C57BL/6 genetic background. The mice were raised in strict compliance with the regulations of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Northeast Normal University, China. All animal experimental protocols were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethical Review Board of Northeast Normal University (20211220). A 12-hour light/dark cycle was adopted to maintain their circadian rhythm for breeding purposes at a constant temperature. The day of birth is designated as postnatal day (P) 0.

Western blotting

The Western blotting procedure was conducted in accordance with the protocol detailed in our prior study [30]. Briefly, the fresh mouse tissues or cells were immersed in a RIPA Lysis Buffer, which contained a cocktail of protease inhibitors. Loading buffer was added to the sample, which was then boiled for 5 min. Subsequently, SDS-polyacrylamide-gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) was performed. The primary antibodies used in this study were rabbit anti-PRMT5 (Abcam, ab109451), rabbit anti-Histone H3 (ABclonal, A2348), rabbit anti-H3R2me2s (ABclonal, A2373), rabbit anti-H4R3me2s (Abcam, ab5823), rabbit anti-PRMT7 (CST, 14762), rabbit anti-CACNA1D (Affinity, DF10012), mouse anti-GAPDH (ABclonal, AC002), mouse anti-FLAG (Sigma, F7425), mouse anti-beta-III Tubulin (TUBB3) (Abcam, ab78078), mouse anti-GFAP (Sigma, G3893), mouse anti-Aldolase C (Santa Cruz, SC-271593).

Immunostaining

Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium solution. Subsequently, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and 4% paraformaldehyde were sequentially perfused through the circulatory system. Samples were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight and then dehydrated in 30% sucrose solution.

Sections were washed three times with PBS, heated in citrate buffer at 98 °C for 5 min, washed again with PBS, and then incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight (antibody diluent: 0.2% Triton X-100 and 2% BSA in PBS). In the following day, three cycles of PBST (PBS plus 0.1% Tween-20) washing were conducted, and the sections were then incubated with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h under dark conditions. Finally, the slides were mounted using polyvinyl alcohol (PVA). The primary antibodies were rabbit anti-PRMT5 (Abcam, ab109451), rabbit anti-CDP (Santa Cruz, sc-101003), rat anti-Ctip2 (Abcam, ab18465). Cell nucleus staining was performed using DAPI (GENVIEW, GD3408).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from fresh mouse cortex or cultured neurons using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). cDNA was synthesized from RNA using TransScript® All-in-One First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Transgene). Real-time PCR analysis was performed using PerfectStart® Green qPCR SuperMix (Transgene) on a Thermo Scientific PikoReal 96 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher). The relative expression levels of mRNA were normalized to the endogenous gapdh and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primers used for Real-Time PCR are listed in Table S2.

Behavioral assays

In behavioral experiments, mice were housed in same-sex but mixed-genotype groups and served as littermate controls. All experiments were conducted and analyzed in a double-blind manner, with at least 1 h of habituation to the behavioral testing room provided beforehand.

The open field apparatus was a square box with dimensions of 40 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm, without a lid, and the floor was divided into a central zone (20 × 20 cm) and a peripheral zone. The total distance traveled, average speed, and time spent in different zones during 20 min of free exploration were tracked and recorded using the EthoVision XT system (Noldus, Wageningen, the Netherlands). The total distance traveled and the average speed can be used to evaluate the spontaneous locomotor ability of mice, while the time spent in the central zone can reflect their anxiety levels.

Rotarod was performed as previously reported [31], the rotation speed increased from 5 rpm/min to 40 rpm/min within 5 min at a constant accelerating rate. The time that the mouse stayed on the rod and the maximum rotational speed at which it fell off were recorded. The animals underwent three trials, with a 20-minute interval between each trial. The average value of the three trials was calculated as the result.

Hindlimb clasping was performed by gently lifting the mouse from the base of its tail. The mouse was secured at this position for 30 s. If the hind limbs are spread out away from the abdomen, a score of 0 is given; If one hind limb is retracted towards the abdomen for at least 50% of the observation period, it is scored as 1; If both hind limbs are partially retracted towards the abdomen for at least 50% of the observation period, it is scored as 2; If both hind limbs are fully retracted towards the abdomen for at least 50% of the observation period, it is scored as 3 [32]. Each animal underwent three trials, with a 1-hour interval between each trial. The average value of the three trials was calculated.

Ledge test was performed by placing a mouse on the edge of an empty cage. Mice walked steadily along the edge of the cage and attempted to return to the cage. Wild-type mice typically walked along the edge of the cage without losing balance and climbed back into the cage in a controlled manner. The behavior of each mouse was evaluated according to the following criteria [32]: score 0, The animal walks steadily along the cage wall and lowers itself into the cage in a coordinated, paw-first manner. Score 1, The mouse slips while traversing the wall but otherwise remains coordinated. Score 2, The mouse fails to engage its hind paws effectively or lands on its head instead of its paws when descending. Score 3, The mouse falls from the wall while walking or attempting to descend, drops directly into the cage, or sways and refuses to move despite gentle prodding.

For long-term oral nimodipine treatment (40 µM; MCE, HY-B0265), drug-containing drinking water was provided in light-protected bottles. The age at which mice began drug administration ranged from P21 to P25. Behavioral assessments, including hindlimb clasping and open field tests, were conducted every 5 days starting from the first day of drug administration. Body weight and water consumption were recorded at the same 5-day intervals, yielding an estimated daily nimodipine intake of approximately 3.3 mg·kg⁻¹·day⁻¹.

Transcriptomics sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the primary motor and somatosensory cortices of P21 and P80 WT and cKONex mice using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). RNA samples were then subjected to quality control followed by strand-specific mRNA-seq library construction. Sequencing was performed at Beijing Genomic Institute (BGI). The RNA-sequencing data were analysed on an online system (Dr. Tom, http://biosys.bgi.com) provided by BGI. Sequence alignment was performed against the GRCm38 genome assembly with HISAT2 (v2.1.0). The raw transcriptomics sequencing data generated in this study are in the process of being deposited into the NCBI BioProject database. The accession number will be made available prior to the final publication of the article. During the peer review process, the datasets are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Primary culture of cortical neurons

The cortices of P0 mice were carefully dissected in chilled Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS) and subsequently digested with 0.25% papain (Worthington) at 37℃ for 20 minutes. The neurons were plated onto an 18 mm glass-bottom culture dishe that had been coated with poly-L-lysine (PLL) (Sigma) and incubated with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’ s Medium mixture F12 (Gibco) containing 10% fetal bovine serum. The adherent neurons were subsequently maintained in neurobasal-A medium (Gibco) supplemented with 2% B-27 (Gibco) and 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco).

ChIP-qPCR

For mouse brain samples, fresh motor cortex tissue (150–200 mg) was minced into pieces of approximately 1–2 mm³. For HEK293T cells (purchased from Procell), 3 × 10⁷ cells per assay were prepared. Subsequently, the samples were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde and quenched with 0.125 M glycine. It should be noted that the tissue suspension was homogenized into a single-cell suspension using a homogenizer before nuclear processing. Cells were resuspended in nuclear extraction buffer (Tris-HCl (10 mM, pH 7.4), MgCl₂ (1.5 mM), CaCl₂ (1 mM), sucrose (0.25 M)) to isolate nuclei. The samples were sonicated in buffer containing 1% SDS, EDTA (10 mM), and Tris-HCl (50 mM, pH 8.0) to yield chromatin fragments. Then lysate was immunoprecipitated with anti-IgG (negative control), anti-PRMT5 (Abcam, ab109451), anti-H4R3me2s (Active Motif, 61187) antibodies immobilized on ChIP-Grade Protein G Magnetic Beads (CST, 9006). DNA fragments were digested with Proteinase K (Abclonal, RK20561) and subsequently purified using the AFTSpin Multifunction DNA Purification Kit (Abclonal, RK30100) prior to real-time PCR analysis. The ChIP primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4.

Electrophysiology recording

Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were acquired using an EPC-10/2 amplifier (HEKA, Germany). Microelectrodes were fabricated from borosilicate glass tubes (Sutter Instruments) and had resistances of 3–6 MΩ. Whole-cell patches with series resistances < 15 MΩ were used for recordings. For action potentials (AP) recordings, following intraperitoneal administration of 1% sodium pentobarbital (40 mg kg⁻¹, i.p.) for anesthesia, mice (P45) were rapidly decapitated and the brains were quickly removed and placed in ice-cold cutting solution containing: KCl (2.5 mM), NaH2PO4 (1.25 mM), NaHCO3 (26 mM), D-Glucose (11 mM), MgSO4·7H2O (10 mM), sucrose (234 mM). Coronal acute brain slices (300 μm) were prepared using a vibrating microtome (Leica, VT 1000 S). Slices were transferred to oxygenated artificial cerebral spinal fluid (aCSF) and incubated at 34 °C for 20 min, then moved to room temperature for an additional 1 h while being continuously perfused with a gentle stream of 95% O₂ / 5% CO₂. The aCSF contained NaCl (124 mM), KCl (2.5 mM), NaH2PO4 (1.2 mM), NaHCO3 (24 mM), D-Glucose (12.5 mM), HEPES (5 mM), MgSO4·7H2O (2 mM), CaCl2·2H2O (2 mM), pH 7.4. Action potentials (APs) were evoked by a series of incrementally increasing depolarizing current steps (0, 50, 100, 150, 200 pA).

Calcium channel current recordings were performed as described previously [33]. Cultured motor cortex neurons (DIV11) were measured for calcium channel current recordings. The pipette solution contained (in mM): CsMeSO3 (135); CsCl (5); MgCl2 (1); MgATP (4); HEPES (5); and EGTA (5); with 290mOsm adjusted with glucose and pH 7.3 adjusted with CsOH. The bath solution contained (in mM): TEA-Cl (135); HEPES (10); CaCl2 (10); with 300mOsm, adjusted with glucose and pH 7.3 adjusted with TEA-OH. Endogenous N- and P/Q-type Ca2+ currents were blocked by ω-conotoxin GVIA (1 µM, Sigma Aldrich) and ω-conotoxin MVIIC (1 µM, Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min [34–36], to isolate L-type currents. Whole-cell currents were evoked by depolarizing voltage steps, and the current amplitude was measured at − 10 mV.

Stereotaxic surgery

Mice were anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane and positioned in a stereotaxic apparatus (RWD Technology, Shenzhen, China). AAV2/9-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6m (purchased from Braincase) was stereotaxically injected into the M1 at AP: 0.85 mm, ML: ±1.65 mm, DV: −0.85 mm. The virus (200 nL) was infused at a flow rate of ~ 70 nL min− 1. Optical fiber (200 μm outer diameter, 0.37 numerical aperture, 2-mm length) was implanted ~100 μm above the viral injection site. The optical fiber was secured with skull screws and dental cement. Behavioral testing began 3 weeks after surgery, allowing time for viral expression and mice recovery.

Fiber photometry

The implanted optic fiber was connected to the photometry system (ThinkerTech Nanjing Biotech Ltd, Nanjing, China) through a patch cord. Prior to the experiment, the patch cord was bleached at 0.02 mW. The output power at the fiber tip was set to 20 µW at 405 nm and 40 µW at 470 nm for recording. Simultaneously, neuronal Ca²⁺ signals and behavioral videos were recorded. Voffset for 405 nm and 470 nm was measured under complete darkness. Data were analyzed with custom-written MATLAB code provided by ThinkerTech, which is based on the MATLAB R2022a. The signal dynamics were quantified using the following formula: ΔF/F (%) = (F - Fbaseline) / Fbaseline×100 (F represents the signal value at any given moment; Fbaseline represents the mean fluorescence intensity during the baseline period).

Record Ca²⁺ signals during 20 min of spontaneous locomotion in the open field, and then analyze the area under the curve (AUC) after normalizing to ΔF/F. Under tail-suspension conditions, we recorded calcium signal transients in mice as they transitioned from immobility to mobility. We analyzed events in which the immobility/mobility duration exceeded 2 s.

Compound muscle action potential (CMAP)

The protocol was modified from previous reports [37, 38]. Mice were induced into anesthesia via inhalation of isoflurane/oxygen. The hindlimbs were lightly extended and the hind paws were secured. The proximal sciatic nerve in the hindlimb was stimulated using an insulated 0.25 mm electrode needle. The cathode was inserted into the proximal region of the hind limb, and the anode was inserted into the subcutaneous tissue overlying the sacrum, closer to the body midline. The grounding electrode was inserted into the subcutaneous tissue on the contralateral side. The active recording electrode was placed subcutaneously over the gastrocnemius muscle, while the reference electrode was inserted subcutaneously at a 30° angle adjacent to the Achilles tendon on the same side, with the needle tip retained 2–5 mm beneath the skin. The sciatic nerve was stimulated using square-wave pulses with a duration of 0.1 ms and an intensity ranging from 1 to 10 mA, and the evoked CMAP response was recorded using a physiological signal acquisition system (RM6240E). The stimulus intensity gradually increased until the amplitude of the CMAP response no longer increased. On this basis, a 120% intensity of the maximum stimulus was applied to record the amplitude of the maximal CMAP response. The amplitude of the CMAP was measured from the maximal negative peak to the maximal positive peak of the biphasic wave.

High-resolution respirometry

Cortical tissue (10 mg) from P70-P90 mice was homogenized on ice in a tube containing MIR05 respiratory buffer (HEPES (20 mM), sucrose (110 mM), KH₂PO₄ (10 mM), taurine (20 mM), lactobionic acid (60 mM), MgCl₂ (3 mM), EGTA (0.5 mM), pH 7.1, 1 mg mL− 1 fatty acid-free BSA, and catalase (280 U mL− 1)). Mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured using O2k (Oroboros Instruments).

Tissue homogenate (1 mg) was introduced into the Oxygraph-2k-chamber containing MIR05 respiration buffer. Following acquisition of baseline respiration, complex I-linked substrates were titrated sequentially (pyruvate (5 mM), glutamate (10 mM), malate (2 mM)). After oxygen flux reached steady state, the proton-leak respiration supported by complex I was recorded; ADP (2.5 mM) was titrated, the ensuing steady-state oxygen flux was assigned as complex I-mediated maximal oxidative phosphorylation; cytochrome c (10 µM) was added to confirm outer-mitochondrial membrane integrity; succinate (10 mM) was titrated, and the ensuing steady-state oxygen flux was taken as the maximal oxidative phosphorylation capacity of Complexes I and II; FCCP (1.5 µM) was titrated to induce maximal electron transport system (ETS) capacity; following inhibition of Complex I with rotenone (0.5 µM), the maximal electron transport capacity of complex II was determined; complex III was inhibited with antimycin A (2.5 µM) to quantify residual non-mitochondrial oxygen consumption (ROX); Ascorbate (2 mM) plus TMPD (0.5 mM) were supplied to assay Complex IV respiration. When the O₂ concentration dropped below 50 µM, the chamber was opened and the measurement was terminated. Mitochondrial oxygen-consumption data were analyzed using the native software suite of the O2k instrument.

Measurement of mitochondrial Ca2+ concentration

Rhod-2-AM (HY-D0989, MCE) was first incubated with particular amounts of NaBH₄ for 10 min at 4 °C to generate dihydrorhod-2-AM. Then, the samples were incubated with 5 µM dihydrorhod-2-AM and 0.02% (w/v) Pluronic F-127 (ST501, Beyotime) for 30 min at 37 °C. To confirm the mitochondrial localization of the Rhod-2 probe, cells were labeled with MitoTracker Green (C1048, Beyotime) for 20 min. MitoTracker Green and dihydrorhod-2-AM were excited using confocal laser lines at 488 nm and 546 nm, respectively.

Dihydroethidium (DHE) detection

P75 mice received two intraperitoneal DHE injections (27 mg kg⁻¹ in 200 µL each) with a 30 min interval. 18 h after the final DHE injection, mice were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde. Brains were post-fixed overnight, cryosectioned, and visualized by confocal microscopy using 546 nm excitation [39, 40].

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.6 and are presented as the mean ± SEM. Fluorescence intensity was measured and analyzed using ImageJ. The statistical tests were used for the data analyses: two-tailed unpaired t-test. For data comparisons of more than two groups, one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA was used. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001;****p < 0.0001.

Results

PRMT5 deficiency in excitatory neurons leads to hyperactivity and reduced lifespan

We previously found deletion of Prmt5 in neural stem cells resulted in microcephaly and hyperactive behavior in mice [19], which indicated the function of PRMT5 is critical for regulation of motor development. To test this notion, we firstly investigated the scRNA-seq data [41] to examine the expression pattern of Prmt5 in E14.5 dorsal telencephalon cells. These data showed Prmt5 highly expressed in radial glial cells, as well as immature and mature neurons (Figure S1A). To specifically investigate the role of PRMT5 in postmitotic excitatory neurons, we generated conditional knockout mice (cKONex) by crossing Prmt5flox/flox mice with Nex-Cre (Neurod6-Cre) mice (Fig. 1A), which displayed Cre recombinase activity in the cortex and hippocampus, but not in the striatum, thalamus and cerebellum (Figure S1B-C). Western blot and qRT-PCR analyses both confirmed that the knockout efficiency was approximately 70% in the cortex of cKONex mice (Fig. 1B-D). To verify the knockout specificity, we stained PRMT5 with glutamatergic neuron markers CDP (layer II-IV) or CTIP2 (layer V-VI) in cortical sections (Figure S1D). PRMT5 expression was almost completely absent in both CDP and CTIP2 positive cells (Figure S1E-F). cKONex mice were viable at birth, and their brain anatomy were indistinguishable from littermates (Figure S1G). However, cKONex mice exhibited a sharply shortened lifespan, with a median survival time of 75 days (for both male and female) (Fig. 1E-F).

To examine the role of PRMT5 in motor behavior regulation, we performed the open field test (OFT) for male cKONex mice from postnatal day (P) 21 to 60. The cKONex mice showed hyperactivity in the OFT in an age-dependent manner, with the most pronounced difference at P60 (Fig. 1G-H). We then examined the locomotion of male and female heterozygous mice (Prmt5flox/+, Nex-Cre), as well as female cKONex mice at P60. Female cKONex mice were also hyperactive, but both male and felale heterozygous mice did not show a sign of hyperactivity (Fig. 1I-J). Moreover, both male and female cKONex mice showed constant high travel speeds at the latter stage (10–20 min) in the OFT, which is a typical phenotype of hyperactivity (Fig. 1K-L). Though cKONex mice more frequently acrossed the center area in the OFT (Fig. 1M), they did not spend more time in the center zone (Figure S2A-B).

Besides hyperactivity, cKONex mice also showed defects in hindlimb clasping reflex from P21 (Fig. 1N-P). Hindlimb clasping is an abnormal reflex characterized by the involuntary adduction and retraction of the hind limbs. The cKONex mice also showed motor learning deficits in rotarod and ledge tests (Figure S2C-I). More importantly, we found that cKONex mice gradually had difficulty in movement after P60 and then even paralysis before their death (Fig. 1Q). However, the compound muscle action potential was not impaired in cKONex mice at this time point (Figure S2J-K).

In cKONex mice, PRMT5 deletion started from E14 in the embryonic mouse brain, which occurred much earlier than the onset of motor development. It is possible that the observed hyperactivity and hindlimb clasping reflex deficits are due to defects during embryonic development. Thus, we also crossed Prmt5flox/flox mice with CamkIIα-Cre mice (cKOCamkIIα mice, Figure S3A) to test whether the phenotypes observed in cKONex mice still persisted. Results showed that cKOCamkIIα mice exhibited hyperactivity but not hindlimb clasping reflex defects (Figure S3B-D).

Altogether, these findings indicate that PRMT5 deficiency in excitatory neurons led to severe movement dysfunction and impaired lifespan in mice.

PRMT5 negatively regulates Cacna1d expression

To explore the molecular mechanism of movement dysfunction in cKONex mice, transcriptomic analysis was performed on the primary motor (M1) cortex samples from P21 cKONex and WT mice (Fig. 2A). We identified 617 differentially expressed genes, with 249 up-regulated and 368 down-regulated, using a threshold of |log2(cKONex/WT)| > 0.5 and Q value < 0.05 (Fig. 2B). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of calcium signaling pathway in the differentially expressed genes (Fig. 2C). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis indicated that PRMT5 is involved in biological processes, such as regulation of ion transmembrane transport and locomotory behavior (Fig. 2D). Molecular functions encompassed voltage-gated ion channel activity, ion channel activity, calcium ion binding, and calcium channel activity (Fig. 2E). These results suggested that PRMT5 regulated the expression of calcium channels to influence calcium signaling linked to movement control. We then used Venn diagram to screen the common genes from sellected differentially expressed gene groups, and identified five common genes that are Cacna1h, Cacna1d, Cacna1g, Cacna1i, and Trpm4. The Cacna gene family encodes α1 subunits of voltage-gated calcium channels (CaVs), while Trpm4 encodes a calcium-activated non-selective cation channel that mediates monovalent cation transport (Fig. 2F-G). Quantitative PCR analysis revealed results consistent with the transcriptomic findings: in the cortex of cKONex mice, Cacna1d and Cacna1i expression levels were significantly elevated, whereas Cacna1h expression was significantly reduced (Fig. 2H). In addition, we performed RNA alternative splicing analysis using the same transcriptomics data. Although PRMT5 deletion significantly altered global RNA-splicing events (Figure S4A), no splicing variant of Cacna1d mRNA was identified.

To assess whether PRMT5 regulated Cacna gene family expression in a cell-autonomous manner, neurons from Prmt5flox/flox mice were cultured and infected with Cre-expressing lentivirus (LV) at DIV11 (Fig. 2I and Figure S4B). Three days post-infection, neurons with LV-Cre showed approximately 90% reduction of Prmt5 mRNA versus LV-mCherry (Figure S4B). Meanwhile, the expression of Cacna1d significantly increased, but the expression of other Cacna genes remained unchanged (Fig. 2J), suggesting a negative correlation between PRMT5 and Cacna1d expression. Furthermore, inhibiting the activity of PRMT5 methyltransferase with the specific inhibitor EPZ-015666 (10 µM) also elevated Cacna1d expression (Fig. 2K). Furthermore, the expression of Cacna1c, another neuronal L-type calcium channel subtype, showed no detectable change upon either PRMT5 loss or its functional inhibition (Fig. 2H-K). Altogether, these findings suggest that PRMT5 negatively regulates the transcription of Cacna1d expression under physiological conditions.

PRMT5 represses Cacna1d expression via H4R3me2s modification

Our transcriptomics analysis identified Cacna1d as a major target for PRMT5-mediated transcription regulation, which is important for postnatal motor development. We examined the expression of Cacna1d at different postnatal stages. The qPCR analysis showed Cacna1d expression was highly dynamic during postnatal development, which displayed a peak at P25 and followed by sharply reduction at P30 (Fig. 3A). Thus, these data strongly indicated an epigenetic mechanism is involved in Cacna1d expression regulation. As a protein arginine methyltransferase, PRMT5 has been shown to regulate gene transcription through histone methylation. Among classical PRMT5 modification sites, H4R3me2s typically suppresses transcription, whereas H3R2me2s generally activates transcription [14, 42]. We found the protein level of CACNA1D was indeed elevated in the cortex of cKONex mice, and H4R3me2s was significantly reduced (Fig, 3B). In contrast, H3R2me2s was only moderately decreased in cKONex cortex (Fig. 3B). To confirm the alterations of H4R3me2s and CACNA1D expression resulted from PRMT5 deficiency, we overexpressed PRMT5 in WT and cKONex neurons (Figure S5). As expected, the expression of PRMT5 significantly elevated the level of H4R3me2s but reduced the expression of CACNA1D in cKONex neurons (Fig. 3C).

We then investigated whether PRMT5 regulated Cacna1d expression in mouse brain via H4R3me2s modification. The putative promoter sequence of Cacna1d (predicted by promoter 2.0) was identified from − 3500 bp to + 80 bp, with the transcription start site (TSS) defined as position + 1 (Fig. 3D). We then performed ChIP-qPCR to verify if PRMT5 and H4R3me2s bind to Cacna1d promoter in mouse genome. Primers were designed at ~ 500 bp intervals, covering the whole predicted promoter sequence (Fig. 3D). Results showed PRMT5 and H4R3me2s both displayed high binding affinity at the region from -1987 bp to -1532 bp (R5) upstream of the Cacna1d TSS (Fig. 3E), suggesting that the R5 region is responsible for regulating Cacna1d expression. Further, dual-luciferase reporter assays using constructs that containing Cacna1d TSS upstream sequences were employed to test promoter activity. Interestingly, the − 2000 bp to TSS fragment (pGL-2000) exhibited high promoter activity (Fig. 3F), supporting that PRMT5 binding sites may closely correlate with transcriptional regulation of Cacna1d. To highlight the association of PRMT5 with CACNA1D expression in human, we performed ChIP-qPCR using cultured HEK293 cells in vitro. Consistent with the results obtained in the mouse genome, the PRMT5 and H4R3me2s both displayed high binding affinity at the region from -740 bp to -221 bp in human genome (R3) (Figure S6A-B). The R3 region itself exhibited promoter activity though the longer − 2000 bp fragment containing R3 showed stronger activity (Figure S6C). Importantly, one highly similar sequence (73.5% identity) was identified in the genome of mouse R5 and human R3 regions, respectively (Figure S6D). Altogether, these data demonstrate PRMT5 represses Cacna1d expression via H4R3me2s at the promoter region.

PRMT5 deficiency increases neuronal excitability

Cacna1d encodes the pore-forming α1D subunit of L-type voltage-gated calcium channels (Cav1.3), which mediates calcium ion influx into cells. Clinically, gain-of-function mutations in CACNA1D have been associated with neurodevelopmental disorders, tremor, and seizures (Table S1). Given our findings that PRMT5 deficiency selectively upregulated Cacna1d expression, we next investigated whether this molecular change would translate into altered calcium currents. Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed on cortical neurons from cKONex and WT mice to measure L-type calcium currents. We observed that cKONex neurons exhibited increased calcium currents compared to control neurons (Fig. 4A-B). Importantly, re-expression of PRMT5 reversed this elevated current (Fig. 4C-D). While our recordings captured total L-type current, which may include contributions from other channels such as Cav1.2, the selective upregulation of Cacna1d (Cav1.3) and the unchanged expression of Cacna1c (Cav1.2) strongly suggested that the enhanced current was primarily mediated by Cav1.3. These results highlighted a critical role of PRMT5 in regulating the functional expression of Cav1.3 channels.

Since Cav1.3-mediated calcium currents play an important role in modulating neuronal excitability, we further investigated whether PRMT5 regulates neuronal excitability using whole-cell patch-clamp recordings. Our data showed that PRMT5 deficiency enhanced the excitability of neurons from acute cortical M1 slices (Fig. 4E-H), while the resting membrane potential remained unchanged (Fig. 4I). In the following experiments, we examined the effects of Prmt5 deletion on neuronal activity in vivo. AAV-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6m was injected into the M1 of cKONex and WT mice unilaterally (Fig. 4J). Using live calcium imaging assays, we observed increased spontaneous activity of M1 neurons in cKONex mice in comparison with WT mice (Fig. 4K-L). We then applied a tail suspension test to specifically compare the M1 neuronal activity during the immobility-to-mobility transition. We found that the neuronal activity of M1 cortex of cKONex mice was significantly increased compared to controls (Fig. 4M-Q). Taken together, these findings showed that PRMT5 modulates Cav1.3 channel activity, which in turn regulates M1 neuronal excitability.

PRMT5 deficiency leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in cortical neurons

Persistent neuronal excitation and calcium overload can disrupt cellular energy metabolism and promote oxidative damage, which may underlie the shortened lifespan of cKONex mice. We isolated motor cortex from P80 cKONex and WT mice for transcriptomic analysis, a time point coinciding with severe phenotypic manifestation. A total of 1,171 differentially expressed genes were identified, including 648 upregulated and 523 downregulated genes (Fig. 5A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment in MAPK signaling and calcium signaling (Fig. 5B), which are both key regulatory pathways of mitochondria. We further analyzed the transcriptomics data by Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) and found the expression of genes related to oxidative phosphorylation deficiency was mostly increased in cKONex mice (Fig. 5C). Thus, these data indicated that mitochondria function in cKONex mice is significantly impaired at P80.

We therefore asked whether the observed paralysis of cKONex mice at around P80 resulted from impaired mitochondrial bioenergetics in neurons. Excessive calcium accumulation in the cytoplasm often leads to mitochondrial calcium overload and ultimately impairs mitochondrial function [43]. We found that PRMT5 deficiency indeed triggered elevated calcium levels within the mitochondria of cultured neurons (Figure S7A-C). Then, we assessed mitochondrial respiratory capacity using high-resolution respirometry on cortical samples from the M1 cortex of P75 mice. Compared to control samples, cKONex cortical samples exhibited a significant reduction in the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) that was mediated by complex I and a decreasing trend of OCR for complexes II and IV (Fig. 5D-E). In contrast, proton leak and maximum electron transfer system (ETS) capacity were comparable between the two groups (Fig. 5D-E), indicating a specific defect in oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) rather than a generalized loss of mitochondrial integrity. Impaired OXPHOS often leads to increased generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). To test that, we performed dihydroethidium (DHE) staining to detect superoxide anions. We did observe a significant increase in DHE fluorescence intensity in the cortex of P75 cKONex mice (Fig. 5F-G), indicating substantial ROS accumulation and oxidative stress. Collectively, these data demonstrate that PRMT5 deficiency initiates a damage cascade from calcium dyshomeostasis to impaired mitochondrial OXPHOS, which in turn promotes oxidative stress. These defects could ultimately contribute to neuronal metabolic crisis and functional decline.

Given the severe mitochondrial deficits, we investigated whether cKONex neurons ultimately undergo apoptosis. Intriguingly, we found no evidence of cleaved caspase-3 activation or DNA fragmentation via TUNEL staining in the cortex of P75 mice (Fig. 5H-I), ruling out occurrence of apoptosis in cKONex mice. We therefore hypothesized that the cKONex neurons survive in chronic and sub-lethal distress, which might provoke a non-autonomous response from the glial compartment. Immunoblot analysis confirmed the reactive astrocyte marker GFAP was massively increased, while the levels of the neuronal marker TUBB3 and astrocyte marker Aldolase C remained unchanged (Fig. 5J). These data revealed a fact that the intrinsically oxidative-stressed neurons elicit reactive gliosis in the cKONex brain. This neuroinflammatory milieu, rather than direct apoptosis of neurons, likely exacerbates synaptic dysfunction and contributes to the progressive dysfunction of motor neurons.

Nimodipine ameliorates hyperactivity and extends lifespan of cKONex mice

Nimodipine is an L-type calcium channel blocker with high lipophilicity, which reduces intracellular calcium levels by inhibiting calcium influx. Thus, it may have a beneficial effect on locomotor ability in cKONex mice. Drug administration initiated from P21 to P25, and hindlimb clasping and open field tests were performed every 5 days (Fig. 6A). Results showed that the movement distance in the OFT of nimodipine-treated cKONex mice remained at a similar level to WT controls, whereas the untreated cKONex mice displayed age-dependent hyperactivity (Fig. 6A-C). However, we observed no improvement in hindlimb clasping or rotarod defects with nimodipine treatment (Figure S8A-D). These data suggest the enhanced Cav1.3 activity is directly responsible for hyperactivity, whereas other circuit-level deficits underlie hindlimb clasping and motor learning impairments. More importantly, nimodipine treatment significantly extended the lifespan of cKONex mice by 42.9% (cKONex mice: 72.21 ± 17.05 days; nimodipine- administered cKONex mice: 103.17 ± 11.70 days) (Fig. 6D), which strongly suggested the reduced lifespan in cKONex mice was associated with calcium imbalance.

To assess whether the beneficial effect of nimodipine resulted from recovered calcium influx, we applied patch clamp recordings on cortical neurons of cKONex mice that were incubated with or without nimodipine (10 µM). As expected, nimodipine treatment significantly decreased the amplitude of calcium currents in cKONex neurons (Fig. 6E-F). Given our finding that the enhanced L-type current in cKONex neurons is primarily mediated by Cav1.3 (Fig. 4), these results suggest that nimodipine rescues excessive calcium influx by blocking Cav1.3-containing channels. Consistent with the recovery of whole-cell calcium currents, nimodipine treatment also rescued the elevated mitochondrial calcium levels in PRMT5-deficient neurons (Figure S7A-C). Then calcium imaging was used to analyze the effect of nimodipine in vivo. During voluntary movement, the treated cKONex mice showed a notable reduction in calcium signal compared to untreated cKONex mice (Fig. 6G). We next applied the tail suspension test to compare the calcium signal upon transition from immobility to mobility. Treatment with nimodipine significantly reduced calcium activity in cKONex M1 cortex, returning to a level that was similar to WT mice (Fig. 6H). Further analyses of peak amplitude and area under the curve (AUC) for all events also showed the recovery effects of nimodipine on cKONex mice, while no effect of nimodipine was observed on WT mice (Fig. 6I-J). These findings demonstrate that nimodipine rescued hyperactivity and lifespan defects of cKONex mice by inhibiting Cav1.3 activity.

Discussion

While the impact of PRMT5 in neurogenesis is well-documented, its functions in postmitotic neurons remain largely unexplored. Our study reveals a critical role of PRMT5 in excitatory neurons for controlling motor function and lifespan during postnatal development. PRMT5 regulates Cacna1d expression to maintain calcium homeostasis via catalyzing H4R3me2s. The elevated intracellular calcium level enhances neuronal excitability and impairs mitochondrial function. These data identify PRMT5 as a key regulator of calcium homeostasis during postnatal neuronal development.

Cortical neuron development requires precise regulation of calcium signaling. Calcium imbalance can lead to severe developmental defects, including abnormal dendritic arborization, synaptic instability and reduction of neurotransmission [44–46]. In the current study, we identified a novel PRMT5-Cacna1d axis that is particularly critical for maintaining calcium homeostasis during postnatal motor development. We demonstrated that PRMT5 serves as a negative regulator of Cacna1d in the postnatal mouse brain. Calcium influx and calcium activity were both significantly elevated in PRMT5-deficient neurons, which can be largely rescued by a Cav1.3 channel blocker. Although whole-cell recordings capture total L-type current [47], the unchanged Cacna1c (encoding Cav1.2) expression and upregulated Cacna1d (encoding Cav1.3) expression indicate that the increased current in cKONex neurons is primarily mediated by Cav1.3. Thus, these data clearly delineate the role of PRMT5 in maintaining calcium homeostasis via CACNA1D. Clinical studies have confirmed that mutations in CACNA1D are closely associated with epilepsy, motor developmental delay and autism spectrum disorders (Table S1) [48–50]. Notably, gain-of-function mutations enhance Cav1.3 channel activity, leading to excessive neuronal calcium influx that recapitulates our observations in cKONex mice [51, 52]. In contrast, loss-of-function mutations in CACNA1D have been reported to cause hypotonia, ALS, and impaired motor function [53–55]. Thus, the phenotypic abnormalities observed in cKONex mice can be attributed to the functional dysregulation of CACNA1D. The most compelling evidence supporting the central role of this axis in motor behavior regulation is the rescue effect of nimodipine, a specific Cav1.3 channel blocker. Administration of nimodipine restored calcium homeostasis and ameliorated hyperactivity. Thus, PRMT5-Cacna1d axis mediated calcium signaling is essential for movement control.

PRMT5 has been shown to be involved in the mRNA alternative splicing processes, which is a canonical strategy to regulate the function of CACNA1D [56–58]. However, our data showed that PRMT5 did not affect Cacna1d mRNA splicing but regulated Cacna1d expression via a histone methylation manner. We demonstrated that PRMT5 binds the Cacna1d promoter and represses Cacna1d expression through catalyzing H4R3me2s. Cross-species sequence analysis identified a 34 bp motif with 73.5% identity located within the PRMT5-bound regions of human (-740 bp to -221 bp) and mouse (-1987 bp to -1532 bp) Cacna1d promoters. This short conserved motif may represent a regulatory element critical for PRMT5 recruitment, and its positional drift suggests evolutionary plasticity of promoter architecture. PRMT5 deficiency in vitro and in vivo both significantly elevated Cacna1d expression and neuronal excitability. These findings reveale a previously unknown epigenetic mechanism for controlling Cacna1d expression in the brain. Epigenetic regulation of other calcium channels has been reported for Cav2.2 [59]. The advantage of the PRMT5 mediated epigenetic regulation lies in its ability to provide precise spatiotemporal control of Cacna1d expression. As our qPCR analyses showed, Cacna1d expression was highly dynamic during postnatal development. It maintained a low expression level at the early stages and reached a peak at around P25. This expression shift enables the rapid formation of synapses within the developing neurons at this period [60, 61]. However, the Cacna1d expression dramatically decreased at P30, which prevents calcium overload. In agreement with our data, epigenetic regulation of calcium homeostasis has been shown to prevent precocious maturation of human neurons [62]. The loss of PRMT5 disrupts the ability to dynamically regulate the Cacna1d expression, demonstrating a critical role of PRMT5 in the developing mouse brain.

Interestingly, the median lifespan of cKONex mice was only 75 days, significantly shorter than that of WT mice. Though no neuronal cell death was observed, we did find several mitochondrial-metabolism-related defects in PRMT5-deficient neurons, such as reduced OCR of Complexes I, accumulation of ROS and presence of reactive gliosis. All these defects were associated with excessive calcium influx into mitochondria [63, 64]. The premature lethality observed in cKONex mice likely resulted from severe impairment of neuronal function driven by Cav1.3-mediated calcium toxicity. The fact that pharmacological inhibition of Cav1.3 channels with nimodipine significantly prolonged the survival time of cKONex mice strongly implies that the calcium overload is the causal factor of mouse lethality. This highlights a therapeutic potential of targeting Cav1.3-driven calcium toxicity in neurodegenerative diseases.

In our study, we successfully rescued hyperactivity and lifespan defects, but not the hindlimb clasping phenotype in cKONex mice. The coordination of hindlimb movement requires both concerted neuronal activity in the motor cortex and properly formed neural circuits. As we found that cKOCamKIIα mice did not show hindlimb clasping defects, which indicated the time window of such defects was much earlier than the onset of motor development in mice. Thus, the failure to rescue hindlimb clasping behavior should be due to abnormal neural circuits that could not be rectified by Cav1.3-targeting drugs. On the other hand, while we focused on Cacna1d in the current study, PRMT5 regulates numerous targets in the developing neurons as revealed by transcriptomics data. It is plausible that defects in mRNA splicing or other transcriptional programs may contribute to the full pathological spectrum of cKONex mice. Successful rescue of hindlimb clasping by drugs has been previously reported in a dystonia mouse model, which is suggested to result from suppression of cholinergic interneurons in the striatum [65]. Thus, PRMT5 may have subtype-specific regulatory mechanisms in different motor circuits, and single-target intervention against Cav1.3 is insufficient to fully reverse all motor defects.

In summary, this study identified a PRMT5-Cacna1d epigenetic mechanism in post-mitotic neurons that is essential for motor development and mice survival. These data link enhanced Cav1.3 activity to neuronal hyperexcitability and mitochondrial dysfunction, underlying the pathogenesis of movement disorders (Fig. 7). Thus, it offers new therapeutic potential for Cav1.3-targeted drugs in the treatment of neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative movement disorders.

Working model of PRMT5 repressing Cacna1d expression through H4R3me2s to maintain calcium homeostasis

Loss of PRMT5 increases Cav1.3-mediated calcium influx, elevates neuronal excitability, and impairs mitochondrial function, leading to hyperactivity and shortened lifespan in mice. Blocking Cav1.3 with nimodipine rescues hyperactivity and extends lifespan.
