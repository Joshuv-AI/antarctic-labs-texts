Student teacher inter brain coupling forecast cross semester academic fluctuation in real world classrooms
Authors: Xiaomeng Xu, Dan Zhang, Yu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudent performance fluctuates across learning, raising questions about what drives these changes and whether instruction equitably supports diverse learners. Although teachers likely shape these trajectories, the pathways linking student–teacher interaction to individual performance variation remain unclear. The present study investigates student-teacher inter-brain coupling in authentic classroom through three-semester longitudinal hyperscanning. Using wearable hyperscanning technology, a total of 4,175 longitudinal EEG recordings were collected from 107 junior and senior students across 393 regular Chinese and math classes. While static models revealed a negative association between student-teacher inter-brain coupling and math performance, which likely reflects teacher selection effects, dynamic analyses showed that increases in coupling, especially in the high-beta band, predicted subsequent improvement in both subjects. These findings highlight the functional significance of inter-brain coupling in real-world classroom learning, providing robust ecological validity and supporting its potential as a measurable marker of effective pedagogy.Subject terms: Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Across diverse educational settings and cultures, student performance is inherently dynamic, marked by differentiation and fluctuation over time. These fluctuations invite deeper reflection on educational quality and equity, specifically what drives changes in students’ performance and whether classroom instruction supports students with diverse learning needs in equitable and effective ways. As learners progress, their learning trajectories diverge, shaped by a complex interplay of individual, social, and instructional factors. Among these factors, teacher-student interaction is a central classroom process and a primary channel through which instruction is enacted. Accumulating evidence suggests that the quality of these interactions is associated with educational outcomes, including cognitive achievement, non-cognitive development, and longer-term trajectories1–4. A broad empirical literature links higher-quality teacher student interaction, such as cognitively supportive questioning, feedback, and dialogic exchange, to better learning outcomes1,5,6. Such interactions are also associated with lower academic anxiety, higher autonomy and self-regulated learning, and higher self-efficacy and motivation, which together support students’ longer-term educational development7–10. Yet, in everyday practice, educators often face difficulties in discerning which students are keeping pace, which are falling behind, and how their own instructional practices contribute to students’ learning outcomes. Addressing this gap requires deeper insights into how student-teacher interactions drive dynamic trajectories, especially among students with varying learning profiles. Such insights are essential for developing adaptive, evidence-based pedagogical strategies that foster equitable learning environments and empower all students to thrive.

However, effectively measuring the quality of student-teacher interactions presents significant methodological challenges. These interactions are inherently complex, encompassing both observable behaviors, such as questioning and responding, and implicit cognitive and affective exchanges11–13. For instance, experienced teachers can intuitively tailor their instruction through such interactions to align with students’ zones of proximal development, crafting learning experiences that resonate with students’ current abilities and potential for growth14,15. Therefore, dynamically assessing the quality of student-teacher interactions is crucial for gaining a deeper understanding of students’ learning outcomes. Yet, objectively and accurately quantifying these interactions remains elusive. Self-report questionnaires offer valuable insights into participants’ subjective appraisals of classroom experience, while also being susceptible to subjective biases and social desirability effects16,17. To complement this perspective, neurophysiological measures can offer additional, more implicit information about process-level coordination during instruction.

Recent advances in EEG-based hyperscanning offer a transformative approach to study student-teacher interactions in ecologically valid classroom settings. Building on these properties, hyperscanning quantifies inter-brain coupling, which contemporary accounts increasingly interpret as a process-level signature of interpersonal alignment during communication, reflecting coordinated attention, shared task engagement, and mutual adaptation in moment-to-moment information processing18–21. Prior work has linked inter-brain coupling to learning outcomes and interactional processes relevant to knowledge construction22–25, suggesting that inter-brain coupling may serve as a potential indicator of learning outcomes and, indirectly, reflect the quality of student-teacher interaction; however, its role in authentic classrooms remains underexplored, particularly across longitudinal timescales. Conducting longitudinal research in real classrooms requires devices that are lightweight and portable, minimally disruptive to teaching and learning, easy to fit within short breaks (a 10-min between-class window), and relatively low-cost to enable large-scale deployment. Wearable EEG headbands meet these practical constraints and therefore make large-scale, naturalistic longitudinal classroom research feasible.

In the present study, we used a two-channel wearable EEG headband positioned over prefrontal sites (Fp1/Fp2). Prior studies have shown that oscillatory activity measured over frontal and prefrontal sites, is closely linked to core learning-relevant processes such as cognitive control and working-memory as well as affective states26–28. Moreover, wearable EEG hyperscanning work in ecologically valid classroom settings has demonstrated that signals indexed from prefrontal channels (including Fp1/Fp2) are feasible to record and can capture learning-relevant coupling patterns that relate to educational outcomes29,30. Finally, key challenges in longitudinal classroom research, such as confounding factors (e.g., prior knowledge, teacher bias) can be addressed through longitudinal econometric modeling, which isolates the predictive power of inter-brain coupling dynamics on academic progress31.

To unpack the relationship between student-teacher inter-brain coupling and learning outcomes, we adopted both static and dynamic modeling approaches, offering distinct yet complementary analytical perspectives. The static model assesses how coupling relates to concurrent academic outcomes, capturing more superficial, state-like associations. However, such associations are prone to endogeneity, especially due to complex, unobserved confounders in real classrooms—such as differences in students’ prior knowledge, cognitive abilities, motivation, learning strategies, and teachers’ self-selection in allocating attention based on student needs or performance31. For instance, teachers may attune more closely to lower-performing students, leading to higher coupling values negatively associated with outcomes. To address these concerns, we further developed a dynamic framework that uses longitudinal variation to relate changes in coupling to changes in academic performance. Specifically, we model wave-to-wave changes in outcomes (Δscores) as a function of baseline coupling and changes in coupling (ΔTI). This change-based specification reduces confounding from time-invariant student characteristics and helps attenuate bias from teacher selection based on static achievement levels. Consistent with difference-based modeling logic in longitudinal analysis32–34, we include baseline coupling as a covariate to account for initial status and improve internal validity. Together, the static and dynamic models offer a more robust, multidimensional understanding of how neural coupling between students and teachers relates to learning in naturalistic classrooms.

The study is based on a longitudinal investigation involving 107 students in junior and senior high school. A total of 393 classroom sessions were recorded across Chinese and math subjects in authentic learning environments. Neurophysiological data were collected using portable wearable EEG devices from both teachers and students over the course of three academic semesters, encompassing six waves of valid longitudinal data. Given the distinct functional significance of different EEG frequency bands, separate analyses were conducted for each band. For each participant, we calculated the average levels of student-teacher inter-brain coupling, as well as standardized scores representing students’ learning outcomes, for each half-semester, which we refer to as a ‘wave’ in the context of this longitudinal study. To infer the predictive role of inter-brain coupling, we employed a dynamic model based on longitudinal changes and a static model based on concurrent associations. The study examines whether student-teacher inter-brain coupling, particularly its longitudinal dynamics, is associated with and can help track changes in students’ academic development over time in ecologically valid classroom contexts.

Results

In this study, 107 students (60 males, aged 12–15) in their first year of junior and senior secondary school and eight teachers (four teaching Chinese and four teaching math) participated in the study. EEG recordings were collected during Chinese and math classes over three semesters (1.5 years in total), with one recording week per month capturing 1–2 daily sessions (40 min each). In total, 393 class sessions were recorded, including 182 Chinese classes (88 junior, 94 senior) and 211 math classes (113 junior, 98 senior). Prior to each session, students were equipped with EEG headbands, participated in regular, teacher-planned instruction aligned with the school’s standard curriculum (Fig. 1a–c).

Students’ learning outcomes were assessed through half-semester examinations, with scores provided by the school administration. Each half-semester was operationally defined as a single wave. Scores were then normalized against the entire student cohort for further analysis, with higher normalized scores indicating better performance. Figure 1d–e illustrates the temporal changes in Chinese and math scores, respectively. Students were stratified into quartiles based on their grade rankings: 0–25%, 25–50%, 50–75%, and 75–100%, with the 75–100% range representing the top 25%. Notable fluctuations in student rankings were observed across performance categories over time.

The research employed the total interdependence (TI) value to quantify the student-teacher inter-brain coupling, conducting analyses across multiple frequency bands20,29,35. For each student, the student-teacher TI value was calculated between that student and their respective teacher during each class session and then averaged and standardized for each wave. The differences in TI values and scores were calculated between two consecutive waves, and this analysis was extended across various frequency bands including broadband (1–40 Hz), delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), low-beta (13–18 Hz) and high-beta (18–30 Hz) frequency bands. To explore the potential causal relationship between student-teacher inter-brain coupling and learning outcomes, the dynamic and static models were constructed to capture the relationship between student-teacher TI and test scores across waves (Fig. 1c). The dynamic model captured the relationship between changes in TI (∆TI) and changes in test scores (∆Score) across consecutive waves, while the static model examined the association between TI and test scores within each wave.

Changes in student-teacher inter-brain coupling forecast students’ academic improvement

The study first employed a dynamic model to investigate whether increases in student-teacher inter-brain coupling could forecast improvements in student performance. The model used the changes in learning outcomes (Δscores) between the two consecutive waves as the outcome variable, with the baseline student-teacher coupling values (TI values) and changes in coupling values (ΔTI values) as predictors. Using Δscores allows the analysis to focus on within-student changes over time and helps attenuate confounding from stable between-student differences in achievement when assessing how coupling dynamics relate to performance dynamics. A Bayesian linear mixed-effects (LME) model was employed, which allowed us to account for the nested structure of the data by controlling for repeated measures across students and semesters. The Bayesian approach is well-suited for small samples and enables distribution-free inference, offering a flexible framework for modeling complex neuro data36.

The estimation of the dynamic model was conducted separately for the full student sample, the junior student subsample, and the senior student subsample. Results for the full sample are presented in Fig. 2, while the corresponding results for the junior and senior students subsamples are provided in the Supplementary Information (see Fig. S1). After controlling for the baseline TI values, the analysis showed that ΔTI values significantly predict changes in learning outcomes, operationalized as Δscores between consecutive waves, in both Chinese (Fig. 2a) and math (Fig. 2b). For both Chinese and math, increases in ΔTI within the high-beta frequency band were positively associated with improvements in student scores across the full sample (Chinese: posterior mean = 0.256, 95% HPDI [0.003, 0.516]; math: posterior mean = 0.266, 95% HPDI [0.042, 0.483]). For these high-beta ΔTI effects, the 95% HPDIs do not include zero and the corresponding nonparametric tests yield p < 0.05, providing convergent Bayesian and frequentist evidence for a positive association. In practical terms, students who showed larger increases in high-beta student-teacher inter-brain coupling between waves tended to exhibit greater improvements in their Chinese and math test scores over the same intervals.

In the junior student subgroup, changes in both broadband and high-beta TI values significantly predicted improvements in Chinese scores (Δbroadband TI: posterior mean = 0.520, 95% HPDI [0.004, 1.063]; Δhigh-beta TI: posterior mean = 0.643, 95% HPDI [0.163, 1.114]). By contrast, no credible effects were observed in the senior student subgroup for either subject, as the 95% HPDIs included zero. TI values were also found to significantly predict academic improvement, especially within the junior student subgroup. For Chinese, TI values in the broadband and high-beta frequency bands similarly exhibit a significant positive predictive effect on score increases (broadband TI: posterior mean = 0.628, 95% HPDI [0.047, 1.142]; high-beta TI: posterior mean = 0.797, 95% HPDI [0.297, 1.276]). For math, TI values in the broadband and delta, and bands are capable of positively forecasting increases in scores (broadband TI: posterior mean = 0.509, 95% HPDI [0.184, 0.811]; delta TI: posterior mean = 0.383, 95% HPDI [0.045, 0.722]). This implies that higher coupling values with teachers can also account for the progress in learning outcomes. All statistically significant findings were consistently validated through both Bayesian and parametric estimation (see Tables S1, S2 and Fig. S1 for full results).

To explore the lagged effect of student-teacher coupling on Δscores across multiple subsequent waves, TI and ΔTI were employed to forecast Δscores with lags ranging from 0 to 4 waves. For instance, if ΔTI represents the change in TI values from wave 1 to wave 2, then with a lag wave of 0, the corresponding dependent variable would be the change in scores from wave 1 to wave 2, yielding results consistent with those depicted in Fig. 2a–b. When the lag wave is set to 1, the dependent variable shifts to the change in scores from wave 2 to wave 3. The outcomes are depicted in Fig. 2e (Chinese) and Fig. 2f (math), with only the significant results at lag 0 displayed in the figures. The results suggests that student-teacher coupling does not have a predictive effect on subsequent changes in academic performance.

Student-teacher inter-brain coupling forecast students’ academic achievement

As a comparison, the study implemented a static model to forecast the coefficients between learning outcomes within the same wave and student-teacher inter-brain coupling (TI values). The results, as shown in Fig. 3a, indicate no significant association between inter-brain coupling and student performance in Chinese classes across different subsamples. However, in math classes, a significant negative association was observed between student-teacher inter-brain coupling and student performance (Fig. 3b). Specifically, in the full sample, theta-band TI was negatively associated with math scores (theta TI: posterior mean = −0.141, 95%, HPDI [−0.265, −0.018]). Among senior secondary students, significant negative associations were observed between math performance and TI in the delta, theta, and low-beta frequency bands (delta TI: posterior mean = −0.194, 95%, HPDI [−0.382, −0.007]; theta TI: posterior mean = −0.283, 95%, HPDI [−0.473, −0.088]; low-beta TI: posterior mean = −0.217, 95%, HPDI [−0.421, −0.008]), as shown in Fig. S2. Figure 3c present the model fitting results for the frequency bands that showed significant effects in the static model. The model outcomes from both Bayesian and parametric estimations are presented in Tables S3, S4. The study subsequently employed TI and ΔTI values to predict student performance with temporal lags ranging from 0 to 4 waves (Fig. 3d). No significant associations were identified when forecasting academic outcomes across multiple lagged waves.

Comparing the predictive effects of student-student and student-teacher inter-brain coupling on academic achievement

As active participants in classroom learning, students also engage in meaningful interactions with one another. Inter-brain coupling among peers has been shown to reflect shared attention and group-level engagement, with potential impacts on individual learning outcomes20,29. As a comparative analysis, we examined large-scale real-world EEG data to calculate the inter-brain coupling between individual students and their class-average peers, as well as between students and high-performing classmates. Both static and dynamic models were employed to assess the predictive value of these peer-based coupling measures for students’ academic outcomes.

The results of the dynamic model based on student-student coupling are presented in Fig. 4. Figure 4a–b displays the predictive effects of student-class inter-brain coupling on academic progress. No significant association was observed between student-class coupling and changes in Chinese scores. However, in the case of math, increases in alpha and low-beta ΔTI were positively associated with improvements in academic performance (alpha ΔTI: posterior mean = 0.208, 95%, HPDI [0.021, 0.377]; low-beta ΔTI: posterior mean = 0.217, 95%, HPDI [0.03, 0.412]). These results suggest that higher student-class inter-brain coupling in specific frequency bands may facilitate math learning in real-world classroom settings. Figure 4c–d shows the dynamic prediction results based on student-excellent inter-brain coupling. No significant associations were found between ΔTI and academic improvement across the full student samples. The analysis results for the sub-samples of junior and senior high school students are presented in Fig. S3, with trends consistent with those observed in the full sample analysis. Compared to student-teacher coupling, student-student inter-brain coupling showed a significant effect only in predicting improvements in math scores based on student-class coupling. In contrast, student-teacher coupling exhibited significant predictive power for academic growth in both Chinese and math subjects.

Assessing the subject specificity of student-teacher coupling effects

To examine whether the predictive power of student-teacher inter-brain coupling is subject-specific, we conducted cross-subject analyses by using Chinese-class TI values to predict math performance and vice versa. Both dynamic and static models were applied. As shown in Fig. S5, no significant effects were found in either direction. This pattern suggests that the predictive effects of inter-brain coupling are not generalized across academic domains but instead reflect subject-specific dynamics of classroom interaction.

Forecasting learning outcomes across different student performance groups

Previous analyses revealed a negative association between student-teacher coupling and math achievement in the static model, suggesting that teachers may selectively attune more closely to lower-performing students during routine instruction. This suggests that the predictive relationship between neural coupling and academic performance may differ across achievement levels. To investigate this further, we categorized students into four groups based on their baseline performance: top 25% (75–100%), 50–75%, 25–50%, and bottom 25% (0–25%). Dynamic and static models were then estimated separately for each subgroup, and model fit indices were compared. As shown in Table 1, the predictive effects of student-teacher coupling varied across performance groups. For Chinese, the dynamic model yielded the most significant estimates within the top 25% of students. In contrast, for math, the best model fit in the dynamic analysis was observed among middle-performing students (25–75%), while the static model showed the strongest fit for the bottom-performing group (bottom 25%). These findings confirm that the predictive association between student-teacher inter-brain coupling and academic performance differs across achievement levels, indicating that the relationship is modulated by students’ prior academic standing.

Discussion

This study investigates the impact of student-teacher inter-brain coupling values on learning outcome in authentic Chinese and math classes over six waves (i.e., three semesters) at a middle school. Using both static and dynamic models, we examined momentary associations and temporal fluctuations in student-teacher inter-brain coupling in relation to academic performance. The study found that increases in coupling over time significantly predicted academic improvement in both subjects, while static coupling values were negatively associated with math performance, a pattern that is plausibly driven by teacher attention allocation and selection processes in momentary classroom interactions. No significant lagged effects were observed across models. Comparisons with student-student coupling revealed that student-class coupling predicted both short- and long-term math performance but showed no effect for Chinese, whereas coupling with high-performing peers was only associated with short-term outcomes. Taken together, the dynamic findings demonstrate cross-subject consistency, while the static and peer-related patterns vary by subject, indicating that teacher-aligned versus peer-aligned coupling may support learning through different mechanisms depending on disciplinary demands. These findings highlight student-teacher inter-brain coupling as a robust, classroom-embedded neurophysiological indicator that tracks learning-relevant interaction dynamics over time and offers an additional lens for understanding and evaluation of instructional processes in ecologically valid settings.

This study, through the application of dynamic modeling, reveals that students who experience enhanced inter-brain coupling with their teacher during class tend to achieve larger academic gains over time. Specifically, the high-beta ΔTI effects indicate that within-student strengthening of coupling across waves is associated with within-student improvement in academic outcomes, after accounting for baseline coupling. These findings highlight the practical value of coupling-based indices as potential indicators of instructional quality and student teacher interaction quality in ecologically valid classroom settings. By translating complex coupling estimates into intuitive summaries and visualizations (for example, showing which student subgroups exhibit higher TI or larger ΔTI), such indicators can provide teachers with an additional, neurophysiological lens on classroom interaction processes, supporting reflective evaluation of teaching practices and helping identify students whose interactional trajectories may benefit from more targeted pedagogical support. On the contrary, the static model, by identifying associations between student-teacher inter-brain coupling and concurrent academic performance, offers insight into short-term, surface-level relationships between coupling and learning outcomes. It reflects how teachers’ instructional strategies and attention distribution manifest in specific classroom contexts. In real classrooms, however, teachers often adopt adaptive interaction strategies based on students’ prior performance—a phenomenon known as teacher self-selection37. Consistent with this logic, the negative static association observed in math is plausibly explained by teacher self-selection, whereby teachers concentrate step-by-step guidance and focused support on students who are behind, yielding higher coupling among lower achieving students. Prior research similarly cautions that coupling values alone are insufficient for reliably forecasting academic outcomes38, as static models may encounter uncontrolled confounding in predictive contexts. In contrast, the dynamic model, by capturing temporal fluctuations in coupling and linking them to longitudinal changes in academic outcomes, which makes the estimates less sensitive to stable between student differences and partially attenuates teacher selection based on static achievement levels. This enables a more defensible interpretation of the benefits of high-quality student-teacher interaction in terms of coupling dynamics tracking learning dynamics over time.

It is also notable that the static association was subject dependent, emerging in math but not in Chinese. A plausible explanation is that math, as a comparatively “hard” discipline39, is typically organized around constrained knowledge structures and a fixed progression of solution steps, which encourages whole-class pacing, frequent monitoring, and stepwise error-focused scaffolding; such support is often directed toward students who are slightly behind because their difficulties are most visible and most remediable during instruction, thereby concentrating student-teacher interaction among lower-performing students within a wave and yielding a negative static coupling-score association29,40. In contrast, Chinese learning, as a comparatively “soft” discipline, often involves open-ended meaning making, interpretation, and expressive quality. Thus, multiple responses may be acceptable and instructional trajectories are less tightly constrained, so teacher interaction may be less directly aligned with contemporaneous test standing and static coupling-score links may weaken. This cross-sectional subject asymmetry is consistent with the idea that the functional meaning of classroom coupling depends on disciplinary learning demands and interaction organization29, even when longitudinal coupling change remains beneficial across subjects.

To explore the unique advantage of student-teacher coupling, the study compared student-teacher inter-brain coupling with student-student coupling in predicting academic outcomes. Results showed that student-class coupling significantly predicted score fluctuations in math over time. In contrast, student-excellent coupling was associated only with momentary performance and lacked longitudinal predictive power. Notably, these effects were subject-specific: student-class coupling was more predictive in math, while student-excellent coupling was more relevant in Chinese. These findings align with prior research suggesting that alignment with the class average supports learning in analytical subjects like math, whereas high-achieving peers may serve as better cognitive benchmarks in Chinese29. This subject contrast suggests that peer-based coupling may operate through discipline-specific mechanisms: in math, coupling with the class average may index whether students remain synchronized with a shared instructional pace and stepwise problem-solving trajectory, whereas in Chinese, coupling with high-performing peers may better capture access to advanced discourse and strategy benchmarks that students can model or compare against. Crucially, across both subjects, student-teacher inter-brain coupling consistently demonstrated the strongest and most stable predictive power for both immediate performance and long-term improvement.

In addition to these findings, the main effects in the dynamic model were most prominent in the high-beta frequency band, whereas the static results were primarily associated with theta-band synchrony. This divergence suggests that different aspects of teacher-student interaction are captured by distinct neural dynamics. High-beta coupling has been linked to top-down cognitive control41,42, goal-directed processing43, integration of complex information, and long-term memory44, aligning with the notion that sustained improvement reflects deeper, effortful engagement45,46. In contrast, theta-band activity is associated with attentional allocation and episodic encoding47,48, reflecting more immediate, surface-level responsiveness to instruction. Thus, while high-beta coupling may signal long-term alignment in cognitive processes conducive to academic growth, theta synchrony may indicate moment-to-moment sensitivity to pedagogical input. These frequency-specific patterns underscore the multifaceted nature of classroom neural coupling and highlight the value of combining static, dynamic, and experimental approaches to capture the neural correlates of effective teaching. Notably, our findings revealed no significant lagged effects, suggesting that the predictive influence of inter-brain coupling is limited to contiguous academic terms, likely due to the dynamic nature of adolescent performance, with the impact of neural coupling most evident within the relevant teaching wave.

Finally, several associations showed grade-level heterogeneity across the junior and senior subsamples. For student-teacher coupling, the core dynamic association linking coupling change to achievement change was more pronounced in the junior sample, whereas the corresponding static coupling-score association appeared more evident in the senior sample. This contrast may reflect developmental and instructional shifts from junior to senior stages (e.g., curricular pace, lesson organization, and how teachers allocate attention), and is therefore treated as a potential boundary condition. At the same time, some findings were consistent across grades: for student-student coupling, student-class coupling significantly and positively predicted math achievement in both junior and senior subsamples, suggesting a stable benefit of maintaining synchronization with the class-level learning rhythm in analytical tasks. Overall, these discrepancies are treated as potential boundary conditions, plausibly related to developmental-stage differences in instructional goals, curricular demands, and student learning characteristics.

Several limitations of this study warrant discussion. First, to minimize disruption to authentic classrooms, we used wearable, portable devices with a limited number of channels. Future studies could strengthen robustness by using higher-density wearable EEG systems when feasible, integrating multimodal physiological signals (e.g., skin conductance, heart rate). Second, our findings indicate that coupling-achievement relations may vary across classroom contexts and developmental stages, and these subgroup patterns should be interpreted cautiously as potential boundary conditions. Future research is needed with more targeted designs to clarify the specific factors underlying these differences. Third, although academic test scores provide a quantifiable and widely used indicator of learning outcomes, relying on scores alone is inherently limited and may not capture broader cognitive, motivational, and socio-emotional dimensions of learning that could also relate to classroom interaction and coupling. Finally, we emphasize that the present contribution is primarily empirical: although inter-brain coupling offers a promising, process-sensitive marker of classroom interaction, its precise theoretical meaning and underlying mechanisms remain to be specified and tested in future work.

In conclusion, this study systematically explored the impact of student-teacher coupling on learning outcomes in authentic classroom settings, providing neurophysiological evidence across multiple dimensions. The findings show that increases in student teacher inter-brain coupling are a reliable predictor of academic gains, indicating that coupling-based indices can serve as a complementary, process-sensitive signal for understanding learning trajectories in ecologically valid classrooms. For teachers, this increase offers an efficient way to assess instructional efficacy, while providing students with personalized feedback to understand their performance and adjust study strategies, demonstrating this approach’s practical value in education.

EEG data collection

The EEG data collection took place in authentic classroom environment and spanned a total duration of 12 months, covering three academic semesters. Data acquisition extended over four months per semester, with each half-semester identified as a distinct ‘wave’. Within these waves, data collection took place for a week every month, during which one to two sessions were recorded daily from four separate student classes. In total, 393 class sessions were recorded, including 182 Chinese classes (88 junior, 94 senior) and 211 math classes (113 junior, 98 senior). A total of 4175 valid EEG recordings were obtained across sessions, including 2057 from Chinese classes, with each student contributing one recording per session. Each lesson had a duration of 40 min, during which teachers conducted their planned teaching activities, and students engaged in their regular learning tasks. The recorded lessons reflected routine, non-intervention instruction aligned with the school’s standard curriculum. In this context, a typical 40-minute Chinese or math lesson was predominantly teacher-led, with approximately 20–25 min devoted to whole-class instruction and guided explanation, 8–12 min of guided or independent in-class practice, and 3–8 min of brief whole-class questioning or peer discussion; short small-group activities, when used, generally occupied a limited portion of the lesson. This description is intended to characterize the usual instructional format of the recorded classes rather than a separately coded distribution of activity types. Both students and teachers equipped the EEG headbands before each session and removed them subsequently, as directed by the research team.

EEG data were captured using a dual-channel headband featuring dry electrodes, specifically positioned at the Fp1 and Fp2 regions of the frontal cortex. Prior hyperscanning studies, including EEG and fNIRS, indicate that prefrontal activity and inter-brain coupling can be meaningfully captured in classroom and interactive learning settings and have been linked to learning-relevant outcomes22,29,30,49. In the present study, data were recorded at a sampling rate of 250 Hz using the Brainno device (SOSO H&C, South Korea). The reference electrode was clipped to the right ear, serving as the ground for Fpz.

Learning outcomes data collection and preprocessing

Data on the students’ academic achievement were gathered through their midterm and final examination scores in Chinese and math over three semesters, encompassing a total of six examinations, provided by the school administration.

We utilized rank-based normalized scores to represent students’ learning outcomes, enabling a consistent and equitable comparison of student performance across various exams and cohort sizes. First, exam difficulty and raw score distributions can vary across waves, making raw scores not directly comparable, whereas rank information captures each student’s relative standing and is less sensitive to shifts in the score scale. Second, because cohort size differed slightly across waves, a rank-based metric provides a natural adjustment for these differences while preserving the comparability of performance.

For each exam wave and subject, students were first ranked on a scale from 1 to N, where N represents the total number of students in the cohort. The highest-performing student was assigned a rank of 1, progressing sequentially to the student with the lowest score, who received the rank of N. This approach effectively mitigating the issues of score saturation and truncation and highlights the relative performance among students, enhancing the robustness of the model. EEG data preprocessing

The EEG data for each student were preprocessed through a five-step procedure for both channels, consistent with methodologies employed in previous studies29,35. The preprocessing pipeline included epoching, missing-data identification, robust detrending, band-pass filtering, and ocular-artifact mitigation. First, EEG data from each channel were segmented into 30 s non-overlapping epochs, consistent with prior real-classroom inter-brain coupling studies using the same strategy and to support stable frequency-domain (cross-spectral) estimation under naturalistic recording conditions29,50,51. For a 40-min session, this yielded 160 epochs across the two channels per student. Next, epochs with absolute amplitudes continuously below 0.1 μV were marked as missing, indicating that no valid EEG signal was recorded. The signals were then detrended using a polynomial fitting approach52, and denoised with a 1–40 Hz band-pass filter (Hamming window) to suppress high-frequency components and impulse noise. Ocular artifacts were subsequently mitigated using the multi-scale dictionary learning (MSDL) method53, which learns stereotyped eye-movement waveforms in the time domain and is well suited for low-channel EEG because it operates effectively under single-channel measurements without requiring explicit artifact-segment detection. Ocular artifacts were screened every 5 s. In total, 569460 ocular or related artifact instances were detected across 191432 epochs (approximately six detections per minute); 445547 instances (78%) were corrected using MSDL, and the remaining epochs with absolute amplitudes exceeding the predefined rejection threshold (±150 μV) were discarded during epoching, as such values substantially exceed typical EEG amplitudes expected for neural activity. The effects of each preprocessing step on the EEG data are illustrated in Fig. S6. This figure shows an example segment of the raw EEG data, the data after detrending, the filtered data, and the effect of ocular artifact removal, thereby confirming the effectiveness of each preprocessing step.

Additionally, 33 of the participants also participated in a separate research project35, in which resting-state EEG (eyes-open and eyes-closed) was collected from the same experimental cohort. As reported in ref. 35, the eyes-closed resting-state spectrum exhibited a clear alpha-band peak, supporting the reliability of the EEG recordings; hence, the quality-control plot is not reproduced here.

Student-class inter-brain coupling

The student-class inter-brain coupling value for a given student is computed as the mean of all TI values between this student and the rest of the class of each session. For Chinese, the average proportion of valid student-class coupling segments for each session was 57.8%, while for math, it was 57.1% (Fig. S7c, d).

Student-excellent Inter-brain coupling

The student-excellent inter-brain coupling value for a given student is computed as the mean of all TI values between this student and the top four student in the class. For Chinese, the average proportion of valid student-class coupling segments for each session was 37.5%, while for math, it was 45.9% (Fig. S7e, f).

Following this, the inter-brain coupling values within each wave (i.e., half-semester) were averaged for each student. To ensure the quality of the data, student samples with valid sessions constituting less than 20% within a wave were omitted from the analysis. These averaged TI values were then normalized to a range of [0, 1] for each frequency band.

Lag inter-brain coupling analysis

Student teacher inter-brain coupling in classrooms may exhibit lagged synchrony, reflecting temporally ordered speaker-listener processes such as information transmission, anticipatory processing, and interactive prediction. Prior EEG work in naturalistic instruction suggests that coupling at specific lags (teacher precedes 3 s or 6 s) can be meaningful and may relate to learning outcomes30. Building on this possibility, the present study conducted a lag coupling analysis to examine whether student teacher TI values peak at non-zero temporal offsets and to determine the lag value used for the primary analyses.

A within-dyad resampling (bootstrapping) procedure was implemented. For each student-teacher dyad, broadband TI values were recalculated at multiple lag settings by shifting one person’s epoch time series forward or backward relative to the other in stepwise increments. Lags from −5 to +5 were examined (Fig. 7), where negative lags indicate that the student time series precedes the teacher time series and positive lags indicate that the student time series lags behind the teacher. To establish a dyad-specific baseline distribution that reflects non-matched classroom alignment, a large set of “mismatched” TI values was generated by pairing epochs across non-corresponding sessions within the same dyad across all available lesson combinations (e.g., Day 1 Chinese Session 1 paired with Day 2 Chinese Session 2, or Day 1 Chinese Session 1 paired with Day 2 Math Session 3). Under this framework, TI values computed from matched classroom alignment were expected to exceed the baseline derived from non-matched session pairings.

Regression analysis

Linear mixed-effects (LME) models were fitted to the data to investigate the relationship between student-teacher TI and academic achievement, which allow to effectively partition the overall variation of the dependent variable into components corresponding to different levels of data hierarchy55. According to the dependent variables, two kinds of models are designed. The dynamic model (formula 4) uses the difference in test score between wave t and the previous wave as the dependent variable, representing the improvement of learning achievement, while the static model (formula 5) uses standardized test score as dependent variable. Given the potential non-independence of EEG data within each individual students or time wave, the two categorical variables are employed to classify the observations into separate groups, to account for correlated observations in the data56.

Bayesian estimation was employed to analyze the relationship between student–teacher inter-brain coupling and academic performance, using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling to approximate the posterior distributions of model parameters57,58. This approach was chosen for its flexibility in estimating complex hierarchical models and its ability to provide full posterior distributions of parameters, offering richer information than traditional point estimates. Bayesian methods do not rely on large-sample asymptotic or strict distributional assumptions, making them particularly suited for small to moderate sample sizes and nested data structures frequently encountered in educational neuroscience research.

Parameter estimates were summarized as posterior means with 95% highest posterior density intervals (95% HPDIs). In general, when the 95% HPDI for a regression coefficient includes zero, we interpret this as indicating little or no stable association between the predictor and the outcome. Conversely, when the 95% HPDI does not include zero, we regard this as strong posterior support for a non-zero effect. A positive posterior mean indicates a positive association, whereas a negative posterior mean indicates a negative association.

To ensure the robustness of our reported effects, we adopted a dual reporting strategy: all significant findings reported in this paper meet the threshold of both Bayesian credible intervals (95% HDIs excluding zero) and frequentist nonparametric p-values (p < 0.05). For visualization, we treat effects whose 95% HPDIs exclude zero as having strong posterior support, and we mark those that also meet the p < 0.05 criterion with asterisks in the figures. This convergence across inferential paradigms enhances the credibility and interpretability of our results.

Forecasting of academic improvement

The dynamic model is applied in order to investigate whether changes in student-teacher coupling can predict changes in students’ academic performance over time. This longitudinal, change-based specification was adopted because static models using achievement at a single wave can be strongly influenced by stable between-student differences (e.g., prior achievement and general ability) and by potential teacher selection effects based on observed performance levels. By focusing on within-student change, the dynamic model helps reduce such confounding and directly tests whether coupling dynamics track performance dynamics. The investigation aimed to explore whether the increase in TI values could dynamically affect changes in academic achievement after controlling for the initial impact of TI. In pursuit of this, the model integrated both the baseline TI values and their incremental growth (ΔTI) as independent variables, with the change in standardized rank-based scores between consecutive waves (Δscores) as the dependent variable in the analysis.
