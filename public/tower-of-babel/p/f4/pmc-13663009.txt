Toripalimab-induced isolated ACTH deficiency associated with gastroparesis: a case report
Authors: Chicheng Lu, Weijie Li, Qiyong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune checkpoint inhibitors can cause isolated ACTH deficiency, a rare condition often misdiagnosed due to nonspecific symptoms. We report a cholangiocarcinoma patient on toripalimab and lenvatinib who experienced recurrent gastrointestinal symptom and precordial discomfort for 2 years, mistakenly attributed to drug toxicity or functional disorders. Following surgery, the patient developed refractory delayed gastric emptying, during which IAD was identified through negative imaging examinations, low ACTH and cortisol levels. Glucocorticoid replacement rapidly resolved symptoms. This case highlights that persistent unexplained somatic complaints in ICI-treated patients warrant endocrine evaluation to avoid diagnostic anchoring and recognize atypical presentations of adrenal insufficiency.Keywords: Immune checkpoint inhibitor, Isolated ACTH deficiency, Gastroparesis, Cortisol, Case report

Background

Malignant tumours represent a major global health burden, responsible for 19.3 million new cases and 10 million deaths worldwide in 2020, with the number of new cases projected to reach 28.4 million by 2040 [1]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized cancer treatment by targeting PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 pathways to enhance anti-tumour immunity [2]. However, ICIs can trigger a wide array of immune-related adverse events, among which isolated adrenocorticotropic hormone deficiency (IAD) is an uncommon ICIs-related endocrinopathy that is diagnostically challenging because its early manifestations, such as fatigue, nausea, anorexia and vague abdominal discomfort, are nonspecific and easily attributed to disease progression or concomitant targeted therapy [3]. Although it is a rare occurrence, given the massive and growing application to ICIs, the population of adrenal insufficiency caused by them is gradually increasing. This case report presents a long-misdiagnosised IAD patient with advanced cholangiocarcinoma who, during Toripalimab therapy, developed recurrent gastrointestinal symptom, precordial discomfort and ultimately postoperative gastroparesis. The trajectory that exposes the danger of diagnostic anchoring and provides a new perspective for diagnosis and management by underscoring the imperative to look beyond immediately plausible explanations and maintain a high index of suspicion for Systematic complication.

Case presentation

A 67-year-old male presented to our hospital on July 20, 2025, with a reducible mass at his upper abdominal incision site. He has a history of right bundle branch block, subtotal thyroidectomy surgery and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (cT4N2M1) in the past. His oncological history includes: in June 2023, he underwent exploratory celiotomy, followed by transarterial chemoembolization (TACE). Since July 2023, he had been receiving combination therapy with lenvatinib (8 mg QD) and toripalimab (240 mg Q3W), which effectively controlled his tumor but was associated with persistent abdominal discomfort, initially attributed to lenvatinib’s side effects. Over the subsequent two years, the patient repeatedly experienced a series of non-specific symptoms, mainly manifested as nausea and vomiting, as well as discomfort in the abdomen and the precordial area. The patient has no clear family history and has received good social and psychological support.

On admission, routine laboratory tests were largely unremarkable except for mild dyslipidemia and bradycardia (Average heart rate: 40 bpm). An abdominal CT scan confirmed the incisional hernia and showed stable post-treatment changes from his ICC. On July 28, 2025, he underwent a tension-free repair of the incisional hernia with temporary pacemaker support.

Postoperatively, the patient developed severe, refractory gastroparesis. He began vomiting on postoperative day (POD) 6, which did not respond to metoclopramide, domperidone and mosapride. A nasogastric tube was inserted on POD 7, yielding a daily output of 400–600 mL. An iodine-based contrast scan (3 hours after taking iodoflurane orally) on POD 7 ruled out mechanical obstruction, showing contrast passage into the jejunum and colon. Given the persistent symptoms, a jejunal feeding tube was placed on POD 9 for nutritional support. Although the diagnosis remained unconfirmed, the patient received prolonged symptomatic treatment. He tolerated a liquid diet as of August 25, and the nasogastric tube was removed on August 28. Subsequently, he gradually transitioned to semi-liquid diet, and the jejunal drainage tube was removed on September 9th. Despite these interventions, abdominal discomfort persisted.

The patient experienced extremely slow recovery from postoperative gastroparesis, and had a long history of using immune checkpoint inhibitors, the possibility of ICIs-related disease need be considered. We conducted a gastrointestinal hormone test, and the results showed that only an Increased of gastrin-17 was detected (19.95 pmol/L).

We conducted a comprehensive reassessment of the patient's symptomatology following the initiation of anti-ICC pharmacotherapy, which was predominantly characterized by chronic abdominal discomfort and precordial distress. A retrospective review of inpatient medical records from other departments and external institutions revealed a progressive decline in heart rate, while gastroscopy, coronary angiography, and ancillary investigations demonstrated no significant abnormalities (Figs. 1, 2 for details). Differential diagnoses encompassing metabolic disorders, adrenal insufficiency, thyroid dysfunction, and hypopituitarism were considered. However, the absence of clinically evident edema, the patient's established long-term levothyroxine supplementation, and consistently unremarkable serum potassium and sodium levels throughout the disease course rendered these etiologies less probable (Fig. 3 for details). Notwithstanding these considerations, a complete endocrine hormone panel and pituitary imaging studies were subsequently undertaken to rigorously exclude underlying endocrinopathy. Subsequent tests revealed no obvious pituitary changes in the head MRI and CT, but an 8:00 AM adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) level of 1.63 ng/L and an 8:00 AM plasma cortisol level of 10.8 nmol/L, thus the patient was diagnosed with IAD related to ICIs.

Subsequently, Prednisone 20 mg (8 AM)/10 mg (4 PM) was administered. The treatment effect was significant. Abdominal symptoms were relieved quickly after 1 week and the patient tolerated the treatment well without any adverse reactions. At the three-month follow-up, hormonal re-evaluation revealed an 8:00 AM ACTH level of 1.51 ng/L and a concurrent plasma cortisol level of 16.4 nmol/L. These findings are indicate the necessity for long-term glucocorticoid replacement therapy.

Discussion and conclusions

The most striking feature of this case is that a patient with advanced cholangiocarcinoma receiving toripalimab combined with lenvatinib therapy experienced recurrent episodes of precordial discomfort and abdominal distension over a two-year period, and was diagnosed with ICI-related adrenal insufficiency during the workup of refractory nausea and vomiting following incisional hernia repair. Symptoms rapidly resolved after glucocorticoid replacement therapy. This case not only illustrates the rare gastrointestinal manifestations of ICI-related endocrine adverse events (irAEs), but also reveals the history of misdiagnosis, carrying significant clinical warning and educational implications.

ICI-induced adrenal insufficiency primarily manifests in two forms: secondary adrenal insufficiency caused by hypophysitis, the more insidious IAD. Previous studies suggest that IAD predominantly occurs in elderly males and is associated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor therapy, with cranial MRI typically showing no significant abnormalities and a lower propensity to precipitate adrenal crisis [4–6]. In this case, we observed only a reduction in adrenocorticotropic hormone and cortisol, while other hormones (such as TSH, PRL, LH, GH, and so on) remained normal or showed no significant elevation, and pituitary enlargement was not evident on imaging.

Postoperative gastrointestinal dysmotility is a common multifactorial complication following abdominal surgery, potentially associated with surgical trauma, vagal nerve injury, inflammatory mediators, and opioid use [7]. In this case, the patient underwent incisional hernia repair, the injury to the gastrointestinal tract or vagus is considered unlikely, although it cannot be entirely excluded. Opioid analgesics administered under general anesthesia may also contribute to reduced gastrointestinal motility [8]. Additionally, Lenvatinib may induce gastrointestinal dysmotility through its anti-angiogenic effects on the intestinal vasculature [9]. Although the temporal relationship between glucocorticoid replacement therapy and symptom improvement is highly suggestive, multiple factors may have contributed to the patient's gastrointestinal dysfunction. Accordingly, we propose that IAD likely played a contributory or exacerbating role in the gastrointestinal certain extent, rather than being the sole etiological factor.

Research has demonstrated that cortisol significantly influences gastrointestinal motility, with both elevated and reduced cortisol levels leading to gastrointestinal dysmotility [10, 11]. In this case, the gastroparesis delayed gastric emptying may have been exacerbated by the combination of surgical stress and underlying cortisol deficiency. Concurrent gastrointestinal hormone testing further elucidated cortisol's impact on the gut. In cortisol deficiency, reduced gastric acid secretion weakens the acid-mediated negative feedback inhibition in the gastric antrum, leading to compensatory hyperplasia of G cells and increased gastrin release [12, 13]. Thus, the level of gastrin-17 was elevated, while PG I, PG II, and PG I/II ratios remain normal. Cortisol deficiency may also alter visceral sensitivity and the central processing of discomfort signals. This could provide a neurobiological basis for the patient's persistent complaints of “abdomen and the precordial discomfort”.

From symptom onset to definitive diagnosis, this patient endured over 2 years (Fig. 4 for details). We believe there are three reasons that have led to the delayed diagnosis of this disease: First, IAD is relatively less common than other diseases in the treatment with ICI drugs; Second, the symptom spectrum of IAD is extremely nonspecific [8]. Symptoms such as fatigue, nausea, vomiting, epigastric and xiphoid discomfort, and weight loss are often readily attributed to malignancy, treatment side effects, or adhesive intestinal obstruction; Third, The hospital administering the medication lacked the necessary systematic assessment, clinicians typically consider endocrine disorders only when symptoms progress to classic adrenal crisis (for example, hypotensive shock, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia).

Diagnostic medical history

In clinical practice, ICI-associated gastrointestinal dysmotility is rarely reported and often co-occurs with other irAEs. Cases where IAD is identified as the primary driver at initial presentation are exceedingly rare. The uniqueness of this case lies in several aspects: (1) before the diagnosis of IAD, the patient primarily presented with abdominal and precordial discomfort. While a potential association between these two manifestations, it remains challenging to establish a definitive causal relationship; (2) we found a rare manifestation of a abnormity in gastric hormones after glucocorticoid deficiency; (3) the diagnostic journey evolved from intestinal obstruction, lenvatinib adverse effects, and cardiac neurosis, forming a pattern of "symptomatically plausible, investigationally negative, and etiologically reasonable" misdiagnoses.

Several limitations of this case report should be acknowledged. First, the diagnosis of gastroparesis in this patient was based on clinical features rather than on the formal gastric emptying test. Therefore, the diagnostic evidence is limited and other conditions cannot be ruled out. Second, the natural relief of the patient's gastrointestinal symptoms may overlap with the use of glucocorticoid replacement therapy. We can only confirm that there is a certain connection between the two, and since the discontinuation or reduction of the dosage of glucocorticoids may pose risks to the patient's clinical condition, it is difficult for us to establish a clear causal relationship between the two.

This case demonstrates that IAD may be associated with predominantly gastrointestinal symptoms in patients who receiving ICIs. Key clinical lessons include: (1) persistent unexplained symptoms in ICI-treated patients warrant systematic endocrine evaluation; (2) the absence of classic adrenal insufficiency features does not exclude the diagnosis; (3) diagnostic anchoring on more common etiologies can lead to significant delays in management. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for irAEs when encountering refractory symptoms in patients on immunotherapy.
