Boldly going where no one has gone before: a history of peroral endoscopic myotomy in the United States
Authors: Stavros N Stavropoulos, Phillip S Ge
Published: 2024

Stavros N. Stavropoulos, MD, FASGE, AGAF, FJGES

Becoming a pioneer in medicine takes courage, dedication, and sometimes a bit of daring. In the early 2000s, a young junior faculty practicing in New York City decided to boldly push the frontiers of endoscopy into the realm of third-space endoscopy, or endoscopic surgery. He was far ahead of his time, successfully performing novel procedures that had just barely been invented across the world in Japan and introducing them to the United States, gaining fame and perhaps a bit of notoriety along the way. We are therefore excited to tell the story of Stavros Stavropoulos, MD, FASGE, who is our distinguished guest for the Historical Considerations section of iGIE.

Dr Stavropoulos grew up in Greece and came to the United States after high school, earning his bachelor’s degree in molecular biophysics and biochemistry at Yale University and subsequently his medical degree at Columbia University College of Physicians and Surgeons. After serving multiple years as junior faculty at Columbia University Medical Center, he relocated to Long Island, becoming Chief of Endoscopy and Director of the Program in Advanced Endoscopy at Winthrop University Hospital (presently known as NYU Langone Hospital–Long Island).

It was at Winthrop that Dr Stavropoulos became internationally renowned for his pioneering contributions to endoscopy and endoscopic surgery, becoming the first operator in the United States to perform peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM). Dr Stavropoulos was also a pioneer in endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), submucosal tunneling endoscopic resection, endoscopic full-thickness resection, and other variations of POEM, including gastric POEM (G-POEM) and POEM for Zenker's diverticulum. It was also during those years that Dr Stavropoulos founded the Long Island Live endoscopy course, thereby establishing his institution as a mecca for training an entire generation of likeminded early adopters in third-space endoscopy.

Dr Stavropoulos is currently leading the program of endoscopic surgery and third-space endoscopy at the Mount Sinai South Nassau Hospital. He is a fellow of the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE), American Gastroenterological Association, and New York Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy; a member of the Society of American Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons, and one of a small number of international endoscopists with honorary fellowship to the Japanese Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy, one of the oldest and most revered endoscopy societies in the world.

Phillip S. Ge (P.S.G.): Dr Stavropoulos, thank you so much for taking the time to do this interview. It is truly an honor and privilege. Tell us a little about your own background and training in endoscopy.

I started medical school at Columbia University College of Physicians and Surgeons with the intent of becoming a neurosurgeon, an idea that took hold in my teenage years after I read an inspiring novel centered on a larger-than-life neurosurgeon and his exploits. During medical school, I veered toward neurology and then cardiology. This was because of the influence of mentors that considered these cognitive specialties better suited to “cerebral individuals” (such as they presumed me to be) compared with surgical specialties. Thus, I pursued an internal medicine residency at Columbia-Presbyterian, where I rapidly realized that I enjoyed procedures such as lumbar punctures, Swan-Ganz catheter insertions, and arterial line insertions far more than the “cognitive” aspects of internal medicine.

I frantically sought an escape toward surgery. Specialties that would have allowed me to use my intern year in internal medicine included dermatology and ophthalmology, both highly competitive specialties requiring research and publications. I applied to New York ophthalmology programs and was fortunate to match at St Luke’s-Roosevelt Hospital. However, in the meantime during a gastroenterology rotation as a second-year resident at Columbia, I was exposed to ERCP, EUS, and other advanced endoscopic procedures performed by Charles J. Lightdale, MD, MASGE, and the late Peter D. Stevens, MD. It appeared to me that these minimally invasive surgical procedures encompassed a much broader spectrum of organs and conditions than ophthalmologic procedures. Furthermore, Pete’s enthusiasm for these procedures was contagious.

This ended my pursuit of ophthalmology as I focused on securing a gastroenterology fellowship at Columbia. To that purpose, I engaged in research in the nascent field of celiac disease with Peter H. Green, MD. I put together a comprehensive survey that I then went to extreme efforts to distribute as widely as possible (including standing at the door of local celiac group meetings and distributing copies of the survey). I was a victim of my own success, having to then input many pages of data from hundreds of returned surveys. Peter finally agreed to end data collection at 1612 surveys (and while there were still bags full of mailed surveys coming to his office every week). At that point, given the large population, not even the first decimal digit was changing on the analyzed data (which is what finally convinced Peter to relent). This led to the publication of a seminal study that is highly cited and included as the standard celiac disease reference in board review courses such as the Steinberg course.1

There was very little time for nongoal-directed activities and very little inclination for getting into trouble, as punishment was swift and inescapable. The result of these parental and school influences was top of the class performance in high school and college combined with a reticent, introverted demeanor. This demeanor along with the top grades appears to have deluded my peers into thinking I was some sort of eccentric, withdrawn genius as they named me “most likely to win the Nobel Prize” at graduations. Obviously, I proved them wrong by pursuing clinical medicine (to the disappointment of my college mentors who considered this a “waste”) with accomplishments that unfortunately are far short of a Nobel Prize. Still, I found great satisfaction and fulfillment in clinical medicine.

Coming to the “risk-taking” question, my pioneering efforts in advanced endoscopy were not driven by a propensity for “thrill-seeking” or “risk-taking” behavior but rather by the need to continue to derive intellectual stimulation from a manual task such as endoscopy that can become repetitive and stale in the absence of a constant push for innovation. Innovation, however, always followed thoughtful planning and preparatory work to maximize patient safety. My first EUS-guided rendezvous procedure in 2005 and my first transgastric stent into the liver in 2007 were preceded by thousands of tertiary and quaternary EUS and ERCP procedures. Similarly, my first POEM in October 2009 was preceded by ESD in animals and humans for the prior 5 years from 2004 to 2009. My first endoscopic full-thickness resection and submucosal tunneling endoscopic resection procedures were in 2012 after over 100 ESDs and 50 POEMs. Even these very advanced procedures can be performed safely if they are planned meticulously and are performed after the requisite experience has been obtained. I believe this is reflected in my characterization by my mentor Pete Stevens as a “thinking endoscopist.” I take this characterization as a compliment (although some surgeon friends told me the term “thinking surgeon” is often a euphemism for a slow surgeon). The main thing to always keep in mind is to heed the Hippocratic Oath of “doing no harm.”

Beginnings of Third-Space Endoscopy

P.S.G.: It’s always interesting to hear people’s stories about how third-space endoscopy arrived in the United States. Tell me your story. How did you get exposed to third-space endoscopy?

I was first exposed to third-space endoscopy at the annual New York Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (NYSGE) live course, this most festive of live endoscopy courses, held at Times Square a week before Christmas and memorably opened by Christopher Gostout, MD, MASGE, dressed as an elf on one occasion. The most impressive live endoscopy case of the 2003 NYSGE annual course was performed by Naohisa Yahagi, MD, one of the preeminent Japanese ESD pioneers and masters. He resected en bloc a giant gastric laterally spreading lesion using the just-invented ESD technique (this was before his development of the first ESD knives, the flex and dual knives; his early ESDs were performed with the tip of a snare). The procedure, performed with Yahagi’s trademark elegance even that early in his learning curve, lasted about 8 hours (from the early morning, through the lunch break, and into the late afternoon), an astonishing procedure duration in those days 2 decades ago, before the advent of “extreme endoscopy” and endoscopic surgery. This procedure was undoubtedly the first live demonstration of ESD in the United States and, in fact, almost certainly, the first ESD performed in the United States. It is important to note here that the first pioneering publications of ESD had appeared in Japan, ESD’s birthplace, only about 3 to 4 years before this live demonstration!2, 3, 4, 5

P.S.G.: What was the audience reaction to Dr Yahagi’s live case? What was your reaction, and how did it influence your subsequent career development?

This amazing multihour live ESD by Yahagi generated strong but divergent reactions among the large and varied audience of 700+ attendees ranging from private practitioners to trainees to academic advanced endoscopists, surgeons, gastroenterology nurses, and techs. Most NYSGE course participants were shocked by the day-long case and were of the opinion that a laparoscopic gastrectomy would have been “much shorter and easier” (obviously glossing over the detrimental effects on the patient of a subtotal gastrectomy for this benign premalignant lesion). I belonged to the smaller portion of the audience that was amazed and excited by this incredible demonstration of the power of endoscopic microsurgery, the power to deliver cure for early neoplasms noninvasively with organ preservation. This eye-opening case occurred at a time in my career when “traditional advanced endoscopy,” ERCP in particular, was beginning to be less stimulating in terms of exploring new frontiers (not surprising after close to 2000 ERCPs at that point). This yearning to explore new frontiers beyond ERCP probably also underlies my first EUS-guided rendezvous procedure in 2005 (presented at the NYSGE fellows night by my then-fellow Christopher J. DiMaio, MD, FASGE, to great acclaim) and my first transgastric self-expandable metal stent into the liver in 2007.

P.S.G.: Speaking of which, let’s talk about your training and early career at Columbia University.

I was fortunate to have been trained in advanced endoscopy by Charlie Lightdale and Pete Stevens for 1.5 years (half of the second year and all of the third year of my general gastroenterology fellowship) and then during an additional year with Pete as the first Columbia “fourth-year fellow” in 2001 to 2002 (Fig. 2). As discussed already, at Columbia at that time, Pete performed 800+ tertiary- and quaternary-level ERCPs per year, which basically translated to my finishing advanced fellowship and starting as Pete’s junior partner in mid-2002 with more than 1500 ERCPs under my belt.

Advanced endoscopy with the late Peter D. Stevens, MD. Discussing a case in room 2 (the ERCP room) at Columbia-Presbyterian Hospital, March 2003 (my first year at Columbia as Pete’s junior interventional partner).

Notably, during my advanced fellowship in 2001 and 2002, Pete and I constantly tested new frontiers. In the summer of 2001, we performed what may be the first EUS-guided transluminal biliary stent placement cases in the United States. In back-to-back ERCP cases, 1 memorable Friday evening we performed an EUS-guided double-pigtail stent placement between a dilated bile duct and the duodenal bulb in 2 patients with large masses obliterating the major papilla and obstructing the duodenum. One patient was a well-known museum curator who had an excellent course, never requiring any further biliary intervention until her death 2 years later (she did have some minor postoperative issues in the first 48 hours with pneumoperitoneum because of the use of air at that time for insufflation). The other patient was an uninsured Rastafarian guitarist in a local band who had not sought medical care until he presented emaciated with a body mass index of 15 and a giant 7-cm head of pancreas mass. Unfortunately, after our procedure, he developed a persistent bile duct and duodenal leak that eventually led to his demise in the hospital 3 to 4 weeks later from fungal peritonitis.

P.S.G.: It takes great courage to be on the cutting edge of innovation, with risks both foreseen and unforeseen. What impact did these early cases have on your subsequent development?

I remember that the latter case was really traumatic to Pete, who prided himself in having what was likely the highest safety record of any advanced endoscopist exploring the most advanced frontiers of advanced endoscopy including early NOTES procedures (first transvaginal cholecystectomy in the United States in 2007 along with Marc Bessler, MD, covered by the New York Times). This high safety record was achieved with a chess-like approach to advanced endoscopy that I learned and adopted from him, consisting of methodical incremental adoption of new techniques with careful risk management including well-thought-through “exit strategies” for any possible adverse events, however remote they might be. At that time, he astutely attributed the bad outcome in this patient to “group think” between the 2 of us based on the classic bestselling book by this title that had been published just a year before and that he had just read. Although Pete never again performed any transluminal biliary interventions from 2001 until his untimely death from prostate cancer in 2011, I was able to learn from these lessons and cautiously resume exploration of these techniques with a rendezvous procedure in 2005 and metal stent hepaticogastrostomy in 2007 followed by many more such procedures, leading to a pioneering combined 2-center series of EUS-guided biliary access from Winthrop and Johns Hopkins published in Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (GIE) in 2013 with Mouen A. Khashab, MD, MASGE.6

P.S.G.: And it appears that these early lessons propelled your subsequent pioneering successes in your field, especially with regard to third-space endoscopy. Tell me about your early ESD experience. By the way, what a lot of people may not realize is that ESD was invented before POEM, even though many who wander into third-space endoscopy may erroneously believe it was the other way around.

I welcomed the promise of a whole new field of advanced endoscopy open for exploration ushered by Yahagi’s live ESD at NYSGE 2003. In 2004, I started performing ESD initially in a live animal model and shortly thereafter in patients with gastric adenomas (few as those are in the United States) (Fig. 3). My initial efforts were guided by information from Japanese publications and the relatively limited early videos from Japanese ESD pioneers. It is worth pointing out here that in 2004 even the Japanese experts were early in their learning curve, and ESD was limited to gastric lesions, which are very rare in the United States. ESD for colon lesions (the most abundant lesions in the West) did not become approved in Japan until much later, in 2012, which was also about the time when the popularity of POEM kindled broader interest in ESD in the United States and led to “pilgrimages to Japan” by the first generation of early adopters that followed the initial group of pioneers (eg, Peter V. Draganov, MD, MASGE, and Andrew Y. Wang, MD, FASGE). Pioneers, however, including Sergey Kantsevoy, MD, Mainor R. Antillon, MD (who published the first case report of ESD in the United States in GIE in 2008 on a rectal lesion7), and myself were largely self-taught, closely following the early efforts of Japanese pioneers. My first trip to Japan actually did not occur until 2018 as invited faculty to the prestigious Tokyo Live course, where I had the honor to perform the first live ESD by a Westerner in a Japanese live course (Fig. 4).

I persevered in my ESD efforts in those early years with an increasing number of ESDs from 2004 to 2009 as I incorporated more and more superficial subepithelial tumors as ESD enucleation targets to supplement the meager gastric lesions available in the United States. These multihour cases caused some consternation at Columbia from 2005 to 2007. I recall Pete Stevens walking into room 2 one day as I was struggling with bleeding 2 hours into a gastric ESD of a 2- to 3-cm adenoma in the antrum and jokingly telling me to just snare the darn thing and move on and wondering why I am even bothering with ESD when we do not have appropriate gastric lesions in the United States, and Olympus Corporation (Tokyo, Japan) had asserted that they would not make the knives available in the United States. A classic “never say never” situation as it turned out. At the end of 2007, I moved to Winthrop University Hospital (presently known as NYU Langone Hospital–Long Island) in Mineola, New York, and 2009 was the seminal year where all these early ESD efforts yielded fruit with the advent of POEM.

P.S.G.: So you didn’t even have any official ESD knives! Tell us more about how you did these early ESD cases. Did you have to make your own knives?

One of the major impediments of pioneering ESD efforts in the United States was the unavailability of any dedicated ESD knives. Dedicated ESD knives did not become available in the United States until 2011. Therefore, from 2004 to 2011, improvisation was the order of the day. During this period, in conjunction with my fellow Sven Hida, MD, and a friendly dentist (Jerome Wolfert, DMD), we constructed our own IT knives by attaching to the tip of an ERCP needle-knife a photopolymerizing hard dental enamel resin (Fig. 5). This worked reasonably well but was hampered by the very thin metallic shaft of the typical ERCP precut knife. The game-changer was my discovery deep in the Olympus U.S. catalog of a reusable (autoclavable) ERCP needle-knife (with autoclavable handle) that was thicker than traditional ERCP precut knives (Fig. 6). The traditional thin ERCP precut knives generated very high current densities, even when coagulation currents were used, which resulted in very little hemostasis. Furthermore, these thin precut knives disintegrated when high-voltage currents such as spray coagulation (Erbe, Marietta, Ga, USA) were used for any length of time. In contrast, the thicker Olympus reusable knife behaved exactly like a dedicated ESD knife such as the Olympus dual knife. Furthermore, it came in 2 shapes: cylindrical “round” and spatula-like “flat” resembling the spatula-like knives preferred by surgeons for delicate surgical dissections. This “flat” knife became my exclusive ESD knife and in fact was the knife I used in the first POEM in the United States in 2009 (2 years before ESD knives became commercially available in the United States).

Homemade insulated-tip knife that I created with gastroenterology fellow Sven Hida, MD, and the assistance of dentist, Jerome Wolfert, DMD. We used an ERCP needle-knife with an insulating small sphere at its tip created and attached using photopolymerizing dental composite resin, which hardens upon exposure to a monochromatic light source.

Olympus reusable, thicker ERCP needle-knives (Tokyo, Japan) that proved ideal for endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) and peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM; with superior electrosurgical performance compared with the earlier homemade knife shown in Fig. 5). These knives, particularly the “flat-type” knife, became the main knives for POEM and ESD in my practice until dedicated ESD knives became available in the United States in 2011. They were also very inexpensive because the knives and handle could be autoclaved and reused.

P.S.G.: Experimental procedures. Improvised devices. You were incredibly bold to have started doing all of this in New York City, which is arguably a high-risk medicolegal and business environment. How did you manage to practice in such a challenging environment and then subsequently channel that energy into the renowned Long Island Live course that you launched?

This was a remarkable thing to do in New York, considering the high-risk medicolegal environment, and I am grateful to the early support from the NYSGE, which encouraged innovation even when it involved live demonstration of cutting-edge high-risk pioneering procedures performed by international pioneers from all over the world. The NYSGE live course’s uncompromising focus under the leadership of Gregory B. Haber, MD, MASGE, an incredible endoscopist, promoting innovation with live cases at the edge of the envelope of advanced endoscopy, had an important influence on me in these formative years as a fellow and junior attending in the NYSGE. It emboldened my natural proclivity to adopt the latest technology and techniques and ultimately became the guiding principle for the course that I started at Winthrop in Long Island in 2008, “Long Island Live-Frontiers of Endoscopy,” a course that became the pre-eminent live third-space course in the United States and one of the most valuable educational resources in the United States in the early years of POEM and ESD adoption.

Beginnings of POEM in the United States

P.S.G.: Your early experience with ESD arguably put you in perfect position to adopt POEM, so let’s talk about POEM. You were the first in the United States to perform this procedure, which occurred very shortly after Haruhiro Inoue, MD, performed the first case worldwide. Tell us about the series of events that led up to this historical moment.

My first exposure to POEM was during a 2009 Digestive Disease Week (DDW) dinner organized by Ioannis “John” Katsogridakis, MD, FASGE, an international “patron of advanced endoscopy” so to speak, an international fellow of the ASGE, and organizer of the biennial Athens International Live Endoscopy course. He was also intimately known to all the titans of therapeutic endoscopy in the world, many of whom were memorialized in paintings by Nelly, his wife, an accomplished painter. John is close friends with Jerome D. Waye, MD, MASGE, and Nib Soehendra, MD, to mention just 2 of those titans, and had the honor of being invited in the small group celebrating Nib’s retirement in Hamburg, Germany.

I met John in 2006 when he introduced himself after a lecture I gave at the annual NYSGE course. He was a generous man who organized lavish dinners at hard-to-get restaurants for his famous advanced endoscopy friends converging on DDW every year. At the 2009 DDW in Chicago, Ill, the lucky invitees (and I was one, not because of any distinction but as a close friend of John’s) were treated to dinner at an exclusive just-opened restaurant in Chicago called Tru (Fig. 7). I brought Alberto Larghi, MD, and my wife along, and the other invitees included Thierry Ponchon, MD, Haruhiro Inoue, MD, FASGE, Hironori Yamamoto, MD, FASGE, and other endoscopy masters. I fortuitously sat next to Inoue, and when I asked him what he was presenting at that year’s DDW, he went on to describe in detail and with great excitement his newly invented POEM technique that he was going to present at the video forum 2 days later. He graciously answered questions from the unknown novice next to him and even reassured me that the technique is not especially challenging for an operator with ESD experience. Two days later, I made sure to catch Inoue’s presentation at the DDW video forum where I was shocked and awed along with the rest of the audience.

P.S.G.: Shortly thereafter, you started looking for your own first case. Tell me about the first POEM case in the United States.

The fortuitous turns of fate continued when 4 months later in August 2009 I received a call from a private-practice gastroenterologist in Long Island, Christopher Demetriou, MD, to perform pneumatic dilation on a treatment-naïve, healthy 42-year-old man with newly diagnosed achalasia. This was the first time in nearly a decade of practicing advanced endoscopy that I had gotten a referral of an achalasia patient!

It was fate at work. I explained to the patient that even though POEM was a new procedure I had not previously performed, I had performed a similar procedure (ESD) over the past 5 years that in some ways was more challenging than POEM, and I could attempt POEM with surgical backup by a minimally invasive expert surgeon for conversion to Heller if needed. The patient enthusiastically agreed. I contacted the Institutional Review Board at Winthrop. The Winthrop Institutional Review Board chairman (then chief of oncology Harry Staszewski, MD), who was familiar with my performance of very advanced high-risk procedures at Winthrop from 2007 to 2009 in oncology patients (EUS-guided rendezvous, EUS-guided hepaticogastrostomies, etc) with very high level of safety, after conferring with the chief of gastroenterology (James H. Grendell, MD) agreed that we could proceed under well-controlled conditions.

These conditions included the first 40 cases performed in the operating room with a surgeon in attendance and ready to operate. An outstanding minimally invasive surgeon, Collin E. Brathwaite, MD, who was the up-and-coming director of the Bariatric Center, agreed to provide surgical backup. Thus, the first POEM outside of Japan was scheduled for October 14, 2009, at Winthrop Hospital in Long Island.

P.S.G.: You’ve always been a master of preparation, influenced from your days working with Pete Stevens at Columbia. How did you prepare for this pioneering first case?

About 2 weeks before the procedure, I had planned to attend the Toronto Live therapeutic endoscopy course at St Michael’s Hospital. At the faculty reception of the course, I saw Chris Gostout and approached him to pick his brain about the upcoming POEM because, as an integral part of the Apollo group investigating NOTES procedures, he had co-invented with Kazuki Sumiyama, MD, PhD, FASGE, the submucosal tunnel concept and along with Pankaj Jay Pasricha, MD, FASGE, and the rest of the group had applied this concept to an endoscopic lower esophageal sphincter myotomy in a pig model (published in 2007).8,9

As an aside, I had met Chris Gostout at a wild NYSGE postcourse faculty party in 2005 or 2006 and apparently made an impression on him. With all of us quite inebriated, I remember him pointing to me and shouting, “This guy, used in a human our Barrett’s stripping technique!” referring to me applying to a Barrett’s esophagus patient at Columbia a technique they had developed at Mayo Clinic on a porcine model for rapid Barrett’s eradication.10 The technique involved making vertical mucosal incisions through the entire length of the Barrett’s esophagus with a needle-knife, followed by circumferential incisions at the top and bottom of the segment, and then grasping these strips along the proximal margin with a grasper and stripping them free by forcibly pushing the endoscope distally until the entire strip of Barrett’s was “skinned!” Clearly, this was a much less-demanding technique than ESD that appeared to work reasonably well in the relatively avascular pig esophagus, but not so well in the much more vascular human esophagus as I discovered (aborting this innovative approach and converting to later standard ablation on that particular patient).

Possibly because of this prior memorable interaction, Chris did not bat an eyelid when I told him that I had booked the first POEM in the United States for the following week and was seeking his advice. He was extremely enthusiastic and told me that I did not have to worry about just cutting only the circular muscle layer as recommended by Inoue in our conversation at the Chicago DDW 2009 dinner a few months earlier. He argued that a complete myotomy would be easier, which was in fact true and became our standard technique. He also suggested that I use the small CO2 canisters available in electronic stores to blow dust off delicate electronics and connect them to a submucosal injection needle to rapidly dissect the submucosa using high-pressure CO2 insufflation in the submucosa rather than the pain-staking ESD dissection technique. After buying these small CO2 canisters and bringing them to the operating room, I ultimately decided not to use them (which was fortunate because in retrospect this method would probably have resulted in much wider and less-predictable submucosal dissection than desired).

P.S.G.: So how did the POEM case go?

The POEM mucosal incision and myotomy was performed with the Olympus flat knife as mentioned above, but the entirety of the tunnel was created by blunt insertion in the submucosa of a controlled radial expansion dilation balloon followed by inflation of the balloon to 12 mm, which immediately created 5.5 cm of tunnel (the length of the balloon) with a 12-mm diameter that could accommodate the diagnostic gastroscope used. With 3 sequential applications, an approximately 15-cm tunnel was created in a few minutes. This was the same method described in the seminal study on a porcine model by Pasricha and Gostout et al in 2007.9 This technique resulted in what remains to this date the fastest POEM tunnel creation time in the well over 900 POEMs I have performed so far since we subsequently switched to the slower (but safer and more predictable) technique of submucosal dissection for tunnel creation. (The detailed data on the first 610 POEMs that I performed from 2009 to 2019 were published in 2021 in what remains the largest single-operator Western series.11)

Anyways, the POEM was successfully completed with excellent clinical efficacy and published in GIE in 2010 as a case report, representing the second POEM publication in the world (after Inoue’s series of 17 POEMs in Endoscopy earlier that year).12 Notably, the acronym POEM does not appear in the title of our publication or as a keyword because Inoue had not settled on this acronym yet at the time of submission of our publication.

Harris, MD, presented the case as a video presentation at NYSGE fellows night 1 month after its performance on November 17, 2009 (Fig. 8). Brandt, MD, I had Mike Harris practically memorize Sleisenger and Fordtran’s chapter on achalasia, expecting the detail-oriented “grilling” that Larry is notorious for. Well, we needn’t have worried. POEM, NOTES, and the Future

P.S.G.: Achalasia is a rare disorder, and the advent and popularization of POEM does not change this fundamental fact. Thus, it is interesting that the development of POEM has had an outsized influence on the field, with far-reaching impacts beyond achalasia. As a young and casual observer, I believe this has to do with POEM being really the first successful NOTES procedure. Tell us about the impact that POEM had on the field.

POEM was the first procedure to achieve the ideal of NOTES, that is, performing a traditional surgical procedure (laparoscopic Heller myotomy in this case) using a less-invasive, natural orifice endoscopic approach with equal or superior outcomes. As such, POEM had a revolutionary impact on endoscopy by establishing the field of endoscopic surgery, giving us offshoots such as POEM for Zenker's diverticulum and G-POEM and even, interestingly, jump-starting ESD adoption in the West.

The importance of POEM was recognized immediately and was rapidly promoted through the efforts of the NOSCAR committee, a unique hybrid committee composed of thought leaders from the Society of American Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons and the ASGE, originally assembled to guide development of traditional NOTES. Through strong advocacy by Michael L. Kochman, MD, MASGE, and Daniel J. Scott, MD, FACS, to the Governing Boards of the ASGE and Society of American Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons, respectively, we were able to publish a comprehensive white paper on POEM simultaneously in both society journals (GIE and Surgical Endoscopy) barely 3 1/2 years after the first human POEM in the United States.14,15 Much of the material in this white paper was painstakingly collected by a very detailed international POEM survey (iPOEMS) sent to all 19 pioneering POEM centers from around the world in 2012.13 Drawing from my celiac disease experience, Thomas J. Savides, MD, MASGE, and I put together a very detailed survey that included 240 questions in 16 subsections covering every aspect of POEM. Fortunately, by 2012 we were able to collect and analyze the data online rather than manually entering handwritten mailed surveys like I did back in 1997 for my celiac disease project!1

The strong support from NOSCAR helped bypass the usual initial resistance to disruptive technology by “the establishment.” Such resistance was clearly evident in other quarters from 2009 to 2017, most prominently among influential senior Heller surgeons and surgical society leadership as well as insurance companies and their medical directors. It is worth noting here that because of this resistance, from about 2014 to 2017 insurance companies had declared POEM investigational, which resulted in denial of coverage for many patients. During that dark period, most of us spent inordinate amounts of time and effort on peer reviews and appeals that often still resulted in ultimate denial of coverage by “peer reviewers” ignorant of or inimical to POEM. Consequently, POEM volume dipped during those 3 years before insurance coverage, and ultimately Current Procedural Terminology coding was established (Fig. 20).

My peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM) volume at Winthrop from 2009 to 2020 (slide from Digestive Disease Week 2021 oral presentation of our POEM series). This figure shows about 30% suppression of our volume (from ∼100 to ∼70 POEMs per year) during the period of insurance denials from 2014 to 2017, even higher than the 20% suppression by COVID-19 endoscopy closures in 2020!

I should also note that the NYSGE leadership likewise strongly supported new techniques and technologies. Charlie Lightdale, in particular, had always kept a close eye and an open mind for new technologies, including the burgeoning third-space field. In 2004, he asked me for a video of one of my earliest ESDs to include in his Barrett’s lectures, and in 2012 at an NYSGE annual course satellite seminar he attempted to pacify Philip O. Katz, MD (then visiting faculty from Einstein in Philadelphia, a great academic gastroenterologist and not one to mince words) who castigated me during my lecture on ESD where I presented a video of an ESD for a Barrett’s nodule with intramucosal carcinoma. At that time, Phil, not unreasonably, deemed ESD dangerous and nonsuperior to EMR. Fortunately, he has since warmed up to ESD (and me), and we have collaborated, most recently on American Foregut Society projects. Charlie, co-developer of EUS and always a visionary, recognized the revolutionary impact of POEM and approached me in 2015 to serve as guest editor for a volume of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy Clinics of North America dedicated to third space and endoscopic surgery and boldly titled “The New NOTES” (Fig. 21).16

The April 2016 issue of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy Clinics of North America dedicated to “The New NOTES.” This term, in contrast to the more prosaic “third-space endoscopy,” captures the surgical nature of these procedures and pays homage to their origins in groundbreaking traditional NOTES research. This early research that proved, for example, the relative unimportance of sterility in endoscopic surgery and gave us ingenious techniques such as tunnel endoscopy, made procedures such as peroral endoscopic myotomy and all its offshoots possible. NOTES, Natural orifice translumenal endoscopic surgery.

To quote from the preface to that volume:

“In the ‘post mortem’ of traditional NOTES however, we should remember and appreciate that it is largely the vision and pioneering work of the luminaries of ‘traditional NOTES’ that led to novel techniques (most prominently submucosal endoscopy) and devices (most prominently endoscopic suturing) that are fueling the ‘New NOTES’ revolution. This issue focuses on this exciting rebirth of NOTES in the form of ‘short-range’ endoscopic interventions that breach the gastrointestinal tract wall but stay close to it: interventions such as POEM, submucosal tunnel endoscopic resection (STER), endoscopic full-thickness resection (EFTR), and per-oral pyloromyotomy (POP or GPOEM). Whereas traditional NOTES, with its grand vision of deep incursions into the abdominal and chest cavities and major organ resections, failed to gain wide adoption, it planted the seeds for the ‘New NOTES’ procedures, which are thriving and enjoying rapid growth. These ‘New NOTES’ interventions are finally delivering on the great promise of NOTES, replacing traditional surgical procedures with minimally invasive, scarless ones.”

POEM offshoots rapidly developed within a few years of the first human POEM. G-POEM deserves particular mention because it illustrates the flurry of global collaboration sparked by POEM. The first human G-POEM was performed at Johns Hopkins by Haruhiro Inoue in April 2013 with myself and Mouen Khashab as assistants (published as a case report in GIE in 2013) (Fig. 22).17

Johns Hopkins, April 19, 2013. First human G-POEM. Operator H. Inoue. Assistants M. Khashab and S. Stavropoulos.

P.S.G.: In your opinion, what does the future hold for POEM and third-space endoscopy?

What does the future hold? At some point, we may be able to revisit “traditional NOTES” procedures such as peroral cholecystectomy as we now have operators with extensive experience in endoscopic surgery (particularly tissue resection and hemostasis with endoscopic implements, most notably ESD knives), a critical deficiency in the early days of NOTES. This may be further enabled by new technologies, most promising of which may be flexible robotic endoscopic platforms. Recent reports by ESD experts such as Pinghong Zhou of purely endoscopic natural orifice appendectomy and cholecystectomy at high volumes are in line with this prediction.18,19 In an encore performance of a “stuff of legends” procedure at Long Island Live, in 2021, 8 years after his legendary submucosal tunneling endoscopic resection in 2013, Pinghong Zhou performed a live NOTES cholecystectomy with Greg Haber and myself as moderators (Fig. 23). As we attempt to expand the boundaries of natural orifice endoscopic surgery in the future, I expect that a major enabling technology will be flexible endoscopic robotic platforms currently in developmental stages.

The stuff of legends, 8 years later, again at Long Island Live, where Pinghong Zhou, MD, FASGE, demonstrated NOTES cholecystectomy in August 2021. NOTES, Natural orifice translumenal endoscopic surgery.

P.S.G.: Despite the vision of pioneers like yourself and others, there are always threats to innovation. In your view, what are the factors that hold back innovation in endoscopy?

There is a constant looming threat to innovation in endoscopy and other fields in clinical medicine. Health care in the United States is increasingly dominated by entities with a short-sighted business approach to health care, which represents a major threat to innovation. Innovation often involves “moon-shot” projects (such as POEM in 2008) that require investment in time and resources without a guarantee of return on this investment, at least not in the short term, and carry increased medicolegal risks. Adoption of new techniques such as POEM or ESD is complicated by insurers that often decline coverage of innovative procedures until there is incontrovertible evidence of their safety and efficacy, but such evidence cannot be collected without someone funding performance of these procedures during their development and early adoption—a “Catch-22” conundrum.

These challenges beg the question of who will fund these procedures during their development and early adoption phases. In the past, academic centers proudly nurtured innovation. However, increasingly they have abdicated this role in clinical medicine where major grants, particularly the large and plentiful government grants for basic science and translational projects, are nonexistent. These institutions increasingly focus on maximizing clinical revenue from established procedures rather than “taking risks” with new procedures or procedures in developmental stages that do not generate substantial revenue. Academic clinicians are now expected to cover all or most of their salary through highly remunerative clinical activities (to the point of becoming indistinguishable from their colleagues working in private practices).

Therefore, academic activities such as research and education and performance of poorly reimbursing innovative procedures such as ESD are essentially financed by the physicians themselves because these activities result in decreased “productivity” as measured by “relative value units.” The revenue value unit system is flawed because it generally rewards performance of high volumes of simple procedures rather than low volumes of lengthy complex innovative procedures despite the major benefits of these complex procedures compared with traditional surgery (including noninvasiveness, minimal postoperative pain, faster recovery, and organ preservation). Decreased productivity directly translates to lower physician compensation. The increasing focus of academic medical centers on revenue and their diminishing support for physician engagement in nonrevenue-producing endeavors such as academic collaboration, research, and education parallels the decline in physician leadership and the rise of an administrator class with ever-enlarging ranks and different priorities. One can only hope that patients will eventually wake up to these facts and demand that academic centers abide by their duty to foster innovation and support physicians in their efforts to develop the cutting-edge therapies of the future.

P.S.G.: One characteristic I have noticed about you over the years is your uncompromising nature and unflinching embrace of the frontiers of your field. Was this a quality you always had since you were young? Or was this something that was developed, trained, and encouraged?

I had a certain intellectual stubbornness for as long as I can remember—a frustrating trait to my parents and teachers who often accused me of trying to “reinvent the wheel.” I often refused to follow advice gained from long experience unless I could deconstruct it and convince myself that it would work reliably and I understood why and how it worked. This spanned the entire spectrum of learning tasks from, say, my father trying to guide me on how to solve certain math problems or how to organize a project back in childhood to my endoscopy teachers’ advice on how to reach the cecum or cannulate the papilla. Overall, this stubbornness may have resulted in longer times to reach a given learning plateau, but all the meandering and experimentation off the beaten path may on occasion result in actually coming up with a “better wheel” (or at least that’s what I would like to believe).

P.S.G.: Along those lines, tell me about endoscopy training. What would it take to train a fellow in endoscopic surgery procedures such as POEM and ESD?

My learning curve publications using cumulative sum methodology indicated about 50 POEM cases for the procedure time curve to flatten out (a surrogate marker for proficiency) and about 250 ESD cases to achieve proficiency benchmarks such as ≥80% R0 resection rate in centers that have high volumes of colon ESD.20,21 Other publications from high-volume expert centers have confirmed these numbers. For example, Pinghong Zhou’s Shanghai group reported a learning threshold of 100 POEMs for proficiency using a composite measure that also included adverse events,22 and multiple European series from high-volume colon ESD centers have reported thresholds of 200 to 250 cases for proficiency using standard benchmarks such as an en bloc resection rate of ≥90%, R0 resection of ≥80%, and ESD speed of ≥9 cm2/h.23 I should also note that these numbers represent the personal learning curves of talented and highly committed expert operators.

It should be obvious then that proficiency in these procedures cannot be achieved by trainees during their 1 year of advanced fellowship in the vast majority of training centers.24 This is especially true with the trainee performing only easier and safer portions of these procedures. The problem is compounded by the fact that many U.S. teachers have not reached mastery of POEM and ESD themselves and that the fellow also needs to invest a substantial amount of time to also learn many other advanced procedures including ERCP and therapeutic EUS. Some advanced endoscopists have proposed that the advanced endoscopy fellowship be expanded to 2 years. However, for now, 1-year advanced fellowships remain the norm.

In view of the above, the advanced fellowship should be regarded as a foundation for further safe self-learning. In addition to reviewing indications and protocols for prevention of adverse events and other didactics, my fellows are encouraged to attend external hands-on courses, and for years, with the assistance of the indefatigable John Day, I have provided for my advanced fellows every 3 months or so an internal hands-on day-long session on ex vivo models that in addition to instruction serves to monitor progress. Hands-on time in human cases is limited to easier safer cases, including rectal ESDs and distal stomach ESDs. Encouragingly, most recent advanced fellowship graduates perform ESD. Performance of POEM is more variable because it depends on having a significant volume of achalasia referrals, which can be challenging without a dedicated motility center with established referrals. However, where these conditions exist, graduates have successfully and safely performed POEM procedures after continued incremental self-training in third-space endoscopy initially focusing on easier ESD cases in the rectum and stomach. For example, Teodor C. Pitea, MD, my advanced fellow from 2011, is probably the highest-volume POEM operator in Arizona with over 50 POEMs per year and Krishna C. Gurram, MD, a more recent graduate, performs POEMs at Elmhurst in Queens but has achieved a level of proficiency that allows him to even perform them at a locum setting as far as North Dakota. I should emphasize here that safety is a paramount concern for POEM, because it is a thoracic procedure that breaches the GI wall and as such has resulted in reported (as well as unreported) cases of intraoperative and postoperative mortality even at the hands of expert operators.

3.However, intellect is only half the equation, with the other half, perhaps the most important half, being ethics. The Hippocratic Oath requires physicians to maintain a high ethical standard. It admonishes physicians to keep the patients’ confidences, “do no harm,” and provide compassionate care to patients without regard to their ability to pay. This code should guide the practice of young physicians. They should treat patients the way they would want their family members treated. They should not let their enthusiasm or pecuniary considerations lead them to perform procedures that are unnecessary or that they have not adequately prepared for and might place the patient at risk of harm. They should strive to deliver the best outcomes tailored to each individual patient while minimizing invasiveness and risk to the patient, even when such care (ESD being a prime example) is financially penalized by our current healthcare environment.
