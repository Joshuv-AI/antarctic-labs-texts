Cytochrome P450 CYP81D11 boosts CO2 assimilation and limits ROS damage under stress
Authors: Thomas Depaepe
Published: 2025

Chloroplasts are remarkable photosynthetic structures that fuel plants with energy-rich molecules, sustaining nearly all life on Earth. Yet, chloroplasts are also highly sensitive to environmental changes. For example, when water runs short or sunlight becomes too intense, the energy absorbed by photosynthetic pigments and its use in downstream photochemical reactions become imbalanced. This condition, known as photoinhibition, leads to the accumulation of damaging reactive oxygen species (ROS) that cause oxidative stress (Li and Kim 2022). Among ROS, singlet oxygen (1O2) is particularly dangerous. Unlike other ROS molecules, 1O2 is not formed by electron transfer. 1O2 is produced by direct energy transfer from chlorophyll or PSII reaction centers to oxygen, creating a reactive form that can damage many biomolecules in its vicinity. For example, peroxidation of lipids in thylakoid membranes by 1O2 can produce reactive carbonyl species (RCS) such as malondialdehyde and acrolein. Fortunately, plants have evolved strategies to prevent photoinhibition.

Carotenoids are protective pigments that shield chloroplasts from oxidative damage by quenching 1O2. 1O2 quenching can occur via 2 different mechanisms: either physically, by direct energy transfer from 1O2 to carotenoid molecules followed by dissipation of excess energy as heat; or chemically, via oxidation by 1O2, resulting in oxidized carotenoid derivatives called apocarotenoids (Ramel et al. 2012a). Carotenoid oxidation can proceed enzymatically as well via specific carotenoid cleavage dioxygenases, some of which are also stress inducible. One of these apocarotenoids, the volatile β-cyclocitral (β-CC), serves as a crucial retrograde signal that is key for transmitting environmental changes from chloroplasts to the nucleus and activating stress-response genes (Ramel et al. 2012b). Many of the β-CC–activated genes are involved in resolving the damaging effects caused by 1O2, including detoxification genes for removing ROS and RCS (D’Alessandro et al. 2018). Nevertheless, the precise mode of action and downstream signaling pathway for β-CC remain unclear. For example, plants can convert β-CC into β-cyclocitric acid (β-CCA), but the latter only activates a subset of β-CC–responsive genes (e.g. drought-related genes) (Shumbe et al. 2017; D’Alessandro et al. 2019). Moreover, the transcriptional regulators class II TGACG SEQUENCE-SPECIFIC BINDING PROTEIN (TGAII)-SCARECROW LIKE 14 (SCL14) and METHYLENE BLUE SENSITIVITY 1 (MBS1) were previously shown to be involved in driving β-CC–mediated gene expression, but their involvement in β-CCA responses remains elusive (D’Alessandro et al. 2018, 2019). Thus, much of the β-CC/β-CCA signaling network remains unresolved and requires further study.

Therefore, Tiwari et al. (2025) set out to untangle more of the β-CC/β-CCA signaling network in a recent study in Plant Physiology. Using a transcriptomic approach, they compared plant responses with β-CC and β-CCA treatments at different time points to identify novel players in the chloroplast-to-nucleus signaling cascade. One of the most strongly upregulated genes across the treatments was CYP81D11, an uncharacterized member of the cytochrome (CYP) P450 monooxygenase superfamily. To determine the roles of CYP81D11 within the β-CC signaling pathway, the authors turned to molecular-genetic approaches. They showed that induction of CYP81D11 expression by β-CC or β-CCA requires TGAII–SCL14, demonstrating that β-CCA also acts via this regulatory complex.

To better understand the function of CYP81D11, the authors overexpressed CYP81D11 in Arabidopsis. Interestingly, CYP81D11 overexpression lines conferred stress tolerance under both high light and drought conditions, in line with the known role of β-CC in abiotic stress signaling. To further explore the underlying mechanism, the authors then examined several markers for oxidative stress. They found that lipid peroxidation and RCS levels were increased in cyp81d11 mutants but decreased in the overexpression lines, suggesting that CYP81D11 mediates stress tolerance by limiting the accumulation of damaging molecules. However, unexpectedly, CYP81D11 overexpression did not induce classical detoxification genes, suggesting a different mode of action.

The direct substrates of CYP81D11 could not be inferred from in silico studies. Instead, transcriptomic analysis of CYP81D11 overexpression lines revealed a strong upregulation of the expression of certain stress-inducible LATE EMBRYOGENESIS RELATED (LEA) proteins, such as COLD-REGULATED 15A (COR15A), which is involved in protecting plastidial biomolecules such as membranes from stress damage (Thalhammer et al. 2014). Surprisingly, a modest but broad upregulation of photosynthesis-related genes was also observed. To further explore the effects of CYP81D11 on photosynthesis, the authors conducted in-depth physiological analysis. Chlorophyll fluorometry and gas exchange analyses showed that, under high light but not normal light conditions, overexpression of CYP81D11 enhanced quantum yield in PSII and reduced nonphotochemical quenching, indicating less energy lost as heat. Unexpectedly, this effect was not caused by increased abundance of protein levels of photosynthetic complexes or altered pigment content. Interestingly, the authors observed higher CO₂ fixation rates in the CYP81D11 overexpression lines, which likely leads to increased consumption of ATP and NADPH in the Calvin cycle and therefore lowers levels of nonphotochemical quenching (Fig.). By channeling excess energy into carbon assimilation, plants likely avoid 1O2 overproduction, accumulate fewer oxidative stress markers, and ultimately produce more biomass under high light.

Together, these findings reveal an alternative stress tolerance mechanism within the β-CC pathway. Activation of CYP81D11, in a TGAII–SCL14–dependent manner, boosts photosynthetic efficiency under stress and prevents the buildup of 1O₂, lipid peroxidation products, and RCS (Fig.). Despite the importance of CYP81D11, its precise biochemical function remains unclear. The findings in the current study are not consistent with a direct role in β-CC to β-CCA conversion or a function in the detoxification of harmful molecules. Instead, CYP81D11 appears to enhance CO₂ assimilation and energy consumption, perhaps by improving the efficiency of specific reactions or stabilizing protein activity. Pinpointing its in vivo substrates will be key to understanding how its enzymatic activity reshapes carbon metabolism. Structural or computational studies may provide critical insights into CYP81D11 substrates. The authors also propose an alternative mode of action, namely that CYP81D11 overexpression could increase the abundance of certain protective proteins, such as LEA proteins, which shield Calvin cycle enzymes from ROS and stabilize chloroplast membranes.

The pleiotropic functions of cytochrome P450 enzymes remain only partially understood. Given that in some flowering plants up to 1% of protein-coding genes belong to this superfamily (Nelson and Werck-Reichhart 2011), deciphering their roles is essential for understanding plant physiology and development. From an applied perspective, the discovery by Tiwari and colleagues also raises the exciting possibility of engineering crops with enhanced tolerance to drought and high light through β-CC signaling via CYP81D11, particularly since its protective effects appear stress specific and do not impair growth under normal conditions.
