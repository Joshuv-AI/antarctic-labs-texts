Age modulates the link between stress-related neural activity and mortality
Authors: Nidaa Mikail, Noemi Sablonier, Pimrapat Gebert, Isabelle Glarner, Adriana Vinzens, Achi Haider, Atanas Todorov, Susan Bengs, Angela Portmann, Valerie Treyer, Susanne Wegener, Christoph Gräni, Aju P. Pazhenkottil, Caroline E. Gebhard, Vera Regitz-Zagrosek, Felix C. Tanner, Philipp A. Kaufmann, Ronny R. Buechel, Alexia Rossi, Catherine Gebhard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Stress-related neural activity (SNA), as measured by amygdala metabolism, has been linked in prior work to all-cause mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events. In this study, we sought to clarify SNA determinants and test whether age modifies its association with all-cause mortality. Using 2-[18 F]fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose positron emission tomography (18F-FDG-PET), we quantified amygdala metabolism, a surrogate for SNA, in 1,336 patients (mean age 59.4 ± 15.6 years, 37.8% women). Assessing demographic and imaging confounders, associations between SNA and mortality were evaluated in a subgroup of 960 participants with a median 5-year follow-up (IQR 3–9). Higher SNA appears independently associated with greater all-cause mortality across all age groups (HR 1.45, 95% CI 1.08-1.95; p = 0.012). The association is strongest in younger, healthier individuals (HR 7.86, 95% CI 2.92-21.21; p < 0.001) and attenuates with advancing age. Mediation analysis indicates that SNA accounts for 38.2% (95% CI 15.7%-60.7%) of the age-mortality link. Here, we find that SNA is independently associated with all-cause mortality, with effect sizes that diminish with age; if confirmed, incorporating SNA into risk models alongside conventional factors may improve mortality prediction and help identify younger adults, who appear low risk by standard criteria, for closer follow-up and preventive strategies.

Introduction

The persisting high prevalence of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide, despite numerous diagnostic and therapeutic breakthroughs, has led to extending the research areas beyond traditional cardiovascular risk factors (CVRFs)1. Notably, more holistic approaches have recently gained interest, considering the heart as one element of a broader network rather than an isolated organ impaired by extrinsic factors2–6. The role of mental health in the development of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) has been particularly highlighted, especially psychological stress7–9. Along this line, the amygdala, a brain region involved in emotion processing10, and the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), which acts as its counterpart, are currently recognized as cornerstones of the brain-heart axis9,11,12. Therefore, amygdala metabolic activity (AmygA) is increasingly utilized as a surrogate for mental stress, using 2-[18 F]fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose positron emission tomography with computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT) to quantify it9,13. Accordingly, several studies have consistently evidenced the association between high stress-related neural activity (SNA), as assessed by amygdala 18F-FDG uptake, and adverse cardiovascular events9,14–18.

However, several parameters modify SNA, hampering its clinical translation. SNA increases in the case of diabetes, obesity, or myocardial infarction19–22. Age and sex also play a significant role, with AmygA decreasing in healthy aging men but not in women23. In contrast, the amygdala volume decreases after the sixth decade, independent of sex24. Previous work also indicates that mental-stress-elicited autonomic activity varies with sex and pre-existing mental conditions25–28, and that socioeconomic and lifestyle variables, which differ between sexes, are independently associated with SNA14,16,17,29,30.

Studies reporting a role of SNA imaging for the prediction of cardiovascular endpoints were conducted in restricted populations without evident cardiovascular disease (CVD). Consequently, it is unclear how older age, manifest CVD, and other comorbidities affect SNA and its association with outcomes in the real-world cardiovascular population, where these factors are highly prevalent. It is also unknown whether age-dependent alterations of SNA are true age effects or a function of an age-related increase in comorbidities. Hence, this study aimed to identify modulators of SNA and evaluate their influence on the relationship between SNA and outcomes in a large, clinically well-characterized cohort of cardiovascular patients undergoing both brain and heart imaging.

Method

Study design and population

This retrospective longitudinal cohort study included 12,675 patients, from which we identified 4,168 adults (≥18 years) who underwent both clinically indicated 18F-FDG-PET and echocardiography within a 6-month period at the University Hospital Zurich, Switzerland, between January 2005 and August 2019. The primary 18F-FDG-PET indications included cancer suspicion or monitoring, fever of unknown origin, suspected infection, and inflammatory disorders. Echocardiography was predominantly employed for monitoring ischaemic or structural heart conditions and in patients undergoing cardiotoxic treatments. Amongst the screened 4168 patients, 1’336 patients (38% women) met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1), i.e., at the time of 18F-FDG-PET, absence of 1) active cancer, 2) unstable medical conditions, 3) severe disease with <6-month life expectancy, and 4) neurological diseases precluding accurate brain 18F-FDG quantification. In a subset of 960 patients with available follow-up data, a time-to-event analysis was performed over a median follow-up of 5 years (IQR: 3–9). Within this subset, propensity score matching was applied, resulting in a 1:1 matched cohort of 626 individuals. Compared to previous studies in this field, our exclusion criteria were less restrictive, particularly regarding CVD status, allowing for a more comprehensive assessment of real-world factors influencing SNA17. The cohort partially overlaps with the heart-brain imaging registry reported elsewhere31. Follow-up and study outcomes

Follow-up data were obtained through telephone interviews, hospital records, and clinical documents. The primary outcome was MACE, defined as non-fatal stroke, non-fatal myocardial infarction, coronary revascularization, and cardiovascular death. Events were adjudicated by a cardiologist (CG) and a radiologist specialized in cardiac imaging (AR), both blinded to imaging data. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality, encompassing cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular deaths. Patients without events or deaths were censored at the last follow-up date (October 1st, 2022).

Data acquisition and definitions

CVRFs, family history of coronary artery disease (CAD), CVD status (including left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF], CAD, valvular heart disease, arrhythmia), chronic inflammatory or infectious conditions, socio-demographic data, medications, vital signs, and laboratory results, were obtained from hospital records. Cardiac imaging findings were retrieved from echocardiography reports performed within 6 months of 18F-FDG-PET. Laboratory parameters obtained within 12 months (6 days for inflammatory markers) from 18F-FDG-PET were included. All recorded clinical conditions, laboratory values, and medication use were present at the time of SNA acquisition, ensuring that these factors preceded or coexisted with SNA at the time of imaging. Professional skill levels were defined according to the International Standard Classification of Occupations32.

Image acquisition and analysis

18F-Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography with computed tomography 18F-FDG-PET was performed on two PET/CT scanners (Discovery VCT or Discovery RX, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA). After an overnight fast, 18F-FDG was administered via a peripheral vein at a dose of 1.3−1.8 MBq/kg based on BMI (minimal and maximal activities: 90 and 300 MBq, respectively). PET images were acquired in 3D mode over 10 minutes, scanning cranially to caudally 45-60 minutes after tracer injection. Non-gated, non-contrast-enhanced CT (120 keV, ~50 mAs) was obtained for attenuation correction. PET data were recalculated to provide images of standardized uptake values (SUV) based on total body weight and injected dose. Image analysis and quantification of 18F-FDG uptake in different regions were performed on a dedicated workstation (AW 5.0 GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA) using PMOD software (version 4.003, PMOD Technologies Ltd.)

Quantification of stress-related neural metabolic activity (SNA)

SNA was quantified using previously validated methods23,33–35. In brief, using the Maximum Probability Atlas in the NEURO tool of PMOD, the brain gyri were automatically segmented. Given the influence of amygdala laterality in emotion processing36–38, right and left AmygA were analyzed separately ([rAmygA] and [lAmygA]). Accordingly, circular regions of interest (ROIs) were placed over the rAmygA and lAmygA as well as the vmPFC, a centre counter-regulating AmygA activity under stressful conditions13. Tracer accumulation was measured as the mean standardized 18F-FDG uptake value (SUVmean) for each ROI. Bilateral SNA (meanAmygA) was calculated by averaging the SUVmean of rAmygA and lAmygA. Given the role of the vmPFC in the stress response, AmygA (individually and averaged) was corrected for the activity (SUVmean) of the vmPFC (SNA [AmygA/vmPFC])13.

Measurement of bone marrow and splenic activation

Hematopoietic tissue activity (HTA) in both the bone marrow and spleen serves as a surrogate marker of systemic inflammation. HTA in the bone marrow was measured as previously described35. Briefly, circular ROIs encompassing the contour of each vertebra with the exclusion of the cortical bone were placed in the thoracic and lumbar vertebrae (T1-L5 or T1-L4 in case of sacralization of L5) to measure 18F-FDG uptake. The SUVmax was determined for these regions, and total HTA was reported as the average SUVmax of the individual’s vertebrae. Furthermore, bone marrow HTA was also determined by computing the average of L3 and L4 vertebral SUVmax, as outlined in prior studies39. Spleen metabolic activity was calculated for each patient by drawing ROIs in the spleen’s three orthogonal planes, i.e., the axial, sagittal, and coronal planes. The SUVmax was measured on each axis, and the mean of the three SUVmax values was calculated, as detailed in a previous report40.

Measurement of arterial inflammation

Arterial 18F-FDG uptake was measured every 3 mm within the ascending aorta from 1 cm above the aortic annulus to the aortic arch. Regions of interest were placed on 1.5 mm-thick axial slices of the aorta. The mean and maximum 18F-FDG uptake intensity of each slice was measured as a meanSUV and maxSUV, which was then corrected for background venous blood activity from the superior vena cava to obtain a Target-to-Background Ratio (TBR), as previously described15. A single aortic TBR value per patient was generated by averaging all individual TBR values.

Statistical approach

Descriptive statistics are reported as mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (25th–75th percentile) for continuous variables, depending on the data distribution. We investigated the linear correlation between age and SNA (AmgyA/vmPFC) using a linear regression model. Additionally, an analysis was performed to examine the interaction between age and sex in their association with SNA. To identify cut-points for the differentiation between high vs low SNA, a classification and regression tree analysis (CART) for time-to-event data based on all-cause mortality in our cohort was performed41. For group comparisons between individuals with high vs low SNA, an independent t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and Chi-square test for categorical variables were applied as appropriate. Multivariable analyses were performed using binary logistic regression with lasso selection based on the Bayesian information criterion. To mitigate multicollinearity between laboratory parameters and comorbidities (diabetes/fasting glucose and C-reactive protein [CRP]/chronic inflammation), two multivariable models were constructed: one including comorbidities without laboratory variables and another replacing comorbidities with corresponding laboratory markers. To ensure model robustness and avoid over-adjustment, covariates that did not significantly impact the model or lacked theoretical relevance were excluded. This approach balances statistical rigour and clinical relevance, reducing the risk of bias while maintaining the model’s interpretability. We applied the area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (ROC), net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) to examine the incremental prognostic value of comorbidities compared to laboratory parameters for predicting the high SNA.

To investigate the effect of age and SNA on all-cause mortality as a patient outcome, we performed Cox regression models and carried out the interaction between age and SNA in both the total population and stratified groups based on cardiovascular (CV) and non-cardiovascular comorbidities. We investigated the interaction effect between age (as a continuous and a binary variable [≤60 vs. 60, based on a median split] and SNA as a binary variable (low vs. high). The outcome was time-to-death, comprising both CV-and non-CV death, with censoring at the last follow-up date of October 1st, 2022.

To assess the independent effect of SNA on all-cause mortality, a propensity score matching strategy was applied as a sensitivity analysis. Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model, with SNA (low vs. high) as an outcome. A nearest-neighbour matching algorithm was applied to create 1:1 matched pairs of low and high SNA until no further matches could be formed. Pairs with propensity scores differences exceeding 0.2 standard deviations (SDs)42 were excluded, resulting in 334 out of 960 cases (35%) unmatched. Cox regression models were then applied to explore the effect of SNA on all-cause mortality in the matched cohort.

Based on prior evidence that SNA varies with age23 and is associated with adverse health outcomes31, we hypothesized that SNA may serve both as a mediator and a modifier of the relationship between age and all-cause mortality. Specifically, we anticipated that elevated SNA would partially mediate the effect of age on mortality, and that this association would differ based on age, indicating a potential interaction effect.

Accordingly, we performed a mediation analysis using a four-way decomposition approach (Stata package med4way,43) to dissect the total effect of age on all-cause mortality via SNA (lAmygA/vmPFC) into four components: (1) the pure indirect effect (PIE), representing the portion transmitted solely through SNA; (2) the reference interaction (INTref), which captures the interaction between age and SNA without age directly influencing SNA; (3) the mediated interaction (INTmed), where age influences SNA and interacts with it in affecting mortality; and (4) the controlled direct effect (CDE), representing the direct effect of age on all-cause mortality independent of SNA. The med4way package estimates the proportion of the total effect attributed to each of these components, with values summing to 100% and potentially being positive or negative. The decomposition analysis was conducted in the longitudinal dataset (n = 960) with available outcome data. We used survival time as the outcome, age as a continuous independent variable, and a binary mediator (low vs. high SNA).

For the adjusted mediation model, we controlled for key covariates identified in the multivariable model of SNA, including sex, CRP, creatinine (µmol/L), fasting glucose (mmol/L), 18F-FDG splenic uptake (SUVmean), heart rate (bpm), diabetes, psychiatric disease, chronic inflammation, history of cancer, and corticosteroid use. Statistical testing was performed within an exploratory framework at a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05 without correction for multiple comparisons. Missing data ranged from 2% to 20% across demographic, sociocultural, laboratory, and cardiac imaging variables. Given this, cases with missing data were excluded without imputation, and the final sample size for each model was reported. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata MP/18 (StataCorp, 2023, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Unadjusted relation between age and stress-associated neural activity

Unadjusted linear regression analysis revealed positive correlations between age and SNA in the total study population, including left (male: r = 0.27, female: r = 0.38), right (male: r = 0.20, female: r = 0.29), and averaged AmygA (male: r = 0.25, female: r = 0.35) normalized to vmPFC activity (Fig. 2A–C). The most pronounced correlation was observed with left AmygA (SNA[lAmygA/vmPFC], Fig. 2B), which was used for subsequent analyses. Women exhibited lower SNA(lAmygA/vmPFC) at younger ages and a steeper increase with age compared to men, though this difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 2B). When lAmygA, rAmygA, and averaged bilateral AmygA were normalized to the temporal lobe instead of the vmPFC, similar results were obtained (Supplementary Fig. 2). Stratification by the presence or absence of cardiovascular or inflammatory comorbidities revealed the strongest age-SNA correlation in individuals without these conditions (male: r = 0.31, female: r = 0.41), with weaker correlations observed in those with cardiovascular (male: r = 0.16) or inflammatory (male: r = 0.21) comorbidities (Fig. 2D–F). Baseline characteristics of the study population

Baseline characteristics stratified by high versus low SNA (lAmygA/vmPFC) are detailed in Table 1. Briefly, among the 1,336 included patients (mean age 59.40 ± 15.60 years, interquartile range 49–71 years; 37.80% women), 39.97% had CVD, and CVRFs such as hypertension (16.47%), diabetes (14.52%), and obesity (13.18%) were common. Patients with high SNA were older (64.59 ± 12.63 vs. 56.08 ± 16.39 years), had higher resting heart rates, and more comorbidities, including coronary artery disease (27.26% vs. 18.90%), structural heart disease (33.78% vs. 21.23%), and inflammatory diseases (30.90% vs. 16.44%). CVRFs such as obesity, diabetes, and dyslipidemia were also more frequent in this group. High SNA patients showed elevated creatinine (125 [IQR 88–229] vs. 92 [IQR 77–128] µmol/L), fasting glucose (6.43 ± 1.95 vs. 5.65 ± 1.48 mmol/L), NT-proBNP (2221 [IQR 484–9045] vs. 445 [IQR 123–2602] ng/L), inflammatory markers, and metabolic activity in bone marrow and spleen. Independent predictors of stress-associated neural activity in the total cohort

Multivariable models were developed to identify independent predictors of high SNA (lAmygA/vmPFC), with sex and BMI included by default. Using the same study patients, two models were created to address multicollinearity: Model 1 excluded laboratory values, and Model 2 included them. Lasso selection based on the Bayesian information criterion was used and only spleen 18F-FDG uptake was included to avoid overlap with bone marrow uptake. Figure 4 depicts estimated odds ratios and 95% CIs for all factors remaining in the models. In Model 1, independent predictors of SNA were diabetes (adjusted OR [aOR] 2.35, p < 0.001), psychiatric disease (aOR 1.99, p = 0.002), corticosteroid use (aOR 1.51, p = 0.042), increased splenic 18F-FDG uptake (aOR 1.57, p = 0.001), chronic inflammation (aOR 1.53, p = 0.011), age (aOR 1.45 per 10 years, p < 0.001), and heart rate (aOR 1.12 per 10 bpm, p = 0.006). In Model 2, independent predictors included psychiatric disease (aOR 1.87, p = 0.008), increased spleen 18F-FDG uptake (aOR 1.43, p = 0.014), fasting glucose (aOR 1.28, p < 0.001), creatinine (aOR 1.02, p < 0.001), and age (aOR 1.45 per 10 years, p < 0.001). Stratification by age, comorbidities, and SNA associations with outcomes

Outcome data for all-cause mortality were available for 960 patients (Fig. 1). Patients were stratified by median age (≤60 years vs. >60 years) and by the presence of cardiovascular and/or non-cardiovascular comorbidities. In Cox regression analysis, high SNA was a strong predictor of all-cause mortality in patients aged ≤60 years, regardless of the presence or absence of cardiovascular or non-cardiovascular comorbidities (Table 3). However, in patients aged >60 years, the association between SNA and all-cause mortality was weaker in those with non-cardiovascular comorbidities and absent in those with cardiovascular comorbidities (Table 3). Furthermore, when both cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular comorbidities were present, the predictive value of SNA for all-cause mortality was lost, irrespective of age (Table 3, right panel). To further investigate this, an interaction term for SNA × age (per 10-year increment) was introduced into the model. The interaction was statistically significant (HR 0.78, 95% CI [0.64–0.94], p = 0.009), indicating that the predictive value of SNA for all-cause mortality decreases with advancing age. Specifically, the hazard ratio for SNA x age (≤60 years) was 2.98 (95% CI [1.93–4.59], p < 0.001), compared to 1.51 (95% CI [1.12–2.04], p = 0.006) for SNA x age (>60 years). Taken together, these results suggest that SNA is a stronger predictor of all-cause mortality in younger individuals, while its prognostic significance diminishes in older individuals, particularly in the presence of comorbidities.

Propensity score matching and outcome analysis in the matched cohort

All individuals with available outcome data (n = 960, Fig. 1) were matched into two groups based on high versus low SNA, controlling for age, sex, and age-related confounders, including socio-cultural variables, cardiac and non-cardiac comorbidities, and CVRFs. This process resulted in a well-balanced 1:1 matched cohort (313 individuals in each group). Table 4 presents the baseline characteristics of the matched cohort, demonstrating that the two groups were comparable across all covariates. Supplementary Fig. 1 illustrates the distribution of propensity scores before and after matching, highlighting the balance achieved between the high and low SNA groups (standardized mean difference <0.1). The association between SNA and outcomes in the matched cohort was assessed using a Cox proportional hazards model (with the Breslow method for ties), thereby isolating SNA as a risk factor for all-cause mortality. The association between SNA and all-cause mortality was significant (1.45 (95% CI [1.08–1.95], p = 0.012), indicating an independent association of SNA with all-cause mortality. In the matched cohort, similar to the total cohort, the interaction term for age (per 10-year increment) × SNA showed a trend toward statistical significance but did not reach it (HR 0.81, 95% CI [0.63–1.04], p = 0.084). Mediation analysis

A four-way decomposition analysis was conducted to evaluate whether SNA mediates the relationship between age and all-cause mortality, to assess its independent contribution, and to clarify its role as an interactive factor. Figure 5illustrates the components of the four-way decomposition of the total effect (TE) of age on all-cause mortality, comprising the controlled direct effect (CDE) of age on all-cause mortality, the reference interaction between age and SNA (INTref), the mediated interaction between age and SNA (INTmed), and the pure indirect effect (PIE) of age on all-cause mortality through SNA. Table 5 shows the results of the unadjusted (left) and adjusted (right) mediation model. In the adjusted mediation model incorporating age and the interaction between age and SNA, only 1.28% (95% CI: 0.06% to 2.51%) of the effect of age on all-cause mortality was attributable to SNA-mediated pathways (INTmed + PIE). When analyzing the interaction components, −13.49% (95% CI: −24.37% to −2.61%) of the effect was attributable to reference interaction (INTref) and −24.70% (95% CI: −38.69% to −10.72%) was attributable to mediated interaction (INTmed). This results in a total of −38.20% (95% CI: −60.74% to −15.65%) of the effect being explained by interaction components, highlighting the role of the SNA pathway in age-related effects on mortality. After accounting for the effects of SNA, the direct effect of age on all-cause mortality remained at −12.21% (95% CI: −23.20% to −1.22%), indicating that a significant portion of age’s impact on mortality is mediated through other factors. Higher SNA was statistically associated with age-related mortality, suggesting an amplifying effect of aging on mortality. To explore whether fasting glucose and creatinine might also mediate the relationship between age and all-cause mortality, we conducted a supplementary mediation analysis considering fasting glucose and creatinine as mediators (Supplementary Table 1). The total effects of age on mortality were similar across both parameters (glucose: excess risk = –0.88; 95% CI: –0.96 to –0.81; creatinine: –0.91; 95% CI: –0.98 to –0.85), with a very small portion of the total effect of age on mortality mediated through glucose (0.31%) or creatinine (0.54%). Discussion

In this study, we comprehensively investigate the relationship between SNA, aging, and outcomes in a meticulously characterized cohort with integrated heart and brain imaging data. Previous research has highlighted the potential role of SNA imaging in cardiovascular risk stratification and monitoring therapeutic interventions for CVD19,29. However, factors such as age, sex, and comorbidities can significantly influence SNA, raising questions about its applicability across diverse cardiovascular populations23–28,35,44–46. Prior studies, often constrained by stringent exclusion criteria, have left the impact of a broader range of confounding variables on SNA in real-world settings unclear, including the direction and magnitude of these effects. Our findings demonstrate that SNA in cardiovascular patients is influenced by multiple factors, including glucose metabolism, kidney function, psychiatric disease, and inflammation, with age emerging as a key modulator. However, although these variables showed the strongest positive associations with SNA, their confidence intervals largely overlap, indicating that their relative effect sizes should be interpreted with caution. Regarding age, a ten-year increase was associated with a 1.5-fold rise in SNA, underscoring the critical role of aging in shaping the heart-brain axis. By considering the laterality of brain functions47, we also found that lAmygA/vmPFC activity shows a stronger association with age compared to rAmygA/vmPFC. This age dependency of lAmygA/vmPFC is important to note, as previous studies have demonstrated that the left amygdala plays a dominant role in emotion processing48–50. Consequently, lAmygA/vmPFC is likely to be more widely applied in future clinical and research applications compared to right or averaged amygdala activity31. Notably, propensity score matching and stratified analyses support that SNA is independently associated with adverse outcomes, including all-cause mortality, across all age groups. However, the strength of this relationship varies with age: SNA is a stronger predictor of all-cause mortality in younger individuals (<60 years) compared to older individuals (≥60 years), where additional age-related risk factors may overshadow the impact of high SNA. Mediation analysis, adjusted for comorbidities, suggests that SNA-related pathways may partly explain age–mortality associations, and that SNA is not just a mediator of aging-related outcomes but also an independent risk factor for mortality. Moreover, we observed that the interaction components (INTref and INTmed) affected the total effect of age on mortality in a non-uniform manner across the lifespan. Specifically, our findings suggest that the health impact of SNA diminishes in older individuals with a high baseline risk profile. Nevertheless, the diminished predictive value in older individuals highlights the need to carefully consider age-related differences when applying SNA as a risk stratification tool in cardiovascular patients.

Our findings provide a broader perspective compared to previous research in this field. While earlier studies primarily focused on healthier and younger populations, our cohort encompasses a wider range of ages and comorbidities, offering unique insights into the relationship between SNA and aging. For example, prior studies have reported an age-related decline in amygdala metabolic activity or volume, particularly in healthy individuals over sixty, as well as an inverted-U-shaped trend in amygdala connectivity23,24,51. These observations contrast with our findings of a linear increase in SNA with age, which likely reflects differences in study populations and imaging methodologies. Additionally, the relationship between amygdala volume and metabolic activity is complex and not always directly correlated. Variability in metabolic rates between brain regions of similar volume may also explain some of the observed discrepancies52,53. Importantly, our analysis revealed that the strongest age-SNA associations occurred in individuals without cardiovascular or inflammatory diseases, while those with these conditions showed weaker correlations. This highlights how comorbidities may modulate the age-related increase in SNA and underscores the importance of considering these factors when interpreting neural stress imaging data.

While women showed lower SNA at younger ages and a steeper increase with age compared to men, this sex difference in age-related SNA changes was not statistically significant. Therefore, we conclude that age-dependent changes in SNA do not appear to vary by sex. This contrasts with our previous study, where we demonstrated an age-dependent decrease in AmygA in men but not in women, in a population consisting of 297 individuals23. Again, these discrepancies across studies may be influenced by differences in sample size and study characteristics, such as the high prevalence of comorbidities in the present study. Hence, it remains unclear whether age-dependent SNA alterations are consistent across both healthy and clinical populations. While we acknowledge the disparities between our study and prior reports, it is important to underscore that our study represents the largest cohort to date in which both heart and brain imaging were conducted.

The unexpected positive relationship between glucose levels and SNA in our study warrants attention, as elevated glucose levels typically reduce 18F-FDG uptake due to competition for GLUT transporters54–56. Additionally, higher glucose can stimulate insulin secretion, promoting glucose and 18F-FDG uptake by peripheral tissues, thereby lowering availability for other cell types such as neurons. However, the modest increase in fasting glucose levels in our cohort (mean 5.95 mmol/L, within normal range) likely does not significantly affect brain FDG uptake. Research suggests that brain glucose consumption varies with aging and between regions57. For example, a rodent study demonstrated reduced glycolytic efficiency in the hippocampus of aged mice despite increased glucose supply, resulting in lower pyruvate production58. Since 18F-FDG uptake reflects glucose uptake but not its breakdown, impaired glycolysis in the aging amygdala may lead to compensatory increases in glucose supply and, consequently, higher 18F-FDG uptake. Furthermore, our study reported a ratio of AmygA to vmPFC activity, whereas prior studies examined individual regions. Notably, we found a significant negative correlation between glucose levels and vmPFC activity (r = −0.189) but no association with AmygA activity (r = 0.009), aligning with earlier findings. These results suggest that the observed association may reflect stress axis dysfunction rather than local glucose competition. If confirmed, the decline in amygdala glucose metabolism associated with aging may open avenues for research in the realm of mental stress among the elderly. In this context, the adverse impact of mental stress could be viewed as a manifestation of neurodegenerative changes within the neural stress networks.

Another unexpected finding within our study was the inverse relationship between a history of cancer and SNA. Remarkably, our two multivariable models independently identified a history of cancer as a predictor of lower SNA. Conversely, recent research involving patients undergoing cancer staging has demonstrated that SNA independently predicts both mortality and cancer progression. In this study, patients in the upper SNA tertile group exhibited significantly lower progression-free survival rates compared to control patients18. Therefore, it is plausible that the association observed in our study may indicate a potential selection bias within our cohort, where cancer survivors with lower SNA levels were included. Additionally, treatments like radiation therapy for cancer may have played a role in altering SNA patterns within this subgroup59. Lastly, since cancer often represents a traumatic experience akin to post-traumatic stress disorders, cancer survivors with intrusive recollections have been found to exhibit a reduced amygdala volume, potentially influencing the intensity of SNA60,61.

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, we deliberately employed broad inclusion criteria, encompassing patients with multiple CVRFs and/or comorbidities. Consequently, our study does not offer insights into the range of AmygA in individuals without such conditions, representing a limitation as we cannot provide information about normal, healthy variations in AmygA. In addition, the study cohort primarily consists of patients who underwent 18F-FDG-PET for clinical purposes, primarily cancer screening. This may potentially restrict the generalizability of our findings to a broader population. We also could not compare baseline characteristics between included and excluded patients, as detailed clinical data were unavailable for the latter. This limitation hinders our ability to fully evaluate potential selection bias arising from the inclusion process. Secondly, because our cohort was predominantly composed of Caucasian patients from Switzerland, the applicability of our results to other populations may be limited, particularly given known socioeconomic influences on SNA14,17. Nonetheless, it is important to note that studies exploring the link between SNA and CVD have primarily originated from Asian and U.S. populations. Therefore, our study, encompassing a European sample, fills a crucial knowledge gap. Further, the lack of data on excluded patients prevented direct comparison with the included cohort and precluded the imputation of missing data, potentially introducing selection bias. Thirdly, there currently exists no consensus regarding standardized quantification methods for SNA using 18F-FDG-PET. However, our approach, which involves adjusting for vmPFC activity, is based on established knowledge of the vmPFC’s role in stress processing, as well as previous findings indicating a stronger correlation between SNA and clinical outcomes when adjusting AmygA for vmPFC activity, in comparison to adjusting for background cerebral activity in non-stress-related regions13,57–59. Additionally, we considered the known lateralization of brain functions and conducted a comparative analysis between left and right AmygA and their associations with age. Lastly, it is crucial to recognize that statistically significant mediation analyses, as observed in our study, do not establish causation.

Our findings provide insights into the heart-brain connection, identifying SNA as a neurobiological mechanism influenced by age. SNA is independently associated with health outcomes, with stronger predictive value in younger individuals, while age-related factors attenuate its impact in older adults. Beyond age, factors such as enhanced inflammation, peripheral sympathetic activation, and glucose metabolism significantly influence SNA. These factors should be carefully controlled in future studies and stress imaging applications. While SNA imaging in older individuals presents challenges, it also highlights opportunities for interventions targeting the stress-inflammation-glucose axis to improve outcomes. Addressing these pathways may reduce the burden of mental stress in older adults, potentially leading to better outcomes.
