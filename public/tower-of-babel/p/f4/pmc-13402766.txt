Advances in Pulse and Oil Seed Industry Coproduct‐Derived Sustainable Food Packaging: Biopolymers, Additives, Processing Technologies, and Functional Applications
Authors: Thilini Dissanayake, Vidheesha Abeysinghe, Cristina Chairez‐Jimenez, Santhosh Ravichandran, Nandika Bandara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe demand for sustainable, functional food packaging from agricultural coproducts such as hulls, meals, fibers, gums, and press residues from pulse and oilseed industries is increasing. These secondary streams from crops such as soybean, canola, hemp, sunflower, lentils, peas, beans, and chickpeas are rich in proteins, polysaccharides, lignin, and bioactive compounds, making them promising sources for biopolymers and functional additives. The objective of this review is to discuss the potential of pulse and oilseed coproducts for biodegradable and active packaging, highlight the impact of extraction, modification, and fabrication techniques, examine packaging functionality reported in the literature, and identify research gaps. The literature indicates a need for systematic studies correlating coproduct composition with functional performance and sustainable modification strategies. Protein, polysaccharides, and cellulose fibers can be tailored to produce films, coatings, and molded packaging structures with desirable mechanical, thermal, and barrier properties. Additives from coproducts, including natural plasticizers, nanocellulose reinforcements, antioxidants, emulsifiers, and antimicrobials, enhance packaging performance and enable the development of active, edible, and smart packaging systems. Fabrication methods, such as solvent casting, extrusion, injection molding, compression molding, blow molding, electrospinning, and 3D printing, enable the customization of packaging properties and structures based on the final application. Despite these advancements, challenges remain in industrial adoption, inconsistency in results between published literature, compatibility issues between additives and polymer matrices, high purification costs, and scalability constraints. Effective utilization of agricultural coproducts supports circular bioeconomy principles, reduces reliance on petroleum‐based plastics, and paves the way for next‐generation, eco‐friendly food packaging solutions.

Introduction

In the broader context of sustainability, food packaging has become a critical challenge, with demand rising rapidly due to global population growth, expanding food production, and the increasing popularity of ready‐to‐eat products. At the same time, large quantities of nondegradable packaging materials, particularly petroleum‐based plastics, are being released into the environment, contributing to pollution and long‐term ecological damage (Siracusa et al. 2008). Although biopolymer‐based packaging has been proposed as an alternative, its adoption has been limited by higher production costs and inferior performance compared to conventional plastics. Properties such as mechanical strength, barrier effectiveness against gases and moisture, and thermal stability are critical in determining the suitability of a packaging material for preserving food quality and safety, while antimicrobial activity can serve as an additional functional advantage in active packaging applications. When these performance factors become challenges, industries (e.g., food production, packaging production, supermarkets, etc.) continue to rely on petroleum‐derived plastics despite their poor biodegradability and nonrenewable nature (Mangaraj et al. 2019; Weligama Thuppahige and Karim 2022). Achieving a balance in the functionality, environmental sustainability, and production cost of food packaging requires biodegradable, cost‐effective materials that match or exceed the functionality of plastics. Innovations in sustainable materials, nanotechnology, and composites offer promising solutions. Developing durable, protective, and affordable alternatives is essential for large‐scale production using existing facilities and reducing plastic pollution (Dissanayake et al. 2025).

Pulses are among the most widely consumed foods globally after cereals and play an important role in sustainable agriculture. They provide protein, fiber, minerals, vitamins, and phytochemicals while also improving soil fertility through nitrogen fixation. Major producers include India, China, and Canada, with chickpeas, peas, and lentils leading production. Processing generates coproducts such as broken seeds, husks, and powders (6%–30% of yield), which are rich in protein and fiber and are mainly used in livestock feed due to their high energy value and digestibility (Luzardo‐Ocampo et al. 2020). Oilseed processing also generates large amounts of coproducts, mainly meals or seed cakes remaining after oil extraction, along with shells and stalks. These materials are often treated as waste, creating disposal challenges. However, growing interest exists in valorizing them as resources for applications such as biodegradable packaging. Developing efficient and sustainable technologies to recover and utilize these materials remains a key challenge (Rodríguez‐Roque et al. 2021). Coproducts generated from the pulse and oilseed industries are diverse in both composition and functionality (Figure 1) (Nartea et al. 2023). Because these coproducts come from widely produced crops, they are abundant and inexpensive, making them attractive for food packaging applications. However, the effectiveness of components derived from pulse and oilseed coproducts depends on how their composition influences packaging performance. Understanding this relationship is essential, as packaging requirements vary by application, with some needing higher strength and others prioritizing low cost and biodegradability (Agarwal et al. 2023). In addition to composition, processing conditions significantly influence the functional performance of coproduct‐based packaging materials. For instance, oilseed meals produced through cold‐press extraction exhibit different physicochemical and functional properties compared to meals obtained via hexane extraction, which can subsequently affect the mechanical and barrier characteristics of the resulting packaging materials (Muangrat and Kaikonjanat 2025). Furthermore, the biopolymer isolation method used for protein or polysaccharide extraction also plays an important role in determining the functionality and performance of these biodegradable films (Tan et al. 2011). This variability emphasizes tailoring materials to specific packaging needs. Understanding how pulse and oilseed coproducts influence mechanical, thermal, barrier, and antimicrobial properties can guide their use in sustainable packaging. Numerous studies have explored biopolymer‐based packaging (Dissanayake et al. 2025; Perera et al. 2023; Rhim and Ng 2007; Taherimehr et al. 2021), but a comprehensive discussion on the role of agricultural coproducts in this field is still needed.

Major oilseed and pulse crops and their associated coproducts.

Despite increasing research, packaging functionality results remain inconsistent, even with the same raw materials or additives. Limited explanations exist for these variations, highlighting the need to better understand the factors influencing packaging performance to support effective material use and industrial development. The present review aims to fill this gap by identifying the major coproducts generated in pulse and oilseed processing, analyzing their functional and chemical properties relevant to packaging applications, discussing the isolation and processing techniques employed to convert them into packaging materials, reviewing the possible causes of inconsistencies in packaging functionality results across published studies, and identifying future research directions for improving performance and scalability. By mapping the potential of these underutilized resources, this review seeks to support the development of cost‐effective, biodegradable, and functional food packaging alternatives that align with sustainability goals. This approach promotes a circular bioeconomy by turning agricultural waste into biodegradable packaging, adding value to farming, supporting the food industry, and boosting economic sustainability.

Functional and Chemical Characteristics of Coproducts Derived From the Oilseed and Pulse Industry

Role of Isolation Techniques on Functional and Chemical Characteristics

The isolation process of target compounds from coproducts is highly important because it directly influences the purity, yield, and functional properties of the final extract. Among the available methods, solvent extraction remains the most conventional and widely used technique for isolating both proteins and polysaccharides. This process is often supported by pretreatments, which enhance extraction efficiency by improving the release of compounds. Typically, solvent extraction involves three main steps: mixing the solvent with the coproduct, centrifuging to separate the phases, and filtering to obtain the extract. Although these steps form the general workflow, the exact procedure may vary depending on the nature of the raw material and the characteristics required in the final product (Lamonaca et al. 2025). For example, when isolating proteins from oilseed meals, alkaline solubilization followed by isoelectric precipitation is the most widely used method. Industrial oilseed meals are typically high in fat and are often processed into pellets, which can act as a barrier to efficient protein extraction. To overcome this, pretreatments such as grinding and defatting are commonly applied to improve extraction efficiency. However, the alkaline pH (9–12) used during the solubilization step can lead to partial protein denaturation, which may ultimately affect the functional properties of the isolated proteins. Alternatively, salt‐based extraction (in general at 0.1–1.0 M salt concentration) is another technique in which proteins are solubilized using a salt solution and subsequently isolated (Fang, Chang, et al. 2023).

Reverse micelle extraction is another alternative protein isolation method that uses nanosized water cores surrounded by surfactants in an apolar solvent. The process involves forward extraction, in which soluble proteins migrate into the micelle's water core, and backward extraction, in which proteins are recovered by disrupting the micelles and removing surfactants. Compared to conventional alkaline extraction‐isoelectric precipitation, which generally results in a protein recovery rate of 30%–90% (Hewage et al. 2022), reverse micelle extraction offers several advantages, including protection of protein structure, improved functionality, lower cost through solvent/surfactant recovery, and the potential to extract proteins directly from non‐defatted flours, making it particularly useful for oilseeds (Fang, Gu, et al. 2023; Qin et al. 2025). Hemp protein isolates from non‐defatted and defatted hemp flours show notable differences in composition, structure, functionality, gelling, and aroma. While both had similar protein recoveries, defatted hemp meal yielded higher protein purity and extraction efficiency, whereas non‐defatted hemp meal retained more lipids. Proteins from non‐defatted hemp meal exhibited higher β‐sheet content and surface hydrophobicity, contributing to stronger gels, while proteins from defatted meal formed gels at lower concentrations with better emulsifying activity. Aromatic profiles also differed, with protein from non‐defatted meal containing more lipid‐derived volatiles due to its higher residual oil content (Fang, Gu, et al. 2023). The variation in functionality may result from processing‐induced changes in protein structure, as well as differences in the residual oil content present in the protein materials.

Deep eutectic solvent (DES) extraction is an innovative and green technology that has emerged as a promising alternative to conventional methods for isolating valuable compounds, including proteins, from biological materials. DESs are formed from eutectic mixtures of hydrogen bond acceptors, such as quaternary ammonium salts (e.g., choline chloride), and hydrogen bond donors, such as glycerol, urea, or malonic acid, resulting in a liquid with a low melting point and excellent solvent properties. These solvents can dissolve both polar and nonpolar compounds in protein extraction. DES‐based aqueous two‐phase systems enhance separation primarily through increased ionic strength. Unlike traditional alkaline extraction or solvent precipitation, DES extraction minimizes protein denaturation, reduces the use of toxic chemicals, lowers wastewater generation, and can improve extraction yield and functionality. In addition, DESs are biodegradable, low‐cost, safe, and scalable, making them attractive for industrial applications and clean‐label applications. Despite these advantages, commercial adoption is still limited, and challenges such as efficient DES recovery must be addressed for large‐scale implementation (Hewage et al. 2022).

The study by Hewage et al. (2024) demonstrated that protein characteristics and economic value can vary depending on the isolation technique used, even when the protein source is the same. The authors developed an eco‐friendly DES system to extract high‐purity protein isolates from fava bean flour and compared it with conventional alkaline extraction. Using choline chloride‐glycerol DES under optimized conditions, protein yield (∼65.4%) and recovery (∼23%) were higher than with alkaline extraction, while purity remained similar (∼92%). DES extraction preserved the protein structure, increased the α‐helix content, reduced aggregation, and enhanced the content of sulfur‐containing amino acids, producing finer, more hydrophilic particles with a lighter color. These results demonstrated that DES‐based extraction is a sustainable alternative that improves yield, nutritional value, and functional quality while minimizing protein denaturation (Hewage et al. 2024).

The functional and structural properties of proteins that are affected by the isolation technique play a crucial role in determining the performance of protein‐based biodegradable packaging. Protein denaturation or modification through physical, chemical, or enzymatic treatments can also alter their functional properties to meet specific application requirements. These characteristics directly influence mechanical strength, barrier properties, solubility, and water uptake. For instance, alkaline pH, heat, or cross‐linking treatments enhance tensile strength, flexibility, and thermal stability and reduce water vapor and oxygen permeability. Protein solubility and water absorption are similarly affected by pH, temperature, plasticizer type, and salt content. By tailoring these processing conditions, it is possible to optimize biodegradable packaging materials for specific food applications, highlighting the importance of studying each protein source and isolation technique individually (Senthilkumaran et al. 2022).

Not only protein but also polysaccharide isolation techniques strongly influence the physicochemical and techno‐functional properties of extracted polysaccharides, such as starch and pectin, and thereby packaging properties. Thermal treatments above the starch gelatinization temperature (90°C–100°C) solubilize pectins and partially gelatinize starch, resulting in increased cell wall permeability, higher water holding capacity, and stable swelling behavior. Thermal treatments below gelatinization temperatures (∼60°C) preserve more native starch structure, with heat‐moisture conditions affecting crystallinity and gelatinization enthalpy, although specific techno‐functional properties are less defined. Acid or alkali treatments at room temperature minimize thermal damage while chemically modifying pectins, producing highly permeable cells with intact starch, enhanced thermal stability, and resistance to swelling and shear (Pallares Pallares et al. 2021). Overall, isolation methods and modifications act as tools to tailor polysaccharide properties, such as gelatinization, water permeability, and absorption (Pallares Pallares et al. 2021).

Impact of Chemical Structure and Composition of Coproducts on Functionality of Packaging Materials

The chemical structures of the isolated compounds of the coproducts differ, resulting in differences in the functionality of packaging materials. Nanocellulose is commonly used as an additive in packaging, and its properties can be tailored by changing its structure to enhance performance. Common modifications include sulfation, 2,2,6,6‐tetramethylpiperidine‐1‐oxyl (TEMPO) oxidation, grafting, esterification, carboxylation, and cationization, each of which influences the mechanical, barrier, and functional properties of the resulting packaging (Figure 2). Sravanthi et al. (2025) modified nanocellulose using TEMPO oxidation to increase its surface charge, promoting better dispersion in the film matrix through electrostatic repulsion. As a result, the authors obtained xyloglucan films with improved uniformity, tensile strength, and flexibility compared to films containing unmodified nanocellulose (Sravanthi et al. 2025). Despite excellent reinforcing properties, the hydrophilic nature of nanocellulose reduces the moisture‐barrier properties of packaging materials. The nanocellulose structure was modified by introducing oleic acid to its structure to increase the hydrophobicity of packaging materials by reducing the available OH groups. The authors reported that this interaction is not an esterification; rather, it mainly results from mechanical interlocking, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interactions (Dissanayake et al. 2023). On the other hand, chemical modifications such as esterification have been employed to improve the release behavior and functional performance of packaging materials, thereby enhancing their antioxidant and antimicrobial properties. For example, esterification between cyclodextrin and nanocellulose facilitated the encapsulation and controlled release of carvacrol (Castro et al. 2016). Another study performed by Jaouahar et al. (2024) evaluated the impact of nanocellulose sulfation on film properties. The results showed that sulfation can produce transparent and re‐dispersible nanocellulose while enhancing mechanical robustness.

Illustration of various structural modifications applied to nanocellulose to enhance its functionality in packaging materials.

The structural diversity of proteins plays a critical role in determining both the processing methods and the physical properties of bio‐based films produced from different coproducts. One main reason for these differences is the different amino acids that make proteins. Proteins differ in their amino acid composition, sequence, and resulting hierarchical structures, which ultimately influence solubility, glass transition behavior, and film performance. Moreover, protein structure is strongly influenced by various interactions, including peptide bonds, hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, salt bridges, and disulfide bonds. The amino acid composition, sequence, and interactions between them are reflected in the packaging materials produced from different protein sources (Dissanayake et al. 2025). For example, the main prolamin from corn, zein, contains a high proportion of nonpolar amino acids, making it water‐insoluble and highly dependent on its α‐helix to β‐sheet ratio for processability. Zein with a higher α‐helix content allows better plasticizer diffusion and enables film‐blowing, whereas a high β‐sheet content hinders processing. Pea protein, which contains low levels of cysteine, is easier to process, and heat denaturation can induce intermolecular β‐sheet formation and disulfide bonds. This improves tensile strength and elongation and imparts UV‐blocking capabilities through aromatic amino acids (Shi and Dumont 2014). Canola proteins illustrate how protein fractions influence film performance. Removing the highly hydrophilic napin fraction increases hydrophobicity and leads to films with better mechanical strength and reduced water absorption. Overall, differences in amino acid polarity, cross‐linking potential, and secondary structure organization, such as the abundance of nonpolar residues in zein, govern protein plasticization and restructuring, ultimately shaping the rigidity, flexibility, strength, and barrier properties of protein‐based packaging materials (Shi and Dumont 2014).

Fu et al. (2022) compared the different packaging properties of cellulose nanocrystals incorporated with three proteins: zein, soy protein isolate (SPI), and wheat gluten protein (WGP). The authors observed differences in the properties under the same processing conditions for three proteins. The differences in film properties stem from their unique molecular structures, amino acid compositions, and how they interact with cellulose nanocrystals. Zein is rich in hydrophobic amino acids and therefore forms compact, strong, and opaque films, although water resistance remains limited. SPI, containing both hydrophilic and hydrophobic residues, yields biodegradable but mechanically weak and water‐sensitive films. WGP, with its disulfide‐linked glutenin and elastic gliadin fractions, creates viscoelastic films with limited water resistance. When cellulose nanocrystals are added, they enhance film performance differently. In zein, it produces thinner, more hydrophobic films; in SPI, it forms tight, hydrogen‐bonded networks, but in WGP, it disrupts the native network, increasing porosity, thickness, and water vapor permeability (WVP). This demonstrates how protein structure critically influences the properties of cellulose nanocrystal‐protein composites (Fu et al. 2022).

Packaging Materials From Pulse and Oilseed Industrial Coproducts

Protein‐Based Packaging Materials

Protein‐based films from pulse and oilseed coproducts are among the most intensively studied biopolymers for biodegradable packaging. Meals and cakes from soy, canola, sunflower, hemp, cottonseed, lentil, and pea typically contain 30%–60% protein and 10%–30% dietary fiber, in addition to residual lipids and phenolic compounds (Sunil et al. 2015). These meals can form dense, hydrogen‐bonded networks with relevant oxygen and carbon dioxide barrier properties and acceptable mechanical strength (Ajesh Kumar et al. 2022). However, a critical reading of the literature reveals that most studies still focus on demonstrating that films can be formed, rather than on establishing clear design rules, comparing protein sources, or assessing industrial scalability. Among plant protein films, tensile strength varied widely depending on the protein source and formulation, ranging from 0.2 to 0.6 MPa for pea protein films to approximately 4–18 MPa for soybean protein films. Sunflower oil cake and canola oil cake films also demonstrated relatively high tensile strengths between ∼8 and 10 MPa, indicating their potential to produce mechanically stable films. In comparison, commercial low‐density polyethylene (LDPE) and high‐density polyethylene (HDPE) films typically exhibit tensile strengths of approximately 12 and 27 MPa, respectively. Elongation at break values of protein films ranged from 8% to 123%, with soybean and canola protein films showing comparatively high flexibility, approaching or even exceeding commercial plastics in certain, cases where it is ∼40% for LDPE and ∼65% for HDPE (Lauer and Smith 2020). Barrier properties also differed considerably among protein‐based films. WVP values generally range from 10−14 to 10−5 g/m s Pa depending on the protein source and film composition. Compared with conventional petroleum‐based packaging materials, which generally possess near‐zero water vapor and oxygen permeability, protein‐based films still exhibit inferior moisture barrier properties but can provide competitive oxygen barrier performance. Overall, these comparisons highlight the potential of plant protein‐based films as sustainable alternatives for biodegradable packaging applications while also emphasizing the need for further improvements in moisture resistance and mechanical durability for broader commercial implementation.

Across protein sources, two recurring intrinsic limitations dominate hydrophilicity and brittleness. Lentil, pea, soy, canola, sunflower, hemp, and cottonseed proteins all require plasticizers, most commonly glycerol, to avoid cracking and to impart flexibility, but they will increase the hydrophilicity (Cháirez‐Jiménez et al. 2024; Dissanayake et al. 2022; Ochoa‐Yepes et al. 2019; Wei et al. 2021). Plasticizers reduce intermolecular forces and increase chain mobility but at the expense of higher water uptake and, often, increased WVP. Chemical cross‐linking, such as epoxy compounds, triglycidylamine, and glutaraldehyde, and enzymatic cross‐linking, such as microbial transglutaminase, are used to promote covalent network formation, thereby improving tensile strength and moisture resistance (S. Li et al. 2017; Mirpoor et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2022). Nevertheless, these modifications do not always translate into improved barrier properties; some formulations show reduced WVP, whereas others show the opposite. Rather than being contradictory, these results reflect the delicate balance between network densification, microstructural defects, and plasticizer distribution, a balance that is rarely discussed explicitly.

A further challenge for uniform and repeatable fabrication is the absence of standardized processing conditions. Most protein‐based films are produced by thermal denaturation of proteins in the presence of a plasticizer, followed by solvent casting, thermocompression, or extrusion (Ochoa‐Yepes et al. 2019; Perez et al. 2016). However, small changes in pH, ionic strength, heating rate, concentration, and drying regime can drastically alter aggregation patterns such as random aggregates and ordered fibrils, the proportion of β‐sheets, and the resulting amorphous/ordered microstructure (Bagnani et al. 2023; Z. Liu et al. 2025; Roy Goswami et al. 2025). Consequently, tensile strength, toughness, and barrier properties can differ by an order of magnitude between nominally similar formulations (Cháirez‐Jiménez et al. 2024; Dissanayake et al. 2022; Wei et al. 2021). In the absence of standardized protocols and systematic factorial studies, the field has limited ability to distinguish whether observed differences arise from material composition, processing conditions, or the interaction between both (S. Li et al. 2017; Xu et al. 2022).

Across the plant protein sources, SPI, pea protein isolate (PPI), and canola protein isolate (CPI) offer the most meaningful contrast for comparative analysis. SPI is the most relevant in terms of industrial availability and processing know‐how. When strongly cross‐linked and reinforced, such as epoxides, cellulose nanofibers, or graphene oxide, SPI films can achieve high tensile strength, improved toughness, and enhanced water resistance (Wei et al. 2021; Xu et al. 2022). However, SPI is highly hydrophilic and water‐sensitive, reaching acceptable barrier performance typically requires relatively high additive loadings and complex hybrid systems with poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) or polysaccharides (Karmalm et al. 2009; Qi et al. 2024). From a scalability standpoint, SPI benefits from a well‐established soy fractionation industry, but it may be an extra benefit if there are ways to address allergenicity concerns (González et al. 2019; Song et al. 2011).

PPI and related pea protein systems are valued for their low allergenicity, neutral flavor, and strong techno‐functional properties, such as emulsification and gelation, making them particularly suitable for plant‐based food formulations within the rapidly growing alternative protein market. Films and bioplastics based on pea protein (alone or blended with whey protein isolate or starch) often show good elasticity at higher plasticizer contents (∼50% of protein weight), but their tensile strength tends to be lower than that of optimized SPI networks (Acquah et al. 2020; Perez et al. 2016). Their barrier performance is highly formulation dependent; when starch and protein form an interpenetrating network, WVP can decrease (Ochoa‐Yepes et al. 2019). Conversely, in some systems, phase separation has been associated with increased permeability and brittleness (Yildiz et al. 2022). The isolation cost and availability of PPI grades tailored for materials applications remain limiting factors, and there is comparatively little work on large‐scale processing (e.g., blown films, thermoforming) compared with food applications (Perez et al. 2016).

CPI derived from canola meal occupies a strategically interesting position. CPI and canola meal‐based films reinforce the circular bioeconomy by upcycling a major oilseed coproduct with limited high‐value uses. Based on the studies performed by Dissanayake et al. (2022) and Cháirez‐Jiménez et al. (2024), CPI‐based films reinforced with TEMPO‐oxidized nanocrystalline cellulose and graphite oxide nanoparticles, respectively, exhibit improvements in tensile strength, elastic modulus, and thermal stability, alongside substantial reductions in oxygen permeability upon the addition of graphite oxide. Compared with SPI, CPI shows promising emulsifying and film‐forming behavior but is more difficult to extract at high purity due to the presence of phenolics and complex seed structures. This makes CPI an excellent platform for strategies that aim to work closer to the original meal composition rather than relying on highly purified isolates.

Beyond isolate‐based systems, amyloid fibril formation has emerged as a powerful structuring strategy. For example, proteins from rapeseed cake and soy flour can be converted into amyloid fibrils under controlled heat/acid treatments and thereby change the functionality of the packaging film (Bagnani et al. 2023, 2024; L. Liu et al. 2023; Roy Goswami et al. 2025). These fibrils promote highly ordered β‐sheet‐rich networks that significantly improve toughness and ductility, sometimes by more than threefold, and increase elasticity compared with monomeric protein films. However, improved mechanical performance can come at the cost of higher gas permeability due to microvoids associated with fibril packing. Thus, amyloid‐based systems clearly illustrate that not all improvements in mechanical performance translate into improved barrier function; depending on the application, this trade‐off may be desirable or undesirable.

Other oilseed‐derived proteins (cottonseed, hemp, sunflower) reveal additional distinctions. Cottonseed protein films produced with different plasticizers or via “green” extraction without plasticizers can reach tensile and elongation values close to those of commodity bioplastics at relatively low estimated cost (Selling et al. 2022; Shao et al. 2024). Hemp protein films cross‐linked with transglutaminase or spermidine show that fine‐tuning hydrophobic and hydrogen bonding interactions can strongly modulate not only mechanical properties but also selective permeability to water vapor, oxygen, and carbon dioxide (Mirpoor et al. 2022, 2024). Sunflower protein isolate reinforced with bacterial nanocellulose demonstrates that nanocellulose can offset some of the drawbacks associated with phenolic–protein interactions, improving water resistance and mechanical strength (Efthymiou et al. 2022). However, across these systems, phenolics and nonprotein components create both opportunities (antioxidant activity, active packaging) and challenges (protein aggregation, brittleness, color) and are rarely quantified or systematically controlled.

Studies on protein‐based films from pulse and oilseed coproducts reveal several overarching patterns that are crucial for moving beyond case‐specific observations toward generalizable design principles. Across sources, most protein matrices exhibit the same fundamental restrictions, pronounced hydrophilicity, and mechanical brittleness, which explains the recurring reliance on plasticizers, cross‐linking strategies, and nanofillers. What often appears as contradictory findings in water‐vapor and gas‐barrier behavior can largely be attributed to the interplay among network densification, microvoid formation, and phase compatibility, underscoring that structuring at the microscale and beyond, rather than composition alone, governs functional performance. Comparative evaluation of SPI, PPI, and CPI further highlights that differences in film behavior must be considered alongside practical factors such as extraction complexity, coproduct availability, allergenicity, and market maturity, as these influence raw material accessibility, processing feasibility, regulatory acceptance, and consumer adoption in packaging applications. However, the potential for cross‐study synthesis is hindered by a lack of systematic, side‐by‐side assessments conducted under consistent processing conditions, a gap that continues to fragment the field and delay the development of unified structure–processing–property frameworks. Addressing this methodological heterogeneity is essential if protein‐based films are to evolve from promising laboratory results toward predictable, industrially relevant biopolymers.

Polysaccharide‐Based Packaging Materials

Polysaccharides such as starch, pectins, cellulose, hemicellulose, and associated lignocellulosic residues present in pulse and oilseed coproducts play a dual, often underappreciated role in packaging materials. Purified polysaccharides are well‐known film formers but are often brittle and moisture‐sensitive. In contrast, crude polysaccharide‐rich fractions from hulls and meals can act as reinforcing phases, moisture regulators, or barrier enhancers. Critically, these fractions are not inert fillers; they actively dictate microstructural architecture and thus compromise barrier properties, mechanics, optical behavior, and biodegradability.

A consistent trend observed across lentil, chickpea, and cellulosic residue‐based systems is that film performance is strongly influenced by the composition and distribution of polysaccharide fractions within the matrix. Cassava starch films (commercial cassava starch) with small additions of lentil protein (commercial protein isolate) (0.075% and 1.5% based on weight) exhibit reduced moisture content, solubility, and WVP, as well as enhanced mechanical strength and water resistance (Ochoa‐Yepes et al. 2019). Here, the protein acts more as a structuring agent within a continuous polysaccharide‐rich matrix. In contrast, when poorly compatible polysaccharides (e.g., certain gums) are added to sunflower protein matrices at high levels (0.75% w/v), phase incompatibility can lead to increased WVP and reduced modulus, as reported for locust bean gum (commercial locust bean gum) sunflower protein films (commercial protein isolate) (Alves et al. 2024). Thus, whether a polysaccharide makes the material “better” or “worse” depends on whether it contributes to a continuous network or creates discrete, weak domains.

Soy hulls offer a particularly instructive case. Chemical treatment of soy hulls with alkaline and oxidizing agents yields cellulosic residues that can be processed into films (Regmi et al. 2024; Regmi and Janaswamy 2024, 2025). These materials are rich in cellulose, hemicelluloses, and minor lignin fractions, resulting in films with tensile strength values that increase with cellulosic residue and CaCl2 content and with significantly improved water vapor barrier properties compared with control polysaccharide films. At the same time, they are more opaque and rigid, and their biodegradation rates (half‐lives on the order of 20–25 days in soil) are influenced by the presence of lignocellulosic components, which are generally more resistant to microbial degradation than pure polysaccharide or protein matrices. From a critical point of view, these systems demonstrate that lignocellulosic‐rich residues are excellent barrier enhancers and shelf‐life extenders for fresh produce. Nevertheless, they may not be suitable when high transparency or high extensibility is needed.

Pectin and chitosan‐based matrices further highlight the influence of polysaccharide architecture on the mechanical and barrier properties of films. For example, pectin (commercial pectin) films reinforced with sunflower hull powder (obtained after industrial oil extraction) exhibit reduced moisture content and WVP, along with increased tensile strength, due to the presence of water‐insoluble lignocellulosic components and strong interfacial interactions (De'Nobili et al. 2021). Meanwhile, chitosan (commercial chitosan) films blended with chickpea flour and cross‐linked with citric acid show improved elongation at break and antibacterial activity (Yildiz et al. 2022), illustrating how polysaccharide‐protein hybrid systems can deliver both functionality (bioactivity) and improved mechanical properties. However, the literature rarely quantifies how these properties scale with filler size, morphology, or chemical modification, making it difficult to generalize beyond specific formulations.

Another important but underexploited theme is the integration of protein amyloid fibrils into polysaccharide matrices. Bioplastics produced from soy or okara amyloid fibrils blended with methylcellulose or starch‐pectin matrices (Bagnani et al. 2024; Roy Goswami et al. 2025) show that fibrils can act as reinforcing and toughening elements embedded in polysaccharide networks. The resulting films can achieve mechanical performance comparable to that of some commercial bioplastics, with the added benefit of using wastewater‐derived proteins. However, microvoid formation and modified glass transition profiles can increase gas permeability, again underscoring that higher mechanical strength is not automatically synonymous with better barrier performance.

Overall, research on polysaccharide‐based materials derived from pulse and oilseed coproducts reveals clear but often overlooked relationships between composition, structure, and function that extend beyond simple film formation. The performance of these systems is governed less by the polysaccharide type itself and more by the architecture of the resulting multiphase matrix, for example, whether the polysaccharide forms a continuous network or exists as a dispersed particulate phase. When finely integrated, polysaccharides such as starch, pectin, or cellulose residues can enhance mechanical strength, reduce WVP, and modulate biodegradation through controlled crystallinity and hydrogen bonding. Conversely, poor compatibility or coarse lignocellulosic dispersion often increases opacity, rigidity, and microstructural defects, undermining barrier performance. These contrasting outcomes help explain why results across studies may appear inconsistent despite using similar materials. Notably, studies rarely interrogate how filler morphology, degree of purification, or interactions with proteins, phenolics, and lipids collectively dictate system behavior, limiting the development of predictive property–structure models. As a result, current evidence remains fragmented and highly specific, underscoring the need for systematic, focused mechanism investigations that couple detailed microstructural characterization with functional performance and techno‐economic considerations. Such integration is essential to position rich polysaccharide coproducts as reliable and scalable components in the next generation of biodegradable packaging.

Oilcake/Meal‐Based Packaging Materials

The direct use of whole oilcakes and meals, rather than purified protein or polysaccharide fractions, is particularly attractive from a circular bioeconomy perspective. Sunflower, canola, cottonseed, chickpea, pea, hemp, and other coproducts can be incorporated in milled, defatted, or mildly pretreated forms to form films and bioplastics, thereby avoiding energy‐intensive fractionation and maximizing resource efficiency. However, the same compositional complexity that makes these materials sustainable (proteins, fibers, lipids, phenolics, minerals) also makes them challenging to control structurally.

Sunflower oilcake‐based films exemplify this duality. Ground sunflower oilcake blended with sodium alginate yields edible films with reduced moisture content, water solubility, and WVP compared with pure alginate films due to the presence of lipophilic and hydrophobic compounds (Petraru and Amariei 2022, 2023). At higher loadings (0.5% w/v), the oilcake acts as a multifunctional filler, enhancing water resistance (Petraru and Amariei 2022). Not only meal‐based systems but also flour‐based formulations have shown similar behavior. Chickpea flour films produced with glycerol under different pH conditions showed a similar pattern (Díaz et al. 2019). As the flour content and processing pH are adjusted, WVP decreases, tensile strength and stiffness increase, but flexibility decreases and opacity increases. Injection‐molded pea protein and glycerol films also highlight that relatively high plasticizer levels are often required to overcome the inherent brittleness of minimally processed coproducts (Acquah et al. 2020; Perez et al. 2016). From a critical perspective, these studies suggest that whole‐meal systems can compete in specific niches, such as opaque, rigid, or water‐soluble packaging, trays, or edible films, but will rarely match the optical and mechanical performance of isolate‐based films without further compatibilization. Cottonseed meal (CSM) and canola seed meal illustrate how blending and chemical modification can push whole meals closer to commercial performance. Bilayer films composed of CSM and polylactic acid (PLA) demonstrate that adding a PLA outer layer can significantly increase the tensile strength and modulus, improve water vapor barrier properties, and modulate opacity (Biswas et al. 2023). However, PLA addition also increases cost and partially offsets the “all‐biomass coproduct” narrative. Nanocomposite films based on canola seed meal modified with glycidyl methacrylate and reinforced with cellulose nanocrystals show that carefully designed cross‐linking and reinforcement can transform a heterogeneous meal into a structurally coherent matrix with improved thermal stability and mechanical strength that is capable of protecting fresh produce such as blueberries (Z. Liu et al. 2025). These examples highlight that, in practice, hybrid strategies (meal + compatibilizer + reinforcing biopolymer) are often necessary to achieve high performance. Hemp seed oilcake films, after protein isolation and cross‐linking, further emphasize that starting from a whole meal does not preclude high‐performance materials, but it typically requires at least one purification step (Mirpoor et al. 2022, 2024). In most cases, oilcake‐based systems in the literature are in fact hybrid systems that combine partially purified protein fractions with residual nonprotein components rather than truly raw meals.

Critically, only a few studies address the variability of meal composition, such as differences between mechanical versus solvent extraction, cultivar, growing conditions, and their impact on film reproducibility (Bagnani et al. 2023; Z. Liu et al. 2025; Shao et al. 2024). Even fewer papers report techno‐economic or life‐cycle analyses comparing isolate‐based versus meal‐based routes (Roy Goswami et al. 2025; Shao et al. 2024). Without such data, it is difficult to quantify whether the sustainability benefits of skipping protein isolation outweigh potential performance losses or the added cost of compatibilizers and reinforcements. Taken as a whole, studies on oilcake and meal‐based packaging materials illustrate both the promise and the complexity of using minimally processed coproduct streams. Their heterogeneous composition, which contains proteins, fibers, lipids, minerals, and phenolic compounds, can enable multifunctional behavior, such as improved water resistance or intrinsic antioxidant activity. Nevertheless, it also introduces challenges related to a heterogeneous film matrix and weak mechanical properties. Many studies have demonstrated that incorporation levels affect barrier performance. The desired amount may change from one meal to another, but at appropriate levels, it may positively impact by creating tortuous diffusion pathways formed by fibers and residual lipids. In contrast, higher loadings often promote phase separation, microcracking, or loss of cohesion. These opposing effects highlight the need to treat oilcake‐based films not as simplified versions of protein films but as inherently multiphase composites whose behavior is governed by interfacial compatibilization rather than composition alone. Despite their sustainability advantages, surprisingly little research addresses batch‐to‐batch variability, differences in extraction methods, or realistic processing routes, such as extrusion and thermoforming. Moreover, techno‐economic assessments comparing meal‐based versus isolate‐based routes remain largely absent, restricting our ability to determine when whole coproduct utilization is genuinely advantageous. Collectively, these gaps explain why oilcake materials show promise in targeted applications but have yet to transition beyond proof‐of‐concept. Advancing this field will require systematic studies that integrate microstructural analysis, interfacial engineering, and cost–performance modeling to unlock the full potential of these complex, circular‐economy resources.

Functional Additives Derived From Coproducts

Agricultural and food‐processing coproducts have emerged as valuable sources of functional additives that can enhance the physical, chemical, and biological properties of packaging. Coproducts are secondary streams generated during the processing of agricultural commodities (such as oilseed cakes, bran, husks, or flour) that often remain underutilized or are treated as waste (Yadav et al. 2020). However, these streams are rich in bioactive compounds, structural polysaccharides, proteins, and lipids that can be isolated, modified, and incorporated into packaging films or coatings. By utilizing these resources, the packaging industry can simultaneously reduce waste, create value‐added products, and enhance the performance of biodegradable packaging materials (Lamonaca et al. 2025). This section reviews the main classes of functional additives derived from coproducts, including natural plasticizers, reinforcing agents, antioxidants, emulsifiers, antimicrobials, and bioactive compounds. Natural Plasticizers

Plasticizers are essential additives used to improve the flexibility, processability, and mechanical properties of packaging films (Chang and Nickerson 2015; Karmalm et al. 2009). Conventional plasticizers such as glycerol, sorbitol, and polyethylene glycol are widely used, but they can present drawbacks such as high hydrophilicity, migration, or incompatibility with specific polymers (Chang and Nickerson 2015; Hopkins et al. 2019). Coproducts from agricultural and food industries provide an attractive source of natural plasticizers, often in the form of polyols, organic acids, and lipids. Plant oils and their derivatives, such as epoxidized soybean oil (a coproduct of soybean oil processing), have also been applied as secondary plasticizers, improving film ductility and barrier properties (Almeida et al. 2023).

Reinforcing Materials From Coproducts

Reinforcing agents play a crucial role in improving the mechanical strength, thermal stability, and barrier properties of packaging films. Among these, nanocellulose has emerged as one of the most studied materials (Picot‐Allain and Emmambux 2023). It can be obtained from a wide range of lignocellulosic coproducts, such as husks, pods, and oilseed meals. Through mechanical, enzymatic, or chemical pretreatments, cellulose fibers are broken down into nanoscale structures with high aspect ratios and large surface areas, making them excellent reinforcing fillers (Picot‐Allain and Emmambux 2023). When incorporated into packaging films, nanocellulose creates a dense network that restricts the mobility of polymer chains, thereby reducing gas and vapor transmission rates. For instance, films reinforced with nanocellulose from hemp pulp exhibit improved tensile strength and transparency, which are critical for food packaging (Barbash et al. 2022). Other reinforcing materials obtained from coproducts include lignin and protein nanoparticles. Lignin nanoparticles not only reinforce mechanical properties but also impart UV‐blocking ability (Sun, Li, et al. 2023). The choice of reinforcing additive and its surface modification significantly determines compatibility with the host matrix, which, in turn, influences dispersion, interfacial adhesion, and, ultimately, packaging performance.

Antioxidants From Coproducts

Oxidation is a significant contributor to quality deterioration in packaged foods, particularly those rich in lipids, pigments, and vitamins. Incorporating antioxidants into packaging materials can delay oxidative spoilage by scavenging free radicals or chelating pro‐oxidant metals. Agricultural coproducts are a rich source of natural antioxidants, particularly phenolic compounds, tocopherols, flavonoids, and tannins (Rodríguez‐Roque et al. 2021). For example, oilseed cakes (such as sesame or canola meal) are abundant in glucosinolates, phytic acid, oxalic acid, and erucic acid, which can provide strong radical scavenging activity. The incorporation of these compounds into films not only delays lipid oxidation in packaged foods but also contributes to the stability of the packaging material itself by reducing polymer degradation during storage (Rodríguez‐Roque et al. 2021). The presence of antimicrobial compounds in coproduct‐derived materials does not necessarily guarantee functional activity, as their effectiveness is strongly dependent on concentration, availability, and stability within the matrix. In many cases, these bioactive compounds may be present at levels below the threshold required for antimicrobial efficacy or may be partially bound within the material structure, limiting their accessibility. Therefore, isolation, enrichment, or concentration steps are often required before incorporation into packaging systems to ensure that sufficient active compound levels are available to exert meaningful antimicrobial performance. The mode of incorporation matters, and bioactive‐rich extracts can be directly blended into the polymer matrix, immobilized on the film surface, or encapsulated into carriers for controlled release. However, the compatibility and stability of antioxidants within the matrix remain challenges. For instance, phenolic compounds may interact with protein matrices via hydrogen bonding or covalent interactions, sometimes affecting film flexibility (Ozdal et al. 2013). Antioxidants incorporated into active packaging systems can protect not only packaged food products but also the packaging material itself from oxidative degradation. Through radical scavenging and metal chelation mechanisms, these compounds can improve the oxidative stability and durability of polymer matrices while simultaneously reducing lipid oxidation in food systems (Roman et al. 2016; Tian et al. 2013).

Emulsifiers From Coproducts

Emulsifiers can stabilize emulsions within packaging formulations, coatings, or nanocomposite dispersions that contain immiscible phases. Lecithin derived from oilseed processing (such as soy, sunflower, and canola meal) is a classic example of a coproduct emulsifier. It is widely used not only in food but also in packaging to improve film‐forming properties and emulsification of hydrophobic additives (Nieto et al. 2024). Proteins from coproduct streams (e.g., hemp protein, canola protein) can also act as natural emulsifiers when hydrolyzed or modified to enhance surface activity (He et al. 2024; Sun, Chai, et al. 2023). The presence of natural emulsifiers in packaging materials can enhance the dispersion of bioactive compounds or nanoparticles within the matrix, resulting in films with a more uniform structure and improved mechanical properties. They can also be used in edible coatings that extend shelf life by controlling moisture and gas exchange at the food surface.

Antimicrobials From Coproducts

Microbial spoilage is another major issue in food preservation. Incorporating antimicrobial agents into packaging films provides active protection against pathogenic and spoilage microorganisms. Coproducts offer a wide variety of natural antimicrobials, including phenolic compounds and essential oils. Oilseed meals such as sunflower meal, soybean meal, and sesame meal contain antimicrobial compounds with proven antimicrobial properties (Usman et al. 2023). These antimicrobials can be directly integrated into packaging films or encapsulated to allow controlled release. Their inclusion often leads to synergistic effects, such as simultaneous antioxidant and antimicrobial activity. However, strong interactions with the host polymer may affect film integrity, and sensory impacts (e.g., odor or flavor transfer from essential oils) must be carefully managed.

Salarbashi et al. (2016) utilized ZnO nanoparticles as an antimicrobial agent in soy‐based films. These films could inhibit the growth of Penicillium mycelia by up to 70.44% when 5% nanoparticles were added. Canola meal is an abundant waste material often used in antimicrobial active packaging. For instance, antimicrobial active peptides were derived from the hydrolysis of rapeseed protein isolate and embedded into chitosan films (Zhang et al. 2019), and cinnamaldehyde essential oils were used to make canola protein‐based active films (Dissanayake et al. 2022), showcasing the vast potential of canola coproducts in active packaging systems.

Bioactive Compounds for Active Packaging

Beyond specific categories such as antioxidants or antimicrobials, many coproducts contain a broader range of bioactive compounds, including polyphenols, flavonoids, alkaloids, saponins, and terpenes, that can be utilized to design active packaging. Active packaging not only serves as a passive barrier but also interacts with food or its environment to enhance shelf life and safety. Saponins from soy meal exhibit both emulsifying and antimicrobial functions. Incorporating these compounds into films can lead to multifunctional packaging that delays oxidation and prevents microbial growth (Perinelli et al. 2022). In addition, some coproduct proteins, such as those from canola, hemp, and sunflower, contain phenolic compounds that can act as UV barriers in packaging due to the brownish color they impart (Dissanayake et al. 2022).

The challenge lies in achieving controlled release and maintaining the bioactivity of these compounds during film processing and storage. Microencapsulation, nanoemulsion techniques, and surface functionalization are standard approaches for stabilizing and delivering coproduct‐derived bioactive compounds in packaging. The success of incorporating these additives depends mainly on their compatibility with the polymer matrix. Interactions between additives and polymers influence film morphology, mechanical strength, barrier properties, and release behavior.

For instance, hydrophilic additives such as polyols can enhance the flexibility of hydrophilic matrices (e.g., starch and proteins) but may increase water sensitivity. Hydrophobic antioxidants from oilseed coproducts may be better suited for lipid‐based or composite films, but often require emulsification for homogeneous dispersion. Reinforcing nanocellulose requires surface modification to improve compatibility with hydrophobic polymers such as PLA. Moreover, strong interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonding between phenolics and proteins) can sometimes reduce the release of additives, while weak interactions may lead to rapid migration and reduced effectiveness. Thus, tailoring compatibility through chemical modification, encapsulation, or blending strategies is crucial.

Fabrication Techniques

Sustainable food packaging has emerged as a vital field in the global effort to reduce plastic pollution from petroleum‐based sources and address environmental concerns. The utilization of agricultural coproducts, particularly from the pulse and oilseed industry, presents a promising pathway to develop eco‐friendly, biodegradable, and functional packaging solutions. These coproducts can be transformed into thin films, semirigid containers, rigid containers, and other structures using a range of conventional and emerging processing technologies. Each fabrication method varies in terms of mechanical performance, scalability, cost, and suitability for specific types of biopolymers and applications. Solvent Casting

Solvent casting is one of the most accessible and widely used techniques for producing thin packaging films, particularly at the laboratory scale. In this method, biopolymers such as soy protein, pea starch, or lentil flour derivatives are dissolved in an appropriate solvent, often water or an alcohol–water mixture, along with plasticizers and functional additives. The solution is then cast onto a flat surface and dried under controlled conditions to remove the solvent, leaving a uniform film. Hopkins et al. (2019) studied five different legume (soy, lupin, pea, lentil, and faba bean) protein‐based solvent‐casted films to identify the film characteristics. The authors stated that the highest and lowest tensile strengths were identified in the faba bean films and lupin protein films, respectively. However, the most promising and suitable coproduct was pea protein‐based thin films due to their relatively high tensile strength and elongation at break and low WVP. This technique is particularly well‐suited for hydrophilic polymers and has been used successfully to create edible coatings (Camiletti et al. 2023) and biodegradable films (Akhila et al. 2023) with moderate mechanical properties and barrier performance. Cháirez‐Jiménez et al. (2024) tried to reinforce solvent‐casted canola protein‐based films by adding graphite oxide to improve the water and oxygen barrier properties. The study improved the oxygen barrier properties by 665% compared to neat protein films, demonstrating the potential of the solvent‐casting method for future research.

The solvent‐casting method is a preliminary, lab‐scale method used to assess the compatibility of a biopolymer before scale‐up for pilot or large‐scale production. According to Ghosal et al. (2018), a less oriented crystalline structure was observed in the thermal properties due to the longer solvent evaporation times in the solvent casting method. Furthermore, rapid solvent evaporation could reduce crystallinity and induce greater amorphous properties in flaxseed mucilage films (Akhila et al. 2023). This behavior was observed in most solvent‐cast oilseed proteins, where nanomaterials were incorporated to increase crystallinity and enhance the tensile properties of the packaging material (Abookleesh et al. 2024; Dissanayake et al. 2023). The time taken for solvent evaporation also varies with the climatic conditions unless otherwise controlled, and environmental conditions are supplied, which is again not economically viable. Plasticization plays a vital role in this method. Once the solvent (usually water) evaporates, the polymer matrix is susceptible to brittleness, cracking, and breaking. Film properties vary with the amount and type of plasticizer level, where higher plasticizer levels add more flexibility while reducing the water barrier properties (Chang and Nickerson 2014; Hopkins et al. 2019). However, the other methods could perform regardless of the level of plasticization (Jerez et al. 2007). The limitations of this method include longer drying times, solvent recovery concerns, low water barrier properties, and challenges in scalability for industrial production. In terms of industrial scalability, this method lacks performance compared to other methods, such as electrospinning (Ghosal et al. 2018) and extruded thin films (Ochoa‐Yepes et al. 2019).

Extrusion

Extrusion is a scalable and industrially relevant process used to fabricate films, sheets, and trays from thermoplastic biopolymers. Bioplastics derived from defatted soy flour, pea starch, or other coproduct blends are subjected to heat and pressure as they are pushed through a die to form continuous films. The technique allows for high throughput, uniform thickness, and the integration of functional additives, including antioxidants or antimicrobial agents from seed husks or meal extracts. Melikoğlu et al. (2023) investigated the effect of nanocrystalline cellulose fillers derived from sunflower head waste as a reinforcement material for LDPE‐based extruded plastic films. According to the results, the nanocrystalline cellulose content should be below 15 wt% to obtain improved film properties, such as 20 MPa tensile strength and 250% EB, which is perfect for food packaging. The authors mentioned that the addition of higher fillers could cause uneven blending due to the nonhomogeneous nature of the nanocomposite. Extrusion is advantageous due to its compatibility with existing plastic processing infrastructure and improving the barrier properties of packaging films. Gunning et al. (2014) made hemp fiber from hemp waste material for extruded films and obtained 17.9 MPa of TS. However, challenges persist in processing heat‐sensitive biopolymers and maintaining consistent film quality (Prabha et al. 2021). Ochoa‐Yepes et al. (2019) studied the effect of lentil protein on starch films in both solvent casting and extruded films. Compared with the solvent‐casting method, thermocompression can produce films with higher toughness, whereas cast films are more flexible. Furthermore, starch–protein films prepared by casting showed pinholes, whereas extruded films were homogeneous, indicating the high potential of the extrusion molding method for industrial film preparation. As this method is continuous in production, maintaining a homogeneous film composition is very important. The extrusion method can predetermine the degradation temperature. Processing the packaging materials below that maximum point would allow the material to reach its full thermoelastic potential as a thermoplastic material (Castro‐Criado et al. 2025). Extrusion technology can increase the flexural strength of blend materials, especially those between industrial materials and coproducts, due to the high‐temperature processing parameters. Dey, Rahman, Yodo, et al. (2023) reported that PLA and soy fiber and protein‐induced polymer blends have higher flexural strengths in the mechanical properties of extruded filaments. As extrusion is already established in the food and polymer industries, it offers a technologically mature platform for upcycling oilseed industry residues into biodegradable, circular‐economy packaging solutions (Granados‐Carrera et al. 2025).

Compression Molding

Compression molding is primarily used for producing rigid packaging items such as trays, clamshell containers, and biobased cutlery. In this method, a premeasured amount of biopolymer blend, often reinforced with fiber residues or fillers (e.g., from flaxseed husks or soy hulls), is placed in a mold cavity and compressed under heat. The resulting products have high structural integrity and are suitable for replacing plastic or polystyrene (PS) in single‐use applications. Versino et al. (2021) developed a composite biofoam using sunflower oil press cake incorporated with cassava starch and glycerol through the thermocompression method. Researchers have attempted to find a solution to replace PS foams, which are widely used in food packaging and the transportation industry. The processing conditions were at 170°C for 3.5 min under a hydraulic press with 100 bars. The density of the commercial PS was 0.03 g/cm3, while bio‐foam had a minimum density of 0.15 g/cm3. Furthermore, the water solubility of the PS was 0.1%, whereas it was 5.1% in the biofoam. These higher densities would devalue the quality of the bio foam, even though it is helping the circular bioeconomic process. Another group of researchers developed bioplastic sheets using 41% canola protein, 41% PVA, and 18% glycerol as the plasticizer (Patel et al. 2016). A percentage of 60% CaCO3 was incorporated as the filler material, resulting in a 22.64 MPa tensile strength and a 10.69% EB. The authors suggested that these composites are suitable for the preparation of lightweight cups and spoons, offering a zero‐waste alternative to the food industry.

Injection Molding

Injection molding allows the creation of complex three‐dimensional (3D) shapes such as containers, cups, and lids using thermoplastic biopolymers. Agricultural coproducts can be formulated into granules or composites and melted into mold cavities under pressure. This technique enables precision fabrication and mass production, especially when using blends with thermoplastic starch, protein isolates, or PLA derived from agro sources. Rahman et al. (2023) used soybean hulls and soya protein isolate to produce bioplastic cups. These cups are morphologically very similar to polyethylene cups. Perez‐Puyana et al. (2016) molded (60 × 10 × 1) mm3 rectangular‐shaped blocks using pea protein and glycerol. The authors mentioned that the tensile strength of the pea protein‐based molds is higher compared to that of soy protein‐based bioplastics. However, according to the literature, the tensile strength and elongation at break are 30% and 40%, respectively, in pea protein bioplastics compared with commercial LDPE. Jiménez‐Rosado et al. (2022) conducted a study to compare the differences between the injection molding method and the extrusion method in soy protein bioplastics. In this study, both water and glycerol were used as plasticizers, and the injection molding method resulted in higher mechanical properties than extrusion. Although the extrusion method did not improve the properties as expected, the authors suggested that the method would be the next step toward product commercialization in the packaging industry.

Blow Molding

Blow molding is a key industrial route for converting oilseed‐industry coproducts (soy, canola, sunflower hulls/fibers, and protein fractions) into high‐value, hollow food‐packaging items (bottles, jars, plant pots, and containers). Its importance lies in very high throughput, low per‐unit cost for hollow geometries, and direct compatibility with PLA and PLA‐based biocomposite pellets produced from oilseed fillers. Therefore, waste streams can be upcycled into durable, compostable packaging while preserving established manufacturing infrastructure (Ilyas et al. 2021). However, successful use of oilseed coproducts in blow molding requires careful control of filler particle size and surface treatment, optimized melt rheology (to provide sufficient melt strength and stretchability during the parison/blow step), and often compatibilizers or polymer blends to avoid brittleness and poor dispersion. Rahman et al. (2023) demonstrated feasible formulations and processing routes for PLA‐soybean hull fiber composites and showed how palletization and downstream molding (injection/blow) can produce functional containers when composition and thermal/rheological processing are optimized. Huntrakul et al. (2020) developed transparent sachet pouches using pea protein and acetylated cassava starch to store soybean and olive oil using the blow‐film extrusion method. The sachets provided adequate protection when stored under varying humidity conditions over 3 months.

Electrospinning

Electrospinning is an advanced technique to create nanofibrous films with enhanced surface area, breathability, and functional properties. It involves applying a high‐voltage electric field to a polymer solution, causing it to eject fine fibers that are collected on a grounded surface (Amjadi et al. 2024; Ghosal et al. 2018). Electrospun nanofibers were developed by Aydogdu et al. (2019) using lentil flour and polyethylene oxide (PEO) by encapsulating gallic acid because the electrospinning method is ideal for encapsulating heat‐sensitive bioactive compounds. The incorporation of PEO made the polymer blend more electrospinable by reducing the strong repulsive forces between the protein materials. These materials were used to pack the walnuts in this study, and according to the results, encapsulation was successful, enhancing the oxidative stability of the active food packaging. Furthermore, this method can formulate nano‐ and submicron fibers and effectively incorporate active ingredients such as gallic acid into packaging materials. Amjadi et al. (2024) successfully incorporated black chickpea (BCP) peel anthocyanins into a BCP protein film as a double‐layered active packaging for seafood products. The authors mentioned that, due to the high surface area, the efficiency of the anthocyanin active agents was improved. According to Ghosal et al. (2018), the electrospinning method is better in terms of the preparation of membrane films compared to solvent‐casted films. The electrospun membranes with an amorphous structure were soft and flexible without compromising durability, while membranes made from the same solvent but with semicrystalline regions were stiff and brittle. The authors suggested that the reason for the above behavior is the gaps between the electrospun polymer strands and weak intermolecular cohesion, which gives the chains more freedom to move. As a result, the material deforms more easily under applied stress, and once the stress is released, the polymer chains quickly return to their relaxed state because of the additional free volume. The electrospinning method produces ultrafine fibers with exceptionally high surface area, porosity, and tunable morphology. This allows the creation of lightweight, biodegradable nanofibrous mats with functional properties that conventional methods (casting, extrusion, compression molding) cannot achieve (Alsadat‐Seyedbokaei et al. 2024; Mallavia and Falco 2020). The major limitations of processing industrial coproducts using advanced techniques such as electrospinning are the incompatibility of polymer material with the processing technology and scalability at the industrial level.

3D Printing

3D printing is a highly customizable, emerging technique for producing biodegradable packaging on demand, especially for niche applications or short product runs, due to its high cost (Sahil Panjabrao et al. 2025). Among several methods in 3D printing, inkjet printing and extrusion‐based 3D printing receive the closest attention, with high resolution and simultaneous printing ability. Using biopolymer‐based inks derived from soy protein, chickpea flour, or flaxseed mucilage, customized containers and cushioning materials can be fabricated layer by layer (Thakur et al. 2025). The authors suggested that the fused filament fabrication (FFF) method has higher potential in biopolymer‐based thermoplastic 3D printing packaging applications. Dey et al. (2023) used soybean protein and soy hulls to make 3D‐printed compostable cups suitable for preparing rigid compostable trays for food packaging using the FFF method. The gaps between the two consecutive layers were identified as significant limitations in maintaining cup consistency, resulting in low water retention. Balla et al. (2020) studied the effect of 3D printing technology on soybean hull derivative fibers for food packaging materials. The authors observed some drawbacks in this method, such as nozzle clogging due to wood particle aggregation, microscopic printing‐induced defects on the printing materials, and increased porosity due to water and gas evaporation during printing. The authors suggested that increasing the processing temperature could address some of the shape‐related issues. Solvent‐cast 3D printing is another type of 3D printing where solid polymer materials are mixed with a solvent inside the printing machine, and the solvent evaporates after printing. The major limitation of this method is the additional cost of solvent evaporation and the development of unsuitable mechanical and structural properties as a food packaging material due to solvent evaporation. Compared to the solvent casting method, this method offers greater customizability and faster processing time as advantages, but is limited in mechanical characteristics (Witek‐Krowiak et al. 2024). Despite its limitations, 3D printing has several advantages over other fabrication methods. Morales et al. (2021) reported the high potential and compatibility of combining synthetic and oilseed industry coproducts when using a 3D‐printed composite material formulation by mixing recycled polypropylene with cocoa bean shells. They mentioned that thermal processing should be performed below 230°C due to material degradation and could be applicable for even higher temperatures in shorter processing times. Another advantage of this method is the ability to fabricate multilayer biocomposites with high efficiency. S. Li et al. (2022) reported a sandwiched type of multilayered 3D‐printed packaging material with antimicrobial intelligent capabilities due to proper dispersion of LG essential oils. The authors further identified 3D printing as an efficient active food packaging system compared to the conventional solvent‐casting method, owing to its multilayer compatibility.

Overall, solvent casting is suitable for small‐scale edible and antimicrobial coatings due to its gentle processing and ability to incorporate bioactive compounds, but it is limited by long drying times and low scalability. Extrusion molding and compression molding are industrially relevant, producing films, sheets, and biodegradable plates with higher scalability, although they require careful control of moisture, temperature, and protein properties. Injection molding enables rigid packaging but often requires protein blends or chemical modification to ensure melt processability. Electrospinning allows the creation of functional nanofibers for active packaging. At the same time, blow molding and 3D printing offer options for bottles, containers, and custom designs, although both face challenges with protein melt strength and low mechanical performance.

Although biopolymer‐based films show considerable potential as sustainable food‐packaging materials, several challenges still limit their large‐scale commercialization. From a regulatory perspective, food‐contact materials must comply with safety requirements established by agencies such as the FDA, including migration limits, toxicological safety, allergen labeling, and the use of approved additives and plasticizers. Variability in raw material composition and the presence of residual solvents or bioactive compounds may also require additional safety evaluations before commercial approval.

For example, biodegradable packaging materials intended for food applications must comply with food‐contact safety regulations to ensure that no harmful substances migrate into food products. In the United States, food‐contact materials are regulated by the FDA under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (21 CFR), which requires the safety evaluation of packaging components, additives, and migration limits (FDA 2024). In the European Union, biodegradable packaging materials must comply with Framework Regulation (EC) No. 1935/2004 (European Commission 2026) and Good Manufacturing Practice Regulation (EC) No. 2023/2006 (European Commission 2008), which specify that packaging materials should not transfer substances to food in quantities that could endanger human health or alter food quality. In addition, compostable and biodegradable plastics are often evaluated according to standards such as ASTM D6400 (ASTM 2022) for biodegradability and composability performance. Therefore, in addition to biodegradability, biopolymer‐based packaging materials must demonstrate chemical safety, stability, and regulatory compliance before commercial food‐packaging applications.

Industrial processing feasibility remains another important consideration. Many biopolymer‐based films exhibit high sensitivity to moisture, limited thermal stability, and lower mechanical performance compared to petroleum‐based plastics, which can complicate processing using conventional extrusion, casting, or thermoforming equipment. In addition, achieving consistent film properties during large‐scale manufacturing can be challenging due to batch‐to‐batch variability in agricultural protein sources.

Market readiness and commercialization are further influenced by production cost, shelf‐life stability, consumer acceptance, and competition with inexpensive petroleum‐based materials. Although increasing environmental awareness and regulatory pressure to reduce plastic waste are driving interest in biodegradable packaging, further improvements in barrier properties, scalability, and cost‐effective processing are necessary for wider industrial adoption. Future research should therefore focus on developing biopolymer‐based films with enhanced functional performance while ensuring regulatory compliance and economic feasibility for commercial applications.

Challenges, Future Research Directions, and Conclusions

Agricultural coproducts and food‐processing coproducts are rich in proteins, polysaccharides, lipids, and bioactive compounds, offering great potential for developing functional and sustainable biopolymer‐based packaging. Despite this promise, several challenges limit their industrial application. The functional properties of biopolymers obtained from coproducts vary widely depending on the source material, extraction method, and post‐processing treatments. Protein isolates from canola, soy, hemp, sunflower, and cottonseed exhibit differences in mechanical strength, thermal stability, and barrier performance, making the fabrication of consistent films challenging. Furthermore, many biopolymers are inherently hydrophilic, which increases WVP and limits their use in moisture‐sensitive packaging applications. Incorporating functional additives, such as natural plasticizers, antioxidants, emulsifiers, and antimicrobial compounds, can enhance flexibility, shelf life, and mechanical performance. However, compatibility between the additives and the polymer matrix remains a critical issue. Strong interactions between phenolic compounds and proteins may reduce the release of additives, while weak interactions may lead to rapid migration, impacting both film functionality and food safety. Processing challenges, such as the heat sensitivity of proteins during extrusion, compression, or injection molding, further complicate the production of homogeneous, defect‐free films at an industrial scale.

Future research should focus on developing strategies to overcome these limitations. Tailoring compatibility between coproduct‐derived additives and polymer matrices through chemical modification, encapsulation, or blending can enhance mechanical, barrier, and bioactive release properties. Exploring multilayer or composite films that combine different biopolymers and additives may optimize water‐ and gas‐barrier performance while maintaining structural integrity. Advanced fabrication techniques such as electrospinning and 3D printing provide opportunities to incorporate heat‐sensitive bioactives and produce customizable, high‐surface‐area films. However, scalability and cost remain significant barriers. In addition, correlating the chemical composition and structure of coproduct‐based polymers with their functional performance is essential for targeted material selection. Life‐cycle assessment and techno‐economic analyses will be crucial in evaluating environmental and commercial feasibility, ensuring that sustainable packaging solutions are both eco‐friendly and economically viable. Research on controlled‐release systems for active compounds, such as microencapsulation of antioxidants or antimicrobial peptides, can further enhance the efficacy and stability of functional films.

In conclusion, pulse‐ and oilseed‐based packaging represent a vastly underutilized resource with high potential for sustainable packaging. Advances in extraction, modification, reinforcement, and the design of active or intelligent packaging have demonstrated the ability to produce films and containers with tailored mechanical, thermal, and barrier properties. Overcoming challenges related to moisture sensitivity, additive compatibility, processing complexity, and scalability is critical for successful industrial adoption. Effective utilization of these coproducts not only adds value to agricultural and food‐processing residues but also reduces reliance on petroleum‐based plastics, contributing to a circular bioeconomy and advancing environmentally sustainable food packaging technologies.
