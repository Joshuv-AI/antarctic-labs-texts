Alterations in the ultrasound appearance of seminiferous tubules after gonadotropin treatment in patients with azoospermia because of hypogonadotropic hypogonadism
Authors: Shoichi Nariyoshi, Kazuma Nakano, Gen Sukegawa, Yuji Tsuji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo demonstrate the relationship between ultrasound (US) changes in seminiferous tubules during gonadotropin replacement therapy and spermatogenic progression in patients with azoospermia because of hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (HH).DesignRetrospective observational study.SubjectsTen patients from 2 private male infertility clinics in Japan with azoospermia because of HH received human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) + recombinant follicle-stimulating hormone replacement (rFSH).ExposureGonadotropin treatment with hCG and rFSH.Main Outcome MeasuresSemen analysis and US evaluation of seminiferous tubules.ResultsAfter the initial treatment with hCG alone, hCG and rFSH were administered by self-injection. Sperm appeared within the ejaculate in 9 of the 10 patients treated with gonadotropin replacement therapy. Thick seminiferous tubules, defined as >300 μm in diameter on the US image, were observed just before the first sperm appeared in the ejaculate. Furthermore, an increased density of thickened seminiferous tubules, as observed on US, corresponded to higher sperm counts in the ejaculate.ConclusionAmong patients with HH, a strong correlation was observed between the degree of spermatogenesis stimulated by gonadotropin and the alterations observed in the US images of their seminiferous tubules. To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating alterations in spermatogenesis as intratesticular morphological changes, specifically enlargement of seminiferous tubules, by US imaging.

Male hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (HH) is a condition characterized by reduced secretion of gonadotropins resulting from a disorder of the hypothalamus-pituitary axis, leading to diminished testicular function and impaired spermatogenesis (1, 2). Although male HH is a rare cause of azoospermia (2), it has garnered significant attention because men with HH who are azoospermic respond well to treatment (2) such as gonadotropin replacement or pulsatile gonadotropin-releasing hormone (3, 4, 5). However, the induction of spermatogenesis requires long-term endocrine treatment, which may extend over several years (3, 6, 7). Therefore, assessing alterations in spermatogenesis during endocrine therapy is crucial for prompt decision-making regarding treatment strategies in the modern era of assisted reproductive technology.

Our earlier report established that the identification of thick seminiferous tubules on testicular US in patients with nonobstructive azoospermia serves as a strong indicator of successful sperm retrieval (8). To evaluate whether testicular US is useful for assessing alterations in spermatogenesis in men with HH, we investigated the link between changes in the US images of seminiferous tubules throughout the course of gonadotropin replacement therapy and the advancement of sperm production.

Materials and methods

Under an institutional review board–approved protocol (Ebisu Tsuji Clinic institutional review board number 19003), we conducted a retrospective review of the medical records of consecutive male patients with azoospermia because of HH.

Patients

We identified 10 patients diagnosed with azoospermia because of HH. The diagnosis of HH was based on low serum testosterone levels (<100 ng/dL) combined with low or normal-to-low serum follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH). Normal adrenocortical function and thyroid function were confirmed by normal serum levels of adrenocorticotropic hormone, cortisol, thyroid-stimulating hormone, and free thyroxine. Gonadotropin-releasing hormone stimulation tests, human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) stimulation tests, and pituitary magnetic resonance imaging were also performed; however, genetic testing was not conducted. Among them, 7 had congenital male HH, including 2 who presented with HH accompanied by anosmia (Kallmann syndrome), and 3 had adult-onset male HH (Supplemental Table 1, available online). One additional patient, who had previously undergone surgery for a brain tumor, was excluded from the study because of early tumor recurrence.

All 10 patients received hCG plus recombinant FSH (rFSH) (hCG + rFSH) therapy at our outpatient clinic between May 2008 and March 2025. One patient (#5) had previously started hCG + rFSH therapy at another institution.

Gonadotropin replacement therapy

Gonadotropin replacement therapy was initiated with subcutaneous hCG (HCG MOCHIDA; Mochida Pharmaceutical Co., Tokyo, Japan) injections at a dose of 5,000 IU, 3 times weekly. Three months after pretreatment with hCG, administration of 150 IU of recombinant human FSH (Gonalef Pen; Merck Biopharma Co., Tokyo, Japan) was started 3 times weekly, concomitantly with hCG.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the appearance of sperm in the ejaculate. The secondary outcomes included sperm count parameters (total sperm count, total motile sperm count, and total normal morphology sperm count) and US-determined testicular volume and density of thick seminiferous tubules.

Follow-up tests

Serum hormone levels (i.e., testosterone, LH, and FSH) were measured before endocrine treatment, 3 months after hCG pretreatment, and 1 month after hCG and FSH treatment.

Semen analysis was conducted according to the World Health Organization laboratory manual for examining and processing human semen (9, 10, 11), along with US evaluation of the testes before and during gonadotropin therapy.

Ultrasound

All patients underwent grayscale and color Doppler US performed by board-certified sonographers (S.N., K.N.) at each clinic, using a high-frequency transducer (10–14 MHz, Aloka SSD3500, Aloka; Toshiba SSA-580A, Toshiba). Testicular volume was calculated using the largest dimension on the US images according to the Lambert formula: (length) × (width) × (height) × 0.71 (12). To optimize the US images for visualization of the seminiferous tubules, contrast and brightness were appropriately adjusted using graphics software, as previously described (8). Briefly, the contrast was set to 96%, whereas the brightness was initially set at 50% and gradually reduced to 30%. This gradual decrease in brightness revealed variations in the diameter of the seminiferous tubules. The visualization of thick seminiferous tubules was defined as >300 μm in diameter on the US image. To quantitatively compare the density of seminiferous tubules, the density of white pixels in the US images was used as a proxy for this study. In this study, a threshold of 50 was set to binarize the grayscale images, with pixels ranging from 51–255 being converted to white and those 50 or below being converted to black. The proportion of white and black pixels in the US image was calculated using ChatGPT-4o.

Statistical analysis

Mean with standard deviation or median with range was used as summary statistics for continuous variables, and proportion was used for categorical variables. The distributions of sperm count parameters and plasma concentrations of sex hormones are known to be skewed to the right side; therefore, the median was used for these variables. The posttreatment measures were compared with the pretreatment measures using a paired t test for testicular volume and density of thick seminiferous tubules and using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test for sex hormones and semen parameters. Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided P value of <.05. The statistical analysis was performed using Stata version 13.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

The visualization of thick seminiferous tubules, defined as >300 μm in diameter on the US image, occurred concurrently with or just before the first appearance of sperm in the ejaculate (Table 2).

One patient (#6) remained azoospermic for 25 months after treatment initiation. Neither an increase in testicular volume nor an increase in thickness of seminiferous tubules was observed on US images (Fig. 1E–H). Subsequently, microdissection testicular sperm extraction (micro-TESE) was performed. Microscopic examination of the testicles revealed thick seminiferous tubules in a specific area, and sperm were successfully retrieved from these tubules. Pregnancy was achieved by intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) using retrieved sperm.

In contrast, thick seminiferous tubules were observed within the testes on pretreatment US in patient #9 (Supplemental Fig. 2A, available online). Sperm appeared in the ejaculate after hCG monotherapy and the number of thick seminiferous tubules increased on the US images (Supplemental Fig. 2B). The density of thick seminiferous tubules in the testes further increased with subsequent hCG + rFSH treatment (Supplemental Fig. 2C), leading to a corresponding increase in the number of sperm in the ejaculate. The patient requested to discontinue self-injection after a spontaneous pregnancy. However, significant recurrence of subjective symptoms occurred 1 month after treatment discontinuation. Although endocrine analysis revealed a return to pretreatment levels (i.e., LH level of 0.1 mIU/mL, FSH level of 0.4 mIU/mL, and testosterone level of 1.7 ng/dL), semen analysis still demonstrated favorable parameters (semen volume, 2.8 mL; sperm concentration, 116 million/mL; sperm motility, 58%). Testicular US revealed decreased testicular volume (24–20 mL) and reduced seminiferous tubule diameter 1 month after the resumption of endocrine treatment (Supplemental Fig. 2D). Three months after resuming gonadotropin replacement therapy, however, the testicular volume (25 mL) and intratesticular US images recovered to the levels noted 10 months after treatment (Supplemental Fig. 2E).

Discussion

Studies have reported that 70%–90% of patients with HH achieve the appearance of sperm in the ejaculate after endocrine treatment (3, 4, 5). The mean time for sperm to appear in the ejaculate after starting treatment is approximately 9–18 months, depending on the specific therapy and patient characteristics (13, 14), although in some cases, it may take years (6, 7).

In recent years, the time available for trying to conceive has been reduced because of later marriages and delayed childbearing. Thus, it is not feasible for patients to wait several years for sperm to appear in the semen. Furthermore, ICSI using testicular sperm has become a treatment option, even if sperm do not appear within the semen, in the recent era of assisted reproductive technology (15, 16). Therefore, evaluating the progression of spermatogenesis before the appearance of sperm in the semen is important.

Several factors have been suggested as predictors of sperm appearance in the ejaculate. Larger testicular volume (1, 17, 18), lower gonadotropin levels (4), higher serum inhibin B concentration (1, 18), and no history of cryptorchidism (18) before treatment were consistently identified as significant predictors of successful induction of spermatogenesis. However, these factors are predictive of sperm appearance before treatment and are deemed insufficient for monitoring alterations in spermatogenesis during endocrine therapy.

Previous reports have indicated that testicular volume tends to increase steadily throughout the course of treatment with hCG and human menopausal gonadotropin (7). However, in this study, we observed that testicular volume increased after the initial administration of hCG. It increased further with the addition of rFSH but eventually plateaued and did not show any correlation with the number of ejaculated sperm. In patients with HH, testicular enlargement after gonadotropin treatment typically indicates that sperm production has been initiated. Specifically, the degree of spermatogenic activity can be evaluated by observing ultrasonographic alterations in the density of thick seminiferous tubules.

In the present study, enlarged seminiferous tubules were observed in US images before the initial detection of sperm in the ejaculate (Fig. 1C). Evidence from this study further revealed a significant positive relationship with the increased density of thickened seminiferous tubules, as observed on US images, which corresponded with higher subsequent sperm counts in the ejaculate (Fig. 1D). The responsiveness of these tubules to endocrine treatment was also apparent because their diameter decreased when the therapy was withdrawn (Supplemental Fig. 2D) and rapidly increased when reinstated, along with a recovery in the density of these enlarged tubules (Supplemental Fig. 2E). These outcomes reinforce our previous findings. Our earlier report established that the identification of such thick seminiferous tubules observed on testicular US in patients with nonobstructive azoospermia serves as a strong indicator of successful sperm retrieval (8). Seminiferous tubules exhibiting active and efficient spermatogenesis are known to typically exhibit an increased diameter owing to a higher density of germ cells (19). This observation is consistent with the findings of Ley and Leonard (7), who documented increases in seminiferous tubule diameter, germ cell quantity, and maturity through serial testicular biopsies during hCG and human menopausal gonadotropin therapy in patients with HH. Taken together, the observed enlargement of seminiferous tubules on US images in patients with HH is considered a reliable indicator of improved spermatogenesis. The gradual increase in the density of thick seminiferous tubules in US images, even without the appearance of sperm in the ejaculate, suggests an improvement in spermatogenesis in the testes.

Despite endocrine therapy, 10%–30% of patients remain azoospermic (3, 4, 5). For such patients, testicular sperm retrieval using micro-TESE may represent a viable treatment option. This procedure has a high success rate in retrieving sperm for ICSI (6, 15, 16). However, the appropriate observation period to determine whether micro-TESE should be performed remains unclear. Although some studies advocate considering micro-TESE if azoospermia persists after 1–2 years of endocrine therapy (6), a 2-year observation period seems exceedingly long given the current clinical realities and timelines in infertility care. We observed patient #6 for >2 years, during which no changes were noted in the US findings of the seminiferous tubules and no sperm was detected in the ejaculate. Subsequently, micro-TESE was conducted, and ICSI using the retrieved sperm resulted in pregnancy. Assessment of seminiferous tubules on US images is useful for predicting the optimal timing of sperm retrieval in such situations. We now consider micro-TESE appropriate if azoospermia remains and the US shows no evidence of thick seminiferous tubules after 1 year of endocrine therapy.

Many reports have indicated that micro-TESE achieves a high sperm retrieval rate in patients with HH who remain azoospermic after endocrine therapy (6, 15, 16). However, these individuals typically experience spermatogenic failure. Successful sperm retrieval in such cases often relies on the identification of limited foci of spermatogenesis within the testicular parenchyma. As illustrated by case 6 in our series, sperm were successfully retrieved from a specific testicular region containing clusters of larger-diameter seminiferous tubules in tissues primarily composed of thin tubules. When thick seminiferous tubules are not evident on preoperative US, microscopic exploration of the testicular tissue is necessary to identify the potentially sperm-bearing tubules. The need for a targeted microscopic search within the testicular parenchyma underlies the recommendation of micro-TESE over conventional TESE in this patient population.

In adult-onset HH, hCG monotherapy enhances spermatogenesis (14). In case 9, US revealed the presence of thick seminiferous tubules before treatment. After hCG monotherapy, the density of these thick seminiferous tubules increased, and ejaculated spermatozoa appeared. Observing seminiferous tubule images using US may provide valuable insights for selecting endocrine therapy. This can help determine whether hCG monotherapy may be effective or if combination therapy with FSH is necessary.

Ultrasound-based techniques for evaluating seminiferous tubule morphology in patients with azoospermia include our image processing method (8), as well as approaches previously reported using high-frequency US (20). However, neither approach has been widely adopted in clinical practice, likely because of the specialized expertise and complexity required for the procedure. Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) technology are expected to enhance our image processing techniques, potentially enabling US-based morphological evaluation of seminiferous tubules by users of all skill levels. Furthermore, applying AI technology would significantly simplify the measurement of seminiferous tubule density. We believe that, by streamlining US image processing through AI, this method will gain broader acceptance for visualizing spermatogenesis.

Our method is highly beneficial because it allows for the detection of improved spermatogenesis before it is evident in semen analysis. This capability is crucial for making appropriate treatment decisions for HH, modifying therapy during follow-up, and evaluating the indication for micro-TESE without unnecessary delays. This method holds promise for diagnosing a variety of spermatogenic disorders, including the differential diagnosis of nonobstructive and obstructive azoospermia. Furthermore, this method has the potential to be used for evaluating the effectiveness of treatments for various spermatogenic disorders.

Conclusion

The main finding of this study was the change in the intratesticular morphology observed by US after gonadotropin replacement therapy, particularly the increase in the diameter of the seminiferous tubules. This increase was indicative of improved spermatogenesis. Assessing the diameter of seminiferous tubules using US provides a noninvasive and real-time method for observing changes in spermatogenesis, demonstrating significant clinical value. To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating alterations in spermatogenesis as intratesticular morphological changes, specifically enlargement of seminiferous tubules, by US.
