Mapping mesenchymal diversity in the human small intestine and organoids
Authors: Kelli F Johnson, Xiangning Dong, Yu-Hwai Tsai, Angeline Wu, Sydney G Clark, Abigail Vallie, Sha Huang, Charlie J Childs, Rachel K Zwick, Ian Glass, Katherine D Walton, Ophir D Klein, Jason R Spence
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe organization of diverse mesenchymal populations during human small intestinal development is critical for tissue architecture and function yet remains poorly defined. Here, to construct a comprehensive, tissue-scale map of the developing human small intestine at single cell resolution, we leveraged single-cell RNA-sequencing data to build a Xenium spatial transcriptomics gene panel covering the cell diversity of the human small intestine. We defined five subpopulations occupying discrete anatomical locations within the lamina propria and submucosa—the subepithelial cells, lamina propria fibroblasts, submucosal fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells and CXCL13+ fibroblasts. Our data establish molecular markers to distinguish these populations in both sequencing and imaging data. We leverage this high-resolution atlas to interrogate cell–cell signalling, benchmark pluripotent stem cell-derived human intestinal organoids and to demonstrate how this resource can incorporate relative spatial organization into tissue analysis, with broad implications for modelling development, regeneration and disease.Subject terms: Cell lineage, Physiology, Cellular imaging, RNA sequencing, Intestinal stem cells

Main

The precise organization of the small intestine, from the crypt–villus axis enabling nutrient absorption to the muscle layers coordinating with the enteric nervous system (ENS) for peristalsis, is essential for function. Cellular heterogeneity in both the adult and developing human intestine has been explored extensively using single-cell approaches to study development, homeostasis and disease1–15. While tissue-scale features such as villus morphogenesis and cellular composition are well established for intestinal epithelium, musculature and ENS1,6,7,9,14,16–26, the mesoderm-derived fibroblast populations (also referred to as mesenchyme and/or stroma) remain poorly defined.

In mice, recent work has redefined historical terms such as ‘fibroblast’, ‘myofibroblast’ and ‘intestinal subepithelial myofibroblast’ based on molecular identity, function or localization27–40. Subepithelial ‘telocytes’ or ‘subepithelial myofibroblasts’ are characterized by high Pdgfra and Foxl1 expression, alongside two Pdgfra-low populations: Cd81+/Grem1+ ‘trophocytes’ and Cd34+/Pdpn+ ‘Pdgfra-low stroma’27,41–43. Distinct regional mesenchymal populations have also been shown to line the developing mouse foregut and pattern the gastrointestinal endoderm44, indicating functional roles tied to early spatial localization. Critically, much of this work was conducted in mice and our understanding of fibroblast populations in human development and human organoid development lags considerably45. A comprehensive atlas defining the identities and positions of fibroblast subtypes relative to all other cell types has not been established in humans27.

Here, we systematically define the molecular and spatiotemporal heterogeneity of the developing human intestine, with an emphasis on the poorly defined fibroblast populations. We integrated publicly available single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets spanning 5–21 weeks post-conception1,5,46,47 to identify highly enriched marker genes, which informed the design of a 10X Xenium probe set48–52 incorporating established intestinal markers and genes from key signalling and structural pathways. We interrogated a total of seven different developing human intestines spanning 7–22 weeks post-conception and across the length of the intestinal tract (duodenum, jejunum, ileum and colon). In addition, we examine full-thickness adult duodenum, and two different transplanted induced pluripotent stem cell-derived human intestinal organoid models (tHIOs)53–56.

Xenium imaging produced a cellular-resolution atlas capturing all major cell classes—epithelium, fibroblasts, smooth muscle, endothelium, ENS and immune cells—and their spatial organization. Our data identified smooth muscle cells (SMCs) plus four broad subpopulations occupying discrete anatomical locations: subepithelial cells (SECs)1,7, lamina propria fibroblasts (LPFs)45, submucosal fibroblasts (SMFs)45 and CXCL13+ fibroblasts7. Applying this atlas to tHIOs derived using two distinct methods, we found subtle differences between methods, suggesting that both cellular heterogeneity and spatial organization are important measures of tHIO fidelity when compared to the in vivo human intestine1,5,46–56.

Together, this work provides a spatially resolved, tissue-scale blueprint of the developing human small intestine, establishing molecular signatures to identify and track fibroblast populations across development. By benchmarking tHIOs against this atlas, we show that both cell-type composition and precise cellular topography are critical metrics for evaluating in vitro tissues. We anticipate that these findings will advance organoid design, disease modelling and therapeutic screening. More broadly, this resource transforms our transcriptomic understanding of the gut into a three-dimensional coordinate framework applicable across developmental biology, tissue engineering and gastroenterology with high precision.

Results

Spatial transcriptomics identifies major cell classes in the developing human intestine

Using our previously published scRNA-seq data1 (Fig. 1a,b and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2), established marker genes and genes of interest, we designed a custom 292-gene Xenium panel to identify cell types and states in the developing human small intestine1,3,7,28,29 (Fig. 1c,d and Supplementary Table 3; Methods).

Major cell classes were identified using well-established markers: CDH1 (epithelium), VIM (mesenchyme), PECAM1 (endothelium), PTPRC/CD45 (immune) and TUBB3 (ENS) (Supplementary Table 4). Subclass markers were also included, with a focus on the fibroblast populations identified in the scRNA-seq data1. Xenium spatial transcriptomics was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) archival tissue sections spanning 54–142 days post-conception (DPC) across all intestinal regions16 (Fig. 1e, duodenum, jejunum, ileum, colon, and Extended Data Fig. 1a), broadly matching the scRNA-seq stages (Supplementary Table 1). In total, seven biological specimens were analysed. The analysis focused on the small intestine (475,698 cells; Fig. 1d; Methods); distal ileum (when included in the sample imaging) and colon were excluded due to substantial transcriptomic differences compared to the small intestine40.

Samples were processed in two batches. The second batch included the Xenium In Situ Multimodal Cell Segmentation kit (n = 4; Extended Data Fig. 1b–e), which was not available for our first batch (n = 3; 57, 80 and 142 DPC). This kit combined probes and antibodies targeting cell borders and interiors to improve digital cell body segmentation (Extended Data Fig. 1c). Xenium Ranger and Xenium Explorer (10X Genomics, v2.0.0 and later) were used for segmentation and visualization (Extended Data Fig. 1d,e). Batch 1 samples were segmented using Xenium Explorer’s internal algorithm. Resulting segmentation maps, combined with (X, Y) probe coordinates, were used for clustering and graph-based analysis (Extended Data Fig. 1e). No notable differences in cell-type distribution or identity were observed between segmentation methods (Fig. 1d).

Xenium captures the diversity of cell types identified in scRNA-seq data

Following cell segmentation, transcript data for small intestinal regions (duodenum, jejunum and ileum) were extracted and imported into Seurat57,58 (Fig. 1c,d). Using a pipeline analogous to our scRNA-seq pipeline (Methods), we generated uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) embeddings, assigned cluster identities and quantified cell-type proportion within each sample (Fig. 1c–g). Differences in relative proportions between Xenium and scRNA-seq data were most likely technical artefacts: scRNA-seq used whole-thickness tissue dissociation, which can skew cell proportions59–63, whereas Xenium captured a fixed 5-µm cross-section preserving near-native proportions within the image plane, but are dependent upon the plane of tissue observed.

Interestingly, scRNA-seq showed slightly higher variation in the SMC and immune populations across developmental time (Fig. 1b, dark green and pink) compared to Xenium, where these populations remained consistent (Fig. 1d). The enrichment of immune cells in scRNA-seq probably reflects their preferential recovery during single-cell dissociation. The largest proportional difference between methods was in the epithelium, which was nearly doubled in Xenium versus scRNA-seq (scRNA-seq, 11.4–18.1%; Xenium, 30.3–36.5%) (Fig. 1b,d, gold). Fibroblasts compromised approximately half of the cells in both datasets (scRNA-seq, 47.2–52.4%; Xenium, 37.9–49.3%; Fig. 1b,d, navy). Notably, both platforms identified the muscularis mucosa between 80 and 105 DPC (Fig. 1b,d, light green), consistent with prior studies demonstrating muscularis mucosa differentiation beneath the crypt domain during this developmental window16,18,64. Overall, spatial data enabled visualization of tissue architecture and composition that was obscured in dissociated scRNA-seq datasets.

Identification of diverse fibroblast populations within the developing intestine

Starting with human scRNA-seq data (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 1) we extracted fibroblast populations4,6,7,32–35 (Supplementary Table 4) and computationally removed four populations: (1) SMCs of the muscularis mucosa and muscularis externa, (2) vascular SMCs/pericytes, (3) actively cycling cells and (4) interstitial cells of Cajal (ICCs) (Supplementary Table 5). Removal of these populations, whose highly distinct transcriptomes obscured variation among non-proliferative fibroblasts, allowed the more nuanced fibroblast transcriptomes to be resolved.

After batch correction, scRNA-seq fibroblasts clustered broadly by developmental stage (Fig. 2a), with each stage showing three major subpopulations: SECs, LPFs and SMFs. A fourth population, CXCL13+ fibroblasts, was observed in the most developmentally advanced samples (122–132 DPC) (Fig. 2c), comprising only 5.5% of cells in the scRNA-seq data. Although Xenium fibroblasts did not segregate by age (Fig. 2b), probably reflecting the smaller gene probe set, all four subpopulations (SEC, LPF, SMF and CXCL13+) were identified (Fig. 2d). Highly expressed marker genes for each subpopulation were defined in both datasets (Fig. 2e,f and Supplementary Table 5).

Localization of SECs, LPFs, SMFs and CXCL13+ cells within the intestine

Localization of the SEC (F3+) population

SECs in the developing human intestine have been previously described, marked by high PDGFRA and F3 expression1,5,7. These cells reside immediately adjacent to the epithelium along the crypt–villus axis9,11,12,15,17,19,21,22,38. In the scRNA-seq data, SEC subpopulation proportions shifted as development progressed (Fig. 2g), whereas Xenium proportions remained consistent across time (Fig. 2h), suggesting that dissociation altered SEC subtype proportions during scRNA-seq preparation.

SECs were identified at all developmental stages interrogated (54–142 DPC) (Figs. 2 and 3b(1–4) and Supplementary Table 6), were confirmed to reside directly adjacent to the epithelium, and robustly expressed F3. While F3 was largely specific to the SEC population within the fibroblasts, low-level F3 expression was also observed in the epithelium at early stages (54–57 DPC) and in the muscularis externa after 105 DPC (Fig. 3b(1–4)). DLL1 was expressed broadly across both SECs and LPFs in our Xenium data.

Localization of the LPF (FABP5+/ADAMDEC1+) population

The LPF population resides within the villus core beneath the SEC layer, spanning from the villus shelf/crypt to the villus tip, and expresses FABP5, ADAMDEC1, ALDH1A3 and GPX3 (Fig. 2e,f). While we previously reported GPX3 expression in the villus core at 132 DPC1, its precise distribution was not described. Here, we show that GPX3 is most consistently expressed in LPFs after 122 DPC and into adulthood (Extended Data Fig. 3).

In the adult c) ADAMDEC1 expression is relatively lower than d) GPX3 expression and GPX3 has lost the gradient of expression and is expressed throughout the entire lamina propria compartment. LPF marker expression was dynamic during early development (47-57 DPC), with robust FABP5 and low ADAMDEC1 expression (Fig. 3c(1),d(1)). From 80 DPC onward, LPFs co-express FABP5 and ADAMDEC1 (Fig. 3c(2–4),d(2–4)), after which FABP5 was progressively lost, leaving ADAMDEC1 as the predominant marker. GPX3 and ALDH1A3 also appeared during later stages of development (Fig. 2e,f and Extended Data Fig. 3).

To characterize this temporal progression, we performed pseudo-time analysis using Slingshot65. Reclustered LPFs group predominantly by developmental stage (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b) and Slingshot predicted a trajectory from early FABP5high to late ADAMDEC1high states, with intermediate co-expressing cells visualized and quantified across stages (Extended Data Fig. 4c–h). LPFs comprised 25.0–30.3% of the fibroblasts in the Xenium data across development (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Table 6).

FISH validation confirmed widespread FABP5 expression and absence of ADAMDEC1 at 57 DPC; by 82 DPC, ADAMDEC1 was clearly expressed in LPFs but not in F3+ SECs (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). Although FAPB5 was highly specific to the LPFs within the fibroblasts, it was also expressed in several non-fibroblast cell types including pericytes (RGS5+), endothelium (PECAM1+), epithelium (CDH1+) and smooth muscle (ACTA2+), which dominated FABP5 distribution from 82 DPC onward. LPFs are distinguished specifically by a FABP5+/PECAM1−/RGS5−/ACTA2−/CDH1− signature, making FABP5 a reliable LPF marker only when other expressing populations are filtered from the analysis (Extended Data Fig. 2a–d).

Localization of the SMF (SHISA3+) population

SMFs reside between the crypts/muscularis mucosa and the muscularis externa, identified by markers SHISA3, FIBIN and C1QTNF3 (Fig. 2e,f), of which SHISA3 was the most consistently robust marker across all developmental stages. Early in development, SMFs formed a loosely organized layer several cells thick, from 105 DPC onward, the population expanded to continue to occupy the entire space between the muscularis mucosa and the inner circular muscle layer as the tissue thickened (Figs. 2f and 3e(1–4)). FISH confirmed SHISA3+ SMFs populating the submucosa, consistent with Xenium data (Extended Data Fig. 2c,d). Transcriptionally similar cells have been described as collagen and decorin expressing in prior studies1.

Localization of the CXCL13+ population

The CXCL13+ fibroblast population is small and rare, first appearing after 101 DPC in the scRNA-seq data. In Xenium data, CXCL13+ cells localized near crypt-based SECs, at the villus shelf, within Peyer’s patches or mid-way up the villus immediately adjacent or integrated within the SEC layer (Fig. 3f(4)). FISH confirmed the expression of CXCL13 in rare cells in an independent 125 DPC sample (Extended Data Fig. 2e,f).

Xenium spatial imaging confirmed four distinct non-smooth muscle fibroblast populations in the developing human small intestine: SECs (PDGRFAHI, F3+) adjacent to the epithelium; villus core-localized LPFs with temporally dynamic markers (FABP5+ early, ADAMDEC1+ later); SMFs (SHISA3+) in the submucosa between the muscularis externa and the muscularis mucosa; and rare CXCL13+ fibroblasts scattered within the villus shelf/upper-crypt region of the mucosa, integrated with the SECs or within Peyer’s patches.

Enhancing robustness of spatial transcriptomic data through spatial imputation

The limited number of features in the Xenium gene panel, while sufficient for cell segmentation, cell-type identification and mapping, constrains downstream analysis such cell–cell communication (Supplementary Table 3 and Extended Data Fig. 5). To overcome this, we leveraged CytoSPACE66 to impute scRNA-seq data from one developmental stage (122–132 DPC) onto a similarly staged Xenium map (142 DPC). CytoSPACE assigns the highest confidence spatial coordinates to each scRNA-seq cell within the spatial reference map, enabling spatial analysis at full scRNA-seq feature depth.

We then applied CellChat v267,68 to compare predicted cell–cell signalling pre- and post-imputation. CytoSPACE imputation substantially increased the number of predicted signalling interactions relative to the Xenium panel alone (Extended Data Fig. 5). We visualized predicted epidermal growth factor (EGF), WNT and HH signalling as chord plots, as all three pathways are well-characterized in the intestine with known ligand–receptor directionality1,5,69–79 (Fig. 4b–d). We then overlaid representative predicted interactions on segmented Xenium maps (Fig. 4b’–d’). Our data predicted EGF signalling oriented from ISCs (epiregulin; EREG) and enterocytes (EGF), consistent with known signalling patterns1,56,69 (Fig. 4b,b’), HH signalling from ISCs and enterocytes, which aligns with established expression of hedgehog ligands in the intestinal epithelium70,73 (Fig. 4c,c’), and WNT signalling originating from many cell types, including SMCs of the muscularis mucosa signalling to multiple cell types, including the epithelium and ISCs, consistent with the known role of WNT in intestinal development1,73 (Fig. 4d,d’).

CytoSPACE imputation thus effectively overcame the feature limitations of the Xenium probe set, enabling high-resolution spatial analysis with full scRNA-seq depth. Notably, this approach was robust despite the scRNA-seq and Xenium data originating from non-matched specimens, broadening the utility of both our atlas and existing public datasets.

Creating a spatial atlas of pluripotent stem cell-derived HIOs

We used the human intestinal atlas to interrogate cell types and organization in pluripotent stem cell-derived human intestinal organoids (HIOs). We have previously differentiated HIOs using two different methods with differing cellular complexity: EGF-grown HIOs possess limited cellular complexity in vitro, containing immature epithelium and mesenchyme, whereas EREG-grown HIOs co-differentiate multiple lineages including epithelium, mesenchyme, endothelium and ENS56,80.

In vitro, HIOs lack organizational complexity; for example, they lack a crypt–villus axis; however, following transplant under the kidney capsule of an immunocompromised mouse, HIOs acquire cellular and organizational complexity, developing a crypt–villus axis and distinct smooth muscle layers63,64. Therefore, we interrogated EGF- and EREG-tHIOs using the Xenium probe set56 (Supplementary Table 3).

The human-specific probe set resulted in comparatively low transcript counts and diversity in murine cells present in the tissue sections, enabling computational removal of host mouse cells from further analysis (Extended Data Fig. 6). Most low-expression cells were located at the tHIO periphery where murine tissue remained attached, which was confirmed visually (Extended Data Fig. 6). Filtering by feature count rather than region-of-interest preserved human cells invading mouse tissue while removing mouse cells within the tHIOs (Extended Data Fig. 6f,g).

Despite notable differences in pre-transplant cellular composition between EGF and EREG HIOs54–56, overall cellular composition and tissue architecture were similar after transplantation (Fig. 5c–e). A robust human-derived endothelial population in the EGF-grown tHIO was somewhat surprising given that endothelial cells present in EGF-grown HIO cultures during the first week in vitro are lost over time and are generally absent from EGF tHIOs46,54; however, this is consistent with prior observations of high variability in endothelial abundance in EGF-derived HIOs and VEGF-enhanced survival46, which likely accounts for our current observation.

Cell composition in tHIOs was broadly comparable to the developing small intestine: epithelium comprised ~30% of tHIO cells versus ~33.7% in intestinal tissue, fibroblasts comprised 22.4% (EGF) and 25.2% (EREG) versus 32.8% in tissue, SMCs (muscularis externa and muscularis mucosa combined) were proportionally higher in tHIOs (EGF, 37.1%; EREG, 33.4%) than in tissue (22.5%). ENS and endothelial proportions were also similar across conditions: ENS comprised 2.0% (EGF) and 3.4% (EREG) in tHIOs versus 3.6% in Xenium tissue data, endothelium comprised 8.7% (EGF) and 4.2% (EREG) versus 7.7% in Xenium tissue (compare Fig. 1b, d and Fig. 5c).

We observed that tHIOs broadly self-organized into a multilayered structure resembling the developing human intestine. Each lumen-containing organoid unit possessed a single epithelial layer organized in a crypt–villus-like axis, with ISC-containing crypts and the absorptive villus populations, a lamina propria, a layer several cells thick of muscularis mucosa, a SMF layer containing ENS and endothelium, and two distinct muscularis externa-like layers also containing ENS and endothelium (Fig. 5d,e).

Identification of diverse fibroblast populations within tHIOs

We examined whether SECs, LPFs, SMFs, and CXCL13+ fibroblasts were present and appropriately organized within tHIOs. Most fibroblast subtypes identified in vivo were present in both tHIO conditions except the late-developing CXCL13+ fibroblasts (Fig. 6). SEC proportions were nearly identical between conditions (EGF, 24.2%; EREG, 24.1%) and SMF contributions were similar (EGF, 42.0%; EREG, 47.8%) (Supplementary Table 6). LPFs comprised 18.8% (EGF) and 19.3% (EREG) of fibroblasts, with EGF-tHIOs more likely to be in the immature FABP5+/ADAMDEC1− state and EREG-tHIOs favouring the more mature FABP5−/ADAMDEC1+ state (Fig. 6a’,b’,c and Supplementary Table 6). Both tHIOs also contained a population expressing pan-fibroblast markers (VIM, COL1A1 and COL1A2) but lacking subtype-specific markers (Fig. 6a–c, navy in a’,b’, fibroblast; EGF, 15.0%; EREG, 8.8%).

Fibroblast spatial organization within organoid units closely resembled in vivo tissue (Fig. 6d,e). SECs line the epithelium and LPFs occupy the villus core where villus thickness permitted. Notably, F3 expression was observed in the epithelium of EGF-tHIOs but not EREG-tHIOs, mirroring the epithelial F3 expression seen in immature tissue (54 DPC) but absent after 80 DPC (Fig. 3b), suggesting greater epithelial maturity in EREG-tHIOs. LPFs extended to the muscularis mucosa-like layer, occupying both the crypt shelf and villus core (Fig. 6d(3,4),e(3,4)). Despite other markers indicating relative immaturity, both EGF- and EREG-tHIOs possessed a well-developed muscularis mucosa-like layer within nearly every organoid unit, notable given that the muscularis mucosa does not develop complete rings in tissue until later (122 DPC; Figs. 1 and 3). SMFs populated the region between the muscularis mucosa and the outer muscle layers with varying thickness (Fig. 6d(5),e(5)).

CXCL13+ fibroblasts were absent from both tHIO models. Given their late developmental appearance, their reported association with lymphoid follicle development (that is, Peyer’s patches)7 and the absence of human immune cells in tHIOs, this was not unexpected.

To further compare the organization of the tHIO models and tissue, we calculated the minimum nearest-neighbour distances for all cell types across tHIOs, fetal tissue and adult tissue and performed hierarchical clustering (Extended Data Fig. 7). As proof-of-concept, we measured the distance of every ISC to other ISCs; other epithelial cell types (enterocytes, enteroendocrine cells and goblet cells) and fibroblast subtypes (SMF, SEC, LPF and SMCs) (Extended Data Fig. 7a). We then measured the distance between fibroblast subtypes and their nearest neighbours (Extended Data Fig. 7b–d). EREG-tHIOs consistently clustered with later-stage fetal samples (>105 DPC) across all cell types examined (Extended Data Fig. 7a’–d’). EGF-tHIOs were inconsistent: ISC distances resembled 54–57 DPC tissue (Extended Data Fig. 7a’), while SMF distances were most similar to adult tissue (Extended Data Fig. 7d’), indicating that EGF-tHIO cell-type organization does not reliably correspond to a single developmental stage, in contrast to the more consistently mature spatial organization of EREG-tHIOs.

Cell-type proportions in the adult duodenum showed both similarities and differences relative to fetal tissue and tHIOs (Fig. 7b). Epithelial proportions were comparable across datasets (adult, 36.18%; fetal, 33.7%; tHIOs, 30.1–33.8%). The adult contained a smaller proportion of fibroblasts (17.22%) than fetal tissue (32.8%) or tHIOs (22.4–25.2%), less muscularis mucosa (2.26%) and ENS (1.65%), similar muscularis externa SMC (16.80%) and endothelial (8.88%) proportions, and substantially more immune cells (17.01%) than fetal tissue.

The most striking differences between fetal and adult datasets were within fibroblast subtype proportions (Supplementary Table 5). SEC contributions were comparable (fetal, 16.4%; adult, 17.79%) and CXCL13+ fibroblasts remained rare in the adult (1.33%). However, LPFs comprised the majority of adult fibroblasts (64.67%; Fig. 7g), more than doubling their relatively stable fetal proportion (~25.2%; Fig. 2h). Correspondingly, SMFs declined from the dominant fetal fibroblast population (44.8%) to 16.20% in the adult. This proportional shift was accompanied by a spatial reorganization: the densely arranged SMF layer observed during fetal development became loosely scattered within the adult submucosa amid extracellular matrix and large blood vessels (Fig. 7h–j).

Discussion

Recent single-cell approaches have shed light on rare and previously understudied intestinal populations, revealing that fibroblasts, which are historically viewed as a structurally supportive, homogeneous population, constitute a heterogeneous compartment with distinct functions8,27–29,34,35,38,43,45,84–88. While studies in the human intestine have revealed important fibroblast heterogeneity and localization1–3,5,7, the full complement of fibroblast subtypes and their spatial complexity had not been mapped in the developing or adult small intestine.

Prior spatial transcriptomic efforts characterizing cellular types and domains in the human intestine focused on the developing colon and terminal ileum using Visium technology (10X Genomics)7, which provides micro-domain resolution (6–10 cells per barcode) with greater sequencing depth but without single-cell resolution. The current study used an imaging-based approach achieving single-cell resolution, with the trade-off of a more limited probe panel. These approaches are complementary, and the present study extends the cellular landscape to the developing human small intestine and organoids, contributing insight into fibroblast patterning during intestinal development.

We resolved four transcriptionally and spatially discrete fibroblast lineages: SECs, LPFs, SMFs and rare CXCL13+ fibroblasts. Each population possessed stereotypical spatial localization from as early as 54 DPC (Fig. 8). While prior single-cell studies, including our own, have hinted at fibroblast heterogeneity and positional information1–3,5,7,45, sufficient resolution to map stereotyped tissue architecture was lacking. Here we demonstrate that (1) SECs form a continuous PDGFRAhigh/F3high sheath beneath the crypt–villus epithelium from as early as 7 weeks post-conception; (2) LPFs (FABP5+ and/or ADAMDEC1+) expand within the villus core and crypt shelf during villus elongation; (3) SMFs (SHISA3+) progressively consolidate between the emerging muscularis mucosa and muscularis externa; and (4) CXCL13+ fibroblasts arise late, clustering at the villus–crypt junction with sparse mid-villus distribution at an anatomical locus associated with lymphoid tissue formation.

CXCL13+ cells have been previously identified using scRNA-seq7 as a rare population predicted to cross-talk with immune cells, suggesting a possible role in Peyer’s patch formation and function. This is consistent with established roles for CXCL13 in intestinal lymphoid tissue formation in animal models7,89. The developmental and functional roles of this population in humans warrant further investigation.

Within the lamina propria, FABP5 expression preceded that of ADAMDEC1 in LPFs, with FABP5 expression eventually lost and ADAMDEC1 and GPX3 becoming the predominant markers, suggesting FABP5 marks an early or progenitor LPF state whose maturation warrants follow-up studies. Together, these findings reconcile prior single-cell studies from different groups and establish molecular signatures with defined geographic positions across tissue compartments of the developing and adult human small intestine.

Faithfully recapitulating human tissue heterogeneity and architecture has remained a key limitation of organoid technology. Using the Xenium spatial atlas as a benchmark, we found that tHIOs generated under EGF and EREG differentiation paradigms reproduce almost the full complement of human fibroblast subtypes—SECs, LPFs and SMFs—with correctly ordered spatial topology. Subtle but informative divergences were evident: EGF-derived tHIOs retained epithelial F3 expression and an immature FABP5+/ADAMDEC1− LPF profile, while EREG-derived tHIOs were more mature in both respects. Neither model generated CXCL13+ fibroblasts, probably reflecting relative immaturity and/or absence of immune cells.

These findings highlight several important conclusions. First, cell-type abundance alone is insufficient as a fidelity metric; fibroblast positioning and stage-specific gene expression profiles must also be considered. Second, the presence of mature muscle layers (muscularis externa and muscularis mucosa) does not indicate global cellular maturation since individual cell types within tHIOs appear to mature at different rates rather than on a uniform developmental timeline ex vivo. Third, developmental maturity and incomplete patterning remain key bottlenecks to be addressed through modulation of growth factor gradients, extracellular matrix composition or co-culture with immune cells82. The atlas thus serves both as a quantitative quality control benchmark and as an instructive template for next-generation tissue-engineered intestine.

A limitation of this study is the shallow transcriptomic depth of the 292-gene Xenium panel, which inevitably biases clustering toward preselected markers, limits discovery of rare or novel states and reduces temporal resolution due to limited inclusion of time-dependent genes. However, the high cellular resolution of the spatial data enabled CytoSPACE66 imputation of scRNA-seq data onto the Xenium atlas, effectively mapping full transcriptomic depth onto the spatial framework and enhancing downstream analysis such as CellChat67,68 signalling inference. This approach also provides a framework for integrating high-quality data from other datasets into the atlas to drive future discovery of biological mechanisms such as niche-specific transcriptional programmes, previously unrecognized ligand–receptor interactions and testable hypotheses regarding spatially restricted mechanisms of tissue remodelling.

By integrating single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, we have refined the organization of intestinal cell types underpinning human small intestinal development and provided a quantitative scaffold for organoid engineering and disease modelling. Beyond cataloguing cell types, this work illustrates a generalizable strategy for incorporating transcriptional atlases into spatially resolved blueprints of organogenesis. As spatial multiomics technologies mature, such blueprints will be indispensable for deciphering cell–cell communication, identifying critical cell types and locations in congenital and inflammatory disorders, and engineering transplantable tissues that faithfully recapitulate human biology.

Methods

Human tissue acquisition and IRB

Normal, de-identified human fetal intestinal tissue was obtained from the University of Washington Laboratory of Developmental Biology and was conducted with approval from the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board (IRB; HUM00093465). Normal, de-identified human adult duodenum tissue specimens from research-consented deceased organ donors was obtained from the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF), as FFPE blocks. Tissue from UCSF was obtained through Donor Network West in collaboration with the UCSF Viable Tissue Acquisition Lab (VITAL) Core as described previously83.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publications1, no data were excluded from the analyses and the experiments were not randomized, samples were instead allocated as available. Data collection and analysis were not performed blind to the conditions of the experiments. Data distribution was assumed to be normal, but this was not formally tested. No technical replicates were performed for sequencing and Xenium imaging experiments and samples were distributed along the developmental timeline and not intended as biological replicates and are independent, n = 1.

Markers

The supervised lists of potential marker genes (Supplementary Table 3 and 4) were generated from the initial sequencing data (Figs. 1a and 2a) and mapped to at least one other publicly available human fetal intestine dataset2,3, then validated via FISH and Xenium imaging.

Primary tissue collection, fixation and paraffin processing

Human intestine tissue samples were collected as ~0.5-cm fragments and fixed for 24 h at room temperature in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF), and washed with UltraPure Distilled Water (Invitrogen, 10977-015) for three changes for a total of 2 h. Tissue was dehydrated by an alcohol series diluted in UltraPure Distilled Water (Invitrogen, 10977-015). Tissue was incubated for 60 min for each of the following solution: 25% methanol, 50% methanol, 75% methanol and 100% methanol. Tissue was stored long term in 100% methanol at 4 °C. Before paraffin embedding, tissue was equilibrated in 100% ethanol for an hour, and then 70% ethanol. Tissue was processed into paraffin blocks in an automated tissue processor (Leica, ASP300) with 1-h changes overnight.

scRNA-seq data analysis

The repositories of the raw scRNA-seq data of developing human fetal small intestine, duodenum, jejunum and ileum can be found in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. Data matrices for further analysis were generated using the Cell Ranger pipeline under standard parameters by the University of Michigan Advanced Genomics Sequencing Core.

Single-cell library preparation and transcriptome alignment1

The scRNA-seq sample library was prepared as previously described, with the 10X Chromium Controller using the v2 chemistry. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 with targeted depth of 100,000 reads per cell. Default alignment parameters were used to align reads to the pre-prepared hg19 human reference genome provided by the 10X Cell Ranger pipeline. Initial cell demultiplexing and gene quantification were also performed with the default 10X Cell Ranger pipeline.

Preprocessing and quality control filtering (full data)

Filtered gene expression matrices were imported into Seurat Version 4.1.0 in RStudio v1.4 running on R 4.1.2 for further analysis. Quality control was conducted after merging 16 single Seurat objects. Cells were excluded if they contained less than 500 or greater than 8,000 features, more than 10% mitochondria RNA and the RNA reads were less than 500 or greater than 60,000 to avoid the impact of outliers. The merged dataset was then normalized with a scale factor of 10,000 using the NormalizeData function. Highly variable features were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function. A fast version of the mutual nearest neighbours (MNN) was calculated to correct for batch effects using RunFastMNN. UMAP90 was then constructed by the RunUMAP function with mnn reduction created by RunFastMNN with the first 30 dimensions. Nearest neighbours were found by the FindNeighbors function using MNN reduction. Cell clusters were identified by the original Louvain algorithm embedded in FindClusters with a resolution of 0.3.

Cell type annotation (full data)

The major classes of cell types in the human intestine (epithelium, fibroblast, immune, endothelium, ENS and SMC) were manually annotated using major cell population markers listed in Supplementary Table 3. A cluster of contaminating red blood cells was identified and removed based on differential gene expression analysis. Cell counts of each major cell type across time were calculated by categorizing sample by age within developmental stage (47−75 DPC, 80 DPC, 101 DPC and 110−135 DPC).

Cell type annotation (fibroblast subset)

The fibroblast subset of the scRNA-seq dataset that was analysed for cell subtypes was defined after several rounds of quality control using Seurat v5.2.1 in RStudio 2024.12.1. with R (v4.4.1). First, the previously defined fibroblast population (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 3) was extracted and the RNA assay was normalized using the SCTransform function with the percentage of mitochondrial reads regressing out in a second non-regularized linear, followed by the standard processing pipeline including principal component analysis, UMAP calculation, nearest neighbour discovery and cluster determination.

Then, according to the expression of gene markers listed in Supplementary Table 4 and differential gene expression analysis identified by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, we filtered out clusters that interfered with discovery of fibroblast cell types. Specifically, we removed the active cycling cells (MKi67, TOP2A and PCNA) the SMCs (ACTA2 and TAGLN), pericytes (RGS5, KCNJ8, KCNL8, ABCC9, BGN and CCDC3) and ICCs (KIT and ANO1). We removed these populations for two reasons. First, the clusters were notably different from the remaining cells when viewed on a three-dimensional diffusion map, they caused all other clusters to clump together. Second, these populations (other than the cycling cells) are differentiated, non-fibroblast cell types by the transcriptomic definitions we established for this study. It took several rounds of quality control to entirely remove each population, and for each round of our cell identification and quality control, we followed a similar pipeline and inspected gene expression using dot plots and feature plots. Cell counts (Fig. 2g) of each subcell type were calculated by categorizing samples by developmental stage (47−75 DPC, 80 DPC, 101 DPC and 110−135 DPC).

Xenium slide preparation and workflow

Xenium is a spatial transcriptomics method that permits imaging of hundreds to thousands of genes on a single FFPE sample at subcellular resolution (Extended Data Fig. 1a). A custom fetal human small intestine Xenium probe panel was designed (Supplementary Table 3) based on conventional markers from the adult human, conventional markers from the mouse and previously acquired or published sequencing data in the fetal human small intestine1,3,4,33,34.

As previously described52, archival tissue and tHIO FFPE samples were prepared by trimming excess paraffin from the paraffin blocks, which were then submerged in an ice bath for at least 30 min before sectioning. To prepare for section collection, the slide warmer was preheated 42 °C and used to pre-warm positive-charged microscope slides topped with molecular biology grade water (Corning, 46-000-CM). The blocks were then sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm and serial sections were collected on the prepared slides. After allowing sections to flatten out, the water was removed and sections were examined under the 4× and 10× objectives of a biological microscope to identify the optimal regions of the samples.

Using a single-edge razor blade, the corresponding area of the paraffin block was lightly scored to obtain tissue sections small enough to fit into the 10.45 mm by 22.45 mm sample placement area of the Xenium slide. Xenium slides were removed from −20 °C storage and allowed to come to room temperature 30 min before undergoing the same preparation as the microscope slides. Once the selected sections were placed on the Xenium slide, they were examined again to ensure that the fiducial frame was not obscured by any paraffin or tissue. Following water removal, sections were allowed to dry on the slide warmer for 3 h before slides were moved to a microscope slide jar for transportation to the University of Michigan Advanced Genomics Core for processing and image acquisition using the Xenium Analyzer (10X Genomics, PN-1000481).

Owing to the application of emerging technologies, the two different batches were processed for segmentation differently. In addition to the human fetal small intestine probe set, a segmentation kit (PN-1000661) was included to define cell boundaries of the second batch of samples, which resulted in more accurate cell segmentation and as a result more accurate clustering both by the Xenium Explorer program and externally.

Cell IDs and transcript counts for the small intestinal samples were exported from Xenium Explorer (v2.0.0 and later) and analysed using the pipeline described below.

Images and digital cell masks were generated and exported using Xenium Explorer. Xenium image brightness (+100%) and contrast (+20%) were enhanced equally across all exported Xenium images in Adobe Photoshop CC 2024 before figure assembly in Adobe Illustrator CC 2024.

Xenium data analysis pipeline

Extraction of Xenium data for analysis

Xenium spatial transcriptomic data were exported from Xenium Explorer version (v2.0.0 and later). The cell IDs of the fetal small intestine (duodenum–jejunum–ileum) only were included by using the XE region of interest (ROI) selection tool to define the small intestinal segments and export only cells from those regions. The colon and (for 122–142 DPC samples) distal ileum were imaged but not analysed in this study. The entirety of each tHIO sample, consisting of multiple lumen-containing organoid units were exported and analysed as single samples per condition. The whole-thickness human adult duodenum sample was exported in its entirety and analysed.

Quality control of tHIOs by identifying and removing mouse cells

Before further analysis of the tHIOs could occur, the residual mouse cells were identified and removed from further analysis based on the variety of expressed genes and total probe counts per cell (Extended Data Fig. 6). After validation of the removal of mouse cells and debris, the same pipeline was applied as for the human tissue samples.

Xenium cell type annotation (full data)

All Xenium data was loaded into R v4.4.1 using a standard Seurat 5.2.1 pipeline. After loading individual sample data in, cells with 0 counts were filtered out and excluded from further analysis. Each individual sample was normalized using the SCTransform function, which created a new assay with normalized transcriptomic expression values. Then principal component analysis was performed on the top 30 principal components and was applied to UMAP construction. Nearest neighbours were found with the FindNeighbors function and cells were clustered by the original Louvain algorithm at a 0.3-resolution using function FindClusters. Individual samples were carefully examined using dot plots and feature plots. The top 50 gene markers were generated by a Wilcoxon rank-sum test for each cluster for cell type annotation. The seven individual Xenium-sourced objects were then merged and cells were reclustered following the same pipeline.

When generating UMAP visualizations, cells from the later two stages (105 DPC and combined 122, 136 and 142 DPC) were randomly downsampled to 20,000 to reduce the computational burden (Fig. 1c). Meanwhile, the whole dataset was analysed and quantified to generate cell count data in each cell type across different ages for comparison. Major populations including epithelium, fibroblast, endothelium, ENS, SMCs and immune cells were defined using the gene list in Supplementary Table 3.

Cell IDs annotated using Seurat were imported back into Xenium Explorer and used to visualize the location of intestinal cell populations (105 DPC; Fig. 1f). Each major cell class (epithelium, fibroblasts, SMCs, ENS, endothelium and immune) was then individually labelled to visualize subpopulations (Fig. 1g). To carry out the labelling shown in Fig. 1g, each population highlighted was computationally extracted, subclustered and each cluster was annotated in Seurat, after which cell IDs were re-imported into Xenium Explorer for visualization.

Xenium fibroblast extraction

Different from extracting fibroblast population from a merged count matrices as we did for the single-cell sequencing data, we individually defined the fibroblast population for each sample after rigorous screening, before removing the proliferative cells, ICCs, SMCs and pericytes from the fibroblast data based on marker gene expression (Supplementary Table 4). Then the individual fibroblast objects were merged, clustered, annotated and quantified. Similar to the major cell type population Xenium data, the full fibroblast object was downsampled to reduce computational burden for visualization of the UMAP and to prevent visual bias due to overpopulating the object with the samples of developmental stages 122–142 DPC.

Stacked bar graphs depicting cell count distributions for major cell populations or fibroblast subtypes were generated in either Microsoft Excel (Figs. 1, 2, 5 and 6) or GraphPad Prism (Fig. 7). The annotated datasets were then re-loaded back into Xenium Explorer for visualization of the tissue architecture.

Additional analysis methods

Slingshot pseudo-time analysis of LPFs

For both the scRNA-seq and Xenium datasets, cells from all ages were integrated and LPF cells were subset into three ordered states (early LPF, LPF and late LPF). After standard preprocessing and UMAP embedding in Seurat, objects were converted to SingleCellExperiment format for trajectory analysis. Slingshot was applied using LPF annotations as cluster labels and the UMAP coordinates as the reduced dimensional space. Slingshot infers lineage structure by constructing a minimum spanning tree across clusters and fitting smooth principal curves to estimate developmental trajectories. Pseudo-time values were extracted and added back to the Seurat metadata for visualization on the UMAP. The same procedure was performed independently for both the sequencing and Xenium datasets to enable consistent pseudo-time inference across modalities.

Cell-level mapping between scRNA-seq and Xenium spatial transcriptomics using CytoSPACE

To enable cell-resolved integration between scRNA-seq data and Xenium spatial transcriptomics data, we employed CytoSPACE66 to perform probabilistic cell-level assignment of sequencing-derived transcriptomic profiles onto spatially resolved Xenium cells.

In brief, the scRNA-seq dataset was used as a high-dimensional transcriptomic reference, while the Xenium dataset provided spatially localized cells with limited gene depth. Before mapping, both datasets were preprocessed independently using Seurat v591 and labelled manually. For the scRNA-seq data, standard normalization, variable gene selection and dimensionality reduction were performed. For the Xenium data, spatial cells were retained after quality control filtering and normalized based on detected transcripts.

CytoSPACE was then run using the scRNA-seq expression matrix as the reference and the Xenium expression matrix as the target. CytoSPACE computes an optimal transport-based assignment that matches each Xenium cell to the most transcriptionally similar scRNA-seq cell while respecting global cell-type composition constraints. This procedure generates a mapping table linking Xenium cell IDs (SpotID) to original scRNA-seq cell IDs (OriginalCID).

Imputation of full-transcriptome profiles into Xenium cells

Using the CytoSPACE mapping results, we transferred the full transcriptome information from scRNA-seq cells to their matched Xenium counterparts. Specifically, a named mapping vector was constructed from the CytoSPACE output

This mapping was aligned to the Xenium cell order. For Xenium cells with valid assignments, the raw gene expression counts from the corresponding scRNA-seq cells were extracted and re-indexed to match the Xenium cell barcodes, thereby generating an imputed gene expression matrix at single-cell spatial resolution.

This procedure resulted in a spatially resolved, full-transcriptome expression matrix, where each Xenium cell now carries both its spatial coordinates and the imputed high-dimensional gene expression profile derived from scRNA-seq.

Spatially informed cell–cell communication analysis using CellChat v2

The imputed Xenium expression matrix was then used for downstream spatial cell–cell communication analysis using CellChat67,68. As each Xenium cell retained its original spatial coordinates, we were able to perform CellChat analysis in a spatially aware context. Ligand–receptor interaction inference was conducted using the imputed expression values while incorporating spatial proximity constraints between cells.

This approach enabled us to quantify cell–cell communication networks that reflect both transcriptional states (derived from scRNA-seq) and true spatial organization (captured by Xenium), thereby overcoming the gene panel limitation of Xenium while preserving spatial fidelity.

Before running CellChat, we validated the CytoSPACE output specifically for cell duplications as the program uses a 10-cell minimum to ‘call’ a pathway as present. We found that a vast majority of cells were only duplicated a handful of times; 85.1% of cells were duplicated zero to three times and 99.4% were duplicated nine or fewer times. Only 0.61% of cells were duplicated more than ten times and those were entirely due to populations with a substantial difference in the number of cells present in the scRNA-seq dataset versus Xenium dataset.

After confirming that there would be minimal analytical artefacts due to cell duplications, we ran CellChatv2 on the CytoSPACE results of the 122/127/132 DPC sequencing data mapped onto the 142 DPC small intestine cell map.

Centroid distance calculations

For all human tissues and the two tHIO samples, the centroid for each cell was extracted from the Xenium metadata using R. Nearest neighbour queries were performed in the two-dimensional spatial coordinate space using the RANN implementation of the ANN library. For each cell centroid, we computed the Euclidean distance to the closest centroid among cells of each annotated cell type. The annotations used were the same 20 cell types used for CytoSPACE imputation; greater detail than the general class annotation, but less detail than used for full subtype annotation because some cell types varied greatly depending on developmental stage.

Minimum distance measurements (micrometre) were plotted as box plots indicating Q1, median, Q3 and mean for the minimum distance between indicated cell types within each sample. Select cell types for source and target cell were highlighted. In addition, hierarchical clustering (pheatmap) was performed, comparing the average minimum distance between each of the selected source cell types and target cell types.

Multiplex FISH and immunofluorescence

A list of probes, antibodies, fluorophores and concentrations can be found in Supplementary Table 2. Archival tissues were selected from biologically distinct samples of similar age to at least one Xenium sample per developmental stage defined by the mesenchymal sequencing data. Paraffin blocks were sectioned to generate 5-µm-thick sections within the week before performing in situ hybridization. All materials, including the microtome and blade, were sprayed with RNase Away solution before use. Slides were baked for 1 h in a 60 °C dry oven before deparaffinization and rehydration.

Tissue slides were deparaffinized in Histo-Clear II (National Diagnostics, HS-202) twice for 5 min each, followed by rehydration in two washes of fresh 100% ethanol. The FISH protocol was then performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (ACD; RNAscope multiplex fluorescent manual protocol, 323100-USM) with a 10-min hydrogen peroxide step, a 15-min antigen retrieval step and a 30-min protease plus treatment.

Immediately following the HRP blocking for the last channel of the FISH, slides were rinsed twice with PBS to remove excess detergent and then washed three times for 5 min in PBS, then transferred to blocking solution (5% BSA in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature in a dark, humid chamber. Slides were then incubated with primary antibody (rabbit anti-TAGLN/SM22, ab14106 (1:500) or rabbit anti-αSMA, ab5694 (1:500)) suspended in blocking solution overnight at 4 °C in a dark, humid chamber. The following day, excess primary antibody was rinsed off twice with PBS before undergoing a series of five PBS washes of 10 min each. Secondary antibody and nuclear dyes (donkey anti-rabbit 488 (1:500)), NucSpot 750/780 (1×) and DAPI (1:1,000) were added to blocking solution and slides were incubated in a dark humid chamber at room temperature for 1 h. Excess secondary antibody was rinsed off twice with PBS before going through another series of three PBS washes of 5 min each. Slides were mounted in FluorSave Mounting media (Millipore, 345789-20 ML) and allowed to dry in the dark before being sealed. Once the seal was fully dried, slides were stored in the dark at 4 °C and imaged within a week.

All imaging was done using a Nikon AXR confocal after slides were brought to room temperature. Acquisition and the alignment of multitrack FISH images was performed using DAPI and NucSpot 750/780 (Biotium, 41038) in NIS Elements v5.24.01. A minimum of three ROIs per sample region were imaged. Images were acquired using a 20× air objective with 3.00× digital zoom as multipoint, multichannel z stacks using resonant scanning at 2,048 × 2,048, with fluorescence averaged over four images and enhanced with Nikon Elements denoise.ai to further reduce background.

The z-stack series were then compiled into maximum intensity projections using FIJI92. For publication, a representative image from each sample was selected and the brightness was further enhanced in FIJI. Imaging parameters were kept consistent for all images within the same experiment and any post-imaging manipulations were performed equally on all images from a single experiment. Final figures were assembled using Adobe Illustrator CC 2024.

Stem cell maintenance and differentiation

As previously published, stem cell lines were maintained in an undifferentiated state via media condition and regular splitting until differentiation. Stem cells underwent directed differentiation into definitive endoderm over a 3-day treatment. After three days, endoderm monolayers were differentiated into an intestinal identity. On days 4–6 of hindgut differentiation, spheroids budded from the monolayer and were collected. These spheroids were embedded in Matrigel as previously described93.

Organoid basal growth media was supplemented with EGF (100 ng ml−1 R&D Systems, 236-EG-01M) or EREG (10 ng ml−1 R&D Systems, 1195-EP-025/CF) with Noggin-Fc (100 ng ml−1, purified from conditioned media) and R-Spondin1 (5% conditioned medium) for the first 3 days of culture to pattern a proximal small intestine. On the third day after embedding, media were changed to basal growth media supplemented with EGF or EREG only (no additional Noggin or R-Spondin1) and remained in this media for the duration of the experiments with media changes every 5 days. Organoids were not passaged to avoid disrupting the development and spatial organization of the key cell types seen in EREG-grown HIOs. HIOs were cultured in vitro for at least 28 days then collected for transplantation.

Mouse kidney capsule transplantation

The University of Michigan Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees approved all animal research. All mice were monitored by the investigators and veterinary staff from the University of Michigan Unit for Laboratory Animal Medicine and housed in a specific pathogen-free vivarium under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. Room temperature was maintained within approximately 20–26 °C, and relative humidity within approximately 30–70%, in accordance with institutional animal care standards. Food and water was available ad libitum.

HIOs were cultured in vitro at least 28 days and then implanted under the kidney capsules of adult immunocompromised NOD-scid IL2Rg-null (NSG) mice53–56 (Jackson Laboratory strain no. 0005557). In brief, mice were anesthetized using 2% isoflurane and a left-flank incision was used to expose the kidney after shaving and sterilization of the area of incision with three alternating washes of hibiclens surgical soap and sterile water to prep the area after shaving. Between one and three HIOs were then surgically implanted beneath mouse kidney capsules using forceps. Before closure, an intraperitoneal flush of Zosyn (100 mg kg−1; Pfizer) was administered. Mice were administered a dose of analgesic carprofen during the surgery and an additional dose after 24 h. All mice were monitored daily for 10 days and then weekly until they were euthanized for retrieval of transplanted HIOs after 10 weeks.

tHIO processing for staining and histology

The tHIOs were placed in 10% NBF for 24 h at room temperature on a rocker for fixation. Fixed specimens were then washed 3× in ultrapure DNase/RNase-free distilled water (Thermo Fisher, 10977015) for 30–60 min per wash depending on its size. Next, tissue was dehydrated through a methanol series diluted in ultrapure DNase/RNase-free distilled water for 30–60 min per solution of 25% MeOH, 50% MeOH, 75% MeOH and 100% MeOH. Tissue was either immediately processed for paraffin embedding or stored in 100% MeOH at 4 °C for future paraffin processing or whole-mount staining. For paraffin processing, dehydrated tissue was placed in 100% EtOH, followed by 70% EtOH and perfused with paraffin using an automated tissue processor (Leica, ASP300) with 1 h solution changes overnight. Tissue was then placed into tissue cassettes and base moulds for sectioning. Before sectioning, the microtome and slides were sprayed with RNase Away (Thermo Fisher, 700511).

Five-µm-thick sections were cut from paraffin blocks onto Xenium slides under RNAse-free conditions (10X Genomics, PN-1000460) and submitted to the University of Michigan Advanced Genomics Core for processing and image acquisition using the Xenium Analyzer (10X Genomics, PN-1000481). In addition to the human fetal small intestine probe set, a segmentation kit (PN-1000661) was included to define cell boundaries of the second batch of samples, which resulted in more accurate cell segmentation and, as a result, more accurate clustering both by the Xenium Explorer program and externally.
