Genetic components analysis of physiological and agronomic characters of bread wheat genotypes under climate change in arid ecosystems
Authors: Nour Y. Elsherbini, Haifa A.S. Alhaithloul, Dalal M.N. Alanazi, Maryam M. Alomran, Mamoun A. Abdel-Moneam, Mohamed N.A. El-Hawary, Soad H. Haffez, Nadi A. Al-Harbi, Hani S. Saudy, Khaled Abdelaal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeInappropriate planting time creates conditions that look like the effects of climate change, putting plants under eco-stress. Due to their unpredictable fluctuations, climatic conditions are difficult to control, and therefore the genetic potential of cultivated crops could play a crucial role in this regard. Therefore, the current study attempts to find new wheat genes capable of adapting to climate change to maintain productivity under potential environmental stresses.MethodsAccordingly, six wheat genotypes with distinct agronomic attributes were crossed in a half-diallel model. The genetic components and heritability of antioxidants as well as grain yield for the 6 parents and their fifteen F1 crosses were evaluated under two sowing dates (normal and late planting).ResultsFindings showed that variance of planting dates was significant or highly significant for all parameters except peroxidase activity, proline, and grain yield. The planting date × genotype associations were discovered to be significant for most of the studied traits. Each additive (D) and dominance (H1 and H2) gene effects were significant for most traits under both planting dates, except of additive gene effects for proline, dominance gene effects for spikes number plant−1, under normal planting date, and grain yield plant−1 under late planting date, as well as (H1) for kernels number spike−1 under normal planting date. All traits were given medium or large values for heritability in the narrow sense (h2n.s) under each planting date, with the exception of catalase activity under the late planting date, peroxidase activity, proline content under the normal planting date, spikes number plant−1, kernels weight spike−1, 1000-kernels weight under late planting date, and nitrogen content under both planting date, which had minimal values of heritability in the narrow sense.ConclusionWheat genotypes, i.e. Sids 14 (P1), Sakha 95 (P3), Misr 3 (P6), P3×P4, P3×P6 and P5×P6 were the best parents and crosses for most of the studied physiological and agronomic traits under late sowing date conditions, and it can be recommended to include these genotypes in the wheat breeding program to withstand climatic changes in late planting. Also, early segregating generations may benefit from selection for these traits since additive gene action plays a key role in shaping them.

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is the world’s most consumed cereal, serving as the principal source of food for nearly one-third of the global population [1]. It is known as king cereals since it offers foods that satisfy around 36% of the world’s population and contributes 20% of the essential calories to the world’s population [2, 3]. Accordingly, there is an urgent and ongoing need to improve wheat yield, but climate change may hinder the growth of cultivated areas, reducing the productivity [4–7]. In this regard, climate change is often associated with severe ecological pressures that negatively impact crop growth and development [8–12]. The negative waves of environmental pressures resulting from climate change are more dangerous to agriculture in arid and semi-arid regions [13–16]. Drought [17–19], salinity [20–23], unfavorable temperature [24, 25] are regarded as the major stressors threatening the development of crops in arid areas. It is well known that such stressors result in serious impacts on physiological and biochemical status, nutrient and moisture homeostasis of plant cells and alteration in gene expression [26–30]. Imposing plants to abiotic stresses can seriously influence acquisition of water, reducing cell membrane stability while increasing the electrolyte leakage [31–34]. Further, dysfunction of the photosynthetic apparatus, stomatal movement and cellular oxidation are other major physio-biochemical alteration of stresses [35–37]. Herein, abiotic stresses stimulate releasing excessive amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS), via obstruction of electron transport systems, hence the biological functions of cells are disturbed [38–40]. On the other hand, tolerance to stresses in plants could be ascribed to the potentiality to maintain the photosynthetic system and stimulate the antioxidant defence mechanisms while adjusting the ions homeostasis and plant water relations [41–44].

Several studies were conducted to improve grain yield of wheat under normal conditions [45–50] and stressful conditions such as biotic stress [51–57] and abiotic stress [58–64]. In fact, developing new genotypes that are adapted to the climate change-associated stresses is the key and critical solution to improving crop productivity and sustainability [65–67]. And then, cultivating the appropriate cultivars that tolerate to a specific environmental stress could contribute to decrease the adverse impacts and keeping the agricultural production under stressful conditions [68–71]. In this situation, gene action estimation is critical to an effective breeding effort. That is why understanding and identifying superior parental genotypes is extremely important for plant breeders to establish productive and effective initiatives [49, 63]. Interestingly, selecting parent genotypes solely based on phenotypic performance may be incorrect, as poor combinations can result in phenotypically superior genotypes [72]. Various methodologies are available for analysing diallel crosses, such as Hayman’s and Griffing’s methodologies [73, 74]. These two methods are often used in breeding program data analysis to clarify the purpose of gene activity and the inheritance of traits, such as in crop improvement [75]. Griffing [74] applied his methodology to wheat. However, Hayman’s methodology, which is effective in detecting gene actions such as additive, dominant, and epistatic gene effects, has not been used. This information would be valuable to plant breeders for types of genetic variants in the attributes for which selection is intended, as well as speedy evaluation of yielding capability by finding crosses which will generate superior genotypes [76]. Previous studies of the inheritance of different characters in wheat were mostly based on diallel analysis. High grain output is the primary objective of most wheat breeding programs; therefore, a range of improved breeding techniques are required to raise wheat beyond the current yield limit.

Late planting of wheat has a significant impact on spikes plant−1, spikelets spike−1, and grain weight, leading to a decrease in yield. It has been stated that delayed planting (late December) reduces the grain yield by approximately 38% [77]. Because of frost damage during the germination stage as well as heat stress during the booting and spike initiation stage, late planting of wheat alters the crop development rate, which impacts grain yield and its components [78, 79]. Temperature affects late-sown crop plants, subjecting them to low temperatures during establishment and high temperatures during the reproductive phase, leading to rapid crop maturity. This has an impact not just on yield but also on yield components and other elements, growth, and development [80]. It is often related to a reduced kernel weight [81], a reduced number of spikes per plant and per unit area [82], harvest index, grain number spike−1, and leaf area index [83].

To adapt to climate change and mitigate the decline in crop yields, planting dates should be adjusted, along with the cultivation of heat-tolerant germplasms [84]. Climatic factors, especially air temperature, rainfall, and solar radiation, are among the most important parameters influencing crop productivity [85]. These factors change at various growth stages of different cultivated genotypes depending on the sowing dates, resulting in changes in growth and yield [86].

The sensitivity and tolerance responses to various stresses can be manifested in differences in the genotype’s performance under stress conditions [87]. Exposing crop plants to the effects of global warming and climatic alteration, which results in unfavourable growing conditions, leads to a significant decline in productivity. Plant breeders must therefore have the precise tools necessary to efficiently assess and select genotypes that are most suited to difficult situations in order to handle the possible future effects of climate change.

According to the current study’s hypothesis, combining diverse parental wheat genotypes could result in unique hybrids that are highly climatically adaptive, preserving yield and quality in the face of oxidative stress. Thus, this study sought to determine the parental genotypes of bread wheat cultivated under normal and late planting dates, as well as the genetic components and heritability of enzyme characteristics and grain yield components. The study also sought to assess superior parents and cross combinations from a 6 × 6 diallel cross of the parental genotypes of bread wheat.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The procedures of the current study was performed along two seasons of 2021/22 and 2022/23. The study involved six parental bread wheat genotypes which were Sids 14 (P1), Sakha 94 (P2), Sakha 95 (P3), Giza 168 (P4), Line 1 (P5) and Misr 3 (P6), typifying by a wide range of variability in various parameters. The pedigree and origin of the selected parents are presented in Table 1. During the 2021/22 season, these parental genotypes were sown at different dates to overcome variations in flowering times. All feasible parallel combinations, avoiding reciprocals, formed between the six parents, resulting in fifteen crosses (P1×P2, P1×P3, P1×P4, P1×P5, P1×P6, P2×P3, P2×P4, P2×P5, P2×P6, P3×P4, P3×P5, P3×P6, P4×P5, P4×P6 and P5×P6) as F1 population. Hence, in 2022/23 season, we examined 21 entries (6 parents and 15 F1) in two experiments of distinct planting date tests.

During soil preparation, each experiment received fertilization with 35.7 kg P2O5 ha−1, 57.12 kg K2O ha−1 in a single dose. As well, 178.5 kg N ha−1 supplied in two doses, the first dose was 30% with planting and the second was 70% with the first irrigation at 27 days after sowing (DAS).

The experiment was set utilizing Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD) with three replications. Each replication consisted of 21 rows (genotypes) and two rows (borders), each measuring 4 m in length and 25 cm apart, with a spacing of 20 cm between each plant. Each row was sown with twenty grains, which were then manually drilled. All other cultural techniques, with the exception of planting dates, were followed as indicated for wheat cultivation. To reduce border impact, remove the two outside plants and the two exteriors of each row in each plot.

Assessed datum

Physiological stress indices

From each plot five plants were chosen randomly at 90 DAS to estimate some physiological traits of wheat genotypes under the two planting dates.

Estimation of catalase activity (mgH2O2/gFW)

Catalase activity was assayed as described by Devi [88]. One ml of the enzyme extract was mixed with 2 ml of 0.1 M H2O2 and 3 ml of 0.1 M phosphate buffer at pH of 6.8. After incubation at 27 °C for 5 min, 1 ml of 0.7 N H2SO4 was added to stop the enzymatic reaction. The residual H2O2 was then titrated against 0.01 N KMnO4 till the pink color persists for at least 30 s. In addition to the sample titration, a blank titration was carried out by immediate adding of 1 ml of the enzyme extract and 2 ml of 0.1 M H2O2 to 1 ml of 0.7 N H2SO4 then 3 ml of the phosphate buffer and titration against KMnO4. Estimation of peroxidase activity (U/g/min)

Peroxidase activity was assayed as the increase in absorbance at 420 nm due to the formation of purpurogallin [89]. The reaction mixture contained 3 ml of pyrogallol phosphate buffer (0.05 M pyrogallol in 0.1 M phosphate buffer at pH of 6.0), 0.1 ml of the enzyme extract and 0.5 ml of 1% H2O2.

Estimation of proline (mg/g)

The method adopted for the estimation of proline was essentially that described by Snell and Snell [90]. As a reagent, 4 ml of 1: 1 dilution of syrupy phosphoric acid and 6 ml of glacial acetic acid were mixed then 0.25 g of ninhydrin was added and heated to 70 °C till complete solubility. To 1 ml of the concentrated extract, 1 ml of glacial acetic acid and 1 ml of the reagent were added. The tubes were then heated at 100 °C for 60 min then 1 ml of glacial acetic acid was added to each tube then cooled to room temperature. The volume was adjusted to 5 ml with glacial acetic acid. The optical density of the colour was measured within one hour at 515 nm.

Yield parameters

At harvest (on 27th April and 8th May in the first and second sowing dates, respectively), 10 plants from each plot were collected to assess spikes number plant−1, spikelets number spike−1, kernels number spike−1, spike length (cm), grain weight spike−1 (g), weight of 1000-kernels (g), and grain yield plant−1 (g).

Grain nitrogen content

Total nitrogen in wheat grains was estimated by the improved Kjeldahl–method [91], modified by distilling the ammonia into saturated boric solution and titration in standard acid.

Data analysis

The collected data for each trait in the parents and F1 generation underwent a plot mean analysis. According to Gomez and Gomez [92] description, all collected data were statistically analysed using a randomized complete block design in order to determine the significance of the genotypes and planting date conditions. In order to predict the planting date effects, a combined analysis of the two experiments (normal and late planting date conditions) was conducted [93].

The diallel analysis technique was used for assessing additive and dominant genetic impacts, and genetic variance was partitioned into consistent components by Hayman [73, 94] (Hayman 1954 and 1958). Evaluates of the variation in genetics components were as follows: components of deviation due to additive gene effects (D), mean of covariance of additive and dominance effects over the arrays (F), component of deviation due to dominant gene effects (H1), dominance suggested a symmetry of positive and negative effects (H2), dominance effects as the sum of algebraic values over all loci in heterozygous are phase in all crosses (h2), and component of deviation due to environmental effect. To evaluate each of these components, the standard error was determined [95]. The average degree of dominance at every locus (H1/D)1/2, the proportion of genes with positive and negative effects in the parents (H2/4H1), the proportion of genes that are recessive in the parents (KD/KR), and an indicator of the number of genes that contribute to parameter performance and indicate dominance to a certain degree (K). Additionally, for the F1 generations, Mather and Jinks’ formula [96] was utilized to calculate heritability in the broad sense (h2b.s) and heritability in the narrow sense (h2n.s).

Results

Physiological and agronomic traits of wheat crosses

Physiological traits (Table 3) and agronomic attributes (Table 4) were estimated under two sowing dates (normal and late sowing) for parental and crossed genotypes of wheat. Catalase activity, nitrogen content, spikes number plant−1, spikelets number spike−1, kernels number spike−1, spike length, and weight of 1000-kernels significantly (p < 0.05) changed with variability in sowing time. Late sowing showed greater values of catalase activity, nitrogen content, spikes number plant−1, spikelets number spike−1, kernels number spike−1, and spike length, and lower value of weight of 1000-kernels than that of normal sowing. While, there were no significant difference between sowing dates for peroxidase activity, proline content, kernels weight spike−1 and grain yield plant−1.

Mean values of yield attributes in wheat as affected by sowing dates and genotypes

As for wheat genotypes, the ANOVA exhibited significant differences among the parental genotypes and among crosses. Discussion

The present research exhibited that presence of significant differences between the normal and late planting dates of wheat genotypes for most evaluated traits. This result is agreeing with several previous studies [77, 78, 97–99]. Assumptions of the diallel analysis are diploid segregation, no reciprocal differences, homozygous parents, no multiple alleles, no epistasis, uncorrelated gene distribution, and no genotypic-environment interaction [94]. Failure of any one or any combination of these assumptions invalidates to some degree the conclusion obtained by the means of the analysis. Results showed that the basic hypotheses for diallel analysis seemed to be accurate for all parameters under each planting date, with the exception of peroxidase activity under late planting and spikelets number spike−1 under normal planting. So, these two parameters that had a significant t2 value were removed and its genetic components and parameters were not estimated. Comparison between magnitude of (D) and dominance (H1 and H2) gene effects showed that the additive gene effects were more important than dominance for most of the parameters under each planting date. This was also confirmed by the average degree of dominance (H1/D)1/2 values, which were less than unity, confirming the significance of partial dominant gene impacts in determining all parameters under each planting date, except catalase activity and proline content under each planting date, peroxidase activity under normal planting date, spikelets number spike−1 under normal planting date, kernels number spike−1 and kernels weight spike−1 under late planting date, spikes number plant−1, 1000-kernels weight, and nitrogen content under each planting date, which are controlled by over dominance gene effects. These results are in harmony with other previous studies [100–102]. Positive and highly significant dominance effect (h2) for peroxidase activity under normal planting, catalase activity, spike length under each planting date, kernel weight spike−1, 1000-kernels weight under normal planting date, and nitrogen content under late planting date, suggesting the dominant genes of these attributes were explained by heterozygosity, and dominance tended to act positively. On the other hand, the degrees of residual dominance impact were not significant, which might be explained by the existence of a considerable amount of cancelling the dominant effects in the parents. The positive F value for all parameters under each planting date condition except spikes plant−1, kernels spike−1 under each planting date, spikelets spike−1, spike length under late planting date, kernel weight spike−1, and 100-kerenl weight under normal planting date suggests that the frequency of dominant alleles was greater than the recessive ones in the parents. A similar result was observed by El-Hawary [100], Abd El-Hady et al. [101] and Al-Timimi et al. [102].

The values of (H2/4H1) were approximately equal to the ratio 0.25 for all studied parameters under each planting date except catalase activity under each planting date, proline content under late planting date, spikelets spike−1, kernel weight spike−1, grain yield plant−1 under each planting date, spike length under normal planting date, 1000-kernels weight under late planting date, and nitrogen content under normal planting date. Identifying the positive and negative alleles were evenly allocated among parents. The other parameters for each planting date were not approximately equal to the ratio of 0.25, suggesting that the positive and negative alleles have been allocated unevenly among the parents. Similar findings were reported [1, 101, 102]. For all parameters, the (KD/KR) ratio was more than one under each planting date, revealing the abundance of dominant alleles, except spikes number plant−1 and kernels number spike−1 under each planting date, spike length under late planting date and spikelets number spike−1, and 1000-kernels weight under normal planting date, which were less than one revealing an excess of recessive alleles in parents. According to the number of gene groups (h2/H2), the outcomes obviously demonstrated that each one of the studied attributes was controlled at least by one gene group, with the exception of catalase activity under late planting date, peroxidase activity under normal planting date, spike length, and kernels weight spike−1 under normal planting date that were controlled at least by two gene groups. While 1000-kernels weight under normal planting dates was controlled by at least four gene groups. These results agreed with of Chaudhari et al. [1] and Abd El-Hady et al. [101].

Heritability in a broad sense estimates the ratio of total genetic variance to the phenotypic variance. The results show that h2(b.s.) had medium or large values for all parameters at each planting date, revealing that most of the phenotypic variability for the earliness component was due to genetic effects. The observed moderate or large h2(n.s) values for all parameters under each planting date, with the exception of catalase activity under late planting date, peroxidase activity, proline content under normal planting date, spikes number plant−1, kernels weight spike−1, 1000-kernels weight under late planting date, and nitrogen content under each planting date, which had low values of h2(n.s), clarifying the role of additive gene action in controlling these traits. Hence, selection for these parameters might be used in the first segregating generations. catalase activity under late planting date, peroxidase activity, proline content under normal planting date, spikes number plant−1, kernels weight spike−1, and 1000-kernels weight under late planting date, and nitrogen content under each planting date, which had low values of heritability in a narrow sense. These outcomes are in general agreement with Chaudhari et al. [1] and Hassan and Al Jubouri [103], suggesting that selection could be less effective in the segregated generations.

Conclusions

Generally, the effect of planting dates showed significant variations among normal and late planting dates for all studied attributes, and wheat varieties and its crosses had significant or highly significant differences for most studied parameters. The planting date × genotypes associations were significant for most parameters. Wheat genotypes of Sids 14, Sakha 95, Misr 3, P3×P4, P3×P6 and P5×P6 were the best parents and crosses for most of the studied physiological and agronomic traits under late sowing date conditions, and it can be recommended to include these genotypes in the wheat breeding program to withstand climatic changes in late planting. Each additive (D) and dominance (H1 and H2) gene effects were significant for most parameters under normal and late planting dates. Most characters were given medium or large values for heritability in the narrow sense (h2n.s) under both planting dates. Regarding the significant role of additive gene action in determining these parameters, early segregating generations may benefit from selection for them.
