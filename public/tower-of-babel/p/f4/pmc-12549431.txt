Linoelaidic acid alleviates COM-induced kidney injury and crystal deposition via the PPARγ/MAPK signaling pathway
Authors: Wei Qi, Xu Yan, Cunyao Li, Junfeng Jing, Yanbin Zhang, Junhua Xi, Chaozhao Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRenal injury and calcium oxalate crystal deposition are the core pathological processes of kidney stone formation, and their molecular mechanisms have not been fully elucidated while the clinical intervention methods are limited. Many studies have shown that the intestinal microbiota can produce various metabolites that affect renal function through the gut-kidney axis and participate in the formation of kidney stones. This study aims to identify the microbial metabolites related to kidney stone patients and explore their mechanism of action in kidney stones. Microbiome and metabolome analyses are used to search for microbial-related metabolites in the intestines of kidney stone patients. Using COM crystal-treated TCMK-1 cells as a model, the functions of metabolites are evaluated by detecting cell viability, apoptosis rate, cell-crystal adhesion, reactive oxygen species and MDA levels in vitro. In vivo, a glyoxylate-based kidney stone mouse model is established, and the effects of metabolites on renal function are assessed by HE staining, PAS staining, creatinine and urea nitrogen levels. The effects of metabolites on renal injury and crystal deposition are evaluated by Tunel, Von-Kossa staining, CD44 and OPN level detection. Transcriptional sequencing combined with western blot is used to search and verify the related signaling pathways affected by metabolites. It is found that linoelaidic acid is significantly decreased in kidney stone patients. Supplementing linoelaidic acid in vitro can significantly reduce COM crystal-induced cell apoptosis, crystal adhesion, ROS and MDA levels, and increase cell viability. In kidney stone mice, supplementing linoelaidic acid reduces renal tubular damage, crystal deposition, and improves renal function. Mechanistically, linoelaidic acid upregulates the expression of PPARγ in kidney stones to inhibit the activation of the MAPK signaling pathway. Introduction

The incidence of kidney stones is constantly increasing worldwide [1, 2]. Multiple kinds of kidney damage, such as acute kidney injury, chronic kidney disease, and renal fibrosis, can be led about by kidney stones [3]. Calcium oxalate (CaOx) stones contribute to over 80% of kidney stones, thus being the most common type. Calcium oxalate monohydrate (COM) is the main mineral component of kidney stones [4]. Currently, there are several effective treatment methods available for kidney stones. The most commonly used method is surgery, such as extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy, ureteroscopy, and percutaneous nephrolithotomy. However, the recurrence rate of the removed stones within the first 5 years is as high as 50% [5, 6]. Therefore, it is necessary to have a deeper understanding of the mechanism of stone formation and to search for more effective therapies for preventing the occurrence and recurrence of stones.

In recent years, numerous studies have shown that the gut microbiota can influence renal function through the gut-kidney axis, thereby leading to various kidney diseases [7]. According to recent studies, intestinal barrier dysfunction, hyperoxaluria, and kidney inflammation are caused by the persistent overabundance of Bacteroidota in the transplanted group’s fecal microbiota in rats with kidney stones [8]. Furthermore, renal stone production and susceptibility may be significantly affected by dysbiosis of the gut microbiota through processes include controlling urine metabolites, altering intestinal oxalate metabolism, and triggering systemic inflammatory responses [9]. Although several studies have reported the association between microorganisms and kidney stones, the overview of the gut microbiota in kidney stone and its functional contribution to kidney stone have not yet been systematically characterized. The gut microbiome plays an important role in endogenous host metabolism through the production of metabolites. Microbial metabolites are an important factor linking the gut microbiota to kidney stone [10]. As biochemical converters, the gut microbiota can transform the complex chemical space presented by dietary and host nutrients into a metabolic environment [11]. These metabolites can participate in the formation of kidney stones through various mechanisms such as immunity, inflammation, and oxidative stress [12].

In this study, we conducted 16S rRNA sequencing and quantitative metabolomics sequencing on samples from clinical kidney stone patients and healthy controls. We found that the metabolite linoelaidic acid, which is related to the gut microbiota, was significantly reduced in kidney stone patients. Linoelaidic acid (LA) is a polyunsaturated fatty acid. In diabetes, LA, as an active component isolated from guihua, can inhibit α-amylase and α-glucosidase, the formation of advanced glycation end products, and oxidative stress, and alleviate the pathological process of diabetes [13]. We established kidney stone cell and animal models to discover that LA is related to kidney stones, and demonstrated its inhibitory effect on the progression of kidney stones in cells and mice. Further studies on the potential mechanism of LA revealed that LA reduces renal injury and oxidative stress through the PPARγ/MAPK signaling pathway. Our research reveals the mechanism of action of metabolites from gut microbiota, providing potential strategies for the prevention and treatment of kidney stones.

Materials and methods

Clinical sample collection

This study recruited 12 patients with idiopathic CaOx stones (10 males and 2 females; age range 28-71 years, median 57 years) and 5 healthy controls. The patients were confirmed to have hypercalciuria (> 250 mg/day) and/or hyperoxanuria (> 40 mg/day) through FTIR stone analysis (Bruker Optics) and two consecutive 24-h urine collections. After fasted for the whole night, samples of serum and feces were collected in the morning. Patients with underlying diseases such as diabetes and hypertension, urinary tract infections, congenital urinary system dysplasia, and urinary tumours were excluded. About 200 mg for the fecal samples were put in freezers and sterilized cryovials before being sent direct into the lab and stored at -80 °C. Blood was drawn from the venous veins, and the tubes were then gently shaken and centrifuged for 10 min at room temperature at 3000 rpm. For subsequent examination, the supernatant (serum) was gathered in 1.5 ml cryovials and kept at -80 °C. Every participant signed out a written informed permission form, and the study and all the experimental procedures were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (No. 20200707).

16S rRNA Sequencing

The PowerSoil® DNA isolation kit (Mo Bio Laboratories) was used to extract total genomic DNA from fecal samples according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The Qubit 3.0 fluorescence spectrometer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA), 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit were applied to evaluate the quality and quantity of the extracted DNA. With the Illumina (Vazyme Biotech) VAHTS Universal Plus DNA library preparation kit, a paired-end library with an insert size of around 350 bp was generated. The library was sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Beijing BioMarker Technology Co., Ltd.) using a 150 bp paired-end sequencing mode. From the Illumina raw sequence reads, two matching FASTQ files were created using base calling. After filtering out low-quality reads and adapter cutting, clean sequencing data were used for further bioinformatics research.

A preliminary analysis of the sequencing data was conducted using QIIME2 and the R software (v3.2.0). The α diversity differences between the groups, including the Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices, were evaluated with the Kruskal–Wallis test. β diversity was investigated via principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). In addition, using default settings, Metagenomseq used default parameters to identify species with abundant differences between groups.

Q300 Quantitative metabolomics

Before conducting the metabolomics analysis, the serum was thawed at 4 °C, and 20 μL of the serum was transferred to a pre-cooled 96-well plate. 120 μL of pre-cooled methanol solution containing an internal standard was added to each well, and the mixture was vortexed for 20 min. The final mixture was centrifuged for 20 min at 4 °C and 4000 g. Following transferring the supernatant to a new 96-well plate, each hole got 20 μL of derivatization reagent and 330 μL of a 50% methanol solution. After centrifugation at 4 °C (4000 g for 30 min), 135 μL of the supernatant was extracted and distributed to a new 96-well plate. Each well was filled with 10 μL of the internal standard. The 96-well plate was sealed and subjected to Q300 chip analysis (Shanghai Metropolis Biotechnology Co., Ltd.).

Preparation of COM

The preparation method of COM crystals is as described previously [14]. Mix 10 mmol of calcium chloride with 10 mmol of sodium carbonate in a 10 mmol triethanolamine buffer solution containing 90 mmol of sodium chloride (pH 7.4), to obtain a final concentration of 5 mmol of calcium chloride and 0.5 mmol of sodium carbonate. Then incubate at room temperature overnight. The prepared COM is collected by filtration and sterilized under high pressure before being used for subsequent experiments.

Cell culture and treatment

The mouse renal tubular epithelial cells (TCMK-1) were purchased from Hunan Fenghui Biotechnology Co., Ltd. TCMK-1 cells were cultivated under standard conditions at 37 ℃ with 5% CO₂in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco (USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, USA). Before using 100 μg/mL COM to stimulate, we exposed TCMK-1 cells to different concentrations (6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100 μM) of LA (MCE, China, HY-W071746) for 12 h. Cells were subsequently transferred to a new setup with DMEM medium and 0.5% fetal bovine serum for additional culture.

Cell viability assay

(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-Diphenyltetrazolium Bromide (MTT) is used to assess cell viability. TCMK-1 cells were cultured in petri dishes until 80% confluence and then counted. The cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with different doses of LA for 12 h before stimulation with COM (100 μg/mL). Then, 10 μL of MTT solution was added to each well and incubated for 4 h. The optical density at 570 nm was analyzed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime, China).

Cell apoptosis assays

TCMK-1 cells were collected and stained with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (C1062S, Beyotime, China) to measure cell apoptosis. After incubation at room temperature in the dark for 20 min, the samples were analyzed using a flow cytometer. The apoptotic cell population was quantitatively analyzed using the FlowJo software.

Calcein-PI staining

The cells were inoculated into a 24-well plate and treated with COM. At the same time, LA (or not) was added under specified conditions. Then, the culture medium was removed and an appropriate amount of calcein AM/PI working solution (PF00007, proteintech, China) was added to the cells. The cells were incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. The images were observed using a fluorescence microscope (CX41, Olympus, Japan) and quantitative analysis was performed.

Crystal-cell adhesion assay

The cells were cultured in 6-well plates until they reached 80% confluence. After washing the cells with PBS, they were placed in DMEM medium containing 100 μg/mL COM crystals, and either LA was added simultaneously or not at all for 12 h of cultivation. Subsequently, the cells were vigorously rinsed three times with PBS to remove the unbound COM crystals. Finally, pictures were taken under a microscope, and the number of attached crystals in at least 3 random high-power fields in each well was counted.

ROS detection

After 0.25% trypsin was added to the cells, they were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for five minutes at 4 °C. The 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescent probe (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was incubated with the cells in serum-free media in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. A flow cytometry (Agilent, USA) and FlowJo software were adopted aiming to detect the ROS level.

LDH and MDA assay

The lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity in the cell culture medium was evaluated using the lactate dehydrogenase kit (C0016, Beyotime, China). After induction, the cells were combined with 10% of the total volume of the cell culture medium (the lactate release solution) and incubated for 1 h. After five minutes of centrifuging the cells at 400 g in a centrifuge, 120 μL of the supernatant were taken from each well and added to a fresh 96-well plate. The absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, the MDA detection kit (S0131, Beyotime, China) was applied to determine the MDA content in the cells.

Animal model

Six-week-old male BALB/c mice (weighing 20–25 g) were obtained from Shanghai Slaccas Laboratory Animal Co.. All mice were randomly divided into 3 groups (n = 5): Control group, model group, and model + LA group. According to a previous study [15] to establish a mouse model of kidney stones, 80 mg/kg glyoxylate (Gly, MCE, China, HY-79494) was intraperitoneally injected daily for 6 consecutive days. The mice in the model + LA group were given LA (200 mg/kg) by gavage every day for 2 weeks before glyoxylate injection. After 6 days of glyoxylate injection, the mice were euthanized. Neck dislocation was used to sacrifice the mice in order to obtain samples for further investigation. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the "Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals" of the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University, and were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (No. LLSC20200654).

RNA-sequencing analysis

Mouse kidney tissues were transported under low-temperature conditions to Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) for transcriptome sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq platform. The raw sequencing reads were then processed using the BMKCloud online bioinformatics platform (www.biocloud.net). Differential expression analysis between the two groups was performed using the DESeq2 package. Genes with a log₂ fold change > 1 and an adjusted p-value (padj) < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Histology staining

Using histological procedures, including 4% paraformaldehyde fixation, dehydration, wax infiltration, embedding, and sectioning (4 μm), mice kidneys were processed into wax-embedded sections. The kidney sections were then stained with hematoxylin–eosin (HE) according to standard procedures and observed using a polarized light microscope (CX41, Olympus, Japan). The periodic acid-schiff (PAS) staining method (product of ebiogo) was used to stain the kidney sections, following the instructions offered by the manufacturer. The PAS-stained kidney samples were evaluated to detect renal injury.

Von-kossa staining

Kidney sections were stained using the Von-kossa staining kit (ebiogo) to determine the crystal deposition. Brieffy, after dewaxing, the sections were placed in Von Kossa silver solution and irradiated with strong light for 120 min, treated with 5% sodium thiosulfate solution for 2 min, then stained with hematoxylin–eosin, dehydrated and trans-parent, and photographed for analysis.

Renal function and urinary analysis

Using the commercial kit (produced by Nanjing Jian-cheng, China) and following the manufacturer’s instructions, the activities of urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (Cr) in serum were determined by colorimetry. Using the commercial kit (produced by COIBO, China) and following the manufacturer’s instructions, the content of oxalic acid in urine was measured.

TUNEL staining

Apoptosis of renal tissue cells was detected by using the terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nicke-end labeling (TUNEL) staining kit (Roch, China). Protease K (2 mg/ml) was added to the tissue sections and incubated for 10 min. After 30 min of room temperature incubation, 50 μl of TdT incubation buffer was added, and the sections were directed to incubate for 60 min at 37 °C in the dark. Then using a microscope to observe and take images.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Following the manufacturer’s suggestions, we used the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, New York) to purify total RNA from fresh kidney tissues and cells. The first-strand cDNA (2 μg) was made with a Superscript II kit (TAKARA, Japan). The primers were produced by Sangon Biotech Corporation (Shanghai, China). The 2−ΔΔCt method was used for assessing the data, and GAPDH was chosen as the normalization value for reference. All primer sequence are in the supplementary materials (Table S1).

Western blot

Kidney tissues and cell pellets were extracted with RIPA lysis buffer (Biyun Tian, China) to obtain total proteins, and the protein concentration was determined using BCA protein assay. Equal amounts of protein (50 μg per sample) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad, USA). The membranes were incubated at room temperature with 5% skimmed milk for 1 h, followed by incubation with the following primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: CD44 (1:1000, Affinity, DF6392), OPN (1:2000, Proteintech, 22,952–1-AP), PPARγ (1:1000, Abclonal, A0270), JNK (1:10,000, Proteintech, 66,210–1-Ig), phosphorylated JNK (1:1000, Abclonal, AP0631), p38 (1:2000, Affinity, BF8015), phosphorylated p38 (1:1000, Affinity, AF4001), and GAPDH (1:10,000, Proteintech, 60,004–1-Ig). After washing, the membranes were treated for 2 h at room temperature with the secondary antibody coupled with horseradish peroxidase (HRP). The enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit was used to detect protein bands. ImageJ software was used to quantify the band intensities, with GAPDH acting as the internal reference.

Statistic analysis

The result report is presented as Mean ± SD. Student’s t-test was used to determine the statistical significance of the value differences between the two groups. One-way analysis of variance was used for the statistical analysis of value differences among multiple groups (SPSS, Armonk, NY, USA). A value of p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Composition of gut microbiota in the control and kidney stone groups

In this study, fecal samples from 5 healthy controls and 12 patients with kidney stones were subjected to 16S rRNA sequencing to evaluate the composition of intestinal microbiota between the two groups. The gut microbiota’s variety and quantity were assessed using the Chao1, Simpson, and Shannon indexes (Fig. 1A). Between the two groups, there were no apparent modifications in the microbial group’s diversity. The evaluation of the β -diversity of the intestinal microbiota using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on the Bray–Curtis distance matrix indicated that there was no significant difference in the fecal microbiota between the two groups either (Fig. 1B). These findings suggest that the diversity and community abundance of the gut microbiota may not be impacted by the illness situation. Furthermore, we examined the relative abundances of the top 12 gut microbes at the phylum and genus categorization levels in the control and kidney Stone groups. At the phylum level (Fig. 1C), the intestinal microbiota is mainly composed of Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Proteobacteria and Actinobacteriota. Firmicutes is the dominant group, accounting for more than 50% of all groups. At the genus level (Fig. 1D), the intestinal flora is mainly composed of Bacteroides, Faecalibanterium and Escherichia_Shigella. In the kidney stone group, the proportion of Escherichia_Shigella was significantly higher than that in the control group. Previous studies have shown that Escherichia_Shigella is a potential pathogen. The increase of Escherichia_Shigella bacteria in patients with ovarian cancer is associated with chronic inflammation and the weakening of the immune system. These bacteria may affect the normal function of the immune system by triggering inflammatory responses. Thereby promoting the growth and spread of tumors [16]. Species abundance data between the two groups were analyzed using the Metagenomseq method, and a total of 22 genus-level differential microorganisms were screened based on p value < 0.05 (Table S2). From this, it can be inferred that patients with kidney stones have dysbiosis.

Linoelaidic acid was significantly decreased in kidney stones

We used quantitative metabolomics sequencing for identifying potentially vital metabolites and investigate the role of the gut microbiota in kidney stones. A total of 196 metabolites were identified in this study. The average abundance composition of each metabolite type in all samples is shown in Fig. 2A. Among them, organic acids, amino acids, short-chain fatty acids and fatty acids are the main types of metabolites, accounting for 47.87%, 40.24%, 4.92% and 4.59% respectively. OPLS-DA showed a significant difference in serum metabolites between the Control group and the kidney stone group (Fig. 2B). Variable importance in projection (VIP) and Correlation coefficients (Corr. coeffs) were used to screen reliable metabolic markers. Based on the results of the OPLS-DA model, a total of 76 metabolic markers (Fig. 2C) were screened. Meanwhile, we could also use unidimensional screening to identify 27 potential metabolic markers (Fig. 2D). We finally obtained 26 potential biomarkers that met both one-dimensional and multi-dimensional screening criteria (Fig. 2E). The 26 potential metabolic markers are: Lysine, Histidine, Sarcosine, Alanine, Glycylproline, Lactic acid, Tyrosine, Asparagine, Phenylalanine, Aspartic acid, Aminoadipic acid, 2-Hydroxycaproic acid, Glycine, N-Acetylserine, Maltose|Lactose, Methionine, Isoleucine, Leucine, Xylose, Fructose, Tryptophan, GLCA, Linoelaidic acid, 3-Hydroxyphenylacetic acid, Propionylcarnitine and Valerylcarnitine(Fig. 2F). To further explore the internal correlations between differentially expressed metabolites in the samples and the differential microbiota, we conducted a correlation analysis. We found that only Linoelaidic acid levels were significantly negatively correlated with CAG_56 and significantly positively correlated with unclassified_Enterobacteriaceae (Fig. 2G). The expression level of Linoelaidic acid in the kidney stone group was significantly lower than that in the Control group (Fig. 2H). It is suggested that the change in the content of Linoelaidic acid may be related to the change of intestinal flora. Furthermore, we also evaluated the ability of Linoelaidic acid in diagnosing kidney stones. Receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis showed that the area under curve (AUC) of Linoelaidic acid Youdaoplaceholder0 0.833, suggesting its potential as a new diagnostic marker for kidney stone (Fig. 2I).

Linoelaidic acid inhibits COM-induced apoptosis

To further investigate the mechanism of LA in renal injury related to kidney stones, we used TCMK-1 cells as the in vitro model of renal injury caused by COM. Firstly, we detected the safe concentration of LA in TCMK-1 cells through the MTT assay (Fig. 3A) and the impact of varying LA concentrations on COM-induced TCMK-1 cell viability and the gradual increase in COM-induced TCMK-1 cell viability after LA treatment (Fig. 3B). One prevalent mechanism in the pathophysiology of kidney stones are injury to renal tubular epithelial cells (TEC), causing necrosis and apoptosis [17]. We used flow cytometry to determine the effect of LA on COM-induced apoptosis of TCMK-1 cells. The results showed that LA inhibited apoptosis in a dose-dependent manner (Figs. 3C and 3D). The results of the Calcein-PI staining experiment showed that COM increased cell death in TCMK-1 cells, while LA treatment could block this effect (Figs. 3E and 3F). An increase in Lactate dehydrogenase levels may be associated with physiological factors or pathological diseases [18]. The determination results of our Lactate dehydrogenase levels indicated that different concentrations of LA would significantly inhibit COM-induced cell damage (Fig. 3G). That is, increased cell toxicity and inhibited cell viability induced by COM could be rescued by LA.

Linoelaidic acid ameliorates renal calcium oxalate crystal deposition in glyoxylic acid-treated mouse

Next, a kidney stone mouse model was established to investigate the protective impact of LA. HE and PAS staining revealed that in comparison to the control group, the renal epithelial cells of mice in the model group were swollen, with many vacuoles in the cells. There were necrotic cell fragments and interstitial edema in the renal tubules. After treatment with LA, the edema of mouse renal tubular epithelial cells was alleviated and the intracellular vacuoles were reduced (Fig. 5A). Von-Kossa staining revealed crystal deposition in the renal interstitial of mice, and LA treatment significantly reduced the crystal deposition induced by glyoxylic acid (Fig. 5B). Cr and BUN is one of the main indicators of kidney damage [21]. Compared with the control group, the levels of serum BUN and Cr in the model group mice increased, and the levels of BUN and Cr were significantly decreased after LA treatment (Figs. 5C and 5D). The primary promoter of crystal formation, oxalate accelerates up the crystallization or aggregation of stones by triggering a number of processes [22]. Thus, the body’s metabolism of oxalate significantly influences the formation of kidney stones. Our results showed that LA treatment significantly reduced the increase in urinary oxalic acid levels in the model group mice (Fig. 5E).

Linoelaidic acid ameliorates renal injury in glyoxylic acid-treated mouse

TUNEL staining showed that there were more TUNEL-positive cells in mice tissues of the model group. The results revealed that LA treated reduced apoptotic cells (Fig. 6A). OPN is a secretory glycoprotein that increases when there is kidney injury. Studies have shown that OPN promotes the adhesion of kidney stones through its interaction with its receptor CD44 [23]. Therefore, we detected the expression of OPN and its receptor CD44 in mice with kidney stones. Real-time PCR showed that the increase of CD44 and OPN induced by glyoxylic acid was inhibited after LA treatment (Figs. 6B and 6C). Besides. Our Western blot results were consistent with those of Real-time PCR. After LA treatment, the protein levels of CD44 and OPN were inhibited (Fig. 6D and 6E). In summary, our data here demonstrate the protective role of LA against stone formation and crystal-induced renal injury in vivo.

Linoelaidic acid upregulates PPARγ to inhibit the activation of the MAPK pathway

To deeply explore the potential mechanism of LA in the progression of kidney stones, we conducted RNA sequencing analysis on cells supplemented with LA and their control cells. The volcano map of differentially expressed genes (Fig. 7A) shows that a total of 168 differentially expressed genes were identified, among which 96 genes were significantly up-regulated and 72 genes were significantly down-regulated. GO analysis of these differentially expressed genes indicated that the differentially expressed genes were mainly involved in immune responses, extracellular matrix remodeling and chemokine activities (Fig. 7B). According to KEGG analysis, genes with differential expression were significantly enriched in related pathways including PPAR signaling pathways, chemokine signaling pathways, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions. (Fig. 7C). Peroxisome Proliferator Activated Receptor (PPAR) is a kind of ligand activated nuclear transcription factor, is a member of the nuclear hormone receptor superfamily, including PPARα, PPAR and PPARγ three subtypes [24]. Evidence indicates that PPAR-γ is a key regulatory factor during intestinal inflammation. Reduced PPARγ expression might promote the synthesis of inflammatory factors, which could stimulate the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway [25, 26]. Western blot research revealed that in COM-induced TCMK-1 cells, LA decreased the protein expressions of p-p38 and p-JNK and increased the expression of PPARγ (Fig. 7D and 7G ). Consistent with this, glyoxylic acid significantly inhibited the expression of PPARγ in mouse renal tissues. The expression of p-p38 and p-JNK was increased. Supplementing LA significantly reversed the expression of PPARγ, p-p38 and p-JNK (Fig. 7F and 7G). These findings suggest that LA may be involved in the progression of kidney stones by up-regulating PPARγ and inhibiting the MAPK signaling pathway.

Discussion

Kidney stones, as a globally prevalent urinary system disease, have a complex formation mechanism involving genetic, environmental and metabolic factors. In recent years, the association research between gut microbiota and its metabolites and human diseases, especially kidney stones, has gradually become a hot topic. The intestinal microbiota and its metabolites regulate oxalic acid metabolism, immune balance and metabolite profile through multiple pathways, becoming an environmental factor that cannot be ignored in the pathogenesis of kidney stones. Many studies have shown that targeting the gut microbiota and its metabolites may be a promising disease treatment strategy, but the functions of the metabolites of the gut microbiota in kidney stones remain largely unclear.

Our research provides a comprehensive analysis of the gut microbiome and related metabolites in patients with kidney stones. Our data indicate that there are significant differences in the gut microbiome and serum metabolome between patients with kidney stones and healthy controls. Further analysis revealed that the content of Linoelaidic acid, as a metabolite related to the intestinal flora of kidney stones, significantly decreased in the kidney stone group. Furthermore, the results of ROC analysis suggest that LA has good diagnostic performance.

Linoelaidic acid, also known as 9, 12-octadecadienoic acid, is a polyunsaturated fatty acid containing 18 carbon atoms. Polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) are regarded as key elements in mammalian cells and are usually classified into omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids. Due to their anti-inflammatory and antibacterial properties, Therefore, it plays an important role in the prevention and treatment of various health problems [27]. Studies have shown that PUFAs mediate inflammation by inhibiting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Thereby protecting renal function [28]. A previous study showed that the combination of PUFA and ursodeoxycholic acid could increase the levels of bile phospholipids and bile acids, reduce cholesterol saturation and the production of mucin. Thereby dissolving cholesterol gallstones in mice [29]. In addition, PUFAs ameliorate renal stone-induced renal tubular damage via miR-93-5p/Pknox1 axis [30]. At present, there are relatively few reports on the functional research of polyunsaturated fatty acid Linoelaidic acid. A study shows that the unsaturated fatty acid Linoelaidic acid derived from Marine fish (Ophisthopterus tardoore) exhibits effective anti-cancer activity against MCF-7 [31]. Furthermore, Linoelaidic acid can enhance adipogenic differentiation in stromal cells derived from adipose tissue by inhibiting the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in vitro [32]. However, the function of Linoelaidic acid for kidney stones remains unknown at present.

The first stage of stone development is the attachment of CaOx crystals to renal tubular epithelial cells. Crystals of CaOx have the direct ability to damage tissues [3]. Apoptosis of renal tubular epithelial cells following injury leads to fibrotic and inflammation environments [33]. In this study, we described the impact of LA supplementation on the progression of kidney stones. In COM-induced renal epithelial cells, COM-induced apoptosis and increased crystal deposition were consistent with previous research results [34, 35]. We speculate that LA may be embedded in the forming calcium oxalate lattice through its hydrophobic ends, interfering with the ordered arrangement of ions, thereby inhibiting the nucleation, growth rate and aggregation tendency of the crystal. Our results indicate that supplementing LA can significantly reduce COM-induced apoptosis and crystal deposition. We obtained cell-consistent results in glyoxyacid-induced kidney stone formation mice. LA treatment can effectively counteract kidney injury caused by kidney stone formation and crystal deposition. Furthermore, due to the antioxidant effect of PUFAs, we speculate that LA, as a type of PUFA, may play an antioxidant role in kidney stone. Our experimental results confirmed our speculation that supplementing LA would significantly reduce the increase in ROS content induced by COM.

To further investigate the protective mechanism of LA in kidney stones, we sought the related pathways affected by LA through transcriptome sequencing. The results of our transcriptome sequencing demonstrate that the PPAR signaling pathway is greatly enriched in the genes that are differentially expressed.. The PPAR signaling pathway is a core metabolic regulatory network activated by fatty acids and their derivatives. Through three subtypes (α, β/δ, γ) tissue specific expression and functional regulation of the body’s energy balance, lipid and glucose, steady state, and inflammation [36]. Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) is a member of the ligand-activated nuclear transcription factor superfamily, which participates in fat and cholesterol metabolism by inducing or inhibiting a series of genes and proteins. A growing body of evidence has confirmed that the activation of PPARγ has anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties [37, 38]. Based on these findings, we propose the hypothesis that LA may alleviate kidney damage caused by stones by activating the activity of PPARγ. The results of our western blot experiment indicated that LA significantly activated the expression of PPARγ in COM-induced kidney stones. PPARγ needs to form a heterodimer complex with RXR-α and then bind to the PPAR response element to affect the transcriptional activity of the target gene [39]. Studies have shown that telmisartan (TLM) by PPARγ-AKT/STAT3/p38 MAPK-Snail pathway inhibition of oxalate and calcium oxalate crystals induced epithelial-mesenchymal transition [40]. In addition, Zhang, et al.[41] studies show that Aqueous Polygalae Radix extract (PRE) alleviates D-galactose-induced oxidative stress in the mouse liver and brain by modulating PPARγ/MAPK. MAPK is a major intracellular signaling pathway that can regulate many important physiological/pathological processes. Among them, p38 can be activated under various environmental stresses, including inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress [42]. We found that the expressions of p-p38 and p-JNK were significantly increased in COM-induced kidney stones. western blot verification indicated the activation of the MAPK signaling pathway. In contrast, the levels of p-p38 and p-JNK in the LA treatment group decreased, indicating that LA intervention inhibited the MAPK pathway to achieve antioxidant effects. In this study, we found that LA inhibits the activation of the MAPK signaling pathway by increasing the level of PPARγ in kidney stones, thereby alleviating renal injury, crystal deposition and oxidative stress. This study provides a new perspective for the intestinal microbiota-related metabolite LA to become a potential therapeutic pathway for kidney stones.

We admit that there are some limitations in this study. One major limitation is the limited number of clinical samples, with both cells and animal models derived from mice. In addition, the glyoxylic acid used in this study induced diffuse deposition of crystals in the renal tubule lumen in the mouse model. However, It is important to note that the CaOx crystals deposited in the kidneys due to hyperoxaluria mainly cause renal calcification, rather than typical, excretable kidney stones. In patients with idiopathic CaOx stone formation, the Randall plaque is considered to be the anchoring point for the excessive growth of CaOx crystals. Hyperoxaluria exacerbates the process of Randall plaques being exposed to the urine space by promoting CaOx supersaturation, renal tubular crystal retention, and renal epithelial oxidative damage, thereby possibly promoting the nucleation of CaOx crystals on the Randall plaques and accelerating the clinical stone formation[43]. Although our experiments have demonstrated that LA significantly reduces the kidney crystal deposition induced by hyperoxaluria by alleviating oxidative stress, our model did not directly assess the effect of LA on clinical stone formation based on the Randall plaques. The interaction between the mechanisms related to hyperoxaluria and the pathology of the Randall plaques, as well as their specific contribution to clinical stone formation, requires further specialized research in models that are closer to clinical stone formation. The role of LA in the formation of disease stones requires more research support. Therefore, future research should focus on verifying this discovery in a larger population and deeply analyzing its molecular mechanism in multiple models.

Conclusion

This study, through multi-omics analysis, found that the significant decrease in the level of the intestinal microbiota-related metabolite LA is an important metabolic consequence of intestinal microbiota imbalance in patients with kidney stones. Through in vitro and in vivo functional studies, it was found that supplementing LA inhibits the activation of the MAPK signaling pathway by increasing the PPARγ level in kidney stones, thereby alleviating the core pathological process of kidney stones. Our research has for the first time established the key protective role of LA in the occurrence and development of kidney stones. Our research results indicate that LA may become an important target for the treatment or diagnosis of kidney stones.
