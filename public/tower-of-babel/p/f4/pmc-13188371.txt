Augmedicine: Ultrasound: Training in ultrasound using XR – a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Tessa A. Mulder, Robin C. Jacobs, Manon S. Zuurmond, Adel Qaddoumi, Madelien V. Regeer, Friedo W. Dekker, Martijn P. Bauer, Alexandr Srámek, Alexandra M.J. Langers, Beerend P. Hierck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPoint-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is a diagnostic tool increasingly used. For novices, challenges such as probe orientation and hand-eye coordination make POCUS difficult to master. Extended reality (XR) can enhance POCUS training by projecting 3D anatomical models and offering real-time feedback on probe positioning. We developed the ‘Augmedicine: Ultrasound’ XR application, which enables heart POCUS training without the need for an instructor or test subject. The goal of this study was to determine whether XR-based training is non-inferior to standard POCUS training.MethodsMedical students in their internal medicine clerkship were randomized to XR-integrated POCUS training or standard training under direct instructor supervision. Competence was assessed through a practical test, where students obtained two ultrasound images and measured e-point septal separation (EPSS) and tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE). Expert-determined values served as references and were used to determine non-inferiority. Secondary endpoints included test completion time and image quality.ResultsFifty-six students participated: 28 in the XR group and 28 in the standard training group. The mean difference in EPSS between groups was 0.3 mm (95% CI − 0.8 to 1.3 mm), and for TAPSE 0.5 mm (95% CI − 1.4 to 2.6 mm). In both cases, the confidence intervals remained within the predefined non-inferiority margins, indicating comparable performance between XR-based and standard training. There was no significant difference in the time taken to complete the test, and image quality was slightly higher in the XR group compared to the standard group.ConclusionXR-based POCUS training proved to be non-inferior to standard training in acquiring and measuring basic cardiac ultrasound images. Beyond comparable performance, the XR approach offers clear benefits in flexibility, scalability, and reduced reliance on instructors and test subjects—making it particularly valuable for training large cohorts and allowing learners to practice at any time and for any duration. Background

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is a bedside imaging technique focused on addressing specific diagnostic questions. Unlike other imaging modalities, POCUS heavily relies on the operator’s skills. Traditionally reserved for certain medical specialists, ultrasound in general and POCUS in particular is now being adopted by a growing number of physicians and incorporated into medical school curricula [1]. This expanded adoption presents significant challenges in scaling training effectively while maintaining quality [2].

The current standard in POCUS education is structured, hands-on training on test subjects under direct supervision. This standard approach ensures high-quality training through real-time feedback and close instructor guidance. However, it is highly resource-intensive: it requires skilled faculty to supervise each session and relies on the availability of volunteers or patients for practice [3, 4]. These limitations are compounded by the steep learning curve for mastering critical skills, including 3D anatomical thinking, probe orientation, and hand-eye coordination [4].

Increasingly, 3D simulation software and extended reality (XR) applications—including Virtual Reality (VR), Augmented Reality (AR), and Mixed Reality—are being developed as a solution to these challenges [5–8]. XR enriches real-world environments by adding interactive 3-Dimensional (3D) projections, offering unique opportunities for active learning in authentic yet controlled learning environments, without the restraints of standard training. Trainees benefit from repetitive, self-paced learning, which is enriched with automated personalized feedback, while maintaining the possibility of collaboration with peers and instructors. Its use in ultrasound education is especially promising, as XR allows for the projection of a 3D anatomical model inside a phantom or patient, providing immediate feedback on probe orientation and corresponding ultrasound images [9]. For example, Ebner et al. 2019 [10] showed that their AR application improves the accuracy of kidney measurements during an ultrasound task. An additional benefit lies in the conservation of teachers and volunteers for hands-on practice when part of the training can be done with the AR application. This proves particularly valuable in situations where large numbers of students need to be trained.

Building on these advancements, we developed an XR application, based on AR, specifically focused on echocardiography training. This application aims to improve the learning process by combining 3D anatomical visualization with ultrasound image generation, providing users with direct feedback on their probe positioning and the resulting ultrasound views. Echocardiography, being the most widely utilized POCUS application [11], provides a range of difficulty levels, from basic image generation to advanced tasks like valve function assessment. This makes it suitable for learners of varying expertise while also offering possibilities for objective tracking of skill development over time. Additionally, cardiac anatomy and physiology prove difficult and present unique challenges for novices, making echocardiography an ideal focus for a tool designed to improve anatomical understanding and probe-handling skills.

If XR-based training can offer comparable learning outcomes to standard training, it could significantly enhance accessibility and scalability, allowing students to practice independently without the constraints of instructor availability. Therefore, the aim of this study is to determine whether XR-based echocardiography training is non-inferior to standard POCUS training in terms of ultrasound image acquisition and measurement accuracy.

Methods

Study design

This study was a randomized controlled trial (RCT) comparing training in point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) using an augmented reality (AR) application, ‘Augmedicine: Ultrasound’ to standard instructor-guided training. The study was conducted at the Leiden University Medical Centre (LUMC) with medical students in their internal medicine clerkship. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the CONSORT guidelines. Participants were randomly assigned (1:1) to either the XR training group or the standard training group using block randomization with a block size of two. Randomization was performed using the Sealed Envelope online tool [12]. Outcome assessors were blinded to the training method. See Fig. 1 for a visualization of the study design. The study protocol is available on the ISRCTN registry (10.1186/ISRCTN13887304) [13].

Participants

Only participants without prior ultrasound training were included. Participants were excluded if they had performed ultrasound more than once prior to the study, had prior participation in a study with an XR ultrasound application and participated in extracurricular activities related to anatomical education, such as dissection courses.

Baseline measurements

At baseline, participants completed a questionnaire assessing their age, hand dominance, and prior experience with ultrasound and/or XR. To evaluate their anatomical knowledge, they completed an anatomical knowledge test developed specifically for this study. The test consisted of 10 very short answer questions targeting spatial anatomical knowledge of the heart and thorax. Content and face validity of the test were established by two independent anatomy experts. In addition, all participants performed a standardized Mental Rotation Test (MRT) to assess baseline spatial ability [14]. The questionnaire, anatomical knowledge test and MRT can be found in the supplementary files.

The XR application

The ‘Augmedicine: Ultrasound’ application is developed by the Leiden Learning and Innovation Centre in collaboration with LUMC and Utrecht University. It is designed for the Microsoft HoloLens 2 and aims to facilitate point-of-care cardiac ultrasound training. The application displays a 3D model of a beating human heart within a male human 3D model, and a mobile phone functions as an ultrasound probe, simulating the interaction of the probe with the anatomical structures. A life-size 3D-printed phantom of the thorax is included to provide haptic feedback. See Fig. 2 for an image of the application in use. Multiple users can join the session as viewers. Face and content validity of the application was established through evaluations of different stakeholders, with feedback indicating that it was a useful tool for training novice users in cardiac ultrasound [15]. A video demonstration of the application is available in the original validation publication.

(Standard) POCUS training

In standard POCUS training, students receive a lecture and demonstration, followed by multiple group-based hands-on sessions where they practice ultrasound on each other or a volunteer under instructor supervision. The learning goals of the training are to obtain four echocardiography views: the parasternal long-axis (PLAX), parasternal short-axis (PSAX), apical 4-chamber (A4C), and subcostal 4-chamber (SC4AC). The standard POCUS training was mirrored in our study: all participants first attended a morning lecture followed by a group-based practical session in which they practiced ultrasound under supervision, using a healthy volunteer as model. To ensure that every participant received exactly the same amount of time to practice, we chose to replace the afternoon group sessions of the standard training - where 6 participants receive 2 h of group training with one instructor for two hours - by a 20 min individual training with one-on-one supervision with an experienced instructor on a healthy volunteer.

XR-based training

Participants in the XR training group followed the same morning session as the standard training group. In the afternoon however, they received a short briefing on the XR application and Microsoft HoloLens, followed by a tutorial module within the application. They then trained independently for 20 min using the app, practicing the same four echocardiography views as the standard training group.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of this study was to assess the non-inferiority of XR-based training compared to standard training, based on two key measurements: e-point septal separation (EPSS) and tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE). Although these measurements extend beyond the specific skills directly targeted by the XR application – image acquisition through improving hand–eye coordination and 3D anatomical understanding - they provide a quantitative and objective surrogate for the ability to obtain the correct image and to interpret the relevant anatomical structures.

The EPSS measures the shortest distance between the anterior leaflet of the mitral valve and the intraventricular septum during diastole. In healthy volunteers, this distance typically ranges from 0 to 7 mm and is associated with left ventricular function [16]. TAPSE measures the longitudinal motion of the tricuspid annulus, typically above 20 mm in healthy volunteers, and reflects right ventricular function [16]. An example of these measurements is shown in Fig. 3. Participants were required to obtain a parasternal long-axis (PLAX) image for EPSS and an apical four-chamber (A4C) image for TAPSE on a healthy volunteer. These images and measurements were recorded. Because different healthy volunteers were used across examination days, and EPSS and TAPSE measurements may vary both between and within individuals throughout the day, expert reference values were established immediately before each assessment. Two expert echocardiographers performed the measurements separately, and the mean of their values was used as the reference value. The block randomization ensured balanced exposure to the same volunteer on each examination day. Non-inferiority was assessed by comparing the mean differences between the XR-based and standard training groups relative to these expert values. The test was administered by a blinded examiner with no ultrasound experience.

Secondary outcome measures included the time taken to complete various parts of the test (e.g., time to obtain the optimal image, time to measure EPSS and/or TAPSE, and total time), the number of readjustments, defined as the number of times a participant acquired a new image after discarding a previous one due to dissatisfaction with image quality, as well as an optimal image score, which was assessed by two independent experts, blinded to the training the operator underwent, using a previously validated scoring form for point-of-care echocardiography [17].

After the training participants in the XR group were asked to fill in a short questionnaire on the potential presence of cybersickness complaints [18].

Statistical analysis

The sample size for this study was calculated based on the mean differences in TAPSE and EPSS measurements between the standard and XR-based training groups, with the goal of establishing non-inferiority. Given TAPSE’s larger expected SD, the sample size was calculated based on TAPSE measurements, assuming a mean of 20 mm and SD of 5 mm among novice practitioners [19]. XR-based training was considered non-inferior if the TAPSE measurement differed by less than 4 mm from standard training, meaning that the 95% confidence interval (CI) of the mean difference could not include 4 mm. A minimum of 27 participants per arm was required to achieve 90% power at an alpha of 0.05 [20].

For the primary outcome, non-inferiority was evaluated by comparing the mean differences in EPSS and TAPSE measurements from expert values. The non-inferiority margins were predefined as 3 mm for EPSS and 4 mm for TAPSE, based on clinical relevance and known standard deviations [19, 21]. To assess consistency between expert values, both the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and the mean difference with 95% CI were calculated. Non-inferiority was concluded when the 95% CI of the mean difference remained entirely within the predefined non-inferiority margins.

Baseline characteristics and secondary outcome measures were compared between groups using t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables, and chi-squared tests for categorical variables. Inter-rater agreement and reliability for the anatomical knowledge test and optimal image scoring were assessed using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for inter-rater agreement, and Cronbach’s alpha for reliability. To examine associations between baseline variables and ultrasound competence, we explored correlations (Pearson's or Spearman's). Subsequently, we performed multivariable regression analyses. We assessed associations of the baseline variables with individual ultrasound test variables (e.g., EPSS, TAPSE). To investigate whether the effect of specific baseline characteristics differed by training group, we additionally included interaction terms (e.g., anatomical knowledge × group) in the regression models. In addition to treating predictors as continuous variables, anatomical knowledge and mental rotation ability (MRT) were also analysed in tertiles (low, medium, high). All parametric continuous data were presented as means, and categorical data as proportions and percentages. Statistical analysis was performed using R Studio version 4.1.3 (Posit).

Ethical consideration

The study was conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2 October 2013), and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). The protocol of this study was approved by the Educational Research Review Board of the LUMC on February 13, 2024 (reference number: OEC/ERRB/20240213/1). All participants provided written informed consent.

Results

Between March and May 2024, 56 participants were included, 28 in the XR group and 28 in the standard training group. All participants were students from the Leiden University Medical Centre (Leiden, the Netherlands), who recently started their first clerkship at the department of internal medicine. There was a high participation rate: every 4 weeks, approximately 8 students start their internal medicine clerkship at LUMC, with 6–7 students voluntarily participating in the study. Ultrasound test

All participants performed the ultrasound test on a volunteer after the training sessions. Four participants could not perform all measurements: two participants in the standard training group could not visualize the A4C and therefore did not perform the TAPSE measurement and two participants in the XR group did visualize the PLAX but measured a completely different structure with a very large implausible value. Consequently, two participants were excluded from the non-inferiority analysis of TAPSE and two from the non-inferiority analysis of EPSS. The mean difference from the expert value for the EPSS measurement was 2.7 mm (SD 1.7) in the XR group and 2.5 mm (SD 2.2) in the standard training group (mean difference 0.3 mm; 95% CI -0.8 to 1.3 mm) (Fig. 4). For TAPSE, the measurement was 4.0 mm (SD 3.0) off the expert value in the XR group and 3.5 mm (SD 4.3) in the standard training group (mean difference 0.49 mm; 95% CI -1.4 to 2.6 mm). There was no significant difference in the time taken to complete the test, however the time to visualize the PLAX in order to measure the EPSS (time till optimal image) was longer in the XR group: 177 s versus 128 s, p-value 0.039. The optimal image score was slightly higher in the XR group compared to the standard group; 10 out of 13 versus 9 out of 13, p-value 0.042. In supplementary file 1 all ultrasound test results can be found. The reliability of the outcome measures was confirmed within our cohort: the mean difference between expert measurements was 0.2 mm for both EPSS (95% CI − 1.1 to 1.5 mm) and TAPSE (95% CI − 0.6 to 1.7 mm). Additionally, inter-rater reliability of the optimal image score was good (ICC = 0.81).

Anatomical knowledge and visuospatial abilities

All participants completed a MRT and an anatomical knowledge test prior to training. No significant correlations were found between MRT scores and any of the ultrasound performance outcomes (EPSS: R = 0.13, p = 0.33; TAPSE: R = 0.07, p = 0.62; total time: R = − 0.22, p = 0.10; optimal image score: R = 0.08, p = 0.56). Similarly, anatomical knowledge scores showed no significant associations with performance outcomes (EPSS: R = − 0.02, p = 0.88; TAPSE: R = 0.004, p = 0.98; total time: R = 0.001, p = 0.99; optimal image score: R = 0.15, p = 0.27). These findings remained consistent across multivariable regression analyses, in which neither mental rotation ability nor anatomical knowledge significantly predicted ultrasound performance. No significant interaction effects were found between training group and either baseline characteristic. The inter-rater agreement for the anatomical knowledge test was excellent (ICC = 0.95). Reliability, measured with Cronbach’s alpha, was low (0.47).

Cybersickness

Moderate cybersickness symptoms were reported by four novices. Three novices reported severe issues that could be related to cybersickness (fatigue, headache, stomach awareness), though one had pre-existing stomach complaints. These complaints resolved swiftly after removing the headset, except for the pre-existing stomach complaints. There was no correlation between ultrasound test performance and cybersickness complaints.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial evaluated the effectiveness of XR-based cardiac ultrasound training compared to standard instructor-guided training in medical students. The application was tested as an addition to hands-on ultrasound training on a real person, specifically for novice learners, as it appears to be most beneficial in this context [15]. We found that XR-based training was non-inferior to standard training in terms of objective ultrasound measurements (EPSS and TAPSE), which also includes interpretative steps beyond the specific image-acquisition skills targeted by the XR application. While time to acquire the PLAX view was slightly slower in the XR group, image quality scores were higher. These findings are particularly relevant given that mastering cardiac ultrasound typically requires intensive, one-on-one guidance from experienced instructors. Our results demonstrate that, at least in the early stages of training, such instructor involvement can be at least partially replaced by the XR-based application—without compromising performance.

Performance in the ultrasound test aligned with what could be expected given the duration of the training. In the XR group, two participants obtained the correct PLAX view but measured an implausible EPSS value. When these outliers were included in the analysis the CI of the mean difference widened, and non-inferiority could no longer be statistically confirmed. However, we chose to exclude these measurements, as these errors did not reflect inadequate image acquisition skills, but rather incorrect anatomical interpretation of the EPSS measurement, resulting in values that could not be meaningfully compared with expert reference standards. In the standard training group, two participants were unable to obtain the A4C view and therefore could not provide any TAPSE measurement, representing a true image-acquisition limitation. To allow a fair comparison between groups, two participants per group were therefore excluded from the non-inferiority analyses. The fact that all XR participants were able to obtain the A4C view, while two participants in the standard group were not, may suggest that XR training provides an early advantage in spatial orientation and probe-handling. Not surprisingly, the EPSS values measured by novice learners were on average slightly higher than expected in healthy volunteers (mean novice EPSS 5.2 mm), which likely reflects minor differences in image plane acquisition and calliper placement by novices.

Our findings are consistent with earlier studies on immersive training modalities. Hu et al. demonstrated that participants who received integrated VR-enhanced anatomical training as part of their ultrasound course outperformed those who received the same ultrasound training with a digital atlas-based anatomy review, which may relate to improved psychomotor training and anatomical understanding [22]. Similarly, Khoo et al. found improved one-month retention in a VR-trained group versus simulator-instructor-led trained controls [8]. Other prior studies have focused on procedural training or remote mentoring using XR, whereas our study uniquely evaluates XR in independent image acquisition and quantitative measurements, reinforcing earlier evidence of XR’s positive impact on ultrasound skill development [7, 23, 24].

Interpretation and theoretical framework

Several mechanisms may explain why XR-based training was found to be non-inferior to standard ultrasound training, despite the assessment being performed on real patients rather than in the XR environment. This misalignment may have disadvantaged the XR group, who had less time to train on human subjects and therefore may have lacked exposure to important real-world factors such as acoustic shadowing, rib interference, and patient communication. Nevertheless, their performance was equivalent.

A large part of the effectiveness of the XR application likely lies in its ability to provide learners with interactive 3D visualizations of anatomy. By allowing learners to interact with holograms in a truly 3D space, the application enhances depth perception and offers a realistic representation of anatomical structures. Learners can actively observe how ultrasound beams traverse the body and how probe positioning affects the resulting image, which helps them understand how to “slice” through anatomy in the correct planes. This practical insight supports spatial orientation and improves understanding of complex anatomical relationships, skills essential for accurate probe manipulation and image interpretation in POCUS [9, 25].

Prior research confirms that 3D visualization supports ultrasound competence development, particularly in novice learners who often struggle with spatial integration of anatomical knowledge [9]. These benefits may be partly explained through Cognitive Load Theory. 3D visualization likely reduces extraneous cognitive load by visually and interactively organizing spatial information, thus freeing up working memory capacity for germane processing, i.e., the active construction of knowledge and procedural schemas [26]. This free working memory can be used to focus on learning how to perform and interpret ultrasound, rather than mentally reconstructing anatomy from flat images.

Interestingly, no significant relationship was found between either anatomical knowledge or visuospatial ability and performance on the ultrasound test. This is in contrast with earlier studies and our meta-analysis [27]. Several explanations are possible. First, the anatomical knowledge test may not have captured the depth or type of knowledge most relevant for the procedural aspects of POCUS, such as dynamic spatial transformations or real-time image interpretation. Moreover, although face and content validation confirmed the test’s relevance, the very short answer question format with only 10 questions may have limited its sensitivity to practical application. Additionally, it is possible that participants reinforced or refreshed relevant anatomical knowledge during hands-on practice, particularly the aspects most critical for the final assessment. This may have reduced the impact of baseline differences in knowledge on test outcomes.

As for visuospatial ability, particularly mental rotation was found to be a moderate predictor of ultrasound skill acquisition [27]. However, the relationship between aptitude and performance is often context-dependent and may be less pronounced when instructional methods offer strong support. In our XR training, the application provided rich visual cues and spatial guidance, likely reducing the need for learners to rely on their own mental rotation skills. If this were the sole explanation, we would have expected differences to emerge in the standard training group, where such support was lacking—but this was not the case. Another explanation is that the MRT test, which focuses on mental rotation, does not capture the full spectrum of visuospatial abilities relevant to ultrasound. Other components, such as visuospatial perception—the ability to accurately judge spatial relationships between anatomical structures—are also important in POCUS but were not measured in this study [27, 28].

There were some differences in test performance between the groups: participants in the XR group acquired the PLAX view more slowly, whereas the A4C view was completed in a similar amount of time and yielded a slightly higher image quality score. This discrepancy may be explained by the test sequence: the PLAX view with EPSS measurement was performed first, possibly requiring XR participants to adjust to performing ultrasonography on a real person after training in a XR environment in comparison to the standard training group that was already performing ultrasound on a real person just before the test. The higher image quality scores in the XR group were primarily determined by the quality of the A4C view. The one-point difference in this score can be clinically relevant, as it can determine whether or not the right ventricular function can be accurately evaluated.

The reliability of the ultrasound test was low, likely due not only to the small number of items and participants, but also to the inclusion of multiple underlying domains. This reflects a broader issue: ultrasound competence is not a single construct but in all likelihood a combination of spatial reasoning, anatomical understanding, and psychomotor skills. When each test item targets a specific component, reliability naturally decreases. In contrast to OSCEs that allow for compensatory scoring or global judgments, our objective test relied on strict criteria for EPSS and TAPSE measurements, which increases comparability but narrows what is assessed. While this improves standardization, it may limit the ability to capture overall competence. Thus, the low Cronbach’s alpha may reflect the multidimensional nature of ultrasound competence and the brevity of the test, rather than a design flaw. Future research with more items and participants is needed to explore this further and develop robust, multidimensional assessment tools for ultrasound training.

Strengths & limitations

Strengths of this study include its randomized design and blinded outcome. Objective measures such as EPSS and TAPSE offer quantifiable outcomes for ultrasound competence. However, the high participation rate supports the generalisability of our findings to students at this stage of training. In addition, the XR intervention was embedded into a realistic, blended training structure, underscoring its practical feasibility for curriculum integration.

Limitations include the small sample size, although adequately powered for our non-inferiority analysis, with robust non-inferiority margins. Two participants per analysis could not be included due to missing or invalid measurements, but this did not affect the conclusions, as the 95% CIs remained fully within the non-inferiority margins. Consistent with methodological guidance, no post-hoc power calculation was performed, as the achieved precision is reflected in the width of the confidence intervals [29]. Furthermore, cybersickness complaints were reported in approximately one quarter of participants in the XR group. These complaints did not negatively impact performance outcomes; however, they may still represent a practical barrier for some learners. The low reliability of some instruments (e.g., image score, anatomy test) likely reflects the limited number of items and the inclusion of multiple domains, rather than a lack of conceptual validity. Participant numbers were too low to meaningfully explore potential subdomains within these assessments.

Educational implications & future directions

XR-based training offers clear practical advantages over standard ultrasound training, particularly in settings with limited faculty or growing student numbers. As our application allows for autonomous, repeated practice with feedback, it may enhance both student-directed learning and curriculum flexibility. In practical terms, the XR application could be implemented similarly to our study design: as a partial replacement of standard training during a dedicated ultrasound training day—either in small group sessions or as a blended, asynchronous component that students can complete in their own time. Moreover, the platform is technically adaptable to other POCUS domains, such as renal or pulmonary ultrasound, which broadens its potential educational value. While XR can support and partially replace instructor-guided practice in the early stages of training, it cannot fully replace POCUS training. XR offers a unique advantage by allowing learners to visualize 3D cardiac anatomy and probe–image relationships in a way that is not possible when scanning healthy volunteers alone. However, clinical competence also requires experience with real anatomical variation, artefacts, and patient interaction. Before broader implementation, for example, as part of a longitudinal ultrasound curriculum or in postgraduate training programs, further validation is required to determine the optimal training duration, long-term skill retention, and transfer to clinical practice. In parallel, the development of reliable, multifaceted assessment tools remains essential to adequately capture the complexity of ultrasound competence and support progress tracking over time.

Conclusion

XR-based POCUS training is non-inferior to standard training in terms of image acquisition and measurement accuracy. It offers clear advantages in accessibility and autonomy, while reducing the demand for teacher time, equipment, and standardized patients. Our results support the integration of XR into ultrasound education, especially as a complementary tool in blended learning environments.
