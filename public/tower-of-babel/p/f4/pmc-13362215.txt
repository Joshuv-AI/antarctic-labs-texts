Preferences for HIV testing services among men who have sex with men in China: a discrete choice experiment
Authors: Qing He, Jieyi Dong, Li Cheng, Qiang Sun, Jia Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHIV testing coverage among men who have sex with men (MSM) in China remains sub-optimal, hindered by stigma, economic burden and low self-perceived HIV risk. To overcome these barriers and enhance testing uptake, it is critical to investigate testing preferences among this population and evaluate how different HIV testing characteristics affect their choice of testing options.MethodsFrom March 1 to October 30, 2025, a discrete choice experiment was performed to quantify MSM’s preferences across two provinces (Shandong and Guangdong) representing relatively low and high HIV prevalence in northern and southern China, respectively. Respondents were presented with hypothetical testing profiles, varying in service mode, testing accuracy, mortality reduction, testing interval, cost and time to receive test results. Mixed logit models were applied to estimate individual preferences, relative importance, willingness-to-pay (WTP) and predicted testing uptake.ResultsAmong 553 eligible respondents, 308 (55.7%) were from Shandong, with a mean (SD) age of 33.5 (10.7) years. Overall participants were mainly concerned about cost, with testing frequency, notification time and mortality reduction also playing a crucial role in motivating testing uptake, surpassing the importance of testing accuracy and mode. The highest WTP was observed for increasing the testing frequency from annual to semiannual ($28.0). The estimated testing uptake rose to over 90% if testing cost was substantially reduced. Notably, our findings illustrated complex preference dynamics and highlighted the significance of tailoring strategies. Compared with frequent testers, respondents with low-frequency testing experience showed significantly weaker preferences for health benefits and stronger preferences for higher testing accuracy. However, frequent testers ranked accuracy as the least important attribute and exhibited significantly stronger preferences for free services relative to those with limited testing experience.ConclusionsAmong MSM, the availability of semiannual testing programs led by community-based organization with greater health benefits and shorter waiting time for precise results have the potential to increase HIV testing rates, if provided for free. Our findings enrich the current understanding of testing decision-making and inform differentiated strategies. Furthermore, these results help prioritize health system innovations when resource constraints preclude the use of all strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-026-01964-6.

Introduction

Screening of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) has always served as a cornerstone of the global HIV response, not only facilitating rapid treatment initiation to end the acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) but also contributing to curb the HIV epidemic [1]. Timely HIV diagnosis, commonly defined as within 12 months of acquisition, is the first step to accessing HIV prevention and subsequent treatment. Although substantial progress has been achieved through expanded testing within the HIV care continuum, barriers to service access and effective testing uptake, particularly among key populations disproportionately affected by HIV, remain [2]. The lack of optimized HIV testing strategies for further promoting HIV testing uptake among priority populations may result in a proportion of undiagnosed infections, impeding progress toward the 2030 goal of “95-95-95” (95% of people living with HIV know their infection, 95% of those know their status initiate treatment, and 95% of those on treatment achieve viral suppression) [3].

In 2022, the relative risk of acquiring HIV infection among men who have sex with men (MSM) was 23 times higher than that among the wider population worldwide [4]. MSM are also the fastest growing group of people living with HIV in China, with the proportion of reported cases attributed to MSM rising from 2.5% in 2006 to 25.2% in 2024. The national HIV prevalence among MSM is estimated at 8%, with significant provincial variations and rate reaching up to 20% in some high-burden regions [5]. Since 2003, the HIV sentinel surveillance network has increasingly expanded access to HIV testing in China. Existing national policies prioritize the expansion of service coverage without specific guidelines on recommended testing frequencies. This policy gap is potentially problematic because previous studies have indicated that HIV testing uptake is suboptimal among MSM. A study conducted in northern China found that only 71% of MSM had undergone HIV testing, and merely 52% had completed ≥ 2 tests in their lifetimes [6]. Another study in southern China defining frequent testers as those who did so at least twice per year revealed that less than half of MSM were tested frequently [7]. Indeed, the underutilization of HIV testing services among MSM stems from multiple barriers. At the individual level, these include fear of public disclosure of sexual orientation, economic burden and low self-perceived risk of HIV acquisition. Compounding these individual factors are structural barriers, including counselor attitudes, elevated implementation costs and long waiting times for HIV diagnosis [8].

Although MSM now have access to a diversity of HIV testing options, overall insufficient testing uptake and late detection of HIV/AIDS indicated that service delivery may not be well-aligned with user preferences [9]. Barriers and motivators to HIV testing vary across subgroups persistently, and understanding such heterogeneity in preferences is of utmost importance for optimizing HIV testing strategies in the future [10]. Based on specified HIV testing and counseling programs, prior studies eliciting individual preferences have largely been implemented in high-income countries or high HIV prevalence settings such as the UK and South Africa. Findings from these studies were often context-dependent and have limited generalizability to low- and middle-income countries such as China, where local implementation environments and socioeconomic conditions differ significantly [11, 12]. Moreover, the majority of studies have explored factors associated with testing uptake from a single dimension, failing to capture the heterogeneity in preferences among different populations at risk of HIV acquisition [13, 14]. These limitations may lead to subjective biases and restrict the evidence for moving beyond “one-size-fits-all” strategies toward differentiated universal HIV testing and treatment strategies.

Grounded in the economic theory of utility maximization, the discrete choice experiment (DCE) has been used extensively as a quantitative method to investigate user preferences by presenting individuals with hypothetical scenarios [15–17]. From the perspective of consumers, this robust method is increasingly applied to inform policymaking and evaluate key features of health products or service delivery models that are of value to individuals [18]. Given the substantial heterogeneity in HIV testing resources and capacity across China, identifying preferences among high-risk populations is imperative for developing a combination of testing modalities and optimizing resource allocation [19, 20]. This study employed a DCE to quantify how specific service attributes influence HIV testing uptake among Chinese MSM and to examine how these preferences vary according to sociodemographic and behavioral characteristics. The findings are intended to inform the design of client-centered and promising testing strategies in China as well as provide methodological and practical insights for other resource-varied settings aiming to expand HIV testing coverage.

Methods

This study was conducted in two provinces, Guangdong in southern China and Shandong in northern China, representing regions with distinctly different epidemiological profiles of HIV–relatively high and low prevalence, respectively. In 2024, the incidence rates of HIV were 6.17 per 100,000 population in Guangdong and 2.76 per 100,000 population in Shandong [21–23]. These two provinces are the most populous in China, ranking first and second regarding population, each with populations exceeding 100 million. All research procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Public Health at Shandong University (IRB No: 20221103). The entire DCE was performed in accordance with the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) Good Practice Guidelines (eTable 9) [24].

Attribute and level identification

The attribute levels in DCE were experimentally allocated to provide descriptions of hypothetical alternatives. This study determined HIV testing attributes and their levels through a multi-step process, including literature review, expert consultations, focus group meetings, in-depth interviews, pilot studies and research team discussions. First, we extracted associated factors from prior literature on HIV testing preferences. The commonly used attributes in these studies were related to the technology itself and regular service delivery. Second, the attributes of HIV service delivery were further refined from qualitative interviews with providers and users (eTable 1–2). To ensure the realism and variation of HIV testing scenarios, we adjusted attribute levels based on actual data from the study sites, such as current service costs and performance metrics associated with HIV testing. Further details are available in the Supplement.

Once attributes and levels were preliminarily identified, we developed pictorial representations of each level that would be used in the DCE. Subsequently, expert consultations were carried out involving experts with backgrounds in epidemiology, medicine and health economics. They evaluated the appropriateness of level setting, as well as the rationality and relevance of attributes. Following the questionnaire finalization, we conducted pilot testing to verify its acceptability and comprehensibility. Based on pilot feedback and research team discussions, graphics and accompanying text of the survey were modified accordingly. The selected attributes included: (1) Mode of HIV testing service; (2) Accuracy of HIV testing; (3) Mortality reduction associated with timely HIV diagnosis and treatment; (4) Frequency of HIV testing; (5) Waiting time for the test result notification; (6) Testing cost. Ultimately, the DCE design aligned with the guidelines for integrated HIV services, namely the principles proposed by the World Health Organization (WHO): confidentiality, counseling, correct test results, connection/linkage [25]. Notably, “mortality reduction” in this study referred to the respondents’ expected reduction in HIV-related mortality if they chose to test for HIV. (Table 1; eTable 3 in the Supplement).

Experimental design

A D-efficient experimental design matrix was generated using R version 9.4, resulting in a total of 18 choice tasks. To decrease the number of choice tasks answered, all tasks were divided into 2 blocks of 9 tasks each, with individuals randomized into either block during data collection [26]. Each choice task offered two alternatives, labeled “option A” and “option B”. The full DCE survey was provided in the Supplement. The final questionnaire comprised an introduction to the research background and attribute definitions, 9 DCE choice tasks, sociodemographic and behavioral inquiries.

Data collection

To capture a wide range of HIV testing experience and preference, we used convenience sampling to recruit men who represented diverse sociodemographic characteristics, testing histories and sexual self-identity through referrals from local community-based organizations (CBOs). The inclusion criteria were: being HIV-negative, aged 16 years or older, assigned male at birth, having ever had sex with a man, and being both available and willing to participate. Individuals who had no cognitive ability to complete the survey or refused to participate were excluded. Respondents were recruited between March 1 and October 30, 2025. Investigators were recruited from local CBOs and trained on the standard operating procedure, including how to guide respondents to complete the DCE questionnaire. Trained investigators conducted an introduction to the research background and cognitive interviews, using pictorial illustrations to help respondents clearly comprehend each attribute. Subsequently, a practice task familiarized respondents with DCE choice tasks. As the first step of the formal investigation, participants were asked to choose their preferred HIV testing option from two hypothetical options. And in the second step, they were asked to answer whether to undergo the testing program as they chose in real life. Target enrollment for the study was 250 MSM per site (N = 500 total). Sample size estimate was based on the number of attributes, levels and choice tasks. Further details are available in the Supplement.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 18 (StataCorp MP). Sociodemographic data were summarized using descriptive statistics. All attributes other than cost were treated as classification variables coded by dummy variables, whereas the cost parameter was modeled as a continuous variable to allow for willingness-to-pay (WTP) estimation.

The categorical attributes were effects-coded, with reference levels designated for each attribute. We assumed the parameters of levels followed a normal distribution. Parameter estimations were calculated relative to the reference level within each attribute. The mean of a parameter signified the average preference value that respondents attributed to a specific level, and the standard deviation (SD) characterized the preference heterogeneity. An alternative-specific constant was included, reflecting utility generated by the opt-out option relative to the non-opt-out options. Both multinomial logit (MNL) and mixed logit (MXL) models were employed as candidate regression models. The model fit was assessed via the Akaike information criterion and Bayesian information criterion, with lower values indicating better models.

The MNL model included only main effects that provided averages of the preference. The MXL model assumed that the probability of choosing a scenario from a set of alternatives was a function of the levels that characterize the alternatives and a random error term that adjusts for individual-specific variations [27]. Unlike the MNL model that estimated only a set of coefficients capturing the mean preference weights of levels, MXL model yielded both the mean effect and the SD of effect. where Un represented the utility obtained by participants n from selecting a given testing service; Vn denoted the observable (fixed) utility, εn represented the unobserved (random) utility. β0 was the alternative-specific constant, reflecting intrinsic preferences unrelated to specific levels. Coefficients β1 to β12 represented the marginal utility associated with each level. A positive β coefficient indicated that the level combination was preferred relative to the reference level, whereas a negative β coefficient indicated the opposite. The higher the absolute value of β coefficient, the stronger was the preference for that level relative to the reference level.

Meanwhile, we employed a mixed logit model with interaction terms (MXL-I) that included parameter estimates for both levels and interactions. The MXL-I model incorporated two-way interaction terms between all levels and participant’s frequency of HIV testing in past 12 months. Respondents were thus categorized into two groups: low-frequency testers (no more than two tests), and high-frequency testers (at least three tests). A sensitivity analysis was performed to examine how sociodemographic characteristics may have influenced preferences of low- and high-frequency testers. The analysis entailed rerunning the MXL-I analysis among sub-samples stratified by age, employment, education, residency, income level and marital status.

WTP referred to the fee that individuals were willing to spend or want to be compensated for the change at a certain attribute level in a specific program. It was calculated as the ratio of the coefficient of a level to the coefficient of out-of-pocket cost. In addition, relative importance was calculated by dividing the range of parameter estimates for a given attribute, by the sum of parameter estimate ranges for all attributes. Accordingly, we conducted subgroup analyses by study site, age, HIV testing experience, income, education, residency, marital and health status to explore differences in the preferences.

Based on the current service delivery and characteristics, we also constructed a prevailing base scenario and various hypothetical scenarios where the attributes were improved. By comparing hypothetical scenarios against the baseline, we could determine the change in the predicted uptake from a policy perspective. The overall scenario analysis demonstrated the effectiveness of strategy optimization in enhancing HIV testing. Further details are available in the Supplement.

Results

Respondent characteristics

After excluding 7 respondents who did not complete the questionnaire, 553 eligible participants were included in the analysis. Among them, 308 respondents were from Shandong and 245 were from Guangdong. The vast majority were relatively young and not living with chronic diseases (90.4%), with mean age of 33.5 years. 34.4% of MSM reported < 3 HIV tests in past 12 months. Low-frequency testers were more likely to be recruited from Guangdong (68.9% vs. 31.4%), to have income less than $705 per month (43.2% vs. 24.2%), and to lack permanent residency status (52.6% vs. 34.2%), compared to high-frequency testers (Table 2).

Model estimates

The signs of coefficients from the MNL, MXL and MXL-I analyses were generally in the expected direction, giving reassurance as to the validity of statistical models (eTables 4, 5, 6 and 7). Hereafter, we report findings from the MXL model, as it was considered a better fit to the data according to the model fitting results, in addition to being the more theoretically appropriate model to use. The main-effects MXL analysis revealed that participants consistently preferred a semiannual program that was free of charge, with shorter waiting time for precise HIV testing results, achieved 90% mortality reduction and were provided by CBO (Table 3). Each attribute contained at least one level with statistically significant SD estimates of the coefficient, thus implying substantial preference heterogeneity across the sample for all DCE attributes.

Interaction

The MXL-I analysis in eTable 6 showed that, compared with high-frequency testers, those with low-frequency testing experience expressed significantly stronger preferences for moderate diagnostic accuracy (β = 0.60, p < 0.01). They also reported greater likelihood of future testing uptake but weaker preference for 90% mortality reduction (Opt-out: β=-1.84, p < 0.001; Lower 90%: β=-0.56, p < 0.01). Compared with low-frequency testers, those with high-frequency testing experience exhibited significantly stronger preferences for free HIV testing (β = 1.15, p < 0.001). Sensitivity analysis further confirmed that compared to low-frequency testers, stronger preference for free testing among high-frequency testers was consistent within each stratum of employment, education, residency and marital status (eTable 7).

Relative importance

After rescaling, the cost of HIV testing emerged as the most critical attribute. Comparatively, the remaining 5 attributes were merely half or one-third as important as cost. Attributes were categorized into 3 tiers in descending order of relative importance: the first tier included out-of-pocket cost; the second tier encompassed testing frequency, notification time and mortality reduction; the third tier included testing accuracy and mode. Among high-frequency testers, the relative importance of attributes was similar to that in the overall sample: the first tier included cost; the second tier encompassed testing frequency, mortality reduction and notification time; the third tier included testing mode and accuracy. Low-frequency testers also ranked testing frequency as the second most important attribute. Among this subgroup, attributes were ordered as follows: the first tier included cost; the second tier encompassed testing frequency, accuracy and notification time; the third tier included mortality reduction and testing mode (Fig. 2).

Subgroup analysis

Although all subgroups assigned the highest preference weight to cost, distinct preference patterns were observed for other attributes (eFigure 1–8). Participants from Shandong and those with high income prioritized the result notification time and rated service mode as the least important attribute. Respondents from Guangdong regarded testing accuracy as more significant than the other attributes. A similar pattern emerged when comparing unemployed and employed individuals: the former group prioritized testing accuracy, whereas the latter group ranked it lowest and placed greater emphasis on testing frequency. Likewise, the relative importance of attributes was generally similar between individuals of different education or marital status.

Scenario analysis

In comparison with the base scenario, 47.1% of participants were estimated to endorse the new scenario if result notification time reduced from 14 to 7 days, all else being equal (Table 4). The estimated uptake was the lowest among all comparisons. Conversely, it was predicted that reducing testing cost to $7.05 or offering free testing services could attract over 90% of MSM to undergo HIV testing. Meanwhile, the uptake would increase to 96.5% if the testing frequency was changed from annual to semiannual. Similarly, additional scenario analyses indicated that 57.6% of high-frequency testers were estimated to endorse the new scenario if the notification time reduced from 14 to 7 days, the estimated uptake was the lowest among all comparisons. Among low-frequency testers, 39.4% of respondents were estimated to endorse the new scenario if mortality reduction increased from 10% to 50%. Further details are available in the eFigure 9.

Willingness to pay

According to model estimates of choices with cost attribute coded as a continuous variable (Table 3), we derived WTP values representing the monetary values of improvement in attributes (eTable 8). Participants were willing to pay $8.8 (95% CI, 3.6 to 14.0) for improved HIV diagnostic accuracy. And they were willing to pay $16.4 (95% CI, 11.0 to 21.9) if the service delivery was changed from hospital-based to CBO-led. Furthermore, shortening notification time of HIV result from two weeks to the same day yielded a WTP of $24.3 (95% CI, 19.4 to 29.2). Increasing frequency from annual to semiannual corresponded to the highest WTP of $28.0 (95% CI, 23.3 to 32.8). The comparison of WTP indicated that respondents valued improvement in testing frequency more than that in other attributes.

Discussion

To assess MSM’s preferences for key attributes of HIV testing, we conducted a DCE based on the WHO building blocks for differentiated service delivery. Out-of-pocket cost exhibited the strongest preference weight, while testing frequency, notification time and health benefits were also core drivers of future HIV testing uptake, with greater relative importance than testing accuracy and mode. Using this experimental design, we quantitatively identified distinct preference patterns across sub-samples stratified by demographic and behavioral characteristics. These patterns reflected socioeconomic disparities in trust in health providers, HIV risk perception and vulnerability to HIV, thereby highlighting the complex dynamics of testing preferences and the critical need for tailored strategies [28].

Most MSM in our study were reluctant to undergo HIV testing at hospitals or health departments, likely due to concerns about confidentiality, anonymity and fear of disclosure of sexuality [29–31]. In this context, prior studies have emphasized that community-based peer-led testing and home self-testing programs support timely diagnosis among individuals with unknown HIV status, particularly in resource-constrained settings [32–34]. Concurrently, considerations should be given to expanding such initiatives to low-frequency testers, who often lack familiarity with HIV testing procedures [35]. To alleviate individual psychological burden associated with repeated testing, innovative and novel approaches should be adopted to streamline service procedures [36]. Similar to previous research, our findings indicated that HIV self-testing (HIVST) programs were less preferred than CBO-led peer-testing, suggesting limited appeal of self-testing among MSM [11, 37]. To improve the sustainability of current services, formal national HIVST guidelines, approved quality-reliable testing kits, and strengthened support for local CBOs may be crucial for HIVST uptake [29].

A distinct feature of this DCE design was its focus on regular service packages rather than purely hypothetical alternatives, which may further amplify the avoidance intention among most participants for repeated testing. Compared with respondents with limited testing experience, frequent testers were significantly more likely to select the opt-out choice, which had not been identified in prior studies. This result may be attributed to concerns about the accumulated time and financial costs of repeated testing, as well as diminishing marginal perceived benefits for most frequent testers [8, 9]. Recurrent negative test results may also reduce subjective HIV risk perception among key populations, thereby lowering individual motivation for regular testing [10]. Beyond testing frequency, existing research has prioritized shortened result turnaround time and structural interventions to promote linkage to HIV care, thereby improving the benefits of early antiretroviral therapy (ART) [38–40]. In our study, respondents paid attention to health benefits and efficient service delivery for HIV testing. Compared with frequent testers, low-frequency testers expressed significantly stronger preferences for higher testing accuracy but weaker preferences for health benefits, likely reflecting their greater concern and unfamiliarity with testing procedure.

Although HIV testing guidelines for high-risk MSM are well-established, implementation conditions and financial affordability remain major barriers [41, 42]. Our study found MSM’s preference for semiannual and free testing program, supporting prior evidence that testing cost and mode strongly influence individual preferences [37]. From the providers’ perspective, implementation costs must also be considered to support frequent outreach and client recruitment, including expenses regarding personnel, updated diagnostic technologies and point-of-care settings. Studies in Africa and the United States have similarly highlighted the financial sustainability of HIV testing programs [33, 43]. Notably, differentiated service delivery requires not only optimized testing strategy but also complementary pre-exposure prophylaxis to design comprehensive packages [44]. This is also critical for effectively prioritizing testing resources and maximizing public health benefits. As testing coverage expands and the positive rate declines, the actual per-case cost of newly diagnosed infections has increased across many regions in China. This suggests that it is necessary to optimize resource allocation by tailoring “one-size-fits-all” strategies to provide differentiated services. More importantly, there is a call for a policy shift from simply curbing the spread of infectious diseases to achieving the public health goal of pursuing maximum benefits, thus contributing to the End HIV strategy.

The study has several limitations. First, DCE results might not be generalizable to all contexts. Existing literature suggested that response in choice experiments predicts behavior, but this association is far from perfect. More evidence on the experience of HIV testing may be needed, while choice experiments can narrow the field of candidate interventions. Second, all hypothetical scenarios only considered a limited number of attributes. In our study, those selected attributes were the one most concerned by target population according to prior literature review and interviews. Too many attributes would increase respondent workload, potentially affecting their decisions and reducing the reliability of results. Third, our study simplified assessments of concerns about diagnostic accuracy and expected reduction in HIV-related mortality. It remains unclear how specific issues along these two attributes may shape individual preferences. To help respondents fully comprehend this DCE, well-trained investigators explained the attributes and conducted cognitive interviews in detail, using pictorial illustrations with a practice task before the formal survey. What’s more, given the nature of DCE design, there may not be a flawless analytic approach to obtain a comprehensive result. However, we have conducted sensitivity analyses applying different models with consistent results. Lastly, study sample recruited from these two large provinces may not be representative of the entire Chinese MSM. Nevertheless, we have covered respondents across a range of sociodemographic characteristics and HIV testing history through purposive sampling. Notably, target population who had never previously tested were also recruited.

Conclusion

Chinese MSM preferred free, 6-monthly HIV testing programs led by CBOs, with greater health benefits and same-day provision of precise test results. Reducing testing cost and supplementing conventional facility-based testing with CDC-led peer-testing are promising approaches to increase HIV testing uptake among MSM. Findings from this study have some implications for service providers and policymakers to develop person-centered, differentiated HIV testing strategies. Moving forward, it is profound to integrate preference evidence with implementation science to optimize HIV testing strategies, particularly in the era of rapid ART initiation.
