Neuroendocrine Carcinoma of the Head and Neck: A Two‐Decade Institutional Experience
Authors: Micah K. Harris, Katie M. Carlson, Nathan Lu, Anthony Tang, Matthew E. Spector, Joshua D. Smith, Jessica H. Maxwell, Kevin J. Contrera, Seungwon Kim, Steven B. Chinn, Angela Mazul, Shaum S. Sridharan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesPrimary neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) of the head and neck is rare (< 1% of cases) and aggressive, with poorly defined management strategies. This study evaluated clinical characteristics, treatment patterns, and survival outcomes in a single‐institution cohort, identifying factors associated with improved survival.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted of patients with head and neck NEC treated at a tertiary center. Variables associated with 3‐year overall survival (OS), disease‐specific survival (DSS), and recurrence‐free survival (RFS) were assessed using Cox regression and Kaplan–Meier curve analyses.ResultsSixty‐nine patients (59.4% male, 95.7% White) were included. The most common subsites were sinonasal (36.2%) and laryngeal (30.4%). Most tumors were poorly differentiated (73.9%), and 50.7% were T category 3–4. Surgical resection was performed in 68.1% and primary radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy in 20.3% of patients. Overall 3‐year OS and RFS were 55.5% and 56.1%, respectively. On multivariable analysis, surgery remained associated with improved 3‐year OS (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR]: 0.21, 95% CI: 0.06–0.66, p = 0.007) and DSS (aHR: 0.33, 95% CI: 0.11–0.99, p = 0.050) when controlling for tumor stage, histologic differentiation, tumor site, and age at diagnosis. Chemotherapy and radiotherapy were not associated with survival.ConclusionsHead and neck NEC carries a poor prognosis. Undergoing surgical resection was strongly associated with improved 3‐year OS and DSS in our cohort, highlighting its importance when feasible. Multicenter collaboration and prospective studies are warranted to refine optimal treatment strategies for this rare and challenging malignancy.

Introduction

Primary neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) of the head and neck is exceedingly rare, estimated to comprise < 1% of all head and neck cancers. There are scattered reports throughout the literature consisting primarily of smaller cohorts and case series, with non‐sinonasal tumors being particularly scarce [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. NECs are differentiated from more common malignancies such as squamous cell carcinoma by immunohistochemical stains, such as chromogranin and synaptophysin [1]. NECs of the head and neck are typically aggressive and carry a poor prognosis, with most reports identifying < 30% overall survival at 5 years [2]. Due to their overall rarity, there is a lack of consensus regarding appropriate treatment approaches.

In terms of classification, the World Health Organization (WHO) updated its guidelines in 2022, grading well‐differentiated neuroendocrine tumors as G1 to G3, with the term neuroendocrine carcinoma being reserved for poorly differentiated tumors, which are further differentiated into small cell and large cell subtypes [8]. These categories are primarily distinguished based on histopathologic analyses such as differentiation, mitotic rate, Ki‐67 index, p53, synaptophysin, and chromogranin, amongst others [8].

Treatment paradigms for head and neck NEC remain non‐standardized due to their rarity [1]. Approaches to chemotherapy and radiotherapy often mirror that of more common NECs, particularly those of the lung [9]. Meanwhile, surgical candidacy and technique is often approached similarly to that of more common head and neck cancers, such as squamous cell carcinoma [10]. This involves a combination of surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy in various configurations. Nevertheless, long‐term outcomes remain dismal, indicating the need for an improved understanding of disease management.

In this study, we investigated clinical and sociodemographic characteristics and survival outcomes for NECs of the head and neck treated at a single tertiary institution.

Methods

Study Design and Data Collection

Patients with NEC of the head and neck managed at the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center (UPMC) were identified. Patients with NEC metastatic to the head and neck were excluded. Approval was obtained through the UPMC institutional review board. Given that the majority of our patients were diagnosed prior to the 2022 WHO classification update, all malignancies were referred to as NECs, rather than differentiating between neuroendocrine tumors vs. carcinomas.

Data were retrospectively collected by electronic medical record review from patients between 2001 and 2022. Data collected included age at diagnosis, gender, race (Caucasian vs. other), date of latest follow up, disease status at last follow up, as well as history of smoking and alcohol. Disease information included tumor site, histology, tumor grade, histologic subtype, T category, N category, M category, perineural invasion, surgical margins, and extranodal extension. Treatment variables included surgical procedure, chemotherapy, radiation therapy (RT), and chemoradiotherapy (CRT).

Pairwise deletion was used for missing values, in which cases were excluded from specific analyses where the specific data were missing, rather than being removed from the entire dataset.

Analysis

Descriptive statistics included median ± interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and frequencies, and proportions for categorical variables. Independent samples t‐test was performed for numerical variables. A p‐value of ≤ 0.05 was considered significant. Probabilities for 3‐year overall survival (OS), disease‐specific survival (DSS), and recurrence‐free survival (RFS) were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Given that NEC survival outcomes are typically poor, we focused on 3‐year survival outcomes. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for 3‐year OS, DFS, and RFS. Kaplan–Meier curves were presented with log‐rank p values. The Cox proportional hazards assumption was tested for all models, and time‐varying models were used when the assumption was violated.

We performed multivariate logistic regression for 3‐year OS, 3‐year DSS, and 3‐year RFS, adjusting for site (sinonasal vs. non‐sinonasal), T category (T1‐T2 vs. T3‐T4), N category (N1‐N2 vs. N3‐N4), M category (M0 vs. M1), chemotherapy, primary radiotherapy, surgery, histology, gender, and race (Caucasian vs other). Amongst the 47 patients who underwent surgery, we examined margin status, perineural invasion, and extranodal extension. Low T category was defined as T1‐T2 tumors. Low N category was defined as N0‐N1 tumors. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 28 (Chicago, Illinois USA) and R statistical software (version 4.5.1). Figures were created using GraphPad Prism version 10 (Boston, Massachusetts, USA) and R statistical software (version 4.5.1).

Results

Demographical and Clinical Variables

A total of 69 patients were identified, with a median age at diagnosis of 67 years (IQR: 56–75) (Table 1). The cohort comprised 41 males (59.4%), and was predominantly Caucasian (n = 66, 95.7%), with a small proportion of Black/African American patients (n = 3, 4.3%). The average duration of follow‐up from initial presentation was 53.6 ± 65.9 months. Tumor subsites were most commonly sinonasal (n = 25, 36.2%) or laryngeal (n = 21, 30.4%), with smaller numbers involving the parotid (n = 9, 13.0%), oral cavity (n = 3, 4.3%), cutaneous (n = 3, 4.3%), thyroid (n = 2, 2.9%), oropharynx (n = 1, 1.4%), or unknown primary (n = 5, 7.2%). Histologically, tumors predominantly demonstrated poor differentiation (n = 51, 73.9%), followed by moderate (n = 8, 11.7%), and well (n = 3, 4.3%), while 7 were unknown (10.1%). Regarding tumor staging, 12 tumors (17.4%) were T1, 13 (18.8%) were T2, 16 (23.2%) were T3, 19 (27.5%) were T4, and 9 (13%) were unknown. Twenty‐two patients (31.9%) were N0, 13 (18.8%) were N1, 23 (33.3%) were N2, 5 (7.2%) were N3, and 6 (8.7%) were unknown. Eight patents (11.6%) had metastatic disease at presentation, while the majority were M0 (n = 58, 84.1%), and 3 (4.3%) were unknown. Finally, 41 patients (59.4%) reported a history of smoking, and 33 (47.8%) reported alcohol use.

Treatment

Surgery was performed in 47 patients (68.1%). Forty‐one patients (59.4%) underwent adjuvant chemotherapy, 27 (39.1%) had adjuvant radiotherapy, and 22 (31.9%) had adjuvant chemoradiotherapy. Primary radiotherapy was done in 14 patients (20.3%), with all but one of these patients receiving concurrent chemotherapy. Four patients (5.8%) underwent chemotherapy alone. Four patients (5.8%) received no treatment.

Survival Outcomes

For the overall cohort, 3‐year OS was 55.5% (95% CI: 44.4%–69.3%), 3‐year DSS was 58.7% (95% CI: 47.6%–72.3%), and 3‐year RFS was 56.1% (95% CI: 43.7%–72.0%). For the 47 patients who underwent surgical resection, 3‐year OS was 63.7% (95% CI: 50.9%–79.6%), 3‐year DSS was 68.0% (95% CI: 55.5%–83.4%), and 3‐year RFS was 56.8% (95% CI: 42.8%–75.5%). For the 14 patients who received primary radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy, 3‐year OS was 43.3% (95% CI: 22.6%–82.7%), 3‐year DSS was 43.3% (95% CI: 22.6%–82.7%), and 3‐year RFS was 43.9% (95% CI: 21.7%–88.7%).

3‐Year OS

On univariate Cox proportional hazards regression (Table 2), advanced T category (HR: 2.8, 95% CI: 1.12–6.98, p = 0.027), advanced N category (HR: 3.53, 95% CI: 1.52–8.21, p = 0.003), poor histology differentiation (HR: 7.57, 95% CI: 1.02–55.93, p = 0.047), and surgery (HR: 0.40, 95% CI: 0.19–0.85, p = 0.017) were significantly associated with 3‐year OS. On a multivariable model, variables that remained significantly associated with 3‐year OS were surgery (HR: 0.21, 95% CI: 0.06–0.66, p = 0.007), advanced N category (HR: 2.62, 95% CI 0.94–7.33, p = 0.070), poor histology differentiation (HR: 11.19, 95% CI: 1.35–93.07, p = 0.030), and increasing age at diagnosis (HR: 1.06, 95% CI: 1.02–1.10, p = 0.007), while also controlling for sinonasal tumor site (HR: 1.29, 95% CI: 0.46–3.66, p = 0.630) and advanced T category (HR: 1.99, 95% CI 0.70–5.68, p = 0.200).

3‐Year DSS

The same variables were examined regarding 3‐year DSS (Table 3). On univariate Cox proportional hazards regression, surgery (HR: 0.36, 95% CI: 0.16–0.78, p = 0.010), advanced T category (HR: 2.63, 95% CI: 1.05–6.60, p = 0.039), and advanced N category (HR: 3.69, 95% CI: 1.51–9.00, p = 0.004) were significantly associated with 3‐year DSS. In a multivariable model, undergoing surgery (HR: 0.33, 95% CI: 0.11–0.99, p = 0.050) and poor histologic differentiation (HR: 8.4, 95% CI: 1.06–66.35, p = 0.040) were independently associated with 3‐year DSS while also controlling for sinonasal subsite (HR: 1.09, 95% CI: 0.39–3.06, p = 0.870), advanced T category (HR: 1.53, 95% CI: 0.55–4.29, p = 0.420), advanced N category (HR: 2.82, 95% CI: 0.98–8.10, p = 0.055), and age at diagnosis (HR: 1.02, 95% CI: 0.99–1.05, p = 0.220).

3‐Year RFS

Regarding the risk of recurrence at 3 years (Table 4), variables significantly associated on univariate analysis only included advanced N category (HR 2.85, 95% CI 1.21–6.69, p = 0.02). Other variables, including subsite, margin status, M stage, histology, and primary surgery, were not significantly associated. A multivariate model was then constructed including T category, primary surgery, histology, tumor site, and age at diagnosis. On this model, only advanced N category (HR 6.33, 95% CI 1.89–21.18, p = 0.003) remained significantly associated with survival.

Surgical Resection

Of the 69 patients, 47 (68.1%) underwent surgery (Supporting Information S1: Table 1). On Kaplan–Meier curve analysis (Figure 1), patients who underwent surgery (vs. those who did not) had improved 3‐year OS (HR 0.4, 95% CI 0.19–0.85, p = 0.014) and 3‐year DSS (HR 0.36, 95% CI 0.16–0.78, p = 0.007), though 3‐year RFS was not significantly different (p = 0.780). When compared to those who did not undergo surgery, the surgical subset contained more patients with sinonasal tumors (46.8% vs. 13.6%, p = 0.01), fewer patients with laryngeal tumors (19.1% vs. 54.5%, p = 0.003), and fewer patients with advanced (N2/N3) nodal disease (31.9% vs. 59.1%, p = 0.03). There was otherwise no difference in T category, histology, metastasis, age, gender, alcohol history, or smoking history between the groups. On bivariate regression, while T category was inversely associated with undergoing surgical resection, this did not reach statistical significance (OR 0.73, 95% CI 0.42–1.26, p = 0.270).

Kaplan–Meier curves stratified by primary surgery for (A) 3‐year overall survival and (B) 3‐year recurrence‐free survival, and (C) 3‐year disease‐specific survival.

Among the subset of 47 surgical patients, 37 (78.7%) achieved negative surgical margins. Perineural invasion was observed in 11 cases (23.4%) and extranodal extension was present in 16 cases (34.0%). On univariate Cox proportional hazards regression, positive surgical margins (HR: 1.66, 95% CI: 0.53–5.24, p = 0.39), perineural invasion (HR: 0.71, 95% CI: 0.20–2.50, p = 0.60), and extranodal extension (HR: 2.32, 95% CI: 0.87–6.20, p = 0.10) were not significantly associated with 3‐year OS. Similarly, positive surgical margins (HR: 1.22, 95% CI: 0.34–4.39, p = 0.76), perineural invasion (HR: 0.51, 95% CI: 0.12–2.30, p = 0.39), and extranodal extension (HR: 2.25, 95% CI: 0.79–6.45, p = 0.13) were not significantly associated with 3‐year DSS.

Sinonasal Versus Non‐Sinonasal Origin

Of the 69 patients, 25 (36.2%) had tumors of sinonasal origin (Supporting Information S1: Table 2). When comparing those with sinonasal vs. those with non‐sinonasal tumors, those with sinonasal tumors were younger (58 ± 12.1 vs. 69 ± 10.5 years, p = 0.04), had lower rates of advanced (N2/N3) nodal disease (12.0% vs. 38.6%, p = 0.02), lower rates of tobacco use (36.0% vs. 75.0%, p < 0.001), and lower rates of alcohol use (36.0% vs. 77.3%, p < 0.001). Those with sinonasal tumors were more likely to undergo surgery (96.0% vs. 59.1%, p = 0.001). On Kaplan–Meier curve analysis (Figure 2), patients with sinonasal tumors (vs. all other subsites) did not have any difference in 3‐year OS (HR: 0.86, 95% CI: 0.40–1.86, p = 0.70), 3‐year RFS (HR: 0.75, 95% CI: 0.31–1.84, p = 0.53), or 3‐year DSS (HR: 0.82, 95% CI: 0.36–1.84, p = 0.63).

Discussion

Primary NECs of the head and neck are both rare and aggressive, leading to poorly defined treatment algorithms and poor survival outcomes. In this single‐institution cohort study of 69 patients with NEC of the head and neck, we found that while patients tended to present with advanced disease, undergoing surgery remained independently associated with significantly improved 3‐year OS and DSS.

Due to its rarity, there are few reports in the literature on primary NECs of the head and neck, particularly those of non‐sinonasal origin [1, 2, 3]. In our cohort, we report on 69 patients, the majority (63.8%) being of non‐sinonasal origin. Consistent with the known aggressive biology of these tumors [11, 12], most of our patients presented with advanced disease and with poor differentiation on histology. Overall survival was poor, with only 55.5% of patients remaining alive at 3 years after diagnosis. These data mirror that of prior reports. In a SEER analysis of over 1300 head and neck NEC cases, median survival was found to be 25 months overall [13]. Similarly, a series of 23 nonsinonasal NECs demonstrated 2‐year OS of 53% and 5‐year OS of 33% [14].

While larger national cohorts have been reported, our study holds the advantage of allowing granular evaluation of variables associated with improved survival outcomes, which is particularly important given the lack of a standardized treatment approach to head and neck NEC. A key finding was that undergoing surgical resection of the primary tumor was independently associated with improved survival. In practice, surgery may be reserved for those with resectable and thus more favorable disease. However, we found no statistical difference in age, gender, alcohol or smoking history, T category, histology, or metastasis in those who underwent surgery versus those who did not (Supporting Information S1: Table 1). Patients who underwent surgical resection were less likely to have advanced (N2/N3) nodal disease and more likely to have involvement of the sinonasal subsite. To control for these potential confounders, a multivariate logistic regression model was designed that accounted for T category, N category, histology, age at diagnosis, and tumor location. Despite this, surgical resection remained strongly associated with improved 3‐year OS (aHR 0.21) and DSS (aHR 0.33). These data show that surgery remains independently associated with improved survival while accounting for other surrogates of disease status, demonstrating the central role it plays in achieving disease control when feasible.

Prior reports agree that incorporation of surgery into multimodality therapy improves disease control and long‐term survival. In a meta‐analysis of laryngeal atypical carcinoid tumors, patients who underwent surgery had improved DSS (60.2%) compared to primary radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy (53.2%, p = 0.05) [7]. In a cohort of very locally advanced sinonasal carcinomas treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy, patients who subsequently underwent surgery had improved progression‐free survival (36.3 vs. 3.7 months) and improved OS (51.5 vs. 10.6 months) compared with non‐surgical patients [15]. Notably, however, surgery was not associated with 3‐year RFS in our cohort.

Interestingly, traditional pathological risk factors such as positive surgical margins, perineural invasion, and extranodal extension were not significantly associated with 3‐year OS or DSS in our surgical subset. This may reflect limited statistical power due to small sample size, but it also may suggest that the inherently aggressive tumor biology of NECs may outweigh the prognostic impact of individual histopathologic features. The impact of histopathologic markers in NEC is an active area of investigation, and may offer an important complement to these traditional pathologic risk factors, which are inherently subjective [12].

The role of chemotherapy and radiotherapy remains less clear. While these therapies are frequently employed, outcomes vary, with some studies showing benefit in small‐cell or advanced tumors and others showing no independent survival advantage after adjustment for stage and differentiation [6, 11, 16]. In our cohort, neither chemotherapy nor radiotherapy independently predicted survival. Chemotherapy and radiotherapy regimens for head and neck NEC are generally extrapolated from more common sites, primarily NEC of the lungs. As such, this inherently affects the efficacy of these chemotherapy and radiotherapy regimens for NEC of the head and neck [9, 17]. Moreover, chemotherapy and radiotherapy are often used as “second‐line” approaches in advanced, unresectable disease. Of the 47 patients who underwent surgery in our cohort, 40.4% had T1–T2 category disease, compared to 27.3% of the non‐surgical patients, though this did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.15).

Finally, we evaluated sinonasal tumors in comparison to those of non‐sinonasal origin (Supporting Information S1: Table 2). Patients with sinonasal tumors demonstrated lower rates of smoking and alcohol use, possibly due to their association with other risk factors such as environmental and occupational [18]. Sinonasal NECs also had lower rates of advanced nodal disease (12%), consistent with sinonasal tumors typically having lower rates of regional disease, with one NCDB analysis showing a 14% rate of advanced nodal disease [19]. Moreover, sinonasal tumors are prone to aggressive local spread with close proximity to critical structures. In a SEER database analysis, Kuan et al. also demonstrated that sinonasal NECs had improved survival with surgery [20]. Overall, we did not find any difference in survival outcomes between sinonasal and non‐sinonasal cohorts (Figure 2). While more granular evaluations were beyond the scope of this study, future studies should seek to utilize larger cohorts to evaluate optimal treatment modalities between NECs of different anatomic subsites.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, particularly in determining which patients were offered surgery versus non‐surgical management. Second, the relatively small sample size limits statistical power and may explain why certain known prognostic features, such as extranodal extension and positive margins, were not significantly associated with outcomes. Third, treatment regimens were heterogeneous, reflecting institutional and provider preferences rather than standardized protocols, which may confound survival comparisons across treatment groups. Additionally, follow‐up duration varied considerably among patients, potentially underestimating late recurrences or deaths.

Conclusion

Head and neck NEC remains highly aggressive with poor survival outcomes. In our cohort of 69 patients, surgical resection was independently associated with improved 3‐year survival, underscoring its critical role in disease management when feasible. Further multicenter collaboration and prospective studies are warranted to better define optimal treatment approaches for this rare and challenging disease, particularly for tumors of non‐sinonasal origin.
