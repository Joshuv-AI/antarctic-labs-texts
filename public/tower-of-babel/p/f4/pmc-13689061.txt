Enhanced detection of sepsis pathogens in hospitalized febrile neutropenia patients using blood culture broths with TaqMan array card
Authors: Furqan Kabir, Hammad Afzal Kayani, Dur e Shahwar, Rufei Ma, Sara Iqbal, Akram Hussain, Jawaid Hussain, Adil Kalam, Fatima Aziz, Sehrish Munir, Farrukh Qazi, Jie Liu, Eric Houpt, Erum Khan, Junaid Iqbal, Asad Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn resource-limited settings, febrile neutropenia (FN) is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality among immunocompromised individuals particularly those undergoing chemotherapy for malignancies. The clinical diagnosis and management of disease is challenging, due to the diverse range of causative pathogens and overreliance on the traditional diagnostic techniques. TaqMan array card (TAC), a microfluidics-based technique that allows simultaneous and rapid detection of multiple pathogens, addressing these diagnostic needs.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to determine the role of TAC in improving pathogen identification in hospitalized patients with FN.MethodsThis cross-sectional study enrolled 155 FN patients admitted to Aga Khan University and Hospital, Karachi, Pakistan. 9 samples were excluded due to failed post-extraction quality control, yielding 146 samples for final TAC analysis. Both blood cultures and TAC assays were performed on the collected broths. Enrichment strategies were employed to reduce the impact of PCR inhibitors and increase microbial biomass, thereby enhancing the diagnostic sensitivity of TAC.ResultsTAC identified a diverse range of pathogens compared with traditional blood culture. Of 155 study participants, 61 (39.1%) were culture positive. At the Ct ≤ 35, at least one pathogen was identified in 37 TAC positive samples. The most frequently detected bacteria were E. coli (13/146, 8.90%), K. pneumoniae (11/146, 7.53%), A. baumannii (4/146, 2.74%), and P. aeruginosa (4/146, 2.74%). TAC showed diagnostic sensitivity of 23.5% for E. coli and 19.6% for K. pneumoniae at the primary Ct ≤ 35 threshold, with specificity above 98% for both. In multivariable penalized logistic regression, K. pneumoniae, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and A. baumannii were independently associated with higher mortality. Antimicrobial resistance profiling demonstrated carbapenem and cephalosporin resistance across all three blood culture types.ConclusionThe findings of this study suggest that TAC identifies a wider range of pathogen compared with traditional blood culture in FN patients. Only blood culture was able to detect Candida tropicalis in this experiment; TAC was unable to identify any fungal infection, highlighting the panel’s current flaw. Multicenter studies are needed to validate these results and assess the effectiveness of TAC in broader clinical settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-14004-z.

Background

Febrile neutropenia (FN) is a potentially lethal condition commonly affecting immunocompromised patients, particularly those undergoing chemotherapy for malignancies [1]. It is characterized by fever, with an oral temperature ≥ 38.3 °C or a temperature of 38.0 °C sustained for a minimum of one hour, with a significant reduction in absolute neutrophil count (ANC) i.e. <1.5 × 109/L (1500/mm3) [2, 3]. Clinical management is challenging due to impaired innate immunity and chemotherapy-associated mucosal barrier injury, which together predispose patients to infections caused by common pathogen including Gram-negative bacteria (Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae), Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus), fungi, and parasites. The clinical manifestation of these infections is often non-specific and progress rapidly, making early detection and pathogen identification crucial for the selection of a suitable antimicrobial regimen and for improving clinical outcomes.

The incidence and outcomes of FN vary based on malignancy type, chemotherapy regimen, comorbidities, prophylaxis, and access to healthcare facilities. Globally, FN affects approximately 10–50% of patients suffering with solid tumors and up to 50–70% of patients with hematologic malignancies [4]. In resource limited settings, the incidence may be higher because of delayed diagnosis and limited supportive care, with a mortality rate range from 2 to 6% in children and can surge up to 50% if antibiotic therapy is not received during the first 48 h of onset [5, 6]. In Pakistan, there is a dearth of population-level estimates as most of the data are hospital-based with focused on specific subgroups rather than national surveillance. A hospital-based paediatrics prospective cohort from Lahore reported the incidence of 25% of cases monthly in patients undergoing chemotherapy [7] and a tertiary-care hospital of Karachi, Pakistan reported a 5.98% hospital-based incidence of chemotherapy-induced FN [8].

Identification of the causative pathogen in FN is challenging due to nonspecific clinical presentation, heterogeneous etiology, low pathogen biomass, types of specimens, and frequent antecedent antibiotic exposure [7, 9]. Traditional diagnostic methods such as blood culture have long been the gold standard for identifying bacterial infections but their sensitivity is limited by factors such as prior antibiotic use, presences of intracellular, fastidious, and slow-growing bacteria [10]. Molecular methods such as nucleic acid amplification (NAA), particularly multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) can simultaneously detect a wide range of pathogens. However, blood derived PCR inhibitors such as hemoglobin, lactoferrin, and immunoglobulins can reduce amplification efficiency and can lead to false-negative results particularly in low biomass infections which are common in FN. Several factors can affect the diagnostic sensitivity and reproducibility in FN patients such as variation in nucleic acid extraction, recovery from low input specimens, and recovery of PCR inhibitors [11, 12].

To enhance PCR sensitivity and reliability, allowing more precise pathogen detection in FN, enrichment strategies such as blood culture broth incubation are employed to increase microbial biomass and reduce the impact of PCR inhibitors [13]. Building on this, the TaqMan array card (TAC), a multiplex real-time PCR based on microfluidics, provides a valuable tool for concurrently identifying 24–384 pathogenic targets. Its ability to analyze a small sample volume of just 1–2 µL combined with constant amplification settings, reliable cycle threshold (Ct) values, makes it a thorough method for the detection of pathogens in FN [13, 14].

Although TAC has been assessed in acute fever and diarrheal illness, its use in the setting of FN, a population with diagnostic difficulties such as low pathogen biomass, prior antibiotics usage, and a broad etiological spectrum that includes bacteria, fungi, and viruses, has not been fully characterized. This is the first study from LMICs to assess the combination of TAC-based multiplex detection and blood culture broth enrichment using nanotrap concentration in FN patients, constituting a unique workflow for the detection of bacterial, viral, and fungal from blood culture broth. This study aims to assess the effectiveness of TAC in detecting bacterial, viral, fungal, and parasitic targets in patients with FN.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional study design was employed, including analysis of blood culture bottles from hospitalized patients with fever and neutropenia. The inclusion criteria were (i) fever, with oral temperature ≥ 38.5 °C at the time of blood collection for broth (ii) the absolute neutrophil count (ANC) of < 1500/ml. Patients who had undergone surgery and/or were hospitalized for more than 03 days for illness in the preceding 02 weeks (i.e., within 14 days of last hospitalization) were excluded. The study was approved as an “exemption” and the need for consent to participate was waived by the Ethics Research Committee of Aga Khan University, Pakistan (ERC# 2025-10905-33164), as the study was conducted on leftover/discarded samples with proper de-identification.

Study setting

This study was conducted at Aga Khan University Hospital (AKUH), a not-for-profit tertiary care academic medical center in Karachi, Pakistan. Blood culture bottles were collected from the clinical and diagnostic laboratory, AKUH between April to November 2025.

Sample collection and transportation

Blood culture broths were collected, following routine diagnostic procedures and workup from Clinical and Diagnostic Laboratory, AKUH. The liquid medium collected from blood culture bottles (aerobic, anaerobic, or paediatric) after standard incubation is referred to as blood culture broth. Samples were transported to Infectious Diseases Research Laboratory (IDRL-AKUH) under cold chain conditions (2–8 °C) and processed on the same day. The time interval between blood culture positivity and research processing varied and was not standardized since samples were obtained following the completion of a routine clinical diagnostic workup. This pre-analytical variability is acknowledged as a limitation.

Sample enrichment and concentration

The entire sample volume (avg. 42 ml) from the blood culture bottle was aspirated using disposable 10 ml syringe (BD, USA) and transferring it to 50 ml conical tubes. Sample concentration was done, using Nanotrap microbiome particle A (mb-A) in combination with enhancement reagent 3 or ER-3 (Ceres Nanosciences, Manassas, VA, USA). Briefly, 1000 µl of mb-A and was mixed with 750 µl of ER-3 to the sample, which is then incubated for 30 min. at room temperature with periodic mixing after every 5 min to facilitate the enrichment process. The supernatant was carefully removed, using a dyna magnetic (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) and retained the pellet for subsequent washing steps with nanotrap buffer-2 (Ceres Nanosciences, Manassas, VA, USA) followed by removal of the supernatant using magnetic rack. Finally, the pellet was dissolved in 250 µL of DNA/RNA shield (Zymo Research, USA). Stabilized pellets were processed in batches within 3 days of collection [15].

Total nucleic acid (TNA) extraction

Total nucleic acid (TNA) was extracted following modified MagMAX ultra nucleic acid isolation kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) protocol. Briefly, the pellets obtained during enrichment and concentration step were mixed with MagMAX lysis buffer and spiked with extrinsic controls MS2, PhHV (to monitor the recovery of RNA and DNA targets respectively). The resulting suspension was then incubated at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by magnetic separation and ProteinaseK digestion. TNA was captured, using magMAX magnetic binding beads, followed by sequential washing steps with wash buffers and ethanol. Finally, beads were eluted using 200 µL elution buffer. An additional step of PCR inhibitors removal was performed, using OneStep PCR inhibitor removal kit (Zymo Research, USA) as per the manufacturers’ recommendations. TNA was stored at -80 °C in an ultra-low temperature freezer, until it was processed for TAC.

TaqMan array card (TAC) processing for pathogens detection

The TAC was customized for 56 pathogens known to cause sepsis or sepsis like illness, including 23 bacteria, 28 viruses, 03 parasites and 02 fungi (TAC layout is presented in supplementary Table: S1). TAC assay was performed as described previously [16–18]. Briefly the reaction mixture was prepared, using TNA and AgPath-ID One-Step RT-PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), while the QuantStudio 7 Flex platform (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for thermal cycling and data generation. A Ct value of ≤ 35.00 was used to define primary positivity, as previously described [16]. To detect low-biomass pathogens, especially viruses, which may be clinically significant in neutropenic patients despite lower amplification signals, Ct < 40 was further examined as a secondary exploratory threshold.

Each assay on the TAC was considered as valid only if (i) Extraction and PCR blanks remained negative for that assay/gene target. (ii) MS2 and PhHV remained positive within samples for the validity of RNA and DNA based targets respectively. To reduce the possibility of cross-contamination and false-positive results, quality control procedures such extraction blanks, PCR blanks, and dual internal controls were employed in each batch. The S. aureus TAC targets are specific and distinct from coagulase-negative staphylococci, reducing CoNS cross-reactivity. Extraction and PCR blanks were monitored in every run. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as median (M) and interquartile range (Q₁–Q₃) for continuous variables and as counts (n) and percentages (%) for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test (Z) for continuous non-parametric data and Pearson’s chi-square test (χ²) or Fisher’s exact test for categorical data. Univariable logistic regression was used to evaluate associations between pathogen positivity or clinical variables (Age, WBC, neutrophil count, and ANC) and clinical outcome (Deceased = 1, Discharged = 0), with variables with p < 0.10 entered multivariable logistic regression to calculate adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Spearman correlation was applied to assess associations between pathogens and laboratory markers. Associations with leukemia diagnosis were analyzed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, and ORs were calculated for 2 × 2 tables where appropriate. All analyses were performed in R (tidyverse, readr, broom) with two-sided p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. For demographic comparisons, Bonferroni correction was employed for (Table 1; threshold p < 0.003). Regression analyses were not formally corrected for multiplicity; all findings are exploratory.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 155 blood cultures were collected from eligible FN patients, which were then enriched and further processed through TNA extraction, 09 samples were excluded due to failed post-extraction quality control. A total of 146 samples were analyzed using TAC and blood culture were performed for all the 155 enrolled patients (Figure: 1).

Among the 146 patients, the median age was 21.5 (7.0, 48.8) years with majority (88, 60.3%) being male (Table 1; placed at the end of the manuscript). The patients were admitted to 16 different wards, while PAEDS-ONCO (30.8%), HAEM-ONCO (24.0%), and ONCO (19.2%) collectively account for approximately three-quarters of admissions. The clinical diagnosis for these patients included febrile neutropenia (64, 43.8%), leukemia (27, 18.5%), septic shock (12, 8.2%), and dengue (6, 4.1%), as shown in Table 1. 32 (21.9%) patients were deceased. The deceased patients were older than those discharged (P = 0.013), and they were mostly from HAEM-ONCO, ONCO, PAEDS-ICU, and NICU (P < 0.001). Most of the deceased patients were diagnosed with septic shock (10, 31.3%), compared with 1.8% (2/114) among discharged patients (P < 0.001).

Pathogen detection with blood culture

Among the 146 samples, 52 (35.6%) were positive for at least one type of blood culture. For the three types of blood culture, 82, 69, and 63 samples were tested, yielding 28 (34%), 17 (25%), and 22 (35%) positive results for aerobic, anaerobic, and paediatrics culture, respectively. Escherichia coli was the most frequently detected pathogen 13 (8.9%), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae 11 (7.5%). Acinetobacter baumannii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa were each detected in 4 (2.7%) patients. Staphylococcus aureus1 was detected in 1 (0.7%) and Staphylococcus aureus2 was detected in 2 (1.4%) patients, while Streptococcus pneumoniae was detected in 1 (0.7%) patients. Significantly higher culture positivity was observed for deceased patients 23 (71.9%) than that of discharged patients 29 (25.4%), with 68.2% (15/22) versus 21.7% (13/60) for aerobic, 68.8% (11/16) versus 11.3% (6/53) for anaerobic, and 70.0% (7/10) versus 28.3% (15/53) for paediatrics culture (P < 0.05). Candida species were detected by blood culture in 3 patients (all Candida tropicalis); however, these were removed from the final analyses, because TAC wasn’t performed on these samples.

Antimicrobial resistance patterns of culture-confirmed isolates

Culture-confirmed isolates from aerobic, anaerobic, and paediatrics blood types were subjected to antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Figures 2 and 3, and 4 show resistance trends stratified by antibiotic class. There was a significant load of multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria among aerobic blood culture isolates, with 23 (65.7%) isolates resistant to cephalosporins and 23 (46%) isolates resistant to carbapenems (Fig. 2). Similar resistance patterns were seen in anaerobic isolates, with 21 (77.8%) isolates exhibiting cephalosporin resistance and 19 (52.8%) isolates exhibiting carbapenem resistance (Fig. 3). Cephalosporin resistance 13 (54.2%) isolates and fluoroquinolone resistance 10 (43.5%) isolates were the most often noted trends among paediatrics blood culture isolates (Fig. 4). Carbapenem resistance was consistently found in all three culture types, underscoring the clinical difficulty of empirical antibiotics.

Pathogen detection with TAC

At the primary Ct ≤ 35 threshold, pathogens were detected in samples across eight target organisms (Table 2). E. coli (13/146, 8.90%), was the predominant bacterium detected by TAC, followed by K. pneumoniae (11/146, 7.53%), A. baumannii (4/146, 2.74%), and P. aeruginosa (4/146, 2.74%). S. aureus was detected via two independent TAC targets: target 2 (2/146, 1.37%) and target 1 (1/146, 0.68%), which represent the two different gene assays contained in the panel for this organism. Other bacteria detected included S. pneumoniae (1/146, 0.68%). Interestingly, one virus was identified, Parvovirus B19 (1/146, 0.68%). HHV-6 was only identified in the secondary exploratory analysis (Table S2, Ct < 40) and was not detected at Ct ≤ 35.

Diagnostic accuracy of TAC versus blood culture

The diagnostic accuracy of TAC at the primary Ct ≤ 35 threshold is presented in Table 3. For most individual pathogens, overall sensitivity was low (range 2.0–23.5%), while specificity was high (98.9–100.0%), indicating that TAC had a poor positive predictive value in this FN population with substantial prior antibiotic exposure. The AUC values showed poor discriminatory performance for individual pathogen detection, ranging from 0.51 to 0.61. K. pneumoniae (19.6%) and E. coli (23.5%) showed the highest sensitivity. Pathogen association with mortality

Given sparse event counts for several pathogens, Firth’s penalized logistic regression was applied as the primary analysis. Standard logistic regression results are presented in Supplementary Tables (Table S4a, S4b, S5a, S5b) as sensitivity analysis. At the primary Ct ≤ 35 threshold (Table 4a), both E. coli (OR 4.91; 95% CI 1.58–15.28; p = 0.01) and K. pneumoniae (OR 4.88; 95% CI 1.45–16.41; p = 0.01) were significant in univariable analysis. A. baumannii was also significantly associated with mortality (OR 8.97; p = 0.03). In multivariable analysis (Table 4b), E. coli (OR 4.32), K. pneumoniae (OR 4.50), P. aeruginosa (OR 8.72), and A. baumannii (OR 12.57) all reached statistical significance (p ≤ 0.03 for each), similar analyses were carried out, using Ct ≤ 40 cutoff (Supplementary Tables S6a, S6b).

Quantitative Ct value analysis

The mean Ct values by blood culture positive status are presented in Fig. 5. The heatmap demonstrated differences in mean Ct values of detected pathogens between culture-positive and culture-negative patients. Overall, culture-positive patients exhibited lower mean Ct values for most pathogens, suggesting a higher pathogen burden compared with culture-negative patients. Notably, E. coli (33.6 vs. 40.1), K. pneumoniae (32.6 vs. 41.0), and Parvovirus B19 (32.1 vs. 37.7) showed substantially lower mean Ct values in culture-positive patients. HHV-6 and Staphylococcus aureus 1 displayed relatively high Ct values in both groups, indicating lower nucleic acid concentrations despite detection. The observed pattern suggests that pathogens detected in culture-positive patients were generally present at higher loads than those detected in culture-negative patients.

Comparison of mean Ct values between discharged and deceased patients revealed notable pathogen-specific differences (Fig. 6). For most pathogens, deceased patients exhibited lower mean Ct values than discharged patients, including A. baumannii (30.2 vs. 40.8), E. coli (34.2 vs. 38.3), K. pneumoniae (33.7 vs. 38.2), and P. aeruginosa (33.6 vs. 40.4). This pattern suggests a higher pathogen burden among patients with fatal outcomes. In contrast, Staphylococcus aureus 1 and Staphylococcus aureus 2 demonstrated comparable Ct values between groups. HHV-6 and Parvovirus B19 were not detected among deceased patients, whereas they were detected at relatively high Ct values among discharged patients.

Discussion

FN is a life-threatening condition that significantly increases morbidity and mortality in immunocompromised patients. In this cohort, mortality was higher among older patients and those admitted to intensive care units, hematologic oncology, and neonatal intensive care wards, consistent with established risk factors for adverse outcomes in FN [19]. Septic shock, was the leading cause of death, underscoring the need for the precise and early identification of causative agent to ensure targeted antibiotic therapy [20]. Prior studies emphasized significance of targeted antibiotic therapy guided by microbiological identification in reducing the mortality rates in FN patients, specifically those who are at a higher risk of severe infections. Blood culture confirmed Gram-negative predominance, with Klebsiella pneumoniae followed by Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. These findings are consistent with the results of previous studies showing E. coli and K. pneumonia as major contributors of bacterial infections in FN patients, especially those with hematological malignancies or receiving chemotherapy [21]. Despite low frequency, P. aeruginosa and other multi-drug-resistant pathogens have been linked to higher mortality in FN patients highlighting the importance of rapid pathogen identification and tailored antimicrobial therapy [22, 23]. Antimicrobial resistance profiling of culture-confirmed isolates represented carbapenem and cephalosporin resistance in all three blood culture types. This underscores the clinical challenge of empirical antimicrobial selection at this center and the necessity of rapid pathogen identification to direct de-escalation.

TAC applied to enriched blood culture broths, identified a broader range of pathogens compared with traditional blood culture. At primary CT ≤ 35 threshold, TAC concordance with blood culture was consistent with prior studies conducted in febrile illness settings. TAC showed 71% sensitivity compared with blood culture in a study for acute febrile illness (AFI) outbreak investigation and surveillance [24], while Ferdousi et al. showed similar agreement patterns in hospitalized patients with a similar predominance of Gram-negative pathogens [25], TAC identified bacterial and viral co-detections missed entirely by blood culture in multi-pathogen surveillance [26]. At the primary Ct ≤ 35 threshold, the diagnostic accuracy analysis consistently demonstrated high specificity but limited sensitivity, indicating minimal bacterial biomass from prior antibiotic exposure in this cohort. TAC-positive, culture-negative data are referred to as discordant findings because blood culture has acknowledged limitations in FN and is not a reliable reference standard.

The quantitative Ct analysis (Figs. 5 and 6) offers new information on the burden of pathogens. The mean Ct values for E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and Parvovirus B19 were consistently lower in culture-positive individuals, indicating larger pathogen burdens in confirmed bacteremia. Deceased patients had lower mean Ct values for A. baumannii, E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and P. aeruginosa among outcome groups, indicating a correlation between increased pathogen burden and unfavorable outcomes. However, a variety of host and pathogen variables affect mortality in FN; therefore, Ct levels should be seen as part of a more comprehensive clinical evaluation rather than as stand-alone indicators of outcome.

TAC also detected viral pathogens, including Parvovirus B19 and HHV-6, highlighting clinical strength of TAC over traditional culture. In immunocompromised FN patients, Parvovirus B19 can cause pure red cell aplasia and exacerbate cytopenias [27] while HHV-6 reactivation is associated with marrow suppression and encephalitis [28]. These results, however, could indicate primary infection, reactivation from latent reservoirs, or incidental persistence of viral DNA because TAC identifies viral nucleic acid rather than active replication. Clinical correlation, quantitative PCR, or confirmatory serology are necessary to differentiate between these scenarios. Interestingly, both viruses were identified only in patients that had been discharged, with high Ct levels, indicating presence of the virus at very low amounts, and no role of the virus in causing the deaths of these patients. In several TAC-positive samples, polymicrobial detection was observed involving two or three pathogens. True polymicrobial bacteraemia is rare in immunocompetent hosts but can occur in FN patients with central venous catheters, mucosal damage, or extended hospital stays, making co-detection of organisms like K. pneumoniae and E. coli is biologically plausible because [29]. As an alternative, low-level background DNA from non-dominant organisms could co-amplify close to the test detection threshold, resulting in signals that are more indicative of analytical sensitivity than true infection. In this study, culture confirmation and formal clinical adjudication were not available for secondary detections. As a result, polymicrobial TAC results should not be taken as verified co-infections but rather as signals that generate hypotheses. Standardized adjudication, such as culture confirmation, Ct thresholds, and clinical correlation, should be used in future research to differentiate between assay-related co-amplification and real polymicrobial bacteraemia.

P. aeruginosa, E. coli, and K. pneumoniae were found to be associated with high mortality. Multivariable analysis identified a significant correlation between K. pneumoniae and a higher risk of mortality at both Ct thresholds, the wide confidence intervals reflect limited statistical power and considerable uncertainty. This finding align with the study by Ceken et al.’s study, which identified pneumonia and advanced age as major risk factors for mortality in patients with FN [30]. The cross-sectional study design makes it difficult to draw conclusions about causality; infections found may indicate the severity of an illness rather than its cause. No significant association was found between pathogen identification and traditional infection biomarkers such as white blood cell count and absolute neutrophil count. This suggests that these biomarkers are nonspecific and unreliable for diagnosing FN [19]. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, requiring confirmation in larger prospective cohorts.

From a clinical implementation perspective, TAC provides a few several benefits in resource-limited settings, such as the ability to detect 56 targets simultaneously from a single reaction, compatibility with Pakistan’s tertiary care laboratories [31]. However, routine deployment of TAC is limited due to its significantly higher per-sample cost (estimated USD 80–120 per run) compared to standard blood culture (USD 15–25). Importantly, actual turnaround time was not measured in this study due to the post-diagnostic sample collection design and claims regarding diagnostic speed cannot be made based on available data.

The study findings offer valuable insights; however, certain limitations should be considered. It was a cross-sectional study that captures pathogen detection and clinical outcomes at a single time point, which prevents the formation of causal link between detected pathogens and mortality. The study was conducted on a small number of hospitalized patients with FN from a single hospital (single-center study), limiting the generalizability of the results.

We couldn’t determine whether our study has a lesser turnaround time than culture, since we used blood culture bottles followed routine diagnostic workup and this could be the reason that certain pathogens (particularly viruses) might be undetected. Furthermore, prior antibiotic exposure, a major driver of culture false-negativity and reduced pathogen biomass, was not captured due to the ethics-exempted discarded-sample design. A future multicenter study with defined blood culture incubation time points is needed, and outcome tracking are needed to validate these findings and assess the clinical impact of rapid pathogen detection in this population.

For the diagnosis of FN, TAC should be used in conjunction with blood culture rather than as a replacement, enhancing the detection of a wide range of pathogens, particularly those that are challenging to culture or present in low biomass. In the future, a more targeted panel might focus on the pathogens that are most prevalent in FN cases, improving diagnostic processes, speed and accuracy of pathogen identification.

Conclusion

In conclusion, TAC identified a wide range of bacterial and viral pathogens compared with conventional blood culture. TAC did not detect any fungal pathogen; Candida was identified exclusively by blood culture. Compared to traditional techniques, TAC identified a broad spectrum of pathogens, including co-infections and low-abundance organisms, providing useful diagnostic signals in a population with unique challenges such as low pathogen biomass and previous antibiotic exposure. The findings highlight the potential of TAC as a complementary diagnostic tool in resource-limited settings, future multicenter studies with larger sample size are required to validate this workflow and assess its role in early detection.
