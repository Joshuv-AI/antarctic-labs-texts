Introduction

Colocynth (Citrullus colocynthis), also known as bitter apple or desert gourd, is a plant with a long history of use in traditional medicine across the Mediterranean, Asia, and Africa. Known for its potent anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and purgative properties, colocynth has been used to treat conditions such as arthritis, constipation, and skin disorders like eczema and wounds. Preliminary research also suggests potential benefits in managing diabetes and even anticancer properties due to its cytotoxic effects. However, the plant is highly toxic due to the presence of cucurbitacins, which can cause severe gastrointestinal distress, kidney damage, and even death if consumed in large quantities [1–3]. The plant contains cucurbitacins, which can cause severe gastrointestinal irritation, electrolyte disturbances, and, in extreme cases, multiorgan dysfunction [4–6]. Despite its widespread availability in Somalia, awareness of its toxic effects remains limited. Here, we present a case of colocynth poisoning in a patient from Somalia. It is the first reported case of colocynth poisoning from Eastern Africa. Given its widespread use in herbal medicine, this study raises awareness about its potential toxicity and contributes valuable clinical insights for managing similar cases in the future.

Case report

The patient is a 64-year-old Black Somali male of Cushitic ethnicity who presented to the Emergency Medicine Department of Hargeisa Group Hospital in Hargeisa, Somalia, with complaints of bloody vomiting and diarrhea for two days. His symptoms began shortly after ingesting an unspecified amount of Citrullus colocynthis (colocynth) fruit, which he had taken in an attempt to relieve constipation. Approximately 2 hours after ingestion, he developed profuse bloody diarrhea, hematemesis, and altered consciousness. By the time he arrived at the hospital two days later, active vomiting had ceased, and the diarrhea had subsided, but he remained in an altered mental state.

The patient is a farmer with limited formal education. He has a low socioeconomic status, living in a rural area with his extended family. He relies on traditional medicine for healthcare needs. He is known to have depression but is not on any medications. His diet mainly consists of staple grains, dairy, and occasional meat. He typically consumes cooked foods but exhibits questionable food safety habits, such as irregular handwashing before food preparation and sometimes consuming food without proper storage or refrigeration. His family history is significant for hypertension in his older siblings and type 2 diabetes in his father. No known history of gastrointestinal disorders or malignancies. He has no history of substance use, including alcohol or tobacco.

On admission, the patient had a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 10. Random blood sugar was 142 mg/dl. His blood pressure was 60/40 mmHg, his pulse was 120 beats per minute, his respiratory rate was 18 breaths per minute, and his oxygen saturation was 95% on room air. Electrocardiogram findings revealed sinus tachycardia but no other abnormalities. He had pale conjunctivae and a muddy sclera. On chest examination, the patient had good air entry. On cardiovascular system showed no abnormalities. The nervous system examination revealed that the pupils were mid-sized and reactive, both directly and consensually, to light. The musculoskeletal system showed no abnormalities, and the skin examination revealed no edema or rash. Abdominal examination revealed a scaphoid abdomen with no sign of fluid collection.

Initial laboratory investigations revealed significant abnormalities. A complete blood count (CBC) revealed leukocytosis with a white blood cell (WBC) count of 28.6 × 103/µL, an elevated granulocyte count of 27.2 × 103/µL, and a markedly low lymphocyte count of 0.028 × 103/µL. His hemoglobin was 16.8 g/dL, hematocrit was 48.2%, mean corpuscular volume (MCV) was 85 fL, and platelet count was 182 × 103/µL. Renal function tests indicated acute kidney injury, with creatinine elevated to 2.5 mg/dL and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) at 72 mg/dL. Liver function tests were also abnormal, revealing significantly elevated aspartate aminotransferase (SGOT/AST) at 426 U/L, alanine aminotransferase (SGPT/ALT) at 391 U/L, total bilirubin at 1.8 mg/dL, direct bilirubin at 0.6 mg/dL, and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) at 289 U/L. The electrolyte levels were within the normal range, with sodium at 143 mmol/L, potassium at 4.4 mmol/L, chloride at 104 mmol/L, and calcium at 2.4 mmol/L. Abdominal ultrasound revealed no abnormalities.

Serologic tests for viral infections, including hepatitis C virus, retroviral infection, and hepatitis B surface antigen, were negative. Stool examination revealed whitish stool without any signs of infection, ova, or parasites. Coagulation studies revealed a prolonged prothrombin time of 16.7 s, an activated partial thromboplastin time of 32.1 s, and an international normalized ratio of 1.6.

The patient was started on intravenous fluid resuscitation. He initially received normal saline (0.9% Nacl). After the patient received 2000 ml of fluid, his blood pressure rose to 90/60 mmHg. The patient was also given 1 g of intravenous Ceftriaxone twice daily and 500 mg of metronidazole intravenous three times daily empirically. (Table 1).

Over the course of a patient’s hospital stay, the patient’s condition becomes more stable. By the third day after admission, the patient had fully gained consciousness and was fully oriented. His renal and liver function had normalized, and his vital signs remained stable. After 5 days of hospital stay, the patient was discharged in stable condition, with normal laboratory values, including creatinine, liver enzymes, and complete blood count (Table 2). A follow-up visit one month after discharge confirmed his full recovery, with no residual symptoms or complications (Data Supplement S1).

Discussion

Colocynth (Citrullus colocynthis), also known as bitter apple or desert gourd, is a medicinal plant with a long history of use in traditional medicine for its anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and purgative properties [7]. It contains various bioactive compounds, including polyphenols, alkaloids, resins, saponins, and glycosides, which contribute to its medicinal properties [3, 8]. It has been employed to treat conditions such as arthritis, constipation, diabetes, and skin disorders like eczema and wounds [3, 9–11]. Preliminary research suggests potential anticancer effects due to its cytotoxic properties [12]. However, colocynth contains cucurbitacins, which make it highly toxic, and its misuse can lead to severe side effects [4–6, 13]. In modern phytotherapy, the toxic dosage of C. colocynthis ranges from 600 to 1000 mg, leading to adverse effects such as colic, diarrhea, hematochezia (bloody stools), nephrosis (kidney damage), and vomiting. Lethal doses (≥ 2 g) can cause severe complications, including convulsions, paralysis, and potentially death due to circulatory collapse.[14, 15] Case reports have highlighted its toxicity, such as three instances of acute toxic colitis, where patients experienced dysenteric diarrhea and colonoscopic findings of mucosal congestion and hyperemia with exudates, but no ulceration or pseudopolyp formation [6, 14, 16]. other common adverse effects include liver injury and kidney damage, leading to acute renal failure [4, 6, 8]. Hypoglycemia has also been reported in some cases after taking toxic dose of the plant [6].

The diagnosis of colocynth-induced toxicity was strongly supported in this case by the absence of any prior history of bleeding, ruling out conditions such as bleeding disorders or gastrointestinal hemorrhages; a clear temporal relationship between colocynth ingestion and symptom onset within 2 h, consistent with its known toxic effects. The rapid onset of symptoms, which contrasts with more insidious or delayed presentations; negative stool examination like bacterial gastroenteritis or sepsis; and the patient’s rapid recovery within 5 days with supportive care, aligning with the self-limiting nature of colocynth toxicity. There were no evidences of any medication used by the patient and there was no other substance ingestion histoty.

In the present case, Citrullus colocynthis had toxic effects on multiple systems, including the immune system, gastrointestinal tract, cardiovascular system, kidney and liver. There is limited direct evidence specifically linking Citrullus colocynthis to leukocytosis. However, some Hussen et al., suggested that the plant’s toxic effects, particularly it is inflammatory and irritant properties, could contribute to immune system activation, which might indirectly influence white blood cell counts [8]. The presence of cucurbitacins and other irritant compounds in Citrullus colocynthis has been associated with gastrointestinal inflammation and systemic toxicity, which could theoretically trigger an immune response, including leukocytosis.

Gastrointestinal toxicity, characterized by acute colitis with dysentery, was directly correlated with the pharmacological action of the plant’s constituents, as the stool examination were negative for parasites. This finding aligns with Rezvani et al., who reported cases of colitis induced by colocynthis [6]. The Liver damage was evidenced by elevated levels of ALT and AST, and bilirubin, which is consistent with other similar case reports, in which hepatocyte necrosis was observed. [4–6, 8] Additionally, renal injury was noted, which Al-Yahya et al. attributed to the degeneration of epithelial cells in the proximal convoluted tubules and periglomerular lymphocyte accumulation [5]. The patient in this case also exhibited moderate to severe hypotension. This hypotension may have contributed to the renal insult with pre-renal azotemia and could be attributed to severe volume depletion from watery diarrhea or a direct cardiovascular effect of the plant, such as vasodilation or myocardial depression.

Conclusion

This case highlights the multiorgan toxicity associated with colocynth ingestion, including gastrointestinal hemorrhage, hepatotoxicity, acute kidney injury, and hypotension. The rapid onset of symptoms, absence of infectious causes, and prompt recovery with supportive care strongly support the diagnosis of colocynth-induced toxicity. Healthcare providers should be vigilant in recognizing the toxic effects of colocynth.
