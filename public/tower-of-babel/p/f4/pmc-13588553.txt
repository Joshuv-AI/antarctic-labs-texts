Co-occurring Conditions and Education Services Among Transition-Age Adolescents With Autism

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVES:This study describes sociodemographic characteristics, co-occurring mental and behavioral health conditions, services provided in individualized education programs (IEPs), and transition planning goals for adolescents with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) in a population-based sample.METHODS:The study included 4311 adolescents aged 16 years in 2022 from the Autism and Developmental Disabilities Monitoring Network. All lived within defined catchment areas in 9 states and had an ASD diagnostic code or special education exceptionality. Health and education records from when the adolescents were aged 12 to 16 years were reviewed.RESULTS:Adolescents with ASD had high prevalence of language delay (51.6%), attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (51.1%), and anxiety (42.7%). Few adolescents had an IQ or adaptive test in their records (25.5% and 19.1%, respectively). Most adolescents with an IEP had a transition plan in their records (94.8%), and most had the federally-required postsecondary education (95.7%) and employment goals (95.5%), whereas fewer had the optional postsecondary living goal (43.3%). The prevalence of many elements on the IEP and transition plans varied widely by site. The prevalence of some co-occurring mental and behavioral health conditions and use of school services differed by median household income.CONCLUSIONS:Adolescents with ASD have high prevalence of co-occurring mental and behavioral conditions, with limited information on recent IQ and adaptive testing, potentially impacting service needs and receipt. The variability in the prevalence of IEP elements and service provision across different sites highlights the complexities in addressing the diverse transition needs and health outcomes for adolescents with ASD.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD), a developmental disability characterized by social communication impairments and restricted, repetitive behaviors,1 has a prevalence of 3.2% among children aged 8 years.2 Many individuals with ASD1 (“individuals with ASD” and “adolescents with ASD” are used throughout the manuscript following CDC guidelines on person-first language, although it should be noted that some people with ASD prefer identity-first language) experience co-occurring mental and developmental conditions, including attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), anxiety, and mood disorders, which impact functioning and service needs.3-14 Lower socioeconomic status (SES) is linked with increased risk for mental health conditions, such as ADHD and behavioral disorders,15-17 and intellectual disability (ID),18 including among youth with ASD.18,19 Adolescents with ASD from lower-income households experience more symptoms of co-occurring conditions, unmet health care needs, and poorer health than higher-income peers.13,19

The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) is a US federal law requiring public schools to develop individualized education programs (IEPs) outlining a student’s goals, supports, and services.20 IDEA does not set a standardized transition curriculum; instead, transition planning is individualized within the IEP. IDEA requires that formal transition planning must begin by age 16 to prepare students for postsecondary education, employment, and independent living, including goals such as college preparation, vocational training, or independent living skills. Intellectual (cognitive) and adaptive (daily living skills) functioning assessments inform IEP and transition planning and may determine eligibility for adult services.20-24 For example, adolescents with ASD and co-occurring ID, often defined using IQ and adaptive scores, are more likely to receive school-based services and postsecondary transition planning than those without ID.11

Despite IDEA mandates for transition planning, many adolescents and young adults with ASD face a decrease in supports and services as they age out of public education and pediatric health care, although their service needs persist.25,26 Understanding the needs and transition experiences of adolescents with ASD is critical for informing equitable public health planning and transition support systems. This study uses surveillance year (SY) 2022 data from the Autism and Developmental Disabilities Monitoring (ADDM) Network to describe sociodemographic characteristics, co-occurring conditions, IEP services, and transition goals for adolescents with ASD.

Methods

Surveillance Sites and Data Collection

Nine ADDM Network sites collected adolescent data for SY 2022 in defined areas of their state: Arkansas (AR), Georgia (GA), Maryland (MD), Minnesota (MN), Missouri (MO), New Jersey (NJ), Texas-Laredo (TX-L), Utah (UT), and Wisconsin (WI). Site personnel linked records from health and education sources to identify children with ASD.2 Sites met applicable Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 Privacy Rule, institutional review board, and confidentiality requirements to access health records as well as Family Education Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA) and IDEA requirements for education records.

ASD Case Definition

An adolescent met the ASD case definition if they were aged 16 years in 2022, lived in the study area at least 1 day in 2022, and had either a special education classification of ASD or an ASD ICD–Ninth Revision (ICD-9) or ICD–Tenth Revision (ICD-10) code (ICD-9: 299 range or ICD-10 F84 range excluding F84.2 [Rett syndrome]) in their records.

Data Collection and Variable Definitions

Health and education records from ages 12 to 16 years (2018–2022) were reviewed for demographics, selected co-occurring conditions commonly reported in previous literature (eg, ADHD, anxiety, depression, epilepsy, and language delay) documented in evaluations, IQ and adaptive tests, and IEPs. IEP meeting participants; school services; transition plan presence; transition services (services identified on a transition plan to prepare the student for postsecondary activities); postsecondary goals in employment, education, and independent living; and anticipated diploma type (regular high school diploma or certificate of completion/alternative degree) were collected from the most recent IEP with a transition plan. If no transition plan was present, the most recent IEP without a transition plan was collected.

All sites collected IQ and adaptive test results from 2018 to 2022; GA also collected tests from 2006 to 2018 to evaluate timing and frequency of testing during childhood and adolescence.

Adolescents were classified as having ID if their most recent IQ test score was 70 or below or, in the absence of a score, their IQ test contained an examiner’s statement of ID. Adaptive scores were categorized as profound (most recent adaptive test score <20), severe (20–34), moderate (35–49), mild (50–70), borderline (71–85), average (86–115), and above average (≥115). Adaptive tests were categorized as ID not otherwise specified if an examiner indicated functioning in the ID range on a test.

If statements on an evaluation indicated that the adolescent had no spoken language or relied primarily on gestures or single words to communicate, they were classified as nonverbal or minimally verbal. If verbal ability was not indicated, status was classified as “unknown.”

The 2022 American Community Survey 5-year estimates for neighborhood median household income (MHI)27 were linked by census tract and grouped by those living in low (≤$65 625), medium (>$65 625 to $101 473), and high (>$101 473) MHI tertile census tracts.

Analytic Methods

There were 4311 adolescents with ASD. Among adolescents with at least 1 developmental evaluation, we calculated the percentage with documented diagnoses of selected co-occurring conditions overall and by MHI tertiles. Poisson regression models were used to estimate prevalence ratios of co-occurring conditions in adolescents living in high compared with low MHI census tract tertiles with 95% CIs, adjusting for site, sex, and race and ethnicity (American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian or Pacific Islander, Black, Hispanic, white, 2 or more races, unknown). Individuals of Hispanic ethnicity of any race were categorized as Hispanic. Missing covariate data were categorized as “unknown.” Adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) were considered significant when 95% CIs excluded 1.0.

For GA, we calculated the median number of IQ and adaptive tests per adolescent and the ages for any test and most recent test. We calculated IEP prevalence among those with an education record overall and by site. For those with an IEP, we reported participants in the most recent IEP meeting (student, parent/guardian, teacher, LEA), types of services received, and presence of a transition plan. Among those with a transition plan, we calculated the prevalence of transition needs assessment, services, postsecondary goals, and anticipated diploma type. We reported all components by MHI using aPRs. IEP and transition plan data from TX-L were suppressed because of small sample size. Given the descriptive nature of the analyses, no adjustment was made for multiple comparisons; findings should be interpreted with caution.

Prevalence estimates with a relative SE of at least 30% were suppressed because of limited statistical precision, as were prevalence ratios involving at least 1 suppressed estimate. R software (version 4.4.0) was used for all data analysis.

Results

Description of Sample

Most adolescents with ASD were male (77.1%) (Table 1). Overall, 54.0% were white, 21.1% Black, 15.9% Hispanic, 3.7% Asian or Pacific Islander, 3.0% 2 or more races, and 0.5% American Indian or Alaska Native. MHI tertile distribution varied across sites from 9.8% (UT) to 63.3% (AR) in low MHI, 24.0% (WI) to 44% (TX-L) in medium MHI, and 7.7% (AR) to 51.0% (MD) in high MHI.

Presence of ICD code and special education exceptionality varied by site; 70.0% of adolescents had an ASD ICD code (range: 38.4%–82.5%), and 60.3% had an ASD special education classification (48.7%–86.6%) (Supplementary Table 1). Less than a third (30.3%) of adolescents had both an ICD code and special education classification (range: 14.0%–38.7%).

IQ and Adaptive Testing Patterns

Most adolescents did not receive an IQ (74.5%) or adaptive test (80.8%) between ages 12 and 16 (Table 1). In GA, expanded data collection increased IQ availability from 34.3% (ages 12–16) to 50.6% (all ages) and adaptive score availability from 27.8% (ages 12–16) to 54.0% (all ages) (Supplementary Table 2). In the expanded data collection in GA, median age at the time of testing was 9.0 years (IQR: 5.8–12.7) for IQ and 9.3 years (IQR: 5.9–13.1) for adaptive tests. By age 16 years, adolescents in GA had a median number of 2.0 IQ tests (IQR: 1–3) and 2.0 adaptive tests (IQR:1–4). The median age for most recent IQ test was 12.3 years (IQR: 9.2–15.0) with a range of 2.5 to 16.9 years and 12.0 years (IQR: 8.8–14.9) with a range of 2.0 to 16.9 years for most recent adaptive test.

Co-occurring Conditions by MHI

At least 1 developmental evaluation was available for 2628 adolescents (61.0%). Most adolescents with an evaluation had ever been diagnosed with at least 1 of the selected co-occurring mental and developmental conditions (87.3%) (Supplementary Table 3). The most common conditions were language delay (51.6%), ADHD (51.1%), anxiety (42.7%), and general developmental delay (36.7%) (Supplementary Table 3).

Adolescents in the high MHI tertile were more likely to be diagnosed with anxiety (aPR: 1.14, 95% CI 1.07–1.22) and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) (aPR: 1.12, 95% CI 1.02–1.23) than those in the low MHI tertile (Figure 1, Supplementary Table 3). Adolescents in the high MHI tertile were less likely to be ever diagnosed with general developmental delay (aPR: 0.86, 95% CI 0.80–0.94) and language delay (aPR: 0.92, 95% CI 0.86–0.98), compared with those in the low MHI tertile.

Characteristics of IEPs and Transition Plans

The prevalence of IEP components varied by site (Figure 2, Supplementary Table 4). Overall, 82.6% of adolescents had an IEP, ranging from 58.2% (UT) to 98.6% (AR).

Among students with an IEP, 59.2% were present at their most recent meeting (range: 31.4%–82.3%) (Figure 2, Supplementary Table 4). Most adolescents had a parent or teacher (general or special education) present (90.2% and 92.3%, respectively), with little variability by site. The prevalence of members from a local education agency (LEA) attending the most recent IEP meeting was 65.0% overall but varied widely by site (14.0%–97.7%). The most frequently documented services were speech-language (55.4% overall, 34.3%–70.6%), transportation (31.8% overall, 6.0%–53.1%), occupational therapy (14.7% overall, 6.8%–29.8%), and counseling/psychological services (13.0% overall, 0%–33.4%). Most adolescents with an IEP had a transition plan (94.8% overall, all sites ≥90%).

Of those with a transition plan, most had a transition needs assessment (86.6%, 63.7%–100%) (Figure 2, Supplementary Table 4). Overall, 91.4% of adolescents with a transition plan had at least 1 transition service (76.4%–99.7%). Among those with a transition plan, 95.7% had a postsecondary education goal (89.4%–99.4%), and 95.5% had a postsecondary employment goal (85.0%–100.0%). The prevalence of a postsecondary living goal was 43.3% overall (11.0%–89.3%). Most students with transition plans expected to complete school with a regular diploma (73.6%), with wide variation by site (0%–100%). Completion of school with an alternative diploma or certificate of completion was expected for 16.0% (0%–30.5%).

There was no difference in IEP prevalence by MHI tertile; however, students in the high MHI tertile were less likely to attend their IEP meetings and less likely to have a teacher present than those in the low MHI tertile (aPR: 0.89, 95% CI 0.81–0.98; aPR: 0.96, 95% CI 0.93–0.99, respectively) (Table 2). Twice as many students with at least 1 co-occurring condition in the high MHI tertile had counseling services documented in their IEP compared with the low MHI tertile (aPR: 2.41, 95% CI 1.60–3.65). Students in the high MHI tertile less frequently had transportation services (aPR: 0.69, 95% CI 0.59–0.82), social work services (aPR: 0.38, 95% CI 0.26–0.56), and school health/nurse services (aPR: 0.32, 95% CI 0.15–0.68) in their IEP than students in the low MHI tertile. There were no significant differences in the prevalence of transition plans or any transition plan components by MHI tertile.

Half of those with a postsecondary education goal indicated higher education, whereas 40.9% reported a goal of other training (Table 3). Of students with a postsecondary employment goal, 61.6% reported a goal of competitive employment, 14.2% self or supported employment, and 4.8% employment through a shelter or day program. Among those with a postsecondary living goal, the most common was “live at home” (43.2%) followed by “live independently” (39.9%) and “live in a supported setting” (16.9%).

All students in MN and NJ anticipated receiving a regular diploma, whereas nearly a third in GA and MD expected to receive an alternative diploma or certificate of completion (30.5% and 30.2%, respectively) (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

Most adolescents with ASD lacked documented IQ or adaptive tests from ages 12 to 16 years, possibly because of evaluations in private settings not captured in our data or decreased testing with age. Alternatively, the absence of testing could indicate there were no concerns about co-occurring ID. GA data indicate that expanding the age range for data collection could improve the availability of this information because most testing occurred before age 12. The relevance of childhood IQ and adaptive scores to an adolescent’s functioning is unclear because evidence suggests that these scores may change over time, although some studies indicate relative stability across development.28-32 IDEA recommends re-evaluating special education students every 3 years, but it does not require specific tests.20 The absence of recent IQ and adaptive testing could hinder adolescents transitioning into adulthood, particularly because services like Medicaid home and community-based services waivers may require recent assessments of cognitive and adaptive limitations alongside a developmental disability diagnosis.33 If IQ and/or adaptive tests continue to be used for eligibility and service provision, increasing testing in adolescence may increase service eligibility and better represent current needs.

The most frequent co-occurring mental and behavioral conditions among adolescents with ASD were language delay, ADHD, and anxiety. Previous studies reported similar estimates,11,12 and these conditions are more common among adolescents with ASD than in the general population.34-36 Low SES significantly predicts mental health issues in children,15,17 including those with ASD.19 However, our findings differ somewhat; OCD and anxiety were more common in the high MHI tertile, whereas only language delay and general developmental delay were more prevalent in the low MHI tertile. Other mental and behavioral conditions showed no significant differences between MHI groups. These results should be interpreted with caution because timing of co-occurring diagnoses was unavailable.

Students in the low MHI tertile were more likely to receive transportation, social work, and school health services. Students in the low MHI tertile with at least 1 co-occurring condition were less likely to have counseling services in their IEP than those in the high MHI tertile. This suggests that, despite widespread IEP and transition plan use, access to specific school-based services may differ by socioeconomic context, potentially reflecting variation in school resources, provider availability, and barriers to accessing mental health services. Although the Department of Education reported higher rates of mental health services in schools serving students eligible for free/reduced-price meals (as a proxy for SES), an inverse association was reflected in our data. Inadequate access to mental health clinicians and funding are cited as key barriers to service provision.37

More than 90% of adolescents had a transition plan on their most recent IEP; those whose IEP was from before age 16 may not have been required to have a transition plan. Like previous findings, over 95% of adolescents with a transition plan had education and employment postsecondary goals.11,12,38 However, IEP and transition planning practices varied by site. Although federal regulations mandate a transition plan by age 16,20 several states, including MD, NJ, UT, and WI, require this planning to begin by age 14.39,40 Other studies indicate that students with ASD have little involvement in their transition planning41-44 despite evidence that participation may improve their high school outcomes45,46; we found about 59% of students with ASD attended their most recent IEP meeting in ADDM communities.

Postsecondary living goals, not mandated by IDEA, are consistently less prevalent than other postsecondary goals.11,12,38,47,48 Previous work found that adolescents with ASD and ID were more likely to have a postsecondary living goal compared with those without ID.11,12,47 Through personal communications, one county reported that discussions of postsecondary living goals for diploma-track adolescents typically occur after academic goals and testing are completed, often during their junior or senior year. This practice could explain the low rates of postsecondary living goals observed in our data, consistent with previous studies.38,48 Although no research was found on the influence of postsecondary living goals on postsecondary outcomes, certain living skills taught in school predict positive outcomes.45 Given that students with ASD tend to have lower levels of community engagement compared with peers with other disabilities,49 independent living goals could represent an opportunity for improvement in transition planning.

Most students planned to complete school with a regular high school diploma, whereas some anticipated receiving alternative degrees or certificates of completion, which are not always accepted by colleges or employers. Although most states offer certificates of completion, not all provide alternative diplomas, and differing graduation requirements may account for variations by site.

Although understanding IEP documentation is valuable, our data do not assess the quality of IEPs, their implementation, or the satisfaction of providers, parents, or students. Existing literature highlights perspectives from providers, parents, and students, revealing instances where documented services are not delivered as specified, the quality of services falls short of expectations, and goals are perceived as unachievable or unmeasurable.50-52 Despite presence at IEP meetings, parents may feel excluded because of ineffective communication and unfamiliar terminology.52 Future research could examine how specific services (eg, vocational training, life skills instruction) contribute to the achievement of postsecondary goals and community engagement for adolescents with ASD.

This analysis depends on the completeness and quality of records, which varied by site. Although population-based, results are not representative of all adolescents with ASD, including those with a 504 plan or no educational supports. Data collection is limited to participating sources and may not capture evaluations or services received outside these records. ASD identification based on either health or education records may reflect differences in diagnostic and eligibility practices across systems and contribute to heterogeneity and cross-site variability. In addition, ID, which is associated with both socioeconomic status and service needs, could not be fully accounted for because of missing data; associations between MHI and service receipt should be interpreted with caution. This report presents the first cross-sectional analysis of adolescents aged 16 years in 2022 from the ADDM Network, enhancing the generalizability of adolescent surveillance data.

Conclusion

Adolescents with ASD had a high prevalence of co-occurring mental and behavioral conditions, such as language delay, ADHD, and anxiety, along with differing documentation of counseling services based on MHI and IEP services that varied by site. Many adolescents with ASD lacked recent IQ and adaptive tests, which inform assessment of cognitive and daily living needs and service eligibility, potentially limiting effective planning and access to appropriate services in adolescence and adulthood. These findings reflect complexities in service provision and variations in practices across the United States, providing data for health and education providers and advocates to understand transition needs and planning and reduce health disparities among adolescents with ASD.
