A ERSF-VIPA framework: scalable wildlife movement modelling for conflict mitigation
Authors: Xiaoyi Chen, Jie Li, Xinyu Cao, Yin Yang, Colin A. Chapman, Xiao Li, Ruijing Qiao, Xiaohuan Wang, Feiling Yang, Dejun Kong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
1. Effective conservation planning and conflict mitigation can hinge on accurately modelling wildlife movement paths (WMPs), yet progress is hindered by both a shortage of reliable methods and limited data. The critical challenge, therefore, is to devise limited-data models that faithfully reproduce elusive species’ movements and deliver actionable insights for human–wildlife conflict management. 2. We introduce the Enhanced Resource Selection Function–Vector-network Iterative Pathfinding Algorithm (ERSF-VIPA), a novel framework for simulating WMPs with limited data. Drawing on historical occurrence records of Asian elephants (Elephas maximus), we assume individuals make rational, goal-driven decisions based on local environmental knowledge. The ERSF employs a random forest on a hexagonal grid to estimate nonlinear resource-selection probabilities, while VIPA conducts an iterative, node-to-node search across that hexagonal vector network—scoring each candidate by combining selection probability with cubic distance coefficients to ensure ecological validity and energetic efficiency. 3. The model demonstrates high accuracy, with 90.3% of the 68 simulated paths approximating the observed paths with an average maximum deviation of 418 m. These findings underscore the model’s robustness and its capacity to translate limited tracking data into actionable insights for conservation. 4. ERSF-VIPA operates using only coarse, non-continuous historical data that lack precise timestamps or spatial accuracy. By operating with minimal data requirements, ERSF-VIPA demonstrates exceptional extensibility and broad applicability for reconstructing movement paths of elusive wildlife species. Its proven accuracy in simulating Asian elephant paths further positions it as a potentially powerful decision-support framework for real-time animal monitoring and proactive human–wildlife conflict mitigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40462-025-00602-0.

Introduction

Accurate simulation of wildlife movement paths (WMPs) underpins our understanding of resource acquisition, gene flow, and ecological connectivity [1, 2] and steers effective conservation planning, corridor design, and conflict mitigation [3, 4]. However, most existing methods demand high-resolution movement data: Brownian Bridge Movement Models (BBMMs) require frequent GPS fixes [5], and Step Selection Functions (SSFs) typically depend on sequential location records with known or estimable time intervals to model stepwise movement decisions [6]. Such data remain elusive for large, cryptic, or potentially dangerous species, such as elephants (Elephas maximus), lions (Panthera leo), and snow leopards (Panthera uncia), despite advances in camera traps、animal mounted sensors, and drone surveillance [7–10]. Developing models with reduced data dependency while maintaining accurate prediction of WMPs has emerged as a crucial research priority.

Existing approaches address parts of the challenge but fall short under limited-data conditions. Diffusion-based tools (e.g., MIGCLIM, DispFit) capture large-scale spread dynamics but rely on simplified assumptions such as uniform dispersal [11, 12]. Brownian bridge movement models (BBMMs) reconstruct fine-scale paths but require dense, temporally continuous GPS data. Least-cost path (LCP) approaches assume complete spatial awareness and produce globally optimal routes, often ignoring localized, stepwise decisions. The randomized least-cost path algorithm（RSP） [13] adds stochasticity but remains rooted in global optimization and lacks behavioral specificity. Circuit theory, in contrast, models movement as a random walk across resistance surfaces, accommodating route redundancy but offering simplified ecological representation [14]. Pathfinding algorithms such as Dijkstra’s and A* efficiently compute shortest paths on cost surfaces but are primarily applied to scenarios like airport routing and robot pathfinding. While RSFs estimate habitat associations under sparse data, they cannot simulate movement paths [15]. Integrated step selection functions (iSSFs) do enable path simulation with movement kernels and environmental covariates but their dependence on high-resolution tracking data limits applicability in data-poor contexts [16, 17]). Crucially, few frameworks marry low data dependency with decision-based, memory-informed routing essential for socially complex species.

To bridge this gap, we introduce the Enhanced Resource Selection Function–Vector Iterative Pathfinding Algorithm (ERSF-VIPA). Grounded in rational, goal-oriented movement and local environmental knowledge, ERSF-VIPA operates on a hexagonal environmental grid under two optimization principles—minimizing travel distance and maximizing resource gain. The ERSF module replaces logistic RSFs with a random forest to estimate nonlinear habitat suitability across hexagon cells. The VIPA module then treats hexagon centroids as nodes in a vector network, iteratively selecting each next node by scoring candidates on combined resource-selection probabilities and cubic distance coefficients. ERSF-VIPA reconstructs complete WMPs using only start–end points, without temporal or directional inputs. Applied to historical Asian elephant data in Yunnan, China, it achieved over 90% concordance with observed paths and can forecast likely trajectories by screening high-probability endpoints, offering a potentially powerful framework for corridor design, real-time monitoring, and early warning of human–wildlife conflicts.

Data and methods

Study area

The study area encompasses the transition zone between the Autonomous Prefecture of Xishuangbanna Dai and Pu’er city in Yunnan Province, China (101°33'−100°34'E, 22°18'−22°39'N [18]). This region has a subtropical monsoon climate, with mean annual temperatures ranging from 18 to 22 °C and annual precipitation between 1,200 and 1,600 mm, characterized by pronounced wet and dry seasons. These climatic conditions support a variety of vegetation types, such as tropical rainforests, monsoon forests, and evergreen broad-leaved forests, which provide critical habitat and forage resources for wildlife, such as Asian elephants. Topographic complexity is a defining feature of the area, with elevations of 500 to 2,000 m, creating heterogeneous microhabitats [19].

Human activities are primarily agricultural, with crops such as rice, tea, coffee, rubber, and tropical fruits produced in the area. This agroecological landscape sustains an estimated population of 189 Asian elephants, whose habitat use is strongly influenced by resource availability and terrain characteristics [20]. Telemetry and field observations indicate preferential use of lowland evergreen broad-leafed forests, bamboo thickets, and forest-farmland ecotones, particularly in areas below 1,000 m in elevation with gentle slopes ( <15°) [21]. These habitats offer abundant forage, easier mobility, and better access to water, making them ideal for both feeding and movement. Elephants travel long distances to natural salt licks (locally known as“nitrate ponds”), which are key sources of sodium and other minerals essential vital for maintaining physiological functions [22].

Data

Tracks of the movement of Asian elephants

Asian elephant movement data were extracted from the literature [23], comprising 233 occurrence points and 34 primary movement paths. The 233 occurrence points were used as inputs for the ERSF, whereas the 34 movement paths, with 68 of their start or end points, served as reference paths for VIPA modelling and validation.

These paths were not derived from GPS telemetry or direct animal tracking. Instead, they were reconstructed by linking discrete signs of elephant presence (e.g., dung, footprints, bark stripping) recorded by reserve staffs during routine ground patrols and by systematic transect-based field surveys along 91 trails totaling ~307 km. Presence points were connected based on spatial proximity and ecological continuity to generate linear, short-distance routes that approximate actual movement paths in fragmented landscapes.

This dataset has two key limitations. First, the temporal resolution is lacking, as precise timestamps for individual occurrence points are not available, making some data unsuitable for the SSF, which requires information about sequential movement decisions. Second, the movement direction is uncertain. Since the records do not specify the direction of elephant movement, each path was simulated bidirectionally (i.e., from the start to end and vice versa), following established protocols [18].

To address these limitations, we employed an iterative modelling approach, where movement paths were simulated from two different starting points (both ends of a path) to assess the impact of directionality on movement paths. Furthermore, our model only used the starting and ending points of the 34 movement paths for path simulation, rather than incorporating the process points from the original survey tracks into VIPA (Fig. 1c). ERSF-VIPA model

The model is grounded in two fundamental assumptions: (1) Asian elephants know their environment and have a destination in mind, and (2) Asian elephants choose a cost-effective route accordingly. Based on these assumptions, the model operates under two key principles: (1) Asian elephants select the shortest and most direct path to conserve energy and time, and (2) they optimize path selection by incorporating environmental characteristics. These principles are implemented through two interconnected modules (Fig. 3).Fig. 3ERSF-VIPA model: At each step, the candidate neighbor with the highest composite score is selected. Based on the hexagonal grid with resource selection probabilities (Fig. 2c2), the VIPA module is used to conduct pathfinding simulations (Fig. 2c1), where the pathfinding grid represents the potential movement network of Asian elephants. The resource selection probabilities derived from the ERSF module serve as conditional probabilities for each step in the VIPA iterative model, thereby fulfilling Principle (2) of the model. Simultaneously, we introduce a distance coefficient to compare the differences in distance from neighbouring points in six directions (around the current point) to the end point, supporting the implementation of Principle (1). Starting from the origin, neighbouring points are iteratively evaluated and selected, ultimately simulating a path from the starting point to the ending point within the elephant’s potential movement network. This approach ensures that the simulated paths are both biologically plausible and energetically efficient, effectively capturing the trade-off between resource availability and distance [28].

ERSF module

Traditional resource selection function (RSF) models typically rely on linear regression, which likely does not adequately capture complex nonlinear relationships between environmental variables and species occurrence [29]. To overcome this issue and better handle imbalanced categorical data, we replaced the linear regression component of the traditional RSF with a random forest (RF) model [26], thus formulating the enhanced resource selection function (ERSF). For a mathematical overview of the classical RSF model, please refer to Appendix 2.

RF are non-parametric ensemble classifiers that consist of multiple decision trees trained on bootstrapped samples of the data. Each tree learns recursive, hierarchical splits of the predictor space using random feature subsets, which allows the ensemble to flexibly capture nonlinear relationships and higher-order interactions among variables. Unlike parametric models, RF does not assume any predefined functional form between predictors and response variables, making it especially suitable for capturing threshold effects, variable importance shifts, and non-monotonic ecological responses.

This modification improves the ability of the model to capture intricate nonlinear interactions while improving its robustness and predictive accuracy. Notably, the ERSF module allows researchers to substitute RF with resource selection algorithms suitable for their target species or study area (e.g., logistic regression) based on their specific study needs, as detailed in Appendix 6.

Data acquisition and pre-processing

The initial step in our approach involved assembling datasets containing both target variables, representing Asian elephant occurrence (“occurrence points”) and absence (“non-occurrence points”; see Appendix 3 for generation details), and environmental covariates, such as terrain, land use, and distance metrics (Table 1). To minimize bias from differences in variable scales, all environmental predictors were standardized by centering them to a mean of 0 and scaling to a standard deviation of 1 before model training.

Random forest model training

The RF model was trained using pre-processed environmental covariates and target occurrence data. The dataset was split into training (70%) and testing (30%) subsets to ensure the generalizability of the model. A total of 100 decision trees were used as base learners within the RF ensemble, enabling the model to learn the complex hierarchical paths of resource selection.

During training, the RF algorithm automatically evaluated split conditions across environmental variables (e.g., elevation, NDVI, distance to road), capturing nonlinear dependencies and ecological thresholds without manual feature engineering. Each tree was grown to maximum depth (no pruning), allowing for highly flexible partitioning of the predictor space.

Model performance evaluation

After training, the RF model was applied to the test dataset and its predictive performance was assessed using standard classification metrics, namely, accuracy, precision, recall, and the F1 score. Furthermore, model performance was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and its corresponding area under the curve (AUC) value. AUC values approaching 1 indicate a discriminatory capacity, confirming the model’s effectiveness in distinguishing between selected and non-selected resources.

Honeycomb hexagonal lattice grid integration

Upon validation of the high predictive accuracy of the ERSF model, we integrated a hexagonal honeycomb lattice grid of the study area to spatially represent the probabilities of resource selection. Each hexagonal honeycomb grid cell was assigned a side length of L = 30 m, which is consistent with the scale selection rationale detailed in section “VIPA module” (1). Here, L denotes the edge length of individual hexagons. The probability estimates for resource selection were incorporated into the attribute set for each node in the grid, each uniquely identified by its designated ID for precise spatial reference, forming a spatially explicit basis for the optimization of movement paths with the VIPA module.

Honeycomb hexagonal grids offer several advantages for ecological modelling and pathfinding. Compared with traditional square grids, hexagonal honeycomb lattices mitigate boundary effects and provide a more accurate representation of spatial continuity, making them particularly suitable for modelling wildlife movement paths [30]. A key geometric benefit is that all neighboring cells are equidistant, reducing directional bias and enabling consistent transition costs in all directions [31]. Furthermore, uniform spatial coverage of honeycomb hexagonal grids facilitates the fine-scale detection of subtle variations in the resource distribution, leading to precise estimations of resource selection probabilities and improving the reliability of movement simulations [32].

VIPA module

The VIPA in this study is a novel model designed to simulate WMPs. The logic behind the VIPA is as follows. Starting from the location of the Asian elephant as the selected node within the honeycomb hexagonal grid, the initial movement of the elephant is simulated. The current point is then used as the reference point for subsequent movements. The algorithm filters the six neighbouring points surrounding the current point and calculates the distance from each of these neighbouring points to the destination. In addition to the distance coefficient, the algorithm also accounts for the influence of the probability of resource selection. The point with the highest score is chosen as the next current point. This process continues iteratively, with the herd gradually progressing to the end point, simulating the actual movement path of the elephant herd [33].

Following previous studies, a 30 m grid scale was adopted as it offers a practical balance between spatial detail and computational efficiency [34].

Asian elephants exhibit daily movements that often span large areas, with localized high-density activity in key habitat areas [36]. To effectively evaluate the simulated paths, a 600 m threshold was adopted based on empirical evidence. Specifically, in Mengyang Sub-Reserve, 84.7% of human-elephant conflicts and 83.6% of feeding activity occur within 600 m of forest edges [37]; moreover, terrain analysis reveals an average ridge-to-river spacing of 550 ± 120 m [38], and corridor planning in Yunnan and India is commonly based on intervals of 550 to 650 m [39]. As a result, this threshold captures core movement behaviours and key activity zones.

Results

To assess the effectiveness of the ERSF-VIPA model in simulating wildlife movement under data-limited conditions, we organized the results into two main components. First, we evaluated the performance of the ERSF module in predicting resource selection probability. Then, we verified the VIPA module’s capacity to simulate movement paths based on resource selection probability. The combined results demonstrate the model’s utility in generating biologically realistic and ecologically meaningful predictions of elephant movement.

Discussion

Integrating behavioural ecology into computational pathfinding with limited data

Wildlife movement is influenced by a combination of behavioural strategies and environmental interactions [40]. Wildlife often make movement decisions on the basis of energy conservation, resource optimization, and familiarity with the landscape [41]. For example, studies have shown that some animals, such as elk (Elaphurus davidianus) and caribou (Rangifer tarandus), adjust their movement paths in response to forage availability and predation risk, indicating a balance between energy intake and safety.

In our study, we observed that Asian elephants tend to select the shortest and most direct routes to conserve energy and time while also optimizing their routes based on the local environmental characteristics. This behaviour aligns with findings from other studies, suggesting that large mammals often rely on learned spatial knowledge and local environmental cues rather than possessing a complete global perspective of their habitat [42].

In contrast to conventional models, the ERSF-VIPA framework more accurately captures the decision-making processes of large, cognitively advanced species. For instance, although A* algorithms have been used in structured systems such as airport routing [34], they lack ecological sensitivity. LCP models have guided corridor design in protected areas like Yellowstone and the Western Ghats [14], but they assume global landscape knowledge and static resistance, which do not reflect the local, experience-informed decisions of species such as elephants. Similarly, BBMMs have been used to reconstruct fine-scale movement patterns of wolves and elk based on high-resolution GPS data [5], yet their reliance on continuous data limits their applicability in data-scarce scenarios.

As demonstrated in Appendix 8, the ERSF-VIPA achieves higher accuracy than above models in replicating realistic movement paths, capturing the nuanced logic of elephant navigation. In general, the ERSF-VIPA offers improved ecological validity and greater flexibility than other methods for simulating movement under data-limited conditions.

Model generality and flexibility

The ERSF-VIPA framework balances ecological specificity with broad applicability through key features enhancing generality and flexibility.

Its modular structure allows adaptation to diverse contexts. The ERSF module, which estimates resource selection probabilities, is not restricted to RF but can integrate various models: generalized linear models (GLMs), generalized additive models (GAMs), or RF/MaxEnt, enabling customization for species with varying ecological strategies.

The VIPA module’s scoring mechanism (Eq. 6) supports flexible customization: the numerator can incorporate virtually any habitat selection model (e.g., RF, GAM, GLM), while the denominator can use any function to weigh and transform distance’s impact on scores.

Conflict-risk management and model application

The ERSF-VIPA framework offers robust and versatile support for managing human–wildlife conflicts. By characterizing a target species’ resource-selection profile, practitioners can infer likely movement paths. While real-time application is a potential promising direction, the current framework supports inferred movement predictions when exact destinations are unknown. A recommended four-step workflow is provided below:

Define habitat preferences

Managers should first delineate the focal species’ habitat preferences within the study area. For experts and seasoned conservationists, this often relies on established ecological knowledge [43].

Map frequent and avoided areas

Identify zones the species commonly uses versus those it typically avoids. For Asian elephants, flat riverine valleys usually serve as foraging grounds, whereas steep ridgelines pose significant barriers [44]. In many protected areas, such habitat-use data already exist or can be inferred from prior studies [45], streamlining these initial steps.

Generate resource-selection probability layers

Run the ERSF module to produce a spatial grid of resource-selection probabilities across the landscape, based on the outputs of Steps 1 and 2.

Simulate movement paths

Specify known or hypothesized start and end points and execute the VIPA module to simulate potential movement paths.

In real-time management, precise animal destinations are often unknown and difficult to predict. The ERSF-derived probability grid can highlight a suite of likely endpoints—hexagonal cells with elevated selection probabilities (e.g., the blue balloon markers in Fig. 7). Randomness and uncertainty of decisions regarding movement paths

Despite the overall high accuracy of the model (90.3% of the simulated paths fall within 600 m of the observed paths), certain deviations remain, as observed for paths such as T26 (Appendix 5). These discrepancies can be largely attributed to inherent randomness in decision-making about wildlife movement. Animals do not consistently follow fixed routes but adjust their paths in a nondeterministic manner in response to local environmental variations and unforeseen disturbances [25]. Furthermore, the limitations of historical survey data, namely, the absence of precise temporal and directional information, further compound uncertainties in path reconstruction. For example, factors such as transient environmental dynamics, human disturbances, and social interactions are difficult to quantify, thus limiting the ability of models to fully capture dynamic movement paths [46]. Additionally, representing complex group movements as single polyline segments may oversimplify the heterogeneous and shifting nature of herd dynamics [47]. These challenges underscore the need for future models to incorporate more dynamic and probabilistic approaches that better account for the intrinsic randomness and external uncertainties inherent in wildlife movement.

Limitations and future vision

This study relies on historical presence data reconstructed from field signs (e.g., dung, footprints), not GPS-collared movement tracks [23]. These inferred paths lack precise timestamps and directionality, but they remain valuable for understanding movement patterns in data-limited regions [48]. ERSF-VIPA is designed to work under such constraints, providing a viable simulation tool where high-resolution tracking is unavailable [49].

Currently, the model selects the highest-scoring neighbor deterministically, which may oversimplify real animal behavior. In reality, animals often make probabilistic or suboptimal choices due to exploration or environmental variability [50]. Future improvements should introduce stochastic sampling from candidate nodes, allowing multiple path realizations and supporting probabilistic surfaces for more realistic simulations [51].

Temporal mismatches between movement data and environmental covariates (e.g., NDVI) may introduce bias, although we minimized this by aligning data wherever possible. Still, future models should examine sensitivity to covariate timing.

Lastly, while the ERSF-derived probability grid identifies likely endpoints, it may generate too many candidates in real-world scenarios, complicating timely decision-making. Without further filtering or prioritization, this could lead to resource misallocation [52]. Future work should allow repeated runs of the algorithm to generate multiple paths that can be aggregated into probabilistic surfaces or heat maps, thereby enhancing practical applicability [53].
