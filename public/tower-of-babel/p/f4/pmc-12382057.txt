Nonrestorative sleep is associated with self-injury behavior and suicidal ideation in Chinese adolescents: a longitudinal study
Authors: Yanzhe Wang, Qingyu Wang, Weijie Gong, Xingchen Shang, Jiayin Ruan, Wei Liang, Zheng Lin, Sha Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies showed that nonrestorative sleep (NRS) was associated with various adverse outcomes. This study aimed to explore whether NRS was associated with self-injury behavior, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts.MethodsAdolescents attending Grades 7–12 in Nanjing, China, were invited to participate in this longitudinal school-based survey from January 11 to 23, 2024 (T1) and from May 16 to 26, 2024 (T2). The Nonrestorative Sleep Scale (NRSS), Perceived Stress Scale, Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression-10, and Family APGAR Index were employed to assess NRS, stress, depression, and family function at T1, respectively. The self-injury behavior, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts during the last four months were measured at T2. Multivariable logistic regressions were applied to investigate the associations of NRS with self-injury behavior, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts. Stratified analyses were conducted to explore gender-specific effects, and the area under curve was calculated to explore the predictive ability of NRS.ResultsTotally, 322 adolescents (57.1% male, mean age 15.4 ± 1.86 years) completed the two-wave surveys. The prevalence of self-injury behavior, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts in adolescents was 9.3%, 12.7%, and 5.9%, respectively. Reduced NRS symptoms (higher scores of NRSS) were associated with a decreased likelihood of self-injury (odds ratio, OR = 0.89, P < 0.001), suicidal ideation (OR = 0.90, P < 0.001), and suicide attempts (OR = 0.93, P = 0.015). After accounting for sociodemographics, stress, depression, and family function, reduced NRS symptoms significantly decreased the odds of self-injury behavior (adjusted odds ratio, AOR = 0.88, P = 0.002) and suicidal ideation (AOR = 0.92, P = 0.009) but not suicide attempts (AOR = 0.93, P = 0.082). Subgroup analysis showed that the associations of NRS symptoms reached statistical significance exclusively in female participants. The optimal risk-screening threshold of NRSS was 36.5.ConclusionsReduced NRS symptoms might decrease the risk of self-injury and suicidal ideation. Relieving NRS symptoms with appropriate interventions would be advantageous for adolescents’ health.

Background

Self-injury and suicide have been significant public health concerns and societal issues worldwide, especially in children and adolescents, placing a high burden on the family, school, and society [1]. Both non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) and suicide involve deliberate physical damage to one’s body [2], and some researchers stated that they differ fundamentally in intent—self-injury serves as a maladaptive coping mechanism to regulate emotions, whereas suicide-related behaviors aim to permanently end consciousness and feeling by terminating life [3]. Nevertheless, extensive studies have demonstrated a robust association between self-injury and suicidal behaviors [2]. For example, Joiner’s Theory of Acquired Capability for Suicide indicated that self-injury may cultivate acquired capability for suicide by systematically reducing innate fears of pain and death through repeated exposure to self-harm [4]. The global prevalence of self-injury in adolescents falls between 13% and 45%, with the highest rates observed at the ages of 14–17 years [5, 6]. Furthermore, according to a population-based study of 82 countries, 14% of adolescents have suicidal thoughts during the last 12 months [7]. In 2020, suicide ranked as the second leading cause of death for adolescents aged 10–14 years and the third for those aged 15–24 years [8]. Notably, the prevalence of self-injury and suicidal ideation among Chinese adolescents was estimated to be about 48.8% and 23.5%, respectively, highlighting the urgent need for prevention efforts [9, 10]. In addition to the medical treatment cost, suicide attempts at a young age continue to cause significant health and social problems during midlife, causing long-term health care and social needs [11]. Therefore, self-injury and suicide can lead to substantial healthcare costs for individuals and society in the long run [12].

The determinants of self-injury and suicidal behavior have attracted wide attention to developing effective prevention strategies and early intervention programs, especially for vulnerable groups [13]. Adolescent self-injury and suicide stem from an intricate combination of genetic, biological, psychiatric, psychosocial, social, cultural, and various other factors [1]. Sleep disturbances are increasingly recognized as a crucial factor in self-injury and suicide, and they have been identified as prominent warning signals and contributing risk factors by many suicide organizations, such as the National Institute of Mental Health [14]. Sleep interventions have also been involved in many mainstream therapies to prevent self-injury and suicide [15]. Among adolescent populations, impaired sleep quality (Odd ratio, OR = 2.18) was a higher risk of NSSI [16]. Clinical evidence showed that individuals with insomnia demonstrate significantly higher rates of suicide attempts compared to their peers without insomnia (79% versus 53%) [17]. One recent meta-analysis of 19 studies from 9 countries (N = 1,196,271 participants, including 628,525 with sleep disturbances versus 567,746 controls), revealed significantly elevated risks among adolescents with sleep disturbances for both suicide attempts (OR = 3.10) and suicidal ideation (OR = 2.28) [18]. Therefore, the role of sleep disturbances in adolescent self-injury and suicidality warrants careful clinical attention, and we proposed that sleep-focused interventions offer a viable treatment approach for mitigating self-injury and suicidality in adolescents, benefiting from their established value and low perceived stigma that makes it more likely for at-risk individuals to seek treatment [19].

The potential mechanisms underlying the association between sleep disturbances and self-injury and suicidal ideation or behaviors involve emotion dysregulation, dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, impaired prefrontal control, and activation of inflammatory processes. According to the conceptual model of the association of sleep disturbance and suicidality proposed by Perlis et al., significant life stress and mood disturbance induce HPA axis dysregulation, which interacts synergistically with sleep disturbances and impaired social functioning to amplify suicide risk [20]. Adolescents engaging in NSSI also exhibit significantly elevated cortisol awakening responses, indicating dysregulation of the HPA axis [21]. Second, sleep disturbances may impair emotion regulation capacity, depriving distressed individuals of a critical psychological buffer and emotional refuge [22]. Furthermore, extensive research has established sleep disturbances as a robust risk factor for depression, a well-documented predictor of self-injury and suicidal ideation [22, 23]. Third, sleep disturbances were reported to impair frontal lobe cognitive function, potentially compromising judgment and impulse control that may increase the likelihood of engaging in suicidal behaviors [20]. Adolescents may be especially susceptible to these effects because of their not fully developed frontal lobe and emotional regulation networks [24]. Lastly, sleep disruption may trigger the activation of inflammatory processes, including elevated interleukin-6 and C-reactive protein, which were associated with increased risk of suicide [14].

The severity of sleep disturbances in adolescents, especially in Chinese adolescents, is alarming and demands attention. Nonrestorative sleep (NRS), which refers to feeling unrefreshed after normal sleep duration, is one of the most prevalent sleep disturbances in adolescents [25, 26]. NRS used to be one of the symptoms of insomnia according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fourth edition [27], yet 14.8% of people experience NRS without meeting insomnia criteria, as per the insomnia severity index [28]. In a study of 4,175 American adolescents, 43.0% reported NRS [26]. In particular, the prevalence of NRS in Chinese adolescents was as high as 77.13% according to the question, “How often have you felt really refreshed upon awakening in the morning?” with a response of 2–3 days/week or less [25]. Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition reconceptualized it as a distinct diagnostic entity, and a diagnosis of “other specified insomnia disorder” can be made when individuals present with isolated NRS complaints that meet specified thresholds for frequency, duration, and clinically significant daytime impairment [29]. Wilkinson et al. proposed a paradigm—NRS due to medical illness, NRS due to psychiatric illness, and isolated NRS [30]. Experience reported also suggests that NRS on its own had an impact similar to when it occurred with other insomnia symptoms, and people who had NRS alone demonstrated significantly worse functional outcomes compared to healthy controls [31]. Therefore, addressing NRS before it progresses to insomnia is crucial for improving sleep.

Furthermore, whether NRS is associated with self-injury and suicide is still unclear. Till now, only one cross-sectional study has reported its association with suicidal ideation among Korean adolescents, with NRS only measured by a single item [32]. The association of NRS with self-injury, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts has not been explored in longitudinal studies with adjustment of psychological factors with the outcomes to provide more solid evidence for temporal precedence and targeted interventions. It was suggested to emphasize the difference between risk factors and correlates when examining the evidence on sleep disturbances and suicide risk [33]. A longitudinal approach is required to ascertain whether a variable qualifies as a risk factor by monitoring its influence over time before the outcome occurs [33]. Moreover, the association between sleep and suicide appears weaker as follow-up periods extend [19]. It is proposed that shorter windows may generate stronger associations due to more consistent measurements of sleep problems and suicidality, yet these studies are still relatively rare [19]. To fill the gap, this study adopted a longitudinal design with a shorter period (i.e., four months) to explore the association of NRS with self-injury behavior, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts, with the adjustment of potential confounders. Considering the prevalence of suicide and NRS varied in males and females [34, 35] and the gender-specific effect of NRS has also been reported [36], subgroup analysis was then conducted in male and female groups separately to test the gender-specific effects. Lastly, the optimal threshold of the NRSS score was estimated to inform future clinical studies to identify individuals at risk.

Methods

Participants and procedures

A school-based longitudinal survey of students was conducted in Nanjing, China, in two waves (T1: January 11–23, 2024, and T2: May 16–26, 2024). Eligibility criteria included normal cognitive function and the ability to read and understand simplified Chinese. Participants on sleep medications or with a mental illness diagnosis were excluded. In the first wave (T1), the adolescents answered questions on sociodemographics, self-reported health conditions, mental health, and family function. Four months later, in the second wave (T2), the adolescents reported their self-injury, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts during the last four months. Prior to participation, potential adolescents in the classes, which were randomly selected, and their guardians were given a detailed information sheet that outlined the study’s objectives, procedures, possible risks, and expected benefits. Adolescents were encouraged to ask questions and resolve any uncertainties they had about the study. They were informed that their participation was entirely voluntary and that they were free to skip any question or withdraw from the study at any time, whenever they wished, without any consequence. We obtained written informed consent from all participants and their guardians. The ethical approval of this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Nanjing Medical University [No.: (2023) 590].

A total of 454 adolescents attending Grades 7–12 were approached at T1. However, four adolescents were excluded due to self-reported use of sleep medications. Additionally, 128 students who failed to complete the follow-up survey were excluded from the analysis, yielding a final analytical sample of 322 participants. Participant attrition primarily resulted from institutional class reorganization during academic progression and subject transfers, which compromised our ability to track individual students while avoiding disturbing normal teaching operations. Nevertheless, in line with the typical rule of 15 participants per factor for regression, the sample size in this article is deemed sufficient for the analysis [37].

Measures

Outcome measures

Self-injury behavior was asked in the second wave (T2) by “In the past four months, whether you have self-harmed or not?” (Never, once, more than once), suicidal ideation was asked by “In the last four months, have you seriously considered suicide?” (Yes, No), and suicide attempt was asked by “How many times have you tried to suicide in the last four months?” (Never, once, more than once).

Nonrestorative sleep scale (NRSS)

The NRSS is the first tool developed for nonrestorative sleep assessment, featuring four subscales: refreshment from sleep, physical/medical symptoms, daytime functioning, and affective symptoms [30]. The 12-item scale yields a global score of 12 to 60, where higher scores denote a lower level of NRS symptoms [30]. The Cronbach’s alpha of the Chinese NRSS in Chinese adolescents was 0.83 [30].

Center for epidemiologic studies Depression-10 (CESD-10)

The CESD-10 is a short version of the 20-item CESD addressing symptoms of depression frequency and positive affect for evaluating the severity of depression [38]. Each item was assessed on a Likert scale from 0 to 3, with the items measuring positive affect needing to be reverse-coded before analysis. The range of the global scale is 0–30, where higher values suggest more severe symptoms [38].

Perceived stress scale (PSS)

The 10-item Chinese PSS was employed to assess the perceived stress level of adolescents. Items were responded to on a scale of 0 to 4, with higher scores denoting increased perceived stress [39]. The internal consistency of the Chinese PSS exhibited a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.79 [39].

Family APGAR index (APGAR)

The Family APGAR Index assesses family function with five components: adaptation, partnership, growth, affection, and resolve [40]. Each item uses a 3-point Likert scale with 0 for “almost never,” 1 for “sometimes,” and 2 for “often.” Total scores span from 0 to 10, where higher scores represent better family function [40]. The Chinese version of the Family APGAR Index has proven reliable and valid (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.88) [40].

Sociodemographic

Date of birth, gender (Male, Female), grade (Middle school: Grades 7–9, High school: Grades 10–12), and general health condition were also self-reported by the adolescents (very good, good, fair, bad, and very bad).

Statistical analysis

All data were entered in an Excel file and validated by comparing them with the originally collected paper questionnaires. Individuals’ characteristics were described through numbers and percentages and means ± standard deviations (SD), where appropriate. Results of the number of self-injury behaviors and suicide attempts were dichotomized as Yes (once or more than once) or No (Never). Missing values of the responses of NRSS, CESD-10, PSS, and APGAR were imputed by multiple imputations if 50% of items were answered, while other missing values were not imputed [41]. Simple logistic regressions and multivariable logistic regressions were employed to estimate the unadjusted odds ratio (OR), adjusted odds ratio (AOR), and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for the associations of independent variables with self-injury behavior, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts. The multivariable logistic regressions were conducted using the enter method, with all hypothesized predictors (i.e., NRS, depression, stress, family function, and sociodemographic covariates) entering the model simultaneously. This conservative approach was selected because of the small number of independent variables, and it retains all theoretically relevant variables in the regression model regardless of statistical significance, thereby minimizing selection bias while accounting for potential confounders [42]. Stratified analyses in male and female cohorts were applied independently with the adjustment of all the above variables in each model for self-injury behavior, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts to assess the gender-specific effects of NRS. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) with the Package “car” [43]. The VIF quantifies the degree to which the variance of estimated regression coefficients is inflated due to correlations among independent variables in the model [44]. A value of 10 or greater indicates problematic multicollinearity [45]. Lastly, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to assess the predictive value of NRS for self-injury, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts. The area under curve (AUC) was calculated, and the threshold was determined by the sensitivity and specificity based on the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1) [46]. The two-sided statistically significant level was set at α = 0.05. We conducted all data analysis using R version 4.3.2.

Results

Description of the enrolled adolescents

In total, 322 students (57.1% male) with an average age of 15.4 years (SD: 1.86, Range: 12–19 years) completed the two surveys at both T1 and T2. The average global score of the NRSS was 41.27 (SD: 8.30). The prevalence of self-injury behavior, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts was 9.3%, 12.7%, and 5.9%, respectively (Table 1).

Subgroup analysis of nonrestorative sleep and self-injury behavior, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts

Subgroup analysis showed that NRS symptoms were not statistically significantly associated with self-injury behavior, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts in male students. The VIF values ranged from 1.00 to 5.61.

Discussion

This was the first longitudinal study that investigated the associations of NRS with self-injury behavior, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts among Chinese adolescents. After comprehensive adjustment for sociodemographics, health conditions, stress, depression, and family function, we found that reduced NRS symptoms would decrease the risks of self-injury, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts, with notable gender differences in these associations. A NRSS score of 36.5 may serve as a clinically meaningful threshold for identifying individuals at elevated risk of self-harm behaviors.

Our study showed that reduced NRS symptoms (i.e., higher NRSS scores) could decrease the risk of self-injury (OR = 0.88), which was in line with one previous systematic review that included 16 studies showing sleep disruptions, including short sleep duration, poor sleep quality, and sleep disturbances, were associated with NSSI [47]. The impaired recovery caused by NRS might prevent individuals from restoring their physical and mental functions, leaving them stressed to face challenges [48]. The Experiential Avoidance Model posits that acute stress triggers maladaptive emotional responses, thereby increasing vulnerability to NSSI as a coping mechanism for immediate distress relief [49]. Evidence also suggests that while NSSI carries significant harm, it triggers endogenous opioid release (e.g., endorphins) that alleviates distress while providing transient pleasure [50]. Moreover, sleep disturbances were related to the dysregulation of the HPA axis, dopaminergic system, and endogenous opioid systems, promoting the development of NSSI behavioral addiction, and driving its recurrent nature [51, 52]. These processes collectively reflect both automatic negative reinforcement and automatic positive reinforcement dimensions of Nock’s Four-Function Model [53]. Therefore, while previous research has established associations between short sleep duration and poor sleep quality with self-injury [47], this study identifies NRS as a risk factor for self-injury, suggesting that relieving NRS symptoms could be a promising approach to reducing self-injury or self-harm among adolescents.

This study confirmed previous findings from Korea that higher levels of NRS symptoms (i.e., lower NRSS scores) were associated with stronger suicidal ideation [32] and sleep disturbance prospectively predicted suicidal thoughts and behaviors revealed by one previous systematic review that included 41 longitudinal studies [14]. According to the Three-Step Theory of Suicide, when individuals suffer from a combination of pain and hopelessness, suicidal desire occurs [54]. Chinese adolescents are under heavy academic pressure and are susceptible to limited sleep, which may aggravate psychological pain and cause hopelessness, especially under academic failure or adverse family environments [55]. On the other hand, according to the conceptual model of the association of sleep disturbance and suicidality, heavy academic pressure and nonrestorative feelings may serve as life stress and impact mood disturbance, which induces HPA axis dysregulation and amplifies suicide risk [20]. NRS might trigger the activation of inflammatory processes and impair frontal lobe cognitive function, which were related to suicide [14, 20]. Peripheral cytokines may generate neuroinflammatory responses, and these aberrations in neuroinflammation produced by sleep disruption may increase suicide risk through impairing executive control, which shows particular vulnerability to sleep deprivation, exhibiting both the earliest emergence and greatest magnitude of cognitive impairment [20]. It was supported that the prefrontal cortex of suicide decedents expressed heightened pro-inflammatory cytokines [56]. NRS could also be comorbid with various physical and mental symptoms, further elevating the risk of suicidal ideation [57]. Therefore, NRS of adolescents should be considered in future health screenings to reduce the risk of suicidal ideation.

While reduced NRS symptoms predicted less suicidal ideation, it was not associated with suicide attempts in this study. The transition from suicidal ideation to suicide attempts is influenced by multiple factors. The Three-Step Theory of Suicide suggests that an actual suicide attempt occurs when suicidal desire intensifies (i.e., pain exceeds or overwhelms connectedness) and when the individual has the requisite capability for suicide, which includes acquired capability, dispositional capability, and practical capability [54]. Connectedness, such as social support, may mitigate the impact of NRS and make life worth living [58]. The relatively positive family functioning reported by the adolescents (i.e., scoring 6.14 out of 10) in this study could have helped mitigate the adverse effects of NRS, preventing it from surpassing their ability to cope. Moreover, although NRS can cause stress and emotional distress, the adolescents in this study may not have developed sufficient suicidal capabilities to translate this distress into self-harm behaviors [59]. According to the Interpersonal Psychological Theory of Suicide, adolescents might not be able to overcome innate fears of death because of their relative inexperience with life adversities [60]. Lastly, personality characteristics, genetic factors, and restricted access to lethal means might collectively contribute to reduced suicide attempt risk [54]. However, the lack of measurements for suicidal capability in this study limits its ability to draw definitive causal conclusions. Future research could further investigate the influence of suicidal capability on the progression from NRS and suicidal ideation to actual suicide attempts among adolescents.

The associations between NRS and self-injury and suicide attempts reached statistical significance exclusively among female adolescents, with no significant associations observed in male participants. This gender-dependent pattern of NRS effects aligns with prior research demonstrating differential associations between NRS and health-related quality of life across sexes [36]. A potential explanation for this could be that female adolescents demonstrate greater vulnerability to adverse outcomes [13] and higher susceptibility to different kinds of sleep problems compared to their male counterparts [61]. One previous systematic review and meta-analysis of longitudinal studies revealed that while adolescent males and females share numerous common risk factors for suicidal behaviors, gender-specific risk factors for suicide attempts and deaths were also identified [62]. Our study added NRS as a risk factor for self-injury, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts, especially in female groups, which would implicate future interventions that could help. However, our study was limited by a relatively small sample size (N = 322) and low prevalence rates of self-injury, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts. Future research would benefit from larger population-based samples to enhance statistical power and generalizability.

Lastly, the ROC analysis demonstrated that NRS showed acceptable discriminative ability for both self-injury (AUC = 0.763) and suicidal ideation (AUC = 0.752) [63]. The optimal clinical threshold was identified as 36.5 points on the NRSS (possible range: 12–60), serving as a screening indicator of the risk of self-injury, suicidal ideation, or suicide attempts. Therefore, public health practitioners, adolescent sleep specialists, and school health professionals should incorporate NRS screening into adolescent health assessments to enable early identification and prevention of associated risks. However, the model for suicide attempt prediction yielded an AUC below 0.7, indicating the limited discriminatory power of NRS, and NRS might not be suitable for predicting suicide attempts. Moreover, in this study, the prevalence of self-injury behavior, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts were 9.3%, 12.7%, and 5.9%, respectively, which was relatively lower than some previous studies (e.g., 24.7% and 15.4%) [64, 65]. It might be because we only asked for a length of four months instead of 12 months or without a specific duration [7]. Nevertheless, the prevalence was also high from the perspective of protecting the next generation of the world. Furthermore, despite the high prevalence of self-injury or suicide, only about 1:28 young males and 1:18 young females who did self-harm sought help from the hospital [66]. This situation reveals that self-harm and suicide in children and adolescents are not being properly addressed within community settings, highlighting the importance of public attention in response to the increasing global trends of self-injury and suicide [13].

Several study limitations were still worth noting. First, this study only used one single self-reported item to measure self-injury, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts, which would impact the robustness of the results. The single item might result in misclassification of the vulnerable groups, such as overestimated prevalence, since people might not clearly understand the definition of suicidal ideation, and then influence statistical power and dilute the observed effect size [67]. Future studies should adopt validated multi-item tools, such as the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale, for more robust outcome measurement [68]. Second, despite that the sample size is adequate considering the rule of thumb for sample size, a larger sample size is desirable to verify the robustness of the models. For example, given the small absolute number of suicide attempts (N = 19), our study may have constrained the statistical power to identify significant associations in regression models [69]. The reduction in events per variable directly leads to coefficient bias and extreme maximum likelihood estimates, while low outcome prevalence rates compromise model convergence and diminish the precision of effect size estimates [69]. To substantiate these findings, future research designs should prioritize either larger sample sizes or enriched cohorts specifically targeting high-risk populations to improve estimation precision. Third, potential factors such as bullying and sexual abuse that have been shown to be associated with such outcomes could be incorporated into future studies. Fourth, we only include two waves with a length of four months. However, considering the instability of sleep disorders and suicide, the shorter follow-up period might provide more accurate measurements [19]. To explore the potential mediators and the regulation mechanism, further follow-up could be conducted to include more waves with an adequate time duration. Lastly, the exact timing of suicidal ideation and attempts was not available in the current dataset. Future studies should incorporate precise temporal data to enable survival analyses using Cox proportional hazards models, which would provide a more robust examination of temporal relationships between risk factors and suicidal outcomes. Nevertheless, we used one standardized measurement to evaluate NRS, which could depict the NRS condition in more detail and give a quantitative assessment of NRS. Moreover, this study adopted the longitudinal design with the adjustment of important confounding factors such as health conditions, stress, depression, and family support, enhancing the robustness of the evidence by considering temporal precedence and confounding effects.

Conclusion

This longitudinal study highlights that NRS predicts increased risks of self-injury behavior, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts, with these associations exhibiting notable gender-specific variations. Targeted interventions to reduce NRS symptoms may also help decrease the risk of self-injury and suicide and enhance the overall health of adolescents.
