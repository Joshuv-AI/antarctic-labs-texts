Incorporating patient preference into as needed pain orders at a large academic health system

Background

Opioid stewardship programs are key aspects of patient care that can improve patient outcomes.1 One component of these programs, which is imperative for health care organizations, involves incorporating patient preference into pain management practices.2 The Joint Commission (TJC) has highlighted circumstances where “as-needed” (PRN) medication orders may be written to allow administration of a lesser potency pain medication when the patients' reported pain score calls for a higher-potency medication.2 Organizations must ensure that policies and procedures to capture this practice are in place. To respond to the need for opioid stewardship, the Yale New Haven Health System (YNHHS) created the Opioid Stewardship Committee. This committee was created to be an interdisciplinary advisory team to help ensure the safe, effective, and responsible use of opioids to manage pain while minimizing the risks of misuse, addiction, and harm.

In 2019, TJC surveyed multiple hospitals within the Yale New Haven Health System (YNHHS). YNHHS is a large academic medical center comprised of five hospitals, 2681 licensed beds, and sees approximately 153,058 inpatient cases yearly. The TJC surveys conducted on the health system revealed, during chart reviews, that patients often requested lower doses and/or lower potencies of PRN pain medications than those ordered for their reported pain score. Existing PRN pain medication orders in the electronic health record (EHR) and patient care policies lacked clear guidance on how to appropriately document and administer a lower potency PRN medication when requested by the patient. This resulted in the potential for PRN pain medications to be given without a proper order or incurring the risk of a delay in care while waiting for a new medication order to be placed by a clinician. The YNHHS Opioid Stewardship Committee identified an opportunity to enhance patient care and reduce opioid consumption by incorporating patient preference into pain medication orders.

Methods

This project followed the requirements set forth by TJC to seamlessly integrate patient preferences into the health system EHR. Following the 2019 Joint Commission surveys, the YNHHS Opioid Stewardship Committee formed a multidisciplinary team to identify a mechanism to address patient requests for lower-dose/lower-potency pain medications. This team included clinicians, nurses, pharmacists, and informatics analysts across YNHHS. Joint Commission Standards, along with Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) and state requirements for medication orders, were reviewed and were the basis for this initiative. Two Joint Commission Standards that provided project guidance were PC.01.02.07 (the hospital manages and assesses patient pain while minimizing risks) and MM.04.01.01 (medication orders are clear and accurate).3,4 Additionally, guiding principles for this project were adopted from the Joint Commission's Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) topic ‘Medication Administration – Incorporating Patient Preference Into Medication Administration Practices’.2 These included: ensuring pain medication orders were written in a manner that supported deferring to patient preference when a patient requests a lesser dose, potency, or less invasive route of administration; administration documentation reflecting those doses given per patient preference; inclusion of patient preference language in the medication orders does not result in therapeutic duplication and/or higher dose/stronger potency pain medications are not given without a proper order; and that utilization of patient preference language is incorporated into the organization's pain management policy.2 Adult inpatient and emergency department populations were included, while outpatient pain medication orders were excluded, as those are written as prescriptions for patients to take at home. Pediatric pain medication orders were also excluded.

Based on this review, a proposal for incorporating patient preference language into PRN pain medication orders was vetted and endorsed by multiple key stakeholders from the YNHHS Opioid Stewardship, Nursing Professional Governance, and Informatics Leadership committees. Implementing the proposal involved modifying the existing PRN pain orders and Medication Administration Record (MAR) and updating the YNHHS Pain Assessment and Management policy. Education was developed and shared with clinicians, nursing, and pharmacy teams, along with a Tips and Tricks document for this newly developed workflow.

Results

The multidisciplinary team first identified the scope of the work effort needed to modify the PRN pain medication orders. Next, existing PRN pain medication orders were reviewed. Initial reviews of utilization data over a one-month period showed that lower doses of pain medications were most often administered per patient preference in the maternity population, which comprised approximately 30 % of the total initial discovery data. Post-surgical floors, including orthopedics, and medical floors followed in utilization of patient preference.

The YNHHS PRN pain medication orders utilize the Pain Intensity Scale (PIS) for pain score documentation, with indications for mild pain (1–3), moderate pain (4–6), or severe pain (7–10). These orders give nurses the ability to administer pain medications based on the patient's reported pain score. The phrase “may also give for a higher pain score per patient preference” was added to the order instructions for mild and moderate PRN pain medication doses to provide guidance when a patient requests a lower dose or less potent pain medication. Similar verbiage was intentionally not added to the orders for severe pain, as a new order is required when a patient requests a higher dose or more potent medication. This aligns with Joint Commission guidance on deferment to patient preference for a “lesser” dose, potency, or invasive route of administration. A field was also added to the MAR documentation screen, which allows nursing to indicate whether the medication dose was given per patient preference. This allows nurses to document in the EHR when a lower potency PRN pain medication is given for a pain score that is higher than the medication order.

The YNHHS Pain Assessment and Management policy was updated to include requirements for giving PRN pain medications per patient preference with the following guidance: “Upon patient request and with a current clinician order, a lower potency or lower dose PRN pain medication may be administered.” The policy also reflects that patient requests for the lower potency or lower dose must be documented in the MAR.

During the development phase, it was identified that by modifying the current PRN pain scale options to incorporate the new language, this would alter all the reasoning for historical order entries. Utilization was tracked 3 months post-implementation to look for ordering trends and orders with old PRN reasons still in use. When test reports were run, they displayed the updated value despite having been ordered before the go-live date. Thus, there would be no delineation of transition between the old language orders and the updated versions. To address this concern, it was decided to use a three-step approach in the rollout of these changes. By doing this stepwise approach, the team was able to troubleshoot any issues before the final go-live on 1/2/2023.

Between January 2021 to December 2023, approximately two million orders were assessed to see which PRN verbiage was selected upon order signing. Outpatient, pediatric, and orders for breakthrough pain were excluded from the data, as those orders were not included in the initial scope of the project.

The new patient preference reasons were introduced in “Phase 1”, coexisted with the original PRN reasons in “Phase 2”, and ultimately, the old reasons were retired and comprehensively replaced with the new verbiage in “Phase 3”. Following the final go-live, new orders using the retired verbiage decreased to zero. Discussion

Although these standards were outlined by TJC at the time this project was implemented, the initial literature search conducted to identify methods of implementation found no existing studies addressing how to implement patient preference language within a large health system into pain PRN orders.

This intervention was successfully implemented at a large academic health center due to the interdisciplinary efforts of the Opioid Stewardship Committee. By utilizing a team-based approach, the committee was able to ensure that patient preference language was incorporated in a clear and concise manner and optimized the workflow for clinicians. While not a goal of the project, interdisciplinary stewardship efforts were critical for success. Similar academic medical centers with electronic medical records (EMRs) may be able to replicate this method of patient preference implementation. However, even at institutions with similar EMRs, differences in configuration may require additional steps for implementation. While adoption may be more challenging in under-resourced countries, in centers without electronic records, or in areas where nurses cannot document and administer medications based on patient preferences, the core concept can still be implemented—so long as patient preference remains central to opioid stewardship management.

There are some limitations related to data collection for this project. Nursing documentation of the patient's preference, while encouraged, is not a required field in the MAR. Nurses can document patient pain level in the MAR or flowsheet for each administration of PRN medication if they choose to do so. Each nurse may also approach the discussion about pain medication preferences with patients differently, which introduces variability. In the future, standardizing how preferences are documented, implementing a nursing script could help reduce confounding variables. Additionally, pain medication order sets allow for clinician “free text,” enabling providers to enter custom instructions that may omit standardized patient preference language.

Another limitation is that patient satisfaction with the preference-based approach was not objectively measured. No patient surveys were collected to assess perceptions of this intervention, and while anecdotal feedback from nurses was generally positive, it does not provide measurable outcomes. As the primary goal of this intervention was to support compliance with Joint Commission standards, future evaluations should include patient-reported outcomes to better understand the impact of preference-based PRN ordering from the patient and provider perspectives.

It is anticipated that the addition of the new verbiage will continue to impact pain management practices at YNHHS. This intervention remains in place and will be reviewed for optimization based on feedback and adherence data.

Conclusions

The project successfully enhanced the utilization of as-needed pain medication orders by incorporating patient preference options into PRN pain medication orders for adult patients. By adding in “may also give for higher pain score per patient preference,” YNHHS was able to empower patients with decisions involving their pain management and increase patient satisfaction, while maintaining patient safety. This verbiage helps break down the barrier for policymakers to incorporate patient involvement, making it easier for them to request medications they are more comfortable with. Anecdotal feedback from clinicians and nursing staff was positive. This project can be replicated by other health systems that seek to decrease opioid utilization and improve patient experience.
