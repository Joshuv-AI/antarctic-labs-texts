Ultrasound-assisted ionic extraction modulates the structural, interfacial, and digestive properties of Protaetia brevitarsis larval proteins
Authors: Yea-Ji Kim, Ji Yoon Cha, Jeong-Heon Kim, Dong Hyun Keum, Hae Won Jang, Yun-Sang Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study compared proteins from Protaetia brevitarsis larvae (PBP) extracted using water or a salt solution, with and without ultrasonication. Water extraction combined with ultrasonication (DW-US) resulted in the highest extraction yield and protein solubility at pH 6 and above. Ultrasonication significantly increased the mean particle diameter (p < 0.05). Surface hydrophobicity, reflecting the exposure of hydrophobic groups, was highest (468.58 RFU) in salt-solution extraction with ultrasonication (NaCl-US), whereas DW-US showed the lowest value (396.10 RFU). NaCl-US exhibited superior foaming capacity (158.33%), foam stability (118.33%), and emulsion stability (83.35%), whereas DW-US did not differ significantly from water extraction without ultrasonication. However, the emulsifying capacity of NaCl-US (0.103 Abs500) was lower than that of the other PBPs (0.206–0.214 Abs500), possibly because of its excessive foaming capacity. NaCl-US showed the highest in vitro digestibility. Overall, ultrasonication enhanced PBP extraction in water, whereas salt-solution extraction combined with ultrasonication improved its functional properties.

Introduction

With the rise in the global population and increasing environmental pressures, the demand for sustainable and nutritionally adequate alternatives to conventional animal-based proteins has become more urgent than ever (Gil et al., 2024; Kim, Kim, Cha, et al., 2024). Sustainable and functional materials have also been widely investigated in various fields for environmental remediation (Ganesamurthi et al., 2022; Su et al., 2023). Among the available alternatives, edible insects have emerged as a promising solution because of their high protein content, well-balanced amino acid profiles, efficient feed conversion, and significantly lower environmental footprint compared with traditional livestock systems (Choi et al., 2024; Choi et al., 2026). Protaetia brevitarsis seulensis, commonly known as the white-spotted flower chafer, has gained attention in East Asia owing to its historical use in traditional medicine and its potential as a high-value protein source (Kim et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2023). This species is rich in essential amino acids and exhibits biofunctional properties that support its use in food and nutraceutical formulations (Ham et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2026). However, the efficient extraction and preservation of these bioactive proteins remain significant challenges because insect biomass has a complex composition that includes recalcitrant components such as chitin, lipids, and phenolic compounds, which interfere with protein isolation (Kang et al., 2026; Khayrova et al., 2024).

Traditional extraction methods using organic solvents or acidic/alkaline treatments have been employed to recover insect proteins (Khayrova et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2019). Although effective in terms of yield, these methods often involve harsh chemical conditions that may denature proteins, compromise their functional integrity, and raise environmental concerns. Therefore, there is increasing interest in nonthermal and green extraction technologies that can enhance recovery efficiency while minimizing structural damage. Adjusting the ionic strength with salt can facilitate the extraction of muscle proteins from insects. These muscle proteins are particularly suitable substitutes for animal-based proteins. However, unlike proteins from conventional livestock, insect proteins are embedded within complex matrices of cuticular proteins, which complicates their extraction and necessitates more effective extraction strategies.

Ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) has emerged as a promising sonochemical approach that uses acoustic cavitation to enhance mass transfer, disrupt cell walls, and facilitate protein release under mild processing conditions (Khadhraoui et al., 2021). UAE improves both the extraction yield and the functional quality of bioactive compounds from various plant and animal sources by preserving their native conformations and functional groups (Zia et al., 2022). Additionally, the cavitation effect of ultrasound may be influenced by the physical properties of extraction solvents, such as viscosity, surface tension, and ionic strength (Sethi & Rathod, 2025). A previous study reported that oleosin, a protein located on the surface of plant seed oil bodies, exhibited improved extraction yield and functional properties through the combined use of salt extraction and UAE (Sun et al., 2022). Despite this, limited attention has been given to the synergistic effects of ultrasound and ionic strength on proteins from P. brevitarsis seulensis and other edible insects.

Therefore, this study hypothesized that the combination of ionic strength and ultrasonication would enhance the extractability and improve the functional properties of P. brevitarsis seulensis proteins by promoting protein release and structural modification. A comprehensive evaluation of extraction yield, structural properties, protein functionality, and nutritional properties was conducted by comparing water and salt extraction with and without UAE. By bridging sonochemical principles with sustainable protein technology, this study sought to identify optimized extraction strategies for the development of functional insect-based protein ingredients.

Materials and methods

Edible insect preparation and defatting process

Protaetia brevitarsis seulensis larvae were procured from a commercial supplier (Farm Bang, Jeongeup, Republic of Korea). The larvae were washed thoroughly and then freeze-dried to remove moisture. The lyophilized samples were ground using a mixer (JL-1000, HiBell, Hwaseong, Korea) for 1 min. Compositional analysis of the lyophilized samples revealed the following proximate values: moisture, 4.00 ± 0.05%; crude protein, 61.08 ± 0.40%; lipid, 21.18 ± 0.18%; and mineral ash, 5.24 ± 0.15%. Lipids were removed by solvent extraction according to a previously reported method (Kim et al., 2021). Ground insect biomass (200 g) was treated with n-hexane (1 L; Daejung Chemical Co., Busan, Korea) at a sample-to-solvent ratio of 1:5 (w/v). The mixture was continuously stirred at ambient temperature (20 °C) for 60 min using a magnetic stirrer at 300 rpm (MS300HS, Misung Scientific, Yangju, Korea). The extraction was repeated five times until the residual lipid content was less than 1.0%. Subsequently, residual hexane was eliminated by overnight evaporation (18 h) at 20 °C under controlled ventilation. The resulting defatted powder was stored at −20 °C until further processing.

Protein isolation procedures

Two distinct extraction approaches were used to isolate different protein fractions from the defatted P. brevitarsis seulensis matrix (Kim et al., 2019). Water-extractable proteins were obtained using chilled distilled water (DW, 4 °C) with a 2 h extraction period, while salt-extractable proteins were recovered using sodium chloride solution (0.6 M, pH 7.0) under identical temperature and time conditions. The pH of the sodium chloride solution was adjusted to 7.0 to maintain neutral extraction conditions and minimize excessive pH-induced protein modification. Extraction was conducted by stirring the defatted powder in the respective extraction medium at a 1:10 (w/v) ratio using a magnetic stirrer at 300 rpm (MS300HS, Misung Scientific). The extracts were separated by centrifugation at 4000g for 15 min at 2 °C using a centrifuge (Combi 514R, Hanil Scientific, Gimpo, Korea). The acceleration (ACC) and deceleration (DEC) rates were set to step 5 on the manufacturer's 10-step ACC/DEC scale. After centrifugation, the supernatants were filtered through Whatman No. 4 filter paper (20–25 μm particle retention) to remove insoluble residues. The clarified supernatants were used as protein extracts without pH modification, following established protocols (Kim et al., 2019). This procedure yielded two primary protein solutions: water-extractable and salt-extractable (NaCl) fractions. Ultrasonic treatment (US) was applied to both the aqueous (DW-US) and saline (NaCl-US) extraction systems to enhance extraction efficiency and protein functionality. The extraction conditions were selected based on previous studies and our preliminary experiments (Huang et al., 2023; Resendiz-Vazquez et al., 2017). The US conditions were as follows: frequency, 20 kHz; power, 400 W; treatment time, 2 h at 4 °C in pulse mode (10 s on/10 s off), using a 25 mm probe-type ultrasonic processor (VCX 750, Sonics & Materials, Newtown, CT, USA). The probe tip was positioned approximately at the midpoint of the sample solution. Samples were sonicated in a water-jacketed vessel connected to a refrigerated circulator (RBC-9, LAB-House, Pocheon, Korea) to maintain the temperature during sonication. These ultrasound-assisted procedures utilized identical sample-to-buffer ratios and the aforementioned extraction method.

Proximate composition

Proximate composition was determined according to the standardized analytical procedures of the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC, 1990). Moisture content (AOAC 950.46B) was determined by gravimetric analysis after drying at 105 °C for 16 h using a drying oven (SW-90D; Sang Woo Scientific Co., Bucheon, Republic of Korea). Lipid content (AOAC 960.69) was determined by automated Soxhlet extraction (Soxtec® Avanti 2050; Foss Tecator AB, Höganäs, Sweden). Protein concentration (AOAC 981.10) was determined through Kjeldahl nitrogen analysis using automated instrumentation (Kjeltec® 2300; Foss Tecator AB, Höganäs, Sweden). Ash content was determined according to AOAC method 920.153.

pH and color

The pH values were recorded using a calibrated potentiometric instrument (Model 340 pH meter; Mettler Toledo GmbH, Schwerzenbach, Switzerland) following standard electrode procedures. Instrumental color assessment of the protein solutions was performed using a colorimeter (CR-410 Chroma Meter; Konica Minolta, Osaka, Japan). System calibration was performed using a standard white reference plate with the following established parameters: CIE L* value of 97.83, CIE a* value of −0.043, and CIE b* value of 1.98, operating under a 2° observer configuration.

Amino acid and protein quality

Complete amino acid hydrolysis was performed by acid treatment under controlled conditions. Protein extracts (0.5 mL at a 1% concentration) were mixed with 6 M hydrochloric acid (10 mL) in sealed glass ampoules (20 mL capacity). Hydrolysis was conducted under a nitrogen atmosphere at 105 °C for 24 h using a heating chamber (SW-90D; Sang Woo Scientific Co., Bucheon, Republic of Korea). After hydrolysis, the samples were dried under vacuum at 40 °C until completely dry. The dried hydrolysates were reconstituted in 0.02 M HCl (3–5 mL) for chromatographic analysis. Amino acid quantification was performed using an automated ion-exchange chromatograph (L-8800 Amino Acid Analyzer; Hitachi High-Technologies, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a specialized separation column (4.6 mm internal diameter × 60 mm length). Sample preparation included membrane filtration (0.20 μm pore size; Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) before analysis. Certified amino acid standards (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were used as quantitative references.

Extraction yield

The extraction yield of proteins from edible insects was calculated to evaluate the efficiency of UAE according to a previous study (Yue et al., 2021). In the aforementioned extraction process, 2 g of insect powder was used, and after the extraction, the liquid extract was dried in an oven at 105 °C for 24 h to obtain the solid weight. The ratio of the extracted solid weight to the initial insect powder weight was considered the extraction yield.

Protein solubility at different pH levels

Protein solubility was evaluated according to a previous study (Han et al., 2025). The proteins were diluted to 2 mg/mL with each extraction solvent, and the pH of the solution was adjusted from 2 to 10 using 1 M NaOH or 1 M HCl. The samples were centrifuged at 8000g for 15 min to remove the insoluble parts. The protein content in the supernatant at each pH was determined by the BCA protein assay with bovine serum albumin (BSA) as a standard.

Sulfhydryl content

The sulfhydryl content in the proteins was evaluated using Ellman's reagent [5,5′-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid)] and methods from previous studies (Lee, Jo, et al., 2021). To determine the total sulfhydryl content, each protein solution (1 mg/mL, 0.25 mL) was mixed with a denaturing buffer (1 mL) containing 8 M urea and 3% SDS in 0.1 M potassium phosphate (pH 7.4). Then, Ellman's reagent (10 mM, 0.25 mL) was allowed to react with the mixture for 15 min at 25 °C, and the absorbance of the reactant was measured at 412 nm. The absorbance of a blank sample without Ellman's reagent was subtracted from the absorbance of the reactant, and the sulfhydryl content was calculated according to the Beer–Lambert law using a molar extinction coefficient of 14,150 M−1 cm−1. For determining the free sulfhydryl content, 0.1 M potassium phosphate (pH 7.4) was used instead of the denaturing buffer. The total sulfhydryl content and free sulfhydryl content are given by Eqs. (1), (2), respectively:(1)Totalsulfhydrylcontentμmol/mgprotein=A1∕14150×L×P(2)Freesulfhydrylcontentμmol/mgprotein=A2∕14150×L×Pwhere A1 and A2 are the absorbances of the mixture prepared in the denaturing buffer and in 0.1 M potassium phosphate (pH 7.4), respectively; L is the path length (cm); and P is the amount of protein in the reactant (mg).

Particle size and zeta potential

The particle size and zeta potential of the P. brevitarsis seulensis proteins were determined using dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis (Nano-ZS Zetasizer; Malvern Panalytical, Worcestershire, United Kingdom). The proteins in each solvent were prepared at 1 mg/mL. Zeta potential was measured at a controlled temperature (25 °C) using laser illumination (633 nm wavelength) with detection at a 90° scattering angle. These measurements provided insights into the surface charge characteristics and colloidal stability of the protein dispersions.

Surface hydrophobicity

The surface hydrophobicity of the extracted proteins was assessed using 1-anilino-8-naphthalene sulfonate (ANS), as described in a previous study (Zhao et al., 2022), with minor modifications. The protein samples were diluted in each extraction medium to obtain a concentration of 0.1–1.0 mg/mL. Then, 2 mL of each protein sample was allowed to react with 20 μL of 8.0 mM ANS in 10 mM potassium phosphate (pH 7.4) for 3 min at 25 °C. The fluorescence of the reactant was measured at 390 nm excitation and 470 nm emission, and the surface hydrophobicity was determined as the initial slope of fluorescence intensity against protein concentration.

Sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE)

The protein molecular weight distribution was examined using PAGE under denaturing conditions. Protein concentrations were determined using the Bradford colorimetric assay (Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co., St. Louis, MO, USA). The electrophoretic protocol followed an established methodology, with some modifications (Lee, Kim, et al., 2024). Protein samples (20 μg) were prepared by combining with a sample loading buffer (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., CA, USA) at a ratio of 1:3. Heat denaturation was conducted at 100 °C for 5 min prior to electrophoretic separation. Protein migration was conducted using 4%–20% polyacrylamide resolving gels under SDS conditions. Post-separation visualization was performed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue R250 staining (Bio-Rad). Molecular weight was determined using standard protein markers (Bio-Rad).

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)

Structural characterization of the P. brevitarsis seulensis protein extracts was performed using FT-IR spectroscopy (FT/IR-4100 type A; JASCO Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The protein solutions were lyophilized for 72 h, and the spectra of the protein powder were acquired across the mid-infrared region (600–4000 cm−1) at a resolution of 1 cm−1. This analytical approach enabled the identification of characteristic molecular vibrations associated with protein secondary structures, functional groups, and intermolecular interactions within the extracted protein matrices.

Secondary structure analysis (circular dichroism spectroscopy)

The secondary structures of the proteins were analyzed by circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy (Chirascan VX; Applied Photophysics, Surrey, UK). Protein solutions were prepared in each solvent at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL, and 200 μL of each solution was loaded into a quartz cuvette with a 1 mm path length. CD spectra were recorded over the wavelength range of 190–260 nm, and the average of duplicate scans was used to obtain a single spectrum. The relative proportion of secondary structure components (α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn, and random coil) was estimated using CDNN software (Version 2.1, Applied Photophysics).

Foaming capacity and foam stability

The foaming characteristics were evaluated using standardized protein concentrations (1 mg/mL). Sample aliquots (10 mL) were transferred to graduated conical vessels (50 mL capacity) for high-speed homogenization (12,000 rpm, 2 min) to generate foam structures. Foam capacity was calculated by comparing the initial and post-foaming volumes and expressed as a percentage increase, as shown in Eq. (3). Emulsifying capacity and emulsion stability

The emulsifying capacity assessment involved combining a 1 mg/mL protein solution (10 mL) with refined soybean oil (1 mL) under high-speed homogenization (15,000 rpm, 2 min). Following equilibration at ambient temperature (20 °C, 10 min), emulsion samples (50 μL) were diluted with SDS solution (10 mL, 0.3% w/v). Digested protein content

In vitro digestion

To simulate adult digestion under oral, gastric, and intestinal conditions, the enzyme levels, composition of digestive fluids, temperature, time, and motility were designed according to a previous study (Lee, Kang, et al., 2021). Enzymes and chemicals used in the analysis were of analytical grade and purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Saliva for oral digestion (pH 7.0) was prepared with 10.2 mM NaCl, 6.7 mM urea, 0.2 mM uric acid, 33 U/mL α-amylase, and 50 mg/mL mucin. Gastric juice (pH 1.5) was composed of 13 mM HCl, 5.4 mM CaCl2, 0.03 mM BSA, 2000 U/mL pepsin, and 6.0 mg/mL mucin. Duodenal juice (pH 8.0) was formulated using 15.1 mM KCl, 2.7 mM CaCl2, 0.03 mM BSA, 144 U/mL pancreatin (based on the protease unit), and 1500 U/mL lipase. Lastly, bile juice (pH 7.0) consisted of 137 mM NaHCO3, 3.0 mM CaCl2, 0.05 mM BSA, and 60 mg/mL bile extract. Enzyme activity under these conditions was confirmed using bovine serum albumin.

For in vitro digestion, insect protein solutions (5 mL) were allowed to react with 5 mL of saliva for 5 min, and 10 mL of gastric juice was subsequently added and allowed to react for 120 min. Then, 10 mL of duodenal juice and 5 mL of bile juice were added concurrently and allowed to react for 120 min. The samples were mixed with the digestion fluids in a shaking water bath (BS-21, Jeiotech, Daejeon, Korea) at 150 rpm and 37 °C. The extraction solvents of the insect proteins were used as blanks instead of the protein solutions. After digestion, the samples were immediately frozen at −70 °C.

α-Amino group content and 3 kDa fraction in protein digesta

The digested protein content was determined by measuring the α-amino group content and the 3 kDa protein fraction in the digesta according to the methods of Kim et al. (2025). The o-phthaldialdehyde (OPA) reagent, formulated with 50 mM sodium tetraborate, 10 mg/mL SDS, 0.8 mg/mL OPA, and 2 μL/mL β-mercaptoethanol, was freshly prepared. The sample digesta (200 μL) was mixed with 12% trichloroacetic acid (1 mL) by vortexing for 10 s, and the insoluble parts were removed by centrifugation at 2000g and 4 °C for 10 min. Next, the supernatant (150 μL) was allowed to react with OPA reagent (1 mL) for 2 min, and the absorbance of the reactant was measured at 340 nm. The blank digesta was also allowed to react with the OPA reagent, and its value was subtracted from that of the sample digesta. Various concentrations of glycine were used as standards for the α-amino group. To express the α-amino group content in proteins, the protein content in each digesta was measured using the Kjeldahl method and expressed as mM/g protein.

Proteins below 3 kDa after digestion were separated from the whole digesta using centrifugal filters with 10 and 3 kDa cut-offs sequentially. The protein content after filtration was measured using the Kjeldahl method and expressed as a percentage of the protein content in each digesta sample. As a blank, the digesta of the extraction solvent without sample proteins was also centrifuged, and the 3 kDa fraction was collected. The protein content of the blank before and after filtration was subtracted from the protein content of the sample digesta.

Statistical analyses

All experiments were performed using independent protein extracts prepared from separate extraction batches, and each measurement was conducted in triplicate. The quantified results were analyzed as mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance was performed using IBM SPSS statistical software (SPSS Ver. 20.0, IBM, IL, USA). The significance of the variations among the mean values was assessed using Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test with a confidence level of P < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Proximate composition and color

Table 1 presents the proximate composition and color parameters of Protaetia brevitarsis seulensis larval protein extracts as influenced by the type of extraction buffer and ultrasound treatment. The protein content was significantly increased in the DW-US, NaCl, and NaCl-US groups compared with that in DW (P < 0.05), with the highest value observed in the NaCl group (48.50%). These findings suggest that ionic strength and acoustic cavitation enhance protein release during extraction. In NaCl extraction, increased ionic strength may improve the solubilization of salt-soluble and cytoplasmic proteins by shielding charged groups on protein surfaces, thereby weakening electrostatic interactions between proteins and other cellular or matrix components. In ultrasound-assisted samples, acoustic cavitation promotes the mechanical disruption of the insect cellular matrix, thereby facilitating the release of intracellular proteins (Choi et al., 2017; Ni et al., 2024). The fat content was markedly reduced in all groups subjected to NaCl or sonication. This trend likely reflects the efficient separation of lipid components during salt extraction and UAE, supporting previous findings highlighting the defatting effects of both processes (Byanju et al., 2020). Ultrasound is widely used to extract proteins and bioactive components, and the results of this study are similar to those of previous studies in which the protein content increased, and the lipid content decreased when edible insects were extracted using ultrasound (Choi et al., 2017; Rahman et al., 2024). The ash and carbohydrate contents did not vary significantly. Although chitin-containing materials may be physically disrupted from the insect matrix and dispersed as fine chitin-containing particles or chitin-protein complexes by ultrasonication, chitin is generally insoluble in water and neutral salt solutions (Zhao et al., 2019). Color analysis revealed that the CIE L*, a*, and b* values were slightly altered by the treatments. The L* values showed minimal differences across groups, suggesting a limited impact on lightness. However, the a* value significantly decreased in all treatment groups compared with that in DW (P < 0.05), reflecting reduced redness. This reduction could be attributed to the disruption of pigment-protein interactions by sonication and ionic solubilization. The b* values were highest in NaCl-US, potentially owing to the exposure of chromogenic residues or Maillard reaction intermediates during processing. Overall, the changes in chromaticity indicated subtle structural modifications without drastic pigment degradation, supporting the maintenance of visual quality. Studies on color changes during sonication in protein extraction are limited, and ultrasonication during protein extraction can release more pigments owing to cavitation, resulting in color changes in protein isolates (Rahman et al., 2024). Therefore, although the extent of color change may vary depending on the raw material, ultrasound generally enhances pigment release and increases color intensity during protein extraction.

Amino acid and protein quality

The amino acid profiles of Protaetia brevitarsis seulensis larval proteins are presented in Table 2, depending on the extraction method, with and without ultrasonication, and using different buffer systems. Among the essential amino acids (EAAs), lysine and valine were predominant, consistent with the valuable protein-rich nature of insect larvae (Fuso et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2020). Notably, NaCl-US extracts exhibited higher lysine (74.82 mg/g), leucine (55.57 mg/g), and threonine (38.74 mg/g) contents than the other treatments, suggesting that the combined effect of ionic solubilization and ultrasonic disruption facilitates the release of these residues. This increase likely stems from enhanced unfolding of protein structures under ultrasonic cavitation, which exposes internal peptide bonds and improves amino acid recovery (Su & Cavaco-Paulo, 2021). A remarkable observation was the elevated methionine content in the DW-US group (6.40 mg/g), whereas the other treatments showed minimal values (≤ 0.79 mg/g). This may be related to selective solubilization during mild aqueous extraction with ultrasound, a phenomenon observed during protein processing under low-shear ultrasonic conditions (Timoumi et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2025). Non-essential amino acids (NEAAs), namely glutamic acid (Glu), proline (Pro), and aspartic acid (Asp) were predominant in all samples, with Glu and Asp contributing significantly to nutritional value and flavor enhancement. DW-US and NaCl-US showed significantly higher Asp contents (P < 0.05), suggesting that sonication facilitates the Asp-containing protein extraction by weakening interactions within protein matrices (Luo et al., 2022). Interestingly, arginine and serine contents were highest in the NaCl-US group, indicating more extensive protein breakdown and greater exposure of polar residues. However, glycine and proline levels were highest in the DW and NaCl groups, respectively, potentially reflecting less denatured or aggregated conformations. These differences imply that sonication intensity and solvent type interact to influence peptide bond cleavage patterns and side-chain exposure (Abedi & Pourmohammadi, 2021; Xu et al., 2024). The EAA index, which reflects the biological value of a protein relative to the WHO/FAO/UNU amino acid scoring pattern, was highest in the DW-US group (1.30), significantly exceeding those of the other groups (P < 0.05). This finding suggests that ultrasound-assisted water extraction may preserve or enhance the EAA balance more effectively than salt-based methods. Extraction yields and protein solubility

Extraction yield is an important parameter for assessing the efficiency of the extraction process and its feasibility for industrial application. As shown in Fig. 1A, the extraction yield of edible insect protein using DW was higher than that obtained using NaCl solution (P < 0.05). A previous study confirmed that the protein content extracted using water is higher than that extracted using salt solution across different types of edible insects (Kim et al., 2019). The protein extraction yield was markedly higher in the ultrasonication group, suggesting that both ionic strength and ultrasound facilitated solubilization by disrupting the protein matrices and weakening interactions with chitin or lipid constituents. According to a previous study, extraction yields of edible insects varied depending on ultrasonic treatment duration; the extracted protein amount increased exponentially after 5 min of ultrasonic treatment, after which it increased only slightly, with no statistically significant difference (Choi et al., 2017). The increase in protein extraction yield owing to sonication may be attributed to structural damage caused by extensive cell disruption, which releases cellular components into the aqueous solution (Byanju et al., 2020). Indeed, the extraction yield of myofibrillar proteins after ultrasonic treatment was higher than that of the control group and was highest at an ultrasonic power of 300 W (Ni et al., 2024). The pH-dependent solubility profile was analyzed to evaluate protein solubility under broad acidic, neutral, and alkaline conditions, and different trends were observed depending on the solvent and ultrasound treatment (Fig. 1B). The lowest solubility of water-soluble proteins was observed at pH 4, while that of salt-soluble proteins was at pH 3, consistent with their respective isoelectric points (pI). UAE generally enhanced protein solubility, and this effect was more pronounced in water extraction than in salt extraction across the pH range examined. Except for DW-US, the extracted proteins generally showed higher solubility at pH 6. DW-US exhibited maximum solubility at pH 2 while also showing the highest solubility among all treatments under alkaline conditions. The considerable decrease in protein solubility at the pI is attributable to the reduction in net surface charge and electrostatic repulsion, which promotes protein aggregation. The pI of proteins is determined by amino acid sequences and protein structure (Yi et al., 2017). Thus, the differences in minimum solubility pH between water-soluble and salt-soluble proteins may reflect differences in protein composition and isoelectric characteristics (Fang et al., 2023). The increase in protein solubility at pH values above the pI can be attributed to the deprotonation of acidic residues and partial unfolding that exposes charged groups (Qing et al., 2022). In particular, the relatively higher proportion of acidic residues, including Asp, observed in ultrasonically extracted proteins may contribute to the enhanced solubility above pH 6. Additionally, the increase in protein solubility by UAE may be attributed to the release of proteins previously entrapped in or associated with the insect matrix, similar to enzyme-assisted extraction (Ndiritu et al., 2019). The distinct increase in protein solubility at pH 2 in DW-US compared to the DW group also suggests a distinct protein composition attributable to the additional extraction of proteins. Meanwhile, a different trend was reported for water-soluble proteins from Patanga succinta L., in which the highest solubility was presented at pH 2 without UAE, whereas UAE decreased solubility at pH 2 (Kingwascharapong et al., 2021). This discrepancy can be related to the differences in protein composition and extractability between larvae and imago. In larvae, a considerable fraction of proteins originated from hemolymph proteins, which are generally more water-soluble and globular in structure, while imago contains relatively more salt-soluble myofibrillar proteins. These findings demonstrate that the protein composition of P. brevitarsis seulensis differs depending on the extraction solvent and UAE, thereby affecting protein solubility over a wide pH range.

Sulfhydryl content

The sulfhydryl content of the proteins is shown in Fig. 1C. The total sulfhydryl content was the highest in DW-extracted proteins; however, that in DW-US was the lowest (P < 0.05). The free sulfhydryl content of salt-soluble proteins was significantly decreased by sonication. While differences in sulfhydryl content among proteins were due to changes in amino acid composition associated with the extraction method, structural modifications of the proteins may also have contributed. As a chemical effect of ultrasound, cavitation generates hydroxyl radicals from solvents, which induce the oxidation of proteins, resulting in the loss of sulfhydryl groups (Kingwascharapong et al., 2021). Methionine, which increased significantly in DW-US, contains an S-CH3 functional group that does not affect the sulfhydryl content. However, because it is vulnerable to oxidation, the initial oxidation of methionine can induce a chain reaction that influences other oxidation-sensitive residues, such as cysteine (Domínguez et al., 2021), resulting in protein aggregation. In the case of proteins dissolved in salt solution, the electrostatic repulsion of proteins decreases owing to the high ionic strength, which increases the exposure of the hydrophobic residues contained within. Consequently, the free radicals generated during ultrasonic treatment easily react with these free sulfhydryl groups, promoting oxidation. Consequently, a decrease in sulfhydryl groups was induced by UAE in both extraction solvents, even though the main causes were different.

The zeta potential values further confirmed the effect of ionic strength (Table 3). The NaCl-treated samples (NaCl and NaCl-US) showed lower absolute zeta potential values (−9.56 to −9.24 mV) than DW-extracted proteins (−28.13 to −26.43 mV), indicating diminished electrostatic repulsion under saline conditions. Since zeta potential represents the apparent surface charge state of proteins, a reduction in absolute zeta potential generally implies potential colloidal instability and aggregation due to weakened repulsion between protein particles (Lee, Kim, et al., 2024). In water-soluble proteins, ultrasonication significantly reduced the absolute zeta potential, which may have contributed to the increased particle size by weakening electrostatic repulsion. In contrast, the particle size of salt-soluble proteins increased with ultrasonication without a further decrease in the absolute zeta potential value. Although ultrasound-induced partial unfolding can expose charged residues and induce interactions between proteins, changes in surface charge in salt solution can be screened by ions (Li et al., 2024). Taken together, particle enlargement by ultrasound in DW-US was accompanied by electrical destabilization, whereas additional electrostatic destabilization was not observed in NaCl-US, possibly due to ionic shielding.

Surface hydrophobicity is an important indicator of protein structural modification and also affects protein functionality (Tang, 2017). The results of surface hydrophobicity analysis demonstrated notably different effects of ionic strength and sonication (Table 3). Proteins extracted under ionic conditions showed higher surface hydrophobicity, while DW and DW-US treatments resulted in significantly lower values (P < 0.05). In addition, the surface hydrophobicity of salt-soluble proteins was enhanced by ultrasonication to the highest value (468.58), whereas that of water-soluble proteins was reduced to the lowest value (396.10). Under ionic conditions, structural loosening and exposure of non-polar amino acid residues can be induced (Li et al., 2024). Under these conditions, cavitation and shear stress generated by ultrasound readily unfold proteins (Jiang et al., 2014; Tang, 2017). Moreover, the reduced electrostatic repulsion and energy barrier for protein–protein contact under ionic conditions may increase the collision frequency among protein particles during ultrasonication. Therefore, the large protein assemblies observed in NaCl-US may represent labile and reactive protein aggregates composed of unfolded protein subunits, such that surface hydrophobicity and related functional properties may be improved. Meanwhile, the decrease in surface hydrophobicity of water-soluble proteins by ultrasonication may be attributable to oxidative structural modification, considering the low total sulfhydryl content observed in DW-US (Wang et al., 2022).

Protein structure characterization

SDS-PAGE, FT-IR spectra, and the secondary structures of P. brevitarsis seulensis larval proteins obtained using different extraction methods are presented in Fig. 3. SDS-PAGE reveals alterations in the molecular weight distribution resulting from the extraction solvent and ultrasound treatment (Fig. 3A). DW and DW-US samples retain a relatively high-intensity band at approximately 150 kDa, while NaCl and NaCl-US treatments exhibit an additional band at approximately 37 kDa, which can be attributed to salt-soluble tropomyosin (Ni et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Moreover, the intensity of the band at approximately 25 kDa is higher in the NaCl-US treatment than in the NaCl treatment. This protein is estimated to be a hemolymph protein, which is the most abundant protein, although a part of it may be insoluble (Mishyna et al., 2021). The increase in small proteins after UAE in salt solution can be attributed to an increase in additionally extracted proteins rather than fragmentation because P. brevitarsis seulensis larval proteins are highly stable even under high-energy treatment, such as radiation (Lee, Kim, et al., 2024). The above results show an increase in particle size after extraction with salt solution and sonication, despite an increase in the extraction of small proteins. The turbulent forces induced by sonication can form unstable aggregates with noncovalent bonds (Rahman & Lamsal, 2021). As SDS-PAGE was performed under reducing conditions, disulfide-linked polymerization could not be directly confirmed. However, disulfide-linked polymerization by ultrasonication is unlikely to occur because covalent bonds generally decrease protein solubility (He et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021) and cannot fully explain the increased surface hydrophobicity observed in NaCl-US. The FT-IR spectra in Fig. 3B show several peaks and differences among the insect proteins. The peak at 3290 cm−1 was associated with O—H and N—H stretching in the protein structure (Queiroz et al., 2021). It may also be related to chitin-containing components, considering the extracted carbohydrates, although water and salt solutions are not suitable for extracting chitin (da Silva Lucas et al., 2021). The peak at 2925–2931 cm−1 may be related to asymmetric stretching of the —CH2 bond (Kenawy et al., 2018), which reflects hydrophobic groups of protein side chains as well as residual lipid-related components. The peak at 1548–1589 cm−1 corresponded to the C—N stretching and N—H bending in amide II and that at 1394–1400 cm−1 represented COO− stretching in amino acid side chains (Cakmak-Arslan et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021). The peak at 1548 cm−1 in DW-extracted proteins shifted to 1589 cm−1 after extraction in salt solution, suggesting that NaCl extraction altered the local environment of peptide bonds and protein conformation. Alteration of the amide peak is closely related to protein secondary structure modification (Sazonova et al., 2019), and the shift of the peak to a higher wavenumber is related to protein structural unfolding (Li et al., 2020). Therefore, extraction with a salt solution induces protein unfolding. Meanwhile, the peak at 1394–1400 cm−1 showed decreased intensity in salt-soluble proteins, indicating that the amino acid side chains formed a unique complex compared to water-soluble proteins (Xiang et al., 2018). The peak at 1036–1045 cm−1 corresponded to C—O stretching in carbohydrates (Cakmak-Arslan et al., 2020), and differences in peak intensity were noticeable between the extraction solvents. This result is likely due to differences in the type and structure of the extracted polysaccharides. Consequently, the molecular structure of the protein extracts was mainly influenced by the extraction solvent.

Changes in secondary structure are essential for interpreting the conformational rearrangement of proteins, which can be related to protein flexibility. The secondary structural alterations of P. brevitarsis seulensis larvae are demonstrated in Fig. 3C. Only the NaCl-US group showed significant differences in the β-sheet and random coil structure ratios, with the random coil structure decreasing and the β-sheet ratio significantly increasing compared with those of the other proteins (P < 0.05). The ratio of the α-helix structure was lowest in NaCl-US, although the difference was not significant (P > 0.05). These alterations in secondary structure represent protein unfolding and intensive rearrangement of muscle proteins, which are salt-soluble, into a more ordered structure (Lee, Jo, et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2022). Particularly under high-ionic-strength conditions, cavitation-induced structural unfolding was promoted, and substantial changes could be induced. According to a previous study, a low α-helix ratio is related to high intramolecular flexibility and increased protease susceptibility, whereas an increase in the β-sheet ratio may obstruct intermolecular interactions (Zhu et al., 2020). Moreover, in DW without ionic shielding, ultrasonication alone was insufficient to induce notable structural modifications, although ultrasonication of water-soluble proteins induced oxidation of protein residues. In summary, UAE altered the secondary structure of P. brevitarsis seulensis larval proteins, resulting in increased flexibility only in a high-ionic-strength environment.

Foaming and emulsifying functionalities

The foaming capacity and foam stability of the edible insect proteins, depending on the extraction method, are shown in Table 4. The highest values were observed in the NaCl-US group, in which ultrasonication considerably enhanced foaming properties in the presence of NaCl. In contrast, no significant difference was found between the DW and DW-US groups (P > 0.05). These results clearly indicate that the effect of UAE is solvent-dependent, even with respect to the functional properties of proteins. Foam formation and stability are primarily affected by protein surface hydrophobicity, the location of hydrophobic amino acid residues, and the influence of ions in solution (Zielińska et al., 2018). In NaCl solution, the high ionic strength reduces electrostatic repulsion among protein molecules, allowing them to approach each other more closely (Chen et al., 2025). Under these conditions, proteins can be more susceptible to ultrasonic stress, as intermolecular contacts impose structural strain and reduce conformational stability. Thus, ultrasonic cavitation during extraction provides sufficient mechanical stress to disrupt intramolecular interactions, thereby facilitating partial unfolding and the exposure of hydrophobic residues. This structural change, as confirmed by the above results, can enhance the ability of proteins to adsorb at the air-water interface. Meanwhile, partial unfolding by physical energy and the consequent enhancement of foaming properties by ultrasound are predictable, but the effects may vary depending on the treatment timing. The emulsion properties of water-soluble proteins were not affected by UAE, whereas the emulsifying capacity (Abs500) was significantly reduced in the NaCl-US group (0.103), despite its superior foaming ability (Table 4). Compared with foam, oil droplets have higher viscosity and continuity with low interfacial tension, requiring rapid adsorption at the interface (Walstra & De Roos, 1993). To enhance emulsifying properties, the particle size of aggregates with partially unfolded proteins is important for adsorption at the oil–water interface, as is protein flexibility (Tang, 2017). In addition, the low electrostatic repulsion of unfolded proteins facilitates protein–protein aggregation during homogenization for emulsification rather than effective adsorption onto the oil–water interface (McClements, 2004). Thus, the emulsifying capacity of NaCl-US, which had a large particle size under conditions of low electrostatic repulsion, may decrease despite its high surface hydrophobicity and protein flexibility. Differences between foaming and emulsifying capacity have also been reported in Acheta domesticus proteins pretreated with pulsed electric field treatment (Psarianos et al., 2022). Nevertheless, NaCl-US proteins exhibited the highest emulsion stability (83.35%) among the proteins, implying that once a stable interfacial film is formed, it may resist coalescence more effectively because of stronger protein–protein interactions. This finding suggests that protein extraction by sonication improved emulsion stability because of changes in protein structure and surface hydrophobicity, similar to foam stability (Zhu et al., 2018). Meanwhile, after extracting salt-soluble proteins from other insects, ultrasound treatment showed an increase in both emulsifying capacity and emulsion stability, which may be related to the decreased particle size, unlike in the case of P. brevitarsis seulensis larval proteins (Ni et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). In summary, P. brevitarsis seulensis larval proteins extracted using a salt solution with UAE showed improved functional properties because of increased surface hydrophobicity and structural unfolding, although the emulsifying capacity decreased because of the increase in particle size.

In vitro digestibility

Digestibility has emerged as an important factor in the utilization of protein sources as food ingredients. The α-amino group content was used to determine the digestion of proteins into small peptides and free amino acids, and the 3 kDa fraction was analyzed to distinguish the bioavailable portions of the digesta that are sufficiently small to be absorbed through the intestinal epithelium (Kim et al., 2025). The α-amino group contents were significantly increased in both DW-US and NaCl-US by sonication (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A), whereas the 3 kDa fraction was significantly increased only in NaCl-US compared with NaCl (P < 0.05). Conformational modifications induced by the mechanical energy generated by cavitation generally enhance protein digestibility (Aghababaei et al., 2024). However, according to a previous study, the digestibility of P. brevitarsis seulensis larval proteins was not affected by structural modification, although the modification was induced by organic solvents owing to their intrinsic high digestibility (Lee, Jo, et al., 2021). Nevertheless, the α-amino group contents and 3 kDa fraction contents of the proteins were substantially changed by ultrasonication. The increased digestibility of salt-soluble proteins compared with water-soluble proteins can result from the additionally extracted small-sized proteins, as verified in the SDS-PAGE results and unfolded structures. Structural unfolding of proteins in NaCl-US due to sonication, as confirmed by the secondary structure analysis, also favors protease action during digestion. In addition, tropomyosin, which was extracted only in the NaCl and NaCl-US groups and is known to be highly stable, may also become more readily digested after structural unfolding induced by ultrasound. Thus, the proteins extracted using different solvents underwent different structural changes upon ultrasonication. Consequently, P. brevitarsis seulensis larval proteins extracted using a salt solution with ultrasonication showed enhanced protein bioavailability after digestion, as well as improved functional characteristics. Conclusions

The findings of this study demonstrate that a combination of ionic extraction and ultrasonic treatment significantly modified the physicochemical and functional properties of Protaetia brevitarsis seulensis proteins. SDS-PAGE, FT-IR, and secondary structure results confirmed that protein extraction using NaCl solution resulted in differences in the composition and structural characteristics of the extracted proteins. Ultrasonication promoted the extraction of smaller proteins, despite an increase in the mean particle size, thereby significantly altering amino acid composition, extraction yield, and protein solubility. Notably, the enhanced foaming properties, emulsion stability, solubility, and digestibility of the NaCl-US group resulted from the extraction of small proteins and increased molecular flexibility. The results—particularly those related to solubility, foaming and emulsifying properties, and amino acid profiles—suggest that insect proteins extracted by UAE in salt solution have high potential for use as functional protein ingredients. However, further research should focus on the systematic optimization of ultrasound conditions to maximize the effects of UAE on P. brevitarsis seulensis proteins in salt solution.
