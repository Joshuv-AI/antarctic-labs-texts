The field of ATTR amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) has undergone an astonishing transformation over the past decade. One decade ago the disease was considered exceedingly rare, difficult to diagnose (usually requiring endomyocardial biopsy), and without any effective treatment options. Since that time, ATTR-CM has been recognized as being relatively common and a major cause of heart failure in elderly patients,2 with the vast majority of cases now diagnosed noninvasively3 and 3 highly effective therapies approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration.4, 5, 6 This whirlwind of rapid advances is almost unheard of in modern cardiology, likely rivaled only by the changes in aortic stenosis management brought forth by the rise of transcatheter valve replacements.

The effectiveness of the approved treatments has been so impressive that it is almost easy to take the results for granted in this “new normal.” Tafamidis and acoramidis are both oral transthyretin (TTR) stabilizers, and each demonstrated superb efficacy in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial. Tafamidis, studied in a sicker patient population, demonstrated an astounding number needed to treat of 7.5 to prevent 1 death at 30 months and a number needed to treat of 4.5 to prevent 1 hospitalization per year.4 Acoramidis, tested in a later era in a healthier patient population, demonstrated similarly impressive results, cutting the frequency of cardiovascular hospitalizations by 50% over 30 months.5 A third approach, focusing on RNA ‘silencing’ with vutrisiran, reduced all-cause mortality by 35% over 42 months.6 These results rank among the largest treatment effects in all heart failure therapeutics.

As always, with every opportunity comes new challenges. With an abundance of treatment options, which should patients and clinicians choose?

In this issue of JACC: CardioOncology, Judge et al7 provide data on TTR stabilization for acoramidis, both vs placebo and vs tafamidis. The data vs placebo are to be expected: acoramidis demonstrates marked stabilization across every stabilization assay evaluated. The more important, and more complex, question is whether available assays meaningfully distinguish between stabilizer therapies.

TTR normally circulates as a tetramer, that is, 4 TTR subunits bound together. This tetrameric state represents the dominant form of circulating TTR, and in this form TTR is ‘stable’ and does not contribute to amyloid formation. However, a small amount of TTR circulates in a different form, resulting from the dissociation of the tetramers first into metastable dimers and then into unstable monomers; the monomers are prone to misfolding and ultimately depositing as amyloid fibrils.8 The rate-limiting step of the process is tetramer dissociation.9 This dissociation is accelerated by many hereditary TTR mutations, contributing to earlier and more aggressive disease.9

When thyroxine binds to TTR (one of the functions of TTR; the name “transthyretin” is derived from its function as a transporter of thyroxine and retinol), it stabilizes the tetrameric form. The mechanism of both tafamidis and acoramidis is to bind to the thyroxine-binding site, thereby stabilizing the tetrameric form, decreasing circulating monomers, and decreasing new amyloid fibril deposition.

If we could reliably measure the concentration of “unstable” monomeric TTR, we would have a simple way to measure the in vivo physiological effect of TTR stabilizers. Unfortunately, no such assay exists, likely because the monomers are inherently unstable and rapidly either reassemble into tetramers or into misfolded protein aggregates. As such, we are left with imperfect surrogate markers to measure TTR stability, 3 of which were assessed in the study by Judge et al7 published in this issue.

The first assay is the only one that is commercially available: measuring serum TTR levels. Although this assay is straightforward to understand in the context of TTR silencers such as vutrisiran, for which the mechanism is based on lowering hepatic TTR production, the story is much more complex for TTR stabilizers.

TTR stabilizers typically raise measured serum TTR levels, a rise that has been correlated with better outcomes.10 Whether this increase reflects increased tetramer stability alone, or additional effects such as enhanced hepatocyte secretion or reduced lysosomal degradation, remains unclear.8

Multiple other assays to investigate TTR stabilization have been developed, though none is commercially available, and all have caveats in their interpretation (Table 1). The present study used 2 such assays: western blot (WB) and fluorescent probe exclusion (FPE). The WB methodology involves promoting tetramer dissociation by exposing the tetramers to acidic conditions at a pH of 3.8 for 72 hours. Band intensities for tetrameric TTR are then compared at 0 and 72 hours; the more the TTR is stabilized, the less loss of intensity of the band will be seen at 72 hours. Although the methodology is straightforward, the cause of dissociation (exposure to extreme acidic conditions) is not physiologically representative of what is occurring in vivo. In addition, if TTR concentrations are low and the tetramers are well stabilized by stabilizer treatment, the band intensity at 72 hours may be greater than at 0 hours; such a situation yields the physiologically impossible result of “>100%” stabilization.7

The other assay tested in the present study was FPE. With this assay, a fluorescent probe that binds to the TTR thyroxine-binding site is added to a serum sample, and fluorescence is compared between 0 and 60 minutes. The more binding sites that are occupied by a TTR stabilizer, the fewer sites are available to bind the fluorescent probe. After correcting for background fluorescence, the percentage of “stabilization” is therefore calculated (where more fluorescence means less TTR stabilization).

On the plus side, this assay doesn’t rely on artificial physiology (ie, creating an artificial acidic environment). However, this assay also suffers from challenges. First, values of >100% stabilization can occur if “background” fluorescence at time 0 exceeds fluorescence at time 60 minutes, and values of <0% (ie, negative) stabilization can occur if fluorescence is low at time 0 samples. Neither of these is physiologically plausible.7 Perhaps more important, although there are 2 thyroxine-binding sites on tetrameric TTR, data suggest that binding of only a single ligand is sufficient to achieve effective TTR stabilization.8 Because the FPE assay doesn’t differentiate between whether the “first” or “second” site is being occupied, it can underestimate the true physiological stabilization of the tetramer. As an extreme example, imagine 4 TTR tetramers, each of which has 2 thyroxine-binding sites. If 1 stabilizer binds to all 8 thyroxine-binding sites, there will be no remaining sites for the fluorescent probe to bind to. If another stabilizer binds to a single thyroxine-binding site on each tetramer, it would leave 4 sites available for the fluorescent probe to bind to, giving the false appearance of substantial “unstable” TTR.

A final stabilization assay exists called subunit exchange and is likely the most physiologically representative.8 In this method, “tagged” TTR homotetramers are mixed with untagged homotetramers. The more that TTR dissociates, the more mixing of tagged TTR and untagged TTR will occur, forming new “heterotetramers” combining tagged and untagged TTR from dissociated TTR monomers.11 The advantages of this assay are that 1) it is performed in physiological conditions (eg, not the artificial acidic environment of the WB assay); and 2) it is directly detecting the end result of TTR dissociation, rather than the surrogate marker of thyroxine-binding site occupancy as measured by the FPE assay. In a prior study evaluating the TTR stability of tafamidis vs acoramidis using the subunit exchange assay, acoramidis was 4 times as potent as tafamidis at equal concentrations, but at the physiological concentrations achieved by standard dosing, stabilization was very similar.11 In the present study by Judge et al,7 the subunit exchange assay was not assessed.

What, then, does the current study show? In WB and FPE assays using samples from patients with inherited TTR variants, acoramidis demonstrated greater stabilization than tafamidis. In addition, in the acoramidis vs placebo clinical trial (ATTRibute-CM [Efficacy and Safety of AG10 in Subjects With Transthyretin Amyloid Cardiomyopathy]), patients treated with acoramidis achieved modestly higher elevations in serum TTR than patients who had started on placebo and who were subsequently prescribed tafamidis.7 WB and FPE assays from ATTRibute-CM samples showed greater stabilization in the acoramidis-treated group than the tafamidis-treated group, though these were based on very small numbers of tafamidis samples (n = 6 for the WB assay and n = 4 for the FPE assay). In addition, highlighting the methodologic issues with the assays noted earlier, placebo-treated patients had a mean of −48.4% stabilization by the FPE assay across 22 samples. Given that any number <0% stabilization is physiologically impossible, the limitations of this assay should be readily apparent.

So what is a clinician to conclude? Would John Tukey consider these results an approximate answer to the right question, or an exact answer to the wrong question? In short, we don’t know yet. The results from the present study add importantly to our understanding of how acoramidis and tafamidis perform using the stabilization assays tested. However, each of these assays has inherent flaws, and the subunit exchange assay—the most physiologically representative method—previously showed comparable stabilization between acoramidis and tafamidis at clinically relevant drug concentrations.11

In the end, treatment decisions should be guided primarily by the clinical trial data, and we are fortunate to have 3 safe and effective treatment options for ATTR amyloid cardiomyopathy. A head-to-head randomized trial with clinical endpoints remains the only way to definitively resolve the acoramidis vs tafamidis question, but it is also highly unlikely to be performed. Perhaps more plausible would be a prospective mechanistic crossover study comparing stabilization results across all available assays in patients receiving sequential acoramidis and tafamidis.

For now, the right answer to the question of which ATTR amyloid cardiomyopathy treatment to start in most patients is any of them, and the only wrong answer is to choose none at all.
