Does immediate orthostatic hypotension in adults have clinical implications for syncope and falls? An analysis of the Irish longitudinal study of ageing
Authors: Catriona Reddin, Robert Briggs, Silvin P Knight, Rose-Anne Kenny
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsOrthostatic hypotension is a common cause of syncope and falls; however, it is unclear if the association differs by variant. One of the variants is immediate orthostatic hypotension (formerly initial), which may be of lesser clinical importance than other variants. The Irish Longitudinal Study of Ageing offers a unique opportunity to evaluate the association of orthostatic hypotension variants with falls and syncope events in adults 50 years and older.MethodsBlood pressure (BP) and heart rate were measured supine and standing using phasic blood pressure techniques. Initial OH was defined as a drop in systolic BP >40mmHg and/or diastolic BP >20 mm Hg at 10 seconds after standing, with recovery to within 20mmHg of baseline Systolic BP and 10 mmHg diastolic BP by 20 seconds. Orthostatic hypotension was defined as a persisting drop in systolic BP ≥20mmHg and/or drop in diastolic BP ≥10mmHg. The association of orthostatic hypotension variants with syncope and unexplained falls events (primary outcome) over 8 years follow up were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression models.Results4,596 participants were included in this analysis; 953 (20.7%) met criteria for immediate orthostatic hypotension, 1,008 (22.0%) for orthostatic hypotension. Orthostatic hypotension, but not immediate orthostatic hypotension was associated with increased odds of syncope or unexplained falls (Orthostatic hypotension: OR 1.31; 95% CI, 1.04–1.66, Immediate orthostatic hypotension: OR 1.22; 95% CI, 0.93–1.57).ConclusionImmediate orthostatic hypotension is common, and not associated with unexplained falls or syncope. The terminology immediate orthostatic hypotension may be more appropriate.

Introduction

Orthostatic hypotension (OH) is a common cause of syncope and falls [1, 2]. The prevalence of OH is estimated to be 22% among community dwelling adults and 24% in long term care facilities, with prevalence increasing with age [3]. However there is a paucity of evidence regarding the association of OH variants with clinical sequelae.

The diagnostic criteria for OH was defined by expert consensus in 1996 as “a reduction of systolic blood pressure of at least 20mmHg or diastolic blood pressure of at least 10mmHg within 3 minutes of standing”; this did not distinguish OH variants [4, 5]. This definition was extended in a 2012 consensus statement to incorporate OH variants; immediate OH, classical OH and delayed OH [5].

A transient reduction of blood pressure occurring shortly after postural change (supine to upright posture) is a common physiological occurrence. If the reduction in systolic blood pressure exceeds 40mmHg, or diastolic exceeds 20mmHg within 15 seconds this is classified as immediate orthostatic hypotension (immediate OH). Beat-to-beat blood pressure monitoring is required for detection of immediate OH. Given the increased availability of technologies to monitor phasic changes in blood pressure, it is likely that this OH variant will be diagnosed more frequently. However, the clinical consequences of immediate OH are unknown. Classical OH, which we will refer to as OH, is defined as a sustained reduction in systolic BP of ≥20 mmHg and/or diastolic BP of ≥10 mm Hg within 180 seconds of active standing [5].

Additionally, the nomenclature of “initial orthostatic hypotension” has recently been reconsidered by an expert group suggesting use of the term “immediate orthostatic hypotension” [6]. The rationale for this change in nomenclature was the implication that further blood pressure changes would follow an “initial” reduction in postural blood pressure and transient immediate more accurately described the variant. Hence we will refer hereafter to these haemodynamic changes as “immediate orthostatic hypotension”.

There are diagnostic challenges in differentiating between syncope and falls events in older adults, with approximately one third of older adults who experience syncope having amnesia of loss of consciousness [7, 8]. It is estimated that a quarter of syncopal events present as unexplained falls [9]. European Society of Cardiology syncope guidelines acknowledge this challenge and advise that unexplained falls be managed as syncope [10]. Therefore, we will report on the composite of syncope and unexplained fall events as primary outcome.

The TILDA study presents a unique opportunity to evaluate the association of orthostatic hypotension variants, in a population cohort of adults 50 years and older, addressing whether there is an association with adverse clinical sequelae, in particular syncope and falls. This study extends previous findings by establishing the longitudinal relationship between orthostatic hypotension variants [11], immediate OH and classical OH, with incident falls and syncope over an 8 year follow-up.

Methods

Study design

The Irish Longitudinal Study on Ageing is a longitudinal cohort study. The study design has previously been described [12]. In brief, TILDA recruited a sample of community-dwelling adults aged 50 years and older, and their spouses (of any age). Data collection included three components; 1) a computer-assisted personal interview administered in the participants home, 2) a self-completion questionnaire completed by the participant in their own time, 3) a comprehensive health assessment delivered by a research nurse. Baseline interviews (Wave 1) were conducted between October 2009-February 2011. Subsequent waves were conducted at approximately 2-year intervals. Data included is from wave 1 to wave 5, an eight year follow-up period.

Participants were included in this study if data was available for both baseline active stand and at least one follow-up assessment.

Measurement of orthostatic hypotension

Orthostatic blood pressure (BP) was measured during an active stand test at baseline visit using a Finometer® Midi [11]. Participants lay in the supine position for 10 minutes in a quiet room, before standing in a timely manner, with assistance from a research nurse if required. Systolic BP, diastolic BP and heart rate were monitored for a further 2 minutes after standing. Participants were asked to report occurrence of orthostatic symptoms (dizziness or light-headedness) after the 2 minute period.

Orthostatic hypotension was defined as a persisting drop in systolic BP ≥20 mm Hg and/or drop in diastolic BP ≥10 mm Hg at 30, 60 and 90 seconds after standing. Time-point values were 5-second moving averages. This represents the classical OH variant. Immediate OH was defined as a drop in systolic BP >40 mm Hg and/or Diastolic BP >20 mm Hg at 10 seconds after standing, with recovery to within 20mmHg of baseline Systolic BP and 10 mmHg diastolic BP by 20 seconds. Outcome assessment

The primary outcome was syncope or unexplained falls. This was captured during the computer-assisted personal interview administered in the participants home. Secondary outcomes included syncope, unexplained falls, explained falls, falls requiring medical attention and fracture. At Waves 2–5, participants were asked “Have you had a blackout or fainted”, and “Have you had any falls since the last interview?”. If the participant reported a fall event they were then asked, “Were any of these falls non-accidental, i.e. with no apparent or obvious reason?”. An injurious fall event was defined as those who reported injury serious enough to require medical attention.

Measurement of other risk factor exposures

The computer-assisted personal interview captured highest level of educational attainment (primary, secondary or tertiary) and past medical history. Hypertension was additionally measured at the health assessment and using self-report and measured information was defined as high blood pressure or mean systolic blood pressure (SBP) of ≥140 mmHg or mean diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥90 mmHg and/or on an antihypertensive medication. Diabetes mellitus was defined by self-report of physician-diagnosed diabetes mellitus. Cardiovascular disease was defined as a self-reported history of heart attack, angina, or stroke. Chronic disease burden was assessed by self-report of the following conditions: cancer, liver disease, kidney disease, thyroid, arthritis, lung disease. Frailty was defined using the Fried Frailty score as frailty status of non-frail, pre-frail or frail [13]. Body mass index (BMI), calculated as weight (kg) divided by [height (m)]2 was measured at health assessment; weight was measured to the nearest 0·1 kg (Seca 861 Electronic Scales, Seca Ltd, Birmingham, UK) and height was measured to the nearest 0·01 m (Seca 240 Stadiometer, Seca Ltd, Birmingham, UK).

Statistical analysis

Chi-square tests were used to assess associations between categorical variables. We used multivariable logistic regression to evaluate the association between the orthostatic hypotension variants (immediate OH, classical OH) compared to no OH with the following outcomes – syncope or unexplained falls, syncope, unexplained falls, falls requiring medical attention, all falls and explained falls. The association of OH variants with outcome events was evaluated using multivariable logistic regression models overall, and within key subgroups. All models were adjusted for the following variables: age, sex, education, cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, baseline systolic BP, baseline heart rate, frailty, number of prescribed medications. Subgroup analyses were conducted for key subgroups (e.g., age, sex, blood pressure parameters). We chose to evaluate a subgroup analysis by nadir systolic blood pressure less than, or greater than 100mmHg based on retrospective analyses of ambulatory blood pressure monitoring demonstrating a higher risk of falls below 100mmHg [14]. A likelihood ratio test was used to test for interaction between risk factor and subgroups. P for interaction was considered statistically significant when <0.05. Subgroup analyses were exploratory in nature.

Results

A total of 4,596 participants, who had both home interview and health assessment with haemodynamic postural measures, were included in this analysis. 953 (20.7%) met criteria for immediate OH, 1,008 (22.0%) for OH, and 2,635 (57.3%) had no OH (Figure 2). Overall, 2,538 (55%) were female. Table 1 presents baseline characteristics by OH classification. Participants with immediate OH were younger (median 57 years; IQR 53–64) than those with OH (64 years; IQR 57–71) and those without OH (59 years; IQR 54–66) (p < 0.001). Compared to the no OH group, immediate OH participants were more likely to be male (53% vs 44%), employed (50% vs 45%), and to have third-level education (44% vs 36%) (all p < 0.001). Participants with OH were more often female (61%), retired (42%), and frail (2.4% vs 1.2%) (p < 0.001). A fall in the year prior to enrolment was reported in 19% of those with immediate OH, 22% of those with OH, and 18% of those without OH (p = 0.02).

During follow-up, syncope or unexplained falls were reported in 91 (9.5%) participants with immediate OH, 149 (15%) with OH, and 253 (9.6%) with no OH (p < 0.001). OH, but not immediate OH, was associated with increased odds of syncope or unexplained falls (OH: OR 1.31; 95% CI, 1.04–1.66, immediate OH: OR 1.22; 95% CI, 0.93–1.57) (Figure 3, Supplementary Appendix Table S1). There were no significant subgroup interactions (Table 2). Syncope was reported in 38 (4.0%) of those with immediate OH, 43 (4.3%) with OH, and 84 (3.2%) with no OH (p = 0.2). Neither immediate OH nor OH were significantly associated with syncope (OH: OR 1.01; 95% CI, 0.66–1.51, immediate OH: OR 1.52; 95% CI, 0.99–2.31)). Unexplained falls was reported in 62 (6.5%) of those with immediate OH, 118 (12.0%) with OH, and 187 (7.1%) with no OH (p < 0.001). Orthostatic hypotension, but not immediate OH was associated with increased odds of unexplained falls (OH: OR 1.42; 95% CI, 1.09–1.84, immediate OH: OR 1.14; 95% CI, 0.83–1.54) (Figure 3, Supplementary Appendix Table S1).

Falls occurred in 303 (32%) participants with immediate OH, 380 (38%) with OH, and 816 (31%) with no OH (p < 0.001). There was no association between immediate OH or OH with all falls (immediate OH: 1.16; 95% CI, 0.98–1.37, OH: OR 1.14; 95% CI, 0.97–1.34). Falls requiring medical attention occurred in 106 (11.0%) participants with immediate OH, 156 (15.0%) with OH, and 337 (13.0%) with no OH (p = 0.014). Neither immediate OH nor OH were significantly associated with injurious falls (OH: OR 0.97; 95% CI, 0.76–1.24, immediate OH: OR 1.04; 95% CI, 0.83–1.29). Fracture was reported in 108 (11.0%) of those with immediate OH, 106 (11.0%) with OH, and 280 (11.0%) with no OH. Neither immediate OH (adjusted OR 1.52; 95% CI, 0.99–2.31) nor OH (adjusted OR 1.00; 95% CI, 0.77–1.28) were significantly associated with fracture (OH: OR 0.96; 95% CI, 0.75-1.23, immediate OH: OR 1.12; 95% CI, 0.87-1.42) (Figure 3, Supplementary Appendix Table S1).

Discussion

In this large prospective cohort of community-dwelling older adults, we report the association of OH variants with syncope and falls events over a median follow-up of 8 years. While both OH (classical OH) and immediate OH were common, occurring in approximately 1 in 5 participants, immediate OH was not associated with primary outcome of syncope or unexplained falls or secondary outcomes after multivariant adjustment. In contrast, OH was independently associated with an increased risk of syncope or unexplained falls. The findings indicate that immediate OH is a benign variant in contrast to classical OH. This was the case over in 8 year follow up period.

Our findings provide additional rationale to the argument to amend the nomenclature surrounding initial OH to immediate OH [6]. This is the first study to evaluate the prospective association of orthostatic hypotension variants with falls and syncope outcomes over a prolonged duration of follow-up. A previous analysis of the TILDA study evaluated the association of OH variants over a shorter follow up duration (2 year follow-up). In addition to longer duration of follow-up, we also report on the composite of syncope and unexplained falls which is of clinical relevance and consistent with guideline recommendations on how to conceptualise syncope events [10].

The characteristics of individuals with immediate OH varied from those with OH, and were a generally lower risk population. They were younger (median age 57 vs 64 years), with higher level of educational attainment and more often employed and tertiary-educated - all indicators of higher functional status. They also had fewer chronic conditions, lower levels of frailty, and less polypharmacy. Immediate OH was not associated with an increased risk of syncope or unexplained falls, injurious falls, or fractures longitudinally after adjustment compared to no OH. This supports the argument that immediate OH may reflects a physiological exaggeration rather than a marker of pathology in the general population.

We report a slightly lower prevalence of immediate OH than previous meta-analytic estimates which reported a pooled prevalence of 29% (versus 21%) [15]. This difference in prevalence may be explained by differences in population. This meta-analysis was limited to populations aged 65 years and older, and also included a number of studies which enrolled participants from geriatric medicine clinics, who may have had more comorbidities [15]. We are not aware of another prospective cohort study which evaluated phasic blood pressure and syncope and falls events.

We did not report a statistically significant association of immediate OH with syncope. However, number of events was low. We considered unexplained falls and syncope as primary outcome as there can be diagnostic challenging discerning syncope from fall events, particularly in older adults. This approach is also consistent with ESC guidelines which advise that unexplained falls be investigated as syncope [10]. We note that immediate OH is a common diagnosis in tertiary referral syncope units. An analysis of the FAST II prospective study reported that immediate OH was the second most common diagnosis. Of note this cohort was younger with a mean age of 52 [16]. Similarly, a retrospective analysis of presentations to a syncope unit found that the diagnosis of previously unexplained syncope was initial orthostatic hypotension in 11% of patients [17]. This is not reflected in our representative population cohort of older adults, further research to evaluate clinical sequelae in younger adults may be warranted.

In adults with orthostatic hypotension, classifying the variant of orthostatic hypotension may have management implications, particularly in those with co-existing hypertension. Blood pressure management is of integral importance for primary and secondary cardiovascular prevention. However, achieving adequate hypertension control remains a challenge, as highlighted by a NCD Risk Factor collaboration analysis which reported global hypertension control rates of 23% for women and 18% for men with diagnosed hypertension [18]. While there are many reasons for uncontrolled hypertension, perceived falls risk has been reported as a factor that influences hypertension management in older adults [19]. Providing evidence that immediate OH is not associated with a significantly higher risk of syncope or unexplained falls, compared to those without OH, may provide additional reassurance to clinicians regarding blood pressure management in this cohort.

Our study has a number of strengths. The TILDA study is a population cohort and the only nationally representative prospective cohort study to incorporate orthostatic blood pressure measurement using beat-to-beat blood pressure monitoring. Therefore, it is uniquely placed to address this research question. The TILDA study includes an extensive cardiovascular health assessment, ensuring the population is richly phenotyped. Outcomes were assessed over a long duration of follow-up (8 years) and were systematically sought by trained researchers. There are also number of limitations which should be noted. Firstly, the population enrolled were over the age of 50 years, therefore our findings may not be generalisable to younger adults. There was also a low prevalence of frailty which may limit generalisability to a frail older adult population. Secondly, outcomes were ascertained by self-report and therefore may be subject to recall bias, although the cohort were cognitively intact at recruitment. Thirdly, the duration of active stand assessment conducted was 2 minutes and thus would not have captured delayed OH. Therefore, we did not evaluate the association of delayed OH with outcome events. The classification of OH variants requires beat-to-beat blood pressure measurement, therefore availability of this measurement method may limit applicability of findings to clinical practice.

Conclusion

Overall, our findings lend support to the emerging view that immediate OH should be distinguished from classical OH in both research and clinical settings. Immediate OH, was not associated with clinical sequelae and may represent a benign physiological response in most cases.
