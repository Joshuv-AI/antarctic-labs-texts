Intravenous Oxytocin Has no Effect on Ad Libitum Food Intake or Postprandial Plasma Glucose Concentrations in Individuals With Obesity: A Randomised, Placebo‐Controlled, Double‐Blind, Crossover Study
Authors: Ida M Gether, Vivian Kliim‐Hansen, Petur T Olafsson, Miriam G Pedersen, Casper K Nielsen, Anne‐Marie Ellegaard, Mikkel B Christensen, Jens F Rehfeld, Jørn W Helge, Jens J Holst, Bolette Hartmann, Filip K Knop, Lærke S Gasbjerg, Asger B Lund
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionOxytocin is expressed throughout the small intestine, suggesting a role for oxytocin in postprandial metabolism. Accordingly, single‐dose intranasal oxytocin reduces food intake and improves glucose tolerance. We investigated the effects of a continuous intravenous oxytocin infusion on ad libitum food intake, appetite sensations, and postprandial metabolism.MethodsIn a randomised, placebo‐controlled, double‐blind, crossover study, overnight fasted participants with obesity underwent two 4.5‐hour intravenous infusions of oxytocin (0.2 international units/min) and placebo (saline), respectively, on separate days interposed by a wash‐out period of a minimum of seven days. After 60 min of infusion, participants ingested a standardised liquid mixed meal containing 1.5 g paracetamol (for evaluation of gastric emptying). Appetite sensations were assessed by visual analogue scales, and concentrations of plasma glucose as well as glucose and appetite‐regulating hormones were measured. After four hours, food intake (primary endpoint) was assessed by an ad libitum meal test.ResultsTwenty‐four individuals (12 women, median age 41.5 (interquartile range (IQR) 28.5;55.3) years, body mass index 36.2 (32.1;37.6) kg/m2, mean glycated haemoglobin 34 ± standard deviation (SD) 3.5 mmol/mol) completed the study. Ad libitum food intake was similar between oxytocin and saline infusions (mean 714 ± SD 382 vs. 707 ± 385 kcal). Compared with placebo, oxytocin did not affect sensations of appetite nor circulating glucose, insulin, C‐peptide, glucagon, glucagon‐like peptide 1, glucose‐dependent insulinotropic polypeptide, cholecystokinin, or paracetamol.ConclusionIn individuals with obesity, 4.5‐hour intravenous infusion of oxytocin causing supraphysiological plasma concentrations of ~400 pg/mL has no effect on ad libitum food intake, appetite sensations, plasma glucose levels, circulating concentrations of glucose and appetite‐regulating hormones, or gastric emptying.Keywords: appetite, food intake, glucose metabolism, gut hormones, meal test, obesity, oxytocin

Introduction

Oxytocin, a hormone known for its effects in female reproductive functions and social attachment, is suggested to reduce food intake, improve glucose tolerance, and lower body weight [1, 2, 3, 4]. Further, the human small intestine expresses oxytocin [5, 6], and the expression of oxytocin in the small intestine is strongly upregulated after Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass surgery [7]. Thus, gut‐derived oxytocin may be involved in appetite regulation and postprandial glucose metabolism.

Oxytocin is a nonapeptide hormone primarily produced in the hypothalamic paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei and released into the circulation from the posterior pituitary gland upon axonal transport [8]. Outside the central nervous system, oxytocin is expressed in a variety of organs, including the adipose tissue, liver, kidney, thymus, adrenal medulla, testis, prostate, and female reproductive organs (i.e., placenta, uterus, and ovaries) [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. In addition, oxytocin is robustly expressed throughout the human small intestine, particularly in the upper small intestine where enterocytes produce and secrete oxytocin [5, 6]. Nevertheless, plasma oxytocin concentrations do not seem to change in response to meal intake [6, 16]. A previously reported oxytocin‐mediated release of the potent gallbladder‐contracting hormone, cholecystokinin (CCK), suggests a role of oxytocin in the regulation of gallbladder motility [1], and there are some evidence for a stimulating effect of oxytocin on gastric emptying [17, 18]. However, the exact role(s) of gut‐derived oxytocin is uncertain.

Single‐dose intranasal oxytocin reduces ad libitum food and snack intake in men across different body mass index (BMI) categories [1, 2, 19, 20] and increases pancreatic beta cell responsivity to an oral glucose load in healthy men [3] but not in men with obesity or type 2 diabetes [21, 22]. Also, intravenous infusion of oxytocin stimulates insulin secretion in response to an intravenous glucose challenge and during a hyperglycaemic clamp in healthy men [23, 24]. Thus, gut‐derived oxytocin possibly contributes to meal termination and postprandial glucose control in line with other gut‐derived peptide hormones such as glucagon‐like peptide 1 (GLP‐1) [25], and treatment with oxytocin may offer therapeutic benefits for metabolic disorders.

Here, using a 4.5‐hour intravenous oxytocin infusion (0.2 international units (IU)/min; amounting to a total dose of 54 IU), we evaluated the effects of supraphysiological plasma oxytocin concentrations on food intake during an ad libitum meal test as well as appetite sensations and postprandial glucose metabolism during a standardised liquid mixed meal test in individuals with obesity. We hypothesised that oxytocin would reduce ad libitum food intake and appetite while improving postprandial glucose tolerance in individuals with obesity.

Methods

Approval and Registrations

The study complied with the Helsinki Declaration (Seventh Revision, 2013). All participants gave oral and written informed consent. The protocol was approved by the Scientific‐Ethical Committee of the Capital Region of Denmark (H‐23059585) and registered with Clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06189001) and the Danish Data Protection Agency (p‐2023‐14 842).

Participants

Twenty‐four individuals with obesity completed the study. Inclusion criteria were age 18–65 years, BMI 30–40 kg/m2, and body fat mass ≥ 25% for men and ≥ 32% for women (assessed by bioelectrical impedance analysis (Segmental Multi Frequency Body Composition Analyser, MC‐780MA‐N, TANITA, Tokyo, Japan)). Key exclusion criteria were low haemoglobin, kidney, hepatobiliary and/or gastrointestinal disease, previous major intra‐abdominal surgery, glycated haemoglobin ≥ 48 mmol/mol and/or diabetes, and unstable weight, diet and/or lifestyle. Eligibility criteria are detailed in Supporting Information.

Study Design

This randomised, placebo‐controlled, double‐blind, crossover study was conducted at Center for Clinical Metabolic Research, Copenhagen University Hospital—Herlev and Gentofte, Denmark from January to September 2024. Participants underwent a screening visit and two 4.5‐hour intravenous oxytocin or placebo infusions on separate days interposed by a wash‐out period of at least seven days. At screening visits, participants were informed that the present study evaluated the effects of oxytocin on postprandial metabolism and food intake. For premenopausal women, experimental sessions were scheduled at approximately the same day of their menstrual cycle. Figure 1 provides an overview of study design and experimental sessions. The present experimental protocol has been used repeatedly in our research group and consistently demonstrated robustness and shown significant effects of a variety of peptide hormones on ad libitum food intake [26, 27, 28, 29].

Study Preparation

For 48 h preceding each experimental session, participants abstained from alcohol, strenuous physical activity, intermittent fasting, and excessive eating. To monitor adherence during this period, participants kept a food and beverage consumption diary. Experimental sessions were not conducted on Mondays since weekend activities may differ from those on weekdays. Participants ingested a standardised meal (558 g pasta Bolognese; 3192 kJ, 759 kcal; 45 energy (E)% carbohydrate, 16 E% protein, 39 E% fat, or 558 g vegetarian pasta Bolognese; 4475 kJ, 1066 kcal; 52 E% carbohydrate, 14 E% protein, 35 E% fat) between 6 and 8 PM on the evening before each experimental session and a snack bar (50 g Corny Dark Chocolate BIG cereal bar, Semper, Stockholm, Sweden; 864 kJ, 207 kcal; 51.0 E% carbohydrate, 5.7 E% protein, 33.3 E% fat) at ~10 PM whereafter participants remained fasting (including any liquid and medicine) until experimental sessions were initiated at 8 AM the following day.

Experimental Sessions

Upon arrival at the research facilities, premenopausal women delivered a urine sample for analysis of human choriogonadotropin to exclude pregnancy. Participants were examined in the recumbent position with a cannula in each cubital vein for blood sampling and oxytocin/placebo infusion. The forearm for blood sampling was wrapped in a heating pad throughout the experimental session. Oxytocin or placebo infusion was initiated at time −60 min. At time 0 min, a liquid mixed meal [30, 31] (3.6 mL/kg lean body mass; per 100 mL: 1010 kJ, 240 kcal; 49 E% carbohydrate, 16 E% protein, 35 E% fat, Nutricia Nutridrink Compact Strawberry, Danone, Paris, France) admixed 50 mL of water with 1.5 g dissolved paracetamol (for evaluation of gastric emptying [32]) was ingested evenly over 5 min followed by 20 mL of water for mouth rinsing. At time 180 min, participants received an ad libitum meal of pasta Bolognese (per 100 g: 572 kJ, 136 kcal; 45 E% carbohydrate, 16 E% protein, 39 E% fat) or vegetarian pasta Bolognese (per 100 g: 802 kJ, 191 kcal; 52 E% carbohydrate, 14 E% protein, 35 E% fat) and 400 mL of water. Participants were left in undisturbed surroundings and instructed to eat until comfortably full. The meal was weighed before and after the test. Finally, in a snack test, participants were presented with three snack types differing in taste (sweet, salty and bland) but matched for calorie content and macronutrient composition (detailed in Supporting Information). Participants were left for 10 min in undisturbed surroundings and instructed to eat as many snacks as desired. Snacks were weighed before and after the test. Infusion of oxytocin or placebo was terminated after the snack test. To prevent priming of automatic eating behaviour, participants were not exposed to food‐related advertisements from television, printed materials, or other media sources during experimental sessions [33].

Infusions and Randomisation

Unblinded staff members not otherwise involved in the study prepared infusions by diluting oxytocin to a total volume of 500 mL in saline (0.9% sodium chloride, Fresenius Kabi AB, Uppsala, Sweden) containing 0.5% human albumin (5% solution, CSL Behring, Lyngby, Denmark). Placebo infusions were 500 mL saline with 0.5% human albumin. The oxytocin infusion rate (0.2 IU/min (332 pmol/min)) was based on a prior study in healthy men reporting oxytocin‐mediated glucometabolic effects [24]. A prespecified randomisation list was generated using www.sealedenvelope.com, employing a randomly varying block size of four, six, or eight.

Blood Sampling and Laboratory Analyses

Blood was sampled 95, 65, 45, 30, 15, and 5 min before and 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, 120, 150, and 180 min after initiating the liquid meal test. Handling of blood samples and laboratory analyses are detailed in Supporting Information.

Visual Analogue Scale Ratings, Gallbladder Emptying and Indirect Calorimetry

Sensations of hunger, satiety, fullness, prospective food intake, thirst, comfort, and nausea and desire to eat something sweet, salty, savoury and fatty were measured using a standardised 100 mm visual analogue scale (VAS) at 100, 70, and 10 min before and 10, 40, 70, 100, 130, and 160 min after initiating the liquid meal test. Subjective ratings including appearance, smell, taste, aftertaste, and overall rating of the ad libitum meal were measured using 100 mm VAS. Questionnaires are specified in Supporting Information (Tables S1 and S2). Gallbladder and indirect calorimetry measurements and calculations are detailed in Supporting Information.

Endpoints, Calculations and Statistical Analyses

The primary endpoint was ad libitum food intake (kcal) between oxytocin and placebo infusion. Secondary and exploratory endpoints are provided in Table S3. Sample size calculation was based on 80% power and a 5% significance level. With a standard deviation (SD) of 200 kcal and a minimal relevant difference of 120 kcal [1, 26], 21.8 participants were required. Baseline insulin resistance was calculated using the computer‐generated Homeostatic Model Assessment‐Insulin Resistance (HOMA2‐IR) calculator at www2.dtu.ox.ac.uk/homacalculator/ (accessed on December 1st, 2024) [34, 35]. Area under the curve (AUC) was calculated using the trapezoidal method from time −95 to 180 min and baseline‐subtracted AUCs (bsAUCs) considered areas both below and above baseline. Baseline refers to the period before initiation of infusion. Composite appetite score was calculated as (satiety+fullness+(100–hunger)+(100–prospective food intake))/4 [36]. Results are reported as mean ± SD for approximately normally distributed data and median with interquartile range for substantially skewed data in text, tables and figures unless otherwise stated. Results from participants who completed both experimental sessions (n = 24) were evaluated in a linear mixed model with treatment and period as fixed effects, and an unadjusted (primary endpoint) or adjusted (secondary and exploratory endpoints) P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses and missing data are detailed in Supporting Information.

Ad Libitum Food and Snack Intake

Two participants were served a vegetarian version of the ad libitum meal. Compared to placebo, oxytocin had no effect on ad libitum food intake or meal ratings (Figure S2, Tables 2, and S4). Most participants ingested all available water, and water intake did not differ between the two experimental sessions (Table 2). Total snack intake and intake of the three different snack types were similar between oxytocin and placebo infusion (Figure S2 and Table 2).

Plasma Oxytocin Concentrations

At both experimental sessions, baseline plasma oxytocin concentrations were below lower limit of detection (LLOD) (15.5 pg/mL) in 89 of 96 samples. Oxytocin infusion increased plasma oxytocin concentrations to a steady state of ~400 pg/mL within ~55 min. Plasma oxytocin concentrations remained below LLOD throughout the placebo infusion (Figure 2).

Appetite, Comfort, Nausea, Thirst, and Desire for Specific Foods

Sensations of hunger, satiety, fullness, prospective food intake, comfort, nausea, and thirst, respectively, were similar between oxytocin and placebo infusion (Table S5 and Figure S3). Compared to placebo, oxytocin had no effect on composite appetite score (Table S5 and Figure S3). Desire to eat something salty and savoury, respectively, seemed reduced at baseline but the differences were not significant after adjustment (Table S5 and Figure S3). Total responses (assessed by bsAUCs) of desire to eat something salty, sweet, savoury, and fatty, respectively, did not differ between oxytocin and placebo infusion (Table S5 and Figure S3).

Glucose, Insulin, C‐Peptide, Glucagon, and Beta Cell Function

Oxytocin did not affect peak glucose and C‐peptide concentrations but decreased peak concentrations of insulin by ~12% and glucagon by ~11% compared to placebo, however, these effects were not significant after adjustment (Table 3 and Figure 2). Total responses (assessed by bsAUCs) and time to peak concentrations of glucose, insulin, C‐peptide, and glucagon, respectively, were similar between oxytocin and placebo infusion (Table 3 and Figure 2). Oxytocin increased beta cell sensitivity to glucose (evaluated as bsAUCs of C‐peptide/glucose ratio) by ~9% compared to placebo, although, this was not significant after adjustment (Table 3 and Figure 2).

GIP, GLP‐1, CCK, Paracetamol Absorption and Gallbladder Emptying

Compared to placebo, oxytocin had no effect on glucose‐dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP), GLP‐1, CCK, and paracetamol concentrations (Table 3 and Figure 2). Oxytocin had no effect on postprandial gallbladder ejection fraction (assessed by AUC), maximal gallbladder ejection fraction, or time to maximal gallbladder ejection fraction (n = 15) (Table S6 and Figure S4).

Indirect Calorimetry, Non‐Esterified Fatty Acids, Glycerol, and Beta‐Hydroxybutyrate

Respiratory exchange ratio and resting energy expenditure (assessed by kcal per day) were unaffected by oxytocin compared to placebo (Table S7 and Figure S5). Compared to placebo, oxytocin infusion had no effect on total responses (assessed by bsAUCs) of non‐esterified fatty acids, glycerol, or beta‐hydroxybutyrate (Table 3 and Figure S6).

Safety and Adverse Events

No adverse events were observed. Blood pressure, heart rate, plasma potassium and sodium remained within normal ranges throughout experimental sessions.

Discussion

In this randomised, placebo‐controlled, double‐blind, crossover study including 24 individuals with obesity, we report no effects of a 4.5‐hour intravenous oxytocin infusion (0.2 IU/min) on ad libitum food or snack intake. Further, we demonstrate no effects of intravenous oxytocin on appetite sensations, desire for specific foods, plasma glucose, key glucose and appetite‐regulating hormones, paracetamol‐assessed gastric emptying, gallbladder emptying, resting energy expenditure, and nutrient utilisation during a standardised liquid mixed meal test.

In contrast to our findings, single‐dose intranasal oxytocin reduced ad libitum food intake in men regardless of weight status [1, 2]. One study including 25 participants reported a reduction in food intake of mean 122 ± SD 255 kcal [1], while another observed a mean decrease of 147 kcal, though only 10 of 18 participants consumed less after oxytocin compared to placebo administration with four of these responders exhibiting substantial decreases of ~400–800 kcal [2]. Thus, the appetite‐suppressing effects of intranasal oxytocin may be highly variable between individuals. Unlike the fixed ad libitum meal applied in the present study, these previous studies used double‐portion of a participant‐selected meal from a menu and an ad libitum test buffet, respectively, allowing evaluation of nutrient preferences [1, 2]. A non‐significant reduction in fat intake was observed in one of these studies [1], but this was not seen in other studies [2, 19]. Corroborating our observations, single‐dose intranasal oxytocin failed to reduce food intake in a study of men with normal weight during an ad libitum test buffet [19]. Notably, we observed an ad libitum energy intake of ~700 kcal in contrast to the ~1100–1400 kcal reported in studies applying intranasal oxytocin administration and serving the ad libitum meal after an overnight fast [1, 2, 19]. Also, while previous studies only included men, the present study enrolled both men and women. Finally, single‐dose intranasal oxytocin administration elicits a transient increase in plasma oxytocin concentration, whereas continuous intravenous administration results in sustained elevations. Thus, methodological differences between the studies are evident and should be considered when evaluating oxytocin‐mediated effects on energy intake. In line with our results, none of the previous studies reported an oxytocin‐mediated effect on subjective hunger or appetite ratings using VAS [1, 2, 19]. In the present study, intravenous oxytocin did not affect snack intake, whereas single‐dose intranasal oxytocin reduced snack intake in men across weight categories in several prior studies [2, 19, 37] and increased snack intake in women across weight categories with and without binge eating disorder [38]. The absence of an oxytocin‐mediated effect on snacking in the present study should be interpreted cautiously, as snacks were served immediately after the ad libitum meal, potentially reducing capacity for snack intake. We observed no oxytocin‐mediated changes in appetite‐regulating hormones including GLP‐1 and CCK. Similarly, intranasal oxytocin did not affect plasma GLP‐1 concentrations in healthy men during fasting or after an ad libitum buffet meal [19]. However, fasting plasma CCK concentrations increased by ~15% in healthy men after intranasal oxytocin administration [1]. Importantly, a commercial assay was used for the measurement of plasma CCK [1], and the reliability of commercial CCK assays has been questioned [39]. Oxytocin‐induced reduction of energy intake and increase in circulating CCK reported previously involve intranasal administration, where oxytocin may bypass the blood brain barrier and reach the central nervous system. Intranasal oxytocin causes modest increases of oxytocin in human cerebrospinal fluid 75 min after administration [40]. In contrast, circulating oxytocin may not cross the blood brain barrier; human studies show unchanged oxytocin levels in cerebrospinal fluid despite elevated circulating oxytocin levels during labour [41]. Thus, the appetite‐diminishing effects of oxytocin may require entry of oxytocin into the brain, explaining the lack of effects with intravenous compared to intranasal oxytocin administration. We chose an intravenous oxytocin infusion to specifically assess effects of elevated circulating oxytocin concentrations as intranasal oxytocin absorption is complicated by substantial interindividual variability and low bioavailability [42]. Taken together, high inter‐individual variability in the response to oxytocin as well as methodological differences including assessment of ad libitum food and snack intake, participant sex, and route of oxytocin administration may explain the discrepancies in the observed effects.

We observed no significant effects of intravenous oxytocin infusion on plasma glucose or key gluco‐regulatory hormones. Although beta cell responsivity appeared higher during oxytocin compared to placebo infusion, this was not significant after adjustment, which may be reflecting limited power due to the small sample size for this endpoint. Although findings are inconsistent, previous studies suggest glucoregulatory effects of oxytocin. In men with normal weight, single‐dose intranasal oxytocin compared with saline improved glucose tolerance by increasing insulin secretion after a 75 g‐oral glucose load [3]. However, this did not occur in individuals with overweight/obesity [21] or type 2 diabetes [22]. Studies evaluating intravenous oxytocin infusion in men with normal weight also report increased glucose‐mediated insulin secretion [23, 24]. An intravenous bolus of 2 IU oxytocin followed by 4 IU over 60 min compared with saline increased insulin secretion during an intravenous glucose tolerance test without affecting plasma glucose concentrations [23]. Similarly, a continuous intravenous oxytocin infusion (0.2 IU/min) in men with normal weight increased insulin secretion during a hyperglycaemic clamp [24]. Thus, an oxytocin‐induced increase in glucose‐mediated insulin secretion might be limited to individuals with normal weight, suggesting defects in the response to oxytocin with increasing body weight. Reports of positive correlations between circulating oxytocin levels and body weight or BMI [43, 44, 45, 46] support the presence of oxytocin resistance in obesity, although some studies reported negative correlations [47, 48, 49]. Observation of reduced fasting insulin and HOMA2‐IR after single‐dose intranasal oxytocin in men with normal weight or overweight/obesity with no differences between weight groups indicates an oxytocin‐induced improvement of insulin sensitivity in men across weight categories [1]. Conversely, an intravenous oxytocin infusion (0.2 IU/min) in men with normal weight did not affect insulin sensitivity as assessed by a hyperinsulinaemic euglycaemic clamp, the gold standard method for evaluating insulin sensitivity, and even increased fasting plasma glucose and glucagon concentrations [24]. Overall, oxytocin may enhance glucose‐stimulated insulin secretion in individuals with normal weight, but not in obesity and/or type 2 diabetes, while its effects on insulin sensitivity appear inconsistent.

Consistent with the unchanged CCK response observed in the present study, we found no effect of oxytocin on gallbladder motility. Also, we report no oxytocin‐mediated effect on paracetamol‐assessed gastric emptying of a liquid meal as assessed [32]. In contrast, oxytocin infusion (0.33 IU/min) accelerates scintigraphy‐assessed gastric emptying of a semi‐solid meal in individuals with normal weight [17], whereas there was no effect of an oxytocin infusion of lower dose (0.04 IU/min) on ultrasonography‐assessed gastric emptying [18]. Further, intravenous infusion of the oxytocin and arginine vasopressin receptor antagonist, atosiban, slowed ultrasonography‐assessed gastric emptying of a semi‐solid meal, suggesting a stimulating effect of endogenous oxytocin (or arginine vasopressin) on gastric emptying [18]. Scintigraphy remains the gold standard for assessing gastric emptying rate, although ultrasonography and the paracetamol absorption test provide results comparable to scintigraphy on many occasions [50, 51, 52]. Varying meal consistencies, oxytocin infusion rates, and methods for evaluating gastric emptying might explain the contrasting findings.

Intranasal oxytocin was evaluated as a potential body weight‐lowering drug in three clinical studies with contrasting outcomes. In one randomised, placebo‐controlled, double‐blind trial including 20 individuals with obesity, eight weeks of intranasal oxytocin (24 IU) administered before main meals and bedtime with no lifestyle intervention decreased body weight by ~9% and improved serum low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol levels (n = 9) [4]. In comparison, placebo treatment caused a ~2% weight loss (n = 11) [4]. Of note, baseline participant age was ~29% lower (29 vs. 41 years) and BMI was ~20% higher (36 vs. 30 kg/m2) in the group receiving oxytocin compared to placebo, causing considerable metabolic variations between groups [4]. In another randomised, placebo‐controlled, double‐blind trial including 21 individuals aged ≥ 60 years with obesity and sarcopenia, intranasal oxytocin treatment (24 IU) four times daily for eight weeks with no lifestyle intervention did not affect body weight but increased lean body mass by 1.9% compared to a reduction of 0.5% in the placebo group [53]. Further, oxytocin treatment reduced serum LDL cholesterol levels by ~0.5 mmol/L compared to placebo [53]. Importantly, baseline lean body mass and LDL cholesterol levels were ~29% and ~15% higher in the group receiving oxytocin, possibly influencing the potential for metabolic improvement in this group. Finally, in a larger, randomised, placebo‐controlled, double‐blind study including 61 individuals with obesity, eight weeks of intranasal oxytocin (24 IU) four times daily with no lifestyle intervention resulted in no change in body weight (mean treatment difference between the oxytocin and the control group was −0.06 kg), body composition, resting energy expenditure, or metabolic profile including LDL cholesterol levels [54]. In summary, current evidence for a body weight‐lowering effect of long‐term intranasal oxytocin treatment is limited despite reports of reduced energy intake after single‐dose intranasal oxytocin.

In line with our results, intranasal oxytocin did not affect respiratory exchange ratio, substrate utilisation, or circulating non‐esterified fatty acids in men across the weight spectrum [2, 3, 19, 21]. However, one cohort of 25 fasted, healthy men with normal weight or overweight/obesity exhibited decreased respiratory exchange ratio together with reduced carbohydrate and increased fat utilisation after intranasal oxytocin administration [1]. Overall, most studies reject any oxytocin‐mediated effect on human substrate utilisation.

Limitations of the present study include enrolment of both men and women within a wide age range (26–64 years). Nonetheless, we observed variance comparable to previous similar studies including a more homogenous participant group. In post hoc, exploratory subset analyses of women, men, the 12 youngest participants, and the 12 oldest participants, respectively, oxytocin had no effect on any of the endpoints in the present study (Table S8–S19). Importantly, these subset analyses may be underpowered considering the small sample sizes. Another limitation of the present study is that participants were not screened for cognitive control of eating behaviour and were aware that food intake was measured. This may have promoted restrained eating and reduced ad libitum food intake, potentially attenuating detectable treatment effects. An oxytocin infusion rate of 0.2 IU/min caused insulinotropic and glucagonotropic effects during hyperglycaemia and hypoglycaemia, respectively, in healthy, young men without adverse effects [24]. However, we cannot be certain whether this dose was sufficient to induce measurable effects in food intake and glucose metabolism in individuals with obesity. Also, plasma oxytocin concentrations during oxytocin infusion showed notable interindividual variability; possibly attributed to the fixed and not body weight‐adjusted oxytocin dose. Previous studies reporting a satiety‐promoting effect of oxytocin also used a fixed intranasal dose, suggesting that a body weight‐adjusted dosing regimen of oxytocin is not necessary to reduce food intake. Importantly, the satiety‐promoting effects of oxytocin may be induced by transient but not sustained elevations of plasma oxytocin levels. Finally, although all participants exhibited normal HbA1c at screening, fasting plasma glucose during experimental sessions ranged from 4.8 to 6.5 mmol/L. This indicates that some participants may have had impaired glucose tolerance, which could attenuate the effect of oxytocin on postprandial glycaemic responses [22].

In summary, a 4.5‐hour intravenous oxytocin infusion (0.2 IU/min) did not reduce ad libitum food intake in individuals with obesity. In addition, oxytocin infusion had no effect on appetite sensations, desire for specific foods, glucose metabolism, paracetamol absorption, gallbladder dynamics, resting energy expenditure, and nutrient utilisation during a liquid mixed meal test.
