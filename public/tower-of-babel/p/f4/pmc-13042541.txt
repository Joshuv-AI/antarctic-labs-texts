Cervical paraspinal sarcopenia affects ability to achieve PROMIS minimal clinically important difference in patients undergoing anterior cervical discectomy and fusion
Authors: Manasa S. Pagadala, Jacob R. Staub, Tyler M. Compton, Harsha Kaul, Shravan Asthana, Katia Valdez, Robert J. Oris, Jason D. Tegethoff, Jeremy Marx, John Carney, Theodore A. Joaquin, Wellington K. Hsu, Alpesh A. Patel, Srikanth N. Divi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Sarcopenia is an established risk factor for poor outcomes in the general surgery literature; however, this has been less explored in spine surgery. No studies have quantified cervical paraspinal sarcopenia on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to determine the influence on postoperative patient improvement as measured through PROMIS scores. The objective of this study was to determine the influence of cervical sarcopenia on PROMIS score improvement following anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF).Methods:Patients over 18 years of age who underwent ACDF at a single, tertiary academic institution between 2012 and 2018 were included in the study. Multifidus fatty infiltration (FI) and cross-sectional area (CSA) were quantified on preoperative MRI measurements. PROMIS-physical function (PF) and pain interference (PI) scores were collected at baseline and 1-year postoperatively and compared with a minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 4.5 for PF and 4.9 for PI. Bivariate and multivariate analyses compared FI and CSA in patients who met versus failed to meet the MCID for PF and PI components.Results:Seventy-eight patients met the inclusion criteria. The mean age was 54.8, and 29 were female. MCID was achieved in 46 patients for PROMIS-PF and 42 for PROMIS-PI. Greater multifidus FI demonstrated a significant association with increased likelihood of meeting PF MCID on bivariate (P = 0.043) and multivariate (P = 0.033). Multifidus CSA was not associated with meeting the MCID for PF or PI. No correlation was found between multifidus CSA and FI (r2 = 0.0797).Conclusions:Higher multifidus FI was independently associated with meeting MCID for PROMIS-PF scores 1 year following ACDF.

INTRODUCTION

Sarcopenia is the progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass seen in aging patients and may be accelerated by various comorbidities and sedentary lifestyles.[123] Associated with frailty, falls, and mortality, sarcopenia is an established surgical risk factor.[45] General surgery literature has associated sarcopenia with an increased incidence of postoperative complications, mortality, length of stay, and cost.[67] Despite these relationships, sarcopenia is still an emerging topic in spine surgery.

Investigation into the influence of sarcopenia on cervical spine surgery outcomes is currently limited. Sarcopenia is measured through a variety of methods, for example, functional tests versus radiographic quantifications; this has limited more generalized conclusions.[8] Radiographic quantifications of sarcopenia, i.e., muscle fatty infiltration (FI) and cross-sectional area (CSA), are more applicable in spine surgery research given the availability of preoperative imaging.

Interestingly, cervical spinal sarcopenia has shown a differing impact on outcomes following anterior versus posterior approaches to cervical fusion.[910] Following anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF), higher cervical paraspinal FI has been associated with greater patient-reported outcomes (PROs).[9] However, greater degrees of paraspinal FI have been associated with worse outcomes following posterior cervical fusion.[1011] Some studies show a lack of correlation between cervical paraspinal FI and CSA, demonstrating the need to analyze both when investigating the role of sarcopenia on cervical spine surgery outcomes.[1213]

Despite increasing awareness of the detrimental effects of sarcopenia following spine surgery, the impact of quantitatively measured paraspinal sarcopenia on patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) following ACDF has not been investigated. To the authors’ knowledge, this study is the first to assess the impact of paraspinal sarcopenia on PROMIS-physical function (PF) and pain interference (PI) following ACDF using quantitative assessments of paraspinal muscle size and quality.

METHODS

Patient selection

This study was approved under our institutional IRB# STU00213351. A retrospective review was completed of patients undergoing ACDF between 2012 and 2018 at a single, tertiary academic institution. Inclusion criteria were patients over 18 years of age, having completed a cervical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) within 1 year before surgery, and patients who underwent a primary 1–3 level ACDF. Patients were excluded if they did not have preoperative and 1-year postoperative PROMIS scores or underwent an additional posterior fusion. Demographic information was collected for each patient, including age, sex, tobacco usage, diabetes, and body mass index (BMI).

Magnetic resonance imaging analysis, muscle group cross-sectional area, and percent fatty infiltration

Preoperative T1 or T2-weighted, axial MRIs of the cervical spine were evaluated at the C5-C6 disc space to quantify paraspinal muscle morphology. The CSA of the multifidus [Figure 1] was measured through outlining fascial muscle borders in ImageJ v1.54 (National Institutes of Health and the Laboratory for Optical and Computational Instrumentation; Wisconsin, USA). CSA for the multifidus was calculated as the sum of the bilateral muscles. The multifidus percent FI was assessed using the image thresholding function in ImageJ. Two independent readers measured both FI and CSA for all patient MRIs to ensure accuracy. An intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated between the two reviewers. All CSA and FI measurements of the multifidus were reported as averages of the two reviewers.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with Python v3.12.2 (The Python Software Foundation; Delaware, United States). Statistical significance was set to 0.05. The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for PF and PI was set to 4.5 and 4.9, respectively, according to prior literature.[14] For PROMIS PF and PI scores, bivariate analyses were conducted to examine differences between MCID and non-MCID cohorts in demographics, multifidus FI, and multifidus CSA through Student’s t-test. Furthermore, multivariate binomial logistic regression was used to determine the independent role of multifidus FI in meeting MCID for PF and PI when controlling for several covariates: age, sex, and BMI. Multivariate results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with a 95% confidence interval (CI). Linear regression was performed to determine the relationship between multifidus CSA and FI.

RESULTS

Seventy-eight patients met the inclusion criteria for this study. The average age of patients in the overall cohort was 54.8 (49.0–63.8) and 37.1% of patients were female. On bivariate analyses, patients who met MCID were younger in both PF (52.5 vs. 56.6, P = 0.103) and PI (52.2 vs. 56.2, P = 0.133) domains; however, these relationships only trended toward statistical significance [Tables 1 and 2]. No other statistically significant differences in baseline demographics were observed among patients who met MCID for PROMIS-PF or PI on bivariate analysis [Tables 1 and 2].

Excellent agreement between reviewers was shown for multifidus CSA and FI measurements (ICC = 0.91). On bivariate analysis, patients that met the MCID for PROMIS-PF had statistically greater scores at baseline (39.99 vs. 37.77, P = 0.01). Furthermore, multifidus FI was greater in patients who met MCID for PROMIS-PF (33.8% vs. 30.8%, P = 0.043) [Table 1]. No statistically significant differences were observed with respect to multifidus CSA between MCID groups. On multivariate analysis, higher baseline scores were predictive of meeting MCID for PF (OR = 1.20, 95% CI = [1.09–1.29]) [Table 3]. Furthermore, higher preoperative multifidus FI was associated with meeting PF MCID (OR = 2.49, 95% CI = [2.00–3.10]) [Table 3]. These relationships were not observed with respect to PROMIS PI MCID [Table 4]. A linear regression model demonstrated a low correlation between multifidus FI and CSA (r2 = 0.0797) [Figure 2].

DISCUSSION

Sarcopenia is an established preoperative risk factor in general surgery; however, it remains a more recent topic in spine surgery. While several radiographic and functional methods exist to quantify sarcopenia, few studies use objective measurements of cervical paraspinal muscle FI and CSA. The goal of this study was to objectively quantify cervical posterior paraspinal muscle morphology, i.e., FI and CSA, to determine their influence on PROMIS-PF and PI scores. A secondary outcome of this study was to determine the correlation between multifidus CSA and FI. Our results demonstrated that patients who met PROMIS-PF MCID had a statistically greater multifidus FI. Multifidus CSA was not associated with meeting PROMIS-PF or PI MCID. Finally, preoperative multifidus FI and CSA were not strongly correlated based on our objective measurements.

While the negative role of sarcopenia has been generally established in lumbar fusion literature, there presently is a paucity of literature investigating this in cervical fusion patients. A recent paper by Pinter et al. found that in patients with more severe cervical paraspinal sarcopenia, greater improvements in neck disability index (NDI) scores were observed following ACDF.[9] While Pinter et al. utilized the Goutallier classification of muscle degeneration, a categorical scale, the results of this study support these findings through objective measurements of cervical multifidus FI. The Goutallier categorization of muscle FI is a subjective scale; the present study improves upon this through quantitative analysis, demonstrating patients with more severe multifidus FI are more likely to meet PROMIS-PF MCID following ACDF.

Previous literature has demonstrated a poor association between muscle size and fatty content. In another study, Pinter et al. reported that comparisons of several cervical paraspinal muscle CSAs, i.e., obliquus capitis inferior, multifidus, and sternocleidomastoid, were not significantly different amongst various Goutallier grades.[13] The current study mirrors these findings and expands upon this to demonstrate a generally poor correlation between the quantitative measures of multifidus FI and CSA. This supports the independent examination of paraspinal muscle FI and CSA regarding clinical outcomes in spine surgery research.

Other literature, however, suggests that poor paraspinal muscle quality negatively affects PROs following spine surgery. A retrospective study of patients undergoing posterior cervical C2-T2 fusion found that patients with severe degenerative changes to the multifidus, as measured through Goutallier classification, were less likely to meet NDI MCID, PROMIS-PF MCID, and more likely to have worsening pain following surgery.[10] Pinter et al. additionally investigated the effect of sarcopenia in patients undergoing laminoplasty. As laminoplasty is a motion-preserving option with increased reliance on preoperative muscular function, variation of muscle quality in this patient population is likely to have a greater effect. Indeed, that is what Pinter et al. found in their study, with laminoplasty patients with preexisting cervical sarcopenia having worse outcomes, which was in contrast to their findings in ACDF patients.[915] Furthermore, a separate study quantifying sarcopenia through appendicular skeletal muscle index found both postoperative alignment and outcomes to be worse in sarcopenic patients as assessed by SVA and JOA scores, respectively.[16] Outside of the ACDF population, the general consensus supports that preoperative sarcopenia is associated with worse postoperative spinal alignment and lesser improvement in PROs. These findings are in line with the results of our study, where ACDF patients with higher FI showed greater improvement.

Several potential explanations have been proposed to explain the difference between anterior versus posterior cervical fusion outcomes in sarcopenic patients. In patients with cervical spondylosis due to degenerative discs, impaired paraspinal muscle function due to sarcopenia may place additional strain along the posterior column and facet joints, leading to worse alignment and PROs.[17] Under normal physiologic conditions, the anterior column supports approximately one-third of the axial load translated along the cervical spine; the balance in axial load shifts more toward the anterior column through the fusion construct.[14181920] This may relieve strain on the posterior paraspinal muscles and facet joins, potentially explaining the generally greater postoperative improvement of sarcopenic patients undergoing an anterior approach.[21] However, future studies are needed to validate this theory.

Furthermore, unlike patients undergoing ACDF, patients undergoing posterior fusion experience direct surgical trauma to the posterior musculature. As the healing capacity of these muscles is likely diminished in sarcopenic patients, this may yield insufficient postoperative support, leading to both mechanical pain and worsening sagittal alignment. Separately, a potential mechanism explaining the greater improvements following surgery may be associated with the chronicity and degree of spinal stenosis. A recent study from Schönnagel et al. found an association between the degree of lumbar spinal stenosis and degenerative changes within the lumbar paraspinal muscles.[22] This suggests that in part, greater paraspinal infiltration may be reflective of more severe paraspinal stenosis, and therefore, larger improvements in PROs postoperatively.

Several limitations are present in this study. The retrospective nature of this work may potentially be a source of selection bias. Only 78 patients met the inclusion criteria; this relatively small sample size may limit the power of this present study. It should be noted that this study was of a similar size to previous spine surgery literature investigating sarcopenia.[913]

Finally, while the only paraspinal muscle analyzed was the multifidus, the role of other paraspinal muscle quality, as well as overall cervical alignment, may be confounding factors not currently controlled for. This may serve as an avenue for future studies to better determine the role of sarcopenia on postoperative improvement following cervical fusion procedures. While sarcopenic spine surgery literature remains a contemporary topic, future, large retrospective studies should be carried out to better define these relationships as a means of defining expected patient improvement in cervical fusion.

CONCLUSIONS

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to assess the relationship between quantitatively measured cervical multifidus sarcopenia and PROMs following ACDF, as well as the objective relationship between cervical multifidus CSA and FI. The results of this study suggest that patients with more severe preoperative paraspinal sarcopenia are more likely to demonstrate a clinically significant improvement with PROMIS-PF. Current literature surrounding sarcopenic patient outcomes in spine surgery is limited; future studies are needed to better define these relationships.
