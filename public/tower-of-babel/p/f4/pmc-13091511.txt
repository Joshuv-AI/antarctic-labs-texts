Association of comprehensive inflammatory-metabolic status and perioperative outcomes in gastric cancer: insights from four randomized controlled trials
Authors: L.-K. Zhang, Z.-X. Shang-Guan, H.-L. Zheng, H.-H. Zheng, C.-Y. Zheng, W.-F. Chen, J.-W. Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInflammatory and metabolic status play a key role in the prognosis of gastric cancer (GC) patients. This study aimed to develop a novel comprehensive inflammatory-metabolic index (CIMI) to predict the risk of perioperative complications and prolonged hospital stay in GC patients.Patients and methodsThis study included 567 stage II-III GC patients from four randomized, controlled trials RCTs as the development cohort, and 107 patients from an external cohort as the validation set. Preoperative hematological and body composition parameters were used to evaluate inflammatory-metabolic status. The predictive performance of CIMI was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC), precision-recall (PR), decision curve analysis (DCA) curves, and model fit indices [deviance, Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC)]. Subgroup and survival analyses were conducted to further explore the clinical utility of CIMI.ResultsCIMI was calculated using the formula: log[white blood cell count (109/l) × serum globulin (g/l)] × visceral adiposity index (VAI). In the development cohort, the areas under the curve (AUC) for CIMI were 0.748 (post-operative complications) and 0.651 (length of stay >11 days], outperforming existing inflammatory/metabolic indicators. Similar results were observed in the validation cohort, further confirming its robust predictive ability. Additionally, CIMI was significantly associated with intraoperative surgical time and post-operative recovery time. Subgroup analysis showed that, after adjusting for variables, the high-risk CIMI group had a significantly higher incidence of post-operative complications than the low-risk group (odds ratio = 7.77, 95% confidence interval 4.39-13.75, P < 0.001). Survival analysis revealed that in the low-risk CIMI group, patients who initiated adjuvant chemotherapy within 6 weeks (time to chemotherapy <6 weeks) had a significantly better 5-year overall survival rate than those with time to chemotherapy ≥6 weeks (56.0% versus 45.0%, P = 0.043). The online CIMI calculator provides clinicians with a convenient tool to support individualized treatment decision-making.ConclusionsAs a novel inflammatory-metabolic index, CIMI effectively predicts the risk of post-operative complications and prolonged hospital stay in GC patients, providing valuable insights for clinical decision-making and health care system strategies.

Introduction

According to the 2022 global cancer statistics, gastric cancer (GC) ranks as the fifth leading cause of cancer mortality, representing a major public health burden.1 Surgical resection is currently the primary treatment. The prevention and management of post-operative complications have become a key focus.2

Post-operative complications are influenced by multiple factors, including tumor-related pathological characteristics and the patient’s overall condition. Recent studies have increasingly highlighted the critical role of tumor-host interactions in disease progression and prognosis.3 Conceptually, if a tumor is viewed as a ‘wound that never heals,’ systemic inflammation may serve as the central mechanism driving this ‘non-healing’ process.4 Hematological parameters can effectively reflect the systemic nutritional-inflammatory status of cancer patients.5 Previous studies have constructed various systemic inflammatory biomarkers, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR),6,7 platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR),8,9 and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII),10 and confirmed their prognostic value in GC. Existing biomarkers, however, primarily focus on a single dimension of inflammatory status and fail to reflect the interactions between the tumor and host. Therefore, exploring a biomarker that can more comprehensively assess systemic inflammatory status is of great importance.

Body composition is also a critical factor influencing perioperative outcomes.11 Asian populations tend to accumulate fat in the abdominal region, with visceral fat accumulation being more prevalent.12 Studies have shown that assessing visceral obesity using visceral fat area measured at the umbilical level on computed tomography (CT) scan is more objective and accurate.13 Previous research14, 15, 16, 17 has found that excessive visceral fat is one of the common factors increasing the difficulty of surgery, as it complicates surgical field exposure, prolongs operative time, increases intraoperative blood loss, and may affect the efficacy of lymph node dissection, thereby raising the incidence of adverse post-operative outcomes. Therefore, the assessment of visceral fat is of significant importance in the perioperative management.

In recent years, some scholars18, 19, 20 have proposed combining inflammatory markers with metabolic parameters to construct comprehensive inflammation-metabolism indices. Existing inflammatory-metabolic indices, however, vary significantly in parameter selection, weight allocation, and evaluation criteria, leading to a lack of consistency and comparability in their clinical application. Furthermore, there is a lack of large-scale prospective studies validating the predictive value of these indices in GC patients, particularly their potential in predicting perioperative outcomes.

Therefore, this study aims to construct a novel composite inflammatory-metabolic index (CIMI) by integrating data from four prospective studies and to compare its predictive performance with existing inflammatory-metabolic indices, thereby providing valuable insights for clinical decision-making and health care system strategies.

Patients and methods

Design

This study included patients from four randomized, controlled trials (RCTs) conducted by Fujian Medical University Union Hospital between December 2012 and October 2018. The trials were FUGES-01 (NCT02327481), FUGES-02 (NCT02333721), CLASS-01 (NCT01609309), and CLASS-04 (NCT02845986).21, 22, 23, 24

Patients with missing data on age, height, serum globulin, fasting glucose, neutrophil count, platelet count, serum albumin, C-reactive protein levels, cholesterol, lymphocyte count, or CT records were excluded. Finally, 567 patients were included in the development set. Additionally, a retrospective cohort of 107 patients (between January 2014 and April 2016) from the Affiliated Hospital of Putian University was included as a validation set. The detailed inclusion and exclusion process is shown in Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106922. The study was approved by the institutional review board.

Clinical management

All patients underwent standard preoperative staging examinations, including endoscopy and CT scans. According to the Japanese Gastric Cancer Treatment Guidelines,25 patients with post-operative pathological diagnoses of stage II/III were recommended for adjuvant chemotherapy. Guideline recommendations evolved with the publication of large multicenter randomized trials, leading to heterogeneity in the adjuvant chemotherapy regimens used, including two-drug combinations [S-1 plus oxaliplatin (SOX), capecitabine plus oxaliplatin (XELOX/CAPOX), cisplatin plus fluorouracil or capecitabine (ECF/ECX)] and three-drug combinations [fluorouracil, leucovorin, oxaliplatin, docetaxel (FLOT), docetaxel, oxaliplatin, S-1 (DOS), fluorouracil, leucovorin, oxaliplatin (FOLFOX)]. Chemotherapy regimens and dosages were determined by specialists at each center according to guideline recommendations and adjusted based on patients’ clinical characteristics, drug tolerance, and tumor response. Laparoscopic radical gastrectomy was carried out in accordance with the Japanese Gastric Cancer Association guidelines.

Endpoints and definitions

Intraoperative and post-operative complications were defined based on early and late surgical complications listed in the intraoperative and post-operative observation items. Length of stay (LOS) is calculated from the day of surgery to the day of discharge. Chemotherapy delay was defined as an interval exceeding 49 days from surgery to the initiation of post-operative chemotherapy.26 Detailed definitions of complication are provided in the Supplementary Methods, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106922. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time interval from the date of surgery to the end of follow-up or death.

Inflammatory indicator assessment

Routine hematological tests conducted at the initial diagnosis were collected to assess systemic nutritional and inflammatory parameters. The basic nutritional-inflammatory markers included total protein, serum albumin, serum globulin, total cholesterol, hemoglobin, total neutrophil count, total lymphocyte count, total monocyte count, total platelet count, and C-reactive protein in peripheral blood. This study utilized 14 serology-based inflammatory-metabolic markers, including NLR, PLR, lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), WHR, prognostic nutritional index (PNI), SII, C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR), Glasgow Prognostic Score (GPS), lymphocyte-c-reactive ratio (LCR), prognostic index (PI), albumin-to-globulin ratio (AGR), systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), neutrophil-to-platelet ratio (NPR), and systemic nutrition-inflammation index (SNII), as well as three inflammation-metabolism markers based on hematological and anthropometric data, including advanced lung cancer inflammation index (ALI), cancer cachexia index (CXI), and geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI). The definitions of these markers were determined based on previous reports7, 8, 9, 10,27, 28, 29, 30, 31 (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106922).

Body composition measurements

Body composition areas were analyzed using Slice-o-matic software (version 5.0, TomoVision) with predefined Hounsfield unit (HU) thresholds (−29 to 150 HU) to identify and segment muscle and fat regions. This study employed a single-slice assessment method, using the mid-third lumbar (L3) level as a landmark, to obtain preoperative axial abdominal CT images in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format. L3 level32,33 has been shown to highly correlate with whole-body composition mass. CT scans were analyzed by two trained Slice-o-matic analysts. The calculation formulas for body composition-related indicators are shown in Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106922. Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (n) and percentages (%), and comparisons between groups were carried out using the chi-square test. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using the independent t-test, while non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. The predictive ability was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and Pearson correlation analysis. Key parameters significantly associated with the endpoints were identified and combined into a composite index. Logarithmic transformation was applied to stabilize variance fluctuations and improve model fit. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted for candidate hematologic and body composition variables with post-operative complications and prolonged LOS. Subgroup analyses were conducted to identify high-risk populations influenced by inflammation and metabolism. To determine the optimal inflammatory-metabolic marker for predicting perioperative outcomes, the predictive performance was compared with that of CIMI using the area under the curve (AUC), deviance, Akaike information criterion (AIC), and Bayesian information criterion (BIC). The optimal inflammatory-metabolic marker was ultimately determined by integrating model stability metrics and external validation. All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS® version 25 (IBM, Armonk, NY) and R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was established at P < 0.05 (two-sided) for analyses, except for multivariate Cox proportional hazards models, for which it was established at P < 0.10.

Human rights statement and informed consent

All procedures were in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation (institutional and national) and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and later versions. Informed consent or substitute for it was obtained from all patients for being included in the study. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Fujian Medical University Union Hospital and the Affiliated Hospital of Putian University.

Model development and validation

Selection and construction of inflammatory-metabolic index

The existing integrated inflammatory-metabolic indicators demonstrate only moderate predictive performance for the incidence of overall complications and LOS > 11 days (Table 1). To further identify hematological parameters associated with these outcomes, Table 1 showed that white blood cell (WBC) count (AUC: 0.59, Pearson’s P = 0.005, correlation: positive), serum globulin (AUC: 0.61, P = 0.002, correlation: positive), total cholesterol (AUC: 0.58, P = 0.030, correlation: positive), and creatinine (AUC: 0.59, P = 0.009, correlation: positive) were significantly associated with the both endpoints.

Subsequent univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106922) identified serum globulin and WBC count as independent prognostic factors for both endpoints. Based on these findings, we developed a hematology-based inflammatory-metabolic indicator (IMI) by integrating these two key predictors and their correlation patterns.IMI=log[whitebloodcellcount(109/l)×serumglobulin(g/l)]

Correlation and prediction of CIMI and perioperative outcomes

Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to further assess the association between CIMI and perioperative outcomes. CIMI was positively correlated with operative duration (r = 0.167, P < 0.0001), intraoperative blood loss (r = 0.092, P = 0.029), and the time to initiation of post-operative liquid intake (r = 0.142, P = 0.0007). Conversely, a negative correlation was observed between CIMI and the number of retrieved lymph nodes (r = −0.150, P = 0.0003) (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106922).

In addition, ROC curve analysis indicated that CIMI exhibited markedly superior predictive performance for specific post-operative complications compared with GNRI, ALI, and CXI. For predicting digestive tract fistula (AUC: CIMI versus GNRI versus ALI versus CXI = 0.810 versus 0.569 versus 0.563 versus 0.576), abdominal lymphatic leakage (0.839 versus 0.541 versus 0.515 versus 0.495), abdominal infection (0.779 versus 0.663 versus 0.546 versus 0.563), and post-operative pneumonia (0.750 versus 0.635 versus 0.550 versus 0.530) (Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106922).

Robustness of CIMI-based risk stratification

Using the Youden index, the optimal cut-off value for CIMI was determined to be 215. Patients with CIMI ≤215 were categorized into the low-risk group, while those with CIMI >215 were assigned to the high-risk group. Sex, age, BMI, and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status differed significantly between the two risk groups (Supplementary Table S8, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106922).

To assess the robustness of the association between CIMI-based risk stratification and the incidence of overall complications, adjusted logistic regression models were constructed (Supplementary Figure S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106922). In the crude model, the high-risk group had a significantly higher odds of complications [odds ratio (OR) = 6.61, 95% confidence interval (CI) 4.08-10.70, P < 0.001]. Subgroup analyses were consistent, with no significant interactions (P for interaction >0.05). After adjusting for sex, age, BMI, and ECOG (model I), the association remained strong (OR = 7.71, 95% CI 4.42-13.45, P < 0.001). Stratified analyses confirmed higher complication risk in the high-risk group across subgroups defined by operative time, blood loss, lymph node retrieval, and chemotherapy timing (OR range: 4.43-13.05, all P < 0.05). In model II, further adjustment for tumor characteristics did not attenuate the association (OR = 7.77, 95% CI 4.39-13.75, P < 0.001). Subgroup results remained consistent, and no interactions reached statistical significance (P > 0.05). Additionally, similar findings were observed when predicting an LOS >11 days (Supplementary Figure S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106922).

Sensitivity analysis

In multivariable models, WBC and serum globulin (GLB) showed independent associations with both outcomes (complications: WBC OR = 1.070, P = 0.080; GLB OR = 1.078, P = 0.004; LOS >11 days: WBC OR = 1.229, P = 0.017; GLB OR = 1.080, P = 0.049) (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106922). VAI also remained independently associated with both endpoints (both P < 0.001), whereas the remaining body composition metrics were not significant after adjustment (Supplementary Tables S9 and S10, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106922). These results supported the final CIMI construction using WBC, GLB, and VAI and did not change the primary conclusions.

A regression-weighted sensitivity analysis was then conducted. A regression-weighted IMI was derived, and a regression-weighted CIMI was constructed using coefficients from multivariable logistic regression (Supplementary Tables S3 and S11, respectively, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106922). Discrimination was comparable between regression-weighted CIMI and the original CIMI for post-operative complications and prolonged LOS (LOS >11 days) in both cohorts, with no significant AUC differences by DeLong tests (Supplementary Figure S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106922). Finally, WBC was categorized as low (<4 × 109/l), normal (4-10 × 109/l), or elevated (>10 × 109/l) to assess clinical interpretability. Results were consistent with the primary analysis, and AUCs were similar to the original CIMI without significant differences by DeLong tests (Supplementary Figure S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106922).

Survival analysis of CIMI-based chemotherapy timing decisions

For both the CIMI low- and high-risk groups, 5-year OS did not differ significantly between time to chemotherapy (TTC) <4 weeks and TTC ≥4 weeks (CIMI-low group: 52.7% versus 55.2%, log-rank P = 0.951; CIMI-high group: 58.1% versus 56.2%, log-rank P = 0.109) or between TTC <5 weeks and TTC ≥5 weeks (CIMI-low group: 56.4% versus 48.1%, log-rank P = 0.109; CIMI-high group: 57.7% versus 55.3%, log-rank P = 0.650) (Supplementary Figure S8A and B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106922).

When TTC was stratified by a 6- or 7-week cut-off, differential survival patterns were observed between CIMI risk groups. In the CIMI low-risk group, patients with TTC <6 weeks had significantly better 5-year OS compared with those with TTC ≥6 weeks (56.0% versus 45.0%, log-rank P = 0.043). In contrast, no significant survival difference was observed in the CIMI high-risk group (56.8% versus 56.1%, log-rank P = 0.908). Similar trends were observed when using a 7-week cut-off (5-year OS for CIMI-low group: TTC <7 weeks versus TTC ≥7 weeks: 56.9% versus 30.8%, log-rank P < 0.001; 5-year OS for CIMI-high group: 57.9% versus 48.0%, log-rank P = 0.247) (Supplementary Figure S8C and D, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106922). Although no statistically significant difference was found in the CIMI high-risk group (57.9% versus 48.0%, log-rank P = 0.247), a favorable survival trend was noted for patients who initiated chemotherapy within 7 weeks (Supplementary Figure S8 D-CIMI-high group, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106922).

Online dynamic calculator of CIMI for clinical use

Based on the above findings, an online calculator was developed to facilitate clinical application of the comprehensive inflammatory-metabolic index for predicting the risk of the incidence of overall complications and an LOS >11 days in GC patients with stage II-III. The calculator is accessible at: https://onlinecimicalculator.shinyapps.io/CIMIcalculator/ (Supplementary Figure S9, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.106922).

Discussion

This study focused on the impact of preoperative systemic metabolic and inflammatory status on perioperative outcomes in patients with locally advanced GC undergoing laparoscopic gastrectomy. Based on routine preoperative hematological parameters and body composition data, CIMI was developed and validated to explore its association with the incidence of overall complications and post-operative recovery. The results demonstrated that CIMI effectively reflects systemic inflammatory and metabolic status and shows promising value in predicting short-term outcomes. These findings suggest that CIMI may serve as a useful tool for perioperative risk stratification and management in GC patients.

In recent years, increasing attention has been paid to enhancing the inpatient experience and promoting post-operative recovery.34 The occurrence of post-operative complications not only increases the risk of mortality and medical costs, however, but also significantly impairs the overall treatment experience. Accurately predicting the risk of overall complications and prolonged post-operative recovery before surgery holds important clinical value. Previous studies have shown that inflammation plays a crucial role in tumor progression and immune suppression.35 The host inflammatory response, such as tissue edema caused by local inflammation, can exacerbate tissue hypoxia, which in turn further amplifies the inflammatory process. This vicious cycle not only increases the risk of the incidence of overall complications after surgery but may also significantly impair the therapeutic effectiveness in cancer and other diseases.4,36 Several inflammatory indicators were associated with poor prognosis in various malignancies.27, 28, 29, 30, 31 There is still no consensus, however, on the optimal hematological biomarker for predicting short-term post-operative outcomes in GC patients.

In this study, logistic regression and Pearson’s correlation analyses revealed that preoperative WBC count and serum globulin concentrations were independently and positively associated with the incidence of overall complications and functional recovery to discharge level. Previous literature has indicated that elevated globulin concentrations are commonly observed in inflammation-related conditions, including infections, malignancies, hematological disorders, and connective tissue diseases.37, 38, 39 Higher globulin concentrations may reflect a heightened immune-metabolic state, often correlated with unfavorable clinical outcomes. WBC count, as one of the most widely used markers of systemic inflammation, has also been reported to predict prognosis in various cancers.40 A recent review on the relationship between complete blood count and metabolic diseases suggested that WBC enhances the sensitivity and specificity of preoperative markers for predicting metabolic dysfunction.41 In addition, a study by Li et al.42 confirmed that WBC is an effective indicator for evaluating metabolic disorders. Together, WBC and globulin synergistically contribute to the host’s inflammatory-metabolic response. Based on their strong correlation with clinical outcomes, we constructed a hematological IMI and demonstrated that it significantly outperformed previously reported hematological combination models in predicting short-term post-operative outcomes.

Recent studies on the tumor micro-environment have confirmed that increased levels of inflammatory and adipokine-related factors in obese individuals contribute to micro-environmental alterations. Tumor cells rely on nutrients, growth factors, and other molecules derived from adipocytes to support tumor development, invasion, and metastasis.43 In addition, substantial evidence suggests that adipose tissue is not only a major structural component of the human body, but also functions as a proinflammatory endocrine organ. Specifically, visceral adipose tissue secretes a variety of bioactive molecules with systemic inflammatory effects and mediates signaling through autocrine, paracrine, and endocrine pathways, thereby playing a key role in regulating chronic inflammation.44,45 The traditional view holds that the chronic low-grade inflammatory state induced by obesity-related hormones and cytokines contributes to poor clinical outcomes.46,47 A retrospective cohort study demonstrated that visceral obesity is associated with a higher risk of anastomotic leakage, pneumonia, surgical site infection, and prolonged hospitalization.48 In the present study, we found that visceral adipose tissue at the L3 vertebral level was significantly associated with both the incidence of overall complications and prolonged post-operative recovery time in both male and female patients. Its predictive value surpassed that of subcutaneous and intermuscular fat compartments, consistent with findings from previous studies by Sugisawa et al.49 and Tokunaga et al.50 Based on these findings, we further integrated the VAI with IMI to construct the CIMI, aiming to more comprehensively reflect the systemic inflammatory and metabolic status of patients. The multiplicative term was log-transformed when constructing the IMI. This transformation compresses right-skewed values, reduces the influence of extreme observations on parameter estimation, and mitigates scale inflation inherent to a multiplicative structure, thereby improving the robustness and reproducibility of the composite index. Log transformation of multiplicative inflammatory indices is widely used, including for composite indices such as the SII [e.g. ln (SII) or log-transformed SII].51, 52, 53, 54

Previous studies have also proposed integrated indicators combining hematological parameters with body composition measurements to predict short-term outcomes in cancer patients. Bouillanne et al.27 developed the GNRI by integrating body weight and serum albumin concentrations, and found it to be significantly associated with the incidence of overall complications. Similarly, ALI and CXI introduced by Horino et al.30 and Brown et al.31 were linked to post-operative complications and adverse outcomes in cancer patients. When comparing the predictive performance among the four indicators, CIMI consistently demonstrated superior accuracy to GNRI, ALI, and CXI. PR curve analysis further showed that CIMI yielded a higher AUPRC, and DCA revealed that CIMI provided greater net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities compared with GNRI, ALI, and CXI. Further analysis showed that CIMI also outperformed GNRI, ALI, and CXI in predicting specific post-operative complications, including digestive tract fistula, abdominal lymphatic leakage, intra-abdominal infection, and post-operative pneumonia.

Moreover, we found that CIMI values were positively correlated with operative time, intraoperative blood loss, and the time to post-operative oral intake, while negatively correlated with the number of retrieved lymph nodes. These associations may be attributable to the underlying pathophysiological states reflected by the components of CIMI, which integrates VAI, WBC count, and serum globulin. A higher VAI often indicates increased technical difficulty during surgery due to altered intra-abdominal anatomy, which may prolong operative duration and hinder lymph node dissection.55, 56, 57 In parallel, elevated inflammatory hematological parameters may suggest underlying pathological conditions such as tissue edema and friability, both of which can increase the risk of intraoperative bleeding and further prolong the procedure.58 Additionally, previous studies have shown that the duration of anesthesia-induced gastrointestinal suppression is closely related to visceral fat content, with patients exhibiting higher visceral adiposity tending to experience delayed recovery of oral intake.59 This may explain the observed positive correlation between CIMI and time to post-operative feeding.

All patients included in this study were GC patients who received post-operative adjuvant chemotherapy according to clinical guidelines. We observed that patients with delayed initiation of chemotherapy (TTC >4 or 5 weeks) had significantly higher CIMI values compared with those without delay. This suggests that patients with higher preoperative inflammatory-metabolic burden may experience prolonged post-operative recovery, leading to delayed chemotherapy initiation.

Our findings should be interpreted with caution due to several limitations. First, although the data were derived from four prospective clinical studies, the number of external validation centers was limited. Second, the multi-parameter nature of CIMI may limit its direct application in clinical settings. To address this, we developed an online calculator to simplify its use and reduce the risk of manual calculation errors. In addition, only standardized L3-based indices were evaluated, and more granular muscle metrics (e.g. psoas-based measures or inter-muscle ratios) were not assessed, which may provide additional prognostic information. Post-operative chemotherapy initiation is also influenced by post-operative recovery and real-world factors such as treatment access and social support; therefore, drivers of chemotherapy delay beyond CIMI could not be fully captured in the present analysis. Patients who received neoadjuvant therapy were excluded, as such treatments may alter baseline inflammatory-metabolic status; thus, further studies are warranted to evaluate the utility of CIMI in this population.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated CIMI as a novel predictive tool for perioperative outcomes in GC patients undergoing laparoscopic gastrectomy. CIMI demonstrated superior performance in identifying high-risk patients and predicting complications and prolonged hospital stays, providing valuable insights for clinical decision-making.
