Prophylactic hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy is associated with improved disease-free survival in pathologic T4 colorectal cancer: a propensity score–matched cohort study
Authors: Rongpeng Chen, Sihan Hu, Zhicheng Feng, Feng Xiao, Guoqiang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPathologic T4 colorectal cancer (CRC) carries a high risk of peritoneal recurrence despite curative resection. Early postoperative hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) may enhance intraperitoneal cytotoxic exposure and improve recurrence control. We evaluated whether prophylactic HIPEC administered within one week of surgery improves outcomes in pathologic T4 (pT4) CRC.MethodsWe conducted a single-center, retrospective cohort study of consecutive patients with pathologically confirmed pT4 colorectal adenocarcinoma (2018–2022). Prophylactic HIPEC consisted of intraperitoneal 5-fluorouracil (650 mg/m2) perfused for 60 min at 43.0 ± 0.1 °C within one postoperative week. A 1:2 propensity score match yielded a balanced cohort (99 HIPEC; 198 non-HIPEC). The primary endpoint was disease-free survival (DFS); secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS) and site-specific recurrence. Robustness of findings was examined with sensitivity analyses.ResultsAmong 690 screened patients, 471 were eligible (155 HIPEC; 316 non-HIPEC). In the matched cohort, Kaplan–Meier comparisons were not statistically significant for DFS (log-rank p = 0.074) or OS (p = 0.460). In multivariable Cox models, HIPEC was associated with improved DFS (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.58, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.38–0.88; p = 0.012) but not OS (HR = 0.72, 95% CI 0.41–1.24; p = 0.237). Peritoneal, hepatic, and pulmonary recurrence rates were comparable between groups, while other-site recurrence was lower with HIPEC (5.1% in HIPEC and 13.1% in non-HIPEC; p = 0.032). Results were directionally consistent across sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsProphylactic HIPEC delivered within one postoperative week was associated with improved DFS, without an observed OS advantage over the study interval, in patients with pT4 CRC. These data support selective use of early postoperative HIPEC as an adjunct to curative resection, with patient counseling focused on anticipated gains in recurrence control and the current uncertainty regarding long-term survival.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04071-y.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Disease-free survival, Early postoperative period, Hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy, Peritoneal metastasis, Propensity score matching, Recurrence patterns, Pathologic T4

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains one of the most prevalent and lethal malignancies worldwide, with approximately 1.15 million new cases and 577,000 deaths reported globally in 2020 [1]. Among CRC subtypes, T4-stage disease is defined by tumor penetration through the visceral peritoneum (T4a) or invasion into adjacent organs or structures (T4b) and is associated with substantial surgical challenges and a heightened risk of recurrence despite advances in multimodal therapy. Even after curative-intent margin-negative (R0) resection, patients with T4 CRC frequently develop peritoneal metastasis (PM), compromising long-term outcomes [2–4].

Peritoneal recurrence accounts for a considerable proportion of treatment failures in pathologic T4 CRC, with postoperative incidence reported at approximately 15%–25% in prior studies. For example, Uppal et al. noted an 18% peritoneal relapse rate in T4 colon cancer despite complete surgical resection [5]. These observations underscore the challenge posed by undetected microscopic peritoneal dissemination and residual intraperitoneal free cancer cells (IFCCs), which likely contribute to suboptimal disease-free survival (DFS) in this high-risk subgroup [6].

Systemic adjuvant chemotherapy remains the standard of care for resected stage III and high-risk stage II CRC; however, its activity against peritoneal micrometastases is limited by the peritoneal–plasma barrier, which restricts drug penetration into the peritoneal cavity [7, 8]. Consequently, patients with PM often respond poorly to systemic therapy, with median overall survival (OS) of approximately 16 months reported in this setting [9]. These limitations highlight the need for complementary locoregional strategies to eradicate residual peritoneal disease.

Prophylactic hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) has been explored as such a locoregional approach in high-risk CRC. In contrast to purely normothermic intraperitoneal infusions, prophylactic HIPEC can be administered in the early postoperative period (within 1 week of resection), providing hyperthermia-augmented, high intraperitoneal drug exposure. Hyperthermia enhances peritoneal drug penetration, exerts direct cytotoxic effects, and facilitates mechanical clearance of IFCCs, thereby aiming to reduce subsequent peritoneal metastases [10]. Nonetheless, prior clinical studies evaluating prophylactic HIPEC have yielded mixed results, and its role in patients with locally advanced (T4) CRC without visible peritoneal disease remains incompletely defined [11–13].

To address this question, we conducted a retrospective, propensity score–matched cohort study to evaluate whether adding prophylactic postoperative HIPEC (within 1 week of surgery) to curative resection improves outcomes in patients with pathologically confirmed T4 colorectal adenocarcinoma. The primary endpoint was DFS; secondary endpoints included OS and site-specific recurrence.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery, The Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University. The protocol was approved by the institutional review board, and the requirement for additional informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective design.

A total of 690 consecutive patients with pathologically confirmed pT4 colorectal adenocarcinoma who underwent curative-intent resection between January 2018 and December 2022 were identified from the institutional database. Patients were excluded if they had macroscopic peritoneal metastases detected by preoperative imaging, intraoperative exploration, or postoperative pathology (n = 121); underwent conversion to palliative-intent surgery or emergency procedures (n = 25); or had incomplete follow-up or missing key clinical variables (n = 73) (Fig. 1). After exclusions, 471 patients were eligible for analysis: 155 received prophylactic HIPEC and 316 underwent surgery alone (non-HIPEC group; Table 1).

Baseline variables were extracted from electronic medical records and included demographics (age, sex), tumor characteristics (location, T stage, N stage, histologic grade, lymphovascular invasion, perineural invasion), perioperative status (American Society of Anesthesiologists [ASA] physical status), postoperative complications (graded by the Clavien–Dindo system), and adjuvant chemotherapy (regimen and completion status). Adjuvant systemic chemotherapy was categorized as “no/incomplete” (< 6 cycles) or “completed” (≥ 6 cycles). Regimens included FOLFOX (leucovorin/5-fluorouracil/oxaliplatin) or CapeOX (capecitabine/oxaliplatin), typically initiated 6–8 weeks postoperatively according to pathological staging and multidisciplinary recommendations.

Hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC)

For patients planned for prophylactic HIPEC, four multi-holed silicone catheters were placed intraoperatively under direct visualization—two inflow and two outflow—and left in situ. HIPEC was then administered postoperatively within 1 week of the index resection using a closed-abdomen circulation system, as recommended by the 2025 Chinese expert consensus and international guidelines. The perfusate comprised 4–6 L of isotonic solution containing 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) at 650 mg/m2, circulated at 400–600 mL/min. The intraperitoneal temperature was maintained at 43.0 ± 0.1 °C for 60 min.

Follow-up and outcome definitions

Patients were captured in an institutional follow-up database and scheduled for assessments every 3 months after surgery. Surveillance included clinical evaluation, structured telephone follow-up, and thin-slice computed tomography (CT) to determine recurrence or metastatic dissemination.

The primary endpoint was DFS, defined as the time from surgery to the first documented recurrence or death from any cause, whichever occurred first; patients without an event were censored at the last follow-up. The secondary endpoint was OS, defined as the time from surgery to death from any cause. Recurrence sites were classified as peritoneal, hepatic, pulmonary, local, or other.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using Student’s t-test; categorical variables are presented as n (%) and compared with chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

To minimize selection bias, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed in a 1:2 ratio (HIPEC: non-HIPEC) using nearest-neighbor matching. Matching covariates were age, sex, ASA grade, and tumor location. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with SMD < 0.10 indicating adequate balance (Table 1; Supplementary Fig. 1).

Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). In the matched cohort, the primary multivariable model adjusted for T stage, N stage, histologic grade, lymphovascular invasion, and perineural invasion (Table 2). An extended model further adjusted for adjuvant chemotherapy completion and the Clavien–Dindo grade of postoperative complications (Supplementary Table 1). As a sensitivity analysis, a multivariable Cox model was also fitted in the unmatched cohort using the same tumor-related covariates as the primary model (Supplementary Table 1).

Results

Patient selection and cohort characteristics

A total of 690 patients with pathologically confirmed pT4 colorectal cancer were initially considered for inclusion. After applying exclusion criteria (peritoneal metastasis, emergency surgeries, incomplete follow-up, or missing data), 471 patients remained eligible for the final analysis cohort, consisting of 155 patients in the HIPEC group and 316 in the non-HIPEC group (Fig. 1).

Propensity score matching (1:2 nearest-neighbor matching) was performed to adjust for baseline differences between groups, resulting in a matched cohort of 297 patients (99 HIPEC; 198 non-HIPEC).

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics before and after matching. There were significant differences in age (p < 0.001), ASA grade (p < 0.001), and tumor location (p < 0.001) prior to matching. However, after matching, the SMDs for the covariates involved in matching were all below 0.10, indicating adequate balance between the HIPEC and non-HIPEC groups (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Survival and Cox proportional hazards models

Kaplan–Meier analyses in the matched cohort showed no statistically significant difference in DFS (Fig. 2A; log-rank p = 0.074) or OS (Fig. 2B; log-rank p = 0.460) between treatment groups.

Kaplan–Meier survival curves for DFS and OS in the matched cohort. Kaplan–Meier survival curves for DFS (A) and OS (B). P-values from log-rank tests are provided for comparisons between HIPEC and non-HIPEC groups

In adjusted Cox models (Table 2), HIPEC was associated with improved DFS (HR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.38–0.88, p = 0.012), whereas the association with OS was not statistically significant (HR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.41–1.24, p = 0.237). Poor histologic grade was associated with worse DFS (HR = 1.99, 95% CI: 1.28–3.09, p = 0.002) and OS (HR = 3.51, 95% CI: 2.02–6.12, p < 0.001). N stage 2 was associated with worse DFS (HR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.17–3.37, p = 0.012) and OS (HR = 2.11, 95% CI: 1.09–4.09, p = 0.026).

Subgroup analysis

We assessed the HIPEC effect on DFS across prespecified strata—sex; age (< 60 or ≥ 60 years); ASA grade (I–II or III); tumor location (right colon, left colon, rectum); T stage (4a or 4b); N stage (0, 1, or 2); histologic grade (well/moderate or poor); lymphovascular invasion; and perineural invasion (Fig. 3). No significant treatment-by-subgroup interactions were observed. Point estimates generally favored HIPEC (HR < 1.0) across strata, with statistically significant associations in patients aged < 60 years (HR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.23–0.87, p = 0.017) and in left-sided colon tumors (HR = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.17–0.82, p = 0.014).

Recurrence patterns

In the matched cohort, no significant differences were observed in peritoneal recurrence (11.1% in HIPEC and 14.1% in non-HIPEC; p = 0.466), hepatic recurrence (18.2% and 24.2%, respectively; p = 0.236), or pulmonary recurrence (10.1% and 15.7%, respectively; p = 0.191). The rate of other-site recurrence was significantly lower in HIPEC than in non-HIPEC (5.1% and 13.1%, respectively; p = 0.032) (Table 3).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to further validate the robustness of our findings (Supplementary Table 1). In the unmatched cohort, HIPEC was associated with improved DFS (HR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.40–0.82, p = 0.002), while OS was not significantly different (HR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.46–1.15, p = 0.171). Similar patterns were observed in the matched cohort. An extended model additionally adjusting for chemotherapy completion and Clavien–Dindo classification yielded consistent results for DFS (HR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.39–0.92, p = 0.019), with OS remaining non-significant (HR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.42–1.28, p = 0.281).

Discussion

In this propensity score–matched single-center cohort of pathologically confirmed pT4 colorectal cancer, prophylactic hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) delivered within one postoperative week was associated with improved disease-free survival (DFS), whereas overall survival (OS) did not differ significantly between groups. The DFS association remained after multivariable adjustment in the matched cohort and was reproduced in sensitivity analyses, including the unmatched cohort and an extended model additionally adjusting for chemotherapy completion and postoperative complication severity. Site-specific recurrence profiling indicated no between-group differences in peritoneal, hepatic, or pulmonary recurrences, while other-site recurrence was lower with HIPEC, suggesting a reduction in overall recurrence burden without a peritoneal-exclusive effect.

These clinical signals are biologically plausible and consistent with established intraperitoneal pharmacology. Peritoneal recurrence in pT4 disease is plausibly driven by microscopic dissemination of intraperitoneal free cancer cells (IFCCs) through serosal breach and operative manipulation, while the peritoneal–plasma barrier limits the effectiveness of systemically delivered agents within the peritoneal cavity [2, 14, 15]. Intraperitoneal 5-fluorouracil achieves high peritoneal exposure; hyperthermia at 43 °C adds direct cytotoxicity via membrane and protein injury, increases membrane permeability, and augments tissue penetration to a depth of a few millimeters [15–17]. Hyperthermia may also disrupt tumor microvasculature, suppress angiogenesis, and modulate antitumor immune signaling, whereas continuous perfusion promotes more homogeneous convection-driven distribution and physical lavage of IFCCs and debris. Administering HIPEC within the first postoperative week coincides with the period of peak intraperitoneal tumor-cell shedding. It also precedes adhesion formation that can restrict peritoneal distribution of the perfusate, and may therefore reduce implantation risk [18]. Nonetheless, because routine peritoneal cytology and pharmacokinetic assessments were not performed, these mechanistic links remain inferential and should be validated in future studies incorporating standardized intraperitoneal sampling.

Against this mechanistic backdrop, prior trials show that timing, regimen, and endpoint selection critically shape observed benefits. In high-risk but non-metastatic settings, COLOPEC randomized trial—in which oxaliplatin HIPEC was administered predominantly 5–8 weeks after resection—did not reduce peritoneal metastasis or improve DFS/OS, whereas HIPECT4 randomized trial—using intraoperative mitomycin-C HIPEC—achieved a marked reduction in locoregional (peritoneal) recurrences without translating into DFS/OS gains. IOCCRC randomized trial, which employed intraoperative regional 5-fluorouracil, was overall neutral for DFS/OS but suggested benefit in selected colon-cancer subgroups with fewer peritoneal or hepatic failures [12, 19, 20]. Taken together, these data indicate that short- to mid-term control endpoints—peritoneal or locoregional control and global DFS—are the domains most likely to improve, whereas consistent OS benefit remains elusive. Our findings—DFS improvement with OS neutrality using early postoperative 5-fluorouracil HIPEC within one week—fit this pattern and underscore that protocolization (drug, temperature–duration, immediate/early timing) and appropriate patient selection are central to realizing clinically meaningful benefit.

Limitations warrant consideration. As a retrospective, single-center analysis, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded despite adjustment. Selective use of additional postoperative intraperitoneal perfusions within the HIPEC group may have introduced heterogeneity. The lack of routine peritoneal cytology or pharmacokinetic assessment constrains mechanistic interpretation. Capture of non-standard adjuvant targeted or immune therapies was incomplete. Finally, the study was not powered to detect modest OS differences, and generalizability to other institutions or protocols using different drugs, carrier solutions, or perfusion parameters may be limited.

In summary, prophylactic HIPEC administered within one postoperative week after curative resection may reduce recurrence risk and improve DFS in patients with pT4 colorectal cancer, while OS remained unchanged over the observed interval. In light of mixed external evidence and variability in protocols and endpoints, clinical adoption should be individualized. Patient counseling ought to emphasize the expectation of recurrence-control gains, the current uncertainty regarding long-term survival, and the importance of aligning candidate selection and HIPEC parameters with the intended endpoints of care.
