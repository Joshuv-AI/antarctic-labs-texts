A quadral biosignature of IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-12 and IP-10 increases the diagnostic potential for latent tuberculosis among diabetic patients in Uganda
Authors: Phillip Ssekamatte, Diana Sitenda, Rose Nabatanzi, Marjorie Nakibuule, Davis Kibirige, Andrew Peter Kyazze, David Patrick Kateete, Bernard Ssentalo Bagaya, Obondo James Sande, Reinout van Crevel, Stephen Cose, Irene Andia Biraro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundType 2 diabetes mellitus (DM) increases both the risk of acquiring latent tuberculosis (TB) infection (LTBI) and progression to active TB. The immunological mechanisms underlying the increased susceptibility remain poorly understood. This study aimed to elucidate the impact of DM on Mtb-specific cytokine and chemokine responses and identify potential biomarkers for LTBI regardless of DM status.MethodsThis cross-sectional study recruited 164 participants with LTBI-DM (n = 51), LTBI-only (n = 63), DM-only (n = 12) and healthy controls (n = 38) at Kiruddu National Referral Hospital. Cytokine and chemokine responses were measured in ESAT-6 and CFP-10 whole blood stimulated supernatants using the Luminex assay. Linear regression with age as a covariate and false discovery rate (p < 0.050) correction were performed on log2-transformed concentrations. For biomarker analysis, comparisons were performed for LTBI (LTBI-DM + LTBI-only) versus no LTBI (DM-only + healthy controls) groups, and receiver operating characteristic analyses were performed for both univariate and multivariate analyses.ResultsLTBI-DM was associated with decreased Mtb-specific IFN-γ (padjusted=0.006), TNF (padjusted=0.023), IL-12 (padjusted=0.004), IP-10 (padjusted=0.004), IL-8 (padjusted=0.030) and IL-10 (padjusted=0.043) responses compared to LTBI-only. Univariate analysis identified IFN-γ (AUC: 0.80, sensitivity: 81%, specificity: 78%), IL-2 (0.84, 75%, 88%), IL-12 (0.77, 65%, 82%) and IP-10 (0.73, 77%, 62%) as top-performing biomarkers for LTBI identification regardless of DM status. A combined four-marker biosignature yielded an AUC of 0.92 (CI: 0.88–0.97) with 76% sensitivity and 96% specificity.ConclusionDiabetes mellitus impairs Th1, inflammatory, and regulatory cytokine and chemokine responses during LTBI. A multivariate biosignature of IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-12 and IP-10 outperforms single-marker assays (e.g., variability in IFN-γ due to immunosuppression in DM) and offers robust identification of LTBI regardless of DM status, highlighting the additive value of combining mediators that capture distinct immunological axes. The improved biosignature specificity may minimise false positives in DM populations, who often exhibit comorbidities that could mimic LTBI-associated inflammation. Background

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM) is a major tuberculosis (TB) risk factor, increasing the risk for latent TB infection (LTBI) acquisition [1] with a greater than three-fold risk for the development of active TB disease [2]. In 2019, over 15% of the global TB cases were estimated to have DM [3]. However, given the impact of DM on TB risk, the immunological processes for this susceptibility are still unclear. Latent TB infection is an asymptomatic state characterised by persistent Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) in a dormant or low-replication state without clinical signs or radiological evidence of active TB disease [4]. Even though it is estimated that one-quarter of the world’s population is Mtb-infected, and, therefore, has LTBI [5, 6], a small proportion (5–15%) is thought to be truly Mtb-infected with a high risk of incident TB [7]. Nevertheless, this presents a large TB infection reservoir and significant uncertainty regarding the Mtb transmission dynamics and progression to active TB disease [5]. The asymptomatic state can persist for many years [8]; however, DM can compromise the protective immunity [9], leading to reactivation and susceptibility for progression to active TB disease. Therefore, the detection, prevention and treatment of LTBI before it is transmitted or becomes infectious is a key strategy for TB elimination [10].

Type 2 DM is a disease characterised by chronic hyperglycaemia and dyslipidaemia that cause a non-autoimmune heterogeneously progressive loss of islet β-cell insulin secretion in the presence of insulin resistance (IR) [11]. In 2024, more than 589 million people were living with DM, a number that is expected to increase to 853 million people by 2050, with 3.4 million deaths [12]. The increase in the prevalence of DM has been paralleled by an increased incidence of TB, especially in low and middle-income countries (LMICs). About 95% of TB and 75% of DM cases live in LMICs [13–15], with the global prevalence of TB-DM comorbidity being about 15% [3]. The immunological interplay between LTBI and DM is complex; DM-induced hyperglycemia can alter innate and adaptive immune responses [9], leading to compromised cellular responses [16, 17], as well as diminished Th1,Th17 cytokine and chemokine expression profiles [16, 17, 18]. The decreased cytokine and chemokine responses are critical for controlling Mtb infection [19].

The diagnostic landscape for LTBI primarily relies on immunological assays that detect host T-cell memory responses to Mtb antigens intradermally or in plasma. The World Health Organisation currently recommends the tuberculin skin test (TST) and interferon (IFN)-γ release assays (IGRA) to test for TB infection [20]. The two common IGRAs, TB-SPOT.TB and QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus (QFT-Plus) are based on peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) or whole blood early secretory antigen target-6 (ESAT-6) and culture filtrate protein-10 (CFP-10) peptide stimulation to induce an Mtb-specific IFN-γ response that is measured by enzyme-linked immunoSpot (ELISpot) or enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), respectively [21, 22]. While these recommended tests measure T-cell memory responses to Mtb-specific antigens, they quantify responses to only IFN-γ and their sensitivity and specificity can be influenced by immunosuppression [23, 24]. Therefore, additional biomarkers may improve sensitivities and specificities, thus enhancing LTBI detection, especially in the face of DM. Beyond IFN-γ, studies have shown that interleukin (IL)−2, IL-12, granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), IFN-γ-inducible protein 10 (IP-10) and their combinations have promise in distinguishing LTBI from active TB disease and uninfected states [25–29]. Whether combinations of these biomarkers offer improved LTBI diagnostic accuracy in populations with comorbid DM warranted further investigation. This study aimed to elucidate the impact of DM on Mtb-specific cytokine and chemokine responses during LTBI, and to determine potential biomarkers that can identify LTBI regardless of DM status.

Methods and materials

Study participants

Participants with LTBI-DM, LTBI-only, DM-only, and healthy controls (HC) were enrolled in an exploratory nested cohort from October 2017 to March 2018 at Kiruddu National Referral Hospital in Kampala, Uganda. This cohort was part of the TAD study, which assessed the frequency of innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), T cells, and B cells in LTBI and T2DM participants, first described here [30]. Type 2 DM participants were newly diagnosed and, therefore, were not on DM treatment or TB preventive therapy that could affect immune function. Participants underwent a TB symptom screen to rule out active TB. Latent TB infection was diagnosed using a positive QFT-plus ELISA assay, following the manufacturer’s recommendations. Type 2 DM was diagnosed based on glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) levels ≥ 6.5%. All participants were HIV seronegative. A total of 164 participants were recruited, of which 51 had LTBI-DM, 63 had LTBI-only, 12 had DM-only, and 38 were HC.

Interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA; cell stimulation and culture)

The IGRA assay, QFT-plus (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), was performed as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Approximately 1 ml of blood collected by venepuncture was drawn into each of four QFT-plus tubes: Nil (negative control, heparin); TB1 (Mtb-specific ESAT-6 and CFP-10 peptides for CD4+ T cell stimulation); TB2 (Mtb-specific peptides for both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells); and Mitogen (positive control, phytohemagglutinin). The QFT-plus tubes were inverted 10 times post-collection and transported within 3 h to the immunology laboratory, Makerere University, for processing. Samples were inverted again before 37 °C incubation for 24 h. The samples were centrifuged at 2000xg, and the supernatants were harvested and stored at −80 °C until analysis, including LTBI testing and the Luminex® assay.

Luminex

The Luminex experiments utilised supernatants from TB2 cell culture stimulations containing Mtb-specific ESAT-6 and CFP-10 peptides optimised to stimulate CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses. Nil supernatants provided background-corrected Mtb-specific responses from the TB2 stimulations. After thawing and randomising the samples, they were assayed using 17-plex Luminex® kits (R&D Systems, Inc, Minneapolis, MN, USA) to measure the following analytes: tumour necrosis factor (TNF), IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-4, IL-2, IL-12, IL-17 A, IL-1β, IFN-γ, IFN-α, IP-10, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), macrophage inflammatory protein (MIP)−1α, MIP-1β, GM-CSF and c-reactive protein (CRP); to assess several Th1, Th2, Th17, inflammatory and regulatory cytokines and chemokines. The analysis was performed using the Luminex® 200 system with xPONENT 3.1 software (Merck, Millipore). Concentration values and detection limits were determined and extrapolated from standard curves generated with the specific standards from the Luminex kit, utilising the xPONENT weighted 5PL curve-fitting procedure.

Data analysis and visualisation

All data from this study were quality-controlled to remove rows of cytokine and chemokine concentrations with “NA” values. For values below the lower detection limit or above the upper detection limit, we assigned them the corresponding lower or upper values based on the lowest (standard 6) and highest (standard 1) assay standards. All raw cytokine concentrations showed skewed distributions; therefore, log2-transformed data were used for analyses. To identify significant differences in cytokine and chemokine concentrations between individual groups (LTBI-DM versus LTBI, LTBI-DM versus HC, LTBI versus HC and LTBI-DM versus DM), separate linear regression models were fitted for each pairwise combination of groups while adjusting for age. The resulting p-values were corrected for False Discovery Rate (FDR) using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. These were log10-transformed and plotted versus the Log2FC values to generate volcano plots. Cytokine and chemokine concentrations among the two-group comparisons with FDR < 0.050 were considered statistically significant.

For biomarker analysis, the data were re-grouped into two groups: LTBI (LTBI-DM + LTBI-only) and no LTBI (DM-only + healthy controls), regardless of DM status, to assess biomarkers that can identify LTBI. From this, univariate and multivariate biomarker analyses were performed, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) plots and area under the curves (AUCs) were generated for LTBI versus without LTBI. For univariate analysis, we performed the best optimal cut-off, herein indicated as the threshold, 95% confidence intervals (CI) via 2000 bootstrapping replicates, and the p-values were calculated by the Mann-Whitney U-test. The best-performing biomarkers with the AUC, sensitivity and specificity from the univariate analysis, relevance to LTBI pathology, and potential of scalability for clinical translation were combined into a four-marker biosignature. Combined multivariate ROC curves and AUCs were calculated using a generalised linear regression model. Additionally, ROC analysis stratified by LTBI or DM status was performed for LTBI-DM versus DM, LTBI-DM versus HC, LTBI versus HC and LTBI-DM versus LTBI to assess the influence of DM on biomarkers for LTBI identification.

Statistical analysis of participant characteristics was reported after performing the Kruskal-Wallis test. All data and statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The package ‘Limma’ was used for linear regression analysis, utilising an empirical Bayes approach to adjust the standard errors of the estimated log-fold changes [31]. The package ‘pROC’ was used for ROC analyses [32], and ‘ggplot2’ was used to visualise the results for all plots [33]. For completeness, we report p-values (after adjusting for covariates) and adjusted p-values (after adjusting for FDR) for linear regression analysis.

Results

Description of participant characteristics

Among the 164 participants, the median age was 40 years (IQR: 32–48), with significantly higher ages in the LTBI-DM (47 years) and DM-only (43 years) groups compared to healthy controls (34 years; p < 0.0001); 72.6% were female. Diabetic groups (LTBI-DM and DM-only) showed higher systolic (median: 143 and 133 mmHg) and diastolic blood pressures (92 and 88 mmHg); p = 0.003 and p < 0.001, respectively, as well as a higher waist-to-hip ratio (p = 0.010). Glycaemic measures were markedly elevated in the LTBI-DM and DM-only groups, with FBS medians of 12.2 and 14.3 mmol/L and HbA1c medians of 9.4 and 10.0%, respectively, compared to much lower levels in LTBI-only and HC (p < 0.0001). Lipid profiles showed elevated total cholesterol (5.5 mmol/L) and LDL (3.3 and 3.1 mmol/L) levels in the LTBI-DM and DM groups, with significant differences across groups (p = 0.003 and p = 0.002), respectively. These findings describe the expected demographic and clinical characteristics, consistent with the epidemiology of DM, among the diabetic groups (LTBI-DM and DM-only), particularly age, blood pressure, glycemic control, and lipid profiles, as shown in Table 1.

DM is associated with decreased Mtb-specific Th-1, inflammatory and regulatory responses in LTBI-DM compared to LTBI-only participants

Upon linear regression, LTBI-DM participants were associated with decreased Mtb-specific IFN-γ (−0.76 [0.30།0.73], padjusted=0.006), TNF (།0.82 [0.25།0.78], padjusted=0.023), IL-12 (།0.41 [0.53།0.83], padjusted=0.004), IP-10 (།0.65 [0.37།0.73], padjusted=0.004), IL-8 (།0.27 [0.63།0.93], padjusted=0.030) and IL-10 (།0.38 [0.50།0.93], padjusted=0.043) responses compared to LTBI-only participants after FDR adjustment (Fig. 1a). Additionally, compared to the healthy controls, LTBI-DM participants were associated with increased Mtb-specific IFN-γ (0.65 [1.13།3.22], padjusted=0.038), IL-2 (0.80 [1.41།3.51], padjusted=0.012), IL-12 (0.34 [1.09།1.83], padjusted=0.034), IP-10 (0.48 [1.08།2.40], padjusted=0.040), VEGF (0.28 [1.08།1.61], padjusted=0.034), IFN-α (0.54 [1.19།2.47], padjusted=0.022) and IL-10 (0.44 [1.09།2.22], padjusted=0.038) responses after FDR adjustment (Fig. 1b). LTBI-only participants were associated with increased Mtb-specific IFN- γ (1.41 [2.56།6.55], padjusted<0.0001), IL-2 (1.00 [1.80།4.08], padjusted<0.0001), IL-12 (0.75 [1.67།2.67], padjusted<0.0001), IL-4 (0.31 [1.13།1.64], padjusted=0.003), IL-17 A (0.68 [1.32།2.92], padjusted=0.002), IP-10 (1.13 [2.17།4.43], padjusted<0.0001), IL-8 (0.32 [1.11།1.68], padjusted=0.007), GM-CSF (0.87 [1.69།3.36], padjusted<0.0001), VEGF (0.25 [1.06།1.53], padjusted=0.015) and IL-10 (0.82 [1.65།3.13], padjusted<0.0001) responses compared to HC participants after FDR adjustment (Fig. 1c). Lastly, compared to DM-only, LTBI-DM participants were associated with increased Mtb-specific IL-2 (0.98 [1.41།5.05], padjusted=0.049) responses after FDR adjustment (Fig. 1d). IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-12 and IP-10 are potential LTBI biomarkers regardless of DM status

We lastly evaluated the Mtb-specific cytokine and chemokine concentrations as potential biomarkers to identify LTBI regardless of DM status. The cytokine and chemokine data were regrouped into two categories: LTBI (LTBI-DM + LTBI-only) and no LTBI (DM-only + HC). For univariate analysis, ROC curves were plotted per cytokine and chemokine for this comparison, and the resulting AUCs, sensitivities, specificities and 95% CI were calculated (Fig. 2; Table 2). The four (IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-12 and IP-10) cytokines that met the selection criteria were incorporated into a multivariate biosignature, and their LTBI identification effectiveness was tested. IFN-γ [AUC (CI): 0.80 (0.72–0.80); 81% sensitivity and 78% specificity], IL-2 [0.84 (0.77–0.84); 75% sensitivity and 88% specificity], IL-12 [0.77 (0.69–0.77); 65% sensitivity and 82% specificity] and IP-10 [0.73 (0.65–0.73); 77% sensitivity and 62% specificity] were the best individual cytokines at identifying LTBI (Fig. 2a, b, d, g; Table 2). Inclusion into a multivariate biosignature using generalised linear regression modelling resulted in improved AUC, sensitivity and specificity [LTBI vs. no LTBI: 0.92 (0.88–0.97); 76% sensitivity and 96% specificity] (Fig. 3). Additionally, ROC analysis stratified by LTBI or DM status resulted in the following ROC results for IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-12 and IP-10 respectively: 0.82 (0.73–0.82), 0.86 (0.78–0.86), 0.85 (0.77–0.85) and 0.81 (0.73–0.81) for LTBI versus HC; 0.78 (0.66–0.78), 0.80 (0.67–0.80), 0.65 (0.46–0.65) and 0.63 (0.45–0.63) for LTBI-DM versus DM; 0.75 (0.63–0.75), 0.83 (0.74–0.83), 0.70 (0.58–0.70) and 0.63 (0.51–0.63) for LTBI-DM versus HC; 0.63 (0.53–0.63), 0.54 (0.43–0.54), 0.73 (0.64–0.73) and 0.73 (0.64–0.73) for LTBI-DM versus LTBI. Additional results, including sensitivities, specificities and thresholds for the subgroup ROC analysis, are reported in Supplementary Table S2.

Discussion

We used the Luminex® assay to assess the impact of DM on Mtb-specific responses during LTBI, utilising a panel of 17 cytokines and chemokines. We also determined the diagnostic utility of these cytokines and chemokines for LTBI identification. Our study provides insights into the immunological interplay between LTBI and DM, highlighting the modulatory effects of DM on Mtb-specific cytokine and chemokine responses. Our results indicate that (i) DM decreases Mtb-specific Th-1, inflammatory and regulatory responses in LTBI-DM compared to LTBI-only participants, and (ii) IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-12 and IP-10 are potential LTBI biomarkers regardless of DM status.

First, when we assessed the association between DM and Mtb-specific cytokine and chemokine responses by comparing LTBI-DM and LTBI-only participants using a linear regression model, we reported that LTBI-DM participants were associated with decreased concentrations of Mtb-specific IFN-γ, TNF, IL-12, IP-10, IL-8 and IL-10. This is consistent with other studies that reported decreased Mtb-specific Th1 and Th17 cytokine responses in LTBI-DM participants [16, 17]. The impaired cytokines and chemokines have been implicated in immune defence and promotion of survival during Mtb. For instance, IL-12 is key for the induction of IFN-γ and the formation of granulomas by facilitating differentiation, proliferation and activation of antigen-specific lymphocytes [34, 35]. Mice with IFN-γ gene knockouts exhibit poor macrophage activation and exacerbated granulocytic inflammation [36]. Tumour necrosis factor orchestrates the chemokine response in the lung, thereby maintaining the structural integrity of granulomatous formations [37, 38]. These chemokines, including IP-10, recruit lymphocytes to the sites of Mtb infection [39], with IL-8, a neutrophil chemoattractant, induced upon Mtb infection [40, 41]. IL-10 is a critical regulatory cytokine that prevents immunopathology [42]. Its associated decrease in LTBI-DM compared to LTBI-only participants is consistent with another study by Kumar et al. [43]. IL-10 prevents lung chronic inflammation during Mtb, with IL-10 gene-disrupted mice experiencing TB disease progression [42]. The decreased IL-10 production in LTBI-DM could imply decreased immune regulation and increased TB disease pathology. However, the role of IL-10 during Mtb infection is rather contradictory and requires cautious interpretation. Mycobacterium tuberculosis has been reported to induce IL-10 production [44], which impairs Mtb clearance by attenuating phagosome maturation to inhibit T-cell activation in the lung [45]. However, taken together, this immune impairment, orchestrated by DM, implies that a compromised Th1 response, insufficient immune cell infiltration and recruitment, and uncontrolled inflammation may impair the host’s ability to control Mtb replication during DM.

We then determined whether any cytokines and chemokines can be biomarkers for identifying LTBI, regardless of DM status. We reported that IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-12, and IP-10 each demonstrated good performance with univariate analysis, with AUC values ranging between 0.73 and 0.84. These results align with the established literature that positions IFN-γ and IL-2 as indicators of robust T-cell function following Mtb-specific antigen stimulation [46, 47], while IL-12 is key to Th1 lineage commitment [48], with IP-10 as an IFN-γ-dependent chemokine that correlates with TB-specific immunity [39]. It is unsurprising that these biomarkers emerged as top performers, given their established roles in Mtb-driven immune responses. When these four biomarkers were combined using a multivariate approach, the resulting generalised linear regression model reported an AUC of 0.92 and improved sensitivity and specificity. Our results agree with other studies that reported the diagnostic utility of these Mtb-specific biomarkers in univariate or combination analysis for distinguishing LTBI, active TB disease and healthy controls [25–29]. This highlights the benefit of multivariate analysis, where combining different biomarkers can augment diagnostic accuracy [49]. Having IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-12 and IP-10 in a biosignature suggests that different arms of the immune response, including Th-1 differentiation, proliferation and chemotaxis, synergise to determine the protective capacity that controls Mtb infection and replication [19]. When we evaluated the performance of these biomarkers stratified by LTBI or DM status, the biomarkers demonstrated considerable efficacy in identifying LTBI compared to HC, reinforcing the effectiveness of the biomarkers in non-DM populations. However, for comparisons that involved DM groups (LTBI-DM versus DM, LTBI-DM versus HC and LTBI-DM versus LTBI), the performance of the biomarkers to identify LTBI generated lower AUCs, sensitivities and specificities. The decreased performance suggests that DM potentially compromises the ability of these biomarkers to identify LTBI in diabetic patients due to DM-induced immune impairments reported earlier. Taken together, while the multivariate biosignature demonstrates robust LTBI diagnostic potential regardless of DM status, the stratified analysis suggests that DM introduces challenges to LTBI detection.

The study has limitations to consider. Firstly, although symptom screening was used to rule out symptomatic TB disease, this method, in addition to the QFT-plus, cannot definitively distinguish LTBI from asymptomatic or subclinical TB. As a result, this may have caused the misclassification of asymptomatic TB cases as LTBI. This represents a challenge in LTBI research and may have influenced our cytokine and chemokine profiles, as well as the biomarker performance estimates. This cross-sectional study design could have restricted the assessment of temporal biomarker changes and whether they could be used to predict risk for progression to active TB disease. Our biomarker selection strategy, while based on univariate performance, biological relevance, and clinical scalability criteria, did not systematically evaluate all possible multivariate combinations. This approach may have overlooked other biosignatures that could achieve comparable diagnostic performance with greater translational potential. Future validation studies should employ systematic approaches (e.g., LASSO, forward/backward selection) with internal cross-validation and external validation cohorts to prioritise both performance and scalability. Additionally, our biosignature performance estimates may be constrained by the accuracy of the QFT-Plus itself, particularly in DM populations where immunosuppression may affect IFN-γ responses. This study was carried out in Uganda, where factors such as genetics (genetic polymorphisms in IFN-γ or IL-12 genes) [50], local dietary patterns [50, 51], and environmental exposures such as BCG vaccination (high coverage in Uganda) and coinfections [50, 52] might differ from those in other populations, making the results ungeneralisable. Uganda’s adult HIV prevalence is approximately 5.8% [53], with TB/HIV co‑infection remaining a principal driver of TB incidence and mortality [54]. Consequently, our findings, while internally valid for HIV‑negative adults, may not extrapolate to persons living with HIV (PLHIV), who often exhibit aberrant cytokine and chemokine responses. This study had a small DM-only sample size, primarily due to the challenges of recruiting newly diagnosed, treatment-naïve DM patients without LTBI, especially in Uganda, where the urban LTBI prevalence is approximately 77% [55]. This could have limitations in detecting more differences for the association between LTBI and Mtb-specific cytokine and chemokine responses. Lastly, the panel of cytokines and chemokines used was not exhaustive, as additional biomarkers implicated in LTBI, DM or inflammation could provide complementary insights but were not included.

Conclusion

The study findings indicate that DM impairs Mtb-specific Th1, inflammatory and regulatory cytokine and chemokine responses during LTBI. Additionally, IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-12 and IP-10 were promising markers for identifying LTBI regardless of DM status. Efforts to validate these observations in diverse (Asian, Hispanic, etc.) populations, alternative techniques (e.g., Olink) and over longer timeframes will be crucial for understanding the extensive impact of DM on TB risk. This will not only improve the generalisibility of the results or ensure results are not method specific, but largely provide avenues to assess the diagnostic and prognostic performance (risk stratification) of the biosignature in populations that are immunocompromised, particularly DM and HIV, as well as during TB preventive therapy.
