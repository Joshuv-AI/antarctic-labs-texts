The dynamic changes of postoperative symptoms in lower limb Ilizarov bone transport patients: a longitudinal cross-lagged panel network analysis
Authors: Ping Wang, Huijuan Song, Chenghe Qin, Jinghua Yang, Huijie Chen, Xinyue Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients undergoing lower limb Ilizarov bone transport often experience a multidimensional symptom burden postoperatively, including physical, psychological, and social domains, which interact with each other. These symptoms collectively hinder postoperative recovery, yet their dynamic interactions over time remain unclear.ObjectiveTo construct a longitudinal symptom network for postoperative lower limb bone transport patients, identify core symptoms that persistently drive changes in other symptoms throughout rehabilitation, and reveal the dynamic evolution of symptom network structures over the treatment course.MethodsThis prospective study recruited 260 patients undergoing first-time Ilizarov bone transport surgery from two hospitals in Guangzhou, China, between September 2024 and October 2025. Data were collected at three time points: within one week post-surgery (T1), one month post-surgery (T2), and three months post-surgery (T3). Fourteen core symptoms were assessed using the PROMIS-29 and Ilizarov-specific symptom inventory. Cross-lagged panel network analysis (CLPN) was employed to construct longitudinal symptom networks.ResultsComplex interactions were identified among postoperative symptoms in patients with lower limb bone transport. The ability to participate in social roles and activities emerged as the core driving node within the entire longitudinal network. Fatigue acted as a susceptibility node in the symptom network from one week to one month post-surgery, while both fatigue and muscular discomfort functioned as susceptibility nodes from one to three months postoperatively. Furthermore, the early-stage symptom network was primarily driven by a single key symptom; however, as rehabilitation progressed, the symptom system became increasingly complex, exhibiting dynamic characteristics driven by the combined action of multiple nodes.ConclusionRestricted social role participation can be used as a priority target for clinical intervention, while fatigue and muscular discomfort serve as early warning indicators of symptom deterioration. A staged systemic intervention should be implemented for patients: focusing on social function reconstruction within the first month post-surgery, and adopting multi-target collaborative intervention strategies from one to three months postoperatively, in order to break the vicious cycle of symptoms and improve patient prognosis and quality of life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10207-2.Keywords: Lower limb bone transport, Ilizarov technique, Cross-lagged panel network analysis, Dynamic evolution of symptoms in the network

Background

Bone defects pose a significant clinical challenge in orthopedics, often resulting from high-energy trauma, infection, tumor resection, or congenital deformities. The reconstruction of extensive bone defects presents a formidable challenge for surgeons due to the complexity of the bone defects and the high incidence of complications [1, 2]. The Ilizarov technique, a classic method for bone transport and lengthening, provides an effective solution for repairing and reconstructing complex bone defects through its unique tension-stress principle, and similar lower limb reconstruction procedures further highlight the technical and clinical challenges involved in managing complex deformities and bone defects [3]. It helps prevent amputation and maximizes limb function restoration [4, 5]. However, the treatment process is lengthy and edemanding, requiring patients to wear cumbersome external fixators for extended periods and undergo frequent adjustments, with the entire cycle potentially lasting one to two years [6]. During this period, patients face not only physical pain and functional limitations but also often experience psychosocial issues such as anxiety, depression, and sleep disturbances [7]. These symptoms are interconnected and influence each other, forming the core of the patient’s postoperative rehabilitation experience. They directly affect treatment compliance, motivation for functional exercises, and ultimately, the overall therapeutic outcome [8].

However, current clinical focus remains predominantly on surgical success and radiographic evidence of bone healing, often neglecting the long-term, multidimensional symptom burden faced by patients throughout their extended treatment process. Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) provide structured tools for systematically capturing patients’ multidimensional subjective experiences regarding their physical functioning, mental health, and social engagement during treatment, with standardized functional assessment systems increasingly used to evaluate treatment outcomes in musculoskeletal conditions [9]. They enable patients to report their disease experiences, symptoms, and treatment outcomes in a standardised manner [10]. However, despite patients often reporting a complex array of symptoms—such as chronic pain, reduced physical ability, sleep problems, and psychological issues like anxiety and depression—existing research usually examines these symptoms as separate outcomes. This approach fails to reveal the complex interactions that may exist between them fully. Such limitations hinder our understanding of how symptoms interact dynamically over time, potentially creating vicious cycles that slow or impede recovery.

Cross-Lagged Panel Network Analysis (CLPN) combines network analysis with the Cross-Lagged Panel Model (CLPM). By constructing a temporal symptom network that aligns with network theory—which highlights dynamic interactions over time—it clarifies the temporal and prediction pathways between symptoms [11]. Usually used with multi-wave longitudinal data, it examines how symptoms change over time, including both intra-symptom and inter-symptom dynamics [12]. Building a longitudinal symptom network helps identify central symptoms that have a high influence within the network. It shows which symptoms influence others or are affected by them, supporting ongoing optimization of symptom management. This method offers a dynamic, systemic view of patient recovery. At present, studies on symptom-related aspects of bone transport patients show low levels of efficiency and accuracy in guiding symptom management [13]. Consequently, establishing CLPN offers a valuable approach to understanding the dynamic symptom changes and complex interplay between symptoms during the treatment phase for bone transport patients.

In summary, patients undergoing lower limb bone transport endure multiple, interacting dynamic symptom burdens postoperatively, yet current research fails to elucidate their intrinsic interactions and temporal dynamics. This study aims to: (1) construct a longitudinal symptom network for postoperative lower limb bone transport patients; (2) identify core symptoms persisting throughout rehabilitation and their directional influence on other symptoms; (3) reveal the dynamic evolution of symptom network structures over treatment duration. The findings will identify core symptoms that may serve as priority intervention targets for multimodal rehabilitation management; clarified directional relationships can guide the development of more temporally sequenced and targeted interventions. Ultimately, this approach holds promise for breaking vicious cycles between symptoms, improving long-term patient outcomes, and enhancing rehabilitation efficiency and quality of life.

Methods

Study design and participants

In this prospective study from September 2024 to October 2025, we recruited patients undergoing lower limb bone transport surgery from two hospitals in Guangzhou, China, employing convenience sampling. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years, (2) undergoing Ilizarov bone transport surgery for the first time, (3) conscious and lucid without cognitive or communication impairments, and capable of self-reporting, (4) provision of informed consent and voluntary participation in the investigation. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) concurrent participation in other clinical research studies, (2) undergoing additional surgical procedures concomitantly with the bone transport surgery, (3) critically ill status or the presence of severe comorbid conditions (e.g., cardiac, cerebral, hepatic) or other major systemic diseases. This study adheres to the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the institute’s hospital ethics committee (approval number: NFEC-2025-064).

Data were collected via questionnaire at three time points: T1 within one week post-surgery, T2 one month post-surgery, and T3 three months post-surgery. To ensure data integrity and the validity of the longitudinal analysis, specific criteria for participant attrition were established prospectively. Attrition was defined and managed as follows: (1) voluntary withdrawal of consent or non-cooperation by the participant; (2) death of the participant during the study period; (3) occurrence of a major adverse event rendering continued participation impossible. In all such cases, the reasons for attrition were thoroughly documented and reported. Consequently, the final analysis included 260 subjects with complete data at all three time points for CLPN analysis.

Measurements

Demographics, disease-related information

We collected socio-demographic and clinical data on patients with lower limb bone transport, including gender, age, marital status, educational level, occupation before bone transport treatment, current work status, household monthly income, mode of payment for medical care, primary caregiver, type of residence, indications for bone transport, site of bone transport, length of bone transport, number of surgeries, whether there was any combination of Underlying diseases.

Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System 29-item Profile (PROMIS-29)

The PROMIS-29 was used to measure symptom profiles in patients following lower limb bone transport. The scale consists of 29 items, including 7 single-dimension short scales for physical function, anxiety, depression, fatigue, sleep disturbance, ability to participate in social roles and activities (referred to as social roles), and pain interference, as well as 1 single-item scale for pain level. Each dimension consists of 4 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating a greater degree of the measured characteristic. Item 29 assesses pain intensity on a scale of 0–10. Raw scores were converted to T-scores with a mean of 50 and a standard deviation (SD) of 10 in the US reference population.

Ilizarov-Specific Symptom Self-Report Inventory

Postoperative symptoms specific to Ilizarov treatment were assessed using a self-developed self-report symptom questionnaire. This primarily encompassed seven dimensions: pin tract symptoms, paresthesia, joint stiffness, muscular discomfort, limb eedema, external fixator-related discomfort, and treatment-specific concerns. All items employed a numerical rating scale (NRS) ranging from 0 (no symptoms/no impact) to 10 (most severe symptoms/extreme impact), with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity.

Data collection

This study used a questionnaire survey approach, with researchers and team members acting as principal investigators and data collectors, all of whom underwent rigorous and standardized training. Researchers regularly reviewed outpatient and inpatient electronic medical records within the department to identify patients who met the inclusion criteria. After gathering basic information from potential participants, researchers approached them proactively to determine their willingness to participate. Once informed consent was obtained, patients were enrolled in the study. Data collection was conducted face-to-face using the Chinese translation of the instrument. Demographics, disease-related information, and symptom burden data were collected within one week after surgery (T1), along with patient contact details. During follow-up hospital visits at one month (T2) and three months (T3) post-surgery, researchers collected data related only to symptom burden.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized the demographic and clinical characteristics of the total sample. All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 26.0, and R software version 4.4.2 was used to construct the CLPN. For quantitative data that met normality, the mean ± standard deviation was used. For non-normally distributed data, the median (quartiles) [M(P25, P75)] was used. For qualitative data, frequency and percentage (%) were reported. The proportion of missing data across all variables and time points was low (< 10%). Therefore, we adopted a complete-case analysis approach, retaining only participants with full data at all three time points (N = 260).

Before conducting the CLPN analysis, we reversed the T-scores for the two functional domains of physical functioning and social role within the PROMIS-29 to facilitate statistical analysis. Following reversal, higher scores for all symptoms indicate greater severity. To avoid dimensional differences between measurement tools, all data were standardised to ensure comparability.

In R version 4.4.2, the glmnet, qgraph, and bootnet packages were employed to estimate the CLPN. These networks captured transitions in symptom severity from T1 to T2 and from T2 to T3, revealing predictive pathways within the symptom network. Our analysis comprised four key components: network estimation [14, 15], centrality, internal and external predictability measurements [16], and accuracy and stability estimation [17]. We employed the glmnet package for LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation to run the CLPN model, utilizing the qgraph function to generate dynamic networks that incorporate and exclude autoregressive models. Autoregressive effects typically constitute the strongest pathways within the network; however, this is not the primary focus of this study. Furthermore, the autoregressive effect diminishes the visual impact of other pathways within the network. Consequently, we excluded the autoregressive approach in network visualization (the autoregressive effects were retained in the rest of the model estimation). This network comprises 14 nodes, each representing a distinct symptom, with arrows indicating cross-lagged effects. Centrality indicators mainly include out-expected influence (Out-EI) and in-expected influence (In-EI), used to assess nodes’ predictive ability and sensitivity within the network. The highest Out-EI symptoms are considered key drivers of network dynamics and can serve as important targets for clinical intervention.

The stability of the network was evaluated with the bootnet package. First, the precision of edge weights was examined by constructing 95% confidence intervals (CIs) based on 1,000 bootstrap replications. Second, the robustness of node centrality measures was tested through a case-dropping bootstrap approach. This method removes a subset of cases repeatedly and refits the network; if the centrality order remains strongly correlated with the original even after excluding a large portion of the sample, the centrality values are considered stable. We also computed the centrality stability coefficient (CS coefficient), with values ≥ 0.25 indicating sufficient stability. Finally, difference tests in centrality were performed to compare out-strength and in-strength across different symptoms [17].

Results

General characteristics, severity of each symptom, and incidence of participants

This study recruited a total of 286 patients, of whom 260 completed the full follow-up period and were considered valid. Supplementary Table S1 summarizes the demographic and clinical characteristics of 260 patients who underwent lower limb Ilizarov bone transport. The cohort was predominantly male (177 cases, 68.1%), with a mean age of 44.07 ± 15.28 years (range: 18–75). Regarding educational attainment, the majority of patients had a high school or junior high school education (98 cases, 37.7%). Occupational distribution was primarily composed of workers (75 cases, 28.8%), company employees (49 cases, 18.8%), and farmers (45 cases, 17.3%). In terms of current work status, 84 patients (32.3%) were on sick leave. The majority of patients had a monthly household income per capita of less than 5,000 RMB (177 cases, 68.0%). Payment methods for medical expenses were as follows: 106 cases (40.7%) were self-paid and 154 cases (59.3%) were covered by social health insurance. Most patients were married (175 cases, 67.3%), and the primary caregivers were predominantly spouses or children (191 cases, 73.4%). The leading etiology for bone transport was osteomyelitis (106 cases, 40.7%), and the tibia was the most common site of bone transport (136 cases, 52.3%). The vast majority of patients had undergone fewer than 3 surgeries (224 cases, 86.2%). The mean bone transport length was 7.4 cm (range: 4.1–11.2 cm). Fifty-two patients (20.0%) had comorbid medical conditions.

Overall, both-stage networks exhibit a pronounced centralised structure, wherein a small number of key symptoms exert strong cross-temporal predictive influence over other symptoms within the network. During the T1→T2 phase, A6 (social roles) emerged as the most central source node, exerting strong predictive influence on multiple symptoms. Its predictive effect on A1 (physical function) proved particularly pronounced, while simultaneously impacting symptoms such as A2 (anxiety), A14 (treatment-specific concerns), A11 (muscle discomfort), and A13 (external fixator-related discomfort). Upon transitioning from T2 to T3, the network’s overall connectivity became more densely interconnected, yet A6 (social roles) retained its most pronounced cross-temporal predictive influence. Its predictive effect on symptoms such as A1 (physical functioning), A2 (anxiety), and A3 (depression) further intensified, forming an influence chain extending from social restriction to psychological symptoms. In contrast, A5 (sleep disturbance) and A4 (fatigue) did not emerge as core driver nodes within the simplified network, exhibiting markedly weaker outward predictive effects than A6. Overall, social restriction functioned as a core upstream node in the symptom propagation chain across both stages, exerting a sustained and significant driving influence on the post-operative symptom evolution in patients undergoing lower limb bone transport.

Centrality estimation

Centrality Indicators (Out-EI; In-EI) in Cross-Lagging Networks are shown in Fig. 2, used to quantitatively assess the role of each symptom in cross-time predictive relationships—whether it acts as a “source of influence” driving changes in other symptoms or acts as a “receiver” susceptible to other symptoms. All central indicators are shown in Supplementary Table S6.

Centrality maps for T1 → T2 and T2 → T3(including out-EI and in-EI)

Out-EI reflects the influence of a symptom as a “predictive source” on other symptoms across different time points. A higher value indicates that the symptom is more likely to serve as a pivotal node driving changes within the symptom system. During the T1→T2 phase, Out-EI exhibited a pronounced monocentric pattern, with A6 (social roles) demonstrating predictive power far exceeding all other symptoms (value approximately − 6.5, the largest absolute value). Although this value is negative, potentially stemming from competitive predictive structures within the network model, its absolute magnitude substantially exceeds that of other nodes. This indicates that social restriction constitutes the most potent cross-temporal influence source with the strongest net predictive effect in the early postoperative period. However, compared to earlier stages, the T2→T3 phase exhibits a greater number of moderately intense outward-predicting nodes. For instance, A13 (external fixator-related discomfort), A12 (limb edema), and A10 (joint stiffness) all display mild positive or negative outward-predicting values. Moreover, sleep disturbance (A5) exhibits an Out-EI approaching zero at this stage, indicating it ceases to be a primary driving symptom during the later recovery process. This stands in stark contrast to the predictive network observed in the earlier phase.

In-EI reflects the degree to which a symptom is “predicted”, representing its sensitivity to the combined effects of other symptoms at the next time point. During the T1→T2 phase, A1 (physical function) exhibited the highest In-EI, markedly exceeding the negative impact levels of other nodes. This indicates that physical function is one of the most sensitive and vulnerable symptoms in the early postoperative period, prone to deterioration under the combined influence of multiple upstream symptoms. Subsequently, A3 (depression) and A2 (anxiety) follow, with their In-EI values predominantly ranging between − 0.75 and − 0.6. This suggests that physical function, anxiety, and depression constitute the symptom cluster most susceptible to being impacted by other symptoms in the early postoperative period, exhibiting marked “vulnerability”. Compared to the previous stage, the number of susceptible nodes from T2 to T3 has significantly increased. Among them, A1 (physical function), A2 (anxiety), A5 (sleep disturbance), A8(pin tract symptoms), and A14(treatment-specific concerns) all showed high In-EI values (absolute value around − 0.8), indicating that these symptoms are more sensitive at this stage and are easily affected and worsened by upstream symptoms. Overall, changes in susceptibility symptoms also show a trend from a single-center model to a multi-center model.

Cross-structure predictability measurements

Figure 3 shows the In-Prediction and Out-Prediction indicators for the T1→T2 and T2→T3 stages, providing supplementary analysis of the central indicators. In-Prediction reflects the cumulative extent to which a symptom is predicted by upstream symptoms at the next point (i.e., the sum of all margin weights pointing to that symptom); Out-Prediction represents the sum of the predicted strength of the symptom against other symptoms and is a core indicator for identifying the “source of symptom transmission”. This analysis supplements the identification of symptom transmission sources and susceptibility nodes from the perspective of cumulative effects.

Cross-structure prediction capability (in-/out-prediction)

Based on the actual estimates from this study, the symptom prediction structure exhibits significant phase-specific variations. During the T1→T2 phase, the symptom with the highest In-Prediction score was A4 fatigue (approximately 1.28), which was significantly higher than other nodes. This was followed by A14 treatment-specific concerns (approximately 1.15) and A12 limb edema (approximately 1.10). Concurrently, A10 joint stiffness, A2 anxiety, and A13 external fixator-related discomfort also exhibited elevated In-Prediction values (all around 1.00). In contrast, Out-Prediction exhibits a pronounced single-core structure during the T1→T2 phase. Upon transitioning from T2 to T3, the In-Prediction structure exhibits marked alterations. A4 (fatigue) once again emerges as the node most susceptible to influence from other symptoms (approximately 1.50), with its value further increasing compared to the preceding phase. This indicates that fatigue remains a highly sensitive hub within the symptom network throughout the entire recovery process. Additionally, significant improvements were observed in the In-Prediction scores for A11 (muscle discomfort). During the T2→T3 phase, the Out-Prediction remains most strongly influenced by A6 social roles (approximately 5.3) as the primary predictive source node. However, a new core driver emerges at this stage: the Out-Prediction for A1 (physical function) reaches approximately 2.0, markedly exceeding that of other symptoms.

Network accuracy and stability

Figure 4 demonstrates the stability of centrality metrics across varying sampling proportions. Both T1→T2 and T2→T3 stages exhibit high stability in out-EI (correlation coefficients approximately 0.95), indicating robust predictive drive under sample fluctuations. Although the stability of in-EI is slightly lower, it remains at an acceptable level (approximately 0.85–0.90), indicating that the overall network structure and centrality results exhibit good reproducibility. Consequently, the CLPN constructed in this study demonstrates high stability under various sample extraction conditions, ensuring the reliability of the research conclusions.

Discussion

The dynamic evolution of symptom networks and their core driving mechanisms

The patient’s symptoms one week postoperatively were primarily characterized by acute postoperative stress symptoms, including impaired physical and social functioning, sleep disturbance, limb edema, pain interference, and needle-track symptoms. By one month postoperatively, these evolved into common postoperative complications dominated by psychological symptoms, needle-track symptoms, and joint stiffness. As physical function recovered, symptoms at three months postoperatively were chiefly complications arising from the external fixation device, presenting more severely than at T2. Throughout this process, the manifestation of patient symptoms gradually exhibited considerable dispersion and heterogeneity, which is thought to be related to patient self-management and the bone transport rehabilitation process [18].

During the T1→T2 phase, social roles emerged as the most prominent core node, projecting multiple predictive pathways outward with significantly higher edge weights than other nodes. This indicates that social constraints exerted a broad and potent predictive effect across multiple symptom categories within the first month post-surgery. Social roles in T1 primarily predict physical function, anxiety, treatment-specific concerns, muscle discomfort, and external fixator-related discomfort in T2, suggesting that the ability to participate in social roles and activities may be the primary factor driving changes in the cluster of early postoperative symptoms. This may be attributable to the fact that postoperative patients’ restricted social interactions (i.e., weakened social support networks) diminish their capacity to cope with surgical stress. Consequently, they are unable to “buffer” the adverse effects of stress, ultimately leading to the exacerbation of psychological symptoms such as anxiety and depression, alongside physical symptoms including fatigue and muscle discomfort [19, 20]. As the process progressed into the T2→T3 phase, social roles maintained its central position within the network, showing no signs of diminished cross-temporal predictive power. Indeed, its connectivity pattern exhibits greater density, indicating that restricted social engagement remains a persistent driving factor throughout the recovery process, triggering cascading physiological and psychological symptoms. This may be related to psychosocial stress influencing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines through specific physiological mechanisms, thereby affecting inflammation-related symptoms [20]. As recovery progresses, the network structure evolves from a “single-centre” to a “multi-centre” configuration, with the predictive roles of physical function and sleep disturbance strengthening. This reflects the transition in the recovery process from acute stress response to functional adaptation. This dynamic pattern suggests that postoperative symptom management must address the predominant drivers at different stages. However, although the outward predictive edges of physical function, depression, and fatigue increased, they still failed to surpass the centrality of social roles. Their predictive effects more closely resemble secondary propagation pathways forming around the social roles. Overall, the network architecture of this study clearly demonstrates that social roles serve as the dominant driver within the postoperative symptom propagation chain, spanning both temporal phases. It exerts a stable, cross-temporal predictive influence on diminished physical functioning, sleep disturbance, fatigue, pain interference, and needle-track symptoms. This finding suggests that restricted social functioning may play a pivotal role in patients’ postoperative recovery, with its improvement potentially exerting indirect alleviating effects on multidimensional symptoms. This pattern aligns with the common postoperative experiences of activity limitations, feelings of social isolation, and psychological stress, providing a plausible medical rationale [21–25].

Fatigue has the highest in-prediction in the cross-lag network from one week to one month postoperatively, indicating it is highly sensitive to changes in upstream symptoms and serves as a “susceptible node” in the T1 →T2 symptom network system. Similarly, treatment-specific concerns and muscular discomfort are the “susceptibility nodes” in the symptom network system from one month to three months postoperatively. This finding has important clinical monitoring significance: when core nodes such as social roles and physical functions deteriorate, these symptoms fluctuate first and can serve as early warning indicators for worsening symptoms.

Overall, temporal variations in EI indicators reveal distinct mechanisms governing postoperative symptom regulation: early stages are primarily driven by single key symptoms, whereas as recovery progresses, the symptom system becomes increasingly complex, exhibiting dynamic characteristics driven by the combined effects of multiple nodes.

Clinical implications and implications for intervention strategies

The dynamic evolution patterns of symptom networks suggest that clinical interventions should shift from traditional “single symptom management” to a “systemic intervention targeting core driver nodes” model.

Patients undergoing lower limb bone transport should receive phased interventions during rehabilitation. The early phase (within one month post-surgery) centres on social function reconstruction. This study clearly demonstrates that during the T1→T2 phase, the ability to participate in social roles and activities is the most central driver node within the symptom network. This finding suggests that early interventions should prioritise restoring and rebuilding patients’ social functioning, such as establishing structured social support programmes, early rehabilitation, and social integration methods. During postoperative rehabilitation of bone transport patients, attention should be paid to their social attributes, leveraging family and social support systems to help patients adapt to changes in social roles [26]. Families are encouraged to communicate more with patients, increase their social connections and emotional support, encourage patients to participate in rehabilitation activities and social interactions, boost confidence and sense of belonging, promote mental health and social adaptation, and thereby improve quality of life [27]. Clinical intervention can organize patient support groups (online/offline), where patients who have recovered better share experiences, provide emotional support, and offer practical advice. The 1–3 month postoperative period requires multi-targeted synergistic interventions. As the symptom network evolves towards a multi-centred structure, this phase necessitates comprehensive strategies including: strengthening rehabilitation exercises — physical function exhibits high susceptibility across both phases and warrants sustained attention; Preventive management of needle-track symptoms: Given the heightened sensitivity of Pin tract symptoms during the T2→T3 transition, standardised needle-track care protocols and early warning mechanisms should be established. Research indicates that standardised needle-track care significantly reduces the incidence of related complications. Our research group has previously developed an exercise rehabilitation plan for bone transport patients, covering the preoperative stage, hospitalization/transition stage, traction osteogenesis stage, bone mineralization stage, and post-external fixation frame removal stage. This includes aspects such as rehabilitation time, rehabilitation content, and needle path management, which are practical and scientifically relevant and can provide reference guidance for patients with lower limb bone transport [28]. Furthermore, during the early postoperative period, intervention should prioritise acute stress symptoms such as needle-track discomfort, limb swelling, and muscular unease. Concurrently, guiding adaptation to external fixation devices may mitigate psychological symptoms during subsequent rehabilitation and enhance treatment outcomes. Between one and three months post-surgery, particular attention should be paid to shifts in patients’ psychological symptoms, alongside needle-track care and exercise guidance.

Limitations of this study

This study has several limitations. Firstly, although the sample size is substantial (N = 260), all participants were recruited from two hospitals within the same city, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future research could enhance external validity through multi-regional, multi-center collaborations. Secondly, the CLPN model relies on statistical estimation and does not incorporate biomarkers (e.g., inflammatory markers) or environmental factors. Future work could integrate multi-omics data to construct more comprehensive networks. Thirdly, due to sample size constraints, the present study used only 14 symptom dimensions as network nodes for CLPN analysis. Expanding the sample size in future studies and using individual symptom items as nodes would allow for a more granular capture of symptom dynamics. Fourthly, the study included only three time points, which is insufficient to cover the entire long-term rehabilitation process. Future research should include additional follow-up points to examine how the network evolves across the full rehabilitation trajectory. Finally, the CLPN method used here can identify predictive associations between symptoms but cannot establish causal relationships; future studies are needed to explore these causal links.

Conclusion

This study uses a cross-lag panel network analysis method to systematically examine the longitudinal predictive relationships and dynamic evolution patterns of fourteen core symptoms after surgery in patients with lower limb bone transport across three time points (one week after T1 surgery, one month after T2 surgery, and three months after T3 surgery). The research results show: (1) there is a complex interaction between postoperative symptoms in patients with lower limb bone transport; (2) the ability to participate in social roles and activities is the core driving node within the entire vertical network and can serve as a priority target for clinical intervention. (3) fatigue is a “susceptibility node” in the symptom network system one to one month after surgery, while fatigue and muscle discomfort are “susceptibility nodes” in the symptom network system one to three months postoperatively, serving as early warning indicators for symptom worsening. (4) the early stage of the symptom network is mainly driven by a single key symptom, but as rehabilitation progresses, the symptom system becomes increasingly complex, showing dynamic characteristics of multiple nodes acting together. These findings provide a theoretical basis for clinical staging and targeted symptom management.
