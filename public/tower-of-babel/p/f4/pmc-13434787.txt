High-risk pregnancy prediction using Taguchi-optimized machine learning methods and TOPSIS-based model selection
Authors: Maryam Mousavi Nogholi, Ashkan Mozdgir, Youness Javid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-risk pregnancies pose a major challenge in maternal and neonatal healthcare, defined as conditions where medical, social, or obstetric factors threaten the health of the mother or fetus. Traditional clinical tools often overlook individual complexities and delay identification, especially in resource-limited settings where mortality rates remain high. This study addresses these gaps by developing machine learning (ML) models for high-risk pregnancy prediction using data from 62 pregnant women at an Iranian hospital (2014–2016). Features were categorized into demographics, pregnancy-related, and complete case (all features). Preprocessing, feature selection, and hyperparameters were optimized using the Taguchi method. Five supervised models, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Random Forest (RF), Decision Tree (DT), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), were trained and evaluated using multiple performance and statistical criteria. The study contributes an integrated framework combining feature-group comparison, Taguchi-based optimization, and TOPSIS-based multi-criteria model ranking to support balanced model selection. Using real-world hospital-based data from Iran, it jointly predicts major high-risk pregnancy conditions, including IUFD, IUGR, and preterm birth. Results showed pregnancy-related features as stronger predictors, with KNN achieving 88% accuracy and high recall in this category. TOPSIS ranked SVM highest for demographics, KNN for pregnancy-related, and MLP for the complete case. This framework may support clinicians and maternal health centers in earlier identification and prioritization of high-risk pregnancies.

Introduction

High-risk pregnancies represent a critical challenge in maternal and neonatal healthcare, characterized by the presence of medical, social, or obstetric factors that adversely affect the health and well-being of the mother, fetus, or both, leading to elevated risks of mortality, long-term complications, and developmental disorders1,2. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), a high-risk pregnancy is defined as a condition where the life, health, or welfare of the mother or developing fetus is threatened due to pre-existing medical conditions, pregnancy-related issues, or environmental and social factors3. This definition aligns with clinical perspectives that emphasize the potential for adverse outcomes, such as preterm birth, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), intrauterine fetal demise (IUFD), preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, and postpartum complications4,5. For instance, preterm birth is specifically termed as delivery before 37 weeks of gestation, often triggered by infections, hypertension, placental abnormalities, or prior history, resulting in underdeveloped organs like lungs in the neonate and increased neonatal mortality6. Similarly, IUGR occurs when the fetus grows significantly less than expected for gestational age (below the 10th percentile), linked to maternal factors such as chronic diseases, malnutrition, or smoking, and fetal issues, including genetic anomalies, leading to risks like cerebral palsy, stillbirth, and long-term metabolic disorders7,8. IUFD, defined as fetal death after 20 weeks without signs of life, is associated with chromosomal abnormalities, placental problems, maternal infections, or unexplained causes, carrying profound emotional, psychological, and social impacts on families9,10.

The importance and urgency of addressing high-risk pregnancies cannot be overstated, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where 95% of the 287,000 annual maternal deaths occur, many of which are preventable through early detection and intervention3,11. In fragile or crisis-affected regions, maternal mortality rates can be up to five times higher than in stable areas, exacerbated by limited access to quality healthcare3. High-risk pregnancies not only increase maternal morbidity, such as severe preeclampsia, eclampsia, hemorrhage, and mental health issues like perinatal anxiety, but also contribute to neonatal complications, including low birth weight, respiratory distress, and lifelong disabilities12,13. Early identification is essential for targeted prenatal care, lifestyle modifications, and resource allocation, ultimately improving perinatal outcomes and reducing economic burdens on healthcare systems14. Traditional assessment methods, reliant on clinical tools and subjective judgments, often fail to account for individual complexities, leading to delayed interventions and suboptimal results15,16. For example, conventional risk scoring systems may overlook multifaceted interactions in data, resulting in false negatives that heighten the consequences of undetected risks17.

Several factors influence high-risk pregnancies, broadly categorized into maternal, fetal, and environmental domains18. Maternal factors include advanced age (> 35 years), obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² pre-pregnancy), underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m²), hypertension, diabetes (pre-gestational or gestational), autoimmune diseases (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus), smoking, alcohol/substance use, poor prenatal care, low socioeconomic status, and short interpregnancy intervals (< 6 months)19–21. Fetal factors encompass multiples (twins or more), congenital anomalies, genetic syndromes, and growth discrepancies like IUGR8,22. Environmental and social influences involve poverty, low education, urban/rural disparities, high-altitude living, and intimate partner violence23,24. Studies highlight that obesity and smoking synergistically amplify risks: obese pregnant women face higher chances of gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, and cesarean deliveries, while offspring are predisposed to childhood obesity and cardiovascular diseases19. In India, a national survey revealed a 14.4% prevalence of high-risk pregnancies, driven by adolescent/maternal age extremes, chronic conditions (e.g., asthma, thyroid disorders), and prior adverse outcomes like preterm birth or cesarean Sect20. A meta-analysis of 83 studies further classified multiple high-risk factors into physical (diabetes, hypertension), psychological (depression), social-behavioral (poverty, substance use), and obstetric history (prior miscarriages), noting an upward trend in low-income settings21. Autoimmune conditions, such as Sjögren’s syndrome or type 1 diabetes, elevate risks of miscarriage, IUGR, and small-for-gestational-age births25. Gestational weight gain, either insufficient or excessive, correlates with adverse outcomes; underweight women with inadequate gain experience higher rates of preterm premature rupture of membranes, anemia, and small-for-gestational-age infants26,27. Mapping studies in Indonesia underscore age, parity, prior disease history, and chronic energy deficiency as key predictors, with 75.5% of cases linked to obstetric history28.

Traditional prediction methods for high-risk pregnancies primarily involve clinical guidelines, such as the WHO’s risk categorization or tools like the Obstetric Comorbidity Index, which rely on checklists of known risk factors3,29. These approaches, while accessible, are limited by their static nature, subjectivity in clinician interpretation, and inability to handle nonlinear interactions in heterogeneous data15,30. For instance, scoring systems from the 1950s onward often underperform in early prediction of preterm labor or preeclampsia, with sensitivities below 70% in diverse populations16,31. In contrast, machine learning (ML) methods offer a paradigm shift by analyzing multidimensional datasets, integrating demographics, vital signs, laboratory results, and historical records, to uncover hidden patterns and provide personalized, timely predictions32,33. Supervised ML algorithms, such as logistic regression, decision trees, random forests, support vector machines, and neural networks, excel in classification tasks for binary outcomes (high-risk vs. low-risk), achieving accuracies often exceeding 85–95%32,34. For example, ensemble methods like random forests have demonstrated superior performance in predicting hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) by handling imbalanced data and feature interactions35. Neural networks, particularly multilayer perceptrons (MLPs), integrate vital signs (e.g., blood pressure, glucose levels) to forecast risks with 91% specificity for high-risk cases32. Studies in the Philippines utilized semi-supervised decision trees on sparse data, reaching 97% accuracy by incorporating unlabeled samples36. In fetal heart rate monitoring, ML classifiers distinguish abnormal patterns, correlating probabilities with clinical outcomes like IUGR or distress37. For preterm birth (PTB) prediction, support vector machines and logistic regression, augmented with SMOTE for class imbalance, identified diabetes and obesity as key risks with high sensitivity38,39. Deep learning hybrids, such as DT-BiLTCN, achieved 98% accuracy on IoT-sourced maternal health data, emphasizing diastolic/systolic pressure and heart rate40. Comparative analyses favor gradient boosting (90.64%) and LASSO-regularized models for early PIH detection34,41. In low-resource settings, ML models like random forests (85%) and explainable AI variants predict PTB in nulliparous women with 94–97% negative predictive value42,43. Recent advancements include preeclampsia forecasting via routinely collected data (AUC > 0.85) and hybrid ANN-random forest classifiers for risk stratification44,45. Despite these successes, gaps persist in preprocessing optimization and multi-criteria model selection, particularly in underrepresented populations like Iran, motivating this study’s integration of Taguchi for robust feature engineering and TOPSIS for balanced evaluation46,47.

Materials and methods

This study is a secondary analysis of an existing clinical dataset from 62 pregnant women at Firoozgar Hospital, Tehran, Iran. The dataset was obtained from the authors of a previously published study48, and was used to develop a machine-learning model. No new participant recruitment, intervention, or direct contact with human subjects was performed in the present study. According to the source publication from which the dataset was obtained, the original study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Iran University of Medical Sciences and regional authorities, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment, with the right to withdraw at any time. In the present work, only secondary analysis of pre-existing de-identified data was performed.

The research methodology followed a structured flowchart (Fig. 1) outlining the key phases: data collection, comprehensive preprocessing and hyperparameter optimization, model training and evaluation, and multi-criteria ranking. Initially, demographic (e.g., age, education, income, parity) and pregnancy-related features (e.g., history of IUFD, IUGR, gestational hypertension, TPO antibodies, BMI, and common complications) were gathered, with features categorized into three groups: demographics, pregnancy-related, and complete case. Preprocessing steps, including handling missing values (e.g., median, Iterative Imputer, KNN Imputer), standardization (e.g., Standard, Min-Max, Robust), data balancing (e.g., Oversampling, Undersampling, class-weight), and feature selection (e.g., PCA, Filter method, Wrapper method), along with hyperparameter tuning (previously explored via grid or random search methods), were optimized using the Taguchi method to identify robust combinations for each model and category. This integrated optimization minimized sensitivity to noise via signal-to-noise (S/N) ratios and ANOVA analysis, treating the steps as factors with multiple levels (e.g., 3 options per factor for most: missing value imputation methods, standardization techniques, balancing methods, feature selection algorithms, and key hyperparameters like k in KNN or n_estimators in RF). Five ML algorithms, namely K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Random Forest (RF), Decision Tree (DT), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), were then trained and evaluated using a stratified 70/30 train-test split together with 5-fold cross-validation to provide a more stable assessment of model performance under limited-sample conditions. Model performance was assessed using standard metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, and F1-score, derived from the confusion matrix. Finally, the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) was applied to rank models across categories, prioritizing recall due to its clinical importance in minimizing false negatives. This integrated framework ensured efficient, balanced predictions tailored to high-risk pregnancy detection.

Given the real-world clinical nature of the dataset and the limited sample size (n = 62), the study was designed to maximize methodological robustness through a structured optimization and validation strategy. The Taguchi method was employed to systematically optimize preprocessing procedures, feature selection strategies, and hyperparameter configurations while reducing unnecessary experimental variability. In addition, model performance was evaluated using cross-validation to obtain more stable estimates across data partitions. These procedures were adopted to ensure that the reported results reflect consistent performance patterns within the available clinical dataset, while maintaining appropriate caution regarding broader external generalization.

Data collection and preparation

Data were prospectively collected from 62 married pregnant women aged 18–40 years, residing in Tehran, with no history of thyroid disorders, at Firoozgar Hospital between 2014 and 2016. This exclusion of thyroid-related cases was deliberate to focus on non-endocrine influences on pregnancy risks, avoiding confounding factors like hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism that could independently elevate high-risk status. Inclusion criteria ensured a focused cohort suitable for ML analysis, excluding cases with incomplete records. Features totaled approximately 24, divided into:

Data preprocessing

Raw data preprocessing was performed to enhance model robustness and ensure reliable machine learning performance prior to optimization. Given the presence of missing values, mixed data types, and class imbalance in the dataset, multiple preprocessing components were considered, including missing value imputation, feature scaling, class balancing, feature selection, and hyperparameter tuning.

Missing values were handled using three alternative approaches: Median imputation, Iterative Imputer, and KNN Imputer. Feature scaling was performed using Standard (z-score), Min-Max, and Robust normalization techniques. To address class imbalance between high-risk and low-risk pregnancy cases, three balancing strategies were evaluated: Oversampling (e.g., SMOTE), Undersampling, and class-weight adjustment. Feature selection methods included Principal Component Analysis (PCA), filter-based methods (e.g., Chi-square), and wrapper-based methods (e.g., recursive feature elimination). Hyperparameters were tuned using either a grid-search equivalent strategy or a random-search subset strategy over predefined key parameters (e.g., KNN k={3,5,7}, RF n_estimators={50,100,150}).

Hyperparameter tuning was incorporated within the Taguchi optimization framework rather than being treated as a separate post hoc step. In particular, the tuning strategy itself (Grid search versus Random search) was included as one of the candidate factor levels in the Taguchi design, and the final strategy reported for each model and feature category in Table 4 was determined according to the configuration that yielded the most robust performance. The tuned hyperparameters were selected according to the structure of each classifier. Specifically, KNN tuning considered the number of neighbors, weighting scheme, and distance metric; RF tuning included the number of trees and tree-complexity parameters; DT tuning focused on split and depth-related parameters; SVM tuning considered kernel-related and regularization parameters; and MLP tuning included hidden-layer structure, activation function, regularization strength, and learning behavior. The corresponding Taguchi signal-to-noise plots generated in Minitab for each model and feature category are presented in Fig. 2.

The three feature selection strategies were chosen to represent complementary approaches to dimensionality reduction and variable selection. PCA was included as a projection-based method to reduce feature dimensionality and collinearity by transforming the original variables into a smaller set of components. Filter-based selection was included because it offers a computationally efficient way to rank features according to their statistical relevance to the outcome, independent of the final classifier. Wrapper-based selection was also considered because it evaluates feature subsets in relation to model performance and can therefore capture model-specific predictive utility. Rather than assigning these methods to particular models a priori, all three were treated as candidate preprocessing options within the Taguchi framework, and the final choice for each model and feature group was determined according to the configuration that provided the most robust performance.

Optimization using the Taguchi method

To systematically optimize preprocessing strategies and hyperparameter configurations while reducing computational cost, the Taguchi method was employed. Instead of evaluating all possible combinations exhaustively (e.g., 3⁴ × 2 = 162 for five factors with mixed levels), an L18 orthogonal array design was adopted. Taguchi experiments were conducted separately for each machine learning model and feature category. Each trial was executed in Python (scikit-learn), and performance was estimated using 5-fold cross-validation repeated across five iterations per trial. The resulting accuracy values were then analyzed in Minitab. Accuracy was treated as the quality characteristic and converted to a signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio using the “larger-is-better” criterion to identify robust settings that are least sensitive to noise and variability See table 3.

Machine learning models

Five supervised classifiers were selected for their suitability in binary classification of high-risk pregnancies (high-risk: 1; low-risk: 0), with the explicit goal of predicting high-risk status to facilitate timely clinical interventions. Each model was chosen based on its proven efficacy in handling medical datasets with mixed feature types and potential imbalances, as detailed below.

K-nearest neighbors (KNN)

The K-Nearest Neighbors model is a supervised and non-parametric learning algorithm used for classification and regression. In this method, to predict the class of a new sample, its distance to existing samples, such as Euclidean distance, is calculated, and the K closest samples are selected. The class of the new sample is determined by the majority vote of its neighbors. KNN, due to its simplicity and lack of assumptions about data distribution, is used in applications such as pattern recognition, medical diagnosis like disease identification, and recommendation systems. However, this model is sensitive to data volume and the selection of K value48.

Random forest (RF)

Random Forest is a supervised ensemble-based learning algorithm composed of multiple decision trees. This method creates a large number of decision trees on random subsets of data and features, combining the predictions of each tree through voting for classification or averaging for regression to determine the final outcome. Random Forest, due to its resistance to overfitting and ability to manage high-dimensional data, is used in applications such as fraud detection, financial prediction, and biological data analysis49.

Decision tree (DT)

Decision Tree is a supervised learning algorithm that models data using a tree structure of decision rules. Each internal node represents a decision based on a feature, and leaves represent the predicted class or value. This model, due to its high interpretability and simplicity, is applied in classification and regression problems such as risk analysis, disease diagnosis, and customer behavior prediction. However, the decision tree may be sensitive to overfitting unless optimized using techniques such as pruning50.

Support vector machine (SVM)

Support Vector Machine is a supervised learning algorithm that separates data in classification or regression problems by finding an optimal hyperplane. This model achieves high accuracy by maximizing the margin between classes and can handle nonlinear problems using the kernel trick. SVM is highly effective in applications such as image recognition, text analysis, email categorization, and medical disease detection due to its ability to manage complex and high-dimensional data51.

Multilayer perceptron (MLP)

Multilayer Perceptron is a type of artificial neural network that uses multiple layers of neurons to model complex and nonlinear relationships in data. This model includes input, hidden, and output layers trained using algorithms such as backpropagation. MLP, due to its ability to learn complex patterns, is used in applications such as image recognition, natural language processing, and time series prediction. However, it requires precise parameter tuning and high computational resources as limitations52.

Performance evaluation

The effectiveness of the machine learning models was evaluated using a confusion matrix, a critical tool for assessing binary classification performance in high-risk pregnancy prediction. The confusion matrix provides a structured summary of model predictions, comprising True Positives (TP: correctly predicted high-risk pregnancies), True Negatives (TN: correctly predicted low-risk pregnancies), False Positives (FP: low-risk pregnancies incorrectly classified as high-risk), and False Negatives (FN: high-risk pregnancies incorrectly classified as low-risk). These elements serve as the foundation for calculating key performance metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, and F1-score. Each metric offers distinct insights into model performance, enabling a comprehensive evaluation of classification efficacy. In the context of high-risk pregnancy prediction, minimizing false negatives is crucial due to the severe consequences of missing high-risk cases, such as increased maternal or fetal mortality risks. Consequently, recall was prioritized to ensure high sensitivity in detecting high-risk pregnancies, aligning with clinical priorities to reduce undetected risks53,54.

Accuracy provides a general measure of correct predictions across all classes but can be misleading in imbalanced datasets, where it may overemphasize the majority class (e.g., low-risk pregnancies). Precision quantifies the proportion of predicted high-risk cases that are correct, which is vital when false positives lead to unnecessary medical interventions. Recall, or sensitivity, measures the model’s ability to identify all actual high-risk cases, critical in this study to avoid missing critical cases that could lead to adverse outcomes. Specificity evaluates the correct identification of low-risk cases, ensuring minimal misclassification of healthy pregnancies. The F1-score, as a harmonic mean of precision and recall, provides a balanced measure, particularly useful in imbalanced datasets where both false positives and false negatives carry significant costs53,55. In addition to the conventional confusion-matrix-based metrics, balanced accuracy and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) were calculated to provide a more reliable evaluation under class-imbalance conditions. Balanced accuracy considers both sensitivity and specificity by calculating their average, and is therefore less affected by unequal class distributions than conventional accuracy56. It is defined as follows:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{Balanced}}~{\mathrm{Accuracy}} = \frac{{{\mathrm{Specificity}} + {\mathrm{Recall}}}}{2}$$\end{document}

Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) was also used as a balanced measure of binary classification performance. Unlike accuracy, MCC considers all four components of the confusion matrix, including true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, and is particularly useful when the class distribution is imbalanced57,58.The MCC value ranges from − 1 to + 1, where + 1 indicates perfect classification, 0 indicates random prediction, and − 1 indicates complete disagreement between predicted and actual labels. MCC is calculated as follows:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{MCC}} = \frac{{\left( {{\mathrm{TP}} \times {\mathrm{TN}}} \right) - \left( {{\mathrm{FP}} \times {\mathrm{FN}}} \right)}}{{\sqrt {\left( {TP + FP} \right)\left( {TP + FN} \right)\left( {TN + FP} \right)\left( {TN + FN} \right)} }}$$\end{document}

To further assess threshold-independent discriminative performance, receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (ROC-AUC) was calculated. The ROC curve illustrates the trade-off between the true positive rate (TPR) and the false positive rate (FPR) across different classification thresholds54. The ROC-AUC summarizes the overall ability of the model to discriminate between high-risk and low-risk pregnancies across all possible classification thresholds. Precision–Recall area under the curve, also referred to as PR-AUC or Average Precision (AP), was additionally calculated because precision–recall analysis is particularly informative for imbalanced datasets, where the positive class is of greater clinical interest55,59. Higher AP values indicate better ability to identify the positive class, which in this study corresponds to high-risk pregnancy. Average Precision summarizes this curve as a weighted mean of precision values at different recall levels and is defined as follows:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:AP=\sum\:_{n}\left({R}_{n}{-R}_{n-1}\right){P}_{n}$$\end{document}

Finally, the Brier score was used to evaluate the quality of predicted probabilities. It is calculated as follows:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Brier\:Score=\sum\:_{i=1}^{N}({{p}_{i}-{y}_{i})}^{2}$$\end{document}

Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, F1-score, balanced accuracy, and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC). In addition, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, precision–recall analysis, and calibration analysis were performed to provide a broader assessment of discriminative and probabilistic performance. To quantify uncertainty, 95% confidence intervals were calculated for the main evaluation metrics. To assess whether model performances differed statistically across classifiers, one-way ANOVA was performed on cross-validation-based performance values for each evaluation metric within each feature group. The ANOVA results were used as supplementary statistical evidence. To maintain consistency with the main reported results, the best-performing model for each metric was identified based on the reported performance values in Tables 6 and 9, while ANOVA was used only to assess whether the differences among models were statistically significant. A p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Model ranking with TOPSIS

The Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) was utilized as a multi-criteria decision-making method to rank the five machine learning models (KNN, RF, DT, SVM, MLP) for each feature category (demographics, pregnancy-related, and complete case) based on their performance metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, and F1-score). TOPSIS constructs a decision matrix with models as rows and evaluation metrics as columns, normalizes the matrix, and assigns weights to each metric such that their sum equals one to reflect clinical priorities in high-risk pregnancy prediction. Weights were assigned based on clinical priorities, with recall receiving the highest importance to reduce false-negative predictions. The method then identifies ideal positive (best) and ideal negative (worst) solutions, computes the Euclidean distance of each model from these ideals, and calculates a closeness coefficient to rank the models, where higher values indicate superior performance. This approach enables a systematic and objective ranking by balancing trade-offs among multiple evaluation criteria. Implemented in Python using NumPy for distance calculations, TOPSIS effectively supports multi-dimensional model evaluation62.

Accuracy was selected as the quality characteristic because it provides a comprehensive and stable measure of overall model performance that facilitates consistent comparison across different preprocessing pipelines and model configurations. In the context of this study, accuracy is employed as a methodological evaluation criterion, particularly appropriate for a small clinical dataset where class-specific metrics may be more susceptible to sampling variability. At this stage, accuracy is used to support robust and reproducible configuration assessment rather than to express clinical preference.

In contrast, recall was assigned a higher weight exclusively during the TOPSIS-based model ranking and was not used as the optimization criterion within the Taguchi framework. This distinction reflects the fundamentally different roles of the two methods: Taguchi optimization requires a single performance metric to identify stable and robust configurations, whereas TOPSIS enables multi-criteria evaluation and the explicit incorporation of clinical priorities. Emphasizing recall at the TOPSIS stage ensures that the final model selection prioritizes the reduction of false-negative predictions, which is critical in the clinical context of high-risk pregnancy detection.

Correlation results

Figure 10 presents the correlation heatmap of all study variables, including the high-risk pregnancy outcome. The results indicate that maternal mental health indicators exhibited the strongest associations with high-risk pregnancy, with depression showing the highest correlation, followed by anxiety. Clinical and obstetric factors such as gestational hypertension, menstrual history, parity, gravidity, and recent intrauterine growth restriction also demonstrated relatively strong correlations with the outcome. In contrast, anthropometric measures, including weight and body mass index across different time points, as well as socioeconomic variables, showed comparatively weaker associations. The correlation analysis is exploratory in nature, and the identified associations do not establish causal relationships between the predictors and high-risk pregnancy outcomes. Rather, these findings provide an initial overview of variable relationships to inform subsequent model-based analysis.

The heatmap illustrates pairwise correlations among study variables, with warmer colors indicating stronger associations and cooler colors representing weaker ones.

In the reviewed literature, the features most frequently highlighted as influential for adverse pregnancy outcomes and high-risk pregnancy prediction are predominantly pregnancy-related and maternal clinical factors. Across prior studies, recurrent themes include obstetric history and pregnancy complications (e.g., hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, fetal growth restriction related indicators, and other pregnancy complications), along with broader maternal health status variables. Demographic characteristics (such as age or socioeconomic proxies) are often included, but they are typically reported as secondary compared with clinical and obstetric variables. Overall, the literature trend suggests that variables directly reflecting the physiological course of pregnancy and maternal medical condition tend to carry the greatest predictive signal, while purely demographic variables contribute less consistently.

In the present study, the correlation heatmap shows a largely similar pattern: the strongest associations with high-risk pregnancy are concentrated in maternal mental health and key obstetric/clinical variables, with depression and anxiety emerging as the most strongly correlated features, followed by pregnancy-related clinical indicators such as gestational hypertension and other obstetric factors (e.g., menstrual history, parity/gravidity, and recent growth-related complications). This partially aligns with prior work in that pregnancy-related and maternal-condition variables remain dominant; however, our results additionally emphasize psychosocial factors (depression and anxiety) as particularly prominent in this dataset. Taken together, the comparison indicates broad agreement with the literature that pregnancy-related and maternal-condition features are generally more informative than demographic variables, while also suggesting that incorporating maternal mental health measures may provide added value for identifying high-risk pregnancies in settings similar to ours.

Best feature category

Comparative analysis of the three feature categories showed that pregnancy-related variables provided the strongest predictive performance for high-risk pregnancy classification. As reported in Table 6, this feature group achieved the most favorable overall threshold-based results, particularly for KNN, which showed high accuracy, recall, and F1-score. These findings suggest that variables such as recent IUFD, IUGR, gestational hypertension, TPO antibodies, BMI, and common complications contain more direct predictive information than demographic variables alone. The complete-case feature set produced moderate performance, but it did not consistently outperform the pregnancy-related feature group.

Model ranking with TOPSIS

The Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) was applied to rank the machine learning models based on five threshold-based performance metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, and F1-score. The results are presented in Table 13 and visualized in Fig. 11. In the Demographics category, SVM achieved the highest TOPSIS score and ranked first, reflecting the best overall trade-off among the selected evaluation criteria (Table 13; Fig. 11a). In the Pregnancy-related category, KNN ranked first, consistent with its strong accuracy and recall performance (Table 13; Fig. 11b). In the Complete case category, MLP obtained the highest rank, suggesting a more balanced performance when all available features were considered together (Table 13; Fig. 11c). These findings show that TOPSIS provided a structured multi-criteria ranking approach and helped distinguish models based on their overall performance profile rather than a single metric alone.

Discussion

This study examined the performance of multiple machine learning models for high-risk pregnancy prediction using demographic, pregnancy-related, and complete-case feature sets within a small real-world clinical dataset. The findings showed that pregnancy-related features provided stronger predictive performance than demographic variables and the complete-case feature set. Among the evaluated models, KNN achieved the most favorable threshold-based performance in the pregnancy-related category after Taguchi-based preprocessing and configuration optimization. These findings suggest that variables directly related to pregnancy history and clinical status may provide more informative signals for identifying high-risk pregnancies than demographic characteristics alone.

The main methodological contribution of this study is the development of a structured prediction framework. By integrating feature-group comparison, Taguchi-based optimization, and TOPSIS-based multi-criteria ranking, the study provides a systematic approach for evaluating machine learning models under limited-data clinical conditions. This is particularly relevant for maternal health datasets, where small sample size, class imbalance, and the need to balance multiple performance metrics can complicate model selection.

The use of hospital-based data from Iran also adds contextual value, as this population and healthcare setting have been less frequently examined in previous studies on maternal health. Unlike studies that focus on a single adverse outcome, the present framework considers high-risk pregnancy more broadly by incorporating major conditions such as intrauterine fetal demise, intrauterine growth restriction, and preterm birth. From a clinical perspective, such a framework may support future decision-support systems by helping clinicians identify high-risk pregnancies earlier and prioritize monitoring, referral, and preventive care.

The predominance of pregnancy-related and clinical features identified in this study is consistent with prior research highlighting obstetric history and medical conditions as primary determinants of high-risk pregnancy. Kuppusamy et al. reported that factors such as gestational hypertension and prior adverse outcomes were key contributors to high-risk pregnancy classification20. Similarly, Zhang et al. identified hypertension and diabetes as significant predictors in their meta-analysis, reinforcing the importance of clinical variables in predictive models21. Although some studies have reported higher accuracy using larger datasets or deep learning architectures32,40, the performance achieved in the present study is comparable given the limited sample size. Moreover, unlike many previous works that rely mainly on single-metric evaluation, this study applies a multi-criteria ranking approach, providing a more balanced comparison of model performance42.

The strong performance of the KNN model, particularly in the pregnancy-related feature set, can be attributed to its ability to capture local patterns in small and heterogeneous datasets without imposing strict distributional assumptions. This characteristic makes KNN well suited for clinical data with mixed feature types and nonlinear relationships32. In addition, correlation analysis revealed that maternal mental health indicators, especially depression and anxiety, exhibited the strongest associations with high-risk pregnancy outcomes. These findings are consistent with previous studies emphasizing the contribution of psychosocial and mental health factors to adverse pregnancy outcomes and maternal complications16,25, highlighting the importance of incorporating mental health variables alongside traditional obstetric features.

From a clinical perspective, prioritizing recall in model evaluation is critical, as false-negative predictions may result in missed identification of high-risk pregnancies and severe maternal or fetal consequences11,16. The proposed framework demonstrates the potential of integrating optimized machine learning models into early screening and clinical decision-support systems, particularly in resource-limited settings. At the policy level, the findings underscore the importance of incorporating maternal mental health assessment into routine prenatal care and data collection strategies, aligning with global recommendations for proactive maternal health interventions3,11. The use of relatively simple and interpretable models, such as KNN, further supports feasibility and scalability for real-world clinical implementation.

The additional confidence intervals and supplementary statistical analyses provide a more transparent interpretation of the reported results. Although the sample size was limited, the use of cross-validation, Taguchi-based optimization, and multi-metric evaluation helped reduce the dependence of the findings on a single performance measure or a single modeling configuration. The results, therefore, provide consistent evidence regarding the relative usefulness of pregnancy-related features and the performance patterns of the evaluated models within the available clinical dataset. At the same time, external validation using larger, multi-center datasets would further strengthen the generalizability of these findings and support future clinical translation.

Although recent deep learning, transformer-based, and hybrid architectures have shown strong performance in many prediction tasks, their application in the present study was constrained by the small size and tabular structure of the available hospital-based dataset. Under such conditions, highly complex models may be more vulnerable to overfitting and may not provide reliable comparisons without larger cohorts and external validation. Therefore, this study focused on representative and well-established machine learning classifiers to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed Taguchi-TOPSIS framework. Future studies with larger, multicenter, and multimodal maternal health datasets should further investigate whether more advanced architectures can improve predictive performance and clinical generalizability.

From a clinical application perspective, the proposed framework could serve as a supportive decision-assistance tool in hospitals, prenatal clinics, maternal health centers, and resource-limited healthcare facilities. Routinely collected demographic, obstetric, clinical, and psychosocial variables could be entered during antenatal visits to generate an early high-risk pregnancy flag. This output should not replace clinical judgment; rather, it may help clinicians prioritize closer assessment, timely referral, and individualized follow-up for women at higher risk. Such an application is consistent with antenatal care recommendations emphasizing early risk identification, detection of pregnancy-related complications, and referral to appropriate levels of care3,11.

Successful implementation of such a framework in real-world healthcare systems requires careful consideration. Potential challenges include incomplete or inconsistent clinical data, integration with existing medical records or digital workflows, staff training, model interpretability, external validation, and adaptation to local healthcare infrastructure. Therefore, the proposed framework should be viewed as a supportive screening and prioritization tool, and future studies should evaluate its performance and usability in larger, external, and multi-center clinical settings15.

Limitations and future directions

Despite the structured methodology, this study has several limitations. The small sample size and single-center design at Firoozgar Hospital in Tehran limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations and healthcare settings. In addition, the exclusion of women with thyroid disorders, while useful for controlling endocrine-related confounding, may restrict the applicability of the framework to pregnancies affected by thyroid dysfunction. The absence of genetic, biochemical, and longitudinal follow-up data also limits the ability of the models to capture more complex biological and temporal risk patterns. To partially address these constraints, the study incorporated cross-validation, Taguchi-based optimization, confidence interval analysis, and supplementary statistical testing.

Future research should extend this work in several directions. First, larger datasets from diverse geographical, demographic, and clinical populations are needed to evaluate the external validity of the proposed framework. Second, incorporating additional clinical, laboratory, genetic, psychosocial, and longitudinal maternal health variables may improve risk characterization and predictive accuracy. Third, comparative studies between machine learning models and traditional clinical risk assessment tools are required to clarify the added value of data-driven approaches in routine antenatal care. Finally, more advanced machine learning and deep learning architectures should be explored when sufficiently large and externally validated datasets are available, particularly in studies using multimodal maternal health data.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the efficacy of machine learning in predicting high-risk pregnancies, achieving 88% accuracy with the KNN model in the Pregnancy-related category. The Taguchi-TOPSIS framework optimized preprocessing and model selection, highlighting pregnancy-related features as key predictors. These findings offer a scalable approach for early risk detection in resource-limited settings like Iran, contributing to reduced maternal mortality and improved prenatal care. Future translation of this framework into clinical practice will require external validation, workflow integration, and adaptation to local healthcare infrastructure. Overall, the main contribution of this study is the development of a structured and clinically applicable machine-learning evaluation framework, rather than the proposal of a new standalone algorithm. Future work should focus on larger datasets and genetic factors to enhance model robustness and applicability.
