The effects of artificial intelligence ethics awareness, digital literacy, and metacognition on nursing students’ attitudes toward artificial intelligence
Authors: Chohee Bang, Yujin Hur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly transforming healthcare, raising new ethical challenges related to patient rights, transparency, and fairness. Nursing students must therefore develop competencies not only in digital and cognitive skills but also in AI ethics awareness to ensure responsible adoption of these technologies.MethodsA descriptive correlational study was conducted with 121 nursing students in South Korea. Validated instruments measured AI ethics awareness, digital literacy, metacognition, and attitudes toward AI. Data were analyzed using t-tests, ANOVA, Pearson’s correlation, and multiple regression.ResultsAI ethics awareness (β = 0.30, p < .001) and digital literacy (β = 0.38, p < .001) were significant predictors of attitudes toward AI, jointly explaining 31.8% of the variance. Higher levels of ethical awareness and digital competence were associated with more positive perceptions of AI.ConclusionsThe findings highlight the ethical and educational importance of preparing healthcare professionals to navigate AI responsibly. Embedding ethical principles such as autonomy, justice, and accountability into nursing and healthcare curricula, as well as into institutional and national standards, will be crucial for protecting patient rights and promoting fairness in AI-driven healthcare.Keywords: nursing students, AI ethics, digital literacy, metacognition, medical ethics, healthcare education

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly being incorporated into clinical decision-making, diagnostic procedures, and administrative operations within healthcare systems [1]. As AI continues to transform healthcare practice, nurses are expected not only to adopt these technologies but also to uphold ethical standards and maintain patient-centered care in this rapidly evolving digital environment [2]. The integration of AI presents a range of ethical challenges, including threats to human dignity, data privacy violations, algorithmic accountability concerns, and disparities in access to AI-driven resources [3]. Nursing students, as future healthcare professionals, must cultivate an awareness of AI ethics to fulfill their responsibilities in such contexts. Ethical competence, especially in relation to emerging technologies has become a key component of digital professionalism in nursing practice [4].

In this study, AI ethics awareness is defined as an understanding of ethical principles such as transparency, responsibility, and fairness in the application of AI within healthcare [2]. Digital literacy refers to the ability to access, evaluate, and effectively utilize digital tools and information [5]. Metacognition is conceptualized as an individual’s ability to monitor and regulate their own cognitive processes, including planning, evaluating, and adjusting learning strategies [6]. Metacognition has been shown to influence individuals’ openness to new technologies by enhancing critical reflection and judgment, which are essential in evaluating complex digital tools such as AI. Students with higher metacognitive awareness are more likely to adopt a balanced and adaptive stance toward AI, as they can monitor biases, reassess beliefs, and integrate new technological information into decision-making processes [7].

Previous studies have demonstrated that higher levels of digital literacy are associated with better clinical decision-making and adaptability in technology-integrated environments [8, 9]. Similarly, metacognitive abilities have been linked to enhanced academic performance, reflective thinking, and safe clinical judgment in nursing students [10]. Although prior research has explored the separate effects of digital literacy and metacognition on learning outcomes and technology use, limited attention has been given to their combined impact on attitudes toward AI. Moreover, the influence of AI ethics awareness on students’ readiness to use AI ethically remains underexamined. A recent integrative review emphasized the need to jointly foster ethical reasoning, digital fluency, and reflective thinking in nursing curricula to prepare students for AI-enhanced healthcare settings [11].

This study addresses this gap by investigating the effects of AI ethics awareness, digital literacy, and metacognition on nursing students’ attitudes toward AI. The findings aim to provide evidence-based guidance for developing AI-integrated nursing education programs.

From an ethical and psychosocial perspective, attitudes toward artificial intelligence can be understood as a proximal outcome of ethical awareness, reflecting individuals’ evaluative cognitive and affective orientation toward the adoption and use of AI technologies. Ethical awareness does not operate solely at the level of abstract moral judgment; rather, it shapes how individuals perceive, trust, and evaluate emerging technologies in relation to core ethical principles such as fairness, accountability, and respect for human autonomy. In healthcare contexts, where professional values and ethical responsibilities are central, attitudes toward AI therefore represent a meaningful and ethically salient outcome through which ethical awareness may be expressed.

Although previous studies have examined digital literacy and metacognitive abilities in relation to technology acceptance and learning outcomes, limited empirical attention has been given to how AI ethics awareness specifically influences nursing students’ attitudes toward AI within a medical ethics framework. In particular, the ethical dimension of AI-related attitudes among future healthcare professionals remains underexplored, despite growing concerns regarding responsible AI adoption in clinical practice. This study addresses this gap by examining the effects of AI ethics awareness, digital literacy, and metacognition on nursing students’ attitudes toward AI. Rather than proposing a causal or predictive behavioral model, this study aims to conceptually position AI ethics awareness as an ethically grounded determinant of evaluative orientations toward AI among future healthcare professionals.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

A descriptive correlational design was employed. The sample included 121 third- and fourth-year nursing students from accredited universities in South Korea. Using G*Power 3.1.9.7, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 109, based on an effect size of 0.15, α = 0.05, and power of 0.80 for multiple regression with three predictors. Considering a potential dropout rate, a total of 121 participants were recruited.

Instruments

All instruments used in this study have been previously developed and validated in prior research, and the corresponding references are provided.

General characteristics

Participants’ general characteristics included gender, age, religion, academic year, and prior experience with AI-related education.

AI ethics awareness

AI ethics awareness was measured using the Test for Artificial Intelligence Ethics Awareness (TAIEA) developed by Kim et al. [12]. The instrument consists of 24 items rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 24 to 120. Higher scores indicate a greater level of AI ethics awareness. The tool is organized into eight domains, each comprising three items: responsibility, stability and reliability, non-discrimination, transparency and explainability, human-centered service, employment, acceptability and limitations, and robot rights. The Cronbach’s α at the time of development was 0.81. The Cronbach’s α was 0.79 in the present study.

Digital literacy

Digital literacy was measured using a tool developed by Shin and Lee [13]. The instrument consists of 18 items across four domains: Information and Communication Technology (ICT) core competency (5 items), Social Networking Services (SNS) collaboration skills (5 items), basic work application ability (4 items), and adaptability to a software-centered society (4 items). The Cronbach’s α at the time of development was 0.91, and Cronbach’s α was 0.90 in the present study.

Metacognition

Metacognition was assessed using the Junior Metacognitive Awareness Inventory (Jr. MAI) developed by Shin and Choi [14], based on the original MAI by Schraw and Dennison [6]. The inventory consists of 30 items: 15 items assessing metacognitive knowledge and 15 assessing metacognitive regulation. The Cronbach’s α at the time of development was 0.92. The Cronbach’s α was 0.92 in the present study.

Attitudes toward AI

Attitudes toward AI were measured using a tool developed by Schepman and Rodway [15] and translated into Korean by Seo and Ahn [16]. The tool comprises 18 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with six reverse-scored items (Items 2, 5, 7, 9, 14, and 18). Higher scores indicate more positive attitudes toward AI. The Cronbach’s α at the time of development was 0.86. The Cronbach’s α was 0.92 in the present study.

Data collection and ethics

Data were collected through an online, self-administered questionnaire between September 1 and December 31, 2024. Recruitment notices included a survey link, and students who were willing to participate accessed the online form via the provided Uniform Resource Locator (URL). The questionnaire contained an information sheet and an informed consent form. Participants were required to read the study information and provide explicit consent before beginning the survey. Completion of the questionnaire took approximately 10–15 min. Upon submission, participants received a small token of appreciation for their involvement. All participants provided informed consent, and this study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University (IRB No. 1041223-202407-HR-24).

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Participants’ general characteristics were summarized using frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations. The levels of AI ethics awareness, metacognition, digital literacy, and attitudes toward AI were analyzed with descriptive statistics (mean and standard deviation). Differences in these variables according to general characteristics were examined using independent t-tests and one-way ANOVA, with Scheffé post hoc tests applied where appropriate. Relationships among AI ethics awareness, metacognition, digital literacy, and attitudes toward AI were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. Prior to conducting Pearson’s correlation analyses, the normality of AI ethics awareness, digital literacy, metacognition, and attitudes toward AI was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The results indicated no significant deviations from normality (all p > .05), supporting the use of parametric analyses. Finally, multiple regression analysis was conducted to identify the factors influencing nursing students’ attitudes toward AI.

Results

General characteristics

A total of 121 participants were included in the analysis. Of these, 84.3% were female and 15.7% were male. The average age was 22.06 ± 2.10 years. Regarding grade level, 52.1% were in their third year and 47.9% were in their fourth year. 62.8% of the participants identified as religious. A majority (95.1%) reported having prior AI-related education, while 4.9% had no such experience. Among those with AI-related education, 40.5% had learned through major courses, 52.1% through general education courses, and 2.4% through one-time sessions. Participants reported a mean satisfaction score of 6.22 ± 1.49 for AI-related coursework (Table 1).

Differences in study variables by general characteristics

AI ethics awareness, digital literacy, metacognition, and attitudes toward AI, as revealed by general characteristics, showed significant differences based on religion, experience in AI-related education, and type of education (Table 3). Male participants reported significantly higher metacognition scores compared to females (t = 3.667, p = .011). Participants who reported being religious showed significantly higher AI ethics awareness (t = 2.439, p = .017), attitudes toward AI (t = 2.180, p = .031) compared to those who were not religious. Participants with prior AI-related education demonstrated significantly higher levels of AI ethics awareness (t = 3.820, p = .049), digital literacy (t = 2.895, p = .046), metacognition (t = 2.920, p = .010), and attitudes toward AI (t = 3.044, p = .003) compared to those without such experience. Additionally, differences were observed across types of AI-related education. Participants who had taken major or general education courses showed significantly higher digital literacy (F = 4.463, p < .001) and attitudes toward AI (F = 4.953, p < .001) compared to those who had only received one-time session-based education.

Correlations

The Pearson correlation coefficients among the four variables. AI ethics awareness, digital literacy, and metacognition were each positively correlated with attitudes toward AI. The strongest correlation was between digital literacy and attitudes toward AI (r = .530, p < .001), metacognition (r=.401, p < .05), and AI ethics awareness (r = .303, p < .01). AI ethics awareness was positively correlated with digital literacy (r = .359, p < .001) and metacognition (r = .233, p < .01), while digital literacy and metacognition were also significantly correlated (r = .417, p < .01) (Table 4). This indicates that higher levels of AI ethics awareness, digital literacy and metacognition are associated with greater attitudes toward AI.

Regression analyses

To examine the factors influencing participants’ attitudes toward AI, multicollinearity among the independent variables was assessed. The variance inflation factor (VIF) values were all less than 10, and tolerance values exceeded the threshold of 0.01, indicating no multicollinearity issues. The Durbin–Watson statistic was 2.29, which indicated no autocorrelation among the independent variables. Multiple regression analysis revealed that AI ethics awareness (β = 0.30, p < .001) and digital literacy (β = 0.38, p < .001) were significant predictors of attitudes toward AI. Higher levels of AI ethics awareness and digital literacy were associated with more positive attitudes toward AI. These variables accounted for 31.8% of the variance in attitudes toward AI (Table 5). Although several demographic variables showed significant associations with the study variables in univariate analyses, the primary regression model focused on theory-driven predictors (AI ethics awareness, digital literacy, and metacognition). To address potential confounding, additional exploratory analyses were conducted including religion and prior AI-related education as covariates; the results remained consistent, with AI ethics awareness and digital literacy continuing to be significant predictors of attitudes toward AI.

Discussion

This study explored the influence of AI ethics awareness, digital literacy, and metacognition on nursing students’ attitudes toward artificial intelligence. The results demonstrated that AI ethics awareness and digital literacy independently predicted nursing students’ attitudes toward AI. Metacognition was positively correlated with attitudes toward AI; however, it did not show a unique contribution in the multiple regression model. This study contributes to the literature by clarifying how ethically grounded and cognitive competencies are differentially associated with nursing students’ attitudes toward artificial intelligence. While previous studies have largely examined technological acceptance through functional or behavioral frameworks, the present findings highlight the ethical dimension of AI-related attitudes in healthcare contexts. By empirically positioning AI ethics awareness alongside digital literacy and metacognition, this study provides conceptual insight into the ethical foundations through which future healthcare professionals form evaluative orientations toward AI. These findings are consistent with previous literature suggesting that ethical sensitivity and digital competency are essential components in shaping positive perceptions of AI in healthcare settings [11]. Although this study provides important insights, the findings are limited to Korean nursing students. Cultural and educational contexts may influence nursing students’ ethical awareness, digital literacy, and metacognitive abilities. Therefore, future research should adopt cross-cultural and longitudinal designs to examine the generalizability of these results across different nursing education systems.

AI ethics awareness emerged as a key ethical predictor of nursing students’ attitudes toward AI. This highlights that ethical competence is central to how future nurses perceive and engage with AI technologies. Nursing practice is inherently patient-centered, and ethical awareness ensures that AI is applied responsibly to protect patient rights, dignity, and safety.

The high level of AI ethics awareness among participants may be attributed to increased exposure to ethical issues surrounding AI through coursework, clinical experiences, and public discourse. Prior studies have highlighted that reflective learning and ethical case discussions enhance students’ ability to engage critically with AI technologies [2, 4]. Given the expanding role of AI in healthcare, equipping students with the ability to identify and address ethical dilemmas is essential for safeguarding patient rights and ensuring responsible technology use [3]. Given the potential for AI to both augment and challenge nursing autonomy, embedding ethical content in nursing education is critical.

From a bioethical perspective, the strong influence of AI ethics awareness on students’ attitudes toward AI can be interpreted in light of the four principles of biomedical ethics—autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice [17]. These principles provide a normative foundation for ensuring that AI adoption in healthcare protects patient rights, prevents harm, and distributes benefits fairly. In addition, the AI4People framework emphasizes transparency, accountability, and fairness as essential elements of responsible AI use [3]. These ethical considerations are increasingly emphasized in healthcare AI governance, particularly in relation to professional responsibility and ethical competence among healthcare practitioners [4]. Beyond the educational context, embedding these ethical principles into healthcare policy and institutional governance is crucial. National curricula, institutional ethics committees, and regulatory standards should explicitly incorporate AI-related ethical competencies to ensure that future healthcare professionals, as well as healthcare systems, are prepared to address emerging ethical dilemmas in the digital era. This highlights the broader relevance of nursing ethics education to the field of medical ethics.

Digital literacy also showed a significant association with AI attitudes, reinforcing its role in preparing students to work in technology-rich environments. Students with strong digital literacy are better equipped to assess AI tools, understand data interpretation, and apply digital solutions responsibly [9]. High digital literacy may enable better appraisal of algorithmic outputs, fostering a balanced view of AI as a supportive not substitutive element in clinical decision-making [8]. The importance of embedding digital competencies into nursing education to enhance adaptability and clinical effectiveness in AI-supported care. Although metacognition was positively correlated with attitudes toward AI, it did not independently predict attitudes when AI ethics awareness and digital literacy were considered simultaneously. This finding suggests that metacognition may play an indirect or supportive role by enhancing reflective engagement with AI-related information rather than directly shaping attitudes. In the context of nursing education, metacognitive skills may help students critically appraise AI outputs and reconcile them with ethical principles and clinical reasoning, but ethical awareness and digital competence appear to be more proximal determinants of attitudes toward AI. Students with greater metacognitive awareness may be more capable of self-monitoring their thought processes, evaluating information, and reconciling AI recommendations with their own clinical reasoning [18]. This supports the argument that metacognitive skills contribute to critical thinking and informed decision-making, especially in situations where human-AI collaboration is necessary [10].

The observed group differences in attitudes based on religion and prior AI-related education further suggest that both personal values and prior exposure influence openness to AI. This finding aligns with prior work indicating that ethical orientation and familiarity with AI concepts enhance receptivity to technological change [19, 20]. These findings highlight the impact of sustained and structured educational exposure to AI on students’ cognitive and ethical engagement with AI technologies.

The findings of this study suggest several practical implications for nursing ethics education in the context of AI-enabled healthcare, particularly as nurses increasingly engage with AI-supported clinical decision-making that raises new ethical responsibilities in digital health practice [21]. First, AI ethics education should move beyond abstract principles and incorporate case-based ethical discussions that reflect real-world clinical scenarios involving algorithmic decision-making, data privacy, and accountability. Such approaches may help students translate ethical awareness into evaluative attitudes toward AI.

Second, digital literacy training should be integrated with ethical reflection rather than delivered as a purely technical skill. Hands-on activities that involve interpreting AI outputs, questioning algorithmic recommendations, and identifying potential biases may strengthen students’ capacity to engage critically with AI technologies.

Third, reflective learning strategies, such as structured debriefing sessions following AI-supported simulations, may support metacognitive engagement by encouraging students to examine how ethical considerations, clinical judgment, and AI-generated information interact in decision-making processes. Together, these strategies highlight the importance of an integrated educational approach that simultaneously fosters ethical awareness, digital competence, and reflective thinking among future nurses.

Taken together, these educational approaches underscore the growing need for integrative AI education in nursing curricula that aligns ethical reasoning, digital competence, and reflective practice in response to the increasing use of artificial intelligence in healthcare [22]. While the present discussion is intentionally framed within a medical ethics perspective, the findings may also hold relevance for broader discussions on AI-integrated nursing education and professional development, which warrant further investigation in future research.

Building on these integrative insights, nursing curricula should adopt an integrated approach to AI education that combines ethical reasoning, digital literacy, and reflective learning. Specifically, AI ethics education should extend beyond abstract principles to include case-based discussions grounded in real-world clinical scenarios involving algorithmic decision-making, data privacy, and accountability, which have been shown to enhance ethical sensitivity and professional judgment in healthcare contexts [11, 23].

In addition, digital literacy training should be delivered alongside ethical reflection rather than as a purely technical skill. Hands-on learning activities that involve interpreting AI outputs, evaluating algorithmic recommendations, and identifying potential sources of bias may strengthen students’ capacity to critically engage with AI technologies and develop informed attitudes toward AI-supported care [24, 25]. Simulation-based learning that integrates AI-assisted clinical decision-making, followed by structured reflective debriefing, may further support reflective and metacognitive engagement by encouraging students to examine how ethical considerations, clinical judgment, and AI-generated information interact in practice [26, 27].

At the policy level, accrediting bodies and curriculum designers should consider incorporating AI-related competencies into national nursing education standards. Doing so will ensure that future nurses are prepared to deliver safe, ethical, and effective care in AI-enhanced environment.

Integrating these competencies into nursing curricula may enhance students’ readiness to engage with AI technologies responsibly and reflectively. Educational strategies that include ethical case discussions, digital skill-building exercises, and metacognitive training may foster a more informed and adaptive nursing workforce capable of navigating the evolving technological landscape.

Future research should expand on this study by incorporating longitudinal and cross-cultural designs to track changes in AI attitudes over time and across different educational contexts. Additionally, using performance-based assessments rather than self-report measures could provide a more objective understanding of students’ competencies.

Limitations

This study is limited by its cross-sectional design. Additionally, the sample was restricted to South Korean nursing students, and cultural and educational differences may limit the generalizability of the findings. This study also relied solely on self-reported measures, which may have introduced response bias. Performance-based or objective assessments of digital literacy, ethical decision-making, and metacognitive abilities should be included in future studies to provide more robust evidence. Although the analytical approach was limited to correlational and regression analyses, the primary contribution of this study lies in its ethical framing rather than in model complexity. By integrating empirical findings with established bioethical principles, the study offers a theoretically grounded interpretation of AI-related attitudes that is particularly relevant to the field of medical ethics.

Conclusions

This study highlights the significant influence of AI ethics awareness, digital literacy, and metacognition on nursing students’ attitudes toward artificial intelligence. AI ethics awareness emerged as a key ethically grounded predictor, alongside digital literacy, shaping nursing students’ attitudes toward artificial intelligence, indicating the importance of nurturing ethical perspectives in the context of healthcare technology adoption. The findings underscore the need for a multidimensional approach to nursing education that incorporates cognitive, ethical, and technical competencies to ensure students are well-prepared for AI-integrated clinical environments.
