Potential biomarkers and preventative interventions for muscle wasting in adolescent and young adults with cancer: A translational study
Authors: Savannah V Rauschendorfer, Fei Wang, Guanshu Liu, Prince Jeyabal, Corwin Zigler, You Zhou, J Andrew Livingston, Michael E Roth, Behrang Amini, Eugenie S Kleinerman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdolescent and young adults (AYAs) with sarcoma and our mouse model were used to evaluate early and late effects of doxorubicin (Dox) on skeletal muscle and the impact of physical activity on preventing Dox-induced damage. Skeletal muscle changes in 46 AYAs were quantified using computed tomography scans pre- and post- high-dose Dox treatment. Physical activity was monitored via wearable device. Patient blood samples were analyzed for myo-miRNA changes. In mice, we analyzed acute and late effects of Dox vs. Dox + exercise on skeletal muscle histology, Z-disc morphology, collagen deposition, changes in IL-6 and myo-miRNA, and changes in blood flow. During chemotherapy, AYA patients had poor physical activity levels and experienced an average muscle area and density reduction of -13.2% ± 10.9% (p < 0.01) and − 10.9% ± 25.6% (p = 0.01), respectively. In mice, there was an increase in number of abnormal mitochondria, autophagosomes, and disruption in Z-discs in the hindlimb at both 2 and 12-weeks post Dox. In patients and mice, miR-133b, miR-206, and miR-126 significantly decreased after Dox exposure. In the mouse model, exercise prevented all Dox-induced morphologic changes and acute Dox-induced decrease in muscle blood flow. Dox therapy resulted in skeletal muscle loss and reduction in muscle-specific miRNAs (miR-133b, 206, and 126) in plasma from mice and AYA patients, as well as in mouse muscle tissue. Changes in muscle morphology were not seen in mice that exercised, thus indicating the potential use of exercise as an intervention to prevent Dox-induced muscle damage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45296-5.Keywords: Chemotherapy Induced Muscle Wasting, Exercise, Prevention, Adolescent and Young Adults, Cancer, DoxorubicinSubject terms: Cancer, Cell biology, Diseases, Medical research, Physiology

Introduction

Childhood cancer survivors exhibit poor skeletal muscle health, with increased frailty and fatigue1–5, resulting in poorer quality of life (QOL)6. We previously showed that adolescent and young adult (AYA) cancer patients treated with combination chemotherapy containing doxorubicin (Dox) had early skeletal muscle loss7,8. Muscle health is essential for physical fitness, fatigue prevention, and cardiovascular health1,9. Identifying patients at risk for Dox-induced muscle wasting may enable early intervention to reduce fatigue and cardiotoxicity, thereby improving QOL. This requires the ability to monitor muscle deterioration and intervention efficacy during treatment. We previously demonstrated that computed tomography (CT) scans obtained during routine follow-up can be used to assess and track skeletal muscle status in AYAs treated with Dox7,8,10. We demonstrated that better physical function, quantified via sit-to-stand performance at the time of diagnosis prior to chemotherapy, was not associated with a reduced risk for skeletal muscle loss11. This finding suggests that all patients need intervention regardless of their physical function status at the time of diagnosis. While baseline physical fitness has been associated with improved clinical outcomes and increased survival12–14, our data indicated that baseline fitness status does not protect against Dox-induced muscle loss11. Whether innate physical activity levels during chemotherapy impacts muscle preservation is not known.

Muscle (myo) microRNA (miRNA) is a subgroup of small non-coding RNA molecules that are involved in translational repression expressed in striated muscles15. Myo-miRNAs have recently become therapeutic biomarkers of interest due to their regulation of skeletal muscle and potential prognostic value15. Existing literature has primarily focused on miRNAs in the context of dox-induced cardiotoxicity16–18. However, there is a paucity of research examining the extent to which muscle specific miRNAs are correlated with Dox-induced muscle damage.

Exercise can prevent muscle wasting and improve skeletal muscle health and fitness by targeting key signaling pathways that regulate protein synthesis and degradation19,20, thereby maintaining homeostasis. Resistance exercise activates the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) resulting in skeletal muscle hypertrophy via protein synthesis21. Exercise has been shown to improve neuromuscular function, resulting in the protection or improvement of skeletal muscle strength20,22. These findings suggest that exercise during chemotherapy is a potential intervention to prevent Dox-induced muscle damage and fatigue, improving QOL for AYA cancer survivors.

Here we prospectively evaluated muscle loss in AYAs receiving Dox and quantified their spontaneous level of physical activity (PA) during treatment. Understanding the relationship between PA levels during chemotherapy and muscle status will enable the development of tailored exercise protocols based on patient activity patterns and their role in preventing muscle loss. Using our mouse model8, we implemented an exercise protocol to evaluate the effectiveness of exercise in preventing the Dox-induced muscle damage, and determine whether damage seen immediately after therapy recovers or persists. Additionally, we sought to define circulating biomarkers of acute muscle damage to identify patients at-risk, and enable timely intervention with the goal of preventing the development of muscle wasting and fatigue. Skeletal muscle-specific biomarkers that are acutely upregulated may also provide a way to monitor intervention success.

The purpose of this study was to utilize both a clinical cohort (AYA individuals diagnosed with sarcoma) and our mouse model8 to evaluate and quantify the early and late effects of Dox on skeletal muscle, the impact of physical activity in preventing Dox-induced damage, and whether circulating muscle-specific miRNAs correlated with Dox-induced muscle damage.

Methods

Forty-six newly diagnosed AYA sarcoma patients receiving high dose Dox (≥ 250 mg/m2) were identified for this prospective study. The study population was identified from a primary cardiotoxicity study cohort. Cardiotoxicity study patient population characteristics can be found as Supplementary Material 1. Eligibility criteria included individuals 15–39 years old, new sarcoma diagnoses, anticipated high dose Dox treatment (≥ 250 mg/m2), and routine CT scans available prior to the start of treatment and after completion of therapy. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board ethics committee at MD Anderson Cancer Center (PA18-0462). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were informed about the purpose of the study, assured of confidentiality, and provided informed consent prior to participation. Participation was voluntary, and respondents could withdraw at any time without consequence.

Computed tomography

Skeletal muscle was analyzed using routine CT scans at two time points for quantification of skeletal muscle index (muscle area normalized to height and expressed as cm2/m2) and skeletal muscle density (muscle composition/ quality metric expressed as Hounsfield Units (HU) at thoracic vertebra 4 (T4)7,8. CT scans were assessed at diagnosis (Scan 1) and after completion of all chemotherapy (Scan 2).

Spontaneous physical activity during chemotherapy

Spontaneous physical activity was quantified via Fitbit (Fitbit Inc., San Francisco, CA). Data extracted from Fitbit included number of steps per day, average daily heart rate, and average sedentary bout length (average length of all sedentary bouts defined as consecutive minutes of sedentary activity). Spontaneous physical activity was defined as daily life activities excluding structured exercise23. To estimate general activity levels during chemotherapy activity was monitored for 60 days prior to the end of treatment. Fitbit data was considered valid and included in analyses if the device recorded ≥ 10 hr of device wear time in a 12-hour period between 8 am − 8 pm for a minimum of 10 days prior to the end of treatment. Fitbit daily wear-time threshold applied to all participants to avoid possible biases associated with informative device wear on a given day.

Juvenile mouse model

Procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and governmental regulations and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). The present study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. Four-week-old female BALB/c mice were purchased from the National Cancer Institute at Frederick8. This age was selected to model a juvenile/adolescent developmental stage corresponding to the adolescent portion of the AYA population studied. This model was intended to represent an adolescent-stage system rather than the full biological heterogeneity of the AYA population, which spans both sexes and a broad developmental range. Female mice were used to maintain consistency with our previously established juvenile Dox model8 and to reduce biological variability in this initial mechanistic study. This controlled single sex design is commonly used in preclinical mechanistic investigations to define biological pathways under standardized conditions before expanding to sex-comparative studies24. Animals were housed five per cage in individually ventilated cages under controlled temperature and humidity with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water. Environmental enrichment was provided in the form of a bedding nestlet, and mice were group housed to allow for social enrichment.

Mice were randomly divided into three groups (10 mice per group): Control (PBS), Dox, and Dox + exercise. A PBS + exercise group was not included, as the study was specifically designed to evaluate the protective effect of exercise in the context of Dox-induced skeletal muscle injury. Animals were treated with Dox (2.5 mg/kg, IV; TEVA Parenteral Medicines, Inc. Irvine, CA, U.S.A.) or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 10–20 µL/kg, IV; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) twice a week for 2 weeks. The Dox + exercise group received Dox on the same dosing schedule, combined with treadmill walking at 12 m/min for 45 min/day, 5 days/week, for 2 weeks25. Animals were monitored daily during treatment for reduced activity, decreased food or water intake, dehydration, respiratory distress, or abnormal posture. Body weight was recorded at least twice weekly. After therapy, animals were monitored three times weekly until euthanasia at the acute (2-week) or late (12-week) endpoint8. Humane endpoints included > 20% body-weight loss, persistent labored breathing, or immobility lasting more than 24 h. No animals were excluded from analysis unless they met predefined humane endpoints. Blood was obtained by retro-orbital vein puncture before treatment and 24 h after the final Dox dose. Plasma was isolated and stored at -80 °C for IL-6 and miRNA analyses. Soleus and gastrocnemius muscles were harvested at necropsy and stored at -80 °C or fixed for histologic and ultrastructural evaluation. All animals were euthanized using an automated CO₂ delivery system (Euthanex) with CO₂ introduced at 30–70% of chamber volume per minute, followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death, in accordance with AVMA guidelines.

Skeletal muscle blood flow

Skeletal muscle blood flow was assessed by contrast-enhanced ultrasound and three-dimensional (3D) ultrasound imaging and injection of microbubbles using CEU and 3D color Doppler ultrasound (3D-CDU) (Vevo 2100; VisualSonics, Toronto, Ontario, Canada) 24 h after therapy26. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (2.5% induction, 1-1.5% maintenance) and maintained on a heated imaging platform. Contrast agent microbubbles (Vevo MicroMarker; FUJIFILM VisualSonics, Inc.) were injected through the tail vein catheter according to the manufacturer’s instructions26. Three-dimensional ultrasound scanning of the left hindlimb was performed immediately before and after the microbubble injection. Images were acquired using an 18-MHz probe, and acquisition parameters, including frame rate and persistence were kept constant across animals. Analyses were performed by a sonographer who was blinded to the group’s identity.

Evaluation of collagen deposition

Mouse skeletal muscle tissues were assessed for collagen deposition using Masson trichrome staining as previously described8,25. One piece of the soleus and gastrocnemius muscles was fixed with the fixative solution (2% paraformaldehyde and 3% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer, pH 7.3, at 4 °C overnight) for TEM25. Samples were examined in a JEM 1010 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, USA, Inc., Peabody, MA) at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV. Digital images were obtained using AMT Imaging System (Advanced Microscopy Techniques Corp, Danvers, MA)25.

Evaluation of Z-disc using TEM

Damaged Z-disc quantification was adapted from Crameri et al.27. 10 random images per sample were acquired using the “search” function of the electron microscope at ×2,550 original magnification. One section was analyzed per mouse, and 10 fields were randomly selected from different regions within the same section to minimize sampling bias. Quantitative measurements were averaged to obtain a single value per mouse. The total number of Z-discs in each image was quantified by multiplying the number of Z-discs along the x- and y-axes. The Z-discs were then classified as intact (normal straight Z-disc) or disrupted (Z-discs showing morphological changes from the intact Z-discs).

IL-6 levels in mouse and patient samples

The Multi-Analyte ELISArray Kit (Qiagen) was used to quantify the IL-6 levels in the gastrocnemius muscle and plasma samples from 10 different mice from each group.

Validation and quantification of IL-6 were performed using Quantikine ELISA Kits (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN). Color intensity was proportional to the cytokine bound to the antibody. The optical density was measured using a spectrophotometer and compared to that of standard samples to determine protein concentrations. Frozen gastrocnemius tissues were thawed and homogenized in the lysis buffer. For tissue IL-6 measurement, a small portion of gastrocnemius muscle (~ 25 mg per sample) was homogenized in lysis buffer and clarified by centrifugation to obtain protein lysates. For plasma IL-6 measurement, ~ 50 µL of mouse plasma was used per assay. IL-6 concentrations were quantified using ELISA according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with 100 µL of standards and samples loaded per well. Plasma and supernatants from the tissue were collected and analyzed according to the ELISA kit. Blood samples were obtained from 16 patients (8 males, 8 females; Table 3) pre-therapy and 24 h after the completion of cycle 1 chemotherapy. The plasma was isolated, transferred into new RNAse/DNAse-free cryogenic vials and stored at -80 °C.

RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis and miRNA analysis

We selected three miRNAs (miR-133b, miR-206, and miR-126) that have been shown to play a role in skeletal muscle damage28,29. Total RNA was extracted from mouse and patient plasma samples using the miRNeasy Serum/Plasma Kit (Qiagen). During RNA isolation, 3.5 µL of Spike-In Control (cel-miRNA-39-3p) at 1.6 × 108 copies/mL was added to each sample as an internal control for plasma miRNA expression profiling and normalization of quantitative real-time PCR16. Total RNA from mouse muscle was extracted using the miRNeasy Tissue/cells Advanced Micro Kit (Qiagen). For muscle miRNA analysis, a small portion of gastrocnemius tissue (~ 5 mg per sample) was used for RNA isolation according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For plasma miRNA analysis, ~ 100–200 µL plasma was used for RNA extraction. U6 was used as the internal reference gene to normalize the real-time PCR data. The extracted RNA (muscle and plasma) was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the miScript II RT Kit with HiSpec Buffer (Qiagen). In the mouse studies, the miRNA expression level 2^-ddCt, was compared with the control to determine significance (p < 0.05). In the patient studies, a fold decrease of ≥ 0.25 compared with the pre-therapy value was considered positive.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Unpaired t-test was used for histology, Masson trichrome staining and TEM. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) to indicate the uncertainty around the estimated group mean, consistent with preclinical reporting standards and recent mouse doxorubicin–skeletal muscle studies30,31. For each experimental condition, skeletal muscle function outcomes were compared between control, and Dox-treated mice. To examine the effects of exercise, Dox outcomes were compared between Dox and Dox + exercise groups.

Descriptive statistics such as patient age, body mass index, total Dox dosage, time between scan 1 and scan 2, and spontaneous physical activity (steps, heart rate, and sedentary bout length) were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) to describe variability among individuals. For skewed variables, data are presented as median [interquartile range (IQR)]. Paired t-tests were performed for comparison of scan 1 to scan 2 for both skeletal muscle index and skeletal muscle density. Skeletal muscle metrics were indexed to height for normalization. Pearson’s correlation was utilized for observation of the relationship of skeletal muscle outcomes and spontaneous physical activity during Dox treatment. For all comparisons, a value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical computed tomography scans and spontaneous physical activity

The clinical cohort consisted of 23 male and 23 female adolescent and young adults who received an average total Dox dosage of 385.0 ± 99.3 mg/m2 (Table 1). The mean age and body mass index of the patient population was 25 ± 6 years and 28.2 ± 9.4 kg/m2, respectively (Table 1). Osteosarcoma was the most common cancer diagnosis, and the lower limbs were identified as the most common primary disease site (Table 1). There were, on average, 165 ± 81 days between baseline CT Scan 1 and the completion of Dox therapy CT Scan 2 (Table 1).

Given that the threshold criteria for physical activity data inclusion was set as participant device wear time of 10 h between 8 am and 8 pm for a minimum of 10 days during the 60-day monitoring period, only 22 out of 46 patients were retained for physical activity analyses. Patient spontaneous physical activity levels were monitored for up to 60 days. Data is reported as both mean ± SD and median [IQR] in Table 1. Acute and late effects of Dox with or without exercise on soleus and gastrocnemius muscle histology

Body weight was measured twice weekly throughout the treatment period. No statistically significant differences were observed among the Control, Dox, and Dox + exercise groups. Animals were also monitored daily for general health and activity, and no persistent lethargy or reduction in normal cage activity was observed. We previously demonstrated Dox-induced muscle damage with histologic change one week after Dox therapy8. In this study, we sought to determine whether the early histologic changes persisted and were present long-term. We also investigated whether combining exercise with Dox inhibited/decreased Dox-induced muscle damage. Mice were treated with PBS, Dox, or Dox + exercise for two weeks. Mice from each group were euthanized either two weeks (acute) or 12 weeks (late) after therapy. The soleus and gastrocnemius muscles were harvested and evaluated by TEM (Fig. 2). There was an increase in the number of abnormal mitochondria (Fig. 2A-D) and autophagosomes (Fig. 2E-H) in the soleus and gastrocnemius muscles at both the 2 and 12-week time points, indicating that the acute and late muscle damage persisted. Abnormal mitochondria were characterized by disrupted cristae architecture, mitochondrial swelling, and irregular morphology, as visualized by transmission electron microscopy. These early and late changes were not seen in mice treated with Dox + exercise (Fig. 2).

Changes in myofibrillar ultrastructure were also analyzed using 2 different methods32,33. Disturbances in the myofibrillar banding pattern of both muscles were observed at 2 weeks and 12 weeks post Dox (Fig. 3A, C). The number of disrupted Z-discs in the soleus and gastrocnemius muscles was quantified using TEM by randomly selecting 10 different locations within each sample (Fig. 3B, D). To ensure unbiased interpretation, the sample identity was concealed from the evaluator. There was a significant increase in the number of disrupted Z lines 2 and 12 weeks after Dox therapy in both muscles (Fig. 3B, D). These abnormalities were not seen at the early or late time points in either the soleus or the gastrocnemius muscles of mice treated with Dox + exercise (Fig. 3A-D). Taken together, these data confirm that the acute Dox-induced changes in the soleus and gastrocnemius muscles did not resolve over time. Exercise prevented both the acute and late Dox-induced muscle damage and preserved sarcomere structure and integrity.

We previously demonstrated that Dox significantly increased collagen deposition in the soleus and gastrocnemius muscles 2 weeks after therapy8. We wished to determine whether this change persisted 12 weeks after therapy. As shown in Fig. 3E-H, there was significant collagen deposition in the muscles in Dox-treated mice 12 weeks after therapy compared to the control mice. By contrast, there was no significant difference between the control and Dox + exercise-treated mice. These data indicate that exercise during Dox therapy prevented the acute and long-term skeletal muscle damage.

Effect of Dox or Dox + exercise on IL-6 levels in muscle and plasma and muscle-specific miRNAs

Production of IL-6 has been shown to be increased following muscle damage34,35. We therefore determined whether IL-6 was acutely elevated in the gastrocnemius muscle tissue and plasma following Dox therapy. Compared to control mice, Dox-treated mice had significantly higher IL-6 in the muscles and plasma 24 h after Dox therapy (Fig. 4A, B), consistent with its role as a non-specific acute inflammatory marker. This elevation was not observed in Dox + exercise-treated mice. There was no statistical difference between the IL-6 levels in the control and Dox + exercise-treated mice. The elevation in IL-6 in Dox-treated mice was not seen 12 weeks after therapy (data not shown). These data indicate that unlike the histologic changes described in Fig. 2, elevated IL-6 is an acute response which does not persist. The small amount of soleus muscle tissue prevented analysis of IL-6 levels.

miRNA-133b, 126, and 206 regulate skeletal muscle processes and have been shown to be decreased following muscle damage28,36. Plasma levels of miRNA-126, for example, are reflective of soleus muscle injury27. Decreased levels of miRNA-133b and miRNA-206 are associated with sarcopenia28,36. Exercise such as cycling has been shown to increase miRNA 12636. To investigate whether Dox therapy induced changes in the expression of miRNA-133b, 206, and 126, the gastrocnemius muscle and plasma samples obtained 24 h after therapy were analyzed for the expression of these miRNAs. Compared to control mice, all 3 miRNAs were decreased in the muscle and plasma from mice that received Dox but not Dox + exercise (Fig. 4C-H).

To assess whether these findings in mice are relevant to what occurs in patients treated with Dox, we evaluated plasma levels of these miRNAs in 16 patients in the clinical study (Table 3). Blood was collected before and 24 h after the first course of therapy and the plasma miRNAs were quantified. Mirroring our findings in mice, there was a significant decrease in one or more miRNAs in 14 of 16 patients (Fig. 4I-K; Table 3).

Effect of Dox or Dox + exercise on hind limb blood flow

We previously demonstrated decreased blood flow in the hind limb 24 h after Dox therapy8. We hypothesized that the decreased blood flow and loss of oxygen contributed to the Dox-induced muscle damage25. To determine whether exercise prevented the acute decrease in muscle blood flow seen 24 h after Dox therapy, mice were treated with PBS, Dox, or Dox + exercise for 2 weeks. Microbubbles were injected into the mice 24 h after therapy. Decreased blood flow in the hind limb was observed in mice treated with Dox 24 h after therapy but not in mice treated with Dox + exercise (additional files show this in more detail, see Supplementary Materials 2-6 for still images and video clips). There were significant decreases in the percentage of contrast agent (PA%) and regions of interest (ROI) in the Dox-treated compared to control mice. The PA% decreased by 6.64 ± 1.39% (p < 0.05), and ROI volume went from 777.47 ± 85.99 mm3 to 583.19 ± 53.55 mm3 (p < 0.05). In contrast, Dox + exercise-treated group showed no significant changes, with PA% decreasing 0.68 ± 0.79% (p > 0.05). The ROI volume went from 777.47 ± 85.99 mm3 to 764.94 ± 81.12 mm3 (p > 0.05).

Discussion

In the present study, we determined that significant skeletal muscle loss is experienced after high-dose Dox treatment in AYAs with sarcoma. Muscle loss at T4 was accompanied by a significant reduction in skeletal muscle quality after Dox completion. During chemotherapy, patients had poor physical activity levels, and did not reach daily target physical activity recommendation for number of steps (~ 7,000–10,000 steps/day)35,37,38. Patients were found to have roughly 2.4–3.5 times fewer steps than special populations living with disability and/or chronic illness and healthy adult populations, respectively. No relationship was found between the innate spontaneous physical activity level, as quantified via number of steps per day during treatment, and the percent skeletal muscle loss after treatment.

Dox-induced muscle damage was also demonstrated using our mouse model. We demonstrated significant acute histological changes to the soleus and gastrocnemius muscles immediately after Dox therapy which persisted long-term. There was an increase in the number of abnormal mitochondria and autophagosomes in both the soleus and gastrocnemius muscles, and a significant increase in the number of disrupted Z-discs within skeletal muscle both at 2 and 12 weeks. However, we demonstrated that these early and late Dox-induced changes were not seen in mice that exercised during Dox therapy. Our mouse model also found elevated IL-6 in both skeletal muscle and plasma 24 h after Dox therapy. This elevation was not found in mice treated with exercise. While elevated IL-6 levels may reflect systemic and local inflammatory responses, the present study was not designed to directly assess inflammatory cell infiltration within skeletal muscle to identify the cellular source. Future studies examining immune cell involvement may provide further mechanistic insight into Dox-induced muscle inflammation. Moreover, skeletal muscle specific miRNAs 206, 133b, and 126 were significantly reduced in the muscle and plasma of mice treated with Dox therapy only. Mice in the Dox + exercise group did not have a significant reduction in these myo-miRNAs. Mirroring the results found with our mouse model, patient plasma samples from 14 of 16 AYA sarcoma patients treated with 1 cycle of Dox showed a significant decrease in one or more myo-miRNAs.

The lack of relationship between average number of steps during treatment and resultant skeletal muscle area and skeletal muscle quality percent change provides insight in combination with our mouse model findings for future exercise program frequency and intensity development. It can be inferred that the cohort’s innate physical activity levels (2,866 ± 2,460 steps/day) during chemotherapy do not reach a threshold high enough to combat cancer treatment induced skeletal muscle wasting. Therefore, regimented exercise interventions (such as those used in our animal model), which surpass the current average number of steps performed by our patient cohort in the present study, and apply to nationally recommended guidelines39 should be considered to prevent chemotherapy-induced skeletal muscle damage. In the present study, treadmill walking during Dox therapy was able to preserve muscle blood flow (Figure S2) and prevent both the acute and long-term skeletal muscle loss and damage caused by Dox (Figs. 2 and 3).

Dox chemotherapy induced significant acute and late skeletal muscle damage in both our mouse model and clinical investigation in AYA patients with sarcoma. The clinical investigation provided evidence of significant skeletal muscle area reduction and decreased skeletal muscle quality utilizing routine CT scans in AYAs receiving high dose Dox. Our mouse model provided supporting translational evidence and insight to better understand the potential morphological changes which contribute to the appearance of damage. Mouse histology experiments revealed Dox increased the number of abnormal mitochondria and autophagosomes within skeletal muscle. Abnormal mitochondria are associated with functional impairments and contribute to metabolic decrements in skeletal muscle40,41. Autophagic processes are fundamental for the degradation of damaged cellular components such as mitochondria42. During muscle atrophy, there is an increase in autophagy flux42. Impairment of the autophagy system can lead to myofiber degeneration via accumulation of abnormal and/or toxic cell components42. In addition to increased abnormal mitochondria and autophagosomes, mice treated with Dox had a significant increase in Z-disc disruption within hindlimb skeletal muscle tissues. Z-disc are critical for three primary functions within the sarcomere: (1) stabilization (2) force transfer and (3) communication43. Significant disruption of Z-disc occurs after major skeletal muscle damage44. Therefore, we can infer that Dox treatment caused deleterious structural sarcomere change, which directly affected skeletal muscle contractile functionality. However, the treadmill walking exercise intervention prevented Dox associated skeletal muscle damage, demonstrating its potential as an intervention to be implemented clinically during chemotherapy in AYA cancer patients. Aerobic exercise intervention has been previously demonstrated to increase mTOR in mice45. Importantly, mTOR is a well-known regulator of skeletal muscle integrity its upregulation via exercise can lead to skeletal muscle maintenance and resilience46. A PBS + exercise group was not included, as prior studies have shown that moderate treadmill exercise in healthy mice does not induce structural muscle injury but rather promotes adaptive remodeling. The experimental design was therefore focused on isolating the protective effect of exercise in the context of Dox-induced skeletal muscle damage47,48.

Although Dox is known to have a direct impact on intramuscular protein homeostasis, we have previously hypothesized that morphological skeletal muscle damage induced by Dox may be additionally due to impairment of peripheral macro and microvascular structure and function, leading to a significant decrease in the volume of blood perfusion and the amount of contrast agent delivered to the muscle8. In the present mouse study, we again demonstrated a significant reduction in blood flow to the hindlimb after Dox administration using microbubbles and contrast-enhanced ultrasound. By contrast, mice that exercised during Dox therapy showed no acute changes in blood flow, and none of the acute or late histologic or morphologic muscular changes, providing further evidence that exercise during therapy may be an effective intervention to preserve skeletal muscle blood flow and structure. Taken together, these data support our hypothesis that in addition to a direct effect on the muscle tissue, Dox therapy may damage muscle vasculature, resulting in decreased muscle blood flow and contributing to muscle toxicity. This suggests a link between Dox-induced vascular damage and muscular toxicity. Microvascular alterations within skeletal muscle which may contribute to reduced blood flow following Dox exposure were not directly evaluated in the present study. Future investigations assessing vascular remodeling and tissue-level perfusion may provide additional mechanistic insight. Additionally, we have previously shown a similar correlation between Dox-induced cardiac vessel damage, reduced blood flow, and Dox-induced cardiotoxicity defined by functional and structural changes in heart tissue25. Although there is a growing body of literature which examines the impact of Dox on central vasculature49–51, the impact of Dox on muscle blood flow and the vasculature is limited52. Exercise also protected against both the vascular damage and Dox-induced cardiotoxicity25.

miRNAs are noncoding RNAs that target untranslated regions of mRNA to regulate its gene expression via translational repression or degradation. Circulating miRNAs found in blood plasma and urine have the potential to serve as biomarkers due to their ease of quantification and key tissue specificity. We focused on three skeletal muscle specific miRNAs (miRNA-133b, miRNA-206, and miRNA-126) to assess their potential as early biomarkers of acute DOX-induced muscle damage in both muscle tissue and plasma. miRNA 133b is key for myogenesis regulation via cytoskeletal reorganization, cell growth and cell progression53. One of the most abundant miRNAs in skeletal muscle is miRNA20654. miRNA206 is hypothesized to play a role in skeletal muscle development and plasticity54. Moreover, decreased levels of miRNA-133b and miRNA-206 are associated with sarcopenia28. miRNA-126 is associated with angiogenesis and a decrease in miRNA-126 has been shown to impair vascular integrity and impact blood flow55. We found that all three miRNAs were significantly reduced in the skeletal muscle and plasma from mice treated with Dox. By contrast, mice in the Dox + exercise group showed no change in these miRNAs. Taken together, these data support our hypothesis that an exercise intervention has the potential to mitigate Dox-induced skeletal muscle damage and that miRNAs 133b, 206, and 126 can be used to evaluate the efficacy of an intervention acutely. While circulating miRNAs were measured in whole plasma in the current study to capture the integrated circulating signal, accumulating evidence indicates that circulating miRNAs are commonly transported via extracellular vesicles (EVs), RNA-binding proteins, or lipoprotein complexes56–58, which protect them from degradation and facilitate intercellular communication. Skeletal muscle is an active secretory organ, and exercise has been shown to stimulate the release of muscle-derived EVs containing regulatory miRNAs59 that promote tissue crosstalk and contribute to systemic adaptations to physiological stress. Accordingly, the circulating myo-miRNA changes observed in the present study may, in part, reflect EV-mediated signaling from skeletal muscle. Future studies examining EV-associated miRNAs may provide additional mechanistic insight into the regulation, transport, and tissue origin of these biomarkers during Dox-induced muscle injury and exercise-mediated protection. Previous studies have proposed that myo-miRNAs, such as miRNA-126, are regulators of exercise adaptation in skeletal muscle60. Therefore, our studies also suggest that these 3 miRNAs can serve as biomarkers for early muscle damage which, in turn, can assist in identifying AYA patients who have sustained skeletal muscle damage from chemotherapy, allowing for the initiation of an intervention which may decrease the development of fatigue in survivors.

Our mouse model also found elevated IL-6 in both skeletal muscle and plasma 24 h after Dox therapy. IL-6 elevation was not found in mice treated with exercise. Exercise protects against disease associated inflammation61. Contracting skeletal muscle elicits an acute increase in IL-6 which then declines post-exercise. Production of IL-6 via exercise has an anti-inflammatory effect via TNF-α suppression and IL-1ra and IL-10 induction61. Therefore, regular acute bouts of exercise are responsible for management of chronic low-grade systemic inflammation61. However, there are translational study limitations to be considered for using IL-6 as a biomarker for acute muscle damage in sarcoma patients receiving chemotherapy with Dox and ifosfamide. We previously reported that plasma IL-6 levels in mice were increased following either ifosfamide or Dox16. However, decreased heart function and increased circulating levels of cardio-specific miRNAs were only seen in mice treated with Dox16. Plasma IL-6 was also increased in mice showing ifosfamide-induced tumor lysis16. These data indicate that IL-6 lacks tissue specificity and is general biomarker of inflammation. Whereas miRNAs are more specific biomarkers for tissue damage and thus offer greater specificity for monitoring skeletal muscle status and intervention efficacy.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the preclinical model uses a single age and sex (4-week-old female mice) to approximate an adolescent stage and does not capture the full age- and sex-related heterogeneity within the AYA population. Second, the miRNA analysis was conducted on a small sample size due to collection time point feasibility challenges (n = 16). While our targeted analysis of miR-133b, miR-206, and miR-126 revealed consistent reductions post-Dox that were mitigated by exercise, comprehensive miRNA sequencing could further elucidate broader expression profiles altered by Dox therapy and exercise interventions. Such an approach might identify novel candidates involved in mitophagy, autophagy, or vascular repair pathways, providing deeper insights into the molecular mechanisms of Dox-induced muscle wasting and exercise-mediated protection. Future studies incorporating miRNA sequencing in both preclinical models and larger AYA cohorts will be essential to validate and expand these findings. Third, given that the present study was not designed to elucidate determinates of physical activity such as tumor burden or location, the influence of tumor burden and location should be considered potential confounding factors and controlled for in future larger scale investigations. Fourth, as the study focus was on AYAs diagnosed with sarcoma and the chemotherapeutic treatment doxorubicin, these findings may not be generalizable to AYAs with other cancer types or receiving different chemotherapies. Moreover, skeletal muscle loss may vary based on tumor location and associated physical limitations which may impact activity levels. Although CT imaging fields containing the presence of local tumor were not used in the current study, we did not evaluate the influence of variable peripheral tumor location on skeletal muscle loss. Future studies should aim to address these gaps. Fifth, the translational gap between mouse treadmill exercise and human physical activity should be carefully considered.

Additionally, the importance of considering sex as a biological variable in biomedical research has been emphasized in recent guidelines62. Given that the present study utilized only female mice in accordance with our previously published model8, future studies should investigate whether these findings are also seen in males. Sixth, individual skeletal muscle wet weights and cross-sectional area were not measured, as the study was designed to focus on histologic, ultrastructural, and molecular indicators of muscle injury. Structural damage can precede measurable changes in muscle size; therefore, histologic and ultrastructural endpoints may provide sensitive indicators of early injury before overt reductions in whole-muscle mass become detectable. These endpoints may detect early muscle damage even in the absence of measurable differences in whole-muscle mass. Finally, we encountered adherence challenges with PA device wear. The restriction of PA analyses to the 22 patients with valid device wear for at least 10 days (8 am − 8 pm) within a 60-day monitoring period introduces the threat of selection bias if, for example, the patients who wear their device more exhibit different patterns of spontaneous physical activity or different relationships with skeletal muscle outcomes than those who wear their device less. Comparison of baseline characteristics between patients included and excluded on the basis of device wear shows no significant differences (see Supplementary Material 7 ).

Conclusion

In conclusion, high-dose Dox induces persistent skeletal muscle loss and quality decline in AYAs with sarcoma, detectable via routine CT scans, alongside acute reductions in circulating myo-miRNAs (133b, 206, 126) that correlate with damage in preclinical models. These miRNAs offer promise as early, non-invasive biomarkers to identify at-risk patients and monitor interventions, potentially mitigating fatigue and functional impairments in survivors. Our findings underscore exercise as a feasible, protective strategy during therapy – preventing vascular compromise, histologic damage, and miRNA dysregulation in mice – warranting larger clinical trials to translate these benefits to AYA populations and improve long-term quality of life.
