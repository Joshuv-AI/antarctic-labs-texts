Dual Parallel Intramedullary K‐Wires for Arthrodesis of the Finger Distal or Thumb Interphalangeal Joints
Authors: Hui‐Kuang Huang, Chin‐Hsien Wu, Yi‐Chao Huang, Cheng‐Yu Yin, Shu‐Ling Huang, Jung‐Pan Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveK‐wire arthrodesis methods are commonly used during arthrodesis of the finger distal interphalangeal (DIP) or thumb interphalangeal (IP) joints. Here we propose an advantageous approach involving dual parallel intramedullary K‐wires with the K‐wire tips cut to bury underneath the skin.MethodsFrom January 2017 to December 2021, 35 patients (43 joints) underwent finger DIP or thumb IP joint arthrodesis using this method. Radiographic outcomes were evaluated, while functional outcomes were assessed using the visual analogue scale (VAS) for pain and the Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (QuickDASH) score. Patients with at least 1 year follow‐up were analyzed. The preoperative and postoperative functional results were analyzed using the paired t‐test.ResultsArthrodesis union was achieved in 41/43 joints (95.3%). We treated 10 thumb IP joints and 33 finger DIP joints, for which the underlying cause was osteoarthritis and trauma in 37 and six digits, respectively. The average time of K‐wire removal was 8.9 (range, 7–10) weeks after surgery. Twenty‐four patients (27 joints; 22 women, two men) had at least 1 year follow‐up (mean 15.9; range, 12.5–40.8) months. For patients with bone healing, the VAS score improved from 6.6 (range, 5–8) to 0.6 (range, 0–1) (p < 0.001), and the QuickDASH score improved from 57.9 (range, 31.8–77.3) to 14.7 (range, 6.8–20.5) (p < 0.001) at final follow‐up. Both of the two failure cases were in the thumb. There were no other complications.ConclusionsThis technique is simple and cost‐effective and achieves a good union rate. The advantages include the ability to choose variable K‐wire sizes according to the size of the medullary canal and the ease of postoperative care.

Introduction

Arthrodesis of the finger distal interphalangeal joint (DIP) or thumb interphalangeal (IP) joint is performed with the aim of achieving a stable and painless joint. It is predominantly used to treat conditions such as degenerative, inflammatory, or traumatic joint destruction or infection and joint instability. 1 , 2 , 3 Commonly used techniques include the application of K‐wires, intraosseous wiring, headed screws, and headless compression screws, all of which can achieve a satisfactory union rate. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 However, a higher risk of pin track infection is a drawback of K‐wire fixation, while implant prominence is a significant complication of the intraosseous wiring. The headless compression screw method has been widely used for DIP arthrodesis; nevertheless, this technique has some disadvantages, including a possible size mismatch of the phalangeal canal and the screw diameter, increased cost, and the retained hardware. 3 , 8 As each of these methods has several unique advantages and disadvantages; there is still no universal consensus regarding the best method.

In DIP or IP arthrodesis, K‐wire fixation may be chosen based on the surgeon's preferences or other fixation hardware being unsuitable for use. Various configurations of K‐wire fixation can be applied, or K‐wire fixation can be combined with other fixation methods. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Options include using a single K‐wire, which can be augmented with intraosseous wiring, or employing two K‐wires in a crossed or tension band manner. Alternatively, three or more crossed K‐wires may be used. Potential issues include irritation from knots when additional wiring is used, misalignment when crossed K‐wires collide, and increased irritation if K‐wires are inserted from the lateral side. Placing the K‐wires beneath the fingertip can help alleviate this last problem by utilizing the protection provided by the growing nail plate.

To facilitate the K‐wire fixation procedures and minimize the risk of complications, we propose a method using dual parallel intramedullary K‐wires in which the K‐wire tip is cut to bury underneath the skin. This study aimed to: (i) present our experience with this technique; (ii) evaluate the healing of arthrodesis; (iii) and assess the functional outcomes and advantages of this method.

Methods

Patient Enrollment

This was a retrospective single‐surgeon study. We evaluated patients who underwent dual parallel intramedullary K‐wires for finger DIP or thumb IP arthrodesis in the full extension position between January 2017 and December 2021. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the Ditmanson Medical Foundation Chia‐Yi Christian Hospital (No. 2022042) before conducting the study.

The inclusion criteria were: (i) finger DIP or thumb IP arthrodesis including degenerative osteoarthritis, inflammatory arthropathy, infection, and traumatic problems, such as irreparable damage to the relevant flexor and/or extensor tendon, collateral ligaments, and joint, which cause symptoms of pain, deformity, or instability; 3 , 8 and (ii) minimum of 1‐year follow‐up for the final functional outcomes analysis.

In this study, we excluded patients who underwent bone graft either for the bony defect or the digit length restoration to only assess the outcomes of the arthrodesis technique without confounding factors.

Preoperative Image Evaluation

Radiographs were taken for all the involved digits from both the posterior–anterior and lateral views. The canal size, prominent osteophyte, and joint alignment were evaluated. The joint destruction condition was evaluated to determine if there was a need for bone graft during the arthrodesis, although we excluded these patients in this study.

Surgical Procedure

The procedure was performed under either local or general anesthesia, with the patient in the supine position. A digital tourniquet was used to facilitate visualization of the surgical field. We preferred to use the H‐shaped incision or a modified incision in a Z‐shape for the dorsal approach to the DIP joint 5 , 9 (Figure 1). This Z‐shape incision was sufficient to provide good visualization of the surgical field. It further ensures that the distal flap elevation does not extend too distally to avoid injuring the germinal matrix of the nail. Furthermore, the use of this incision can also avoid possible compromised circulation of the proximal nail fold flap, which sometimes occurs with the H‐shaped incision.

Exposure of the Finger Distal Interphalangeal or Thumb Interphalangeal Joint

After the skin incision, the skin flap was elevated as a full‐thickness flap of the terminal tendon. The terminal tendon was incised transversely and reflected the cut ends proximally and distally. To expose the joint, the massive spur was initially removed with a small rongeur. Release of the collateral ligaments helps achieve hyperflexion of the joint, which is not only better for articular joint preparation but is also convenient for further K‐wire insertion.

Preparation of the Articular Surface for Arthrodesis

The degenerated and destroyed articular surface and the osteophyte of the phalangeal condyle of the finger middle phalanx or thumb proximal phalanx were moved by high‐speed burr, small rongeur, or micro curette. By removing the unhealthy cartilage in the joint according to the natural contour of the middle phalanx condyle and distal phalanx base, the articulation is similar to the shape of a “cup and cone.” To achieve a symmetric cup and cone surface, verification of the surface by fluoroscopy is important to avoid arthrodesis deformity or improper bony contact for the arthrodesis, especially for the destroyed or subluxated joints. Excessive bone removal may cause fused digit shortening, for which a bone graft would subsequently be needed to ensure digital length restoration.

K‐Wire Fixation

The K‐wire fixation of the double‐ended K‐wires can be performed with an “anterograde–retrograde” technique. With the DIP or IP joint held in a flexion position, two K‐wires were driven through the prepared “cup” of the distal phalanx base. In the posterior–anterior view, the K‐wires were set apart, with approximately 1–2 mm distance in between, which could be located at both sides of the central line. In the lateral view, both K‐wires were set in the center of the medullary canal. Because the distal part of the medullary canal narrows, ensuring that both K‐wires are advanced together can avoid unsymmetrical placement of the K‐wires. Otherwise, the K‐wire driven first may run towards the center of the canal.

When both K‐wires reached the distal phalanx canal end, they were driven through the cortex of the tip and exited the skin distally. The K‐wires were then withdrawn by driving them from the distal end until the proximal end reached the bony surface of the distal phalanx base, with approximately 1 mm protruding out of the bony surface. If bone grafting is planned to restore the digital length, then a greater protruding length should be left according to the planned length for bone grafting.

Full extension is usually the preferred position for DIP and thumb IP arthrodesis. After achieving this, reduction of the distal phalanx to the planned extension position with the two protruded K‐wires engaging into proximal “cone” was performed. If bone grafting is planned, at this point, it can be set into the space between the “cup and cone.” In our practice, we prefer to use the autologous bone graft for easy space maintenance.

Subsequently, the distal phalanx was manually pushed proximally to compress the arthrodesis junction and checked under fluoroscopy to ensure no slipping of the K‐wires. Subsequently, the K‐wires were driven in the retrograde direction until reaching the subchondral level of the middle phalanx of the finger or proximal phalanx of the thumb. It is important to ensure that the K‐wires stop at the subchondral level without exceeding the cartilage into the joint. Passing the wires through the articular layer and drawing them back will increase the risk of further migration of the K‐wires proximal from the drilled hole.

Before cutting the K‐wires, the junctional site can be manually compressed by pushing the distal phalanx towards the proximal end, where they should be compressed manually again to make the arthrodesis site contact more condensed.

The K‐wires were then cut with a wire cutter by compressing the skin with the cutter blade along the K‐wires to ensure that the cutting level was as proximal as possible to stay buried under the skin. Finally, the extensor tenotomy was repaired to restore the extension mechanism, and the skin was subsequently sutured (Figure 2).

Postoperative Management

After the surgery, no hand orthosis is needed for immobilization except for patients with poor bony quality. We arranged to perform the follow‐up once every 2 weeks in the first month and again 1 month thereafter. The stitches were removed at the 2‐week follow‐up appointment, and the radiographs were checked monthly after the surgery. K‐wire removal was performed as an ambulatory surgery once the junctional healing was confirmed radiographically (Figures 3 and 4). After K‐wire removal, a follow‐up appointment was arranged 1 month later and annually thereafter. Other follow‐up appointments should be arranged if required.

K‐Wire Removal

Local anesthesia was directly injected into the spot of the fingertip where the K‐wire tip could be palpated. Using a No. 11 blade surgical scalpel, a stab wound of approximately 1–2 mm in length was made on the skin of the fingertip overlying the K‐wire tip. A small‐sized needle holder was then inserted into the stab wound to grasp the K‐wire tip for removal.

Outcome Measurements and Analysis

The visual analogue scale (VAS) for pain (where 0 = no pain; 10 = worst pain) and the Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (QuickDASH) questionnaire were used for functional evaluation at the check‐up after K‐wire removal. 10 , 11

The preoperative and postoperative functional results were analyzed using the paired t‐test, with a significance level set at p < 0. 05. All statistical analyses were conducted using the SPSS statistical software (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 24. 0, Armonk, NY: IBM, 2016).

Results

Demographic Characteristics

In total, 35 patients (43 joints) who underwent finger DIP or thumb IP joint arthrodesis were enrolled during the study period. This cohort included 10 thumb IP joints and 33 finger DIP joints, for which the cause of joint problems was osteoarthritis or inflammatory arthritis in 37 digits and trauma in six digits. Eleven patients were excluded as they declined to undergo follow‐up after healing of the arthrodesis site, meaning their follow‐up period did not reach 1 year.

Finally, 24 patients (22 women and two men) with 27 joints were enrolled for the final follow‐up evaluation. Their average age at the time of surgery was 57.3 (range, 20–84) years, and the follow‐up duration was 15.9 (range, 12.5–40.8) months. There were 15 digits involved in the dominant hand and 12 digits in the non‐dominant hand. Further, there were seven thumb IP joints and 20 finger DIP joints caused by osteoarthritis in 23 and trauma in four digits.

Radiographic and Clinical Outcomes

Arthrodesis union was achieved in 41 of 43 (95.3%) joints. The average time of K‐wire removal was 8.9 (range, 7–10) weeks after surgery. For the 24 patients enrolled in the final evaluation, two patients (two joints) failed to achieve arthrodesis union. At the final follow‐up, the 22 patients (25 joints) who achieved healing had VAS score improvements from 6.6 (range, 5–8) to 0.6 (range, 0–1) (p < 0.001), while the QuickDASH score improved from 57.9 (range, 31.8–77.3) to 14.7 (range, 6.8–20.5) (p < 0.001). These patients could return to their previous work and routine activities within 6 months after the arthrodesis surgery.

Complications

There were no patients with nail deformity, infection, or other complications and no cases of parenthesis of the healed scar of the fingertips. Of the two patients (two digits) who failed to achieve healing, one was treated with a screw for revision, and the other achieved fibrous union with less pain.

Discussion

Consideration of Using Dual Parallel Intramedullary K‐Wire

We chose to use parallel K‐wires for fixation in many of our cases for several reasons. While we do not suggest that clinicians abandon the widely‐used headless compression screw or other methods for fixation, our method is advantageous in certain cases. For example, for the headless compression screw, the smallest diameter we have in our institution has a leading thread size of 2.5 mm and a trailing thread of 2.8 mm (Acutrak 2 Micro, Acumed, Hillsboro, OR, USA). Asian people tend to have a smaller stature than Caucasians. According to Song et al., who evaluated Korean DIP arthrodesis cases, the average narrowest diameter on the lateral radiographs of the distal phalanx medullary canal of the little finger was 2.64 mm, ranging from 1.83 to 2.10 mm in the middle phalanx among the index to little finger. 12 Therefore, if the headless compression screw is planned to be used for arthrodesis, it is important to pay meticulous attention to the size of the medullary canal, especially on lateral radiographs. A size mismatch between the medullary canal of the phalanx and commercially available headless compression screws will lead to bony breakage and screw prominence in the narrow aspects of the phalanx. 13

Regarding biomechanical strength, Rigot et al. reported that dual parallel K‐wires fixation, crossed K‐wires, and headless compression screw for the DIP arthrodesis were all equally useful and superior to 90–90 intraosseous wiring fixation. 14 Another reason we prefer the use of K‐wires to intraosseous wiring is that the intraosseous wiring would need to have a wider exposure to the lateral sides of the phalanx for the wire passage and the retained wire knots can cause skin irritation.

We prefer the use of dual parallel K‐wires to crossed K‐wires, as the K‐wire tip can be cut to bury underneath the skin of the fingertip, while the oblique K‐wire from the lateral side of the phalanx hardly needs to be cut to bury beneath the skin due to the smaller amount of soft tissue laterally. Furthermore, in the setting for crossed K‐wires, the second K‐wire could hit the first K‐wire at some point. We can choose to redirect the second K‐wire away from the first one, but repeated drilling of the K‐wire will cause the K‐wires to loosen. If the second K‐wire is continuously driven forward after it has hit the first one, the second K‐wire will bend at the crossing point, potentially causing the fusion alignment to rotate. The use of parallel K‐wires can avoid this potential complication.

Bone Ends Preparation

The bone ends for the arthrodesis can be prepared with a cup and cone shape, straight surfaces, chevron, or as a tenon. 1 , 7 , 15 , 16 The straight surface would be a simple way to provide a large bone contact surface for the junction. However, the bone cuts on both sides of the bone need to be precise to achieve the planned arthrodesis angle, and cutting an excessive amount of bone would lead to digital shortening. The chevron and tenon techniques are more technically demanding. The cup and cone shape could possibly reduce the necessary bone cut and prevent excessive digital length shortening. Furthermore, the position of arthrodesis can be adjusted according to the needs of the patient. 3

Advantages and Disadvantages of Parallel K‐Wire Configuration

While it is known that a higher compression force can improve the union rate, the K‐wire fixation technique has been shown to provide poor compression force across the arthrodesis site. However, by using the dual parallel K‐wires for fixation, the flexor tendon and extensor tendon traction during the digital motion can provide a pulling force on the distal phalanx, which can increase the compression force of the distal phalanx of the arthrodesis site as the two fused phalanx are in the same axis of the smooth K‐wires.

The headless compression screw is supposed to provide a compression force across the junction. However, some studies have reported a union rate as low as 80%. 17 , 18 , 19 One possible reason for this is the large diameter of the trailing threads, which break through the cortex of the distal phalanx, resulting in loss of bony purchase by the screw. 12 Another reason may be using a dumbbell‐shaped Herbert screw, for which the thin shafts will provide less stability than the whole‐length threads conical screw, as the chance of screw piston motion within the bone could increase. 12 , 20 In addition, we propose that if the headless compression screw begins to loosen, the threads of the screw will prevent the distal phalanx from compressing proximally. This instability and non‐compression of the junction are both risk factors of nonunion. Using dual parallel K‐wires for DIP arthrodesis in the extension position, the two phalanxes are on the same axis. The terminal tendons pull the distal phalanx, which increases the compression force along the smooth K‐wire axis. This effect would be similar to that for the interlocking nail fixation for the femoral and tibial diaphyseal shaft fractures. The dynamization procedure of removing the interlocking screw(s) will facilitate compression of the fracture site to improve bone healing. 21 , 22 This is also a reason why we do not prefer to use the cross K‐wire fixation for DIP arthrodesis.

In the technique proposed in this study, the union rate was 95.3%, and the average duration of K‐wire removal for bony union was 8.9 weeks, which is not inferior to the results for headless compression screw fixation. 5 , 12 Therefore, we assume that this parallel K‐wire configuration has advantages in arthrodesis union.

Improving the Union Rate

Both failure cases in our series were thumb IP arthrodeses. One of the patients had rheumatoid arthritis and was receiving regular steroids and other immunologic agents for treatment. Osteoporotic bone was present, caused by this medical treatment. The other case had degenerative arthritis and failed to adhere to the instructions to avoid joint loading. The patient started manual work following the removal of stitches and only 2 weeks after the surgery. In addition, in both, fixation was performed using two 1.0‐mm K‐wires, which were not sufficiently large to provide an adequate dimension for the stability of the thumb IP arthrodesis.

In most circumstances, we used two 1.25‐mm K‐wires for the thumb IP arthrodesis and two 1.0‐mm K‐wires for the finger DIP arthrodesis. However, in some cases with a narrow canal of the phalanx, especially for the little finger in women, we used 0.8‐mm K‐wires for fixation. For cases with a wide canal diameter, especially in the thumb, larger K‐wires may be adapted.

In addition, loading of the operated digits should be avoided before arthrodesis union, and we suggest starting at least 1 month after the K‐wire removal so that the union can be more consolidated.

More Advantages of the Method

A main advantage of using dual intramedullary K‐wires is that variable diameters of K‐wires are available. Correctly‐sized K‐wires can be selected prior to surgery and can even be exchanged for a larger size during the surgery if the size being used is not stable. In addition, using K‐wires for fixation is a good choice for arthrodesis cases with infections or at risk of infection, such as patients with infected arthritis or open wounds (Figure 5).

Radiograph of the left thumb showing (A) preoperative interphalangeal (IP) joint destruction and dislocation; (B) the same thumb after dual parallel 1.0 mm K‐wires fixation for arthrodesis; (C) K‐wire loosening and migration in the 1 month postoperative period; and (D) achievement of fibrous union of the IP joint 1 year after the operation.

With the K‐wires’ ends cut to bury underneath the fingertip skin, it is easy for patients to manage hand hygiene. Exposed K‐wire tips outside the skin not only increase infection rates but are inconvenient for daily life and hand hygiene.

In addition, the use of the K‐wires is more cost‐effective than plate and screw fixation. Moreover, for patients who prefer to have the hardware removed, this K‐wire fixation method could be a good choice.

Limitations

The limitations of this study include its retrospective nature, the small number of cases, and the lack of long‐term follow‐up. The heterogeneous groups of joint destruction and female predominance of the patients may have caused bias. Furthermore, we were unable to compare our results with those of other bone preparation and fixation methods, such as those using headless compression screws, interosseous wiring, and other configuration of K‐wires. This method is limited to the planned arthrodesis position in full extension.

In addition, we would like to highlight the strengths of this study. We demonstrate the benefits of using parallel K‐wires for a wide range of conditions and provide detailed surgical procedures for their implementation. The combination of cutting the K‐wire tips and burying them underneath the skin of the fingertip can be advantageous for postoperative hand hygiene care before removing the K‐wires.

Conclusions

The use of the dual parallel intramedullary K‐wire method for finger DIP or thumb IP arthrodesis with the K‐wire tips buried underneath the skin is a simple and cost‐effective method. This method can achieve a good union rate with a low risk of complications. As such, this method is a good choice for many conditions requiring finger DIP or thumb IP arthrodesis. The advantages include the ability to choose variable K‐wire sizes according to the size of the medullary canal and the ease of postoperative care.
