Population Exposure–Response Efficacy Analysis of Elranatamab (PF-06863135) in Patients with Multiple Myeloma
Authors: Hoi-Kei Lon, Jennifer Hibma, Sibo Jiang, Sharon Sullivan, Erik Vandendries, Athanasia Skoura, Diane Wang, Mohamed Elmeliegy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElranatamab is a heterodimeric humanized full-length bispecific antibody composed of one B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) binding arm and one cluster of differentiation 3 (CD3) binding arm. Results from the MagnetisMM-3 study indicated deep and durable responses in patients with relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM).ObjectiveThe current analysis was conducted to characterize the relationship between free (i.e., unbound) elranatamab exposure and objective response rate (ORR), complete response rate (CRR), progression-free survival (PFS), and duration of response (DOR), and the impact of potential covariates on these exposure–response (E–R) relationships.Patients and MethodsData from four clinical studies and a wide dosing range were used for the E–R analysis. The E–R analyses of ORR and CRR were conducted by binomial logistic regression method, whereas Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves and Cox proportional hazards (PH) model were used for PFS and DOR.ResultsThe analysis included data from 312 response-evaluable patients. The results suggested that higher elranatamab exposure and lower soluble BCMA (sBCMA) were associated with higher probability of achieving objective response (OR) and complete response (CR). For PFS, higher exposure was associated with longer PFS, which is driven by rapid responses or progression events within the initial treatment cycles. At later time points, a flat relationship between exposure and PFS was observed. No E–R relationship was identified for DOR.ConclusionsThe current analyses support the approved initial dosing regimen of elranatamab and the dosing switch to less frequent dosing in responding patients during later treatment cycles.ClinicalTrials.gov IdentifiersNCT03269136, NCT04798586, NCT04649359, and NCT05014412.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11523-025-01168-y.

Key Points

The E–R analyses of elranatamab exposure and efficacy endpoints (ORR, CRR, PFS, and DOR) demonstrated that higher elranatamab exposure is associated with a higher probability of achieving OR, CR, and longer PFS. No association was observed between exposure and DOR.The current analyses support the approved initial 76 mg weekly dose, as well as the dosing switch from the initial 76 mg weekly regimen to switch to every-2-week, and later switch to every-4-week, in the RRMM population.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a hematological cancer where malignant plasma cells proliferate from the bone marrow. The American Cancer Society estimated that approximately 36,000 new cases will be diagnosed, and MM will lead to approximately 12,500 deaths in the USA in 2024 [1]. Despite recent approvals of novel therapeutic approaches, there remains a considerable unmet medical need for new modes of action and targets to treat patients whose disease does not respond to or becomes refractory to standard-of-care therapies, including immunomodulatory drugs, proteasome inhibitors, and anti-CD38 antibodies [2–4].

Bispecific antibodies (bsAbs) are a class of biologics designed to engage two specific epitopes or antigens. Compared with traditional monospecific antibodies, bsAbs, as a single molecule, can modulate the immune system in a combinational fashion, eliciting multiple mechanisms of action (MoA) with their unique and more advanced molecular structures. Through the simultaneous binding to two targets, bsAbs may accelerate the recognition of cancer cells for immune effector cells or trigger multiple signaling pathways, making them a more promising and potentially more efficacious class of biologics. Bispecific antibodies have become a major interest in oncology drug development in the past decade. As of December 2023, 11 bispecific antibodies were approved for the treatment of cancer, and three of these were approved for relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM), including elranatamab [5].

Elranatamab is a humanized, bispecific antibody that targets B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA), a member of the tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily [6–9], on myeloma cells, and CD3 on T cells. It induces cytotoxic T-cell responses against myeloma cells [10] and is approved in the USA and the EU for the treatment of adult patients with RRMM who have received at least one immunomodulatory agent, one proteasome inhibitor, and one anti-CD38 monoclonal antibody [10, 11]. The approved dosage is 76 mg subcutaneously (SC) once-weekly (QW), with a two step-up priming dose regimen (12 mg on day 1 and 32 mg on day 4), reduced to 76 mg SC every 2 weeks (Q2W) after at least 24 weeks for patients who have achieved a response. The elranatamab dosing regimen is further reduced to 76 mg SC every 4 weeks (Q4W) after at least 24 weeks of Q2W dosing. Elranatamab is now also being evaluated as a single agent or in combination for other myeloma indications including less heavily pretreated patients and newly-diagnosed multiple myeloma [12–14].

Clinical efficacy of multiple myeloma therapeutics is measured by assessment of disease response. One widely used uniform response criteria for facilitating evaluation of new and existing treatment strategies is the international uniform response criteria for multiple myeloma, which was established by the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) in 2006 [15]. Since they were first proposed, the response criteria for multiple myeloma have been modified over time, allowing the use of monoclonal protein (M-protein) concentrations, instead of synthetic rates, and including serum free light chain (sFLC) values for the assessment of oligo-secretory myeloma. On the basis of these criteria, the responses can be graded into seven categories, from progressive disease (PD) to stringent complete response (sCR). These categories represent levels of response to the treatment regimen and serve as a quantitative basis to define or derive the clinical efficacy endpoints, such as objective response rate (ORR), complete response rate (CRR), progression-free survival (PFS), and duration of response (DOR), for comparing new and established cancer therapies [16].

The current analysis focuses on exploring the exposure–response (E–R) relationship of elranatamab exposure and clinical efficacy endpoints. Understanding the E–R relationship by modeling and simulation is important for dose selection and optimization, and characterization of E–R efficacy and safety is a crucial part of a new drug application package [17, 18]. This is becoming even more critical since the launch of Project OPTIMUS, an initiative by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [19] that advocates for a change of the traditional oncology drug development paradigm and emphasizes randomized dose-finding trials as a preferred approach for dose optimization. For bispecific antibodies, there are segments of the dosing regimen, such as the priming dose regimen and change of dosing frequency at later treatment stages, that need extra consideration and require the evaluation of the E–R relationship for the clinical endpoint of interest at that specific treatment phase. The approved elranatamab priming dose regimen was optimized on the basis of a comprehensive analysis of multiple priming dose regimens, as previously described [20].

In this article, we report a pooled analysis of ORR, CRR, PFS, and DOR data across four clinical studies of elranatamab, leading to the selection and validation of the optimal maintenance dosing regimen of fixed doses of SC elranatamab. Furthermore, the impact of elranatamab exposures, as well as important covariates, such as baseline soluble BCMA (sBCMA) levels and the presence of extramedullary disease (EMD), on relevant efficacy endpoints is evaluated.

Materials/Subjects and Methods

Overview of Studies in Scope

Pooled efficacy data across four ongoing clinical studies in patients with RRMM, including MagnetisMM-1 (phase 1; NCT03269136), MagnetisMM-2 (phase 1; NCT04798586), MagnetisMM-3 (phase 2; NCT04649359), and MagnetisMM-9 (phase 1/2; NCT05014412) were analyzed. MagnetisMM-1 is a first-in-human phase 1 study where patients with RRMM were administered intravenous (IV) or SC elranatamab [21]. MagnetisMM-2 is a phase 1 study evaluating elranatamab monotherapy in Japanese patients [22]. MagnetisMM-3 is a phase 2 trial of elranatamab monotherapy in patients who are naïve (cohort A) or have previously received (cohort B) prior BCMA-targeted therapy [23]. MagnetisMM-9 is a phase 1/2 open-label trial evaluating an alternative step-up priming regimen, as well as longer dosing intervals with higher doses of elranatamab monotherapy [24].

All the clinical studies reported here are being conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonisation guidelines for Good Clinical Practice. All patients in these studies provided written informed consent. The study protocols and relevant documents were approved by independent institutional review boards or ethics committees at each investigative center. Patient safety was monitored jointly by investigators and safety assessment committees established by the sponsor.

Study Design and Patients

MagnetisMM-1 was divided into dose escalation phases (part 1 and 1.1) and a dose expansion phase (part 2 A). In part 1, each cycle was 3 weeks in duration and adult patients with advanced RRMM received either IV at doses of 0.1–50 µg/kg or SC at doses of 80–1000 µg/kg QW [21]. In part 1.1 and part 2 A, each cycle was 4 weeks in duration. Patients in part 1.1 received 600 mg/kg SC priming doses of elranatamab in the first cycle followed by 1000 mg/kg SC maintenance doses QW or once every 2 weeks (Q2W). Patients in part 2 A received 44 mg SC priming doses of elranatamab in the first cycle, followed by 76 mg SC maintenance doses QW.

In MagnetisMM-2, elranatamab was administered as a single priming dose of 600 µg/kg SC, followed by 1000 µg/kg QW in Japanese patients with RRMM [22].

MagnetisMM-3 was a two-cohort study. Cohort A enrolled patients with no prior BCMA-targeted treatment, and cohort B enrolled patients with prior BCMA-directed antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) or chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy (CAR-T)-cell therapy. It was a phase 2 registration-intent study in patients with IMiD/PI/anti-CD38 antibodies-refractory (also referred to as “triple-class refractory”) myeloma. Patients received SC elranatamab in two step-up priming doses of 12 mg and 32 mg on days 1 and 4 of cycle 1, followed by 76 mg QW in 28-day cycles [23]. If a participant had received QW dosing for at least six cycles and had achieved an IMWG response of a partial response (PR) or better, with responses persisting for at least 2 months, the dose interval was to be changed from QW to Q2W. The first four enrolled patients in MagnetisMM-3 received a single priming dose (44 mg) before implementing the two step-up dose regimen via a protocol amendment.

In MagnetisMM-9, patients with RRMM received SC elranatamab in two step-up priming doses of 4 mg and 20 mg on days 1 and 4 of cycle 1, respectively, followed by 76 mg QW in 28-day cycles (i.e., part 1) [24]. In all studies, elranatamab treatment continued until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, or withdrawal of consent. Also, in MagnetisMM-1, 2, and 9, patients were allowed to have received prior BCMA-targeted therapy.

Study Assessments

Blood samples were collected for pharmacokinetic (PK) and sBCMA analyses for MagnetisMM-1 and MagnetisMM-2: predose, during treatment (on days 1, 2, 4, 8, and 15 of cycles 1–2; and day 1 of cycle 3+), and at the end of treatment (EOT); MagnetisMM-3: predose, during treatment (on days 1, 4, 8, 15, and 22 of cycle 1, days 1 and 15 of cycle 2, and thereafter on day 1 of every third cycle), and EOT; MagnetisMM-9: predose, during treatment (on days 1, 4, and 8 of cycle 1, and on day 1 of cycles 2, 4, and 6, and thereafter every third cycle), and EOT. Serum myeloma-protein (M-protein) was assessed on day 1 of each cycle and EOT in MagnetisMM-3.

Disease assessment in all studies occurred on day 1 of every cycle and was determined by an investigator using IMWG response criteria for multiple myeloma [25]. Samples for assessment of M-protein were collected as part of the disease assessment per IMWG response criteria. An objective response (OR) was defined as confirmed stringent complete response (sCR), complete response (CR), very good partial response (VGPR) and PR, from the date of first dose until the first documentation of PD, death, or start of new anticancer therapy, whichever occurred first. The efficacy endpoints (ORR, CRR, PFS, and DOR) assessed by an investigator per IMWG criteria were assessed across all four studies. In MagnetisMM-3, these endpoints, as assessed by blinded independent central review (BICR), defined by IMWG, were also determined.

Longitudinal sBCMA and serum M-protein data were explored via scatter plots under different dosing frequencies (QW, Q2W, and Q4W) in the MagnetisMM-3 study. Pooled efficacy data, as determined by an investigator per IMWG criteria, was analyzed across all four clinical studies to determine the optimal dosing regimen of elranatamab. Only response-evaluable patients were included in this analysis. The following regimens were analyzed:

Dosing regimens with no priming dose: IV: 0.1, 0.3, 1, 3, 10, 30, and 50 µg/kg QW; and SC: 80, 130, 215, 360, 600, and 1000 µg/kg QW.

Dosing regimens with no priming dose: IV: 0.1, 0.3, 1, 3, 10, 30, and 50 µg/kg QW; and SC: 80, 130, 215, 360, 600, and 1000 µg/kg QW.

Dosing regimens with priming dose (SC): (a) one single priming dose of 44 mg on day 1 (followed by 76 mg on day 8) and then 76 mg QW; (b) one single priming dose of 600 µg/kg on day 1 (followed by 1000 µg/kg on day 8) and then 1000 µg/kg QW; (c) one single priming dose of 600 µg/kg on day 1 (followed by 1000 µg/kg on day 8) and then 1000 µg/kg Q2W; (d) step-up priming doses of 12 and 32 mg on days 1 and 4, respectively, followed by 76 mg on day 8 and then 76 mg QW; and (e) step-up priming doses of 4 and 20 mg on days 1 and 4, respectively, followed by 76 mg on day 8 and then 76 mg QW.

Dosing regimens with priming dose (SC): (a) one single priming dose of 44 mg on day 1 (followed by 76 mg on day 8) and then 76 mg QW; (b) one single priming dose of 600 µg/kg on day 1 (followed by 1000 µg/kg on day 8) and then 1000 µg/kg QW; (c) one single priming dose of 600 µg/kg on day 1 (followed by 1000 µg/kg on day 8) and then 1000 µg/kg Q2W; (d) step-up priming doses of 12 and 32 mg on days 1 and 4, respectively, followed by 76 mg on day 8 and then 76 mg QW; and (e) step-up priming doses of 4 and 20 mg on days 1 and 4, respectively, followed by 76 mg on day 8 and then 76 mg QW.

The regimen for the MagnetisMM-3 study (i.e., 12/32 two step-up priming dose regimen, followed by 76 mg QW was reduced to 76 mg Q2W starting in cycle 7 in participants who achieved a response, and was further reduced to 76 mg Q4W after at least 24 weeks of Q2W dosing).

The regimen for the MagnetisMM-3 study (i.e., 12/32 two step-up priming dose regimen, followed by 76 mg QW was reduced to 76 mg Q2W starting in cycle 7 in participants who achieved a response, and was further reduced to 76 mg Q4W after at least 24 weeks of Q2W dosing).

Elranatamab Exposure Metrics

For binary efficacy endpoints (i.e., ORR and CRR) E–R analysis, the considered free (unbounded) PK exposure metric was the average elranatamab concentration on day 28 (Cave,day28) and average elranatamab concentration up to the time of first response or progression/EOT (Cave,event). These free elranatamab summary exposure metrics were obtained using individual post hoc PK parameters from the population elranatamab PK model [26], which adequately described the PK characteristics of elranatamab by a semi-mechanistic target-mediated drug disposition (TMDD) model with first order absorption.

For time-to-event endpoints (i.e., PFS and DOR) E–R analysis, the daily exposure to elranatamab could vary throughout treatment owing to adjustments such as dosing interruptions or dose reductions. To account for these fluctuations in elranatamab exposure, time-varying average elranatamab concentration (Cave,t) was used in the DOR E–R analysis. The Cave,t values up to the times when each efficacy event (i.e., progressive disease) first occurred in a patient was calculated for that patient, as well as for all patients whose first corresponding efficacy event had not happened yet. The longitudinal Cave,t values were calculated using post hoc PK parameters from the population elranatamab PK model [26] and the time up to each event using the mrgsolve simulations in R. All exposure metrics were explored in both the natural and log-transformed scales.

Modeling Methodologies

The population E–R efficacy analyses were performed using R software version 4.1.3. R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was also used for data manipulation, post-processing, and generation of figures and tables. Summary exposure metrics were merged with the population E–R data files and used to quantify the E–R relationships of the efficacy outcomes.

The elimination process would be stopped and the model would be considered final when the removal of any of the remaining covariates results in a ΔD equivalent to p-value > 0.01.

PFS and DOR Endpoints

For the PFS and DOR E–R analysis, firstly, the graphical exploration of the association between free Cave,t and the odds of each efficacy event were conducted. On these selected efficacy event days, the Cave,t distribution in patients without events was represented as a box-and-whiskers plot. Furthermore, the Cave,t values for patients who experienced the efficacy event on that day (i.e., a progression event) were shown as dots on the box plot. To enhance the visualization of the data, a locally weighted scatterplot smoothing line was included for the Cave,t values of patients in both groups (without events and with events).

Secondly, the relationship between different free elranatamab exposure quartiles and PFS or DOR was also graphically explored using Kaplan–Meier (KM) analysis. The model-predicted average free elranatamab (Cave) up to a certain day in the study (i.e., day 28, 42, 56, and 70) were stratified into quartiles and the corresponding PFS or DOR were summarized in a KM plot to assess whether PFS or DOR differed among the exposure metric values.

Results

Patient Demographics and Baseline Characteristics

Overall, 312 response-evaluable patients were included in the analyses, including 85, 4, 177, and 33 patients who received elranatamab in MagnetisMM-1, −2, −3, and −9, respectively, (Supplementary Table 2). The median ages of patients, respectively, were 65.0, 68.5, 68.0, and 63.0 years. At baseline, 23%, 50%, 40%, and 39% of patients had extramedullary disease, and 30%, 25%, 24%, and 30% of patients had high-risk cytogenetics. Approximately 31% of patients across all studies received prior BCMA-directed therapies (25%, 25%, 34%, and 27%, respectively). Approximately half of the patients across all studies (51%) received five or more prior lines of therapy (55%, 100%, 49%, and 48%, respectively).

All 312 patients were included in the PFS analysis. There were 299 out of 312 patients who were assessment evaluable for ORR and CRR, and 13 unevaluable patients were excluded in the ORR and CRR E–R analyses. Only 153 out of the 312 patients were included in the DOR E–R analysis, as only responders were evaluated.

Relationship Between Elranatamab Exposure and ORR and CRR

The population ORR and CRR E–R analysis was conducted on the basis of the PK and efficacy response data available from patients who received elranatamab as monotherapy across the four studies. There was a total of 299 evaluable patients, 158 (52.8%) were responders, and there were 96 (32.1%) patients who achieved complete responses. The geometric mean (geometric CV%) and median (range) of free elranatamab exposure metrics used in the ORR and CCR analyses was 2730.46 (154) ng/mL and 2575.67 (0–20923.73) ng/mL.

The relationships of free elranatamab exposures versus baseline sBCMA levels and ORR and CRR were explored. Free Cave,day28 appeared to be higher in responding patients than in non-responding patients (Fig. 1), suggesting that higher free elranatamab exposure is associated with the probability of achieving objective response. Elranatamab exposure in patients with low baseline sBCMA appeared higher than in patients with high baseline sBCMA. This suggested that the low baseline sBCMA population is associated with higher elranatamab exposure and a higher likelihood of achieving OR. Similar findings were observed in the comparison of elranatamab exposure in CRR analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1), where there was higher exposure in patients who achieved CRR than the patients who did not.

Discussion

The current study characterized the E–R relationships of elranatamab exposures with four clinical efficacy endpoints: ORR, CRR, PFS, and DOR. These analyses were part of the basis for selection of elranatamab dosage and dosing schedule of elranatamab in RRMM populations. The results of the exposure-efficacy (higher exposure associated with higher probability of ORR and CRR) support the recommended full treatment dosing regimen of 76 mg QW, which was the highest tested dose across the studies. This regimen achieves the highest free elranatamab exposure at a given baseline sBCMA level in the dose range evaluated and results in a higher probability of achieving an objective response versus lower doses, with no expected impact on safety. These results support the full treatment dose of 76 mg QW regimen aiming to saturate sBCMA and maximize elranatamab free drug exposure to increase the probability of ORR. These analyses were based on the static exposure metric, Cave, day28, which is considered a reasonable timepoint to estimate drug exposure leading to a response, as day 28 approximates the median time of first response (34 days). In addition, to account for response/progression events occurring after day 28, as well as the potential impact of dose modifications to manage adverse events (AEs), similar ORR and CRR E–R logistic regression analyses using Cave,event (average elranatamab concentration up to the time of first response or progression/end of treatment) were also conducted.

In addition to the impact of drug exposure, it is also important to consider the clinical relevance and correlation of the disease-related covariates on the E–R efficacy analyses, since many of the disease and treatment-related covariates, such as prior BCMA therapy, are correlated to other disease factors. In the MagnetisMM-3 study [23], patients who had prior BCMA-directed therapies also had a higher median number of prior lines, more patients with > 5 prior lines, and more patients who were penta-drug refractory. Figure 8 shows the correlation of several disease-related covariates assessed in this study. For instance, prior BCMA therapy is moderately correlated (correlation coefficient of 0.39) to the number of prior lines of therapy (using five as cutoff) and slightly correlated (correlation coefficient of 0.18) to the presence of EMD at baseline. High baseline sBCMA concentration appeared to be moderately correlated (correlation coefficient of 0.34) to disease stage R-ISS of 3 and to penta-drug refractory status (correlation coefficient of 0.28). These indicated that one disease-related covariate might be representing the contribution of multiple prognostic factors.

A significant relationship was identified for ORR with elranatamab exposure, regardless of baseline sBCMA levels. Therefore, higher elranatamab exposure was associated with a higher probability of achieving OR. Prior BCMA therapy was also found to be a significant covariate of this relationship, which suggested that patients with prior BCMA therapy were less likely to achieve OR compared with the BCMA therapy-naïve patients. This finding was consistent with the results of the MagnetisMM-1 study, where the ORR observed in BCMA therapy-naïve patients was higher than the BCMA therapy-exposed patients [21]. It is also important to note that, with the use of the full 76 mg weekly SC dose, the predicted probability of achieving OR was approximately 65% and 85% in patients with prior BCMA therapy and in patients with no prior BCMA therapy, respectively. These results demonstrated the effectiveness of the full dose in RRMM in both BCMA-naïve and BCMA-exposed populations.

A significant relationship was also identified for CRR with elranatamab exposure. Therefore, higher elranatamab exposure was associated with higher probability of achieving CR. EMD status at baseline was also a significant covariate of this relationship, which suggested that patients with EMD at baseline were less likely to achieve CR. This finding was also consistent with the results of the MagnetisMM-3 study. The results of the ORR and CRR analyses using Cave,event as exposure metrics also yielded similar findings. The PFS E–R analysis results suggested that higher elranatamab exposure was associated with longer PFS, and having higher elranatamab exposure at the first few treatment cycles is likely to be important for achieving longer and better PFS outcomes. Once a patient responded to elranatamab and the disease did not progress, the effect of elranatamab exposure on PFS in the later treatment cycles became less apparent than in the early cycles. This is further supported by the observations of serum M-protein and plasma sBCMA. The suppression of M-protein and sBCMA after elranatamab administration was maintained in the later treatment cycles after the less frequent dosing regimen began, suggesting the effectiveness of the QW regimen of elranatamab in the early treatment cycles and that the effect of elranatamab continued in the later cycles. The Cox PH analysis and KM analysis of PFS also indicated that higher elranatamab exposure was associated with longer PFS, and these results are consistent with the above finding. This is driven by rapid responses or progression events within the first 2–3 cycles. At later time points, a flat relationship between exposure and PFS was observed. In addition, lower baseline sBCMA and no prior BCMA therapy were associated with longer PFS.

In the DOR E–R analysis, the results indicated that there was no association between elranatamab exposure with DOR. These results suggested that elranatamab exposure, across the treatment time period, did not impact DOR.

In the assessment of these four clinical endpoints, none of the demographic covariates tested were identified as significant predictors, such as: age, body weight, sex, and race. This supports the fixed-dosing approach of elranatamab in patients with RRMM. The current results of E–R efficacy analyses of elranatamab, where high exposures were associated with higher clinical efficacy outcomes (ORR, CRR, and PFS), were consistent with other bispecific antibodies, such as teclistamab [27] and mosunetuzumab [28].

The E–R analyses of PFS and DOR suggested that higher elranatamab exposure was associated with longer PFS; however, higher elranatamab exposure was not associated with achieving longer DOR. The results from both E–R analyses showed that the effects of elranatamab exposure on these efficacy endpoints were more apparent during the initial treatment cycles, which emphasized the importance of using the full dose of 76 mg QW after the priming dose phase in patients with RRMM to have higher probability of achieving longer PFS and DOR. E–R safety analyses were also conducted to evaluate the impact of elranatamab exposures on safety endpoints of interest, i.e., neutropenia, infections, and adverse effects leading to dose interruptions and discontinuations, and all these analyses indicated a flat relationship between exposure and the incidence of these key safety events [29]. These findings from E–R efficacy and safety analyses supported the use of a less frequent dosing regimen in later treatment cycles, i.e., the 76 mg QW switch to 76 mg Q2W, and then the switch to 76 mg Q4W, in patients with RRMM, and the proposed dosing switch approach is likely to maintain the clinical outcomes of keeping the QW regimen throughout the entire treatment period in patients with RRMM.

Conclusions

The E–R analyses of elranatamab exposure and efficacy endpoints (ORR, CRR, PFS, and DOR) demonstrated the E–R relationships of elranatamab exposure with ORR, CRR, and PFS. These suggested that higher elranatamab exposure is associated with a higher probability of achieving OR and CR. For PFS, higher exposure is related to longer PFS, which is driven by rapid responses or progression events within the first 2–3 cycles. At later time points, a flat relationship between exposure and PFS was observed. No E–R relationship was identified for elranatamab exposure and DOR. The current analyses support the approved initial 76 mg weekly dose, as well as the dosing switch from the initial 76 mg QW regimen to Q2W, and the later switch to Q4W, in the RRMM population.
