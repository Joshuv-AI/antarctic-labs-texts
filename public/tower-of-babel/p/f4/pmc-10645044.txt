Procedural fairness in health financing for universal health coverage: why, what and how

As countries strive to make progress towards universal health coverage (UHC), the pressing decisions confronting policymakers and the public bring to the forefront the vital role of procedural fairness. The concept encompasses how people should be informed, the level of their involvement in the decision-making processes and how their interests should be considered when important decisions affecting them are made. Procedural fairness has attracted attention across different policy-making domains, from taxation (Hartner et al., 2008), criminal justice (Tyler, 1988) and environmental management (Ruano‐Chamorro et al., 2022) to priority-setting in health (Daniels and Sabin, 2002; Baltussen et al., 2021).

For health financing policies for UHC, the issue of procedural fairness has heightened relevance. Over the past 3 years, the world has faced successive shocks which have stalled and, in some cases, reversed previous gains in UHC (United Nations, 2023; Kurowski et al., 2023b). With increasing inequities within and between countries (Chancel et al., 2022) and many demands for limited public resources, policymakers will face challenging trade-offs, often reflecting competing values and interests related to fairness, solidarity, individual responsibility and freedom of choice (Kwon, 2003; Sparkes et al., 2019; Velez et al., 2020). These trade-offs give rise to widespread disagreements regarding how funds should be raised, pooled and used, which can persist long after major decisions have been reached.

Health financing decisions exhibit additional characteristics that strengthen the case for prioritizing procedural fairness. First, many decisions are guided by technical assessments with complex assumptions and methodological approaches; a sound process can contribute to stakeholders such as providers, patients and the public comprehending the core rationale of policy options (Abelson et al., 2003; Dryzek et al., 2019). Second, power differences among stakeholders tend to play a significant role in health financing debates (Mwisongo et al., 2016; Sparkes et al., 2019); fairer processes can, by broadening participation and representation and empowering marginalized stakeholders in the decision-making process, contribute to levelling the playing field towards greater equity (Beauvais, 2018). Finally, the benefits of many health financing decisions are experienced over an extended period; decision-making processes must therefore secure the trust and buy-in from different parties to contribute to the sustainability of reforms (Chwalisz, 2020). Yet despite the essential role that process plays, how the core tenets of procedural fairness should be applied to health financing has, with few exceptions (Kapiriri and Martin, 2010; Barasa et al., 2015; Baltussen et al., 2021), been an underexplored area.

With eight articles examining the critical relationship between procedural fairness and health financing, this special supplement contributes to theoretical and empirical advancements in two significant ways. First, it expands on guiding frameworks for fair processes when allocating health-care resources, including the influential accountability for reasonableness (A4R) framework proposed by Daniels and Sabin (2002), by drawing on insights from a wider body of literature. Second, through country case studies in five different settings, it extends thinking on procedural fairness to questions on revenue generation and pooling. Procedural fairness has increasingly been given attention for priority-setting in purchasing (e.g. health benefit design), especially through various applications of the A4R framework (Maluka et al., 2011; Petricca and Bekele, 2018; Baltussen et al., 2021). Comparatively less attention has been given to decisions in the two other core health financing functions of revenue generation and pooling (World Bank, 2018).

A guiding framework for procedural fairness and policy-oriented reflections on its value for health financing

Two commentaries initiate this supplement. First, Kurowski et al. (2023a) present a framework for health financing, comprising three principles—equality, impartiality and consistency over time—and seven criteria organized into three domains: information, voice and oversight. This framework is applied and explored by the supplement’s country case studies. Moreover, the authors emphasize four potential benefits of investing in fairer processes: enhancing equitable outcomes, strengthening legitimacy, building trust in authorities and promoting sustainable health financing policies in support of UHC (World Bank, 2023).

The second commentary by González-Pier et al. (2023) convenes policymakers who have played significant roles in health financing policy in their own countries. Their experiences span a diverse array of countries, encompassing various income levels and roles in governments, health authorities and parliament. This commentary addresses a vital consideration for achieving successful implementation of procedural fairness: the perceived benefits for policymakers. Policymakers might be tempted to expedite technical aspects of health financing policy and implement reforms quickly; however, the authors argue that before the benefits of policy choices become evident, the public trust and political support gained from open and inclusive deliberation can be crucial in defending health financing reforms against opposition rooted in vested interests or ideological biases during their early stages of implementation.

Next, Dale et al. (2023) present 10 criteria for procedural fairness identified by a scoping review of the literature. Reflecting the interdisciplinary nature of procedural fairness, the scoping review draws on the literature from political theory and public administration (including deliberative democracy), public finance, environmental management, psychology and health financing. An important starting point for this scoping review was the A4R framework. Although several of the identified criteria, such as transparency, reason-giving and enforcement, align with the conditions proposed by A4R, the review finds support for the expanded framework presented by Kurowski et al. (2023a).

Country case studies: bridging theory with real-world constraints and opportunities

Five country case studies explore how the principles and criteria for procedural fairness were reflected in concrete health financing decisions, spanning all three functions. These cases underscore the tangible benefits of the proposed framework by showcasing promising practices and assisting in the identification of crucial gaps.

Numerous countries have now laid the foundation for institutions and procedures for setting priorities for a health benefit package, usually informed by health technology assessments (Bertram et al., 2021). The first case study focuses on Thailand, a country that has progressively crafted a systematic, participatory and transparent system for including new benefits into the country’s universal coverage scheme (Kantamaturapoj et al., 2020; Tangcharoensathien et al., 2020; Marshall et al., 2021). Viriyathorn et al. (2023) explore the decision-making process that led to the inclusion of pre-exposure prophylaxis. Their study illustrates favourable conditions for procedural fairness of priority-setting in Thailand, including a robust legislative mandate, institutionalized opportunities for broad representation throughout the decision-making process, long-term investment in institutional capacity for evidence generation and utilization, and close collaboration with civil society. They also highlight a key tension within the applied framework, namely how the well-intended intention to promote impartial decision-making may inadvertently limit the voices of under-represented stakeholders in the decision-making process.

The subsequent four country cases shift the focus towards health financing decisions that have received relatively less attention in terms of procedural fairness. The first is a revenue mobilization decision: the choice to introduce a tax on sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs). Kruger et al. (2023) explore the procedural fairness of South Africa’s decision to introduce an SSB tax—the Health Promotion Levy. They highlight the critical role played by a well-defined legislative process with public consultations mandated by constitutional provisions. Their case also emphasizes the shortcomings of such processes due to the self-selection of well-resourced stakeholders with defined interests, who may not always reflect the views of the entire community.

Dzhygyr et al. (2023) study Ukraine’s decisions, prior to the full-scale war, to use general taxes as the predominant source of financing for the Program of Medical Guarantees with a single set of entitlements regardless of contribution. Their study reveals how deficiencies in the decision-making process, including not respectfully treating those opposing the desirable policy objectives and lack of meaningful engagement with the public, can potentially jeopardize the long-run benefits of technically sound reform initiatives. Moreover, Ukraine’s experience underscores the significance of nurturing ‘deliberative capital’, which involves making investments in institutions that enhance the knowledge and capabilities of stakeholders to participate in health financing policy processes while fostering trust among them.

The Gambia presents a very different setting, where the National Health Insurance Act 2021 represented a progressive step towards UHC in the aftermath of democratic transition since 2017. Njie et al. (2023) study the decision-making processes shaping revenue generation for The Gambia’s new national health insurance scheme. They find that although the executive and legislative branches of government had stated intentions to ensure an inclusive and transparent process, the institutions lacked the necessary resources and operational capacity to effectively implement these intentions. Critically, this led to crucial stakeholders, such as health-care providers, population groups with special needs and community members, with views on operational aspects and equity impact of the scheme, feeling marginalized in the decision-making process.

In some cases, it is possible to implement short-to medium-term measures towards achieving UHC by enacting reforms guided by technical institutions, without formal requirements to engage in extensive consultation or subject it to the legislative process. Binyaruka et al. (2023) explore Tanzania’s improved community health fund reforms—an attempt to address fragmentation by reducing the number of risk pools of community-based health insurance, from 184 district pools to 26 regional-level pools. A valuable lesson from Tanzania is that even in situations where formal procedural requirements may not be explicitly mandated, placing a priority on fulfilling criteria for procedural fairness can make vital contributions to improving the responsiveness of health financing policy, bolstering legitimacy, fostering trust and facilitating effective implementation.

Advancing procedural fairness research in health financing to new frontiers

While the country’s case studies have shed light on many critical areas of procedural fairness in health financing, they also raise three important areas that would benefit from more research and experimentation. First, several cases highlight the difficulties of implementing open and inclusive processes in settings where powerful stakeholders with questionable motivations and self-serving interests hold significant influence (Afsahi, 2022). It is not yet clear which strategies can ensure equitable participation and inclusiveness in such settings. Second, while legislation plays a crucial role in laying the base for procedural fairness, robust institutions are required to put legislative provisions into practice efficiently and equitably (Maluka, 2011). Implementation research examining how countries can enhance the capacity of these institutions over time, identifying the barriers and facilitating actions involved can yield invaluable insights. Third, several cases highlight a significant challenge for health financing policy: the tension between leveraging political windows of opportunity to expedite reforms vs allowing time for broader representation (Binyaruka et al., 2023; Dzhygyr et al., 2023). More evidence is needed on the potential trade-offs, the costs and benefits of prioritizing procedural fairness and practical ways in which procedural fairness can still be sustained when rapid decisions are needed.

In conclusion, this special supplement provides valuable insights into the challenges associated with achieving fairer processes in health financing, as well as the opportunities for reaping the benefits of such efforts. We hope that the key themes addressed in this special supplement, as well as the recently published report on fair processes for financing UHC (World Bank, 2023), will deeply engage policymakers, advisors, practitioners, civil society members and researchers who are eager to contribute to the advancement of fairer processes in health financing.
