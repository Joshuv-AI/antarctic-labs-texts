Irinotecan with trifluridine/tipiracil and bevacizumab for second-line metastatic colorectal cancer: a phase II multicenter study
Authors: Wenwei Yang, Jing Zhang, Ping Liang, Zhaoye Qian, Donghui Wang, Wen Zhang, Baoqi Li, Chengxu Cui, Xiaomin Zhong, Guifu Wu, Yongkun Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients diagnosed with metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) face a constrained therapeutic landscape following the failure of initial treatment. This multicenter, single-arm, phase II trial (NCT06202001) aimed to assess the efficacy and safety of a novel second-line regimen comprising trifluridine/tipiracil (TAS-102), irinotecan, and bevacizumab. Patients with mCRC resistant to prior fluoropyrimidine and oxaliplatin-based chemotherapy were enrolled. Based on a preceding phase I trial, patients received biweekly cycles of oral TAS-102 (30 mg/m² twice daily, days 1–5), intravenous irinotecan (150 mg/m²), and intravenous bevacizumab (5 mg/kg). The primary outcome measure was the objective response rate (ORR). From October 2023 to August 2024, 60 patients were enrolled. As of December 2024, the ORR was 18.3% (2 complete and 9 partial responses), and the disease control rate (DCR) was 83.3%. The median progression-free survival (PFS) was 6.6 months (95% CI, 4.39–8.81), and the median overall survival (OS) was 17.3 months (95% CI, 13.55–21.05). Subgroup analyses indicated that prior resection of the primary tumor was associated with significantly longer median OS (21.9 vs. 16.2 months; p = 0.048) and PFS (8.9 vs. 5.2 months; p = 0.004). The most frequently reported treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) were nausea (100%), neutropenia (86.7%), and anemia (83.3%). The predominant grade 3/4 TRAEs included neutropenia (48.3%), febrile neutropenia (8.3%), and diarrhea (6.7%). In conclusion, the combination of irinotecan, TAS-102, and bevacizumab shows encouraging efficacy and a manageable safety profile as a second-line therapy for mCRC, meriting further investigation.Subject terms: Gastrointestinal cancer, Drug development

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) represents a major and growing global public health challenge, being the third most frequently diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer mortality worldwide.1 This trend is particularly evident in China, where both the incidence and fatality rates of CRC have risen markedly over the last decade, now ranking second and fourth, respectively.2 Treatments for metastatic CRC (mCRC) patients encompass surgical intervention, radiation, chemotherapy, targeted agents, immunotherapy, and multimodal approaches. For the vast majority of unresectable mCRC cases that are microsatellite stable (MSS), the cornerstone of management continues to be chemotherapy integrated with targeted therapies. 3,4 First- and second-line cytotoxic backbones typically involve fluoropyrimidine-based agents administered in combination with oxaliplatin, irinotecan, or both.5 The addition of targeted biological drugs—for instance, antiangiogenic agents such as bevacizumab or, for tumors harboring wild-type RAS, epidermal growth factor receptor inhibitors (cetuximab or panitumumab)—can further improve outcomes and extend survival.6 Additional precision options include human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-directed therapy for HER2-amplified mCRC and immune checkpoint inhibitors, which are approved for the subset of tumors exhibiting high microsatellite instability.7,8 Notwithstanding these advances and improvements in early detection, which have modestly reduced mortality, the aggregate burden of CRC persists, and the prognosis for mCRC remains unfavorable, with a five-year survival rate of approximately 12%.9,10 Therefore, the development of novel and more effective treatment strategies remains a pressing clinical priority.

TAS-102 is an orally administered cytotoxic drug that combines trifluorothymidine with tipiracil, a potent thymidine phosphorylase inhibitor.11 Its antitumor action operates through a mechanism distinct from that of other conventional cytotoxic agents, and preclinical studies have validated its efficacy even against malignancies resistant to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU).12 Clinical trials, including the pivotal RECOURSE and TERRA studies, have subsequently established that TAS-102 monotherapy can extend survival for patients with refractory mCRC.13,14 Nonetheless, the therapeutic impact of TAS-102 as a single agent remains modest, often failing to address the pressing demands of clinical practice. Consequently, there is a critical need to develop effective combination strategies incorporating TAS-102, aiming to enhance response rates and significantly improve survival outcomes in this difficult-to-treat patient population.

Preclinical investigations utilizing both in vitro and in vivo models have shown that the combination of TAS-102 with either irinotecan or bevacizumab results in enhanced antitumor activity against CRC cell lines and human CRC xenografts, suggesting a synergistic interaction.15–17 This foundational evidence was later supported by the large phase III SUNLIGHT trial, which established the superiority of TAS-102 combined with bevacizumab over TAS-102 alone for patients with refractory mCRC.18 Following this, the regimen of TAS-102 with bevacizumab received approval for third-line mCRC therapy. In standard practice, irinotecan serves as the cornerstone second-line agent for mCRC patients whose disease progresses after first-line oxaliplatin-based chemotherapy. Building upon this collective research background, we hypothesized that a triple-combination regimen comprising TAS-102, irinotecan, and bevacizumab could represent a viable and potentially more effective therapeutic strategy for mCRC, with a toxicity profile that may not involve substantial overlapping adverse effects. Our team previously completed an exploratory phase II clinical trial designed to assess the antitumor activity and safety profile of the regimen combining irinotecan, TAS-102, and bevacizumab in mCRC patients whose disease had advanced following first- and second-line standard therapies.19 The enrolled population comprised mCRC patients who were either naïve to irinotecan or had experienced disease progression while on prior irinotecan-containing regimen maintenance treatment. According to the published findings, this combination therapy achieved an overall response rate (ORR) of 25.8% and a disease control rate (DCR) of 93.5%. The median progression-free survival (PFS) was 9.2 months (95% confidence interval [CI], 6.285–12.115). Based on these outcomes, the preliminary analysis suggested that the regimen of irinotecan, TAS-102, and bevacizumab offers encouraging antitumor efficacy alongside an acceptable toxicity profile for patients with mCRC in the third-line or later treatment setting.

Building upon these findings, we postulated that this novel triple-combination therapy may offer superior efficacy compared to established second-line options for patients with mCRC following progression on first-line fluoropyrimidine plus oxaliplatin. Preliminary evidence supporting the feasibility of this regimen in the second-line setting comes from two independent studies conducted by research teams in the United States and Japan.20,21

Building on the results of our completed phase I dose-escalation trial, which established the recommended phase II dose (RP2D) for the triple-agent regimen of irinotecan, TAS-102, and bevacizumab, we subsequently initiated this multicenter phase II study.22 The primary objective of this trial was to assess the therapeutic efficacy and safety profile of this combination in patients with mCRC whose disease had progressed following standard first-line treatment.

Results

Patient characteristics

From October 2023 to August 2024, a total of 60 patients were recruited across the four participating centers. All enrolled patients received at least one dose of the study treatment. The median age was 58.5 years (range, 29–75 years). Left-sided colorectal cancer was dominant (73.3%). Metastatic involvement was limited to a single organ in 25 patients (41.7%), while 35 patients (58.3%) presented with metastases in two or more sites. Among the 48 patients who underwent baseline molecular profiling, RAS or BRAF mutations were identified in 29. Specifically, RAS mutations were identified in 27 patients, and BRAF mutations were identified in 2 patients. Microsatellite instability status was evaluated in 37 patients, with only one exhibiting mismatch repair deficiency or high microsatellite instability (dMMR/MSI-H). Regarding prior surgical history, 37 patients (61.7%) had undergone resection of the primary tumor, encompassing both curative and palliative procedures. All participants had previously received treatment with fluoropyrimidines and oxaliplatin. In the first-line setting, prior use of bevacizumab was documented in 48.3% of patients, and 28.3% had received anti-EGFR monoclonal antibody therapy (cetuximab). Notably, three patients had also been administered immune checkpoint inhibitors combined with chemotherapy and targeted agents as part of their initial treatment. Efficacy

At the data cutoff point (December 31, 2024), the median duration of follow-up was 13.3 months (95% CI, 11.07–15.53). At that time, 40 patients (66.7%) remained alive. All 60 enrolled patients underwent at least one postbaseline tumor evaluation and were therefore included in the efficacy analysis. Regarding treatment exposure, the median number of administered cycles was 6 (range, 3–20). The ORR was 18.3%, comprising two complete responses (CR) and nine partial responses (PR); this result did not meet the predefined primary endpoint. The DCR was 83.3%. Among the 39 patients with stable disease (SD) as their best response, 28 exhibited some degree of tumor shrinkage. The mean depth of response (DpR) was 8.24% (standard deviation ± 30.55%). Detailed efficacy data are presented in Table 2, and the maximum percentage change in tumor burden from baseline for each patient is depicted in a waterfall plot (Fig. 1). The median PFS was 6.6 months (95% CI, 4.39–8.81; Table 2 and Fig. 2a). For OS, data from two patients were censored. The 1-year OS rate was 77.6%, and the median OS was 17.3 months (95% CI, 13.55–21.05; Table 2 and Fig. 2b).

Safety

All 60 enrolled patients were included in the safety assessment. TRAEs of any grade and of grade 3/4 severity were reported in 100% (60/60) and 55% (33/60) of patients, respectively. The observed toxicity profile was predominantly hematological and gastrointestinal, consistent with the known side effects of the constituent agents irinotecan and TAS-102. The most frequently reported TRAEs of any grade were nausea (100%), neutropenia (86.7%), and anemia (83.3%). Neutropenia (48.3%), febrile neutropenia (8.3%), and diarrhea (6.7%) constituted the most common grade 3/4 events. Notably, five patients developed febrile neutropenia, all of whom required therapeutic intervention with granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF). Most adverse events proved to be reversible and clinically manageable through supportive care, dose modifications, or temporary treatment interruptions. Specifically, dose reduction was implemented in 17 patients due to severe toxicities. One patient discontinued the study protocol due to grade 3 vomiting. No treatment-related death was reported. Exploratory analyses

To explore potential prognostic biomarkers, exploratory analyses of factors associated with ORR, PFS, and OS were performed. The relationship between clinical variables and ORR was assessed using univariate followed by multivariate logistic regression. Univariate analysis suggested that prior resection of the primary tumor was associated with a higher likelihood of achieving a CR or PR, whereas an elevated baseline serum CA19-9 level (≥37 U/mL) correlated with a lower probability of response (Fig. 3a). Variables with a univariate p < 0.1 were entered into the multivariate model, which identified neither primary tumor resection nor baseline CA19-9 level as an independent predictor of tumor response (p > 0.05; Fig. 3b).

Subgroup analyses of PFS and OS revealed no statistically significant differences based on age, sex, performance status, primary tumor location, number of metastatic sites, or baseline CEA/CA19-9 levels. Notably, treatment efficacy was maintained in subgroups with RAS/BRAF mutations and in patients presenting with liver metastases. A nonsignificant trend toward improved OS (19.3 vs. 16.2 months; p = 0.155) and PFS (8.1 vs. 5.05 months; p = 0.143) was observed for patients with left-sided versus right-sided primary tumors (Supplementary Fig. 1). Regarding prior interventions, patients who had undergone primary tumor resection exhibited significantly better outcomes. Their median OS (21.9 months) and PFS (8.9 months) were significantly longer than those of patients without prior resection (16.2 and 5.2 months, respectively; Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). In contrast, previous treatment with either bevacizumab or cetuximab did not demonstrate a significant impact on OS or PFS (Supplementary Fig. 2c–f).

Discussion

Despite recent progress in colorectal cancer treatment, the existing second-line treatment of mCRC still cannot meet the clinical demands. This multicenter prospective phase II trial preliminarily evaluated the efficacy and safety profile of a regimen combining TAS-102, irinotecan, and bevacizumab in the second-line setting for mCRC. Based on our current knowledge, this investigation represents the first clinical study in China to assess this specific triple-drug combination as a second-line therapeutic option for mCRC.

SN-38, the active metabolite of irinotecan, acts synergistically with trifluridine—the primary cytotoxic component of TAS-102—to induce DNA damage and arrest the cell cycle at the G2/M phase.17 This mechanistic synergy supports the combination of TAS-102 and irinotecan as a novel therapeutic strategy for mCRC. Furthermore, integrating bevacizumab into chemotherapy regimens is known to enhance clinical outcomes in mCRC by inhibiting angiogenesis. Building on this rationale, our team previously investigated the triple combination of irinotecan, TAS-102, and bevacizumab in heavily pretreated mCRC. The reported outcomes from that study, including an ORR of 25.8% and a median PFS of 9.2 months, compared favorably with existing standard options in the third-line setting. 19

Historically, second-line chemotherapy regimens based on irinotecan have demonstrated modest efficacy in mCRC, with ORRs reported between 4% and 12.5% in prior studies.23–25 The incorporation of targeted agents, particularly those inhibiting vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), has improved outcomes when combined with cytotoxic chemotherapy. Data from multiple phase II/III trials indicate that second-line treatment with FOLFIRI (leucovorin, fluorouracil, and irinotecan) plus a VEGF inhibitor—such as bevacizumab, ramucirumab, or aflibercept—yields a median PFS of 5.7 to 7.2 months and OS of 11.2 to 18.1 months.23–27 The corresponding ORR in these studies ranged from 5% to 24.2%.23–27 In the current phase II trial, we evaluated the irinotecan, TAS-102, and bevacizumab combination as a second-line option for mCRC. The results showed a median PFS of 6.6 months, a median OS of 17.3 months, and an ORR of 18.3%, which are consistent with the outcomes observed in historical control studies. However, the study did not achieve its predefined primary endpoint of an ORR of 30%. Therefore, based on the prespecified statistical criterion, this trial did not demonstrate superiority and is considered a negative study.

Subgroup analysis was conducted to identify which group of patients would benefit from this novel study regimen. Multivariate logistic regression analysis suggested that no variable was an independent predictor of tumor response. In terms of survival benefit, patients with primary tumor resection, either radical resection or palliative resection, had a significantly longer median OS and PFS. The RAS/BRAF genotype and primary tumor location did not significantly affect PFS or OS, which was consistent with the findings of a similar clinical trial from the United States—the TABAsCO study.20 The TABAsCO study also indicated that mCRC patients without a history of bevacizumab treatment experienced significantly longer median PFS (13.7 vs. 6.4 months, p = 0.046) than patients who previously received bevacizumab treatment.20 In our study, however, prior administration of bevacizumab or cetuximab did not significantly affect the PFS or OS of mCRC patients.

Currently, this novel combination has also been explored in other countries, such as Japan and the United States. Two separate phase I studies conducted in Japan assessed this triple-drug regimen as a second-line treatment for mCRC.21,28 These trials reported ORRs of 22.2% and 21%, respectively. The corresponding median OS was 15.6 and 16.0 months, while the median PFS was 2.3 and 5.7 months. Furthermore, a multicenter phase II trial (TABAsCO, NCT04109924) performed at US centers reported an ORR of 14.3%, with a median PFS of 7.9 months and a median OS of 16.5 months.20 Collectively, these efficacy outcomes are largely consistent with the findings from our present study. Notably, however, the median OS observed in our investigation appears more favorable compared to these international datasets.

As mentioned above, our team evaluated this combination in a later-line setting (third-line or beyond) for refractory mCRC, where both the response rate and median PFS were more favorable than the outcomes observed in the present second-line study.19 Several reasons might account for this phenomenon. First, in the later-line cohort, 94.3% of patients had prior irinotecan exposure and progressed during maintenance therapy, suggesting retained tumor sensitivity to irinotecan-based treatment. The present study enrolled irinotecan-naïve patients, whose inherent sensitivity to this agent is initially unknown. Thus, the enhanced efficacy in the later-line setting may be partly attributable to a preselected population with demonstrated chemosensitivity to irinotecan. Additionally, primary tumor location is a recognized prognostic factor, with right-sided CRC generally associated with poorer outcomes than left-sided disease.29,30 The current study had a higher proportion of patients with right-sided tumors (26.7%) compared to the prior later-line cohort (20.0%), which may have contributed to the comparatively lower efficacy metrics. Notably, the cohort in this second-line study also exhibited a diminished tumor response to first-line therapy (ORR 17.6%) relative to historical reports (ORR 40–58%) for mCRC patients receiving first-line doublet chemotherapy plus bevacizumab.31–33 This suggests that the present population may have had more aggressive tumor biology and rapid disease progression, potentially explaining the inferior efficacy outcomes compared to the patients who received this regimen in a later-line setting.

The RP2D for this triplet regimen, established in our preceding phase I dose-escalation trial, was confirmed to be tolerable and associated with a manageable safety profile in patients with mCRC.22 The selected TAS-102 dose of 30 mg/m² represents a balance aimed at maximizing antitumor efficacy while limiting toxicity. As anticipated, the predominant toxicities were hematologic and gastrointestinal. The most common grade 3–4 TRAEs observed included neutropenia, febrile neutropenia, nausea, and anemia. The majority of these events were reversible, and no fatalities were attributed to the treatment. These findings confirm the regimen’s acceptable safety profile and the absence of unanticipated toxicities. This safety pattern is consistent with that reported for other irinotecan-based regimens.20,21,23–28,34,35 Historical data indicate wide ranges for hematological toxicities: all-grade and grade 3–4 neutropenia occurred in 46-100% and 11-100% of patients, respectively, while corresponding rates for thrombocytopenia were 10.7-70% and 0-10%, respectively. The incidence of hematological toxicities in our study falls within these reported intervals. It is noteworthy, however, that the rates of diarrhea—both for any grade and for grade 3 or higher—were lower in our cohort than the ranges documented historically (32.1-99.2% and 0-83.4%, respectively).

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the single-arm nature of the study limits the ability to directly compare the efficacy of this regimen against current standard second-line treatments. Second, UGT1A1 genotyping was not routinely performed, and all patients received a uniform starting dose of irinotecan; consequently, the potential association between UGT1A1 genetic polymorphisms and irinotecan-induced toxicities could not be evaluated. Third, the subgroup analyses were performed post hoc and are therefore exploratory in nature, serving to generate hypotheses rather than to provide confirmatory evidence. Fourth, the OS data are not yet mature, with only 33.3% of events recorded and a median follow-up duration that is shorter than the median OS. This suggests that the current OS estimate may be conservative, and extended follow-up is required for a definitive evaluation. Furthermore, heterogeneity in prior treatments—particularly varying prior exposure to bevacizumab—may act as a confounding factor, as initial use of bevacizumab in a later treatment line could augment its therapeutic effect. Although a prespecified subgroup analysis did not identify a significant impact, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Finally, as a nonrandomized study, our results cannot establish whether the sequential use of irinotecan-based therapy followed by trifluridine/tipiracil plus bevacizumab offers a superior efficacy-toxicity balance compared to established therapeutic sequences. These results should thus be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, underscoring the need for validation in prospective randomized controlled trials involving more homogenous patient populations.

In summary, this phase II investigation demonstrates that the combination of TAS-102, irinotecan, and bevacizumab is feasible and exhibits an acceptable safety profile for patients with mCRC whose disease has progressed after first-line fluoropyrimidine and oxaliplatin-based therapy. In the future, randomized controlled trials could be conducted to obtain more reliable data to guide clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Study design

This open-label, single-arm, phase II trial was carried out across multiple centers, including the Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, Sanhuan Cancer Hospital, Huai’an First People’s Hospital, and Yuncheng Central Hospital. The primary aim was to investigate the antitumor activity and safety of a regimen combining irinotecan, TAS-102, and bevacizumab in patients with unresectable mCRC. Eligible participants had either experienced disease progression on first-line therapy or had a recurrence within six months after completing adjuvant treatment containing fluoropyrimidine (5-fluorouracil or capecitabine) and oxaliplatin. The RP2D, established in our preceding phase I dose-escalation study, was applied. Each 14-day cycle consisted of intravenous irinotecan (150 mg/m² on day 1), intravenous bevacizumab (5 mg/kg on day 1), and oral TAS-102 (30 mg/m² twice daily on days 1–5). Patients received induction therapy for up to 12 cycles, followed by maintenance treatment with TAS-102 and bevacizumab until disease progression, unacceptable adverse events, or patient withdrawal. Dose modifications or treatment delays were permitted in cases of significant toxicity, defined as grade 3 or higher nonhematological events or grade 4 hematological events.

The trial protocol was approved by the respective Institutional Ethics Committees of all participating sites and was conducted in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Prior to enrollment, written informed consent was obtained from every patient. This study is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov under the identifier NCT06202001.

Patients

Patients diagnosed with mCRC at the four centers from October 2023 to August 2024 were recruited. The eligibility criteria for participants were as follows: (1) histologically confirmed adenocarcinoma originating in the colon or rectum; (2) a clinical diagnosis of metastatic colorectal cancer established via computed tomography (CT) and/or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), in accordance with the AJCC 8th edition staging system; (3) at least one measurable target lesion as defined by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) guidelines (version 1.1); (4) an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG PS) of 0 or 1; (5) having previously undergone a first-line oxaliplatin-based chemotherapy regimen; (6) no prior treatment with irinotecan; (7) age between 18 and 75 years; and (8) adequate hematological, hepatic, and renal function. Major exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a known history of hypersensitivity to any component of the study drugs (irinotecan, TAS-102, or bevacizumab); (2) significant active gastrointestinal bleeding; (3) any major surgical procedure or systemic antitumor therapy within four weeks prior to enrollment, excluding diagnostic biopsy; (4) a concurrent active malignancy other than mCRC; (5) severe or uncontrolled comorbid medical conditions; (6) pregnancy or lactation; and (7) any other circumstance that, in the investigator’s judgment, would make the patient unsuitable for study participation.

Study Endpoints

The primary endpoint of this trial was the ORR, which refers to the proportion of patients whose best overall response, assessed per RECIST version 1.1, was either a complete response (CR) or a partial response (PR). Secondary endpoints included PFS, overall survival (OS), and safety. PFS was calculated as the interval from the initiation of study treatment until the first documented occurrence of disease progression or death from any cause. OS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to the date of death. For patients who were alive at the time of analysis or lost to follow-up, survival data were censored on the date of their last confirmed clinical assessment. Safety was evaluated by recording the incidence and severity of adverse events (AEs), which were graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0.

Efficacy and safety assessment

Tumor imaging for response evaluation was conducted at baseline (within 14 days prior to therapy initiation) and subsequently at intervals of approximately every six weeks (corresponding to every three treatment cycles). Additional assessments were performed earlier if clinical suspicion of disease progression arose. Evaluations utilized computed tomography (CT) or, when clinically indicated, other appropriate imaging modalities, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron-emission tomography combined with CT (PET-CT), or bone scans. Tumor response was determined by the investigators in accordance with RECIST, version 1.1. Patients were deemed evaluable for treatment efficacy if they had completed a minimum of one full treatment cycle and had undergone at least one postbaseline radiographic assessment. All patients who received one or more doses of any study drug constituted the safety population for toxicity analyses. AEs were monitored throughout the study, and their severity was classified using the CTCAE, version 5.0.

Statistical analysis

This exploratory phase II trial was designed to detect an improvement in the ORR. Based on historical data indicating an ORR of approximately 15% for relevant therapies, the study aimed to achieve an increased ORR of 30% with the investigational regimen. Factoring in an estimated dropout rate of 10%, a target sample size of 58 patients was determined to provide 80% statistical power with a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. This calculation was performed using Power Analysis for Sample Size (PASS) software, version 15.

Continuous variables are summarized as median values with their corresponding ranges, while categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Time-to-event endpoints, specifically PFS and OS, were evaluated using the Kaplan‒Meier method. The results are expressed as median survival times with 95% confidence intervals, and survival distributions were depicted by Kaplan‒Meier survival curves. Differences in PFS and OS across subgroups were compared using the log-rank test. Multivariate analysis of factors associated with ORR was conducted using logistic regression models. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism (version 10; GraphPad Software, Inc.).

To protect participant confidentiality and comply with regulatory requirements, the individual-level datasets produced and examined in this research are not deposited in a public repository. However, anonymized data may be provided to qualified researchers following a formal request directed to the corresponding authors. Such requests, which should include a scientifically valid study proposal and a detailed analysis plan, will be subject to review and approval by the authors and the sponsoring institution. Prior to data release, requestors will be required to execute a formal data use or access agreement with the study sponsor.
