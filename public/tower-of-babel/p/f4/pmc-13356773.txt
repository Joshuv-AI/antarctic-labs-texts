Sociodemographic Determinants of Timely Hip Dysplasia Ultrasound Screening
Authors: CeAnn Romanaggi, Julianna Mazziotti, Spencer Smith, Jung Yoo, Matthew Halsey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreech presentation is a strong risk factor for developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH), a condition that, when diagnosed late, can lead to poor outcomes. Accordingly, it is recommended that breech-born infants undergo hip ultrasound (US) screening at 6 weeks or receive an anteriorpostierior (AP) pelvic radiograph at 6 months of age to enable early detection and timely intervention. Research evaluating guideline adherence and the factors influencing screening outcomes remain limited. In this study, we investigate sociodemographic factors that influence delayed or missed US screening among breech-born infants.MethodsAn institutional review board–approved, retrospective cohort study of breech-born infants between 2008 and 2023 at a single institution was conducted. Sociodemographic variables as well as presence and/or timing of US screening were extracted from electronic health records. Screening outcomes were categorized by US timing: (1) screened between 4 and 8 weeks (defined as adherent), (2) screened outside this window, or (3) not screened. Comparisons were performed across groups.ResultsTwo hundred fifty-four infants were born breech and met eligibility criteria. Fifty-one percent underwent “timely” screening, 28% were screened outside the recommended window, and 21% had no screening at all. Univariate analysis demonstrated that infant ethnicity, maternal marital status, insurance type, and Area Deprivation Index (ADI) were significantly associated with screening timeliness. In multivariate analyses, infant ethnicity, as well as maternal insurance type, marital status, and ADI were associated with reduced likelihood of adherent screening.ConclusionsDespite the recommended US screening at 6 weeks of age for infants with select risk factors for DDH, half of infants were screened in a timely fashion, while 1 in 5 infants were not screened at all. Several maternal sociodemographic characteristics were associated with untimely or lack of screening. Addressing these factors through targeted interventions is critical to reduce disparities in early identification, timely treatment, and DDH outcomes overall.Key Concepts(1)Fifty-one percent of breech-born infants received a screening ultrasound that was adherent to guidelines.(2)Twenty-one percent of the population for whom screening was recommended did not receive screening at all.(3)Screening completion and timeliness is impacted by maternal marital status, insurance type, and area deprivation index, as well as infant ethnicity.(4)More equitable screening outcomes for developmental dysplasia of the hip will require system-level changes, driven by the collaboration of specialists and primary care providers, which address the social determinants of health.Level of EvidenceLevel III, Retrospective Cohort

Introduction

Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) is a common orthopaedic condition in newborns, with reported incidence varying based on the timing and method of diagnosis [1]. DDH encompasses a broad spectrum of hip abnormalities, ranging from mild instability that may resolve spontaneously to more severe structural pathology, including acetabular dysplasia, subluxation, and irreducible hip dislocation requiring surgical intervention [1,2]. Well-established risk factors include breech presentation, female sex, family history, oligohydramnios, and post-term gestation, with breech presentation consistently identified as a strong risk factor [1,3]. If left untreated, DDH may lead to chronic hip instability, dislocation, and early-onset hip osteoarthritis in adulthood [4]. Therefore, screening strategies that facilitate early detection of clinically significant DDH are critical for preserving long-term hip function.

Evidence-based clinical practice guidelines for DDH screening in the United States emphasize the use of careful clinical examination combined with risk-selective imaging, rather than universal ultrasound (US) screening [5,6]. Accordingly, guidelines from the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons recommend targeted screening for infants with identified risk factors (breech presentation, family history, history of improper swaddling, or history of clinical instability) including an US at approximately 6 weeks of age or an anteroposterior pelvic radiograph at 4-6 months of age [7]. Despite this recommendation, variability in screening is implied by the persistent rates of delayed DDH diagnosis, particularly among infants who are non-White, prefer a language other than English, are from lower income areas, or are publicly insured [8]. Additionally, 50% of pediatricians and 68% of surgeons working at institutions do not have a standard care pathway for DDH, which may contribute to these disparities [9,10]. Even in the presence of a national screening program, adherence to guidelines among clinicians is variable and screening adherence remains low, with only 58.8% of infants in England undergoing adherent screening [11,12].

While existing literature has focused on patient-related factors associated with delayed diagnosis of DDH, which may have been precipitated by improper screening, few studies have examined factors influencing the screening process itself [8,13]. Recent evidence suggests that neighborhood-level socioeconomic measures, such as the Area Deprivation Index (ADI), as well as insurance status, are associated with DDH diagnosis and subsequent treatment patterns; however, additional research is needed to characterize the sociodemographic factors that impact timely screening [14]. This study seeks to address this gap by (1) evaluating DDH screening practices among breech-born infants at a single institution and (2) identifying maternal and infant sociodemographic factors associated with delayed or missed screening. Identifying populations at increased risk of delayed diagnosis is essential for the development of targeted interventions and promoting more effective DDH screening practices.

Materials and methods

This retrospective cohort study included breech-born infants delivered between January 2008 and December 2023 at a single tertiary care institution. Eligible patients were identified through the institutional electronic medical record using the international classification of disease, tenth revision (ICD-10) diagnosis code P03.0 (“Newborn affected by breech delivery and extraction”) and ICD-9 code 763.0 (“Breech delivery and extraction affecting fetus or newborn”). Only infants delivered at the study institution were included to ensure complete capture of perinatal and follow-up data; infants who received care at the institution but were born elsewhere were not included. Patients with missing data required for classification of screening outcomes or independent variables were excluded from the analysis as were patients with no insurance or Veterans Affairs (VA) insurance due to small subgroup sizes. All infants meeting inclusion criteria based on ICD coding were included without additional exclusion criteria, including those with comorbid conditions. Demographic and clinical data were extracted from the electronic health record for both mothers and infants. Maternal variables included national ADI, ethnicity (Hispanic vs non-Hispanic), age, marital status, insurance status, and documented need for an interpreter. Infant variables included sex, gestational age, birth weight, delivery method, and ethnicity.

The primary outcome was the timing of hip US screening. Current guidelines recommend selective hip US screening for breech infants at approximately 6 weeks of age [7,15]. To account for real-world scheduling variability, a ±2-week window was applied to define adherent screening (Table 1). Two outcome groups (adherent and nonadherent) were created to enable clinically meaningful regression comparisons to distinguish factors associated with guideline adherence among those evaluated. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the population data across maternal and infant characteristics. Continuous variables were compared using independent samples t-tests, while categorical variables were compared using χ2 tests. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05. All analyses were compared to the adherent group to identify demographic and clinical factors associated with screening nonadherence. Thus, regression analyses included the following comparisons: adherent versus not screened; adherent versus untimely, and adherent versus nonadherent total.

To assess independent associations, a series of binary logistic regression models were constructed for the univariate analysis. Separate models were developed for maternal and infant characteristics for each outcome comparison. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported for all univariate models. Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed separately for maternal and infant factors. The models assessed associations between maternal and infant variables with screening outcomes using adjustedORs (aORs) and P-values. Infant covariates included sex, delivery method, ethnicity, gestational age, and birth weight, while maternal covariates included ADI quartiles, interpreter need, ethnicity, marital status, and insurance type. Collinearity among covariates was assessed, and all variables demonstrated low correlation (|r| < 0.5); therefore, no variables were excluded from the models.

All variables were included in regression models based on a-priori clinical and sociodemographic relevance. ADI scores were categorized into quartiles based on their distribution within the study cohort, consistent with prior studies that stratify area-level socioeconomic indices into quartiles to facilitate relative comparisons and regression analyses [16,17]. Higher ADI quartiles indicate greater socioeconomic disadvantage. ADI was assigned at the patient level using the residential 9-digit zip code recorded in the electronic health record, which was mapped to corresponding census block groups. All analyses were conducted using Python-based statistical software (Python Software Foundation, Version 3.13). The institutional review board approval was obtained.

Results

Maternal and infant characteristics

A total of 343 breech-presenting infants born at the study institution were identified, of whom 254 met the inclusion criteria after excluding those with missing data (n = 83) and VA insurance (n = 6). Of these, 130 (51%) received hip US screening between 4 and 8 weeks of age (adherent); 72 (28%) were screened outside this window (nonadherent: untimely); and 52 (21%) had no screening documented (nonadherent: no screening). In our sample, mothers were predominantly non-Hispanic (85%), married or partnered (74%), and did not need an interpreter (93%). Over half (65%) had commercial insurance. Infants were born at a mean gestational age of 37.8 weeks and mean birth weight of 3.30 kg, assigned female (57%), born via C-section (79%), and non-Hispanic (79%). Maternal and infant univariate analysis

In univariate analysis using adherent screening as the comparison group, mothers insured by Medicaid (P < .0001) and unmarried (P < .0001) were more likely to have infants that underwent no screening at all (Supplementary Table 1). Hispanic infants were more likely to experience untimely screening (P = .0267). Mother’s ethnicity, need for interpreter, infant’s sex, and delivery method were factors that were not found to be associated with screening outcome.

Maternal and infant multivariate analysis

In adjusted pairwise analyses using adherent individuals as the reference group, several maternal predictors were independently associated with screening status. Infants whose mothers were insured by Medicaid had higher odds of no screening (aOR = 0.26, P = .004) as did mothers who were single or unmarried (aOR = 0.36, P = .0184). These maternal predictors were not significantly associated with untimeliness, suggesting that those with Medicaid insurance and unmarried status experience barriers to completion of screening rather than delays in timing. With adherent screening as the reference group, those with the highest neighborhood deprivation (Q4) compared to the lowest (Q1) had a higher odds of nonadherent screening altogether (aOR = 0.49, P = .0383), suggesting that high neighborhood deprivation is associated with barriers to both screening completion and timeliness (Table 3).

Infant characteristics were not associated with screening outcomes at large (Table 3). However, cesarean delivery was associated with slightly higher odds (aOR = 0.53, P = .0456) of nonadherent screening altogether. Additionally, Hispanic infants had a higher odds of undergoing untimely screening (aOR = 0.47, P = .0345) but not lack of screening (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, conducted at a large academic medical center between 2008 and 2023, nearly half (49%) of the breech-born infants did not receive guideline-concordant screening. Importantly, the sociodemographic characteristics of birthing parents differed significantly between infants who received adherent screening and those who did not. Specifically, a mother with unmarried status, insured by Medicaid, or who lived in a higher neighborhood-level deprivation area was independently associated with reduced likelihood of adherent screening (Table 3). It is well established that these factors reflect social disadvantages requiring structural change rather than the need for individual changes in health-related behavior [[18], [19], [20]].

Marital or partnership status was a strong determinant of screening adherence in both univariate and multivariable analyses. Infants whose mothers were single or unmarried were more likely to receive no screening at all (Table 3, P < .0001). This is consistent with prior research showing that children of married-parent families tend to have greater access to care than those in single-parent households [21]. This finding has been shown to be primarily due to differences in socioeconomic status, which is associated with barriers to accessing healthcare, such as increased rurality, less access to transportation, and less job flexibility [14].

Insurance type was also a significant determinant of screening completion. In univariate analysis, infants born to Medicaid-insured parents were 82% less likely to receive any US screening compared with those with commercial insurance (Supplementary Table 1). This association persisted in multivariate analysis results, with Medicaid-insured infants being 74% less likely to be screened (Table 3; P = .004). Notably, insurance type was not associated with screening timeliness. These findings suggest that commercial insurance status facilitates screening completion regardless of timing, whereas Medicaid insurance is primarily associated with failure to receive screening altogether. This observation aligns with recent studies demonstrating that publicly insured children with DDH are older at initial presentation with a higher risk of bracing failure while commercially insured patients have earlier DDH diagnosis with lower rates of surgical intervention [14,22].

Neighborhood-level deprivation also influenced screening outcomes. In univariate analyses, ADI differed significantly between infants who received adherent screening and those who were not screened. Infants residing in the highest ADI quartile were 4.51 times more likely to receive no screening compared with those in the lowest quartile (Supplementary Table 1). In multivariate analyses, ADI remained significantly associated with screening adherence only when comparing the lowest and highest quartiles, with infants in the most deprived neighborhoods having a 51% lower likelihood of receiving screening between 4 and 8 weeks (Table 3; P = .0383). These findings are consistent with prior work demonstrating delayed DDH diagnosis and higher surgical intervention rates among infants from more deprived neighborhoods [14]. More broadly, higher ADI has been associated with reduced access to primary care and fewer opportunities for preventive screening [23]. Together, these results highlight neighborhood disadvantage as a key contributor to inequities in DDH screening and underscore the importance of improving access to primary care and targeted screening initiatives in underserved communities.

In multivariate models, Hispanic infant ethnicity was an independent predictor of screening timeliness. This is consistent with research demonstrating that children belonging to minoritized racial/ethnic groups receive lower quality of care compared to non-Hispanic White children, even when adjusted for sociodemographic factors [24].

A key strength of this study was the use of multiple paired outcome comparisons, which demonstrated that sociodemographic factors had minimal influence on screening timeliness among screened infants but substantially influenced whether screening occurred at all. These findings suggest that quality improvement efforts should prioritize increasing equitable access to screening before focusing on timing optimization.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Generalizability was limited by the single-center design. The retrospective design restricted available variables as certain demographic factors, such as educational attainment, were not captured in the electronic medical record. Use of ICD-10 coding to identify breech births may have resulted in misclassification if coding was inconsistently applied. Additionally, unmeasured confounders, such as transportation access, may have influenced screening outcomes. Furthermore, the inclusion of all patients identified by ICD coding without exclusion of comorbid conditions (e.g., neuromuscular disorders) may introduce clinical heterogeneity that could influence screening practices.

Consistent with prior literature, our findings reinforce the role of sociodemographic factors in shaping access to orthopaedic care [25]. In the context of DDH, disparities in access to ultrasonographic screening warrant the same attention as traditional clinical risk factors. To our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively evaluate the impact of sociodemographic factors on the timeliness and completion of ultrasonographic screening for DDH in breech-born infants. Though clinical outcomes were not included in our analysis, future research should determine how disparities in screening adherence impact rates of operative management. Additionally, research should qualitatively explore the factors driving nonadherence to screening. Lastly, quality improvement (QI) research aimed at improving existing care pathways might include patient-centered interventions, such as implementing patient navigation services for low-income populations and clinician education to improve patient counseling [26,27]. QI research that implements clinician-level changes to care pathways is warranted as well, such as expanding access to clinic-based screening by training advanced practice providers to perform point-of-care US using the Graf method and integrating hard-stop clinical decision support within the electronic health record to address screening gaps [28,29]. Identifying and addressing social barriers through targeted, equity-focused interventions will be essential for improving screening rates, reducing delayed diagnoses, and optimizing long-term outcomes for at-risk infants.
