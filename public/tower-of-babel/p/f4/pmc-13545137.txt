Animal Models for Drug Discovery in Schizophrenia: A Critical View from a Clinical Perspective
Authors: Inga Dammann-Bawadkji, F Markus Leweke, Cathrin Rohleder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSchizophrenia is a complex neuropsychiatric disorder manifesting with diverse positive and negative symptoms as well as cognitive impairments. Current antipsychotics primarily address positive symptoms and frequently cause substantial side effects, highlighting the need for novel therapeutics targeting alternative pathomechanisms. Animal models are widely used to investigate schizophrenia's neurobiology and guide drug discovery, yet their clinical relevance for proof-of-concept (PoC) studies remains controversial. This Current Opinion critically examines the key limitations of animal models in schizophrenia research from a clinical perspective. The disorder’s multifaceted nature, involving genetic, environmental, and neurodevelopmental factors, makes accurate replication in animals challenging. Additionally, core human-specific symptoms, like hallucinations and thought disorders, cannot directly be modeled, although some underlying cross-species constructs can be operationalized. We argue that animal models are most informative when used to test specific, well-defined mechanistic hypotheses and when readouts are anchored to human-relevant biomarkers and neurophysiology, rather than interpreted as proxies for diagnostic categories. Accordingly, translational utility is not uniform: predictive performance depends on the induction paradigm, the construct validity of behavioral and neurophysiological readouts, and the clinical endpoint being modeled. We advocate for close collaboration between preclinical and clinical researchers to refine existing models, establish new and translationally relevant paradigms, and clearly define their interpretive scope. Integrating complementary advanced in vitro and in silico models may enhance mechanistic understanding and help prioritize hypotheses and candidates. Still, these approaches should be viewed as adjuncts, not superior replacements, because they cannot yet capture the circuit- and systems-level dynamics relevant to schizophrenia.

Key Points

Animal studies can shed light on specific brain and behavior changes relevant to schizophrenia, but often, they do not reliably predict which new treatments will actually succeed in clinical trials.There is no single “one-size-fits-all” animal model: usefulness depends on the specific research question and on outcome measures that truly match the symptoms and biological mechanisms implicated in schizophrenia.Translation is most likely to improve by anchoring to more objective, biological markers and by integrating complementary human-based tools, such as patient-derived cell models and computer simulations.Progress is most likely when preclinical and clinical researchers work closely together and when drug development focuses on well-defined patient subgroups and specific symptom dimensions, rather than treating schizophrenia as a single uniform condition.

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a severe neuropsychiatric disorder with heterogeneous symptomatology, including positive symptoms (e.g., perceptual alterations, hallucinations, disorganized speech, thought disorders, delusions), negative symptoms (e.g., diminished emotional expression, social dysfunction and withdrawal, anhedonia), and cognitive dysfunction (e.g., impaired sensory information processing, memory, and attention) [1, 2].

Currently, diagnosis relies on grouping these symptoms into the syndrome ‘schizophrenia’, complicating reliable diagnosis due to overlap with other psychiatric conditions [3]. Young people often present with subthreshold, mixed, nonspecific symptoms shared across psychiatric and medical conditions and do not yet meet traditional diagnostic criteria [4, 5].

This symptom-based diagnostic approach reflects an incomplete mechanistic understanding of underlying pathologies [6], complicating research and hindering the development of objective diagnostics and more effective treatments, in contrast to progress in other fields, e.g., oncology [7, 8].

Animal models are crucial for elucidating neurobiological mechanisms and identifying therapeutic targets, but human-specific symptoms and the etiological and pathophysiological complexity of schizophrenia limit comprehensive modelling and continue to fuel debate about their translational relevance. This Current Opinion examines the potential and limitations of animal models in schizophrenia research, their significance for safety evaluations, and why conventional preclinical psychopharmacology shows limited predictive validity for therapeutic success.

Historical Milestones and Current Challenges of Antipsychotic Drug Discovery

The discovery of antipsychotics like chlorpromazine and haloperidol in the 1950s initiated pharmacological treatment for schizophrenia. These dopamine receptor antagonists alleviated psychotic symptoms but caused frequent side effects [9–11]. In the 1980s, “atypical” antipsychotics such as clozapine were introduced, featuring weaker dopamine D2 receptor and stronger frontal serotonin 5-HT2A receptor antagonism, reducing motor side effects [6, 12–15].

Despite improved tolerability, current antipsychotics still cause numerous side effects, including extrapyramidal symptoms (e.g., akathisia, Parkinsonism), weight gain, sedation, sexual dysfunction, hyperprolactinemia, and metabolic complications [15–17]. Therapeutically, they predominantly improve positive symptoms and inadequately address the full spectrum of schizophrenia [15, 17]. Evidence for robust improvement in primary negative symptoms inherent to schizophrenia remains limited, and apparent benefits likely reflect fewer secondary negative symptoms arising from antipsychotic treatment [6, 12–15]. Notably, 19.8% of people with acute schizophrenia show no improvement after 4–6 weeks of treatment, and 66.9% fail to reach symptomatic remission [18]. These limitations underscore the need for therapeutics acting beyond dopaminergic and serotonergic systems. Recent advances include the xanomeline-trospium chloride combination (approved by U.S. Food and Drug Administration [FDA]) targeting muscarinic M1/M4 receptors, with reported benefits extending to negative and cognitive symptoms [19, 20], highlighting the potential of mechanistically novel therapeutics.

Challenges in Translating Etiological and Patho-mechanistic Insights into Effective Treatments

As Paul and Potter [21] discussed, advances in molecular pharmacology, genetics, genomics, and behavioral pharmacology are expected to guide the development of improved therapeutics.

Despite advances in elucidating disease etiologies and pathophysiology, a lack of comprehensive understanding of schizophrenia remains [3, 22]. The precise mechanisms underlying its complex symptoms are unclear, and current evidence supports a multifactorial etiology involving interactions between genetic, environmental, and neurodevelopmental factors [22–24]. Large-scale genetic studies implicate both common low-effect variants (e.g. single nucleotide polymorphisms) and rare, higher-impact variants (including copy-number variants [CNV]), with substantial pleiotropy across psychiatric diagnoses, consistent with marked clinical heterogeneity. Environmental risk factors include obstetric complications, infections, birth season, migration, urbanicity, childhood adversity, and cannabis use. Gene-environment interplay is increasingly examined using genome-wide approaches (e.g., polygenic risk scores) rather than underpowered candidate-gene studies [6, 25].

Several factors hinder the development of effective non-dopaminergic treatments for schizophrenia, including persistent gaps in mechanistic knowledge, the disorder’s multifactorial risk architecture, and symptom-based diagnosis in the absence of diagnostic biomarkers and adequate patient stratification [21, 26]. Together, these challenges contribute to the limited translation of preclinical leads into clinically successful compounds.

To clarify where translation can fail, we distinguish disease-induction paradigms (animal models) modeling etiological or pathophysiological mechanisms, phenotyping readouts capturing behavioral, cognitive, or neurophysiological dysfunctions, pharmacological challenge screening assays for compound triage, and regulatory preclinical domains (toxicology, safety pharmacology, pharmacodynamics [PD], pharmacokinetics [PK]). Conflating these categories can obscure whether failures reflect the model, the chosen readout/assay, or misalignment with the clinical endpoint.

These limitations are widely recognized: “Schizophrenia is an exclusively human disorder that is highly heterogeneous […]; therefore, it is highly unlikely that an animal model meeting all criteria […] will ever be established” [26]. Other researchers emphasize that it is “a misconception that a model must recapitulate all or most features of the disease” and that it is “best to talk about models ‘for’ disease research, rather than ‘of’ a disease” [30]. Accordingly, animal models and readouts remain valuable for well-defined mechanistic questions and understanding of molecular, cellular, circuit, and behavioral underpinnings, while Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion (ADME) assays (alongside bacterial/cellular toxicology), animal toxicology, and PD studies across species are crucial for safety assessments and estimating human PK properties [31].

Several preclinically effective compounds have failed in clinical trials [32, 33], whereas other discarded preclinical candidates may still benefit patients [34]. For example, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, i.e., antidepressants, became first-line anxiolytics. Notably, their common preclinical anxiogenic effects mirror clinical experience, where an initial anxiety increase can precede anxiolysis with continued treatment, illustrating the need to align endpoints and treatment duration between preclinical and intended clinical paradigms [35]. However, discordance between preclinical and late-stage clinical trials cannot be attributed solely to model limitations and may also reflect limitations in trial design, population heterogeneity, and treatment history, underscoring the importance of patient stratification [26, 34, 36]. Overall, predictive accuracy from psychiatric animal models and phenotyping readouts remains questionable, as preclinical/clinical measures must be reliable and disorder-relevant [21, 37]. Future advances may enable more rigorous validation of paradigms and readouts that better predict treatment efficacy through new biomarkers.

Current Spectrum of Animal Models for Schizophrenia

Given these challenges, the evaluation of animal models has been guided by validation frameworks developed in the preclinical literature. McKinney and Bunney [38] were the first to propose explicit ground rules for evaluating animal models, arguing that a model should resemble the human condition in its etiology, biochemistry, symptomatology, and treatment response. Building on this framework, Willner [39, 40] later distinguished three complementary forms of validity listed below, which remain widely used in the assessment of animal models and their readouts. In general, disease models should reflect plausible human risk and causal factors and/or display neural or behavioral pathology resembling the human condition [36]. High-quality models and readouts must therefore demonstrate the following types of validity [36, 39–44]:

None of these criteria can be fully achieved for schizophrenia. However, current paradigms are not uniformly inadequate, as their validity depends on the specific induction method, treatment regimen, and readout employed. Validity is therefore domain- and research question-specific, and mechanistically grounded models can achieve meaningful face and/or construct validity within a defined scope. It is also important to distinguish preclinical paradigms according to both their intended function and their conceptual basis. Some procedures, particularly early dopamine challenge paradigms, were developed primarily as compound-screening assays in antipsychotic discovery rather than as disease models. For example, apomorphine-induced climbing and related dopamine challenges were designed to identify dopaminergic antagonists, but they do not model schizophrenia pathophysiology [21, 36, 45]. By contrast, disease-induction paradigms aim to capture etiological or pathophysiological mechanisms, whereas phenotyping readouts assess specific behavioral, cognitive, or neurophysiological dysfunctions. Accordingly, translational evaluation requires clear separation of disease-induction paradigms, screening assays, and phenotyping readouts.

Predictive validity is particularly contentious for traditional behavioral screens. Many assays were developed for compound triage rather than mechanistic disease modelling [36, 43], thereby preferentially identifying drugs resembling reference compounds, biasing discovery towards “me-too” mechanisms [21, 37], and limiting identification of alternative molecular pathway agents [21, 36, 46]. This is compounded by the difficulty of modeling positive symptoms, which dominate current antipsychotic targets, and the absence of gold-standard references for assessing treatments of negative or cognitive symptoms [44]. Consequently, non-responsiveness to D2-receptor antagonists does not necessarily indicate “poor” models but may reflect non-dopaminergic mechanisms and symptom domains that current antipsychotics cannot adequately address. Accordingly, predictive performance should be interpreted relative to intended mechanisms and clinical endpoints, rather than antipsychotic-like activity alone. Translational “failures” can also reflect mismatched or legacy phenotyping readouts rather than the induction paradigm itself. For example, sucrose preference, developed for depression-related consummatory anhedonia, has been applied to schizophrenia under cross-disorder symptom equivalence assumptions [47, 48], despite evidence suggesting anticipatory and reinforcement-learning deficits rather than reduced hedonic capacity [49]. Alternatives with better construct validity for negative symptom domains include effort-based decision making, progressive ratio, implicit reward learning, and affective or cognitive-bias paradigms for diminished expression, while cognition can be assessed with cross-species working memory tasks, cognitive flexibility, and reinforcement learning [50–54]. Apparent “predictive failures” are also shaped by clinical trial design and treatment history. Many multicenter trials enroll chronically treated individuals after brief washout phases, and chronic D2-receptor blockade can induce lasting neuroadaptations altering subsequent drug responsivity—factors rarely incorporated into acute preclinical testing paradigms [55, 56].

Face validity measures the reflection of target phenomena and is primarily assessed using behavioral paradigms, e.g., hyperlocomotion, social withdrawal, cognitive deficits, or impaired sensory-motor gating [57, 58]. Because schizophrenia is complex and partly human-specific, models can only capture isolated symptoms; consequently, face validity “has been overestimated considerably” [57]. Face validity can be strengthened by incorporating neurophysiological and circuit-level phenotypes (e.g., hippocampal hyperactivity, altered dopamine-neuron responsivity, parvalbumin-interneuron dysfunction) that directly anchor to human biomarkers [59–61]. Neurophysiological readouts, e.g., gamma-band oscillations, auditory steady-state responses, and mismatch negativity, are powerful cross-species markers because their core properties are conserved across mammals [62–64]. Progress may therefore come from biomarkers and an expanded definition of face validity beyond symptom-level measures [43].

Construct validity assesses mechanistic similarities between a disease and its model and is theory-driven [42, 43, 46]. Ideally, it replicates human etiology, reproducing neural or behavioral features [36], but constraining factors include complexity, gene-environment interactions, and incomplete neurobiological understanding [44], varying across patient subgroups [21]. Construct validity can be strengthened by multi-hit paradigms reflecting gene-environment interactions [65, 66]. Several neurodevelopmental, genetic, and chronic pharmacological paradigms capture multiple symptom-relevant domains. Without a fully representative model, meaningful interpretation requires fit-for-purpose scoping: stating the symptom-relevant domain(s) and mechanistic construct(s) and limiting translational claims to that scope.

Pharmacological Models

Pharmacological models comprise a heterogeneous group of paradigms based on perturbation of neurotransmitter systems implicated in schizophrenia, and their translational relevance depends strongly on the specific manipulation, regimen, and readouts used [46]. Early paradigms were developed as compound-screening assays and used dopaminergic system stimulation (e.g., amphetamine, apomorphine) [68, 71], inducing stereotyped behaviors, disruptions in sensory-motor gating, selective attention, latent inhibition, and hyperlocomotion [71, 72]. Notably, hyperlocomotion primarily reflects psychomotor activation driven by increased dopamine transmission in limbic or ventral striatal circuits and, despite sometimes being presented as a “positive-symptom-like” behavior, is not a good proxy for positive symptoms linked to associative striatal hyperdopaminergia in humans [73–75].

Glutamatergic drug-induced models use N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonists (e.g., phencyclidine [PCP], ketamine, MK-801), which induce hyperlocomotion and sensory-motor gating deficits [58, 67, 68, 76]. Translational relevance is regimen-dependent: sub-chronic/chronic PCP administration can produce behavioral, neurochemical, and functional-network alterations better aligned with schizophrenia-related findings, whereas acute NMDA antagonism can yield nonspecific effects and oscillatory changes diverging from clinical abnormalities [76–78].

Serotonergic psychedelic paradigms (e.g., LSD, mescaline) can disrupt sensory -motor gating and alter locomotor and exploratory behavior [79]. However, acute psychedelic exposure is best viewed as drug-induced psychosis-like state modeling with limited etiological construct validity for schizophrenia.

Overall, pharmacological models should not be treated as a uniform class. Acute dopaminergic challenge paradigms reproduce a relatively narrow set of phenotypes and retain their main value as screening assays anchored to D2-receptor antagonism. By contrast, translational relevance within the glutamatergic models is regimen-dependent, with sub-chronic/chronic NMDA-antagonist (e.g., PCP) paradigms capturing a broader range of behavioral, neurochemical, and network-level abnormalities relevant to schizophrenia than acute NMDA antagonism [80, 81]. Predictive validity is therefore paradigm-, regimen-, and endpoint-specific and should not be generalized across the pharmacological category, particularly for negative- or cognitive-domain therapeutics or mechanistically novel targets. By modeling neurotransmitter dysregulations implicated in schizophrenia [82–84], these paradigms can offer construct validity but typically capture acute symptoms rather than schizophrenia’s chronic and progressive course. Given that 66.9% of patients do not reach symptomatic remission despite antipsychotic treatment [18], dopaminergic imbalances may represent a final common pathway rather than a primary cause in all cases. Hence, acute dopaminergic challenge paradigms are no longer regarded as heuristic [41] and should be distinguished from (sub)chronic NMDA hypofunction and other mechanistically richer pharmacological approaches. Their interpretive scope should be restricted to the constructs or endpoints they were designed to assess.

Neurodevelopmental Models

The neurodevelopmental hypothesis proposes that schizophrenia symptoms arise from early neurodevelopmental abnormalities, including prenatal/perinatal insults, occurring well before symptom onset [85, 86]. These “etiological models” do not directly manipulate neurotransmitter systems but aim to reproduce putative causes and downstream mechanisms [41].

Neurodevelopmental models include neonatal hippocampal lesions, prenatal methylazoxymethanol (MAM) exposure, maternal immune activation (MIA), neonatal immune challenges, and other prenatal/neonatal stressors (e.g., restraint stress, maternal separation, maternal malnutrition, obstetric complications). By perturbing development, these paradigms lead to post-pubertal phenotypes relevant to schizophrenia [44, 46, 59, 87–90], including neurochemical disruptions (e.g., dopaminergic dysregulation), neuroanatomical alterations (e.g., cortical atrophy, enlarged ventricles, neuronal disorganization), and behavioral alterations (e.g., social withdrawal, disrupted sensory-motor gating, cognitive deficits), providing reasonable face and construct validity [44, 59, 87–90]. Construct validity is strongest where manipulations map onto established early-life risk factors implicated in schizophrenia [91]. Antipsychotic responsiveness is reported but varies across models, regimens, and readouts, consistent with partial and endpoint-dependent predictive value [60, 68, 92–94]. Beyond behavior, many neurodevelopmental models also recapitulate translational neurophysiological phenotypes (e.g., altered oscillatory activity and excitation/inhibition balance markers, including interneuron-related alterations), providing additional cross-species anchors [95, 96].

Schizophrenia reflects gene-environmental interactions throughout life and early life impacts [23, 97]. ‘Dual/multi-hit’ models combine factors (e.g., perinatal maternal infection plus adolescent stress) [66] and frequently span induction categories (e.g., genetic susceptibility plus environmental insults) to better approximate interacting schizophrenia risk architectures and gene-environmental interplay [65, 66].

Limitations include non-specific brain damage (lesion models) and confounds from drug/agent exposure or surgical interventions [61]. Prenatal manipulations such as MAM/MIA can increase fetal loss or morbidity; however, when established protocols are followed, experienced investigators typically report survival and complication rates comparable to other prenatal or early-life experimental interventions [87, 88].

Environmental Models

Environmental (‘etiological’) models are based on human risk factors for schizophrenia that closely overlap with neurodevelopmental models, since maternal stress, infection, and adolescent risk exposures all influence brain development. Factors like high-potency cannabis use, severe infections or immune activation, and psychosocial stress [3, 98–101] have been reproduced in animals [102–110]. Because they derive from human-identified risk factors, these models possess comparatively strong construct validity and produce schizophrenia-like phenotypes (e.g., hyperactivity [105], anhedonia [110], social deficits [103], impaired sensory-motor gating [104, 109], cognitive deficits [102, 103, 105, 109], hyperdopaminergia [108]), supporting good face validity. Some readouts also show antipsychotic sensitivity, reflecting partial and endpoint-dependent predictive validity [103, 109].

Their main limitation is that they often operationalize single risk factors, thereby missing the disease’s multifactorial complexity. Cannabis exposure illustrates this well, being neither necessary nor sufficient for schizophrenia, and likely depending on interactions with pre-existing vulnerabilities [111, 112]. Accordingly, dual-/multi-hit approaches combining environmental risks with genetic and/or developmental vulnerability may provide more mechanistically grounded paradigms than single-factor designs [113, 114].

Genetic Models

Schizophrenia is multifactorial but highly heritable (~ 80%) and genetically complex, involving common and rare genetic variants alongside environmental factors [3, 22, 115, 116]. Genetic liability does not necessarily imply familial illness: many people with schizophrenia report no clear family history, and environmental exposures (e.g., obstetric complications, childhood adversity or trauma, psychosocial stress, infections, cannabis [6, 25]) contribute to risk and interact with genetic vulnerability [23, 117].

Genetic models manipulate schizophrenia-associated genes to study downstream molecular, circuit, and behavioral consequences. Earlier work focused on candidate genes, e.g., disrupted-in-schizophrenia-1 (DISC1), neuregulin-1 (NRG1) and its receptor ERBB4, and dystrobrevin-binding protein 1 (DTNBP1) [44, 67, 118–120]. Reflecting contemporary human genetics, genetic modeling has shifted towards CNVs and ultra-rare coding-variant (URV) risk architectures identified by large-scale sequencing, including 22q11.2 deletion and SETD1A haploinsufficiency, alongside additional risk genes [121–123]. These models exhibit schizophrenia-like behavioral and cognitive deficits, supporting face validity within a defined scope, and offer comparatively strong construct validity by targeting disease-linked mutations/variations. Many respond to antipsychotic drugs, although effects vary with genetic background and readout, limiting predictive generalization [124–126].

A key limitation is that single-gene manipulations cannot capture schizophrenia’s polygenic architecture or gene-environment interactions [3, 115, 116]. Even with strong construct validity, phenotypes vary across background strains, thereby reducing face validity [41]. Accordingly, combining genetic models with environmental or developmental insults (dual-/multi-hit) can improve etiological relevance and support mechanistic subtyping, rather than expecting a single mutation to reproduce the full clinical syndrome.

Discussion

Animal models have greatly enhanced our understanding of neurobiological mechanisms by allowing targeted manipulations and examination of molecular, physiological, and behavioral effects. However, model-specific limitations emphasize the need for a comprehensive integrative preclinical strategy reflecting schizophrenia’s complexity and heterogeneity. Although animal models cannot replicate human-specific symptoms and capture only selected components, they remain valuable for defined mechanistic questions, target identification, and safety assessments. Nonetheless, their use for PoC studies remains constrained, even when face or construct validity is reasonable.

Therefore, each in vitro assay, in vivo induction paradigm, and phenotyping readout must be selected and interpreted within a defined scope for drug discovery and decision making [37]. Continued refinement and close collaboration between preclinical and clinical experts (e.g., biochemists, neurobiologists, clinical pharmacologists, psychiatrists) is crucial for improving clinical alignment [34, 37]. Integrating clinical insights with technological advances may eventually yield objective markers, complementing behavioral and symptomatic assessments. Cross-species neurophysiological biomarkers (e.g., oscillatory and event-related potential phenotypes) can provide objective anchors for aligning preclinical and clinical studies alongside behavioral outcomes [63, 64].

Translational limitations are bidirectional: symptom-based diagnoses, heterogeneous clinical population (e.g., dominant pathophysiology, illness duration, treatment histories), and endpoints distal to pathophysiology can hinder target discovery and bias clinical programs toward existing hypotheses. This underscores the value of integrating biomarker-informed clinical stratification with mechanistically anchored models [26]. Consistent with mechanistic schizophrenia subtypes, both clinical trials and preclinical research may benefit from portfolios of models, readouts, and endpoints tailored to specific pathways rather than reliance on a single universal animal model.

Human-based in vitro systems, including isolated cells, tissues, and organoids, are increasingly used to study drug-target and pathway effects under controlled conditions. Relevant platforms include patient-derived neuronal cell cultures, glia-containing co-cultures, and 3D-brain organoids [130, 131]. From a clinical drug development perspective, such systems can complement in vivo work by enabling mechanistic testing of candidate compounds and strengthening primary PD investigations of their ability to modify disease-relevant mechanisms and symptom domains—objectives that current animal models address only partially.

However, these systems are unlikely to recapitulate schizophrenia’s whole-brain circuit dynamics, developmental timing, and experience-dependent plasticity. They are therefore best interpreted as mechanistic, hypothesis-generating tools rather than standalone PoC readouts. Conversely, evaluating mechanistically novel antipsychotics exclusively in healthy cell systems can yield false negatives when disease-relevant perturbations or biomarker-defined states are required. Patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) systems (e.g., 2D-neuronal cultures, glia-containing co-cultures, brain organoids) provide complementary access to human genetic backgrounds, cell-type–specific phenotypes, and molecular pathways [132–135], enabling targeted perturbations (including gene editing) and scalable pharmacology, especially when linked to clinically measurable biomarkers. Nonetheless, limitations in maturation, circuit integration, and environmental context reinforce their role as complements, rather than replacements, for in vivo models.

In silico models employ computational simulations and modeling to forecast PD/PK behavior, enabling hypothesis generation, virtual screening, and lead optimization. Examples include molecular docking, quantitative structure-activity relationship, and systems biology modeling using omics data [136–139]. Beyond PD/PK simulations, computational psychiatry and mechanistic modeling can formalize psychosis-relevant constructs (e.g., belief updating under uncertainty, reinforcement learning, precision-weighted prediction errors), generating quantitative predictions testable in animals and humans, thereby supporting bidirectional translation and patient stratification [140, 141]. These approaches are particularly powerful for virtual screening, lead optimization, and testing mechanistic hypotheses, but outputs are constrained by the quality and scope of available datasets. Therefore, they more often refine and prioritize hypotheses than discover wholly novel mechanisms without supporting biology.

This perspective does not oppose the use of animal models: these models remain essential for understanding the onset, development, and manifestation of disorders like schizophrenia and for assessing circuit- and systems-level consequences and safety. Rather, preclinical PoC should be mechanism-led and not rely on any single platform. In vitro/in silico methods can reduce animal use and costs and improve efficiency through high-throughput screening and the incorporation of patient-specific data. Their greatest PoC value typically arises when linked to clinically validated biomarkers and paired with in vivo studies. A practical integration workflow is to (i) start from human genetics, longitudinal phenotyping, and biomarker-defined subgroups; (ii) test mechanisms in patient-derived in vitro systems (including perturbation/rescue); (iii) use in silico models to optimize and de-risk PK/PD and safety assumptions and prioritize compounds; and (iv) confirm target engagement and systems-level effects in carefully chosen in vivo paradigms, iterating as clinical data accrue.

Clinical trial designs must also evolve. Schizophrenia is heterogeneous, with subgroups defined by different neurobiological substrates [21, 142–145]. Transdiagnostic approaches aim to classify individuals by underlying pathological pathways to enable more personalized interventions [4, 146, 147]. Treatment history additionally confounds translation as chronic D2-receptor antagonism can induce durable adaptations in relevant circuits. In developmental models (e.g., MAM), prior first-generation antipsychotic exposure can blunt responses to non-dopaminergic interventions, suggesting some late-stage “failures” reflect population or design effects rather than lack of pharmacological potential [55]. Stratifying patients by dominant pathomechanisms may improve outcomes, but biomarkers remain a barrier [148]. Animal models can contribute to biomarker development, reinforcing the value of interdisciplinary, cross-platform research. Sometimes, stratification is already feasible, e.g., laboratory markers indicating immune dysregulation have guided the identification of a patient subset that is more likely to benefit from immunotherapies [149], enhancing therapeutic outcomes and avoiding ineffective treatments.

Conclusion

Despite advances in understanding schizophrenia’s neurobiology and in refining preclinical paradigms, predicting therapeutic success remains challenging. Animal models provide important mechanistic insights but cannot capture schizophrenia’s multifactorial nature or human-specific symptoms. Incomplete mechanistic knowledge, a lack of diagnostic biomarkers, and reliance on antipsychotics that predominantly target positive symptoms further constrain translation. Nonetheless, reproducible circuit- and neurophysiological abnormalities can support model refinement, target engagement assessment, and bidirectional cross-species translation. Predictive validity should be considered paradigm-, regimen-, readout-, and endpoint-specific, requiring alignment of the specific model implementation with the clinical question. This applies particularly to pharmacological paradigms, where translational claims should be restricted to the specific manipulation, treatment regimen, readouts, and symptom-relevant or mechanistic construct under study.

Progress will depend on continued mechanism-led research, close preclinical-clinical collaboration, and integration of biomarker-based strategies. Complementary in vitro/in silico methods can reduce time, cost, and ethical concerns and de-risk PK/PD and safety assumptions but remain limited by incomplete capture of whole-brain circuit dynamics and dependence on existing datasets. The PoC decisions are strongest when convergent evidence across platforms supports a biomarker-linked mechanism in appropriately selected patient subgroups. Accordingly, portfolios of mechanistically grounded in vivo paradigms and construct-valid endpoints, spanning behavioral and neurophysiological readouts, are needed, alongside clinical trial designs that account for disease heterogeneity and, where feasible, stratify participants by dominant pathomechanisms.
