Genomic analysis of Vibrio cholerae in Western Kenya (2015–2023) reveals persistence of pandemic ST69 and presence of the European-associated ST8 lineage
Authors: Erick C Kipkirui, Vanessa Natasha, Collins Kigen, James W Muturi, Ronald K Kirera, Margaret C Koech, Mary C Kirui, Nancy C Kipkemoi, Janet N Ndonye, Sheilla Jerotich, Luis Pow Sang, Lillian Musila, Elizabeth A Odundo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCholera remains a significant public health issue in Kenya, primarily driven by recurrent outbreaks associated with Vibrio cholerae O1 sequence type 69 (ST 69). Recently, non-O1/non-O139 strains have emerged and are associated with sporadic cases; however limited genomic data hinder our understanding of strain diversity, virulence, and antimicrobial resistance (AMR). This study characterized the genetic diversity, virulence, and AMR profiles of V. cholerae isolated in Western Kenya between 2015 and 2023 through an enteric pathogen surveillance study.MethodsTwenty-four V. cholerae isolates from diarrheal patients aged 2 to 77 years were analyzed. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using E-test strips for azithromycin, tetracycline, ciprofloxacin, chloramphenicol, and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, interpreted according to CLSI M45 guidelines. Hybrid whole genome sequencing (Illumina and Oxford Nanopore) was used to characterize sequence types, virulence determinants, AMR genes, mobile genetic elements, and phylogenetic relationships. The Kenyan isolates were contextualized within a global phylogeny of publicly available genomes.ResultsTwo sequence types (STs) were identified: pandemic O1/ST69 (15/24, 62.5%) and non-O1/non-O139 ST8 (9/24, 37.5%). All ST69 isolates were susceptible to ciprofloxacin, tetracycline, and chloramphenicol. However, 10/15; 66.7% were resistant to azithromycin and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole. All azithromycin-resistant isolates were isolated in 2023. In contrast, all ST8 were 100% susceptible to all antibiotics tested. ST69 isolates carried classical virulence determinants, including ctxAB, seventh pandemic-associated pathogenicity islands, and consistently harbored fluoroquinolone resistance mutations (gyrA S83I and parC S85L). Notably, all ST69 isolates from 2023 possessed a conserved IncC plasmid encoding multiple AMR genes, including blaPER−7, sul1, aadA2, and macrolide resistance determinants, explaining the observed multidrug resistance. Conversely, ST8 isolates lacked cholera toxin genes, carried fewer virulence and resistance determinants, and were detected only in 2019 at a single site. Phylogenomic analysis placed ST69 isolates within the seventh pandemic wave 3, contained in the 13-transmision event (AFR13), were closely related to African and Asian strains. In contrast, ST8 isolates were contained in the third transmission event and clustered with older European and South American lineages.ConclusionOur findings suggest persistent endemic circulation of pandemic ST69 in western Kenya alongside sporadic non-pandemic ST8 lineages. The recent detection of plasmid-mediated multidrug resistance among ST69 highlights ongoing pathogen evolution and underscores the need for sustained genomic surveillance to guide cholera control and antimicrobial stewardship.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12997-0.

Background

Vibrio cholerae is a Gram-negative, facultative anaerobic, curved rod-shaped bacterium that causes cholera, an acute diarrheal disease characterized by profuse watery diarrhea that can quickly lead to severe dehydration and death if not treated [1]. Cholera is primarily transmitted through consumption of contaminated food and water [2] with outbreaks frequently occurring after natural disasters, conflict, and population displacement [3]. V. cholerae naturally inhabits aquatic, brackish, and estuarine environments often associated with planktons or forming part of biofilm communities [4].

Cholera remains a significant global public health burden, with the WHO estimating between 3 and 4 million cases and approximately 100,000 deaths annually [5], many of which are likely underreported in low-resource settings [6]. The pathogenicity of V. cholerae is largely dependent on two factors: the toxin coregulated pilus, which facilitates intestinal attachment, and the cholera toxin, which disrupts electrolyte balance that leads to severe secretory diarrhea. Cholera toxin is encoded by the ctxAB genes, which are carried on the CTX Φ bacteriophage [1, 6]. These virulence determinants are typically located within specific genomic regions, including Vibrio Pathogenicity Islands (VPI-1 and VPI-2) and Seventh Pandemic Islands (VSP-1 and VSP-2), which distinguish pandemic strains from non-pandemic lineages [6].

Since the 19th century, cholera has caused seven pandemics, with the ongoing seventh pandemic being driven by the El Tor biotype of the O1 serogroup [1]. More than 200 V. cholerae serogroups have been identified based on the variations in the O-antigen of the cell surface lipopolysaccharide. However, only two: O1 and O139 are responsible for epidemic and pandemic cholera [3, 7]. The O1 serogroup, specifically the classical biotype, was responsible for the first six cholera pandemics between 1817 and 1923, whereas the seventh pandemic, which began in 1961, is caused by the El Tor biotype of O1. While O1 strains are globally distributed, O139 strains have remained largely confined to Asia [8]. Although pandemic O1 and O139 strains are responsible for most major outbreaks, non-O1/non-O139 V. cholerae (NOVC) are increasingly recognized as emerging human pathogens [9]. These strains typically lack the major virulence determinants associated with pandemic cholera, such as cholera toxin genes ctxAB or toxin co-regulated pilus. However, they harbor alternative virulence factors for example, hemolysins and specialized secretion systems, which enable host colonization and may contribute to gastroenteritis, particularly in people with weakened immune systems [10, 11].

Cholera is generally self-limiting, and mild to moderate cases can be effectively managed with oral or intravenous rehydration fluids to prevent dehydration [1]. In severe cases, antibiotic treatment is recommended to shorten the duration of illness, reduce stool volume, and length of bacterial shedding. Commonly used antibiotics include doxycycline and ciprofloxacin [12]. Doxycycline is recommended as first-line therapy for both adults and children in many countries, while azithromycin or ciprofloxacin are used as alternatives in settings where doxycycline resistance has been reported [13]. However, the emergence of antimicrobial resistance driven by chromosomal mutations or the acquisition of mobile genetic elements such as plasmids, transposons, integrons, and integrative conjugative elements continues to complicate treatment strategies [12, 14].

Africa bears the highest global burden of cholera, accounting for approximately 60% of annual cholera-related deaths worldwide, with the majority occurring in sub-Saharan Africa [15]. Several countries, including the Democratic Republic of Congo, Sudan, Ethiopia, Mozambique, and Zimbabwe, have reported recent outbreaks characterized by high case numbers and mortality [16]. In Kenya, cholera is endemic [17], with recurrent outbreaks reported since the first major outbreak in Turkana in 1971. Subsequent outbreaks occurred during 1997–1999, 2007–2010, 2015–2016 [18] and more recently between 2022 and 2024 [19]. During the 2015 outbreak in western Kenya, investigation identified key drivers of transmission, including poor sanitation, unsafe water sources, and inadequate hygiene practices [20]. Recent epidemiological studies underscore the substantial burden of cholera in Kenya. A spatio-temporal analysis across high risks areas documented 7.316 cholera cases across 22 (69%) sub counties between 2013 and 2022, with the highest case counts in 2015, 2016 and 2022 [21]. National analysis indicates that between 2015 and 2020, 32 of 47 counties reported a total of 30,431 suspected cholera cases [22]), and hotspot mapping for multisectoral interventions highlights that cholera continues to affect large portions of the Kenyan population [23]). Collectively, these outbreaks have contributed to cholera being designated as a priority notifiable disease under Kenya’s Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response strategy [19].

During recent cholera outbreaks in Kenya, particularly those occurring between 2022 and 2023, notable shifts in antimicrobial resistance patterns have been observed, suggesting the emergence of new V. cholerae strains [12]. A study investigating isolates from the 2007 to 2010 epidemic reported high levels of resistance to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (94.6%), streptomycin (92.8%), and nalidixic acid (64.5%), while lower resistance rates were observed against ampicillin (3.6%), amoxicillin (4.2%), chloramphenicol (3.0%), and doxycycline (1.8%). In contrast, isolates from the 2015–2016 epidemic were largely susceptible to most antibiotics, with resistance observed against nalidixic acid. These findings indicate the replacement of earlier multi-resistant strains with new lineages exhibiting distinct resistance phenotypes following the 2007–2010 epidemic [17].

The advent of whole-genome sequencing has substantially enhanced our understanding of the evolution, genetic diversity, and transmission dynamics of V. cholerae. Whole genome sequencing has revealed that pandemic lineages can persist locally over multiple outbreaks, while other lineages can be introduced from other regions. This highlights the importance of genomic surveillance to understand strain persistence, track transmission, lineage evolution and antimicrobial resistance [3]. Until recently, in countries like Kenya, the application of WGS was limited due to cost and infrastructure constraints, leaving gaps in our understanding regarding the genomic characteristics of endemic V. cholerae strains. This study aimed to address these gaps by analysing the genetic diversity, virulence determinants, and antimicrobial resistance profiles of V. cholerae strains collected in Western Kenya between 2015 and 2023. In addition, we compared with global isolates to understand their evolutionary and epidemiological patterns.

Methods

Bacterial isolates

Between 2015 and 2023, a total of 1,045 stool samples from patients with acute diarrhea were collected and examined at three sites in Western Kenya, two is Kisumu County and one in Uasin Gishu County. From these, 24 V. cholerae isolates were identified and included analyzed in this study. Participants were enrolled in an ongoing hospital-based surveillance study titled “Surveillance of Enteric Pathogens Causing Diarrheal Illness in Kenya” (WRAIR # 1549; KEMRI SSC#1549). The study was implemented across multiple referral and county government health facilities in Kenya and enrolled adults and children with acute diarrhoea (symptoms within 14 days). Participants were recruited from eight facilities across seven counties (Kisii, Kisumu, Busia, Kericho, Uasin Gishu, Nairobi, and Lamu). Stool samples were shipped to Microbiology Hub Kericho for identification of diarrheal etiologies. The study employed continuous year-round surveillance, enabling detection of sporadic cases as well as small or unreported outbreaks.

Species identification and phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The V. cholerae isolates were initially cultured on thiosulfate citrate bile salts sucrose (TCBS) agar for selective isolation. Presumptive colonies were further identified using standard biochemical tests including oxidase testing and Gram staining. Species identification was done on automated systems: the MicroScan Walkaway/AutoScan-4 (Dade MicroScan, Inc., Sacramento, CA), or MALDI-TOF MS spectrometer (Microflex, Bruker Daltonics), or BD Phoenix (BD Diagnostic Systems, Sparks, MD) platform, depending on system availability at the time of isolate processing.

Phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed for azithromycin, tetracycline, ciprofloxacin, chloramphenicol, and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole using E-test strips (BioMerieux, France). Briefly, a standardized bacterial suspension equivalent to 0.5 McFarland was inoculated onto Mueller Hinton agar plates, after which E-test strips were placed on the surface on the media. Plates were incubated at 35–37℃ for 16–18 h. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were read at the point where inhibition ellipse intersected on the strip scale. Results were interpreted in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standard Institute guidelines (CLSI M45, 3rd ed., 2018) [24]. V. cholerae isolates were stocked at -80 °C for long term preservation.

DNA extraction

The frozen archived isolates were cultured on sheep blood agar and incubated overnight at 37℃ aerobically. Bacterial culture was suspended in 200 µl of nuclease-free water and genomic DNA was extracted using the Quick-DNA Fungal/Bacterial Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA purity check and concentration were assessed using the Nanodrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and the Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Themo Fisher Scientific), respectively.

Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS)

For long read sequencing, libraries were prepared using the NEBNext reagents (New England Biolabs) Native Barcoding Kit, SQK-NBD114.24 (Oxford Nanopore, Oxford, UK) following the manufacturer’s instructions and sequencing was performed on a MinION flow cell (R10.4.1) using the MinION Mk1B device. Short-read sequencing, libraries were prepared using 100–500 ng of DNA with the Illumina DNA Prep Library Preparation Kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions and sequencing was performed on the Illumina NextSeq 1000 system, generating 2 × 150 bp reads using the P1 (300) cycle reagent cartridge (Illumina, San Diego, CA).

Genomic analysis

For short reads, raw read quality was assessed using FastQC (v0.12.1) [25]. Reads were then trimmed using the BBduk (v39.09) tools, followed by a quality check to assess the trimmed reads. For long reads, base calling, trimming, and filtering to remove low- quality reads (Phred quality score Qscore < Q8) were performed using the Dorado base caller. De novo assembly from long reads was performed using Flye v2.9.6 [26]. The resulting assemblies were polished with Illumina short reads using Polypolish v 0.6.1 [27] and assembly quality was evaluated using QUAST (v5.2.0) [28]. Species identification was performed using Mash (v2.3) [29] and FASTANI (v1.34) [30]. Species identification was performed using Mash for rapid genome distance estimation followed by FastANI to calculate pairwise average nucleotide identity, with ≥ 95% ANI used as the species identification threshold. This genome wide approach provides robust species identification without relying on a single gene such as ompW. Multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) was conducted with MLST (v2.23.0) [31]. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes were screened using ABRITAMR (v1.0.19) [32], while virulence genes were screened using ABRicate (v1.0.1) [33] against the NCBI database. Serogroups, virulence factors, pathogenicity islands, wave and pandemic strains were identified using CholeraFinder (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/CholeraeFinder/). This tool predicted the serogroup based on the presence of serogroup specific gene clusters. Isolates containing the rfbV-O1 gene were classified as serogroup O1, those containing wbfZ-O139 as O139 and isolates lacking both markers were classified as non-O1/non-O139. Biotyping was performed using CholeraFinder, which identifies lineage-specific allelic variants of key virulence and phage-associated genes (including ctxB, tcpA, and rstR) to distinguish Classical and El Tor Vibrio cholerae strains.

Lineage determination was performed with Pathogen Watch (https://pathogen.watch). Plasmid typing was performed using ABRicate against the PlasmidFinder database.

Phylogenetic analysis was constructed using 1582 global strains of V. cholerae downloaded from Pathogenwatch (supplemental 1) with representative genomes from 5 continents (Africa = 405, Asia = 458, Europe = 202, North America = 89, and South America = 428) and the 24 isolates sequenced from the study. Core genome phylogeny was constructed using Snippy (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy) to build the core alignment file between each genome and the reference genome V. cholerae NI6961. Recombination events were removed using Gubbins [34], and the final phylogenetic tree was constructed using RAxML-NG with the GTR model with 1,000 bootstraps [35]. Visualization and tree annotations were done using iTOL (v6) [36].

Plasmid genome annotation and visualization were done using PGAP (v 4.7) [37] and SnapGene (v 8.1.0, www.snapgene.com), respectively.

Results

Distribution and detection of Vibrio cholerae 2015–2023

A total of 1,045 stool samples from patients with acute diarrhea were tested as part of enteric pathogens surveillance activities at the 3 locations in Western Kenya from 2015 to 2023. Vibrio cholerae was detected in 24 samples, corresponding to an overall positivity rate of 1.2%. Detection varied across the years, with the highest positivity rate in 2019 (9/193; 4.7%), followed by 2023 (5/114; 4.4%), 2015 (8/203; 3.9%), and 2018 (2/118; 1.7%). No V. cholerae positive samples were detected during 2016–2017 and 2020–2022 (Table 1).

Serogroup distribution and sequence types

Isolates were classified into two serogroups: O1 and non-O1/non-O139 (NOVC), based on the presence or absence of O1 and O139 specific gene clusters as detected by CholeraFinder. O1 isolates were identified by the presence of the rfbV-O1 gene cluster, those containing wbfZ-O139 as O139, while isolates lacking both O1 and O139 markers were classified as NOVC. Of the 24 isolates, 62.5% (15/24) belonged to the O1 serogroup, while 37.5% (9/24) were classified as non-O1/non-0139 (NOVC). Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) identified two distinct strain types: ST 69, corresponding to the O1 El Tor biotype, was the most prevalent at 62.5% (15/24), while ST8, associated with NOVC, comprised 37.5% (9/24). All ST8 NOVC isolates were recovered from a single site in Uasin Gishu County in 2019 (Table 4).

Virulence genes profile

Analysis of the 24 V. cholerae genomes identified 18 distinct virulence-associated genes across both O1(ST69) and non-O1/non-O139(ST8) isolates (Fig. 1). All ST69 isolates carried the classical virulence gene determinants characteristic of pandemic El Tor strains: including ctxA, ctxB (cholera toxin subunits), toxR, als, makA, and hylA. In contrast, non-O1/non-O139 (ST8) lacked cholera toxin genes (ctxAB). However, they carried toxR, makA, and hylA. Overall, ST69 isolates exhibited a broader gene profile carrying ~ 72% (13/18) of the identified genes, compared to 56% (10/18) in ST8 isolates.

Antimicrobial resistance gene profile

A total of 15 AMR genes were identified across all the ST types (Fig. 1). These genes confer resistance to multiple antibiotic classes, including β-lactams, aminoglycosides, macrolides, tetracyclines, sulfonamides, and polymyxins. ST69 (O1) isolates carried more antimicrobial resistance genes than ST8 isolates. All ST69 genomes carried mutations associated with fluoroquinolone resistance (gyrA S83I and parC S85L), as well as genes conferring resistance to trimethoprim (dfrA1), chloramphenicol (catB9), and efflux pumps (emrD3). The genes blaPER−7 (extended-spectrum β-lactamase), sul1 (sulfonamide resistance), and macrolide resistance genes (mph(A), msr(E), mph(E)) were detected exclusively in ST69 isolated in 2023. In contrast, ST8 isolates carried genes almE, almF, almG, and the carbapenem resistance gene varG but lacked resistance determinants to sulfonamides, trimethoprim, macrolides, and fluoroquinolones, which were all present in ST69.

Pathogenicity island and seventh pandemic markers

All ST69 isolates possessed the seventh pandemic marker gene VC2346, as well as a full set of pathogenicity islands VPI-1, VPI-2, VSP-1, and VSP-2. In contrast, ST8 isolates carried only the pathogenicity island VPI-2.

Plasmids, class 1 integrons, and mobile genetic elements

Plasmid analysis revealed temporal variation among O1 (ST69) isolates. Between 2015 and 2018, none of the O1 (ST69) strains had plasmids. However, this changed in 2023, when all five ST69 isolates harbored a highly conserved IncC-type plasmid, which had 100% identity to each other and other reported plasmids in ST69 V. cholerae, such as a recent plasmid pVIC11-A (GenBank accession no. CP134059.1) associated with the 2022–2023 cholera outbreak in Lebanon [38].

This plasmid carried multiple antimicrobial resistance genes including aadA2, sul1, blaPER−7, mph(A), msr(E), and mph(E), which together conferred resistance to a range of antibiotics such as streptomycin, sulfonamides, β-lactam antibiotics/cephalosporins, and macrolides, respectively.

A circular BLAST comparison map of these IncC plasmids is shown in Fig. 2. In addition, the 2023 isolates also carried a 2,407 bp class 1 integron within the IncC plasmid. This integron was located adjacent to other antimicrobial resistance genes but directly carried only aadA2 which confers resistance to aminoglycosides such as streptomycin and spectinomycin, and qacEΔ1, associated with resistance to quaternary ammonium compounds and commonly found in the 3′ conserved segment of class 1 integrons. A complete attC recombination site was detected between these resistance genes, confirming the functional cassette structure and suggesting active gene capture potential. All ST69 isolates, regardless of year, carried the mobile SXT integrative and conjugative element encoding resistance to streptomycin, sulfamethoxazole, and trimethoprim. No plasmids or integrons were detected in ST8 isolates.

Discussion

This study provides a genomic characterization of 24 Vibrio cholerae isolates collected in western Kenya between 2015 and 2023. It sheds light on the diversity of strains present in the region, the virulence genes they carry, their antimicrobial resistance profiles, and the mobile genetic elements contributing to their evolution and AMR. The intermittent detection of Vibrio cholerae reflects the episodic nature of cholera transmission in endemic settings such as western Kenya, where outbreaks are often influenced by environmental factors, population movement, and the sensitivity of laboratory test systems. Periods without detected cases may reflect true breaks in transmission, which are common in cholera endemic regions where outbreaks usually occur in waves rather than sustained year-round transmission.

The continued dominance of ST69 aligns with reports from Tanzania [39], India [8], and Malawi [3], where this sequence type continues to drive recurrent epidemic waves. Its repeated detection over multiple years and location within western Kenya suggests ongoing local transmission and endemic establishment, consistent with prior patterns observed during previous outbreaks in the country [17]. These findings indicate that ST69 represents a stable and well-adapted pandemic lineage that continues to persist within the region. In contrast, ST8 isolates exhibited restricted temporal and geographical clustering and were identified exclusively among individuals in a congregate setting. This pattern suggests a localized and self-limited transmission event rather than widespread or sustained transmission. The sporadic and short-lived nature of ST8 infections is consistent with their genomic characteristics. These isolates lacked the classical cholera toxin genes that are critical determinants of epidemic potential and carried a narrow set of virulence-associated genes, indicating a reduced potential to cause large-scale outbreaks. Similar observations have been reported elsewhere where NOVC strains lacking ctx genes are typically associated with isolated cases rather than major outbreaks [9]. Although generally less virulent, NOVC strains are increasingly recognized as emerging pathogens, especially in people with weakened immunity [11, 40].

Analysis of virulence genes revealed clear differences between the two lineages. ST69 isolates harbored a full suite of virulence determinants, including cholera toxin ctxA and ctxB and the major pathogenicity islands VPI-1, VPI-2, VSP-1, and VSP-2, supporting their capacity for efficient intestinal colonization and severe cholera toxin disease. In contrast, ST8 isolates lacked these cholera-specific virulence factors but retained alternative determinants such as toxR, hlyA, and als, which support environmental persistence, hemolysin production, and colonization. This reduced virulence profile aligns with the typical mild clinical presentations of NOVC infections while still indicating pathogenic potential [41].

Clear divergence was also observed in AMR profiles and mobile genetic elements. ST69 isolates exhibited multidrug resistance, carrying determinants conferring resistance to aminoglycosides, sulfonamides, macrolides, β-lactams, and fluoroquinolones. All ST69 isolates carried gyrA S83I and parC S85L mutations commonly associated with fluoroquinolone resistance, whereas these mutations were absent in ST8. Despite the presence of these mutations, phenotypic antimicrobial testing suggested susceptibility to ciprofloxacin. The discordance suggests that the presence of single quinolones determining region mutations, particularly in the absence of additional gyrA or parC alterations is insufficient to confer phenotypically detectable fluoroquinolone resistance.

Prior studies [42] have shown that ciprofloxacin resistance requires the accumulation of multiple target site mutations and the presence of additional resistance mechanisms such as overexpression of efflux pumps or plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance determinants. Our findings therefore suggest that these mutations may contribute to reduced ciprofloxacin susceptibility that remains below established clinical breakpoints.

The observed phenotypic azithromycin resistance among ST69 isolates recovered in the year 2023 is associated with the presence of IncC plasmids carrying macrolide resistance genes. This finding points to an increasing trend of azithromycin resistance over time and raises concern about the long-term efficacy of this antibiotic. IncC plasmids are broad-host range, highly mobile elements commonly found in multi-drug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria and have been implicated in the dissemination of resistance across diverse pathogens [43]. Importantly, a genomic epidemiological study by Lassalle [44] demonstrated that multidrug-resistant IncC plasmids can spread horizontally between co-circulating V. cholerae lineages, including both epidemic and non-epidemic strains, thereby accelerating the emergence of resistant variants during outbreaks. In this context, the detection of IncC plasmids in Kenyan ST69 isolates suggests that similar horizontal transfer dynamics may be contributing to the local expansion of multidrug resistance. Although ST8 isolates in this study did not harbor such plasmids, their co-circulation with ST69 indicates a potential risk for future acquisition of resistance determinants.

This reinforces the role of IncC plasmids as important drivers of resistance dissemination across genetically distinct lineages.

The presence of both dfrA1 and sul1 genes provides a molecular explanation for trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole resistance. These genes encode drug-insensitive forms of dihydrofolate reductase and dihydropteroate synthase, respectively, allowing folate biosynthesis to continue despite inhibition by the drug combination. As a result, the bacteria can maintain the essential metabolic functions under antimicrobial pressure. The observed resistance phenotype likely arises from the combined effect of chromosomal dfrA1 and plasmid-borne sul1, which together reduce the efficacy of the drug. Importantly, sul1 is commonly associated with class 1 integrons on conjugative plasmid such as IncC plasmids, promoting horizontal gene transfer. The coexistence of chromosomal and mobile resistance determinants may therefore enhance multidrug resistance in Enterobacteriaceae [45]. Together, these findings highlight how the interplay between chromosomal and mobile resistance determinants enhances multidrug resistance. Their presence therefore increases the risk of local, regional, and intercontinental spread of multidrug resistance of V. cholerae lineages.

Additionally, the presence of the SXT integrative and conjugative element in all ST 69 isolates, absent in ST8, highlights its role in sustaining resistance within pandemic V. cholerae [17]. The detection of resistance determinants catB9, despite phenotypic susceptibility to chloramphenicol, suggests that the gene may not be functionally expressed or may require additional determinants to confer resistance. Similar discrepancies between genotypic and phenotypic resistance have been reported, indicating that the presence of resistance genes does not necessarily translate to phenotypic resistance [12]. Notably, no tetracycline resistance genes were detected in our isolates; phenotypic susceptibility testing indicates that all isolates remained susceptible to tetracycline. Given that tetracyclines, particularly doxycycline, remain the drug of choice for severe cholera cases, continued judicious use is critical to preserve effectiveness amid the increasingly limited therapeutic options.

Phylogenomic analyses demonstrated that ST8 isolates clustered outside the three recognized waves of the seventh pandemic and instead aligned with earlier V. cholerae circulating between the 1980s and 2012. This pattern suggests reintroduction of ancestral, environmentally associated lineages. In contrast, ST69 isolates clustered with the 7th pandemic strains, showing close relatedness to isolates from Asia and Africa. Overall, the phylogeographic and genomic findings highlight a divergent transmission dynamic between the two lineages. These findings highlight the co-circulation of endemic pandemic and transient non-pandemic lineages in western Kenya.

Given that non-pandemic lineages can re-emerge, sustained genomic surveillance remains critical for tracking transmission pathways, emerging resistance, and the long-term evolution of V. cholerae [46].

Conclusions

This study provides genomic data on the genetic diversity, virulence potential, and antimicrobial resistance of Vibrio cholerae from western Kenya. The predominance of the pandemic ST69 lineage, coupled with its increasing acquisition of multidrug resistance elements, highlights ongoing evolutionary adaptation and points to public health risks posed by persistent endemic transmission. The detection of ST8 non-O1/non-O139 isolates highlights the presence of non-pandemic V. cholerae with distinct virulence and resistance profiles. These findings suggest the co-circulation of divergent V. cholerae populations. The detection of additional resistance determinants in the recent ST69 isolates, including IncC plasmids and class 1 integrons, highlights a need to strengthen antibiotic stewardship and the need for genomic surveillance as a pillar to effective cholera control and therapy. Routine sequencing of environmental and clinical isolates will be essential for guiding public health interventions, tracking re-emerging strains, and managing the rising antimicrobial resistance.

Limitations and future direction

While this study offers valuable insights, it had the following limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the number of V. cholerae isolates analyzed (n = 24) represents a small fraction of the total cholera burden in western Kenya during the study period. Although thousands of cholera cases were reported in the region over the study period, and within our surveillance cohort, V. cholerae was detected in only 1.2% (24/105) of stool samples. As such, the isolates analyzed represent only a limited subset and may not capture the full genetic or distribution of circulating strains in western Kenya. Second, the isolates were obtained from a limited number of sites and primarily from healthcare-based surveillance, which may not reflect transmission dynamics at the community level. In addition, the clustering of ST8 isolates within a single site and year limits further the ability to draw conclusions about their wide distribution. Third, environmental sampling was not included and the geographic focus on western Kenya restricts the generalizability of the findings to other regions of the country broader geographic coverage, inclusion of environmental isolates, and larger sample sizes would provide a more comprehensive understanding of V. cholerae diversity and transmission.

Nonetheless, the study provides important genomic data that may be useful for informing future investigations. Expanding genomic surveillance efforts to include more extensive sampling, wider geographic representation, and continued monitoring of mobile genetic elements and resistance-associated determinants will be important for improving understanding of V. cholerae evolution and its implications for disease control.
