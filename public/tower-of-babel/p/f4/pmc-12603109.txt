Synthesis, in vitro antimicrobial activity, and in silico studies of 1-aminoalkyl-2-naphthols
Authors: Frehiwot Beyene, Avijit Mazumder, Solomon Tadesse, Kaleab Asres, Daniel Bisrat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rise of multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens highlights the need for novel antimicrobial agents. This study reports the synthesis of two 1-aminoalkyl-2-naphthol derivatives, 1-(dimethylaminomethyl)naphthalen-2-ol (2) and 1-(piperidin-1-ylmethyl)naphthalen-2-ol (3), via the Betti base reaction using 2-naphthol, formaldehyde, and secondary amines. Structural confirmation was achieved through the 1H and 13C-NMR. Antimicrobial screening against 26 bacterial and 4 fungal strains revealed that compound 3 exhibited potent antibacterial activity against MDR strains, with a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) value as low as 10 µg/mL against Pseudomonas aeruginosa MDR1. Compound 3 also demonstrated superior efficacy against Staphylococcus aureus MDR strains, with an MIC of 100 µg/mL, compared to ciprofloxacin, which had an MIC of 200 µg/mL. Its lower MIC values compared to ciprofloxacin suggests that compound 3 has potential for treating infections caused by multidrug-resistant S. aureus. Compound 2 showed strong antifungal activity against Penicillium notatum and P. funiculosum, with an MIC of 400 µg/mL, outperforming griseofulvin (MIC = 500 µg/mL). Molecular docking further supported these findings, with compound 3 displaying strong binding affinities to E. coli DNA gyrase (‒6.755 kcal/mol; PDB ID: 5MMN) and Candida albicans lanosterol 14α-demethylase (‒7.813 kcal/mol; PDB ID: 5V5Z). Overall, these results underscore the potential of 1-aminoalkyl-2-naphthol derivatives as promising candidates for combating drug-resistant infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23064-1.Keywords: 1-Aminoalkyl-2-naphthol, 2-Naphthol, Betti base, Antimicrobial, Molecular docking, ADMETSubject terms: Biochemistry, Drug discovery, Microbiology

Introduction

Infectious diseases cause about 7.7 million deaths annually, posing a major global health challenge, especially in developing countries1. Africa continues to face a high burden from HIV/AIDS, malaria, tuberculosis, and diarrheal diseases2,3. Bacterial infections are the second leading cause of death globally, following heart disease, while fungal infections pose serious risks to immunocompromised individuals4,5. Over 300 fungal species cause human disease, with Candida, Cryptococcus, and Mucormycetes being the most severe6. The growing threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) exacerbates this crisis, currently causing 700,000 deaths annually and potentially rising to 10 million per year by 2050 if unaddressed7,8. The worldwide spread of MDR infections is escalating this global health crisis9,10.

Betti bases, also known as aminoalkyl or amidoalkyl naphthols, are a unique class of organic compounds formed through the multicomponent reaction of an amine, an aldehyde, and a naphthol. This versatile reaction produces structurally diverse compounds with a broad spectrum of biological activities11. Aminoalkyl naphthols are particularly important in pharmaceutical chemistry, with numerous synthetic methods reported and their derivatives evaluated for diverse biological activities, including antibacterial and antiviral properties12,13. However, their antimicrobial applications remain underexplored despite their pharmacological potential14.

As part of our ongoing research on the antimicrobial evaluation of bioactive compounds15,16, two 1-aminoalkyl-2-naphthol derivatives, 1-(dimethylaminomethyl)naphthalen-2-ol (2) and 1-(piperidin-1-ylmethyl)naphthalen-2-ol (3) were synthesized, via the Betti base reaction using 2-naphthol, formaldehyde, and secondary amines (dimethylamine or piperidine). Although these compounds have been synthesized previously17,18, their antimicrobial activity against a broad range of microorganisms remains largely unexplored. In this study, compounds 2 and 3 were therefore evaluated for their antimicrobial potential against a panel of 26 bacterial and 4 fungal strains, including MDR pathogens. Given the growing threat of MDR infections and the promising pharmacological properties of aminoalkyl naphthol derivatives, their antimicrobial applications, particularly against clinically relevant MDR bacteria and fungi, represent a critical but underexplored area of drug discovery. The present findings are expected to provide valuable insights into their potential as antimicrobial agents and serve as a foundation for future structure–activity relationship studies.

Results

Synthesis of 1-aminoalkyl-2-naphthol derivatives

2-Naphthol derivatives have been well-documented for their antimicrobial potential19,20. Although various methods for synthesizing 1-aminoalkyl-2-naphthols have been reported21,22, the conventional one-pot three-component reaction remains the simplest and most efficient approach, even though some reactions require longer times. Despite these minor limitations, the Betti reaction offers a reliable method for preparing the target compounds. Thus, in this study, two 1-aminoalkyl-2-naphthol derivatives, 1-(dimethylaminomethyl)naphthalen-2-ol (2) and 1-(piperidin-1-ylmethyl)naphthalen-2-ol (3), were synthesized using a one-pot, three-component Betti base reaction. The reaction involves the condensation of formaldehyde, 2-naphthol, and a secondary amine (dimethylamine for 2 and piperidine for 3), in the presence of acetic acid as a catalyst as shown in Fig. 1.

Synthesis of 1-aminoalkyl-2-naphthol derivatives (2 and 3).

The reaction mechanism begins with acetic acid protonating the carbonyl group of formaldehyde, thereby increasing its electrophilicity and enabling a nucleophilic attack by the secondary amine to form an iminium ion intermediate. This intermediate then undergoes electrophilic aromatic substitution with 2-naphthol, leading to the formation of the final products. Both compounds follow the same overall reaction pathway (Fig. 2).

A proposed pathway for the formation of 1-aminoalkyl-2-naphthols.

Structural elucidation of compounds 2 and 3

Compounds 2 and 3 were identified as 1-(dimethylaminomethyl)naphthalen-2-ol and 1-(piperidin-1-ylmethyl)naphthalen-2-ol, respectively, based on the analysis of their 1H and 13C-NMR spectral data. Detailed spectral data are provided in the Material and Methods section.

The 1H-NMR spectral data (Supplementary Fig. S1) of compound 2 showed the presence of six aromatic methine protons: δ 7.12 (1 H, d, J = 8.9 Hz, H-3), δ 7.69 (1 H, d, J = 8.9 Hz, H-4), δ 7.76 (1 H, m, H-5), δ 7.44 (1 H, ddd, J = 8.4, 6.8, 1.4 Hz, H-6), δ 7.29 (1 H, ddd, J = 8.0, 6.8, 1.1 Hz, H-7), and δ 7.81 (1 H, d, J = 7.7 Hz, H-8). These signals are evident with an aminoalkylation substitution occurring at the aromatic 2-naphthol ring. A singlet signal integrating for two protons at δ 4.10 (2 H, s, H-11) provides additional evidence that this methylene group bridges the aromatic ring and the amino group. In the 13C-NMR spectrum (Supplementary Fig. S2) along with DEPT-135 spectrum (Supplementary Fig. S3), a total of 13 carbon signals were observed, including six aromatic methine carbons, one methylene carbon, two methyl carbons, and four quaternary carbons. Particularly, the methylene carbon bridging the amino and aromatic groups appeared at δ 57.94 (C-11), confirming that the reaction occurred between dimethylamine, formaldehyde, and 2-naphthol. The remaining proton and carbon signals are presented in the Materials and Methods section. Thus, based on the 1H and 13C-NMR spectral data, the structure of compound 2 was confirmed as ((dimethylamino)methyl)naphthalen-2-ol, as shown in Fig. 1.

In the 1H-NMR spectrum (Supplementary Fig. S4) of compound 3, a singlet signal integrating for two protons at δ 3.99 (2 H, s, H-7′) indicated the presence of a methylene group bridging the amino and aromatic moieties, supporting the successful reaction between piperidine, formaldehyde, and 2-naphthol. Further evidence was provided by the six aromatic proton signals observed in the 1H-NMR spectrum: δ 6.79 (1 H, d, J = 8.8 Hz, H-3), δ 7.79 (1 H, nr, H-4), δ 7.85 (1 H, m, H-5), δ 7.48 (1 H, ddd, J = 8.1, 7.2, 1.5 Hz, H-6), δ 7.42 (1 H, ddd, J = 7.2, 6.8, 1.3 Hz, H-7), and δ 7.79 (1 H, nr, H-8). These signals confirm that aminoalkylation occurred on the aromatic ring. In total, sixteen carbon signals were observed in the 13C-NMR spectrum (Supplementary Fig. S5) of compound 3. These include six aromatic methine carbons, six methylene carbons, and four quaternary carbons, as evident from DEPT-90 spectrum (Supplementary Fig. S6). Detailed spectral data are provided in the Materials and Methods section. In conclusion, compound 3 was unambiguously identified as 1-(piperidin-1-ylmethyl) naphthalen-2-ol (3) (Fig. 1).

Compound 3 exhibited the strongest antibacterial activity against the multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa MDR 1 at MIC of 10 µg/mL and ZOI of 12.2 mm. This finding holds particular clinical significance, as P. aeruginosa is a prevalent opportunistic Gram-negative pathogen responsible for nosocomial infections, and its increasing drug resistance poses substantial treatment challenges23.

Both synthesized compounds demonstrated equally potent activity against all tested Gram-negative E. coli strains, with a MIC of 25 µg/mL and ZOIs ranging 11.5–15.1 mm. Given that E. coli is one of the most prevalent Gram-negative pathogens causing extraintestinal infections in humans, such as meningitis and pneumonia24, the present results demonstrate potential in combating E. coli-related diseases.

S. aureus is both a commensal and an opportunistic bacterium, responsible for a wide range of infections, from mild skin conditions to severe diseases25. In this study, both the multidrug-resistant strain S. aureus MDR 1 and 2 showed high susceptibility to compound 3, exhibiting a ZOI of 13.5 mm, greater than that of ciprofloxacin, which had a ZOI of 11.6–12.3 mm. These findings were further corroborated by MIC values of 100 µg/mL, comparable to the MIC value of 200 µg/mL observed for ciprofloxacin (Table 1).

However, both synthesized compounds showed the least antibacterial activity against B. pumilus 82 and B. subtilis ATCC 6633. For B. pumilus 82, compound 2 had a ZOI of 7.5 mm and a MIC of 400 µg/mL, while compound 3 showed a ZOI of 7.0 mm and an MIC of 400 µg/mL. Similarly, for B. subtilis ATCC 6633, compound 2 had a ZOI of 7.5 mm and an MIC of 400 µg/mL, whereas compound 3 showed a slightly higher ZOI of 7.8 mm and a lower MIC of 200 µg/mL. Both compounds 2 and 3 exhibited bactericidal activity against the tested microbes at a concentration of 200 µg/mL, except against Bacillus spp., where they showed a bacteriostatic effect.

Antifungal activity

Interestingly, 1-(dimethylaminomethyl)naphthalen-2-ol (2) demonstrated superior antifungal activity compared to the standard antifungal drug griseofulvin. Specifically, compound 2 caused a ZOI of 13.4 mm against P. notatum and 12.9 mm against P. funiculosum. In contrast, griseofulvin showed lower antifungal efficacy, with ZOIs ranging from 11.0 to 12.0 mm against the same fungal species. In broth dilution assay, compound 2 showed its highest antifungal activity against P. funiculosum NCTC 287, with an MIC value of 400 µg/mL, while griseofulvin showed an MIC of 500 µg/mL. In contrast, compound 3 exhibited its highest activity against C. albicans ATCC 1023 and A. niger ATCC 6275, both with MIC values of 400 µg/mL (Table 2). Thus, the present findings indicate that compound 2 exhibits stronger antifungal activity against both tested Penicillium species compared to the reference drug. Additionally, compound 2 showed a fungistatic effect against C. albicans, while displaying fungicidal activity against the other tested fungi at a concentration of 2000 µg/mL. Compound 3 demonstrated fungicidal activity against all four tested fungi at the same concentration (2000 µg/mL).

In silico studies

Molecular docking

Given the promising antimicrobial effects of compounds 2 and 3, elucidating their mechanism of action is essential. Molecular docking, a widely used computational approach for predicting the interactions of bioactive molecules with their potential targets, was employed to investigate the possible mechanisms of action of these compounds. Docking studies were performed against the proposed antibacterial target, E. coli DNA gyrase (PDB ID: 5MMN)26, and the antifungal target, C. albicans lanosterol 14α-demethylase (CYP51; PDB ID: 5V5Z)27.

For the antibacterial target, E. coli DNA gyrase (PDB ID: 5MMN) was selected based on preliminary investigations and previous studies identifying this enzyme as a well-established target for 2-naphthol derivatives28,29. DNA gyrase plays critical role in bacterial DNA replication and transcription, further supporting its relevance in evaluating the antibacterial potential of the synthesized compounds. Molecular docking analysis of compounds 2 and 3 showed promising interactions within the active site of DNA gyrase. As illustrated in the 2D and 3D interaction diagrams (Figs. 3 and 4), both compounds fit well into the catalytic pocket, forming multiple stabilizing interactions (Table 3).

Of the two compounds, compound 3 achieved a docking score of − 6.755 kcal/mol, indicating a favorable binding affinity. The compound establishes two hydrogen bonds with the ASN46 and ASP73 residues via its amine and hydroxyl groups, respectively, and engages in a polar interaction with THR165. In addition, there are significant hydrophobic interactions with lipophilic regions of the binding pocket, such as ALA47, ILE78, ILE94, MET95, VAL43, VAL120, VAL167, further stabilizing its conformation (Fig. 3).

On the other hand, compound 2 exhibited a lower binding affinity, with a docking score of − 6.659 kcal/mol. It also forms two hydrogen bonds with ASN46 and ASP73 via its amine and hydroxyl group, respectively, and engages in a polar interaction with THR165. Moreover, it establishes hydrophobic interactions with lipophilic residues within the binding pocket, including ALA47, ILE78, ILE94, MET95, VAL43, VAL120 and VAL167 (Fig. 4).

The docking protocol underwent validation using the native ligand (1-ethyl-3-[8-methyl-5-(2-methyl-pyridin-4-yl)-isoquinolin-3-yl]-urea) for antibacterial target. When native ligand was redocked into the active site of the E. coli DNA gyrase (Supplementary Fig. S7), it yielded a docking score of − 9.291 kcal/mol, confirming the validity of the docking approach.

Both compounds 2 and 3 were also evaluated for their potential mechanism of action against the antifungal target, C. albicans lanosterol 14α-demethylase (PDB ID: 5V5Z). This enzyme was chosen because lanosterol 14α-demethylase (CYP51) has been reported as a target for naphthol related compounds30 and is a well-established antifungal drug target due to its essential role in maintaining fungal cell membrane integrity.

As illustrated in Fig. 5, compound 3 demonstrated strong binding affinity to the active site of C. albicans lanosterol 14α-demethylase, with a favorable docking score of − 7.813 kcal/mol. Compound 3 formed multiple hydrogen bonds with key active site residues, including MET508 and SER378 which likely contribute to its binding stability. In addition to forming hydrogen bonding, it engaged in a π-cation interaction with HIE377 and three polar interactions with HIE310, SER507, and THR122. Several hydrophobic interactions were also observed with nonpolar residues such as ILE379, LEU376, PHE228, PHE233, PHE380, PRO230,THR122; TYR118, and TYR132, further stabilizing the compound within the enzyme’s binding pocket.

Pharmacokinetics and drug-likeness properties

In drug research and discovery, predicting the physicochemical and medicinal chemistry compatibility profiles of compounds is crucial. Early parameter prediction, such as utilizing Lipinski’s rule of five, enhances the success rate of compounds progressing to lead optimization31. The ADMET 2.0 online tool was used to assess the physicochemical and medicinal chemistry of drug-likeness behavior of compounds 2 and 3. The finding of the present analysis revealed that compound 2 possesses excellent physicochemical properties and is favorable from a medicinal chemistry perspective. In contrast, compound 3 shows higher log P and log D values, indicating slightly greater lipophilicity. However, both compounds comply with Lipinski’s rule of five, suggesting a strong likelihood of good oral bioavailability (Table 5).

In drug design, in silico ADMET prediction models support medicinal chemists by guiding lead optimization and evaluating essential pharmacokinetic and physicochemical properties. They help quickly identify unsuitable compounds and provide early ADMET insights for effective lead development32. In this study, the ADMET 2.0 online server was employed to evaluate absorption, transport, and metabolism of compounds into non-toxic, water-soluble metabolites for excretion. It also provided predictions on blood-brain barrier penetration, gastrointestinal absorption, and interactions with p-glycoprotein substrates and CYP450 enzymes32.

The analyses showed that both compounds had excellent absorption but poor blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration, making them less suitable for treating meningococcal infections (Table 6). Compound 3 exhibited high plasma protein binding (PPB) at 90%, which may affect its therapeutic efficacy33. Both compounds 2 and 3 were identified as inhibitors of CYP1A2 and CYP2D6, suggesting potential interactions with substrates of these enzymes. Neither compound is a p-gp substrate, indicating they are not subject to cellular efflux. However, compound 3 poses a risk of hERG-related toxicity, which could lead to cardiac arrhythmia34.

Discussion

2-Naphthol derivatives are well known as versatile intermediates in synthetic and medicinal chemistry because of their nucleophilic centers, which allow derivatization into a wide range of biologically active scaffolds, including Betti bases, xanthenes, and chromenes35. Among these, 1-aminoalkyl-2-naphthols (Betti bases) have drawn attention due to their pharmacological activities such as antibacterial, antiviral, antifungal, and cardiovascular effects36–38. However, the antimicrobial activity of Betti base derivatives has not been extensively studied, and only a limited number of reports describe their effectiveness against clinically relevant pathogens14,19,39. The present work therefore contributes to filling this gap by systematically evaluating two such derivatives (compounds 2 and 3) against a broad spectrum of bacteria and fungi, including MDR strains.

Our findings show that compound 3, bearing a piperidine substituent, displayed strong antibacterial activity, particularly against S. aureus MDR strains and P. aeruginosa MDR1, with MIC values of 100 µg/mL and 10 µg/mL, respectively. The activity of compound 3 against S. aureus MDR strains was superior to ciprofloxacin (MIC = 200 µg/mL) under the same conditions, which is noteworthy since fluoroquinolones are frontline drugs against resistant staphylococcal infections. Previous studies on aminoalkyl naphthols have reported moderate antibacterial activity, often with MIC values exceeding 200 µg/mL13,39, suggesting that compound 3 represents a significant improvement in potency. Importantly, its high activity against P. aeruginosa, a pathogen notoriously resistant to most antibiotics due to efflux pumps and impermeable membranes23,40, underscores its therapeutic promise to MDR strains.

Both synthesized compounds also exhibited consistent inhibitory activity against multiple E. coli strains (MIC = 25 µg/mL), a result that compares favorably with earlier reports where naphthol-based antimicrobials displayed only modest activity against Gram-negative bacteria19,20. Since Gram-negative organisms are generally harder to target due to the additional outer membrane barrier40, these findings highlight the potential of aminoalkyl-2-naphthols as scaffolds for addressing resistant Enterobacteriaceae infections. In addition, aminoalkyl-2-naphthols are valued for their wide range of biological activities, including hypotensive and bradycardiac effects12,38, as well as their ability to enhance the cytotoxicity of antitumor agents41. Beyond their pharmacological relevance, they also function as effective ligands in asymmetric synthesis and catalysis by coordinating with organometallic reagents42. Furthermore, Betti bases, key representatives of this class, play a vital role as precursors to 1,3-amino oxygenated compounds, which are essential building blocks of biologically active natural products and clinically important drugs such as certain nucleoside antibiotics and HIV protease inhibitors43. The observed broad-spectrum efficacy of compounds 2 and 3 also indicates potential application in polymicrobial infections, where existing treatment options are limited.

With regard to antifungal activity, compound 2 (dimethylamino derivative) was particularly active against two Penicillium species (MIC = 400 µg/mL), outperforming the reference drug griseofulvin (MIC = 500 µg/mL). Although Penicillium species are rarely associated with human infections due to their limited ability to grow at 37 °C, the primary health risk lies in the consumption of food contaminated with mycotoxins produced by certain Penicillium species44. Previous reports have mainly emphasized the antibacterial properties of Betti bases36,37, with far fewer investigations into their antifungal effects. The ability of compound 2 to surpass a clinically used antifungal in activity suggests that its scaffold may be optimized for broader antifungal drug development. Similarly, compound 3 demonstrated significant inhibition of C. albicans (MIC = 400 µg/mL) and A. niger ((MIC = 400 µg/mL), pathogens responsible for life-threatening infections in immunocompromised patients45,46. These findings are consistent with earlier studies showing that structural modifications of naphthol derivatives can enhance their activity against fungal pathogens19,47, but the strong activity observed here provides one of the strongest demonstrations of Betti base efficacy against major fungal pathogens.

Molecular docking results corroborated the experimental antimicrobial findings. Both compounds exhibited favorable binding to E. coli DNA gyrase, a validated antibacterial target. The slightly stronger binding of compound 3 correlates with its stronger antibacterial activity. Both synthesized compounds (2 and 3) demonstrated favorable interactions within the active site of E. coli DNA gyrase, with docking scores of − 6.659 and − 6.755 kcal/mol, respectively. Their binding was stabilized by hydrogen bonds, as well as polar and hydrophobic contacts. Despite structural differences, the compounds exhibited similar binding patterns, interacting with comparable amino acid residues in the active site. Notably, compound 3, which displayed the lowest MIC (10 µg/mL against P. aeruginosa MDR1 and 100 µg/mL against S. aureus MDR strains) and larger ZOIs, also showed a slightly stronger binding affinity (–6.755 kcal/mol) than compound 2 (–6.659 kcal/mol). These results support the hypothesis that stronger enzyme–ligand interactions are associated with enhanced antibacterial activity. Thus, the present findings support the hypothesis that the synthesized compounds may act as DNA gyrase inhibitors and suggest that further structural modifications could enhance their antibacterial potential.

Likewise, both compounds also demonstrated affinity for C. albicans lanosterol 14α-demethylase (CYP51), the validated target of azole antifungals27,48. CYP51 is a key enzyme in the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway, catalyzing the demethylation of lanosterol, a critical step in producing ergosterol, the principal sterol of fungal cell membranes47. Docking results revealed that both compounds form hydrogen bonds with MET508 and SER378, interactions likely crucial for stabilizing and enhancing binding specificity within the active site. Notably, compound 3 also engaged in additional π–π interactions with TYR118 and TYR132, which may account for its stronger antifungal activity. The consistency of these interactions with clinically validated drug targets reinforces the biological plausibility of the observed antifungal effects and highlights these compounds as promising lead structures. Azole-class antifungals, including fluconazole, itraconazole, and voriconazole, act through the same mechanism by inhibiting CYP5148, thereby depleting ergosterol and causing toxic sterol accumulation, ultimately disrupting membrane integrity and inhibiting fungal growth.

The observed docking scores qualitatively correlated with the experimental antifungal activities. Compound 3 exhibited superior activity against C. albicans and A. niger, consistent with its stronger docking score against lanosterol 14α-demethylase (–7.813 kcal/mol) compared to compound 2 (–7.122 kcal/mol). In contrast, compound 2, which displayed lower docking affinity, was generally less active against most fungal strains but showed selective activity against Penicillium species. Although docking scores do not always directly correspond to MIC values, the relative ranking of binding affinities is consistent with the in vitro activities, supporting the proposed mechanisms of DNA gyrase and CYP51 inhibition. However, these mechanisms should be further validated through enzyme inhibition assays.

From a drug-likeness perspective, both compounds complied with Lipinski’s rule of five, suggesting good oral bioavailability. However, compound 3 showed higher plasma protein binding and potential hERG liability, which will need to be addressed in future optimization. Nevertheless, compared with previously reported Betti base derivatives, which often suffer from poor solubility or weak activity12,13, the present compounds combine strong antimicrobial effects with favorable physicochemical profiles, supporting their further development.

This study highlights the antimicrobial potential of Betti bases against MDR pathogens and provides computational insights into the dimethylamino (compound 2) and piperidinyl (compound 3) substituents. While our docking studies suggest potential interactions with DNA gyrase and CYP51, we acknowledge that direct mechanistic confirmation through enzyme assays would strengthen this conclusion, a limitation we have explicitly noted. We recommend that future work prioritize enzyme inhibition assays to validate the docking results. Nevertheless, the computational data offer valuable insights into potential gyrase interactions and can guide the rational design of future derivatives. Overall, these findings underscore the relevance of aminoalkyl-2-naphthols as a promising scaffold and suggest that further optimization and in vivo evaluation would be valuable to assess their potential as antimicrobial agents.

Conclusion

In this study, two Betti base derivatives (1-aminoalkyl-2-naphthols) were synthesized and evaluated for antimicrobial activity. Both compounds demonstrated broad-spectrum efficacy, with compound 3 showing the strongest antibacterial activity, particularly against multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa MDR1 and S. aureus MDR1, while compound 2 showed notable antifungal activity against Penicillium species. Molecular docking confirmed strong binding to E. coli DNA gyrase and C. albicans lanosterol 14α-demethylase, supporting their dual antibacterial and antifungal mechanisms. Both compounds complied with Lipinski’s rule of five, indicating favorable drug-like properties. Overall, aminoalkyl-2-naphthols represent promising scaffolds for antimicrobial drug development. Further studies should optimize selectivity, address toxicity concerns such as the hERG liability of compound 3, and include SAR analysis, formulation approaches, and in vivo validation.

Materials and methods

Materials

The following reagents were used for the synthesis: 2-naphthol, 37% formaldehyde solution, dimethylamine, piperidine, acetic acid, ethanol, chloroform, methanol, hexane, ethyl acetate and silica gel. The synthesis was carried out at the Pharmaceutical Chemistry and Pharmacognosy Laboratory, Addis Ababa University. The structures of the synthesized compounds were confirmed by analyzing their 1H and 13C-NMR spectral data.

Synthesis of 1-aminoalkyl-2-naphthols (2 and 3)

The synthesis of two Betti base derivatives of 2-naphthol was carried out by refluxing 2-naphthol, formaldehyde, and amines in the presence of an acid catalyst, following the procedure described in Fig. 139.

Synthesis of compounds 2 and 3

Compound 2 (1-(dimethylaminomethyl)naphthalen-2-ol) was synthesized via a Betti base reaction by adding 0.5 mL (0.011 mol) dimethylamine dropwise to a solution of 1 mL (0.011 mol) 37% aqueous formaldehyde and 5 drops of acetic acid in 10 mL ethanol. This iminium solution was then added to 1.5 g (0.011 mol) 2-naphthol in ethanol. The mixture was refluxed at 100 °C for 24 h, monitored by TLC, evaporated, and purified by silica gel column chromatography (chloroform/methanol gradient), yielding compound 2 (1.6 g).

In vitro antibacterial and antifungal activity assay

Test microorganisms

The test microorganisms included various drug-resistant hospital isolates collected and characterized at the Department of Pharmaceutical Technology, Jadavpur University, India. A range of Gram-positive (e.g., Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus pumilus) and Gram-negative (e.g., Shigella flexneri, Vibrio cholerae, Escherichia coli) strains were maintained on Nutrient Agar (NA) for bacterial cultures and Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) slants for fungal cultures, stored at 4 °C prior to use in antibacterial and antifungal assays. The purity of each strain was confirmed using standard microbiological, cultural, and biochemical methods before proceeding with sensitivity testing against the test samples.

Disk diffusion method

The antibacterial activity of the synthesized 1-aminoalkyl-2-naphthols was evaluated using the disc diffusion method by comparing their ZOI with ciprofloxacin49. A stock solution (1 mg/mL in 1% DMSO) was prepared and diluted to concentrations of 5–800 µg/mL for antibacterial tests and 50–2000 µg/mL for antifungal tests. Sterile nutrient agar plates were prepared, inoculated, and allowed to dry before placing 6 mm filter paper discs impregnated with the test samples. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and ZOIs were measured. Ciprofloxacin and 1% DMSO served as the positive and negative controls, respectively50,51. For antifungal testing, the same method was applied using Sabouraud dextrose agar. After three days of incubation at room temperature, ZOIs were measured, with Griseofulvin used as the standard reference. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are presented as mean ± SEM.

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) by serial dilution technique

The stock solution was reconstituted using a minimal amount of 0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), a solvent confirmed to possess no inherent antimicrobial activity. For antibacterial activity testing, measured volumes of the stock solution were added to a series of McCartney bottles, each containing a calculated volume of sterile, cooled (40–45 °C) molten nutrient agar, to prepare a final volume of 30 mL per bottle. These were adjusted to produce concentrations of 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800, and 1000 µg/mL for bacterial testing.

For antifungal testing, the stock solution was incorporated into molten SDA to achieve concentrations of 100, 200, 400, 800, 1500, and 2000 µg/mL. The molten media containing different concentrations of the test compounds were aseptically poured into pre-sterilized 70 mm Petri dishes. These plates were stored at 4 °C for 24 h to allow uniform diffusion of the compounds, followed by drying at 37 °C for bacterial and 25 °C for fungal assays for 2 h prior to inoculation.

Each quadrant of the agar plates was inoculated with a loopful (3 mm diameter) of an overnight-grown microbial suspension (10⁵ CFU/mL) using the checkerboard technique. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for bacteria and 25 °C for fungi for 24 h, and the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values were recorded52,53. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Determination of mode of action

To evaluate whether the compounds exhibited bacteriostatic/fungistatic or bactericidal/fungicidal activity, agar plugs were taken from the zones of inhibition and re-inoculated into fresh media. These were monitored for microbial growth after 96 h of incubation at 37 °C for bacteria and 25 °C for fungi. The results were validated by performing viable count assays at 24-hour intervals, using compound concentrations of 200 µg/mL for bacteria and 2000 µg/mL for fungi54,55.

In silico studies

Molecular Docking study

Molecular docking of the synthesized compounds was carried out using Maestro V.13.5 software by Schrodinger 2023-1 Suite, E. coli DNA gyrase (PDB ID: 5MMN) and C. albicans lanosterol 14α-demethylase (PDB ID: 5V5Z), as potential targets for antibacterial and antifungal activities, respectively. Both protein structures of E. coli DNA gyrase (PDB ID: 5MMN) and C. albicans lanosterol 14α-demethylase (PDB ID: 5V5Z) were downloaded from the protein data bank.

Using the Schrödinger 2023-1 Protein Preparation Workflow, the 3D structures of E. coli DNA gyrase (PDB ID: 5MMN) and C. albicans lanosterol 14α-demethylase (PDB ID: 5V5Z) were separately prepared by adding hydrogens, correcting missing or erroneous side chains, adjusting charges and bond orders, removing water molecules, and modeling missing loops. The structures were then optimized using the OPLS4 force field to minimize steric clashes56. Compounds 2 and 3, drawn in ChemDraw and saved in SD format, were imported into LigPrep for 2D to 3D conversion, ionization and tautomer generation at pH 7.0 ± 2.0 (via Epik), and ring conformation generation using default settings. A receptor grid was generated around the co-crystallized ligand with a 20.0 Å radius. Van der Waals radii were softened using a charge scaling factor of 0.8 and a cut-off of 0.15. Docking of the synthesized compounds was performed using standard precision (SP) mode. To validate the protocol, redocking of the co-crystallized native ligand was carried out. All the images in Figs. 3, 4, 5 and 6 were generated using Schrödinger Maestro software (version 13.5, 2023-1; https://www.schrodinger.com/). The generated files were subsequently transferred to Paintbrush for easier management.

Pharmacokinetics and drug-likeness properties

The physicochemical, pharmacokinetic, and toxicity profiles of the synthesized compounds were predicted using ADMETlab 2.032. Compound structures were converted to SDF format using ChemDraw 22.0.0 and submitted to the web server for analysis.

Statistical analysis

The antimicrobial data were analyzed using SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) software, version 26. ZOI are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), including the 6 mm disc diameter. MIC values represent the mean of three independent experiments performed in triplicate. Statistical analysis was carried out using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc multiple comparison test, with p < 0.01 considered statistically significant.
