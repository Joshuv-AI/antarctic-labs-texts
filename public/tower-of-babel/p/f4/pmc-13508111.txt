TyrRu: A Tyramide-Amplified Multimodal Ruthenium Tag for Elemental Mass Spectrometry and Immunofluorescence Imaging
Authors: Rosemary J. Bergin, Ioannis Kohilas, Thomas E. Lockwood, Andrew M. McDonagh, Mika T. Westerhausen, David P. Bishop
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tyramide signal amplification, used in immunohistochemistry to image low-abundance biomolecules, has seen adaptation beyond conventional microscopy and is utilized in flow cytometry and electron microscopy. Here, we applied tyramide signal amplification to image biomolecules via elemental mass spectrometry imaging (MSI). Elemental MSI techniques such as laser ablation-inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS) are used to image biomolecules via metal-conjugated probes, and resolution is typically hampered by a low detection sensitivity. We report the synthesis of a bespoke ruthenium complex (TyrRu) for tyramide amplification and describe its application as a multimodal analyte to image and quantify glial fibrillary acidic protein in mouse brain by complementary immunofluorescence and elemental MSI. Pairing TyrRu and avidin–biotin-HRP amplification produced a signal-to-noise ratio 2× higher than that obtained with a commercial metal-chelating polymer, allowing LA-ICP-MS image resolution of 1 μm. Data obtained using immunofluorescence and LA-ICP-MS showed excellent biomolecule agreement (r = 0.86). TyrRu was also applied alongside primary-conjugated antibodies in a multiplexed analysis, and the enhanced detection sensitivity facilitated the generation of an image with submicrometer resolution after super-resolution reconstruction.

Introduction

Visualization and quantification of proteins in tissue by immunohistochemical (IHC) techniques are difficult for low-abundance proteins. A useful amplification strategy, tyramide signal amplification (TSA), uses the horseradish peroxidase (HRP) enzyme to convert labeled tyramide compounds into reactive intermediates capable of binding covalently with tyrosine residues present in tissue. In contrast with previous enzymatic amplification strategies that rely on the formation of diffusible precipitates, the tyramine intermediates are short-lived and bind covalently, resulting in a signal that is more localized and permanent. − TSA has been applied to a variety of techniques including immunofluorescence (IF), flow cytometry, electron microscopy, and expansion microscopy. TSA has also been applied to the analysis of DNA and RNA through in situ hybridization. , Several different implementations of TSA exist, each with varying degrees of amplification. The conventional approach involves HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies; ,,− however, greater amplification has been achieved using biotinylated secondary antibodies to which HRP-conjugated streptavidin or avidin–biotin-HRP complexes (ABC) attach. Conversely, tissue can be treated with a tyramide-biotin conjugate, to which labeled streptavidin molecules then attach. ,, Even greater levels of amplification have been achieved with minimal nonspecific binding and primary antibody use by utilizing HRP polymer-linked secondary antibodies.

Elemental mass spectrometry imaging (MSI) techniques have been adapted as an alternative to conventional IHC imaging techniques. Here, elemental MSI, encompassing secondary ion mass spectrometry and laser ablation-inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS), is used to image biomolecules in situ via metal-conjugated probes. Elemental MSI also includes imaging mass cytometry (IMC), which is a specific form of LA-ICP-MS developed to image biomolecules. Elemental MSI offers greater sensitivity, quantitative results, and higher degrees of multiplexing compared to immunofluorescence (IF), while being free of issues such as spectral overlap and autofluorescence. Quantification in LA-ICP-MS imaging involves the co-ablation of metal-spiked calibration standards, most commonly consisting of gelatin, potentially with additives for greater laser penetration depending on the laser wavelength used. , The addition of the metal analyte in elemental MSI is typically achieved via the use of metal-chelating polymer or nanoparticle-conjugated antibodies. Limitations of elemental MSI include its low detection sensitivity when acquiring images at higher resolution, its destructive nature, and its comparatively long analysis times. For this reason, multimodal fluorescent tags featuring exogenous metals present unique advantages over standard LA-ICP-MS imaging experiments. Multimodal tags allow for initial screening via IF to locate regions of interest before subsequent LA-ICP-MS analysis, reducing ablation time, simplifying troubleshooting, and allowing for confirmatory results via the two techniques. −

Ruthenium tris­(bipyridine) complexes are ideal candidates for adaptation into multimodal imaging agents due to their well-documented luminescent properties and the biologically exogenous nature of ruthenium. − Additionally, metal complexes have inherent photostability compared to conventional organic fluorophores. , Ruthenium is underutilized in mass spectrometry imaging, and the few examples that exist are not amendable to an antibody-oriented IHC workflow. , Here, we describe the synthesis and application of a novel tyramide-functionalized ruthenium bipyridine complex (TyrRu) for use as a multimodal TSA tag for IF and elemental MSI, increasing the detection sensitivity at higher image resolutions. Our strategy used biotinylated secondary antibodies and ABC to catalyze the deposition of TyrRu onto neighboring tyrosine residues.

Results

Synthesis

The first step in the synthesis of TyrRu (Scheme ) was the formation of a carboxylic-acid-functionalized 2,2′-bipyridine compound capable of amide bond formation with tyramine. The compounds 2,2′-bipyridine-4-carboxylic acid and 4′-methyl-2,2′-bipyridine-4-carboxylic acid have been described previously, but their synthesis is nontrivial and requires toxic organotin, − selenium, or chromium compounds. − Thus, a new carboxyl-functionalized ligand was synthesized from 4-amino-2,2′-bipyridine. Conventional amide coupling of succinic acid onto 4-amino-2,2′-bipyridine failed, presumably due to its poor nucleophilicity, but reaction with succinic anhydride gave 2,2′-bipyridine-4-succinamic acid (L1) in 47% yield. The ring-closed 4-succinimide-2,2′-bipyridine was obtained if reaction times were increased or heating was applied. To purify L1 from residual succinic acid, an understanding of protonation as a function of pH was instructive, as L1 can exist in multiple protonation states including as a zwitterion. 1H NMR titration experiments (see Supporting Information, Figure S18–S20) revealed the pK a of the amine group to be 4.62 and the pK a of the succinic acid group to be 3.68. Adjusting the pH to 4.15 during the liquid–liquid extraction allowed the product to be isolated. L1 was coupled with Ru­(bpy)2Cl2 to give [Ru­(L1)­(bpy)2]­Cl2 in excellent yield (89%). Subsequent amide coupling with tyramine using HATU and purification using silica gel chromatography and an eluent consisting of 85:14:1 ACN:H2O:KNO3 (sat. aq.) gave TyrRu as the nitrate salt with a yield of 45%. 1H NMR stability testing revealed a slow degradation in D2O at a rate of 0.86 mol % day–1 (Figure S22).

Fluorescent Properties of TyrRu

The luminescence spectrum of TyrRu (Figure S14) is similar to that of tris­(bipyridine) ruthenium­(II). The excitation spectrum in ultrapure water revealed multiple peaks including a broad peak with a maximum at 458 nm. The molar absorption coefficient is 14870 M–1cm–1 at this wavelength, which aligns closely with the literature value for tris­(bipyridine) ruthenium­(II). Emission occurs as a broad (Δλ1/2 = 112 nm) maximum at 623 nm, providing a Stokes shift of 165 nm. The quantum yield was determined to be 0.0303, slightly lower than the 0.0400 reported for tris­(bipyridine) ruthenium­(II). TyrRu for Multimodal Imaging

Detection of low-abundance biomolecules by IF is challenging with all immunolabeling procedures, as they often do not produce an adequate signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) to be distinguishable from the inherent autofluorescence of tissues. LA-ICP-MS imaging is also limited by SNR, as this dictates the sampling resolution achievable during LA. When biomolecule abundance is low, the generated plume will not contain enough analyte atoms to produce an SNR that enables high-resolution imaging, resulting in the use of larger laser spot sizes, often above cellular resolution. SNR is typically optimized for both imaging modalities by adjusting the antibody concentration and labeling duration. Here, as it is an indirect labeling technique, altering the antibody parameters does not impact the degree of amplification; rather, the TyrRu concentration and labeling duration were adjusted to obtain optimal SNR for both imaging modalities.

To determine suitable conditions to label glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), three concentrations of TyrRu were investigated: 2.9, 29, and 290 mg L–1, with no notable differences observed in the morphology of the target (Figure S1). There was a clear change in the SNR when the concentration was altered (Table S1). The highest concentration of TyrRu (290 mg L–1) produced signals that saturated the detectors for both LA-ICP-MS and IF, producing suboptimal SNR. The lowest concentration of TyrRu (2.9 mg L–1) allowed detection in both modalities; however, the SNR, while slightly improved against the high concentration, was still low. A suitable concentration was 29 mg L–1, providing an adequate SNR in both modalities (Table S1). A range of TyrRu incubation times was examined with IF at 29 mg L–1 and revealed a trend of increasing signal intensity with time, but with an expected concomitant increase in background (Figure S2 and Table S1). A 10-min incubation time was found to be suitable. Nonspecific binding of TyrRu was assessed by preparing slides that excluded the anti-GFAP primary antibody, the biotinylated secondary antibody, or the HRP-avidin–biotin complex (Figure S3). No nonspecific labeling of TyrRu was observed in any of the controls.

The optimized conditions were applied to the analysis of GFAP in the murine brain. A high degree of conformity between IF and LA-ICP-MS was observed (Figure a and b). The increased LA-ICP-MS detection sensitivity allowed an image resolution of 1 μm, and the key features of GFAP expression are consistent in both images. The IF and LA-ICP-MS correlation is further underscored by the superimposed image (Figure c), with a calculated Pearson’s R value of 0.86. We attribute the small differences between the two modalities to the hydration state, as the tissue section was decoverslipped and dehydrated for LA-ICP-MS acquisition, which caused differences in the tissue edges, cracks, and other features such as the trigone in the lateral ventricle. The amplification afforded by TyrRu allowed for the resolution to approach that of the IF, without impacting image quality (Figure c).

To demonstrate the degree of amplification achieved by using the ABC immunolabeling method combined with TyrRu, an HRP-TyrRu immunolabeling protocol and a primary antibody conjugated with the commercial Maxpar metal-chelating polymer were applied for comparison. ABC-TyrRu in Table and Figure highlights the advantage of pairing ABC amplification with TSA, achieving a 20x enhancement in signal compared to the metal-chelating polymer. Importantly, the increased signal did not result in an increase in noise, with the SNR of ABC-TyrRu being superior to the other two immunolabeling strategies (Table ). The ABC-TyrRu combination (Figure a and c) produced a fluorescent SNR 3x higher than that of the HRP method (Table ), with the LA-ICP-MS SNR for the HRP-TyrRu image unable to be determined as the signal was too close to the background for accurate segmentation. When compared with the commercial Maxpar reagent (Figure S4), ABC-TyrRu exhibited a 2x higher SNR. Figure a and c highlight an advantage of LA-ICP-MS over IF, as minor fluorescent artifacts due to the mounting media were observed in the fluorescent image, which were not present in the LA-ICP-MS image.

Incorporation of TyrRu into a Multiplexed Workflow and for Submicron Imaging

Many elemental MSI analyses take advantage of the unique metal isotopes available to multiplex their application, and when applied in conjunction with simultaneous TOF detectors, can image more than 40 analytes. Here, elemental MSI may still be refractory to the detection of low-abundant proteins; therefore, we examined whether TyrRu could be used alongside primary-conjugated antibodies in a multiplexed application. GFAP in murine brain was again the target for TyrRu amplification, with the addition of an 170Er-conjugated anti-NueN antibody and an Ir-DNA intercalator. Therefore, we targeted 2 neuronal cell types and the intercalator, which is used as a marker of the cell nucleus for cell segmentation purposes. The resulting image is shown in Figure a. Application of TyrRu did not impact the quantification of the primary-labeled biomolecule. The concentrations of GFAP and NueN were individually obtained on consecutive slices and compared with the concentrations obtained on the multiplexed sample (Table S3), with no significant differences in either variance or mean.

a) Multiplexed LA-ICP-MS image of GFAP (red) labeled with TyrRu, 170Er-NeuN (green), and Ir-DNA intercalator (cyan) in murine brain. b) Super-resolution reconstruction (SRR) image of GFAP labeled with TyrRu using the optimal conditions to obtain an image resolution of 250 nm. The scale bars represent 50 μm, and the calibration bar units are in mg kg–1.

Super-resolution reconstruction (SRR) involves the reconstruction of a higher-resolution image by combining multiple images ablated using consecutive offset orthogonal rasters that are acquired at subpixel distance offsets. It was previously applied to improve resolution from the 15 μm spot size used for ablation to 3.8 μm for the analysis of dystrophin in skeletal muscle via LA-ICP-MS, where detection was via a primary-conjugated antibody. Here, due to the increased detection sensitivity of TyrRu, we applied SRR using a 1 μm spot size for ablation. SRR allowed us to image at a resolution approximating that of a 40x IF, and the results are shown in Figure b. Nanoscale image resolution achieved with SRR allowed the observation of GFAP ultrastructure in CNS tissue. Characteristics of type III intermediate filaments in astrocytes include the formation of 1–10 nm rope-like cytoskeletal filaments that may be several micrometers long, as displayed within Figure b. Submicron imaging is necessary to determine subcellular structures such as mitochondria and nucleoli, and the application of TyrRu and SRR can provide such resolution. The resolution was thus improved to achieve an image with a resolution approaching 250 nm, as shown in Figure b.

Discussion

Indirect, amplified immunolabeling techniques to date have not been explored for elemental MSI applications, and the new TyrRu complex facilitates this novel application. Enzymatically amplified labels such as TyrRu allow for increased biomolecule labeling, facilitating both higher-resolution imaging and the investigation of low-abundance proteins. Its multimodality allows for initial screening and coconfirmatory imaging through its fluorescent properties, while also enabling relative quantification across samples via LA-ICP-MS when analyzed alongside spiked gelatin standards. ,

With regards to its application to IF, although the lower molar absorption coefficient and quantum yield of TyrRu likely decrease its fluorescent intensity compared to conventional IF fluorophores, this is compensated by the high amplification achieved through TSA. TyrRu's broad excitation range makes it compatible with a wide range of excitatory sources, including the commonly used Ar laser, while its Stokes shift of 165 nm is well suited for minimizing excitation–emission interference detrimental to signal intensity. , Although the wide excitation range and broad emission peak (Δλ1/2 = 112 nm) may prove beneficial for increasing overall signal output, this has the drawback of decreased multiplexing potential due to spectral overlap. Here, elemental MSI compensates for this due to the wide range of elements and isotopes that are free from overlap.

The most used analyte in elemental MSI is the MAXPAR metal-chelating polymer, to which TyrRu compares favorably, achieving a 2x greater SNR and 20x signal enhancement. While MAXPAR allows for easier and more diverse multiplexing, simply requiring chelation with a lanthanide isotope of choice, there is still potential for synthesizing derivatives of TyrRu using either seven of the stable ruthenium isotopes or employing similar transition metals such as osmium, iridium, or rhodium. TyrRu is also beneficial in its reduced cost of use in comparison to Maxpar, as the indirect nature of the amplification allows for less primary antibody to be used.

TSA has not previously been employed for elemental mass spectrometry imaging; however, it has been used to improve the detection of alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) in immunoassays with detection via ICP-MS. Here, the combination of an anti-AFP2-HRP-functionalized secondary antibody, biotin-tyramide, and Au-NP’s were used to detect AFP in simulated immunoassays of human serum by ICP-MS.

Signal amplification approaches previously employed for elemental MSI have used alternative instrumental conditions and immunolabeling strategies to improve SNR. For example, the addition of nitrogen (N2) to the ICP carrier gas increased SNR for most biologically relevant elements as well as lanthanide-tagged antibodies. This instrumental variation presents a simple-to-implement, broadly applicable amplification strategy for a wide variety of elements. However, this might not assist in the analysis of low-expression biomolecules with poor immunolabeling efficiencies, with only a moderate increase in signal reported for lanthanides. Other immunolabeling-based amplification strategies have recently been adapted for elemental MSI, including SABER-IMC. , SABER-IMC uses DNA-based amplification through exchange reactions, enabling highly multiplexed biomolecule imaging and offering advantages over TyrRu, particularly the ability to simultaneously amplify multiple biomolecules within a single tissue section. However, its workflow is complex, requiring specific concatemers to be designed and manufactured for each target. Additionally, complementary fluorescent–elemental MSI imaging has not yet been demonstrated for this technique. This contrasts with the simplicity of TSA and the bimodal fluorescent–metal detection capabilities of TyrRu. The current work is also more readily adaptable to conventional IHC workflows, requiring only some form of HRP functionalization, either direct or through biotinylation, all achievable using commercially available reagents.

A major limitation of multiplexed elemental MSI using quadrupole-based ICP-MS is the square relationship between signal intensity and spot size, restricting the practical spot size to 4–10 μm. Spot sizes below 4 μm often contain insufficient analyte atoms in each sampling event for detection using standard immunolabeling reagents like MAXPAR. , Most applications that employ elemental MSI, particularly those that use IMC, detect biomolecules to examine complex relationships between multiple proteins and cell types. , Therefore, it was important that TyrRu was able to integrate into a multiplexed workflow. Here, we imaged two cell types and a marker for the nucleus at an image resolution of 2 μm. The Ir-intercalator used here was added, as it is commonly used for cell segmentation purposes in multiplexed elemental MSI analyses, and it was necessary to show that TyrRu did not impact the detection of other cell types (Table S3).

Submicron image resolution is required to show subcellular structures such as mitochondria and nucleoli and, as described above, is often refractory to elemental MSI due to the lack of atoms reaching the detector. The amplification provided by TyrRu facilitated the use of super-resolution reconstruction to obtain submicron imaging of biomolecules by LA-ICP-MS. Submicron imaging was recently reported by IMC using a similar oversampling and deconvolution approach, where they obtained a resolution approximating 350 nm,36 which was improved upon here to 250 nm. Investigating changes in these subcellular structures often requires multiple analyses including imaging for location and laser-capture microdissection of cells from a concurrent slide for biomolecule quantification. Reagent advances such as TyrRu that facilitate the in situ quantification of biomolecules via LA-ICP-MS at subcellular resolution will allow unprecedented access to information on changes induced by disease and greatly reduce the complexity of such analyses.

Conclusion

The synthesis of TyrRu for TSA and its application as a multimodal analyte for the imaging and quantification of GFAP in mouse brain was successfully achieved. TyrRu produced an improved signal and SNR compared to that obtained with a commercial metal-chelating polymer, and its multimodal properties allowed for optimization with IF before quantitative LA-ICP-MS analysis. TyrRu was also applied alongside primary-conjugated antibodies in a multiplexed analysis, with no significant differences observed in the concentrations against individual analyses. The enhanced detection sensitivity facilitated the generation of an image with a resolution of 250 nm after super-resolution reconstruction, which is lower than that typically obtained by 40x IF microscopy. Future directions should explore imaging TyrRu using a confocal, white-light laser-equipped microscope to tailor the excitation and emission to better suit TyrRu, potentially allowing for fluorescent multiplexing and a reduction in autofluorescent artifacts. Isotopically enriched TyrRu or other transition metal derivatives would also allow tyramide-amplified multiplexing by elemental MSI.

Instrumentation

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were acquired using a Bruker Ascend at 400 MHz for 1H and 100 MHz for 13C. All new compounds were characterized by 1H NMR (Figures S5–S7), 13C NMR (Figures S8–S10), and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) (Figures S15–S17). Assignment of the 1H NMR spectra of [Ru­(L1)­(bpy)­2]­Cl2 and TyrRu was aided by COSY 2D NMR (Figures S11 and S12). The 1H and 13C NMR spectra were calibrated to residual solvent peaks based on tabulated values. 13C D2O spectra were calibrated to ethylene carbonate (66.6429 ppm; Sigma-Aldrich CRM). Quantitative NMR (QNMR) was performed using a 90° pulse width with relaxation delays set to a minimum of 7× the maximum measured relaxation time of the integrated regions. Internal standards used for QNMR included ethylene carbonate (Sigma-Aldrich CRM) for all synthesized complexes and maleic acid (purity verified against benzoic acid, Sigma-Aldrich CRM) for L1. The hydration number of the synthesized complexes, rounded to the nearest integer, was determined by QNMR, with slight adjustment to the documented method via the use of ethylene carbonate as an internal calibrant and the aid of a calibration curve (Figure S21) to back-calculate HDO peak integration prior to analyte addition. 1H NMR pK a titration (Figures S18–S20) was performed on L1 in D2O following a method outlined in the literature. Stability testing was performed on TyrRu using the same QNMR conditions and sample by plotting the %reduction of the integral at 6.57 ppm (Figure S22). HRMS was performed by direct injection using an Agilent 6510 high-resolution time-of-flight mass spectrometer operated in positive mode. Samples were prepared by dilution and centrifugation in LC-MS grade acetonitrile (Sigma-Aldrich). UV–visible absorbance spectroscopy (Figure S13) was performed on a Cary 60 dual-beam spectrophotometer at a scan rate of 600 nm/min and data intervals of 1 nm. Fluorescence spectroscopy (Figure S14) was performed on a Shimadzu RF-6000 spectrofluorophotometer using a scan rate of 200 nm/min, 0.5 nm data intervals, and a 10 nm slit size. Quantum yield was determined in aerated ultrapure water via a relative single-point method using tris­(bipyridine)­ruthenium­(II) chloride hexahydrate as a standard.

Materials and General Methods

All reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Castle Hill, NSW, Australia), and all solvents were purchased from Chem Supply (Gillman, SA, Australia), unless stated otherwise. Inert chemistry was performed under an argon atmosphere using a Schlenk line, with glassware dried at 110 °C overnight and reagents cycled onto the line three times prior to reaction. Tissue Preparation

Brain tissue was obtained from C57BL/6 healthy mice aged 6 months, which were housed at a maximum of 5 in a cage, maintained on a 12 h light/dark cycle, and had ad libitum access to food and water. LA-ICP-MS External Calibration Standards

External calibration standards were prepared from gelatin using previously developed methods. , Briefly, 1000 μg mL–1 Ru, Er, Gd, and Ir standards (Choice Analytical; Thornleigh, New South Wales, Australia) were spiked into a 10% porcine gelatin solution made in 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 10 mM EDTA, with 5 mg/mL tryptophan and 1% PEG-20 in ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ cm–1 at 25 °C, Arium Pro Vf, Sartorius, Goettingen, Germany). The gelatin standard mixture was heated to 38 °C until liquid, vortexed, then pipetted into a HybriWell on a standard microscope slide before freezing at −80 °C for 15 min until opaque. Post-freezing, the HybriWell mold was removed, and the gelatin standard was stored at room temperature until subjected to analysis. The gelatin standards were digested and characterized by solution ICP-MS, and the concentrations are presented within Table S2.

Fluorescent Imaging

Fluorescent images were taken using a Nikon TiE2 inverted fluorescence deconvolution microscope using a Plan Apo λ 20x Ph2 DM (0.75 NA). An excitation wavelength of 488 nm (6%, 150 ms) was used, and the amount of emitted light detected was maximized by employing a pentaband filter. All images were captured as Z-stacks, and the slices have been processed with extended depth of focus in FIJI.

Data Analysis

All MSI data is available at https://github.com/davepbish/TyrRu. Visualization and quantification of LA-ICP-MS images were achieved using Pew2 and were used to create CSV’s for text image import into FIJI. SNR was determined using FIJI’s thresholding feature to segment the positive signal from the background for each image and then to obtain the average of the fluorescent intensity units or concentration in mg kg–1 for each segment. The quantified LA-ICP-MS images were used to determine SNR as a point of normalization to account for differences in ionization in the ICP between Ru and Gd. The concentrations presented in Table S3 were determined after segmentation, where an F-test (variance) and t-test (means, equal variance) was performed to assess differences in the variance and mean of the data sets. The Pearson’s correlation coefficient was determined using FIJI’s Coloc 2 plug-in, using the registered LA-ICP-MS and fluorescence images depicted in Figure a and b.

SSR images were processed using a homemade script based on Westerhausen et al., available at https://github.com/davepbish/TyrRu. Briefly, two offset orthogonal raster scan layers were summed together in the z-axis to create an image, and then the image was smoothed using a Gaussian kernel with dimensions equivalent to the spot size of the laser. To minimize beam-related spreading of the signal, a Richardson–Lucy Total Variance Regularization and a synthetic Gaussian point spread function with 10 iterations were applied.
