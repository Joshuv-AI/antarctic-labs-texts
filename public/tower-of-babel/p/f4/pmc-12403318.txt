Assessment of drought tolerance in peach × almond hybrids to identify promising rootstocks
Authors: Mehdi Fallah, Alli Imani, Mousa Rasouli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Drought is a major environmental stress severely restricting plant growth, development, and productivity in arid regions. In this research, seven interspecific peach × almond hybrids (‘GF677’, ‘GN15’, ‘GN2’, ‘TT’, ‘35.1’, ‘34.2’, and ‘50.10’) were evaluated for their drought stress tolerance to identify promising rootstocks. Drought stress treatments were applied at 40%, 60%, and 100% field capacity (FC). Following a two-month stress period, morphological, physiological, and biochemical traits, along with the concentrations of 10 elements in leaves and roots, were measured. Under severe drought stress, all rootstocks exhibited reductions in plant height, trunk diameter, new branch growth, root and shoot fresh and dry weights, and relative water content, with an increase in the number of necrotic and fallen leaves. Drought stress significantly impaired PSII efficiency. At 40% FC, rootstocks ‘TT’ (0.749) and ‘GF677’ (0.740) exhibited the highest QYmax, while rootstocks ‘50.10’ (0.650) and ‘34.2’ (0.688) had the lowest. Under 40% field capacity (FC) stress, protein content and the activities of APX and CAT decreased, while proline, antioxidant capacity, electrolyte leakage, and aldehyde levels increased. Under drought stress, ‘TT’ displayed higher leaf concentrations of N (2.08%), P (0.17%), Ca (1.45%), Mg (0.56%), Fe (184.71 mg kg−1), Cu (5.07 mg kg−1), and Zn (18.77 mg kg−1). In contrast, rootstock ‘50.10’ exhibited the highest concentrations of Cl (4.83%), P (0.24%), and Zn (18.85 mg kg−1) and the lowest concentrations of N, K, Ca, and Fe. Finally, this research introduces ‘TT’ rootstock as a promising drought-tolerant rootstock.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07247-6.

Introduction

Drought, a critical abiotic environmental stress, significantly limits plant growth, development, and productivity [1]. Drought stress in plants can result from low rainfall, extreme temperatures, or intense light [2]. Horticultural crops, particularly Prunus fruit trees, experience reduced yields under drought stress [3]. Plants have evolved diverse strategies to cope with drought stress, enabling growth, development, and reproduction despite water scarcity [4] while maintaining physiological activity even at significantly reduced water content [1].

Photosynthesis is one of the most critical indicators of plant health, and its efficiency under drought conditions is a vital determinant of drought tolerance [5]. Evaluating PS-II activity is useful for identifying genotypes that are better suited to specific environmental conditions, thereby aiding in plant adaptation [6]. Chlorophyll fluorescence imaging, a fast and non-invasive technique, effectively measures PS-II efficiency, allowing for the rapid identification of plant responses to abiotic stress. We can assess photosynthetic efficiency by analyzing chlorophyll fluorescence kinetics [7]. Drought stress can trigger the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within plant cells, including superoxide, singlet oxygen, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radicals. Excessive accumulation of these ROS can lead to oxidative damage to membrane lipids, proteins, DNA, and nucleic acids in plants [8]. Through long-term evolution, plants have developed a sophisticated network of antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT), to efficiently neutralize harmful substances like superoxide (O2−) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) [9]. Maintaining high levels of antioxidant enzyme activity contributes to drought stress alleviation by enhancing the plant’s capacity to withstand oxidative damage. Consequently, the ability of antioxidant enzymes to ROS and mitigate their damaging effects is closely linked to plant drought stress tolerance [10].

The main breeding objectives for Prunus rootstocks include ease of propagation and rooting, favorable nursery characteristics, graft compatibility, control of scion cultivar growth and size, vigorous root growth and development, and tolerance to environmental stresses (drought, alkaline soils, soil moisture extremes) and soil-borne pests and diseases [11, 12]. Therefore, a crucial component of an effective Prunus (especially almond) rootstock breeding program is the selection of drought stress rootstocks. In recent decades, almond breeding programs have increasingly prioritized enhancing almond trees’ resilience to environmental stresses. These efforts have resulted in the development of several rootstocks that are more tolerant to abiotic stresses, such as drought [13]. Almond cultivars exhibit diverse physiological, morphological, and biochemical responses to drought stress [14]. Recent studies have thoroughly investigated the effects of drought stress on various almond cultivars and genotypes, as well as other fruit tree species [15–21]. For instance, the ‘Masbavara’ cultivar exhibited superior osmotic adjustment, higher water potential, reduced evapotranspiration, enhanced photosynthetic rates, and greater water use efficiency compared to the ‘Laurent’ cultivar [15]. A study evaluating drought stress at 100, 80, 60, and 40% field capacity in various almond genotypes revealed that genotype ‘A-7−100’ exhibited the highest photosynthetic efficiency. According to this study, genotypes ‘A−100−13’ and ‘A−100-8’ also demonstrated substantial drought tolerance [16]. Another study assessing the drought tolerance of four almond cultivars at 100, 75, 50, and 25% field capacity revealed that drought stress significantly declined morphological traits such as plant height, root length, fresh and dry weight of shoots and roots and leaf area index while increasing proline accumulation and malondialdehyde (MDA) content in all cultivars [17]. A study assessing drought tolerance in Prunus species revealed that P. webbii exhibited greater drought tolerance than commercial rootstocks [18]. Physiological and molecular evaluations of two almond cultivars, ‘Filippo Ceo’ and ‘Tuono’ under drought stress in Italy, revealed that ‘Filippo Ceo’ exhibited greater drought tolerance compared to ‘Tuono’. ‘Filippo Ceo’ maintained higher relative water content (RWC) and, good levels of photosynthetic pigments, and accumulated high levels of proline, which was correlated with increased expression of drought-responsive genes. In contrast, ‘Tuono’ experienced more oxidative damage and showed a weaker physiological response [19]. In a study, the morphological, physiological, and biochemical responses of the ‘Divadona’ peach cultivar, grafted onto two different rootstocks (Rootpac 20 and Rootpac 40), showed that Rootpac 40 had higher drought tolerance. The relative stem diameter of ‘Divadona’/Rootpac 40 decreased less than that of ‘Divadona’/Rootpac 20. Scions grafted onto Rootpac 40 displayed the highest antioxidant enzyme activity and maintained higher levels of essential nutrients under stress, with a smaller reduction in nitrogen content [20]. Furthermore, evaluation of peach cultivars and genotypes under water stress (FC = 30%) for 5 weeks in Thailand showed that the local cultivar ‘Red Angkhang’ and the hybrid ‘42047T1’ showed greater tolerance to drought stress. The tolerance mechanism in these two rootstocks was different: Red Angkhang responded to water deficit only by increasing root dry weight, while the hybrid 42047T1 also accumulated sorbitol [21].

This research was part of a breeding program that included morphological, physiological, biochemical, and elemental evaluations. This study aimed to identify and develop drought-tolerant rootstocks suitable for Iran’s climate and similar arid regions.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The rootstocks included 7 interspecific hybrids of peach and almond: ‘GF677’, ‘GN15’, ‘GN2’, ‘TT’, ‘35.1’, ‘34.2’, and ‘50.10’ (Table 1). In this study, ‘GF677’ and ‘GN15’ were used as control treatments. Other hybrids were developed through a breeding program conducted in 2012 and 2015 at the almond research orchard of Horticultural Science Research Institute (HSRI), Karaj-Iran. They possess significant potential for stress tolerance. The rootstocks were propagated through tissue culture to ensure uniform rootstocks for applying drought stress in pot conditions. In addition, after sufficient growth, they were transferred to a greenhouse condition (with a temperature of 22–25 °C and a humidity of 60%) in early 2023. Subsequently, in May 2023, the rootstocks were transplanted into 25-liter pots and placed outdoors. The rootstocks were three years old when the drought stress treatment was applied.

The hybrids used in this research had parents with moderate to high drought tolerance. Previous studies have shown that peach-almond hybrids often possess high potential for drought tolerance [22]. Peach rootstock ‘Nemared’ exhibits tolerance to root-knot nematodes, low waterlogging tolerance, and moderate drought tolerance [23]. The Spanish almond cultivar ‘Garfi’ exhibits favorable morphological characteristics and is readily propagated clonally [24]. The almond cultivar ‘Tuono’ has moderate [21] to high [25] drought tolerance. Cultivar ‘Flaminia’, a hybrid between ‘Fayette’ and ‘Fairtime’, shows moderate drought tolerance [26]. Additionally, bitter almond and the Iranian cultivar ‘Shahrood-12’ possess high drought tolerance [32].

Drought treatments

The soil used in pots consisted of 37.5% sand, 32.5% silt, and 30% clay, with a pH of 7 and an electrical conductivity (EC) of 1.08 dS m−1. The pots were maintained under uniform outdoor conditions for three months to allow the rootstocks to acclimate to outdoor conditions and achieve adequate growth before the stress application stage (May 1 to August 1, 2023). The pots were irrigated with water at an EC of 0.5 dS m − 1 during this period. Then, drought treatments were applied for 2 months, from August 1 to the end of September 2023. The climatic conditions included minimum, maximum, and average temperature, annual cloud, and humidity averages of this area in 2023 (Fig. S1a, b; https://www.worldweatheronline.com). Drought stress treatments were applied at 40%, 60%, and 100% of field capacity. Field capacity (FC) was initially measured to determine the required irrigation amounts.

Calculating irrigation requirements

Field capacity (FC) of the soil in the pots was determined using the gravimetric moisture method. To ascertain soil water content, pots were saturated until water drained through the base. Evaporation was minimized by covering each pot’s soil surface with aluminum foil. Pots were weighed daily until their weights stabilized. A soil sample was taken from each pot, and their wet weight was (WW) recorded. The dry weight (DW) was determined by oven-drying the soil at 72 °C for 24 h. Soil water content was then determined to be 25% using the following equation: Mass Moisture Percentage (MC%) = [(WW - DW) / DW] × 100. To accurately calculate FC, the bulk density (ρb) of the soil used in the pots was determined using the formula ρb = Weight of soil / Volume of soil, and it was found to be 1.10 kg/m³. Subsequently, using the formula FC = MC% × ρb, a field capacity of 27.5% was obtained [27].

To calculate the irrigation water required at FC, a pot of soil was oven-dried at 72 °C for 24 h to determine the dry soil mass (DM), which was 18.94 kg. The moist soil mass (MW) was then calculated using the formula MW = DM × (1 + FC), resulting in 24.14 kg. Subtracting DM from MW yielded an irrigation water volume of 5.2 l at FC [27]. Therefore, the volume of water required to achieve 100% FC (control treatment) was calculated as 5.2 l. Accordingly, the water requirements for mild stress (60% FC) and severe stress (40% FC) treatments were calculated to be 3.12 and 2.08 l, respectively. Pots were irrigated based on their weight loss, using 5.2, 3.12, and 2.08 l of water for the 100% FC, 60% FC, and 40% FC treatments, respectively, throughout the two-month period.

Evaluation of morphological traits

Before applying the treatments, morphological traits (i.e., rootstock height (mm/day), trunk diameter (mm), new flash diameter (mm), number of branches, and number of leaves) were measured. The drought treatment was applied for two months, and stress symptoms were observed at the end of the period (Fig. 3a). Subsequently, the morphological traits were remeasured at the end of the treatment period. The extent of leaf necrosis in the upper and lower parts of the branches was recorded, and the percentage of necrotic leaves was calculated (leaves with necrotic 1–50% and 51–100%). Besides, to determine the percentage of fallen leaves, the number of fallen leaves was documented throughout the experimental period, and the total number was calculated [28]. Also, the relative water content (RWC) of leaves was calculated using the formula described by Yamasaki and Dillenburg [29]. Abbreviations are shown in Table 2.

Evaluation of destructive traits

After the drought stress treatment period, the shoots and roots’ fresh and dry weights were measured. For this purpose, the shoots were cut at the crown section, and their fresh weight was recorded. Roots were carefully removed from the potting soil and washed with distilled water, and after removing excess moisture, their fresh weight was also measured. Subsequently, the shoots and roots were separately placed in an oven at 75 °C for 48 h, and their dry weights were determined [30].

Evaluation of chlorophyll fluorescence

To measure chlorophyll fluorescence, we initially used a portable pulse-amplitude-modulated (PAM) fluorometer, specifically the PAM-2100 model made by Walz in Germany. Then, the FluorCam device was used to capture images of the photosynthetic efficiency of the leaves. The leaves were used to measure the maximum quantum efficiency of Photosystem II (PSII) (Fv/Fm) using a Handy FluorCam FC 1000-H (Photon Systems Instruments, PSI, Czech Republic). From each treatment, three intact leaves, still attached to the plants, were darkened for 90 min by covering them with aluminum foil. This experiment was conducted early in the morning. After dark adaptation, the fluorescence detection was performed with a high-sensitivity charge-coupled device (CCD) camera, and four fixed LED panels were operated. Mages were captured during brief measuring flashes in darkness. At the end of these flashes, the samples were exposed to a saturating light pulse (3900 µmol m− 2 s−1), which led to a temporary saturation of photochemistry and reduction of the primary quinone acceptor of PSII [31]. After reaching steady-state fluorescence, two consecutive series of fluorescence data were digitized and averaged: one during the short measuring flashes in darkness (F0) and the other during the saturating light flash (Fm). Fv was calculated using the formula Fv = Fm - F0 from these two images. The Qymax was then calculated as (Fm - F0)/Fm. Finally, average values and standard deviations per image were calculated using FluorCam version 7.0 [31].

Protein and antioxidant enzyme activity assays

Preparation of Buffers: to prepare the first buffer (phosphate buffer, pH = 6.8), 0.34 g of KH2PO4, 0.7 g of PVPP, and 0.3 g of Na-EDTA were dissolved in 30 mL of distilled water, and the volume was adjusted to 50 mL. For the second buffer (phosphate buffer, pH = 7.2), 0.44 g of K2HPO4, 0.7 g of PVPP, and 0.3 g of Na-EDTA were dissolved in 30 mL of distilled water, and the volume was brought up to 50 mL. Subsequently, 39% of the first buffer was mixed with 61% of the second buffer to prepare a phosphate buffer with a final pH = 7. To prepare the oxidizing water buffer (H2O2, 225 mM), 450 µL of H2O2 was mixed with 20 mL of the phosphate buffer. A guaiacol buffer (45 µM) was prepared by combining 112 µL of guaiacol with 20 mL of the phosphate buffer. The extraction of plant samples was carried out following Akbarpour et al. [32]. These extracts were subsequently used to measure the protein, activity of guaiacol peroxidase (POD) and catalase (CAT). POD activity was determined by monitoring the change in absorbance at 470 nm.

Proline content analysis

About 0.5 g of fresh leaf tissue was ground in liquid nitrogen, transferred to 15 mL tubes, and mixed with 10 mL of 3% sulfosalicylic acid. The mixture was incubated in an ice bath for 10 min and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. In a new set of tubes, 2 mL of the supernatant was combined with 2 mL of ninhydrin and 2 mL of acetic acid. Simultaneously, standard proline solutions (0, 4, 8, 12, 16, and 20 mg/L) were prepared without extracts. All samples were incubated in a water bath at 100 °C for 1 h, cooled in an ice bath for 10 min, and mixed with 4 mL of toluene. After vertexing for 20 s, absorbance was measured at 528 nm, and proline content was calculated using the method described by Imani et al. [33].

Determination of electrolyte leakage (EL)

Electrolyte leakage (EL) was measured by placing 0.5 g of leaf tissue from each cultivar in glass vials containing 25 mL of distilled water. The samples were incubated on a shaker at 24 °C and 120 rpm for 24 h. Afterward, the initial electrical conductivity (LT) was measured using an EC meter (Metrohm 644 model). The vials were then placed in a water bath at 120 °C for 1 h, shaken for an additional two hours, and the final electrical conductivity (LO) was measured. The EL percentage was calculated using the following equation [34]: EL (%) = (LT / LO) × 100.

Evaluation of peroxidation of lipids characteristics

Fresh leaf tissue (0.2 g) was ground in 0.1% trichloroacetic acid, centrifuged (10,000 rpm for 5 min), and the supernatant was mixed with 20% trichloroacetic acid containing 0.5% trichloroacetic acid. After heating at 95 °C for 30 min and cooling, the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. Absorbance at 455 nm was measured for malondialdehyde with non-specific absorbance at 600 nm subtracted. The MDA concentration (nmol/g FW) was calculated using the formula: 6.45 × (A532 − A600) − 0.56 × A450 [35].

Total antioxidant capacity assay

Total antioxidant capacity was measured by mixing 50 µL of the extract from the previous step with 950 µL of 0.1 mM DPPH solution. The mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Blank (zero) and standard samples were prepared using 1 mL of extraction solvent and 1 mL of 0.1 mM DPPH solution, respectively. The absorbance of both standard and test samples was measured at 517 nm using a spectrophotometer [32].

Determination of elemental concentrations in leaves and roots

To measure the nutrient elements, the leaves and roots were separated, thoroughly washed, and placed in an oven at 75 °C for 48 h at the end of the experimental period. After drying, the samples were ground into a powder using an electric grinder. The samples were then ashed by heating them to 550 °C, and the resulting ash was extracted using 10 mL of 2 N hydrochloric acid and distilled water, bringing the volume up to 50 mL. Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, iron, zinc, copper, sodium, and chloride concentrations were measured according to the relevant protocol [36, 37].

Statistical analysis

A factorial experiment was conducted to evaluate the effects of seven rootstocks and three drought levels on rootstocks’ growth. Each treatment combination was performed in triplicate. For morphological, physiological, and biochemical experiments, 63 samples were analyzed, encompassing 7 rootstocks, 3 treatments, and 3 replicates. Destructive sampling to determine fresh and dry root/shoot weight involved 42 samples, specifically 7 rootstocks under 2 treatments (control and severe drought) with 3 replicates. Lastly, elemental analysis of roots and leaves was carried out on 18 samples, which included 3 rootstocks (sensitive and tolerant) across 2 treatments (control and severe drought) and 3 replicates. The data obtained from morphological, physiological, biochemical traits, and nutrient element measurements were statistically analyzed using SPSS software (Version 20) under a completely randomized design. Mean comparisons were performed using Duncan’s multiple range test in SPSS (Version 20). Coefficients of variation (CV = SD/mean *100) were estimated as the indicator of variability. Graphs were created using Excel (Version 2021). Meanwhile, correlation estimation between traits and cord diagrams was performed using the R software package (RStudio 2022).

Results

Morphological traits

Rootstock height decreased with increasing drought stress. Under severe drought stress (40% FC), rootstocks ‘50.10’, ‘34.2’, and ‘GN2’ exhibited the lowest growth, with a height increment of 0.50 mm/day. In contrast, rootstocks ‘GF677’ and ‘GN15’ showed the highest increment (Table S1, Table 3). Regarding the trait of fallen leaves, no leaf shedding was observed in the control treatments during the experimental period. However, the percentage of fallen leaves increased with increasing drought levels in all rootstocks. The highest fallen leaves were observed at severe stress (40% FC) in rootstocks ‘50.10’ and ‘GN2’, with 60.29% and 57.42%, respectively. Moreover, the lowest fallen leaves under severe stress were observed in rootstocks ‘GF677’ and ‘TT’, with 25.50% and 27.27%, respectively (Table 3). The highest increase in branch number at different stress levels was observed in rootstock ‘GN15’. Rootstocks ‘GN15’ and ‘GF677’, with trunk diameter increases of 4.15 mm and 3.08 mm, respectively, had significantly higher trunk diameter increases than the other rootstocks in the control treatment. Moreover, rootstock ‘50.10’ had the lowest trunk diameter increment of 0.41 mm under severe drought stress (40% FC). Regarding the trait of necrotic leaves (leaf burn), the results showed that necrotic leaves increased with increasing drought stress, especially at 40% FC. Among the different rootstocks, ‘TT’, ‘GN15’, and ‘GF677’ had the lowest levels of necrotic leaves, indicating their tolerance to drought stress. Conversely, rootstocks ‘50.10’, ‘34.2’, and ‘GN2’ had the highest levels of necrotic leaves, indicating their sensitivity to drought stress (Table 3).

Chlorophyll fluorescence

Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters were measured by evaluating the minimum fluorescence (F0), maximum fluorescence (Fm), variable fluorescence (Fv), and the ratio of variable fluorescence to maximum fluorescence (Fv/Fm), or the photosystem II efficiency (Qymax). According to the ANOVA results (Table S2), both the main effects and the interaction of drought stress and rootstock type on chlorophyll fluorescence parameters were significant at the 1% probability level. The results showed that as drought stress increased, the F0 value increased while the Fm, Fv, and Qymax values decreased in all rootstocks. The comparison of means (Table 4) was conducted regarding photosystem II efficiency (Qymax) as the most important chlorophyll fluorescence parameter. The results showed that among the different rootstocks under drought stress (40% FC), ‘TT’ and ‘GF677’ had the highest values, 0.749 and 0.740, respectively. Meanwhile, rootstocks ‘50.10’ and ‘34.2’ had the lowest PS-II efficiency with values of 0.650 and 0.688, respectively. In addition, chlorophyll fluorescence was measured using a FluorCam. Differences were recorded using various colors. According to Fig. 1, PS-II efficiency decreased under drought stress, and its level varied among different rootstocks.

Fresh and dry weight of shoots and roots, height and number of roots

Results showed that increasing drought stress led to a decrease in the fresh and dry weight of shoots and roots and the number of primary roots in all rootstocks. The highest fresh and dry shoot weight under non-drought conditions was observed in rootstock ‘GF677’, while the lowest shoot weight was found in rootstock ‘GN15’ under 40% FC drought. The highest fresh and dry root weight was also observed in rootstock ‘GF677’. Besides, the lowest fresh root weight was found in rootstocks ‘GN15’, ’50.10’, and ‘34.2’, and the lowest dry root weight was observed in rootstock ‘GN15’, ‘34.2’, ‘35.1’, and ‘50.10’ under 40% FC Drought (Fig. 2). Figure 3 presents a visual representation of the branch and root systems of the different rootstocks. Under severe drought stress, rootstocks ‘35.1’ and ‘TT’ exhibited the highest number of primary roots, while rootstocks ‘50.10’ and ‘GN15’ displayed the lowest. Furthermore, root length measurements in different rootstocks revealed that drought stress decreased root volume and length (Figs. 2 and 3). Among the rootstocks subjected to severe drought stress, rootstocks ‘50.10’ and ‘GN2’ exhibited the longest roots, while ‘GN15’ and ‘GF677’ had the shortest (Figs. 2 and 3).

Relative water content (RWC)

RWC declined as drought stress intensified. The comparison of mean values revealed that under severe drought stress (40% FC), rootstocks ‘GN15’ and ‘TT’ exhibited the highest RWC, with values of 46.16% and 45.79%, respectively. In contrast, rootstocks ‘50.10’ and ‘34.2’ displayed the lowest RWC, with values of 28.06% and 32.52%, respectively (Table 5).

Proline and protein content

ANOVA (Table S3) showed that drought stress, rootstock, and their interaction significantly influenced proline and protein content in leaves (p ≤ 0.01). Proline levels increased under moderate stress (FC = 60) in all rootstocks, and under severe stress (FC = 40) in all rootstocks except for the ‘TT’ rootstock. Conversely, protein content decreased under drought stress. Moreover, the results of the comparison of means (Table 5) indicated that under severe drought stress (40% FC), rootstock ‘50.10’ had the highest proline content at 137.8 (µmol g−1 FW), while rootstocks ‘TT’ and ‘GF677’ had the lowest proline content at 67.9–71.9 (µmol g−1 FW), respectively. The highest protein content was observed under 40% FC drought in rootstocks ‘TT’ and ‘GF677’ at 0.41 ml g−1 FW. In contrast, the lowest protein content was observed in rootstocks ‘50.10’ and ‘GN2’ at 0.27-ml g−1 and 0.28-ml g−1 under 40% FC drought, respectively.

Antioxidant enzyme activity

ANOVA results (Table S3) revealed significant main effects of drought stress and rootstock and a significant interaction effect on the activities of the enzymes examined (APX and CAT). Based on the results, the enzyme activities in the studied rootstocks exhibited varying responses to increasing drought stress. The results of the comparison of means (Table 5) showed that in rootstocks ‘GF677’, ‘GN15’, ‘TT’, and ‘GN2’, both APX and CAT activities increased under moderate drought stress (60% FC) compared to the control treatment. However, the activities of this enzyme decreased under severe drought stress (40% FC) in these rootstocks. In contrast, the highest enzyme activities were observed in rootstocks ‘34.2’, ‘35.1’, and ‘50.10’ under control conditions and decreased with increasing drought stress (60% and 40% FC). APX activity was the highest in rootstocks ‘GF677’ (16.84 µmol g−1 FW min−1) and ‘TT’ (15.93 µmol g−1 FW min−1) under moderate stress (60% FC) and lowest in rootstocks ‘50.10’ (4.03 µmol g−1 FW min−1) and ‘34.2’ (5.2 µmol g−1 FW min−1) under severe stress (40% FC). For catalase, the highest activity was found in rootstocks ‘GN2’ (1.33 µmol g−1 FW min−1) and ‘GF677’ (1.03 µmol g−1 FW min−1) under moderate stress (60% FC), while the lowest activity was observed in rootstocks ‘50.10’ (0.79 µmol g−1 FW min−1) and ‘34.2’ (0.91 µmol g−1 FW min−1) under severe stress conditions (40% FC).

Peroxidation of lipids

Comparison of the means (Table 5) showed that under severe drought stress (40% FC), rootstocks ‘35.1’ and ‘50.10’ had the highest aldehyde content (0.300 and 0.288 nmol g−1 FW, respectively), while rootstock ‘TT’ had the lowest (0.140 nmol g−1 FW). Under moderate drought stress (60% FC), rootstock ‘50.10’ had the highest aldehyde content (0.178 nmol g−1 FW), while rootstock ‘TT’ had the lowest (0.118 nmol g−1 FW) (Table 5).

Antioxidant capacity

As with other biochemical traits, statistically significant differences (p < 0.01) were found between different bases for antioxidant Capacity (Table S3). The comparison of mean results (Table 5) showed that rootstocks ‘34.2’ and ‘50.10’ had the highest antioxidant capacity under severe drought stress (40% FC), with values of 54.05% and 42.85%, respectively. Under moderate drought stress (60% FC), rootstock ‘35.1’ had the highest antioxidant capacity (23.59%), while rootstock ‘GF677’ had the lowest (21.68%).

Electrolyte leakage

The comparison of mean results (Table 5) showed that under severe drought stress (40% FC), rootstocks ‘50.10’ and ‘GN2’ had the highest ion leakage percentages (97.39% and 96.50%, respectively). In contrast, rootstocks ‘GF677’ and ‘TT’ had the lowest (87.26% and 90.84%, respectively). Under moderate drought stress (60% FC), rootstock ‘GN2’ had the highest electrolyte leakage percentage (94.15%), and rootstock ‘TT’ had the lowest (85.43%).

Correlations between traits

Pearson correlation analysis was employed to investigate the correlations among these indices (Fig. 4). Results showed a strong positive correlation (P < 0.01) between protein content, electrolyte leakage, fallen leaves, necrotic leaves, and aldehydes. Also, antioxidant enzyme activities (CAT and APX) showed a strong positive correlation (P < 0.01) with RWC and QYmax content. Total protein, RWC, and rate of height increase showed a strong positive correlation (P < 0.01). Additionally, total antioxidant capacity showed a strong positive correlation (P < 0.01) with fallen leaves. Also, shoot dry weight was strongly positively correlated (P < 0.01) with root dry weight. A slight positive correlation (P < 0.05) was observed between CAT, APX, RWC, and QYmax, rate of height increases, and total protein with trunk diameter increase. A strong negative correlation (P < 0.01) was noted here between RWC, protein, and rate of height increase with proline, fallen leaves, necrotic leaves, and electrolyte leakage. Root dry weight showed a strong negative correlation (P < 0.01) with total antioxidant capacity. Furthermore, a slight negative correlation (P < 0.05) was also noticed between fallen leaves, necrotic leaves, and proline with CAT (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Morphological traits

Reduced growth (including decreases in shoot height and diameter, leaf area, and diminished development of new leaves) is recognized as a key indicator of drought stress [38, 39]. The growth environment strongly influences plant height. Because growth results from vital processes that depend on adequate water, water scarcity can lower cell turgor pressure and cell elongation, ultimately leading to decreased height. Our results indicated that with a decrease in irrigation, the rate of increase in plant height is reduced. This result suggests that drought stress affects the growth and height of different rootstocks. The decrease in plant height under water stress occurs due to reduced growth caused by stomatal closure due to a decrease in soil water potential, and stomatal closure is accompanied by a decrease in CO2 assimilation [40]. Previous studies have shown that rootstock longitudinal growth and trunk diameter decline under drought stress in different almond [16] and apple [41] genotypes. Similar studies have shown that under drought stress, P. eburnea and P. eleagnifolia exhibited the shortest heights [42]. In addition, water scarcity negatively affects cell growth and the formation of xylem cells, ultimately reducing their quantity [43]. Drought stress has been shown to reduce stem length and cell growth, inhibit the formation and growth of xylem vessels, and decrease trunk diameter. The observed reduction in growth parameters under drought stress conditions may be attributed to increased activity of the indole acetic acid oxidase (IAAO) enzyme [44]. Furthermore, the genotypes exhibited significant necrosis and leaf abscission increases under severe drought stress conditions. This finding is consistent with previous findings, which reported that increased ethylene production was associated with reduced leaf yellowing and leaf fall in response to drought stress [16, 45]. Under severe drought stress (FC = 40), the rootstock ‘GN15’ uniquely exhibited an increase in stem diameter growth of 2.41 mm. This suggests a stress tolerance mechanism allowing it to maintain radial growth, which is consistent with previous studies on Prunus rootstocks [46].

Chlorophyll fluorescence

Chlorophyll fluorescence analysis offers a reliable and non-invasive method for assessing photosynthetic efficiency in detached leaves under stress [47]. Chlorophyll fluorescence has been widely used to study photosynthetic activity in plants experiencing drought stress [30, 48, 49]. However, plant responses to different stresses are highly variable and depend significantly on the severity, type, and duration of the stress and the specific plant genotype [30]. This study found that while different rootstocks varied in their drought tolerance, all showed a general decline in photosynthetic efficiency when exposed to drought stress. Under normal watering conditions, the maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II in dark-adapted leaves (Qymax) was consistently around 0.80 for all rootstocks by the end of the experiment. This result aligns with findings from numerous other studies on unstressed plants [16, 30, 49]. Qymax decreased significantly as drought intensified, which is consistent with previous research [30, 49]. Qymax levels varied considerably across the different rootstocks. ‘TT’ and ‘GF677’ showed the highest values, while ‘50.10’ and ‘34.2’ had the lowest (Table 3). The differences in PSII efficiency we observed between rootstocks highlight the important role genetics plays in drought tolerance and sensitivity. Rootstocks with higher Qymax values and smaller reductions in PSII efficiency likely have inherent mechanisms that allow them to better tolerate and recover from drought stress [50, 51].

Shoot and root biomass

Our results indicate a reduction in root and shoot biomass in almond rootstocks under drought stress. This finding is consistent with findings from other researchers who evaluated rootstocks and cultivars of the Prunus genus under similar stress conditions [17, 52]. Jiménez et al. [52] demonstrated that after a 23-day period of drought stress in ‘GF677’ and ‘Adesoto’ rootstocks, there was a decrease in leaf weight, root weight, and shoot-to-root ratio compared to normally irrigated plants. In another study, ‘Nonpareil’, ‘Azar’, ‘Sangi’, and ‘Talkh’ almond cultivars were subjected to treatments with no drought stress (100% FC) and drought stress levels of 75%, 50%, and 25% FC. The results showed reduced root and shoot fresh and dry weight across all cultivars [17]. This study’s findings demonstrate that drought stress significantly impacts morphological traits such as biomass traits. Increased drought stress correlates with more severe growth inhibition, likely due to reduced turgor pressure and suppressed cell metabolism [53]. In our experiment, the morphological response to drought stress, an avoidance mechanism, was observed as a regulation of plant growth rate. As a result, shoot and root dry weight declined across the different almond cultivars [17, 54]. The consistently poor performance of the ‘50.10’ rootstock indicates its inherently low vigor compared to other rootstocks [55]. The varying growth patterns among rootstocks highlight the critical need to carefully consider the intended growing environment when making a selection, especially in regions prone to multiple environmental stresses. Rootstock adaptability can be crucial for survival under stressful conditions, as maintaining root system function is essential for water and nutrient uptake [17].

Physiological and biochemical characteristics

Our study provides a comprehensive understanding of the biochemical and physiological responses of peach-almond hybrids under drought stress. The results revealed significant changes in indicators of oxidative stress, osmotic adjustment, antioxidant enzyme activity, and mineral nutrient content, which are crucial for selecting drought-tolerant rootstocks. Our findings indicate that under FC = 40 irrigation, ‘GN15’ (46.16%) and ‘TT’ (45.79%) exhibited the highest RWC, significantly differing from the ‘50.10’ (28.06%). Differences in RWC among almond rootstocks under drought stress have already been reported [17]. RWCis frequently used as a rapid indicator of stress and a measure of a plant’s ability to maintain hydration under stress [56]. It accurately reflects the balance between water uptake and transpirational water loss [57]. RWC under stress can result from several factors: (1) decreased water availability to the roots; (2) impaired root activity and water uptake; (3) disrupted membrane stability and increased electrolyte leakage, leading to water loss; and (4) lowered soil water potential [58].

Recent comparative studies on almond and peach rootstocks under drought stress consistently emphasize the pivotal role of osmoprotectants particularly proline in enhancing drought resilience. These compounds stabilize cellular structures, maintain osmotic balance, and mitigate oxidative damage during drought stress [59–62]. In a study by Yildirim et al., an evaluation of 15 almond rootstocks under controlled drought conditions revealed a significant increase in proline, flavonoids, and phenolic compounds, alongside a reduction in total protein content in some genotypes. These findings align well with the present study, where ‘50.10’ demonstrated the highest proline accumulation under severe drought, whereas ‘TT’ and ‘GF677’ showed the lowest. This variation underscores genotype-dependent osmotic adjustment capabilities [63]. Likewise, research by Jiménez et al. on peach rootstocks, including ‘GF677’ and ROOTPAC®, illustrated that proline accumulation in roots and leaves contributed to osmotic regulation and improved water-use efficiency. These genotypes also showed elevated expression of drought-responsive genes such as P5SC and increased levels of sorbitol and raffinose, reinforcing their biochemical adaptation mechanisms [64]. The observed decrease in protein content among certain rootstocks may be attributed to protein hydrolysis, which releases stress-associated amino acids like proline [62]. This mechanism has been proposed in multiple studies as a complementary strategy for drought mitigation. Collectively, these results support the notion that proline serves not only as a biochemical marker of drought tolerance but also as a strategic trait for rootstock selection [65].

In drought-acclimated plants, certain primary metabolites (e.g., carbohydrates, amino acids, and nitrogenous compounds) play crucial protective roles. These metabolites act as osmolytes, protecting membranes and proteins, and function as ROS scavengers [66]. To counteract high ROS levels, plants depend on an effective antioxidant defense system. This system includes both non-enzymatic components (e.g., ascorbate, flavonoids, glutathione, tocopherols, and phenolics) and enzymatic components (e.g., catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), and superoxide dismutase (SOD)) [10]. Therefore, the ability of antioxidant enzymes to scavenge ROS and lessen their harmful effects is strongly associated with a plant’s drought tolerance. The present study showed a general increase in APX and CAT activity in the ‘TT’, ‘GF677’, ‘GN15’, and ‘GN2’ rootstocks under both moderate (60% FC) and severe (40% FC) drought stress compared to the control (100% FC). These results are consistent with those of Gohari et al. [16]. However, in the ‘34.2’, ‘35.1’, and ‘50.10’ rootstocks, activities of this enzyme initially increased under moderate drought (60% FC) but subsequently decreased under severe drought (40% FC). This result (i.e., a decrease in APX and CAT enzymes under severe drought stress) is in agreement with the results of Jurado-Mañogil et al. [3]. These researchers showed that in all the genotypes studied under drought stress, these enzymes decreased except for Prunus webbii, which showed an increase. This varied response in H2O2-scavenging enzyme activity in the wild almond rootstocks ‘TT’ and ‘GF677’ (which showed high POX and consistent CAT activity at 60% FC) may be related to their greater drought tolerance [3]. The results confirmed the increase in aldehydes [17] and electrolyte leakage [34] under drought stress, indicating damage to the cell wall under stress.

Mineral elements

Drought stress significantly alters nutrient dynamics in plants, affecting availability, uptake, transport, and metabolism. In this study, drought stress led to reduced concentrations of Ca and Mg in both root and leaf tissues, consistent with observations in almond cultivars by Azami et al. [34]. These reductions are likely due to decreased root volume and impaired membrane permeability, which limit cation uptake (K+, Ca2+, Mg2+) under drought stress [67, 68]. Interestingly, K levels decreased in roots but increased in leaves, a pattern already observed in drought-stressed olive cultivars [69]. This redistribution may reflect adaptive mechanisms to maintain osmotic balance and cellular function. N uptake was impaired in leaves but increased in roots, aligning with findings in grafted pear varieties under drought stress [70]. Such shifts suggest altered N allocation strategies to support root function during water deficit. P concentrations declined in both roots and shoots, consistent with previous reports on wheat [67]. However, drought-tolerant almond cultivars showed smaller reductions in leaf P, supporting the notion that P retention may be a marker of drought resilience [17]. Trace elements such as Fe, Cu, and Zn were also affected. Fe deficiency under drought stress can disrupt key physiological processes including respiration, photosynthesis, and ROS suppression [71]. Cu levels declined in leaves, impairing photosynthetic and respiratory enzyme activity [72]. Zn deficiency, known to affect chloroplast structure and photosystem II function, was also evident [73]. Figures 5 and 6 present the elemental concentrations in leaves and roots, respectively, under 40% FC drought. These data reveal significantly lower chlorine accumulation in both leaf and root tissues of rootstocks ‘TT’ and ‘GF677’ than in rootstock ‘50.10’. Moreover, ‘TT’ and ‘GF677’ exhibited higher concentrations of elements crucial for photosynthesis in both leaves and roots under drought conditions (Figs. 5 and 6). Our findings indicate that rootstock ‘50.10’ is more susceptible to drought, while ‘TT’ demonstrates greater drought tolerance.

Correlations analysis

Our results showed a strong positive correlation between protein content, electrolyte leakage, fallen leaves, necrotic leaves, and aldehydes. Antioxidant enzyme activities (CAT and APX) showed a strong positive correlation with RWC and QYmax content, consistent with the findings previously reported by Gohari et al. [16]. A strong negative correlation was noted here between RWC, protein, and rate of height increase with proline, fallen leaves, necrotic leaves, and electrolyte leakage, which is consistent with the findings of Tang [38]. Additionally, total antioxidant capacity showed a strong positive correlation with fallen leaves.

The strong positive correlation between total antioxidant capacity and fallen leaves show that fallen leaves are the result of a physiological process (senescence) that is characterized by a significant increase in antioxidant production to cope with intense oxidative stress. The high antioxidant capacity is not a cause of the leaves falling, but rather a direct result of the biochemical changes that occur as the leaves are in their final stages of life and are being prepared for shedding. The high concentration of these compounds in the fallen leaves is a measurable indicator of this underlying biological process [74]. The positive correlation between proline content and necrotic leaves shows a plant’s response to intense stress. As stress worsens, leading to more necrotic (dead) leaf tissue, the plant increases its production of proline in an attempt to protect itself from damage. Essentially, higher proline levels in conjunction with more necrosis indicate that the plant is under severe stress. While proline acts as a defense mechanism, its elevated presence alongside visible damage suggests that the stress is so overwhelming that the plant’s protective efforts are struggling to prevent tissue death. This correlation highlights the plant’s internal biochemical battle against adverse conditions [75].

Conclusions

This research aimed to identify and introduce new, adaptable, drought-tolerant rootstocks in Iran. A comprehensive study measuring morphological, physiological, and biochemical traits and the levels of 10 elements under drought stress provided a broad and reliable overview of the studied rootstocks performance under drought stress. This information can be used in stone fruit rootstock breeding programs to develop drought-tolerant rootstocks. Under severe drought stress (40% field capacity), rootstocks ‘TT’ and ‘GF677’ showed the optimal growth, photosynthetic performance, and metabolic tolerance (measured by antioxidant enzymes, proline levels, etc.). In contrast, ‘50.10’ and ‘34.2’ displayed the lowest values for these traits. The rootstocks that exhibited greater tolerance to drought stress had higher N, P, Ca, Mg, Fe, Cu, and Zn levels in their leaves, contributing to greater photosynthetic efficiency in the drought-tolerant rootstocks. Therefore, in this study, ‘TT’ is introduced as a new rootstock adapted to the Iran’s climate, along with the previously used ‘GF677’ rootstock, already identified as a potentially drought-tolerant rootstock. Overall, it is anticipated that by evaluating the pomological characteristics of these rootstocks in the future, we can introduce the first Iranian drought-tolerant stone fruit rootstocks. Our goal is to improve productivity and yields for growers through commercialization and orchard plantings.
