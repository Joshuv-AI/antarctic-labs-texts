Potent Cytotoxic Tumor-Infiltrating Lymphocytes Can Be Generated from Immune-Excluded Chondrosarcomas Using Regulatable Membrane-Bound IL15
Authors: Zheng Ao, Rusul Al-Marayaty, Bulent Arman Aksoy, Farres Obeidin, Rachel Burga, Samer Attar, Terrance Peabody, Pedro Hermida de Viveiros, Juliana Chi Kei Ng, Weiqing Jing, Himaly Shinglot, Ali Zhang, Alonso Villasmil Ocando, Nishita Roy, Gauri Kulkarni, Andres Alvarado, Dexue Sun, Dhruv K. Sethi, Michelle Ols, Jan ter Meulen, Seth M. Pollack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Generating TILs from immune‐excluded tumors like chondrosarcoma has been challenging. Engineering TILs to express regulatable, membrane‐bound IL15 enables the generation and expansion of chondrosarcoma‐derived TILs that can kill tumor cells in the absence of exogenous IL2.

Introduction

Ex vivo–expanded autologous tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) are a potent cellular therapy that can induce durable disease regression in multiple immune-inflamed solid tumor types such as melanoma or head-and-neck cancer, and they recently received FDA approval for melanoma as the first cell therapy for any solid tumor (1). However, it is currently unclear whether immunologically colder tumors will be good targets for TIL therapy, because their tumor microenvironment (TME) is often characterized by a paucity of T cells, lack of tumor antigens, defects in antigen presentation, absence of T-cell activation, and low frequencies of high-affinity tumor-reactive T-cell receptor (TCR) clonotypes (2, 3). To increase the efficacy of TIL products, different approaches have been reported, including engineering TILs with inducible secreted IL12, knocking out the immune checkpoint PD-1 or the negative regulator of cytokine signaling SOCS1, and TIL selection using surface markers or co-cultures with neoantigens prior to ex vivo expansion (4–6) However, clinically, some of these methods have had unacceptable safety issues (IL12; ref. 7) or required additional manipulation of TILs with magnetic or microfluidic enrichment steps and a prolonged expansion process that pose manufacturing problems and may lead to reduced clinical applicability (e.g., neoantigen-specific TIL selection; ref. 3). Clinical trials with PD-1 or SOCS1 knockout (KO) TILs are currently underway (8, 9).

We have previously reported a proprietary, IL2-free TIL rapid expansion process that incorporates engineering of regulatable, membrane-bound IL15 (mbIL15) and the usage of feeder cells expressing membrane-bound IL21 (mbIL21) and 4-1BB ligand. Thus, a robust expansion in a rapid expansion protocol (REP) results in a TIL product (cytoTIL15cells: cells engineered with regulatable IL15 expression) consisting predominantly (>90%) of CD8+ T cells, which are less exhausted, have a more “stem-like” phenotype, and do not require concomitant IL2 administration for expansion and engraftment of cells (10, 11). In cytoTIL15 cells, mbIL15 is regulated by a degron-based element (drug-responsive domain) that is responsive to the FDA-approved drug acetazolamide (ACZ; Diamox), and ongoing clinical trials with OBX-115 (clinical correlate of cytoTIL15 cells) have demonstrated robust regulation and promising clinical responses in patients with metastatic melanoma (12, 13).

Whereas the promise for OBX-115 in hot tumors like melanoma is encouraging, we sought to evaluate the technology in an immune-excluded tumor with a challenging TME, conventional chondrosarcoma. These are paucicellular cancers of cartilage composed almost entirely of an immunosuppressive collagenous matrix with few cellular elements, either tumor- or immune-compartment derived (14). Prior descriptions of the chondrosarcoma TME mention few infiltrating T cells, with primarily peritumor lymphocytic and occasional macrophage infiltration (15). Lymphocyte infiltration has been linked positively with prognosis, but to our knowledge, no work has been done to molecularly characterize the TME and investigate potential mechanisms of immune-cell suppression by the collagenous matrix as well as myeloid cells (16, 17). Although isolated patients with chondrosarcoma have been reported to respond to PD-1 inhibitors, the response rates are low and these tumors almost never express PD-L1 or have high levels of HLA expression (18–21). Chondrosarcomas can have immunogenic mutations and express cancer testis antigens as well as neoantigens, but rank among the lowest of all sarcoma types with respect to tumor mutational burden (TMB; refs. 22–24).

Conventional chondrosarcoma is the most common type of bone cancer in adults, affecting approximately 1 in 100,000 individuals, with no current FDA-approved therapeutic options (25, 26). Chemotherapy has little, if any, benefit, and tyrosine kinase inhibitors may grant limited periods of disease stability (27, 28). Sarcomas represent a wide range of different types of cancer, including chondrosarcoma, most of which are immunologically “cold,” with low TMB, an immunosuppressive TME, and decreased T-cell levels (29, 30). Sarcoma types can arise from different tissues, have different biological mechanisms, and have markedly different immune microenvironments reflected in the different responses that they have to cancer immunotherapy (31, 32).

To examine whether cytoTIL15 cells could be generated from a paucicellular, matrix-dominant, immune-excluded tumor, we utilized a multi-omics approach, analyzing primary tumors from 24 patients with chondrosarcoma. We identified key gene expression patterns that were associated with immune exclusion and myeloid-cell enrichment in the peripheral regions of the chondrosarcomas. We then expanded TILs from several chondrosarcoma tumors by incorporating 4-1BB agonism in pre-REP, engineered them with regulatable mbIL15, and further expanded TIL in REP with IL21 and 4-1BB ligand–engineered feeder cells. Using this process, an expansion of TIL in REP was obtained that was compatible with manufacture of a clinical dose of cytoTIL15 cells from chondrosarcoma tumors. IL15 has been shown to reduce the signaling threshold of TCRs in murine model systems, and we show here that IL15 signaling reduced the signaling threshold of a tumor-specific, low-avidity TCR clonotype, providing a possible explanation for enhanced antitumor activity of IL15-engineered TILs (33). These data suggest a path forward using cytoTIL15 cells to develop an adoptive cell therapy for patients with chondrosarcoma.

Materials and Methods

Ethics statements

All patients with sarcoma donating excess fresh tumor for research provided written consent to participate in the Institutional Review Board (IRB)-approved protocol NU21S02, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Deidentified paraffin-embedded samples were collected under a separate IRB-approved retrospective protocol NU21S01; written informed consent regarding collection of these samples was waived. Tumor tissues of patients with melanoma were sourced from Cooperative Human Tissue Network under human subject research agreement. The subjects were deidentified.

Samples and cases

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks from 24 patients with conventional chondrosarcoma were obtained from Northwestern University tissue archives. This study was approved by the Northwestern Feinberg School of Medicine IRB (protocol NU21S01). It is a retrospective study using deidentified archived tissue samples from adult patients with chondrosarcoma treated at Northwestern Medicine Hospital and performed in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Immunohistochemistry

Slides 10-μm thick were cut from FFPE blocks; subsequently, slides were stained with target immunohistochemistry (IHC)-compatible antibodies according to the protocols in Supplementary Table S1. Antibody binding was detected using chromogenic-based technique; slides were first stained by primary antibody then horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibody; positive staining was detected using light microscopy. Slides were subsequently imaged and scored using TissueGnostics microscopy system (TissueFAXS) and Histoquest scoring software, respectively. Representative images were obtained using Olympus BX53 microscope. Some slides were excluded from the final scoring analysis due to poor quality of the tissue.

IHC image acquisition and analysis

TissueFAXS (TissueGnostics GmbH) Gnostics systems, an automated digital microscopy platform, was used to scan IHC-prepared slides. Slides were first loaded into slide trays. Slides were then scanned, and the region of interest (ROI) was then identified per slide for high-power imaging. This was followed by adjusting for focus and white balance, and then high-power images were obtained. Image analysis using Histoquest scoring software version 7.1 (TissueGnostics GmbH) was done. High-power images were first uploaded to the software, tumor and peritumor regions were identified with assistance from a pathologist (F.O), and positively stained cells for specific markers were counted by dividing the total number of positively stained cells in each region by the total number of cells in that region.

DNA and RNA extraction

DNA and RNA were simultaneously extracted using AllPrep DNA/RNA FFPE Kit (Qiagen). FFPE tissue blocks from 11 selected chondrosarcoma cases were used. Tissue sections 10-μm thick were deparaffinized, dried, and lysed with proteinase K digestion. Samples were then cooled on ice and subsequently centrifuged to obtain RNA-containing supernatant and DNA-containing pellet. DNA pellet was then lysed with proteinase K+ digestion buffer and incubated at 90°C. The samples were mixed with Buffer AL (Qiagen) and ethanol (Sigma-Aldrich) to provide optimal DNA-binding conditions and then applied to a QIAamp MinElute spin column. Genomic DNA binds specifically to the silica membrane and was washed with Buffer AW1, Buffer AW2 (Qiagen), and ethanol to remove contaminants. Pure DNA was then eluted in 30 to 100 μL of Buffer ATE and filtered through a genomic DNA–binding filter. The RNA supernatant was incubated at 80°C and mixed with Buffer RLT (Qiagen) and ethanol to provide appropriate binding conditions for RNA. The sample was then applied to an RNeasy MinElute spin column, in which RNA binds specifically to the silica membrane. The bound RNA was treated with DNase to digest contaminating genomic DNA, washed with Buffer FRN (Qiagen) and Buffer RPE (Qiagen) to remove contaminants, and then eluted in 14 to 30 μL of RNase-free water.

Gene expression of bulk RNA

RNA was extracted from FFPE tissues from 11 selected chondrosarcoma cases and analyzed using a custom 770-gene (NanoString PanCancer IO 360 Panel) comprising immune-related genes and genes pertaining to common cancer signaling pathways. As input, we used 100 ng total RNA following the manufacturer's instructions. After the CodeSet hybridization, the samples were washed and loaded on the cartridge within the Prep Station and subsequently analyzed with the nCounter Digital Analyzer and then Q3-normalized.

For comparison of gene expression levels with other soft-tissue sarcomas, bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) using NanoString panel was done on RNA lysate obtained from FFPE bocks of 24 patients with liposarcoma, 18 leiomyosarcoma, 20 pleomorphic sarcoma, and 15 synovial sarcoma. Per sample, 50 ng of RNA in a volume of 5 μL were mixed with 3′ capture probe and 5′ reporter probe, hybridized, run, and then analyzed with the nCounter Digital Analyzer and further normalized and used for comparison.

For differential gene expression analyses (DGEA), raw gene count data were imported, loaded, and modeled via tidyverse (v1.3.2), edgeR (version 3.40.0), and limma (version 3.54.0). Grade (grade 3 vs. lower grades), recurrence versus primary disease, and central versus conventional disease were the only clinical properties with more than two patients in one group and that allowed us to run DGEA. P values from the DGEA analysis were adjusted with false discovery rate (FDR) for multiple hypothesis testing.

For all cases in which gene expression and IHC-based metrics were available, Q3-normalized gene-level counts were used to calculate Pearson correlation between IHC staining metrics and log10-transformed normalized gene counts across all genes and IHC stains, which was facilitated via tidymodels (v1.0.0).

TCR sequencing

Extracted DNA and RNA from 11 selected chondrosarcoma cases of the original 24 cases, five of which had a second round of RNA and DNA extraction by additionally punching the tumor and peritumor regions (including lymphatic clusters in the peritumor and adjacent peritumor region), were used for TCR sequencing (Cellecta, Inc). In summary, for DNA TCR sequencing, DNA (1.5–8 μg in 120 μL) was extended by adding 20 μL of master mix with pool of reverse AIR TCR gene-specific primers (GSP), incubated at 98°C, 1 minute, 68°C, 10 minutes, and treated with 2 μL of primer removal enzyme mix, at 37°C, 20 minutes, 95°C, 5 minutes. Rev GSP-extended DNA product (in 120 μL) was extended by adding 6 μL of pool of forward AIR TCR GSPs, incubated at 98°C, 1 minute, 68°C, 10 minutes, and treated with 2 μL of primer removal enzyme mix, at 37°C, 20 minutes, 95°C, 5 minutes. FwdGSP-extended DNA was diluted in PCR master mix (100 μL), and anchored cDNA fragments were amplified in 2 × 110 μL of Multiplex DNA polymerase reaction mix with universal anchor PCR primers for 24 cycles: (98°C, 20 seconds, 65°C, 10 seconds, 72°C, 30 seconds). Two-μL aliquots of the first PCR were added in 50 μL of master mix for the second PCR step (each tube has unique dual-index primers) and amplified using unique combination of Fwd-P5-Index and Rev-P7-Index for 8 to 11 cycles, treated with primer removal enzyme mix (1 μL) at 37°C for 30 minutes. PCR products were analyzed in agarose gel and fragment analyzer, combined at equal amount if possible based on fragment analyzer smear analysis data (180–450 bp range), and purified using AMPpure magnetic beads (0.6×-1.5× volume). The purified cDNA products were quantitated by Qubit fluorescence measurement and diluted to 10 nmol/L (1.6 ng/μL) for next-generation sequencing (NGS) using NextSeq500.

For RNA TCR sequencing, total RNA (1,000 ng of each sample) was incubated with mix of reverse mouse AIR TCR GSPs in 20 μL of 1× hybridization buffer at 70°C, 5 minutes, 60°C for 60 minutes, cool down to 25°C. Hybridized RNA-RevGSP products were purified with 1.4× volume (28 μL) of SPRIselect beads, and beads were washed 2× with 80% ethanol. The washed magnetic beads were resuspended in 23 μL of 1×RT-extension master mix, and 20.5 μL of master mix solution without beads was incubated at 50°C for 30 minutes and 72°C for 10 minutes. cDNA product (in 20 μL) was extended by adding 20 μL of master mix with pool of forward AIR TCR GSPs, incubated at 98°C, 1 minute, 68°C, 10 minutes, and treated with 2 μL of primer removal enzyme mix, at 37°C, 30 minutes, 95°C, 5 minutes. FwdGSP-extended cDNA was diluted in PCR master mix (60 μL), and anchored cDNA fragments were amplified in 100 μL of Multiplex DNA polymerase reaction mix with universal anchor PCR primers for 21 cycles: (98°C, 20 seconds, 65°C, 10 seconds, 72°C, 30 seconds). Two-μL aliquots of the first PCR were added in 50 μL of master mix for the second PCR step (each tube has unique dual-index primers) and amplified using unique combination of Fwd-P5-Index and Rev-P7-Index for 8 to 11 cycles, treated with primer removal enzyme mix (1 μL) at 37°C for 30 minutes. PCR products were analyzed in fragment analyzer, combined at equal amount if possible (for samples for samples 2, 3, 4, and 5 whole volume of the second PCR reaction) based on fragment analyzer smear analysis data (250–450 bp range), and purified using AMPpure magnetic beads (1.5× volume). The purified cDNA products were quantitated by Qubit fluorescence measurement and diluted to 10 nmol/L (1.6 ng/μL) for NGS using NextSeq500.

Sequencing data were then processed with MiXCR (version 3.4.0) via either “cellecta-human-dna-xcr-umi-drivermap-air” or “cellecta-human-rna-xcr-umi-drivermap-air” workflows depending on the source of the material (DNA or RNA, respectively). MAIT MATCH 1.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/MAIT_Match-1.0/) was accessed on February 05, 2024 to score TCRA CRD3 sequences against known mucosal-associated invariant T cell (MAIT) sequences. All sequences with a hit score higher than 0.9 were labeled to be originating from a MAIT cell clonotype. When checking for overlaps across cases, exact TCRA CDR3 sequences were used to look for matches.

Digital spatial profiling

To perform GeoMx digital spatial profiling (DSP), human chondrosarcoma tissue slides were baked at 60°C for 1 hour, followed by deparaffinization, rehydration, and digestion with proteinase K on the Leica BOND-RX autostainer. Subsequently, the slides underwent in situ hybridization with the GeoMx Cancer Transcriptome Atlas (human RNA probes for NGS; cat. #GMX-RNA-NGS-CTA-4) overnight at 37°C. After stringent washes with 2× SSC plus 50% formamide, the slides was blocked with buffer R (NanoString) and stained with morphology markers, including anti-S100b (Abcam, cat. #ab196175, RRID: AB_2868562), anti-CD3 (Novus, cat. #NBP2-54392AF647, RRID: AB_3095415), anti-CD68 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, cat. #sc-20060, RRID: AB_627158), and syto13 (nuclear marker, NanoString, cat. #121300303). The stained slide was subsequently scanned on the GeoMx-NGS DSP instrument, and 68 ROIs were selected from different cases based on the expression level of S100b, CD3, and CD68. The selected ROIs underwent auto-segmentation using S100b positivity (for tumor segment), CD3 positivity (as a marker for T cells), and CD68 positivity (as a marker of macrophages). The oligo probes on tumor and other cell segments (CD3 and CD68) of the ROIs were separately cleaved by UV light and aspirated into individual plate wells with the collection tip. The collected probes from each well were used to generate the NGS library for RNA-seq following the manufacturer’s instruction (GeoMx, NanoString, Bruker Cellular Analysis); in concept, the probes were hybridized to GeoMx seq code or Pro Code primer and then amplified using PCR. PCR products were then purified and sequenced on the Illumina platform. Generated FASTQ files were then run in the GeoMx NGS pipeline software in which FASTQ files were converted to output DCC files, which were then uploaded to the GeoMx DSP for analysis and further extraction of raw and normalized gene expression data. Downstream analysis was done as below.

DGEA of the GeoMX sequencing data was performed using edgeR, limma, and tidyverse utilities. The comparisons were carried out between tumor and peritumor regions across all patients using annotations provided by the operator. Each case had multiple regions captured via GeoMX, and each region was treated as a separate sample originating from the case. The gene expression modeling incorporated case-specific effects together with the tumor and peritumor annotations but ignored auto-segmentation labels. The P values from the DGEA were adjusted via FDR to account for multiple hypotheses testing.

TIL TCR sequencing

Five-million cryopreserved REP TILs from the NW-001 case were submitted to iRepertoire for TCR sequencing. RNA extraction, RepSeq (ARM-PCR based TCR amplification) processing from RNA, and Illumina NGS sequencing using MiSeq Reagent Nano Kit version 2 (500-cycle) were performed by the vendor. The TCR α and β chain sequencing results as processed by the vendor’s Atlas-based workflow was used for comparisons to other TCR sequencing data sets.

Single-cell transcriptomic sequencing

Five-million cryopreserved pre-REP T cells from a sarcoma donor (NW-008) and a melanoma donor (89164T-006) were submitted to Azenta (previously GeneWiz) for single-cell transcriptome sequencing on the vendor’s 10× Genomics ChromiumTM T-cell GEX RNA-seq platform as individual samples. Cells were prepared for single-cell sequencing by the vendor’s standard workflow (https://www.genewiz.com/public/services/next-generation-sequencing/single-cell-rna-seq). The target number of cells to be captured was set at 10,000, and the target number of reads per cell was set at 100,000.

Single-cell TCR sequencing

Five-million cryopreserved REP TIL from the NW-001 case were submitted to Azenta (previously GeneWiz) for single-cell TCR sequencing on the vendor’s 10× Genomics ChromiumTM T-cell V(D)J RNA-seq platform. Cells were prepared for single-cell sequencing by the vendor’s standard workflow (https://www.genewiz.com/Public/Services/Next-Generation-Sequencing/Immunogenomics/Immuno-Profiling). The target number of cells to be captured was set at 3,000, and the target number of reads per cell was set at 10,000.

CD8+ TCR KO NFAT-luciferase Jurkat reporter assay

CD8+ TCR KO NFAT-luciferase reporter Jurkat cell line (BPS Bioscience, cat. #78757) was purchased from BPS Bioscience in 2024. The reporter Jurkat cell line was cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (ATCC modification) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco) and was engineered between passage 5 and passage 10. Routine Mycoplasma testing was performed in-house using a Mycoplasma testing kit (InvivoGen). Lentivirus expressing the TCR sequences from paired single-cell TCR sequencing were packaged using TransIT Lentivirus System (mirus bio) using HEK293T cells (ATCC). The reporter cell lines were then transduced by spin loading the cells with packaged lentivirus particles at 500g for 1 hour at room temperature. Transduced cells were sorted using SONY MA900 based on CD3 expression. Autologous tumor cells were plated into a 96-well plate and stimulated with recombinant human IFNγ (Peprotech) overnight. Then control peptides including HIV Nef protein epitope (RFPLTFGWCF; GenScript) and Dengue virus NS protein epitope (GTSGSPIVNR; GenScript) were pulsed for 1 hour before loading the reporter Jurkat cells. The Jurkat cells and tumor cell lines were then co-cultured for 16 hours before analysis using the BPS Bioscience One-Step luciferase system (#60690-1).

TIL expansion

To generate TILs, primary tumor tissues were put into a two-step expansion process as previously described (11) Briefly, the pre-REP step involves fragmentation of the tissue into 1- to 3-mm pieces, followed by culture in RPMI-1640 media (Gibco) supplemented with high-dose IL2 (6,000 IU/mL; Miltenyi), 10% human AB serum (BioIVT) (conventional process), and 55 µmol/L of 2-mercaptoethanol (Gibco) or pre-REP process with anti-CD3 (30 ng/mL, RRID: AB_2904535) and 4-1BB agonist antibody (1 µg/mL, RRID: AB_3111852) for 14 to 21 days as described previously (11). The pre-REP TILs were then cryopreserved. To initiate the REP phase, pre-REP TIL were thawed, activated by anti-CD3 (RRID: AB_2904535) (Miltenyi), and transduced with constructs to express either constitutive mbIL15 or mbIL15 under the regulation of a carbonic anhydrase (CA2) drug-responsive domain (DRD). Vector sequences are disclosed in patent literature (US20220133801A1 and WO2023141436A1). The cells were expanded using either irradiated peripheral blood mononuclear cells (iPBMC) for nonengineered TIL controls (1:200 ratio) or iFeeders expressing 41BBL and IL21 (1:10 ratio), as described previously (11).

Flow cytometry

To profile the TIL throughout the pre-REP and REP process by flow cytometry, TILs were obtained and stained either fresh or from cryopreserved samples, blocked by Fc block (BioLegend), followed by surface staining. The cells were then fixed using either Cytofix (BD Biosciences) or Cytofix/Cytoperm (BD Biosciences) if followed by intracellular staining. For nuclear transcription factor staining (Foxp3), the cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Foxp3 fixation/permeabilization kit (Invitrogen). Antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table S2. Samples were acquired on BD symphony flow cytometers. Data were analyzed with FlowJo version 10 software. Gating strategy illustrated in details in Supplementary Figs. S1–S4.

Polyfunctionality assay

To profile the polyfunctionality of the TILs, TILs were incubated in media, with anti-CD3/CD28 stimulation (ImmunoCult, Stemcell Technologies, cat. #100-0784) or with PMA/ionomycin (Cell Stimulation Cocktaikm Invitrogen, cat. #00-4970-03) for 6 hours. Brefeldin A (BioLegend) and monensin (BD Biosciences) were added 1 hour into the incubation to block protein transport. After the 6-hour stimulation, cells were stained with surface staining then fix/permeabilized for intracellular staining for flow cytometric immune phenotyping and intercellular cytokine analysis (Supplementary Table S2).

Phospho-ERK assay

cytoTIL15 cells were rested overnight at 1.5 × 106 cells/mL in TIL complete media—RPMI-1640 media (Gibco) supplemented with 10% human AB serum (BioIVT) and 55 µmol/L of 2-mercaptoethanol (Gibco)—supplemented with 0, 1, or 25 µmol/L ACZ (Hikma). The following day, cytoTIL15 cells were harvested, washed in plain RPMI media (Gibco), and then resuspended in plain RPMI media with 0, 1, or 25 µmol/L ACZ. ACZ-conditioned cytoTIL15 cells were TCR-stimulated on 24-well plates coated with 1 μg/mL anti-CD3 (OKT3 clone; Miltenyi, RRID: AB_2904535) for 10 minutes at 37°C. After 10 minutes, cells were fixed with BD Cytofix, permeabilized with BD Permeabilization Buffer III, and then stained for lineage markers (Supplementary Table S2; Supplementary Fig. S4) and phosphorylated ERK (pT202/pY204; BD, RRID: AB_10895978).

Patient-derived cell line development

To generate autologous patient-derived cell lines (PDc), unused fragments from pre-REP were digested using a tumor dissociation kit (Miltenyi) using a gentleMACS tissue dissociator. An aliquot of the tumor digests (1–2 × 106 cells) was then plated into a collagen-coated plate to allow cell adherence in MesenCult-hPL media (Stemcell Technologies). When reaching confluence, cells were passaged using Trypsin-EDTA (0.25%; Gibco). Tumor cell identity was validated by immunofluorescence by SOX9 staining (Cell Signaling Technology, cat. #82630T). Samples were imaged using Olympus iX83 microscope using 20x objective lens. PDc was validated as Mycoplasma-free using the Mycoplasma testing kit (InvivoGen). PDc tumor original was validated using WES sequencing to confirm whether tumor-related nongermline mutation is present.

3D tumor spheroid modeling

To generate 3D tumor spheroids from autologous PDc lines, tumor cells were harvested and resuspended in MesenCult hPL media supplemented with 10 µmol/L Y-27632 (Stemcell Technologies, cat. #72307). The cells were then plated in AggreWell 400-microwell culture plates to form spheroids containing 1,500 cells per spheroid (Stemcell Technologies) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The spheroids were then resuspended gently using a 10-mL transfer pipette 24 hours after seeding and reseeded into a 96-well ultra-low attachment PhenoPlate (Revvity) at 40 spheroids per well. To set up co-culture with TILs, TILs were counted and resuspended in TIL media (RPMI-1640 + 10% human serum +50 µmol/L 2-Mercaptoethanol) and added to the spheroids with final co-culture media at 50% MesenCult hPL and 50% TIL media. Cell death was visualized by Incucyte caspase-3/7 dye (Sartorius). To visualize TIL infiltration, TILs were first stained with CellTracker Deep Red Dye (Invitrogen) for 20 minutes before they were washed, counted, and added to the co-culture.

Tissue clearing and immunofluorescence staining

Tumor spheroids and TIL co-cultures were harvested at 24 hours and immediately fixed with 10% formalin. For whole-mount staining, tumor spheroids were stained with anti-CD3 (Miltenyi, cat. #REA613) and tissue clearing using Invitrogen CytoVista 3D culture tissue clearing kit following the manufacture’s manual. The stained tumor spheroids were then imaged at Nikon Bioimaging Lab using Nikon AX-R NSPARC confocal system equipped with NIS-Elements software (v5.42.03). Data were visualized and analyzed using Imaris 10.2.0.

Computational analyses

The codes supporting all custom analyses are available at https://github.com/obsidiantx/sarcoma-manuscript and organized as individual Jupyter notebooks (version 1.0.0) with names consistent with the corresponding theme. In short, all analyses were conducted in R Programming Language (version 4.2.2). Standard data extraction, transformation, and loading tasks were completed through tidyverse family packages (version 1.3.2). Single-cell sequencing analysis were done through utilities provided by Seurat (version 4.3.0), bulk sequencing analyses (including but not limited to DGEA) were done through limma (version 3.54.0) and edgeR (version 3.40.0). Summaries of modeling and testing outcomes were conducted via broom (version 1.0.2) and tidymodels (version 1.0.0). Detailed list of R packages used is available under https://github.com/obsidiantx/sarcoma-manuscript/blob/main/analyses/Packages%20and%20environment.ipynb.

Reanalysis of previously published data sets

For comparison with other sarcoma types, we accessed a previously published gene expression data set that was generated through the NanoString platform and spans a cohort of 18 leiomyosarcoma, 24 liposarcoma, 15 synovial sarcoma, and 18 undifferentiated sarcoma cases (29). We used the raw estimated gene counts to combine it with the gene expression data generated for this study (consisting of 11 chondrosarcoma cases). We have used well-known immune marker genes (CD3E, CD4, CD68, CD84, IL2RB, KLRK1, LILRA3, LGALS9, LITB, NCAM1, SDGA1, SIGLEC1, TGFBR2, TNF, and VCAM1) to look for relative upregulation of any of these in comparison in chondrosarcoma cohort compared with any other subtypes (based on the average expression). This additional data set and the analysis details are also shared on the code repository (https://github.com/obsidiantx/sarcoma-manuscript/blob/main/analyses/Other%20sarcomas.ipynb).

Results

Chondrosarcoma patient selection

To study the TME of chondrosarcoma, 24 patients diagnosed between 2006 and 2022 with grades 2 and 3 conventional chondrosarcomas were selected. The mean age at diagnosis was 56 years (range, 41–103). The majority of the cases were located in the extremities (58.3%), with fewer patients presenting with chondrosarcoma in the chest, pelvis, or back. At the time of resection, a negative tumor margin (>0.1 cm) was achieved in 58.3% of the cases and <0.1 cm in 20.8% of cases, whereas the rest of the cases showed positive margin (12.5%). Nonetheless, 50% of cases developed recurrence in 3.2 years (SD 3.7) and 41% of cases developed distant metastasis in 3.6 years (SD 4). Patients had a median overall survival (OS) of 3.2 years (95% confidence interval, 1.6–8.2). Cases that were diagnosed recently and did not develop metastasis or recurrence were censored at the last follow-up date, which could potentially underestimate the relapse-free survival, metastasis-free survival, and OS (Table 1).

TME profiling of chondrosarcoma tumors

The TME of conventional chondrosarcomas has not been characterized in depth using molecular techniques. Therefore, we used IHC (n = 24), and, on samples with good RNA yield/quality, nCounter gene expression analysis (n = 11), TCR sequencing (n = 5), and spatial transcriptomics (GeoMx; n = 4) analysis on a cohort of chondrosarcoma tumors (Fig. 1A). First, we ran tissue scrolls obtained from 11 patients on NanoString nCounter panel. We found an inflammatory gene signature when we compared it with other types of sarcoma (leiomyosarcoma, synovial sarcomas, dedifferentiated liposarcomas, and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcomas; Supplementary Fig. S5; ref. 29). Because these tumor samples included both tumor and the peritumor area, we sought to compare immune-cell infiltration in peritumor versus tumor areas, as previous literature suggests that chondrosarcoma tumors possess a dense extracellular matrix that drives immune exclusion (17). To assess this, we performed DSP using DSP GeoMx NanoString technology targeting three different cell types [CD3+ (T cells), CD68+ (macrophages) and S100+ (tumor cells)], which allowed for both spatial and gene expression discovery at the same time (Fig. 1B). An enrichment of CD3+ and CD68+ cells in the peritumor regions with very low infiltrate of those cells into the tumor region was seen; furthermore, a high enrichment of immune cell lineage marker–related genes (CD3E, CD8A, CD68, CD4, and NCAM1) as well as immune checkpoint–related genes (ENTPD1, PDCD1, and LAG3) in the peritumor region was also detected (Fig. 1C; Supplementary Fig. S6). Additionally, when we punched tumor and peritumor regions containing immune clusters and analyzed the T-cell clonotypes by TCR sequencing, we detected a higher diversity of TCR (unique CDR3s) in the peritumor region, especially in the immune clusters of peritumor regions, as compared with intratumor regions (Fig. 1D). To further confirm this observation, we ran comprehensive IHC staining with an extended panel, including CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8, CD20, CD14, CD68, CD206, CD56, and MECA-79. With all immune-related markers, we saw a numerically higher level of immune-cell infiltrate in the peritumor regions compared with intratumor regions (Fig. 2A). Our prior GeoMx and IHC images revealed the presence of immune-cell clusters at the peritumor regions residing just outside the tumor areas. To test the level of organization within those immune-cell clusters and determine whether they represent tertiary lymphoid structures, we performed IHC analysis of MECA-79 as a marker of high endothelial venules, which have a critical role in lymphocyte homing and signify high level of lymphoid organization. We found high expression of MECA-79 within the peritumor region but not within the tumor (Supplementary Fig. S7). We also ran a correlation analysis between IHC infiltration score and the NanoString gene signature dataset. This analysis revealed a high correlation between regions containing CD45+ immune cells with immune related-genes such as CD2 and PDCD1. Conversely, there was a negative correlation of antigen-presentation pathway genes such as TAPBP with NK-cell (CD56+) infiltration (Supplementary Fig. S8). We also ran a correlation analysis on genes associated with CD8+ intratumor vs peritumor infiltration ratio to determine what drives an intratumor infiltration vs an immune-excluded phenotype. We found expression of tumor-associated antigens such as MAGEC1, MAGEB2, and MAGEC2 correlated with intratumor CD8+ T-cell infiltration as well as expression of the immune checkpoint PVR, whereas elevated expression of fibrotic and extracellular matrix regulators (CDH2, CDH11, and GPC4) corresponded to a higher peritumor ratio, indicating immune exclusion (Fig. 2B). MAGEC1 showed a strong correlation with intratumor CD8+ T-cell infiltration (Fig. 2C, Pearson r = 0.8714, P = 0.0005), indicating the MAGE cancer testis antigens could be immunogenic in chondrosarcoma.

TIL generation from chondrosarcoma donors

TIL generation has been reported from highly immune-infiltrated “hot” tumors, such as melanoma and non–small cell lung cancer, and used as a cell therapy product that has resulted in durable responses in some patients (34, 35). However, TIL generation from immunologically “cold” tumors has been challenging and has resulted in limited manufacturing success and is often associated with limited clinical responses (36). A few recent studies attempting TIL generation from sarcoma tumors have reported a relatively low success rate and low TIL yield (37, 38), although none of these studies included chondrosarcoma. It has recently been reported that adding a 4-1BB agonist to a TIL pre-REP process can increase the reliability of TIL expansion and create a desirable TIL phenotype (39). To study whether TIL could be generated from sarcoma using an improved pre-REP process with a 4-1BB agonist, especially the immune-excluded, cartilaginous, and paucicellular chondrosarcoma tumors, we evaluated TIL generation with and without mbIL15 engineering using samples from six sarcoma tumors, including three chondrosarcomas. Despite the low immune infiltration in the initial sarcoma tumors as compared with other immunologically “hot” tumors such as melanoma and non–small cell lung cancer, we observed reduced yet manufacturing-relevant pre-REP yield from sarcoma tumors (Fig. 3A). We compared a pre-REP process containing the 4-1BB agonist antibody against a more conventional pre-REP process (supported only by high-dose IL2) using tumors from three donors and observed a significant increase in pre-REP yield with the 4-1BB agonist antibody process (Fig. 3B).

We further characterized our pre-REP TIL products by flow cytometry. Our pre-REP process enriched for CD8+ T cells, whereas a variable CD4+/CD8+ ratio was observed in the conventional pre-REP TIL (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, we compared the pre-REP TIL transcriptome by single-cell RNA-seq. Compared with melanoma pre-REP TIL, chondrosarcoma pre-REP TIL showed similar cell division profiles by cell cycle and Ki67 expression (MKI67) and similar effector molecule expression, including a high frequency of granzyme B (GZMB) and granulysin (GNLY; Fig. 3D).

Following pre-REP, TILs were put into our previously described REP process for further expansion (11). Briefly, pre-REP TILs were virally transduced to express either constitutive or regulatable mbIL15 and expanded with irradiated K562 feeder cells engineered to express 4-1BBL and mbIL21. REPs of TIL engineered with regulatable mbIL15 contained ACZ to drive expression of the mbIL15 controlled by the CA2 DRD. REPs of TILS engineered with constitutive mbIL15 did not contain IL2 or ACZ. Pre-REP TIL from all three chondrosarcoma donors expanded well (Fig. 4A) and enriched for IL15 expression (Fig. 4B). TILs expressing regulatable mbIL15 (cytoTIL15 cells) showed ACZ dose-dependent expression of mbIL15 (Fig. 4C). As controls, pre-REP TILs from the same donor were also expanded with irradiated PBMC feeder cells and high-dose IL2, referred to as nonengineered TILs. Both nonengineered TIL and cytoTIL15 cells showed a predominant effector memory phenotype (Tem, CD45RA–CD45RO+CCR7–CD62L–) with an ∼20% Temra (CD45RA+CD45RO–CCR7–CD62L–) population seen in the first donor (Fig. 4D). We have also profiled the REP TIL with more in-depth activation/exhaustion markers and found that cytoTIL15 cells showed a trend toward higher activation marker expression, including CD25 and CD27, and a lower PD-1 expression indicating lower exhaustion profile (Fig. 4E; Supplementary Fig. S9). We then profiled the polyfunctionality of the TILs by stimulating them in vitro with either anti-CD3/anti-CD28 or PMA/ionomycin, followed by transport inhibitor treatment and intracellular staining of CD107a, IFN-γ, TNF-α, granzyme B, perforin, and IL2. The TILs treated with activation reagents showed high polyfunctionality across all donors (Fig. 4F). Additionally, we profiled the TCR V beta repertoire via flow cytometry. Both donor 1 and donor 3 showed a skewed TCR V beta usage, whereas donor 1 showed a 68% TIL usage of the V beta 11 (TRBV25-1), and donor 3 showed a 76% TIL usage of the V beta 2 (TRBV20-1; Fig. 4G).

Cytotoxicity of chondrosarcoma TILs against autologous tumor spheroids

To test the functionality of the REP TIL, we first set up 2D co-culture of the TIL against autologous tumor cell lines. Notably, we observed greater killing of autologous tumor cell lines by cytoTIL15 cells than with the nonengineered TIL in all three donors tested (statistically significant for two donors). The killing was ACZ-dependent, which indicated a role for mbIL15 in enhancing TIL cytotoxicity (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Fig. S10; Supplementary Video S1). Upon analyzing supernatants of the co-culture, we also observed increased IFNγ secretion that was MHC-dependent in two of the three donors (Fig. 5B).

To further understand the increased tumor recognition seen following mbIL15 engineering, we tested their cytotoxicity in a 3D spheroid co-culture model. Tumor spheroid models provide the benefit of studying TIL–tumor interaction in a more physiologically relevant setting that includes hypoxic cores and extracellular matrix barriers, which are particularly important for matrix-rich tumors like chondrosarcoma (40). Additionally, spheroid models also allow for observation of TIL infiltration, which is a critical step for the success of adoptive-cell therapy in solid tumors. Using spheroid models, we identified a greater impact of mbIL15 engineering on TIL cytotoxicity compared with the 2D model (Fig. 5C). To see whether the increased killing observed in 3D models could be attributed to increased infiltration and function of cytoTIL15 cells, we imaged the TIL infiltration process during time lapse and quantified TIL infiltration over time. Indeed, cytoTIL15 cells infiltrated the autologous tumor spheroids more effectively than nonengineered TILs (Fig. 5D; Supplementary Video S2). We then imaged the tumor spheroids after 24-hour co-culture via whole mount staining of tumor marker SOX9, TIL marker CD3, and effector molecule granzyme B (Fig. 5E; Supplementary Video S3). We then quantified the infiltration depth of cytoTIL15 cells and nonengineered TILs. The cytoTIL15 cells not only infiltrated deeper into the autologous tumor spheroids but also expressed granzyme B deeper in the spheroids (Fig. 5F).

IL15 induced lowering of the TCR signaling threshold

Given the skewed TCR repertoire observed in two of the three donors’ REP TIL we profiled, we ran further profiling of the TCR repertoire of the first donor (donor 1) by single-cell TCR sequencing. This led to identification of three dominant TCR sequences, each constituting ∼30% of the REP TIL TCR repertoire, with two of them sharing the usage of TRBV11-3*01 (Fig. 6B). We then engineered these three paired TCR V alpha and V beta sequences into a CD8+ TCR KO NFAT-luciferase reporter Jurkat cell line to study the TCR reactivity against autologous tumor cell lines. Previously, it has been reported that IL15 could lower the TCR signaling threshold through phosphorylation of ERK (Fig. 6A; ref. 33). Hence, we included a dose titration of soluble IL15 in our co-culture assays. C1-28 TCR was used as control as previously reported (33, 41). Although two of the dominant TCRs did not show a luciferase signal in this assay, indicating non–tumor specificity, the third showed signaling only in the presence of soluble IL15 and in an IL15 dose-dependent manner (Fig. 6C). This indicated that whereas this TCR clone was tumor antigen–specific, the presumably very low TCR avidity required soluble exogenous IL15 to trigger signaling downstream of the TCR. As IL15-induced TCR sensitization has been reported in previous literature through phosphorylation of ERK (33), we investigated whether IL15 signaling in cytoTIL15 cells could also induce ERK phosphorylation. By providing cytoTIL15 cells with 0, 1, and 25 µmol/L of ACZ, we did observe an IL15-dependent phosphorylation of ERK in the presence of TCR stimulation provided by anti-CD3 (Fig. 6D).

Discussion

The FDA recently approved the tumor-derived autologous T-cell immunotherapy (TIL cell therapy) lifileucel (Amtagvi) as the first-ever cell therapy product for solid tumors, in this case metastatic melanoma (1). Lifileucel, and all nonengineered TIL cell therapies, requires concomitant administration of potentially toxic, high-dose recombinant IL2 for expansion, engraftment, and antitumor activity of the TIL. To avoid these toxicities, we have developed an engineered TIL-expressing regulatable mbIL15, which has shown promising safety, efficacy, and persistence in a phase I trial in patients with metastatic melanoma resistant to immune checkpoint inhibitors (12).

Although attempts to treat other tumors with an immune-inflamed microenvironment, such as head-and-neck and cervical cancers, with TILs have been somewhat successful, developing a TIL strategy for treating sarcoma has met many challenges (42, 43). Some subtypes, such as undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, do have T-cell infiltration and PD-L1 expression and have a 20% response rate to PD-1 inhibitors, so they may be good candidates for TIL cell therapy (19, 29). Others, like synovial sarcoma, are capable of responding to engineered T-cell products but have such an immune-suppressive TME that they lack infiltrating T cells almost completely and virtually never respond to PD-1 inhibitors (44, 45). TILs have been conventionally manufactured from sarcomas generally without a focus on specific subtypes. Whereas preclinical studies demonstrated feasibilities to manufacture TIL from soft tissue sarcomas, only 40% to 56% of the TIL preparations were reactive when tested against autologous tumor material (37). Clinically, one study treated four patients with some of the least immunogenic sarcoma subtypes with TILs following injection of a stimulatory peptide and did not see RECIST responses (46). Another study, also enrolling predominantly less immunogenic subtypes, treated eight patients without responses (47). Still, many investigators believe that TIL cell therapy may be effective in more immunogenic subtypes such as undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, myxofibrosarcoma, or alveolar soft part sarcoma.

Chondrosarcoma is chemotherapy-resistant and has no FDA-approved therapies, although tyrosine kinase inhibitors may temporarily stabilize disease and PD-1 inhibitors may have a response rates in the 10% range (28, 31, 48). We hypothesized that chondrosarcoma would qualify as the prototypical immune-excluded tumor and confirmed this through the most extensive characterization of the chondrosarcoma TME performed to date, demonstrating infiltration in the peritumor area with nearly every cell type examined, but with very few of these cells infiltrating the tumor stroma itself. The rare but impressive responses that have been observed suggest that this rare tumor might be an ideal sarcoma type to explore whether TIL cell therapy could be effective in an immune-excluded tumor (20). Furthermore, of the rare T cells that were able to infiltrate the tumor, a high proportion of these were MAIT cells, and chondrosarcoma tumors frequently express the noncanonical peptide loading complex, MR1, which can present antigen to MAIT cells (49).

Despite the truly “immune-excluded” phenotype for these tumors, which have significantly lower levels of T cells in comparison with other tumors and are mainly composed of an immunosuppressive collagenous matrix rather than tumor cells, we were consistently able to generate TIL products with cytokine release at similar levels: 255 to 3916 pg/mL IFNγ secretion comparable with those generated in melanoma and non–small cell lung cancer–derived TILs and cytotoxicity against autologous patient-derived cells and tumor spheroids (50). Furthermore, the cytoTIL15 cells expansion process incorporating IL15 engineering and IL21 and 41BBL supported REP considerably increased the efficacy of these products (1.9 ± 0.74-fold increase of area under curve in cytotoxicity assays), presumably also by enhancing the killing ability of lower-avidity TCR-expressing clones, as demonstrated for one TCR clonotype that we investigated experimentally. These TIL products showed oligoclonal TCR repertoire, had a favorable memory phenotype, and were polyfunctional with regard to cytokine production.

Although the data demonstrate the importance of the peri-tumor area for chondrosarcoma, they raise interesting questions about its importance in all cancers. Currently, when tumors are not being resected and a biopsy is performed to collect tumor for TIL product generation, collection of tumor stroma is often considered critically important. Based on these data, we would propose sampling of tumor periphery for any tumor thought to be immune-excluded, including immune-excluded variants of tumors considered to be generally highly immune-infiltrated, such as melanoma (51).

The finding that chondrosarcoma tumors nearly always express MR1 and have infiltrating MAIT cells raises questions regarding what specific antigen might be triggering this MAIT response or whether TCR-independent mechanisms are responsible for it (52), and warrants further exploration. Although other tumors (hepatocellular carcinoma, lung cancer, gastrointestinal cancers, and others) are known to have MAIT infiltrations, these are generally tumors of mucosal surfaces, with high levels of MAIT in the surrounding normal tissues, so this is an unusual finding. It would be of interest to determine whether the intratumor MAIT cells have an immune-suppressive phenotype, perhaps contributing to the immune-excluded phenotype of chondrosarcoma (53). Furthermore, these findings suggest there may be factors allowing for increased trafficking of these unique T cells, and this is also an important topic for further exploration.

Although TILs could consistently be generated from each sample, the cytoTIL15 cells allowed for superior yield and a more potent product, both with respect to cytokine production and tumor killing. These cytoTIL15 cells had a trend toward higher expression of memory markers such as CD25 and CD27 and lower expression of the exhaustion marker and checkpoint PD-1. These data demonstrate that whereas TIL production may generally be feasible for immune-excluded tumors, cytokine-engineering, such as with IL15, may have particular value to enhance TIL activity for immune-excluded tumors.

One particularly compelling observation was that of a tumor-antigen specific TCR clone with very low TCR avidity, which killed tumor only after exposure to exogenous IL15, and this was required to trigger signaling downstream of the TCR. Although confirmation of this hypothesis would require efforts to identify the cognate antigen to further confirm the avidity of TCR, the observation provides additional rationale for IL15 engineering for adoptive T-cell therapy, as this may enhance the activation of TCRs that recognize shared tumor antigens, which are often shown as low avidity.

One important limitation to this study was the lack of in vivo data. While a number of established chondrosarcoma tumor lines have been targeted with antigen-specific T cells in vitro (22), they are difficult to generate, and whereas tumors can be grown in short-term culture, immortal sarcoma tumor lines cannot be reliably generated for autologous functional testing of TILs. Similarly, it is challenging to grow sarcoma tumors in vivo for patient-derived tumor (PDX) models, and we made multiple unsuccessful attempts at PDX model generation. Still, we were able to functionally assess each TIL product through the generation of patient-derived tumor spheroid models, which allowed both for examination of TIL cytotoxicity and tumor spheroid infiltration. Although still lacking the complete TME and tumor vasculature modeling, recent studies have shown ex vivo/in vitro 3D models could have clinical relevance when assessed in a CAR T-cell therapy trial (54). Although small patient numbers are often a limitation in studies of rare tumors such as chondrosarcoma, the consistency of the data suggests the robustness of our observations.

These results support exploration of TIL cell therapy in all immune-excluded tumors and specifically in chondrosarcoma. Furthermore, the data demonstrate the particular importance IL15 engineering of TIL in the immune-excluded setting.
