Large Language Models and Retrieval‐Augmented Platforms for the Diagnosis and Management of Periodontal Diseases: A Blinded Expert‐Rated Comparative Study of 11 Systems
Authors: Yaniv Mayer, Bertha Demetriou, Giulio Rasperini, Eran Gabay, Rok Gašperšič, Darko Božić, Javier Calatrava, Ofir Ginesin, Hadar Zigdon Giladi, Ricardo Faria Almeida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo compare retrieval‐augmented systems with general‐purpose large language models (LLMs) on standardised periodontal clinical vignettes.Materials and MethodsEleven AI systems were evaluated: nine general‐purpose LLMs, one general‐purpose retrieval‐augmented platform (Perplexity) and one medical‐domain retrieval‐augmented platform (OpenEvidence). Introduction

Periodontal diseases affect more than half of the adult population worldwide; severe periodontitis affects approximately 11% and is the sixth most prevalent human disease (Kassebaum et al. 2014; Peres et al. 2019). The burden includes tooth loss, reduced quality of life and bidirectional associations with diabetes and cardiovascular disease (Eke et al. 2018; Sanz et al. 2020).

The 2017 World Workshop introduced a staging (I to IV) and grading (A to C) framework and formalised definitions for necrotising periodontal diseases and abscesses (Caton et al. 2018; Papapanou et al. 2018; Tonetti et al. 2018), and the European Federation of Periodontology (EFP) issued S3‐level guidelines for Stage I–III (Sanz et al. 2020) and Stage IV periodontitis (Herrera et al. 2022). Inter‐examiner agreement remains only moderate even among trained periodontists, supporting a role for decision support tools (Abrahamian et al. 2022). Practice also spans acute emergencies (Herrera et al. 2018) and complex scenarios integrating clinical, radiographic and systemic information (Jepsen et al. 2018).

Large language models (LLMs) have emerged as candidate decision support tools across medicine and dentistry (Chau et al. 2025; Thirunavukarasu et al. 2023). In periodontology, ChatGPT identified the stage, grade and extent of untreated periodontitis correctly in 59.5%, 50.5% and 84.0% of cases (Tastan Eroglu et al. 2024). Whether models differ systematically remains unsettled: GPT‐4 outperformed GPT‐3.5, Gemini and periodontics residents on board‐style examinations (Sabri et al. 2025), whereas evaluations against EFP benchmarks and in peri‐implant disease reported variable performance (Chatzopoulos et al. 2025a, 2025b; Koidou et al. 2025; Nantakeeratipat 2026). A scoping review identified periodontology as one of the least explored LLM domains (Umer et al. 2024).

Hallucinations, that is, fabricated clinical information, remain a major safety concern (Roustan and Bastardot 2025); across 300 physician‐validated vignettes, six leading LLMs showed hallucination rates of 50%–82% (Omar et al. 2025). Retrieval‐augmented generation (RAG) conditions responses on retrieved external evidence (Lewis et al. 2020) and has been identified as a critical gap in dental AI (Umer et al. 2024). No periodontal study has directly compared a medical‐domain RAG platform against general‐purpose LLMs and a general‐purpose RAG platform within one vignette‐based framework across the full disease spectrum.

The present study compares the diagnostic correctness, patient safety, freedom from hallucinations and management plan completeness of 11 leading LLMs across standardised acute, chronic and complex periodontal vignettes. The null hypothesis is that no significant differences exist among models on these four parameters.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This prospective, blinded, vignette‐based benchmarking study evaluated multiple conversational AI platforms (general‐purpose LLMs, a general‐purpose RAG platform and a medical‐domain RAG platform) on standardised synthetic vignettes covering the diagnosis and management of periodontal diseases, building on prior in silico evaluations (Chatzopoulos et al. 2025a, 2025b; Koidou et al. 2025; Tastan Eroglu et al. 2024). Reporting follows the TRIPOD‐LLM reporting guideline for studies using LLMs (Gallifant et al. 2025), complemented by STROBE for observational elements and the STARD‐AI framework for diagnostic elements.

Large Language Models (AI Intervention)

Eleven commercially available LLMs were selected (Table 1), spanning seven developers across three countries and including one general‐purpose RAG platform (Perplexity) and one medical‐domain RAG platform (OpenEvidence), the latter retrieving and synthesising peer‐reviewed biomedical literature (Lewis et al. 2020). Retrieval‐augmented platforms dynamically route queries to backend foundation models that are not user‐controllable, so RAG‐platform comparisons are interpreted at the system level.

All platforms were accessed through their most advanced web interface during March to April 2026, with the most capable response mode enabled where available. Each vignette was submitted as a standalone prompt under platform‐default sampling, and conversation history was cleared so that every prompt was processed de novo (Chatzopoulos et al. 2025a, 2025b). Responses were saved verbatim and deidentified with randomised codes before evaluation. Full procedural detail appears in Appendix S1.

Dataset Creation (Synthetic Clinical Vignettes)

Thirty synthetic vignettes were created, each a realistic patient presentation including demographics, medical and dental history (medications, smoking, diabetes control, other risk modifiers), clinical findings (plaque and bleeding scores, probing depths, attachment loss, recession, furcation involvement, mobility, suppuration), radiographic findings and vital signs where applicable. Vignettes were divided equally into three categories of 10:

Category A (Acute Periodontal Emergencies, e.g., acute abscesses, necrotising periodontal diseases, pericoronitis with threatened airway, herpetic gingivostomatitis, mucous membrane pemphigoid, postsurgical haemorrhage, lateral abscess vs. vertical root fracture).

Category B (Chronic Periodontitis Staging, Grading, and Treatment Planning across Stages I– IV and grades, including molar‐incisor pattern, periodontitis as a manifestation of systemic disease and special situations such as pregnancy, antiresorptive therapy and orthodontic requests).

Category C (Complex Decision Making, e.g., tooth retention vs. extraction/implant, furcation‐involved abutments, regeneration patient selection, mucogingival and crown‐lengthening surgery, peri‐implantitis vs. periodontitis recurrence, full‐mouth rehabilitation, dual antiplatelet therapy, endo‐periodontal lesions and multiple systemic comorbidities). The complete vignettes appear in Supporting Information S1.

Initial drafts were generated with Claude Opus 4.7 (Anthropic, San Francisco, CA, USA), then independently reviewed, rephrased and clinically refined by an expert periodontist (Y.M., > 10 years' experience) and validated by a second clinician (B.D.); discrepancies were resolved by consensus before the set was locked. No modifications were permitted once data collection began.

Standardised Master Prompt

An identical standardised master prompt preceded every vignette in each session. It assigned the model the role of an experienced periodontist and required, with reference to the 2017 AAP/EFP World Workshop Classification and the EFP S3‐level guidelines, a primary diagnosis (with staging, grading and differentials), a determination of whether the situation was a dental or medical emergency, a step‐by‐step management plan with any adjunctive therapy, referrals and follow‐up and a prognosis. The verbatim master prompt is reproduced in Appendix S1.

Blinded Expert Evaluation

Six board‐certified periodontists (each > 10 years' clinical experience) independently graded the responses. The 30 vignettes were partitioned into six non‐overlapping blocks of five, one per evaluator; each evaluator scored all 11 LLM responses to their five assigned vignettes (55 per evaluator; 330 total per parameter), forming a randomised block design with vignette as the within‐block unit and LLM as the treatment.

Evaluators were blinded to model identity: responses were presented in randomised order under deidentified codes, with no discussion until all assessments were complete. Each response was scored on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = Dangerous/Poor to 5 = Excellent/Evidence‐based) across four parameters: Diagnostic Correctness, Patient Safety, Freedom from Hallucinations, and Completeness of Management Plan (Table 2). A composite score was the arithmetic mean of the four parameters. Two platforms produced output with distinctive features that could not be removed: Perplexity embeds inline numbered citations, and OpenEvidence presents source‐anchored, structured responses with named references. Because these features could not be masked, blinding was partial; raters may have scored citation‐bearing, structured responses more favourably independent of content, a potential source of bias (revisited in Section 4).

A hallucination was operationally defined as any claim referencing a non‐existent procedure, instrument or anatomical structure; recommending a medication, dose or route that does not exist or is contraindicated; citing or paraphrasing a non‐existent or substantively misrepresented guideline, study or author; or making a numerical claim contradicting the reference standard.

Reference Standard

The framework comprised the cumulative clinical expertise of the evaluators together with their shared familiarity with consensus literature, anchored to the 2017 World Workshop Classification (Caton et al. 2018; Papapanou et al. 2018; Tonetti et al. 2018), the EFP S3‐level guidelines for Stage I to III (Sanz et al. 2020) and Stage IV (Herrera et al. 2022), consensus definitions for acute periodontal and endo‐periodontal lesions (Herrera et al. 2018) and the consensus report on periodontal manifestations of systemic conditions (Jepsen et al. 2018). For scenarios beyond formal guidelines, scoring was informed by author consensus from indexed case series, reviews and textbook chapters (Supporting Information S2). References were checked against PubMed; citation presence alone did not raise scores.

Adjudication Sub‐Study

A stratified random subsample of 66 responses (20%, balanced across models and categories) was re‐scored by an independent board‐certified periodontist (O.G.), blinded to original scores, model codes and block assignments.

Sample Size and Statistical Analysis

Sample size was determined a priori for the Friedman test (k = 11; vignettes as blocks); n = 30 provided estimated power above 0.95 for a medium effect on Kendall's W, and exceeded the paired‐observation count recommended for stable Wilcoxon signed‐rank inference (Noether 1987; Conover 1999; full derivation in Appendix S1). Within‐category analyses (10 vignettes each) were powered for the omnibus Friedman test (Eisinga et al. 2017) but not for pairwise contrasts, which are reported descriptively.

For each model, median and IQR (primary) and mean and SD (secondary) are reported for the four parameters and the composite, with 95% confidence intervals (CI) truncated at the rubric ceiling of 5.00 where applicable, together with the proportions of responses scoring ≤ 2 and exactly 5.

The primary comparison used the Friedman test, with vignette as within‐block unit and LLM as treatment. Pairwise post hoc comparisons used Conover–Iman with Holm–Bonferroni correction across 55 contrasts per parameter; effect sizes are Kendall's W. Two prespecified Wilcoxon signed‐rank families were also Holm‐corrected: OpenEvidence versus each other model (10 contrasts) and Perplexity versus each non‐RAG model (9 contrasts). A Nemenyi critical difference diagram was constructed for the composite. As rater‐adjusted inferential analyses, a linear mixed‐effects model was fitted to each parameter and the composite, with LLM as fixed effect and random intercepts for evaluator and for vignette nested in evaluator; OpenEvidence served as the reference category. An LLM × category interaction was tested, and a proportional odds ordinal regression provided a cross‐check.

Results

Overview of Model Performance

All 11 models returned responses to each of the 30 vignettes, yielding 330 scored responses per parameter. Mean composite scores ranged from 3.28 (Grok 3 Expert) to 4.86 (Perplexity), and all models exceeded the Adequate threshold of 3.0 on the mean. The two RAG platforms occupied the top two positions: Perplexity ranked first (mean 4.86, SD 0.28; median 5.00 [IQR 4.75–5.00]) and OpenEvidence second (mean 4.62, SD 0.42; median 4.75 [IQR 4.50–5.00]), followed by Claude 4.7 Opus (mean 4.42). The lowest positions were Gemini 3 Thinking (3.51), Mistral (3.38) and Grok 3 Expert (3.28). Per‐model statistics appear in Table 3 and ranked means in Figure 1.

Inter‐Examiner Agreement (Calibration Phase)

Krippendorff's alpha was concordant (0.83–0.93). Examiner pairs agreed within one Likert point on 87% of Safety, 78% of Hallucination, 89% of Completeness and 76% of Accuracy ratings.

Adjudication Sub‐Study

Sixty‐six responses (20%) were re‐scored by an external blinded periodontist. Agreement was ICC(3,1) = 0.74 (95% CI: 0.62–0.83) for the composite, and 0.68, 0.78, 0.71 and 0.63 for Accuracy, Safety, Hallucination and Completeness. Score pairs agreed exactly in 54% of cases and within 1 Likert point in 89%. The inter‐model ranking from external scores was concordant with the main‐study ranking (Spearman ρ = 0.91).

Inter‐Model Comparisons

The Friedman test was significant for every parameter and for the composite (Table S1). For the composite, χ 2(10) = 137.9 (p < 0.001). The overall effect size was small (Kendall's W = 0.17). This overall value masked much larger effects within each clinical category (W = 0.82 acute, 0.45 chronic, 0.79 complex). Freedom from Hallucinations was significant overall (p < 0.001) but not within the chronic or complex categories (p = 0.65 and 0.18).

Pairwise comparisons of the composite used the Conover–Iman test with Holm correction (Table S2; Nemenyi diagram, Figure S1). Three models formed the top group: Perplexity (mean rank 2.29), OpenEvidence (3.05) and Claude 4.7 Opus (4.21). These three did not differ from one another, and the group was separable from every model ranked below Claude 4.6 Sonnet (5.50). Three models formed the bottom group: Mistral (8.36), Gemini 3 Thinking (8.52) and Grok 3 Expert (9.28). These three were indistinguishable from one another but scored significantly worse than every model above DeepSeek R1.

Two prespecified retrieval‐augmented contrasts were also examined. OpenEvidence outperformed every general‐purpose model except Claude 4.7 Opus (significant for eight of nine contrasts; p = 0.21 vs. Claude 4.7 Opus). Perplexity outperformed every non‐retrieval model (p ≤ 0.0015). Perplexity edged OpenEvidence in the descriptive contrast (p = 0.032), but the mixed‐effects model showed no significant difference between them. The LLM × category interaction was largest for Completeness (p < 0.001).

The mixed‐effects model confirmed this rank order (composite p < 0.001). Variance was dominated by differences between evaluators (random‐intercept variance 0.32), with little attributable to vignettes (0.02) and a residual of 0.34. Mean differences relative to OpenEvidence are given in Table S3 and Figure S2. The proportional‐odds ordinal model returned concordant findings (Figure S3).

Subcategory Analysis by Clinical Category

Within‐category Friedman tests were significant on the composite in all three categories, with very large effects in acute emergencies (χ 2(10) = 59.1, p = 5.3 × 10−9, W = 0.82) and complex decision‐making (χ 2(10) = 70.8, p = 3.1 × 10−11, W = 0.79), and a medium effect in chronic periodontitis (χ 2(10) = 40.7, p = 1.3 × 10−5, W = 0.45). Perplexity led in every category (means 4.90, 4.72, 4.95 in A, B, C). OpenEvidence was second in chronic (4.70) but was narrowly displaced by Claude 4.6 Sonnet in acute (4.92 vs. 4.83) and by Claude 4.7 Opus in complex (4.62 vs. 4.35). The LLM × category interaction was significant for every parameter and the composite (composite LR χ 2(20) = 42.1, p = 0.003), indicating context‐dependent differences (Figure 2).

Notable Performance Patterns

Median Likert scores by model and parameter are shown in Figure 3. Perplexity achieved a median of 5.0 on every parameter; OpenEvidence achieved 5.0 on all four parameters and 4.8 on the composite. The four lowest ranked models reached a median of only 3.0 on Accuracy and Completeness, while Hallucination medians were at or above 4 for all models.

The proportion of vignettes with at least one parameter scored ≤ 2 ranged 14‐fold across platforms, from 3.3% for Claude 4.7 Opus to 46.7% for Gemini 3 Thinking (per‐model values in Table 3). The proportion of composite scores reaching 5 ranged from 0.0% (GPT 5.5 Instant, Gemini 3 Thinking, Grok 3 Expert) to 73.3% (Perplexity).

Mean response length varied roughly 30‐fold across platforms (~60 to ~1900 words). At the platform level, length was strongly associated with mean composite score (Spearman ρ = 0.94, n = 11). This ecological association does not establish that length drives scores within responses. Adding log word count to the response‐level model, length strongly predicted the composite (β = 0.48 per log‐unit, p < 0.001) and between‐model differences attenuated sharply (composite p = 0.02; Diagnostic Correctness and Completeness no longer significant), with the leading tier no longer separable. Because length is largely a between‐model attribute collinear with model identity, these adjusted estimates are unstable (Table S4). Length and model quality cannot be cleanly separated in this design.

Discussion

This study tested the null hypothesis that 11 LLMs would perform equivalently on standardised periodontal vignettes. The null hypothesis was rejected: performance differed significantly among platforms and, more strongly, across clinical contexts, and the two retrieval‐augmented platforms formed a distinct top tier on every parameter. The most clinically relevant finding lay beneath the mean scores: although all 11 models reached at least adequate mean performance, the proportion of responses judged potentially dangerous ranged widely, a dissociation that represents the principal safety message of this work.

The strong performance of the RAG platforms is most readily explained by RAG's capacity to offset the static training‐bound knowledge of conventional models by conditioning responses on retrieved external evidence (Lewis et al. 2020), a mechanism recently linked to improved accuracy and reduced hallucination in biomedical applications (Liu et al. 2025). The pattern is not, however, fully explained by retrieval alone. A reasoning‐oriented model without explicit live retrieval joined the leading group, suggesting that advanced internal reasoning may partly compensate for the absence of grounding. More notably, the general‐purpose RAG platform outperformed the medical‐domain RAG platform. Were curated biomedical retrieval the sole determinant, a domain‐specific tool would not be expected to rank below a general one. This difference did not reach significance in the mixed‐effects model and must be interpreted with caution, since neither platform's backend model is user‐controllable. Discrimination was greatest in acute emergencies, where time‐critical triage penalised models omitting decisive steps, and in complex decision making, where integration of competing systemic and dental considerations rewarded stronger reasoning. Differences were smaller in chronic staging and grading, where the rule‐based 2018 framework is well represented in training data.

These findings extend earlier periodontal work confined to staging and grading, which reported moderate and uneven classification accuracy (Tastan Eroglu et al. 2024), by demonstrating robust but context‐dependent separation across the full disease spectrum. They are consistent with a recent comparative dentistry evaluation in which general models differed significantly across diagnostic criteria (Meer et al. 2026), with evidence that a specialised periodontal retrieval pipeline outperforms general chatbots on expert questions (Fanelli et al. 2025), and with a large assessment of frontier models in which differential diagnosis was the weakest reasoning step and off‐the‐shelf models were judged unsuitable for unsupervised clinical use (Rao et al. 2026). The wide variation in potentially dangerous responses is consistent with reports that leading models may elaborate confidently on fabricated clinical detail (Omar et al. 2025), and the tendency of non‐retrieval models to fabricate literature addresses the dental AI grounding gap identified by Umer et al. (2024). A decision support tool cannot be regarded as merely usually safe, because a single confident but dangerous recommendation may cause serious harm.

Several limitations qualify these conclusions. Each vignette was scored by a single evaluator, with evaluator level accounting for the largest single source of variance (random‐intercept variance ≈0.32 vs. ≈0.02 for vignettes; residual 0.34) and external adjudication agreement that was moderate (ICC(3,1) 0.74 composite). The unadjusted confidence intervals in Table 3 should therefore be interpreted alongside the rater‐adjusted mixed‐effects estimates (Table S3, Figure S2), which constitute the inferential anchor. Response length was strongly associated with composite performance (Spearman ρ = 0.94); adjusting for it sharply attenuated between‐model differences, but because length is a fixed platform attribute collinear with model identity, the two could not be disentangled. The advantage of the leading systems therefore cannot be attributed to content rather than length, and designs that match or manipulate response length are needed. Claude Opus 4.7 was used to produce initial drafts of the vignettes and was also one of the 11 evaluated systems, ranking third overall; the locked, expert‐rephrased vignettes and blinded presentation were intended to mitigate residual stylistic alignment. The principal safety metric (any parameter ≤ 2) is by construction an OR across four correlated dimensions and is therefore mechanically higher than any single‐parameter dangerous rate. The 2018 classification and EFP S3‐level guidelines do not address every clinical question raised, and for scenarios beyond formal guidance, scores may partly reflect reasonable clinical disagreement. Finally, the study was conducted in silico using text‐only vignettes, omitting radiographic, cone‐beam and tactile information central to periodontics; prospective validation against real cases incorporating imaging is the natural next step. Because commercial models are updated continuously, these results are a March–April 2026 snapshot. Several evaluated systems have since been superseded by newer releases, and the findings may not transfer to those versions. This rapid obsolescence is intrinsic to LLM benchmarking and calls for periodic re‐evaluation and version‐anchored, TRIPOD‐LLM‐concordant reporting (Gallifant et al. 2025). Each model–vignette response was generated only once under platform‐default, non‐deterministic sampling, so a single draw per cell does not capture the within‐model variability introduced by stochastic decoding; replicate generations with explicit variance estimation are needed to confirm the stability of the observed ranking.

Within its limits, this study provides the first blinded, expert‐rated comparison of retrieval‐augmented and general‐purpose language models across the breadth of periodontal practice. It establishes that evidence‐grounded retrieval platforms currently offer the most reliable guidance while exposing a range of potentially dangerous responses wide enough to make undifferentiated clinical adoption inadvisable, shifting the relevant question from whether these models can address periodontal problems to which architectures can do so safely, and positioning retrieval‐augmented platforms as supervised clinical adjuncts rather than autonomous decision makers.

Conclusion

Eleven large language models all reached at least adequate mean performance on standardised periodontal vignettes, yet expert ratings differed substantially across models and clinical contexts. The two retrieval‐augmented platforms, Perplexity and OpenEvidence, ranked highest and outperformed most general‐purpose models on every parameter, although the present design cannot establish retrieval as the sole reason for their advantage, which could not be separated from response length. The proportion of potentially dangerous responses ranged from 3.3% to 46.7%, a spread that argues against undifferentiated clinical use. These tools should assist rather than replace specialist judgement, with every recommendation verified against current guidelines before informing patient care.
