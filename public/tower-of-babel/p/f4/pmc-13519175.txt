Association Between Talar Morphology and Subchondral Bone Sclerosis in the Medial Gutter of Patients With Chronic Lateral Ankle Instability
Authors: Shunsuke Nakamura, Naohiro Uezono, Kento Shimanoe, Takayuki Ide, Shogo Tsutsumi, Suguru Saho, Masaki Dogomori, Yusuke Fujimoto, Toshiro Ijuin, Noboru Taniguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Chronic lateral ankle instability (CLAI) is associated with an increased risk of posttraumatic osteoarthritis, with early degenerative changes often beginning in the medial gutter. Although altered loading toward the anteromedial talus has been reported, the contribution of talar morphology to early subchondral adaptation remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the association between talar morphology and subchondral bone sclerosis in the medial gutter using quantitative computed tomography (QCT).Methods:Twenty-seven patients with CLAI (34 ankles) who underwent arthroscopic ligament repair and 30 control patients (34 feet) without ankle trauma or deformity were retrospectively analyzed. QCT was used to quantify volumetric bone mineral density (BMD). The talar side of the medial gutter was divided into 8 subregions, and BMD ratios were calculated by normalizing regional BMD to fibular BMD. Radiographic and axial computed tomography–based morphologic parameters, including anterior opening angle of the talus (AOT), posterior width (PW), anterior width, and trochlea tali length, were measured. Group comparisons and Spearman correlation analyses were performed.Results:BMD ratios in the medial gutter were significantly greater in the CLAI group than in controls, particularly in anterior regions (areas 1, 2, and 5; all P < .05). Compared with controls, CLAI patients demonstrated a larger AOT and smaller PW, as well as significantly greater talar tilt angle (11.6° vs 4.6°; P < .01) and anterior drawer distance (7.2 mm vs 5.1 mm; P < .01). In the CLAI group, significant positive correlations between BMD ratio and AOT (r = 0.48-0.60) and significant negative correlations with PW (r = −0.41 to −0.49) were observed after false-discovery-rate correction. No consistent morphology-BMD associations were observed in controls.Conclusion:In CLAI, subchondral bone density is increased in the anterior medial gutter and is associated with a larger AOT and smaller PW, suggesting anteriorly biased loading patterns and localized subchondral adaptation. These findings suggest an association between talar morphology and region-specific subchondral adaptation in CLAI.Level of Evidence:Level III, comparative study.Keywords: chronic lateral ankle instability, talar morphology, subchondral bone sclerosis, quantitative computed tomography

Introduction

Ankle sprains are the most common type of sports injury. Despite appropriate treatment, 15% to 20% of patients develop chronic lateral ankle instability (CLAI), and surgical intervention is often indicated in patients with persistent pain or instability refractory to conservative management.1,2 CLAI is frequently associated with posttraumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA) of the ankle.3,4 As osteoarthritis (OA) progresses, conservative treatment becomes increasingly difficult; therefore, preventing the progression from CLAI to PTOA is highly clinically important.

Although the ankle is among the most frequently injured joints in sports—most commonly through lateral ankle sprains—it develops OA far less often than the knee or hip do.5-9 When ankle OA does occur, it tends to develop at a younger age and progress more rapidly, resulting in prolonged pain, functional limitations, and a considerable socioeconomic burden. 6 These characteristics highlight the need to identify risk factors that drive early degenerative changes in the ankle.

Mechanical stress applied to the talocrural and subtalar joints due to ligamentous dysfunction has been suggested as a key factor associated with the development of ankle OA. 10 In varus ankle OA, the medial gutter is the initial site of osteoarthritic involvement.11,12 Recent computed tomography (CT)–based investigations have shown that subchondral bone changes may precede radiographic OA. Nakasa et al 13 reported increased mineralization in the medial gutter of CLAI patients using CT-based osteoabsorptiometry, suggesting early subchondral adaptation to altered mechanical loading. However, prior studies have not clarified whether talar morphology contributes to these early adaptive changes. Clarifying this relationship may improve early risk stratification and inform clinical decision making in patients with CLAI.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between talar morphology and subchondral bone sclerosis of the medial gutter in CLAI using quantitative computed tomography (QCT). A detailed assessment of subchondral bone changes may help elucidate early pathologic processes leading to PTOA. The primary outcome of this study was the relationship between talar morphology and medial gutter subchondral bone sclerosis. The secondary outcomes were (1) the differences in radiographic parameters between the CLAI and control groups and (2) the regional distribution of subchondral bone sclerosis within the medial gutter.

Methods

Patients

The CLAI group consisted of 34 ankles from 27 patients. Demographic characteristics were summarized on a per-limb basis; therefore, the sex distribution represents the number of affected ankles rather than the number of individual patients. This group comprised 17 male and 17 female ankles. The diagnosis of CLAI was established based on a comprehensive assessment including medical history, physical examination, ultrasonography, and stress radiography. Eligible patients were required to have undergone preoperative CT imaging and arthroscopic lateral ankle ligament repair. CLAI was diagnosed based on clinical symptoms and stress radiographic findings, defined as a talar tilt angle ≥6° and/or an anterior drawer distance ≥6 mm. The median symptom duration in the CLAI group was 11 months (IQR, 6-27.75 months), and the median duration of conservative treatment was 6 months (IQR, 3-9.25 months). All patients underwent at least 3 months of conservative management, including rehabilitation, and surgical treatment was considered when symptoms and instability persisted despite nonoperative treatment. The number of previous ankle sprains could not be reliably quantified because several patients could not accurately recall recurrent episodes. Exclusion criteria included incomplete clinical or imaging evaluation (n = 7), a history of previous ankle surgery (n = 2), systemic joint hypermobility (n = 1), and rheumatoid arthritis (n = 1). The control group consisted of 34 ankles from 30 individuals. Demographic characteristics were summarized on a per-limb basis; therefore, the sex distribution represents the number of ankles rather than the number of individual participants. This group comprised 14 male and 20 female ankles. Control participants had undergone ankle CT examinations for conditions unrelated to ankle instability, including fibular tendon dislocation, Achilles tendinopathy, accessory bone disorders, and soft tissue tumors. None of the control participants had a history of ankle trauma, clinical ankle instability, radiographic deformity, osteoarthritic changes, or previous fractures. In addition, all CT images were reviewed, and cases with apparent talar deformity or malalignment were excluded.

QCT Analysis

Axial QCT data were obtained for the distal tibia, talus, fibula, and calcaneus and exported in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format. Imaging was performed using an IQon Spectral CT system (Philips Healthcare) with a slice thickness of 1 mm, a pixel size of 0.35 mm, a tube voltage of 120 kVp, and a matrix size of 512 × 512. A calibration phantom containing hydroxyapatite rods (BMAS 200; Kyoto Kagaku) was scanned simultaneously with each subject to enable quantitative assessment of bone mineral density (BMD). Three-dimensional finite element (FE) models were generated from the segmented data sets and analyzed using Mechanical Finder software (version 11.0; RCCM). The trabecular bone and the inner cortical layer were discretized using 2-mm tetrahedral elements, whereas the outer cortical shell was represented by 0.4-mm-thick 3-node shell elements. Assessment of Subchondral Bone in the Medial Gutter

Subchondral bone changes were evaluated on the talar side of the medial gutter. This region was subdivided into 8 predefined areas using anatomical reference lines based on the distal tip of the medial malleolus and the apex of the talar dome (Figure 1A and B). BMD was measured using 3-mm cubic regions of interest (ROIs) placed within the trabecular bone of each predefined area on axial CT images while avoiding inclusion of the medial cortical bone (Figure 1C-F). To account for interindividual differences in bone quality, BMD was also measured at the center of the fibula at the ankle level (Figure 1G). Regional BMD values were normalized to fibular BMD, and the resulting BMD ratios were compared between the CLAI and control groups.

Radiographic and Morphologic Evaluation

Varus stress radiographs were obtained using manual maximal force, and the talar tilt (TT) angle and anterior drawer distance (ADD) were measured (Figure 2A and B). Standard weight-bearing radiographs were used to assess the tibial articular surface (TAS) angle and tibial medial malleolar (TMM) angle on anteroposterior views, as well as the tibial lateral surface (TLS) angle on lateral views (Figure 2C-E). 16 All radiographic measurements were performed independently by 2 observers.

Measurement of radiographic and CT parameters. (A) Talar tilt (TT) angle was measured on inversion stress anteroposterior radiographs as the angle between the tibial plafond and the talar dome. (B) Anterior drawer distance (AAD) was measured on stress lateral radiographs as the distance between the posterior lip of the distal tibia and the posterior aspect of the talar dome. (C) Tibial articular surface (TAS) angle was measured on weightbearing anteroposterior radiographs as the angle between the longitudinal axis of the tibial shaft and the tibial plafond. (D) Tibial lateral surface (TLS) angle was measured on weightbearing lateral radiographs as the angle between the longitudinal axis of the tibial shaft and the distal tibial articular surface. (E) Tibial medial malleolar (TMM) angle was measured on weightbearing anteroposterior radiographs as the angle between the longitudinal axis of the tibial shaft and the articular surface of the medial malleolus. (F) Talar morphologic parameters were measured on axial sections of a 3-dimensional talar model reconstructed from CT data using Mechanical Finder software. Trochlea tali length (TTL) was defined as the anteroposterior length of the trochlear articular surface. Anterior width (AW) and posterior width (PW) were defined as the mediolateral widths of the anterior and posterior aspects of the trochlear surface, respectively, measured perpendicular to the TTL axis. (G) The anterior opening angle of the talus (AOT) was measured on axial CT images as the angle formed by lines connecting the medial and lateral margins of the anterior and posterior aspects of the trochlear surface. CT, computed tomography.

The DICOM data from CT scans were imported into Mechanical Finder software to generate 3-dimensional models of the talus. Talar morphologic parameters, including trochlea tali length (TTL), anterior width (AW), and posterior width (PW), were measured on axial sections of the talar model according to the method described by Daud et al 17 (Figure 2F). The anterior opening angle of the talus (AOT) was measured on axial CT images according to the method described by Sonnow et al 18 (Figure 2G). For this measurement, 2 lines are drawn that pass through the medial and lateral surfaces of the talar trochlea. The angle (α) between these lines is the AOT.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median with IQR and were compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney U test. The Hodges-Lehmann estimator was used to estimate the median difference between groups, and corresponding 95% CIs were calculated. Differences were expressed as the CLAI group minus the control group. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. Associations between BMD ratios and morphologic parameters (AW, PW, TTL, and AOT) were evaluated using Spearman rank correlation coefficients. Because multiple Spearman correlation analyses were performed across 8 medial gutter subregions and 4 morphologic parameters, Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction was applied to control for multiplicity. Statistical significance was defined as a P value <.05. Interobserver and intraobserver reliability were assessed using SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 27.0; IBM Japan Co, Ltd). Additional statistical analyses were conducted using JMP Pro software (version 16.0; SAS Institute).

Ethical Approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kagoshima University.

Because of the retrospective design, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. Instead, information about the study was publicly disclosed on the institutional website, and patients were given the opportunity to opt out of participation.

In addition, a significant negative correlation between AW and the BMD ratio was observed in area 4 (r = −0.44, adjusted P = .03).

Discussion

This study quantitatively evaluated subchondral bone density in the medial gutter of the talus using QCT and examined its relationship with talar morphology in patients with CLAI. The principal finding was that subchondral bone density was preferentially increased in the anterior portion of the medial gutter in the CLAI group compared with controls, and that these changes were significantly associated with specific morphologic features of the talus, even after correction for multiple comparisons. Among the morphologic parameters, AOT and PW showed the strongest correlations with the BMD ratio, with both relationships most pronounced in the anterior subregions. These findings suggest that characteristic talar morphologic features in CLAI may be associated with anteriorly concentrated mechanical stress and localized subchondral sclerosis. In addition, differences in stress radiographic and morphologic parameters indicate that medial gutter sclerosis occurs in the context of broader alterations in ankle alignment and stability rather than talar morphology alone.

The morphologic analysis revealed that the AOT and TMM angle were significantly greater, whereas the PW was smaller in CLAI patients than in controls. Previous studies on osteochondral lesions of the talus have similarly shown greater AOT and TMM angles,18-20 suggesting that these geometric characteristics may be associated with ankle instability and altered load distribution. Because the talus is anatomically wider anteriorly than posteriorly, plantar flexion reduces the congruency of the tibiotalar joint. 20 A larger AOT and smaller PW may therefore accentuate this loss of constraint, allowing greater anterior translation of the talus during motion and potentially contributing to increased susceptibility to recurrent sprains. These results support the hypothesis that altered talar morphology may be associated with ankle instability and anteriorly biased load transmission. Biomechanical studies have shown that ATFL insufficiency increases both anterior talar translation and rotational instability. 21 Therefore, in ankles with a larger AOT and a smaller PW, the combination of reduced osseous constraint and lateral ligament insufficiency may further alter tibiotalar contact mechanics, potentially promoting anteromedial talar translation and subsequent stress concentration within the medial gutter. The increased BMD observed in the anterior medial gutter may represent adaptive subchondral remodeling in response to these repetitive mechanical stresses.

Our findings are consistent with those of previous reports linking CLAI to subchondral adaptation in the medial gutter. Nakasa et al13,22 and Sakurai et al 23 demonstrated increased mineralization in this region using CT-based Hounsfield unit analysis, suggesting that instability may be associated with localized bone remodeling prior to radiographic osteoarthritis. In the present study, volumetric QCT allowed a more detailed assessment of trabecular BMD and confirmed that this subchondral sclerosis predominantly occurs in the anteromedial region. Importantly, these associations remained significant after correction for multiple comparisons, reinforcing the robustness of the observed morphology-BMD relationship. This distribution aligns with mechanical analyses showing that increased talar tilt and anterior translation increase compressive stress in the anteromedial talar region. 24 Taken together, these data suggest that a larger AOT and a smaller PW are associated with anteriorly biased loading patterns and localized subchondral adaptation in the medial gutter. However, the present cross-sectional study does not establish whether these morphologic characteristics contribute to subsequent cartilage degeneration, structural progression, or clinical outcomes.

Previous morphologic studies have also reported that a flatter talar dome (larger radius) and smaller tibiotalar sector correlate with CLAI,25-27 whereas tibial plafond varus has been suggested as an additional intrinsic risk factor. 27 However, few investigations have focused on axial talar geometry in relation to subchondral adaptation. The present analysis fills this gap by demonstrating that variations in AOT and PW on axial CT are directly associated with localized increases in subchondral bone density, reflecting altered stress distribution associated with instability. The morphology-BMD associations observed only in CLAI suggest that increased AOT and reduced PW may characterize a subgroup prone to anteriorly shifted loading, a pattern not observed in controls. These findings suggest that morphologic assessment may help identify patients with altered mechanical loading patterns. However, this study was not designed to establish diagnostic cutoff values for AOT or PW or to define surveillance or treatment algorithms. These observations should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating and require prospective validation before clinical application.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective study with a relatively small sample size, and selection bias may exist. Although the control participants were selected from conditions unrelated to ankle instability and cases with apparent talar deformity or malalignment were excluded, subtle alterations in mechanical loading associated with these conditions cannot be completely ruled out and may have influenced the morphologic comparisons. In addition, the study may have been underpowered to detect small differences in some subregions of the medial gutter. Although multiple comparisons were addressed using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction, the possibility of residual type I error cannot be completely excluded. Second, the QCT assessment focused only on the talar side of the medial gutter and did not evaluate the tibial side or cartilage morphology. Third, the cross-sectional design does not establish causality between morphologic changes and subchondral adaptation. In addition, the study cohort consisted of surgically treated patients from a single tertiary referral center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to patients managed nonoperatively or in a different clinical setting. Race and ethnicity data were not collected as part of this study. Future longitudinal studies with larger and more diverse populations, incorporating motion analysis or finite element modeling, are warranted to clarify the temporal and mechanical relationships underlying these changes.

Conclusion

In patients with CLAI, subchondral bone density was higher in the anterior medial gutter. A larger anterior opening angle of the talus (AOT) and a smaller posterior width (PW) were associated with these regional changes, suggesting that altered talar morphology may be associated with anteriorly biased loading patterns and localized subchondral adaptation in the medial gutter findings that occurred in the context of broader radiographic alterations in ankle alignment and stability.
