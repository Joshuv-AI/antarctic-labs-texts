Exercise training-induced extracellular miR-136-3p modulates glucose uptake and myogenesis through targeting of NRDC in human skeletal muscle
Authors: Mutsumi Katayama, Elena Caria, Dimitri Van Simaeys, Anna Yagüe Sanz, Romain Barrès, Kenneth Caidahl, Oscar PB Wiklander, Samir El Andaloussi, Per-Olof Berggren, Juleen R Zierath, Anna Krook
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Endurance training significantly increased miR-136-3p content in exosome-enriched extracellular vesicles (EVs) in human serum. This study provides evidence that pancreatic islets are a likely source of circulating miR-136-3p and shows that cultured human islets release miR-136-3p into the medium, suggesting islet-skeletal muscle communication via EVs.•Transfection of miR-136-3p into human myotubes increased glucose uptake and induced a glycolytic shift in mitochondrial metabolism. These effects were independent of insulin signaling, suggesting a novel pathway for glucose regulation.•The nardilysin convertase (NRDC) gene was confirmed as a direct target of miR-136-3p through luciferase assays in HEK293 and expression analysis in human myotubes. Both exercise training and inactivity modulate NRDC expression in skeletal muscle, and both miR-136-3p overexpression and NRDC silencing affected gene sets related to myogenesis, cell proliferation, and metabolism. This suggested a role for NRDC in long term metabolic adaptation.Keywords: Extracellular miRNA, Endurance training, Human skeletal muscle, miR-136-3p, Nardilycin convertase (NRDC)

Introduction

Regular exercise training confers health benefits by inducing an integrative adaptation that positively affects nearly all organ systems.1,2 In particular, skeletal muscle displays both metabolic flexibility and plasticity to adapt to external stimuli such as physical activity. Maintenance of skeletal muscle function contributes to overall health.3,4 In addition to metabolic and structural remodeling in skeletal muscle in response to exercise, physical activity also enhances overall immune function, with exercise training-induced responses linked to the prevention of secondary aging and non-communicable diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular, and metabolic diseases.5,6 However, the molecular transducers and mechanisms by which exercise improves metabolic health are still largely unknown.

Skeletal muscle acts as a secretory organ with tissue-derived factors termed myokines affecting growth and metabolism.7 Myokines are defined as peptides, proteins, or metabolites released from skeletal muscle cells with roles in metabolic regulation, both locally within the myocyte as well as in other tissues.8 Extracellular vesicles (EVs), including exosomes, mediate intercellular communication involved in several aspects of cell biology, ranging from regulation of immune responses, to waste management, to cellular migration, with important roles in metabolic diseases including type 2 diabetes.9, 10, 11, 12 Exosomes are membrane-bound EVs, which are produced in the endosomal compartment of most eukaryotic cells.13 EVs may act as exerkines, signaling between organs after exercise, with EV-associated microRNAs (miRNAs/miRs) emerging as potential mediators;14,15 in particular, exosomal miRNAs have emerged as potential exerkines.

MiRNAs are small non-coding regulatory RNAs that regulate gene expression at the post-transcriptional level16 through binding to complementary regions of the three untranslated regions of target mRNA transcripts and downregulating gene expression, thereby controlling cellular and biological actions, including metabolism.17,18 Furthermore, miRNAs can be encapsulated in EVs, thereby mediating organ crosstalk.19 Indeed, accumulating evidence suggests that miRNA transfer between cells is mediated by EVs, which also serve to protect cargo miRNAs from endogenous RNase activity.20 Muscle-derived EVs have been shown to participate in adaptations in beta cell mass in mice following diet-induced insulin resistance.10 However, the precise role of exosomal miRNAs in organ crosstalk remains to be fully elucidated. While skeletal muscle releases exosomal miRNAs in response to acute exercise or training for organ crosstalk, other organs also release exosomes into systemic circulation. Thus, exercise-induced exosomes are candidate exerkines that mediate organ crosstalk to confer beneficial effects on whole-body metabolism.21,22 Although exosomes reflect the signature of the source cell,23 the origin of serum-derived exosomal miRNA is difficult to derive. Thus, pin-pointing the mechanisms by which extracellular miRNA mediate organ crosstalk is a challenge.

Although the precise mechanisms underlying the beneficial effects of regular exercise remain incompletely understood, skeletal muscle functions not only as the primary locomotor organ but also as a key communicator, transmitting exercise-induced signals to other organs while also receiving regulatory inputs in return. As outlined above, exerkines and myokines emanating from skeletal muscle have been studied and identified as mediators of its systemic effects.8 However, research focusing on skeletal muscle as a recipient organ remains more limited. A deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing interorgan communication in response to exercise, particularly its bidirectional nature, could provide valuable insights into the broader health benefits of physical activity and inform targeted therapeutic strategies centered on skeletal muscle function. Thus, we surveyed serum-derived extracellular miRNAs of exosome-enriched EVs from healthy individuals before and after a 3-week endurance exercise training program. Specific miRNAs, including miR-136-3p and miR-139-5p, were increased in EVs derived from serum after endurance training. We provide evidence that pancreatic islets are a potential source of miR-136-3p and that miR-136-3p upregulates glucose metabolism and modulates mitochondrial function in skeletal muscle. Thus, we propose that miR-136-3p may contribute to pancreas/skeletal muscle cross talk. Finally, we provide evidence that nardilysin convertase (NRDC) is a direct target of miR-136-3p and that NRDC mRNA is downregulated by exercise training. Collectively our results highlight the role of exosome-enriched EV-derived miRNAs as exerkines that fine tune the adaptive response of endurance training on metabolism through organ cross talk.

Materials and methods

Study participants

Exercise training program

Experiments were performed with approval from the local ethical committee (2007/717-31/1-4), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants under the Declaration of Helsinki. Twelve sedentary healthy volunteers (4 males and 8 females) participated in this study. None of the subjects were involved in any regular exercise training for more than 2 h per week for at least 2 years prior to the study. Baseline investigations included an evaluation of physical exercise capacity and blood testing for basic factors. Subsequently, the volunteers participated in a 3-week supervised endurance training program consisting of 2 exercise sessions per day—one in the morning, one in the afternoon (either ergometer cycling for 50 min or running for 40 min)—6 days a week. As the previously sedentary participants started directly with 2 supervised training sessions per day, the exercise was adapted to each participant so that the participants were able to perform the full session. Throughout each training session, heart rate was continuously monitored. Individualized exercise intensities were determined for each participant based on the relationship between heart rate and oxygen uptake. Thus, the intensity increased as the participants became fitter. All subjects rested at least 1 day and, 40–70 h after the last training session, all baseline measurements were repeated. Monocyte chemotaxis has previously been studied in this cohort, and details of exercise intensity and evaluation of physical exercise capacity are described.24 Clinical characteristics of participants included in this study are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Sample collection

Blood samples were drawn into collection tubes without anticoagulant, allowed to clot for 45 min, and centrifuged at 1200 × g for 10 min at room temperature. Serum was dispensed into the cryovials and immediately frozen in a –80°C freezer until use. In the present study, serum from 12 of the original participants was used. To assess serum quality, we used miR-451a and miR-23a-3p as indicators of hemolysis. This confirmed that the serum samples were not hemolyzed.

Cell culture

For primary human skeletal muscle cultures, satellite cells were isolated from vastus lateralis skeletal muscle and differentiated as described.25 For measurement of gene expression and cell assays, myoblast cells were differentiated into myotubes as previously described;26 however, to enable an analysis of miRNAs released from the myotubes, the post fusion medium did not contain the fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA). Assays were performed at Day 10, after initiation of differentiation, when multinucleated myotubes were observed. Human embryonic kidney (HEK293) cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in high-glucose (4.5 g/L) Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) FBS. Human islets were obtained from the University of Uppsala islet isolation core and were cultured in CMRL 1066 media (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 1% GLUTAMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1% HEPES (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 5 mM sodium pyruvate (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and 12 µM nicotinamide (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Islets were handpicked to ensure optimal purity and to minimize acinar contaminants.

Isolation of exosome-enriched EVs and EV-carried RNA (evRNA) extraction

EVs and evRNA from human serum

EVs were isolated with a miRCURY Exosome Isolation Kit–Serum and Plasma (Exiqon, Vedbaek, Denmark; now available via QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). A miRCURY RNA Isolation Kit (Exiqon) was used to extract evRNA, together with an RNA Spike-in Template Kit (Exiqon), using MS2 RNA (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) as carrier RNA according to the manufacturer’s instructions, as previously described.27 Exosome-enriched EVs were analyzed using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), also as described.27

evRNA extraction from culture medium

Exo RNeasy Maxi Kit (QIAGEN) was used for the evRNA extraction from the cell culture medium.

Quantification of miRNA expression

For quantification of miRNA in evRNA, we first used a human serum/plasma Focus miRNA polymerase chain reaction (PCR) Panel in a quantitative PCR (reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR))-based approach (Exiqon), as described.27 Briefly, for the first screening, we used human serum/plasma Focus miRNA PCR Panels (96-well (V43.AF); Exiqon) to determine levels of 179 human miRNAs. RNA input was standardized based on starting volume of serum used for evRNA extraction. To monitor the RNA quality of extracted RNAs, several exogeneous control probes were added when evRNA was extracted; these confirmed there were no issues that might influence the quantification process (e.g., transcription and amplification). The RT-qPCR was performed with 40 amplification cycles in StepOne (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the cycling parameters recommended by Exiqon. Raw data were processed using StepOne software Version 2.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to assign the baseline and threshold for the threshold cycle (Ct). For determination of the technical variation between the Exiqon serum/plasma Focus miRNA PCR Panel plates, the interplate calibrator (UniSp3) was analyzed. Ct values of the interplate calibrator were analyzed and found to be highly similar across all samples. The normalization and analysis of the PCR panel plate results were provided by Exiqon GenEx software (Version 6; Exiqon), and global normalization was performed. For validation of the results of the PCR panel plate in a second screening, we used a miRCURY LNA miRNA PCR System (Exiqon) to assess the expression level of individual miRNAs. RNA input for RT-qPCR was standardized based on starting volume in the case of serum or cell culture medium. Expression levels of miRNA were quantified using the miRNA-specific LNA PCR primer and normalized with Ct values of U1 small nuclear RNA (RNU1A1). Relative expression was calculated using the comparative Ct method.28 The processes of RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and amplification were monitored using exogeneous RNA Spike In Kit (Exiqon).

Labeling and loading miR-136-3p into EVs and fluorescent microscope imaging

MiR-136-3p was labeled with Cy3 using Silencer small interfering RNA (siRNA) Labeling Kit with Cy3 Dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and loaded into exosome-enriched EVs with Exo-Fect siRNA/miRNA Transfection Reagent (System Biosciences, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The labeling and loading of miRNAs into exosome-enriched EVs followed manufacturer’s protocol. One day after exosome transfection, cells were washed with PBS, stained with Hoechst 33342 (Merck), and visualized by fluorescent microscopy (Zeiss Axio Vert A1; Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Oberkochen, Germany). Images were captured and analyzed using Image J Version 1.53a software (https://imagej.net/ij/; National Institutes of Health, USA).

miRNA transfection and silencing by RNA interference

Myotube cells were transfected using a double transfection protocol: 48 h after differentiation (Day 6), and again 48 h later (Day 8) with 10 nmol/L Ambion miR-136-3p (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or siNRDC (Silencer Select; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Control cells were transfected with a scrambled miRNA mimic (mirVana miRNA Mimic, Negative Control #1; Thermo Fisher Scientific) or a negative control siRNA (Silencer Select, Negative Control #2; Thermo Fisher Scientific). For myotubes, each transfection was performed for 5 h, with transfection complexes formed in reduced-serum media (OptiMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA was isolated using miRNeasy Kit (QIAGEN), and cell assays were performed at Day 10. For myoblast cells, transfection of miR-136-3p or siRNA was done twice, on Days 0 and 2, after initiating differentiation under the same condition described above. Cells were harvested on Day 4 for RNA extraction.

Total RNA extraction and mRNA expression analysis

For gene expressions analysis, total RNA was extracted from cells with miRNeasy Kit (QIAGEN), quantified through spectrophotometry, and reverse transcribed using a high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RT-qPCR was performed to measure the expression level of genes with SYBR Green Master Mix Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or TaqMan Fast Universal PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using StepOne Plus (Thermo Fisher Scientific) (primer list in Supplementary Table 2). The glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) gene was used as a reference, and relative quantification values were calculated.

Microarray analysis

For miR-136-3p target screening, mRNA content from miR-136-3p-transfected cells was profiled using GeneChip Human Gene 2.0 ST arrays (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and differential expression of transcripts was determined with a paired class comparison with a univariate test using a random variance model comparing gene expression of control (miRNA mimic transfected cells) vs. miR-136-3p-transfected cells by Expression Console software (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA, USA), as described.27 Human Clariom D Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for the functional investigation of miR-136-3p and NRDC, and data were analyzed by Transcriptome Analysis Console 4.0 (TAC 4.0) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Microarray data were submitted to the National Center for Biotechnology Information Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), and data can be found under the GEO series accession numbers GSE253978 and GSE282291.

Luciferase activity measurement

The last exon of the NRDC gene (NM_001101662) was synthesized and cloned into the luciferase reporter vector, pMirTarget vector (OriGene) (Invitrogen GeneArt Gene Synthesis Services; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates 24 h before experimentation. The untranslated region (UTR) promoter plasmids (100 ng/well) were transfected into HEK293 cells in 96-well plates using the transfection reagent Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 1 ng/well of Renilla luciferase vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) for normalization.27 In parallel, an miRNA mimic for miR-136-3p or a scrambled miRNA mimic as a negative control was co-transfected with or without Anti-miR™ miRNA Inhibitor for miR-136-3p (10 nM or 25 nM; Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 24 h, the cell culture medium was collected and processed for a luciferase assay using the Steady-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega) and then normalized with the Renilla activity. Assays were read in the CLARIO star (BMG LABTECH, Ortenberg, Germany), normalized by Renilla luciferase activity. The relative unit was calculated based on values using a control vector having a luciferase gene.

Immunoblot analysis

Western blot analysis was performed as described.27 Protein content of the supernatants was determined by BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce Biotechnology, Rockford, IL, USA). Membranes were stained with Ponceau S to confirm equal loading of samples and quality control for the transfer procedure and Ponceau S staining results were utilized for normalization in the analysis. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies directed to NRDC (sc-137199; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), AMP-activated protein kinase α (AMPKα) (#2532; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), Akt substrate of 160 kDa (AS160) (#2670; Cell Signaling Technology), phospho-AS160 (#8730; Cell Signaling Technology), protein kinase B (AKT) (#9272; Cell Signaling Technology), phospho-AKT (Ser473) (#9271; Cell Signaling Technology), or puromycin (MABE343; Merck Burlington, MA, USA). Membranes were incubated with species-appropriate horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody before proteins were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence (Amersham ECL Western Blotting Detection Reagent; GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Little Chalfont, UK).

Publicly available data analysis and meta-analysis of transcriptomic data

To determine whether NRDC gene expression changes after exercise training, publicly available data in human trials were selected from GEO and analyzed using the GEO2R web tool. Transcriptomic studies of acute aerobic and resistance exercise in human trials were analyzed using the MetaMEX database.29

Cell metabolic assays

Metabolic assays of glucose incorporation into glycogen, glucose oxidation, glucose uptake, and lipid oxidation were performed as described.27,30 Oxygen consumption was measured in human skeletal myotubes using a Seahorse analyzer as described.29 Lactate released from myotube cultures was measured using a colorimetric assay as described.26 Protein synthesis activity was determined by assessing puromycin incorporation into newly synthesized protein using the SunSET assay as described.31

Bioinformatical and statistical analysis

Putative target sites were probed in silico by target prediction algorithms (TargetScan),32 and the Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/)33 was used to survey tissue-specific expression of NRDC. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed with GSEA software (GSEA 4.0.3; Broad Institute, Boston, MA, USA) and the gene ontology libraries for Hallmark gene sets (Human MSigDB Collections).34,35 A gene set enrichment bubble plot was drawn using SRplot.36 Data are presented as mean ± SEM and were analyzed using paired Student’s t test. Analyses were performed using GraphPad Version 10.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Clinical characteristics

Clinical characteristics of the study participants, training intensities, and blood chemistry are shown in Supplementary Table 1. As expected, training improved oxygen uptake at peak exercise (maximal oxygen update (VO2max)), which is a hallmark of adaptation to aerobic exercise training at a whole-body level. When separating by sex, the increase in maximal aerobic capacity is more pronounced in women as compared to men, and only the men decrease in fat mass (Supplementary Table 1B). The exercise protocol (twice daily, 6 days per week, for 3 weeks) was designed to provide a robust metabolic and physiological stimulus. While this training volume may not be the most common in clinical practice, similar high-frequency training regimens have been utilized in research settings to maximize metabolic adaptations within a limited timeframe.4,37 The proportional VO₂@RER1.0 (a full name and refers to the VO2 level when the respiratory exchange ratio (RER) equals 1.0) increase, which aligns with expected adaptations to the training, suggests that training was primarily performed near the lactate threshold level.

Differentially expressed miRNAs in EV-associated evRNAs derived from serum after training

We previously found that the abundance of miR-20b-5p is increased in exosome-enriched EVs prepared from the serum of men with type 2 diabetes.27 Using a similar strategy, we isolated exosome-enriched EVs from serum, extracted RNA as evRNA, and determined miRNA expression profiles of evRNAs following endurance training. Size distribution and concentration of EVs in serum as determined by NTA did not differ before and after the training intervention (Supplementary Fig. 1). First, miRNA expression was profiled using an RT-qPCR panel with evRNAs extracted from 5 subjects (2 men and 3 women). In this first screening, 8 extracellular miRNAs—including miR-136-3p, miR-28-5p, miR-208a-3p, miR-139-5p, miR-92b-3p, miR-375, miR-1, and miR-421—were upregulated among the 179 miRNAs included in the qPCR panel (p < 0.05, middle column in Table 1). Based on the p value and fold change (FC) value (p < 0.05 and FC > 2), we selected these 8 miRNAs to analyze in all subjects.

Next, we validated these results using individual miRNA assays in evRNA isolated from the entire cohort. This analysis confirmed that expression of miR-136-3p, miR-139-5p, and miR-1 was increased in serum exosome-enriched EVs by exercise training (right column in Table 1). Of the 3 miRNAs that were found to be highly expressed in serum exosome-enriched EVs following exercise training, miR-1 has been consistently detected in human skeletal muscle and serum after acute exercise or training.22 MiR-139-5p is a tumor regulator, strongly associated with cell proliferation, invasion, and cancer progression.38, 39, 40, 41 In contrast, less is known regarding the targets of miR-136-3p and its mechanism of action. Notably, the miR-136-3p locus on the human genome is associated with skeletal muscle phenotypes, including exercise-induced effects, and metabolic diseases that influence both the quantity and quality of skeletal muscle over time.42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47 Thus, we prioritized our subsequent analyses around the functional role of miR-136-3p in human skeletal muscle.

Pancreatic islets release and skeletal muscle cells uptake miR-136-3p

Next, we aimed to identify the potential source of the elevated serum miR-136-3p levels following training. In human skeletal muscle, miR-136-3p is lowly expressed, and levels are unaltered after exercise training.48,30 The expression of miR-136-3p was detected in human pancreatic islets.49 The miR-136-3p expression is highly specific to beta cells, along with other miRNAs encoded by the DLK1-MEG3 miRNA cluster, with islets from donors with type 2 diabetes showing a specific reduction in miRNA-136-3p.46 Thus, we measured the expression of miR-136-3p and the islet-specific miRNAs miR-375 and miR-200c in culture medium from both human myotubes and isolated islets. In human myotube culture medium, miR-375 was undetectable and miR-200c expression was low (Fig. 1A) as compared to miR-1, a well described muscle microRNA (myomiR). Compared to miR-200c, miR-136-3p was also lowly expressed but detectable in myotube culture medium. Along with pancreatic islet-specific miRNAs miR-375 and miR-200c, miR-136-3p was detected in media from cultured human pancreatic islets (Fig. 1B). This confirms human pancreatic islets release miR-136-3p into the culture media, making islets a possible source of serum miR-136-3p.

Exercise triggers metabolic processes and structural remodeling of myocytes, with exercise-induced adaptations improving skeletal muscle performance.4 To test whether the exercise training-induced external miRNA miR-136-3p could be taken up by skeletal muscle, we loaded Cy3-labeled miR-136-3p into EVs and added the miR-136-3p-loaded EVs to the culture medium of human myotubes. We used EVs collected from human serum and EVs collected from HEK293 culture medium as vehicles for the labeled miRNAs (Fig. 1C and 1D). Human myotubes, as shown in the bright-field images (left in Fig. 1C), are aligned and fused to form multinucleated myotubes, and the fluorescence image (right in Fig. 1C) shows Cy3-miR-136-3p is detected in the whole cytoplasm of human myotubes. Cy3 fluorescence was not detected when cells were exposed to Cy3-miR-136-3p not loaded into EVs (Supplementary Fig. 2). Thus, EVs appear to be required for Cy3-miR-136-3p delivery into human myotubes, with similar efficacy noted using EVs derived from human serum or HEK293 culture medium (Fig. 1D). To determine whether the miRNA sequence is directing targeting to myotubes, we loaded EVs with control RNA sequences different from miR-136-3p-labeled forms using TexasRed (TexasRed-control RNA, Fig. 1E) and noted a similar uptake of miR-136-3p. Thus, miRNAs are efficiently delivered into myotubes, and while delivery is dependent on EVs, this process appears to be independent of EV origin and miRNA sequence.

NRDC is an miR-136-3p target in human myotubes and is altered in response to exercise training and inactivity in human skeletal muscle

To identify possible miR-136-3p target genes in human skeletal muscle, we performed a microarray-based expression analysis. Using this approach, we identified candidate mRNAs that are down-regulated following transfection of miR-136-3p into human myotubes. We focused on genes that were downregulated after miR-136-3p transfection, considering level of downregulation, the existence of putative miR-136-3p target sites, and tissue-specific expression (Table 2). Ten candidate genes were selected and, of these, 5 had possible miR-136-3p target sites and 4 were skeletal muscle tissue-specific according to the Human Protein Atlas. Of these candidates, NRDC was markedly downregulated following miR-136-3p transfection in human myotubes. This gene also had a conserved miR-136-3p target site in its 3’ region and displayed marked tissue specificity in skeletal muscle in the Human Protein Atlas dataset (Table 2 and Fig. 2A and 2B). Next, to investigate whether miR-136-3p directly targets the NRDC gene, we expressed luciferase reporter for the NRDC 3’ region containing the predicted miR-136-3p target sites in HEK293 cells. Overexpression of miR-136-3p decreased luciferase activity for NRDC 3’UTR (Fig. 2C). This decrease was recovered in a concentration-dependent manner in response to an increase of the miR-136-3p inhibitor (Fig. 2C). Overexpression of miR-136-3p markedly reduced both NRDC mRNA expression and protein abundance (Fig. 2D and 2E). These data indicate that miR-136-3p binds to the 3’ region of NRDC resulting in reduced NRDC mRNA.

NRDC is an N-arginine dibasic convertase belonging to the metalloprotease family, with functions that vary depending on its cellular localization. NRDC acts as a peptidase in some contexts and as a transcriptional cofactor in adipocytes and pancreatic beta cells.50 However, despite the high levels of expression in skeletal muscle,51 the role of NRDC function in skeletal muscle or response to exercises or training is unknown. To determine whether skeletal muscle NRDC is altered after exercise training, mRNA expression levels were analyzed using the publicly available MetaMex datasets29 of human skeletal muscle responses to exercise and inactivity. NRDC expression was unaltered after acute aerobic exercise (Supplementary Fig. 3A) or acute resistance exercise (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Selecting datasets in MetaMex and GEO databases with a similar training protocol as is used in this current study, we found that 6 weeks (Fig. 2F) and 2 months (Fig. 2G) of endurance training were associated with a downregulation of NRDC mRNA in human skeletal muscle. Furthermore, limb immobilization was associated with an upregulation of NRDC mRNA in human skeletal muscle (Fig. 2H). Taken together, this indicates that NRDC responds to exercise training and inactivity of comparatively longer durations, suggesting a role in long-term adaptation of skeletal muscle to physical stress.

miR-136-3p and NRDC regulate myogenesis co-ordinately and independently

To assess whether miR-136-3p and NRDC affect metabolism and functional properties of skeletal muscle, we transfected primary human myotubes with miR-136-3p or silenced NRDC expression and then conducted a microarray analysis of gene expression. Differential expression gene (DEG) analysis found 448 genes altered in miR-136-3p-transfected myotubes and 177 genes in the NRDC-silenced myotubes (Fig. 3A). Notably, only 19 genes with common DEGs were identified (Fig. 3B). GSEA of the microarray data showed several common gene sets associated positively or negatively in miR-136-3p-transfected and NRDC-silenced myotubes (Fig. 3C). The gene sets of cholesterol homeosis and hypoxia were enriched both in miR-136-3p-transfected and in NRDC-silenced myotubes, in addition to gene sets of epithelial mesenchymal transition, protein secretion and down-regulated in response to ultraviolet (UV) radiation. Furthermore, gene sets relating to cell cycle check points (including mitotic spindle, E2F targets, and G2M checkpoint) were identified. This suggests that the miR-136-3p transfection and the NRDC silencing results in similar cellular responses and, as such, they may target similar signaling pathways. GSEA data of the gene expression profiles identified two gene sets, including oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid metabolism, in miR-136-3p-transfected myotubes. Additionally, the glycolysis gene set was positively enriched in NRDC-silenced myotubes (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. 2). The gene sets related to oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid metabolism, and glycolysis constitute major metabolic pathways that produce energy needed to sustain cellular homeostasis. In addition, several genes related to metabolism were enriched. Additionally, gene sets related to cell proliferation and muscle growth were enriched, implicating miR-136-3p and NRDC in cell proliferation and growth. While the gene set of myogenesis was negatively enriched in miR-136-3p-transfected myotubes, it was positively enriched in NRDC-silenced myotubes. To determine the relationship between miR-136-3p transfection and NRDC silencing on myogenesis, we transfected human myoblast cells at the point when differentiation was initiated. We then analyzed the expression of skeletal muscle differentiation marker genes. In response to miR-136-3p transfection, the expression of the creatine kinase M-type (CKM) gene decreased (Fig. 3D). CKM is highly expressed in skeletal muscle and heart muscle, with levels rapidly increasing at the onset of differentiation (Fig. 3F). Myoblast determination protein 1 (MYOD) is upregulated following NRDC silencing (Fig. 3E). MYOD controls skeletal muscle differentiation, and MYOD expression decreases as myoblast cells undergo differentiation (Fig. 3G). Thus, miR-136-3p transfection in myoblasts decreased CKM expression, thereby blunting the increase in CKM mRNA during the onset of differentiation, without affecting MYOD expression. In contrast, NRDC silencing in myoblasts increased MYOD expression, which decreased with differentiation, without affecting CKM expression. These findings are consistent with the enrichment directions of the GSEA results, whereby the enrichment of the gene set of myogenesis was negatively enriched by miR-136-3p overexpression and positively enriched by NRDC silencing.

miR-136-3p upregulates mitochondrial function and glucose uptake, while silencing miR-136-3p and NRDC induces a glycolytic shift in human myotubes

Oxidative phosphorylation is the process where energy is used through a series of protein complexes in the inner-membrane of mitochondria to produce ATP, and training improves the adaptation of oxidative phosphorylation to physical exercise in the skeletal muscle.52 Notably, we found that the gene set of oxidative phosphorylation was enriched in miR-136-3p-transfected myotubes (Fig. 3C). To determine the effects on oxidative phosphorylation in mitochondria of miR-136-3p-transfected or NRDC-silenced human myotubes, we used the Seahorse XFe24 analyzer Mitochondrial Stress Test. We found that miR-136-3p transfection increased the maximal oxygen consumption rate, while NRDC silencing was without effect (Fig. 4A). Both miR-136-3p transfection and NRDC silencing increased acidification to a similar extent (Fig. 4B). Basal and maximal respiration was also increased in miR-136-3p transfected myotubes (Fig. 4C). Both miR-136-3p transfection and NRDC silencing caused a glycolytic shift in the myotube mitochondrial capacity.

In addition to oxidative phosphorylation, GSEA data showed an enrichment of the fatty acid metabolism gene set in miR-136-3p-transfected myotubes, as well as the glycolysis gene set in NRDC-silenced myotubes (Fig. 3C). Fatty acid catabolism affects glucose metabolism. Regular exercise training improves glucose uptake and insulin sensitivity, while inactivity leads to whole-body metabolic dysfunction with impaired glucose control and insulin resistance disease states such as type 2 diabetes and obesity.53,54 Thus, we explored whether miR-136-3p and NRDC regulate glucose metabolism in myotubes. We found that miR-136-3p transfection increased glucose uptake in human myotubes; however, NRDC silencing was without effect (Fig. 4D). Conversely, neither miR-136-3p transfection nor NRDC silencing altered glucose oxidation, fatty acid oxidation, or protein synthesis in primary human myotubes or lactate release from culture myotubes (Fig. 4E and 4G–4J). Both miR-136-3p transfection and NRDC silencing resulted in similar trends for increased glucose incorporation to glycogen in human myotubes (Fig. 4F). Collectively these data suggest that miR-136-3p increases glucose uptake in myotubes independently from the targeting of NRDC.

Increased glucose uptake by miR-136-3p transfection is independent of insulin- and contraction-mediated glucose transport

To investigate mechanisms by which miR-136-3p overexpression increased glucose uptake in myotubes, we determined mRNA expression of the insulin-sensitive glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4), Tre-2/BUB2/CDC16 domain family member 4 (TBC1D4; also called AKT substrate of 160 kDa, AS160), and the energy sensor AMPKα. GLUT4 mRNA content was unchanged by miR-136-3p overexpression (Fig. 5A). Meanwhile, miR-136-3p transfection decreased AMPKα RNA content (Fig. 5A) but did not change AMPKα protein abundance (Fig. 5B). Glucose uptake is controlled by signal transduction at the level of TBC1D4 and translocation of GLUT4-containing vesicles to the cell surface. MiR-136-3p transfection increased TBC1D4 mRNA and protein content in myotubes (Fig. 5A and 5C) while reducing both basal and insulin-dependent phosphorylation of TBC1D4 (Fig. 5D). TBC1D4 is a downstream target of the insulin-activated kinase AKT.55 AKT protein abundance was unaltered by miR-136-3p transfection; however, as observed for TBC1D4, both basal and insulin-dependent phosphorylation of AKT was reduced (Fig. 5E and 5F).

Discussion

Regular physical activity lowers the risk of developing several non-communicable diseases including type 2 diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and most cancers. However, the mechanisms underlying the positive effects of physical exercise on these health benefits remain to be completely understood. Exerkines have emerged as possible mediators of exercise-induced effects.14,56 Indeed, the delivery of exosomes from exercised individuals has been proposed to confer improved systematic metabolism as well as to modulate biological processes in untrained individuals receiving exosomes of trained individuals.57, 58, 59, 60 While endurance training did not affect the diameter or concentration of serum-derived EVs in the healthy individuals studied here, we found several miRNAs were altered. Although some human studies have reported that EVs were increased in response to various exercise regimes, there is a general agreement that the size and concentration of EVs are unaltered.22 A consensus for exercise-induced changes in contents of EVs has yet to emerge, possibly reflecting the fact that exercise modality and duration have differential effects on EVs concentration and content.61, 62, 63 Furthermore, the field is lacking standardized protocols to isolate and distinguish exosomes from other EVs.

We found the exosome-enriched EV content of miR-1-3p, miR-136-3p, and miR-139-5p was elevated after 3 weeks of endurance training. A systematic analysis of exercise-responsive miRNAs highlights that miR-1-3p is consistently increased in response to exercise,22 with effects generally noted in response to acute exercise bouts.64,65 Conversely, exercise-induced changes in miR-136-3p and miR-139-5p have not been reported previously either in response to an acute exercise bout or exercise training. We focused further validation of the exercise-responsive miRNAs on miR-136-3p, since the functional role of miR-136-3p has been less studied than those of miR-1-3p and miR-139-3p. Specifically, the role of miR-1-3p in muscle is well studied, and miR-139-5p has already been linked to the role of proliferation, invasion, and dissemination of a wide variety of tumors.38, 39, 40, 41 While circulating miR-136-3p has been implicated in angiogenesis66 and tumorigenesis,67 and designated a potential biomarker for a number of disease conditions,68, 69, 70, 71, 72 less is known about the physiological role of miR-136-3p in tissue function. We detected increased miR-136-3p following relatively long and intense exercise interventions while, in contrast, miR-136-3p expression is unchanged in skeletal muscle in response to 2 weeks exercise training in young healthy men.30 Furthermore, miR-136-3p is unaltered during differentiation of cultured human myoblasts into myotubes.48 As the packaging mechanisms of miRNAs into exosomes remain incompletely understood, further investigations into the mechanism by which miR-136-3p exosomal levels are upregulated without increased expression in skeletal muscle are warranted. Further studies are required to determine whether there is a selective packaging of miRNAs like miR-136-3p into EVs. Although skeletal muscle is a likely source of exercise-induced exosomes, exercise training does not alter skeletal muscle miR-136-3p content.30 Thus, we considered that other organs are a potential source of exosomal miR-136-3p. Indeed, our in silico and molecular analysis provide evidence that the pancreatic islet may be such a source. We detected measurable amounts of miR-136-3p in media from cultured human islets as well as cultured human myotubes, indicating that both these tissues may secrete miR-136-3p. However, whether this also happens in vivo remains to be established. We also provide evidence that, as with other miRNAs, once miR-136-3p is packaged in exosomes, it is taken up by human skeletal myotubes.

The identification of miRNA target genes provides insight into biological function. It has been established that miR-136-3p regulates tumorigenesis by targeting kruppel-like factor 7 (KLF7).73 Bioinformatic approaches have been used to identify possible miRNA regulators of the phosphatase, phosphatase and tensin homolog deleted from chromosome 10 (PTEN), with miR-136-3p identified and further implicated in PTEN targeting to regulate vascularization and bone formation.66 However, each miRNA has multiple target genes, and this profile is also tissue-dependent. Notably, here we identified NRDC as a direct target of miR-136-3p in skeletal muscle. NRDC has multiple functions, including a zinc peptidase that selectively cleaves dibasic sites,74,75 a specific receptor for heparin-binding epidermal growth factor-like growth factor (HB-EGF),76 and a transcriptional coregulator.50,77,78 The multiple functions of NRDC might depend on cellular localization.79 In pancreatic beta-cells, NRDC regulates glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS)50 and plays a role in the maintenance of thermogenesis in brown adipose tissue.78 Approximately 80% of mice lacking NRDC die within 48 h after birth, and surviving mice are growth retarded and show neurological impairments.80 Although NRDC mRNA is markedly expressed in skeletal muscle, the precise functional role of NRDC in this tissue remains to be determined. Using publicly available data, we provide evidence that skeletal muscle NRDC expression changes in response to exercise training and inactivity. Thus, in similar situations to this study where we note increased EV-carried miR-136-3p, there is a reduction in the target gene NRDC. Whether this is a response to training-induced miR-136-3p in vivo remains to be determined.

Exercise and training enhance glucose uptake and metabolism in skeletal muscle. We show that miR-136-3p transfection in myotubes increased glucose uptake by an insulin-independent mechanism. This effect does not appear to be mediated via changes in GLUT4 abundance or signal transduction via canonical mechanisms involving AKT or TBC1D4. Further investigations are needed to explore the precise mechanism of miR-136-3p-mediated increase in glucose uptake. Additionally, we show that miR-136-3p induces a glycolytic shift in mitochondrial function, and that both miR-136-3p and NRDC play roles in the regulation of myogenesis. As muscle remodeling is a response to repeated exercise bouts, we hypothesize that miR-136-3p primarily regulates skeletal muscle adaptation to exercise, including muscle remodeling. Silencing of NRDC also induced a glycolytic shift in myotubes, suggesting that the effect of miR-136-3p may be partly dependent on NRDC. The function of NRDC may be influenced by its subcellular localization. Mitochondrially localized NRDC recruits mitochondrial chaperones to assist in enzyme folding in brown adipose tissue.81 While the mechanisms controlling NRDC subcellular localization remain unclear, the efficacy of miR-136-3p in silencing NRDC might also be dependent on cellular localization. These findings suggest that miR-136-3p plays a pivotal role in skeletal muscle adaptation to exercise, with its effects likely modulated by NRDC and its subcellular localization, warranting further investigation into the underlying mechanisms of this regulation. While miR-136-3p overexpression in skeletal muscle resulted in over 450 significantly altered gene expressions, silencing of NRDC only altered 200 genes. This suggests that miR-136-3p remodels skeletal muscle beyond the effects of reduced NRDC.

Limitations

This study has a number of limitations, firstly more women than men were included in the study cohort, which might reduce the generalizability of the data. This imbalance may be particularly relevant given that previous studies have reported sex-based differences in miRNA expression and in exercise-induced adaptations. Sex differences have been reported in serum miR-136-3p in rats, while a study of older human patients with brain injury found no differences.72 We did not control for diet during the training study. The women were enrolled without controlling for stage in menstrual cycle, which could also affect the results.82 Although direct evidence on miR-136-3p expression across the menstrual cycle is limited, hormonal fluctuations are known to influence miRNA profiles, and it is plausible that this may contribute to variability in our findings. Similarly, while specific data on sex differences in miR-136-3p expression are scarce, differential expression of several miRNAs between men and women has been documented in other contexts, suggesting that sex hormones may regulate miR-136-3p as well. This lack of control adds biological variability that we could not account for in our analysis. Previous studies have indicated that the training response may be different in men and women;63 however, our sample size does not permit analysis by sex. While human β-cells express high levels of miR-136-3p, we cannot rule out that other tissues contribute to the training-induced increase in serum miR-136-3p. Human islets are a scarce resource, and we did not have sufficient culture media to provide direct evidence that islet-derived miR-136-3p is targeted to skeletal muscle. Post-training samples were obtained after 1–2 days of rest and, thus, in some samples there could be some carry-over after the last bout of exercise. The EV isolation method employed has previously been shown to enrich the exosome fraction, but it is not sufficient to exclusively isolate exosomes and so may also contain other extracellular particles. The training-mediated reduction of the miR-136-3p target gene, NRDC, in skeletal muscle is noted from publicly available data from trained individuals and not directly in the original cohort, as we did not have skeletal muscle samples to analyze. Together, these limitations—particularly the lack of sex- and cycle phase-specific controls, as well as the indirect assessment of skeletal muscle targeting and gene expression—mean that caution is warranted in interpreting the mechanistic implications of our findings. These factors underscore the importance of careful consideration when comparing data from different exercise protocols and sample collection. Future studies should aim to control for hormonal status, include balanced cohorts, and assess miRNA expression across sexes and menstrual cycle phases to better understand the physiological significance of miR-136-3p in exercise adaptation.

Conclusion

This study identifies endurance training-responsive miRNAs, with a particular focus on miR-136-3p within exosome-rich EVs, as potential mediators of inter-organ communication. We propose that miR-136-3p is released from pancreatic islets (and possibly other tissues) and delivered to skeletal muscle, where it induces a glycolytic shift in mitochondrial metabolism, partially by targeting the NRDC gene. We also suggest that miR-136-3p finetunes glucose metabolism and myogenesis-related pathways in skeletal muscle cells. This hypothesis could be expected to result in increased miR-136-3p in skeletal muscle miR-136-3p content following training; however, this is not a consistent finding in other training studies. In contrast, a down-regulation of the identified target, NRDC, is a consistent finding, possibly reflecting a rapid degradation or functional consumption of miR-136-3p upon delivery.

Taken together, our findings highlight potential miRNA-driven mechanisms by which exercise adaptations in one organ may influence metabolic function in another, offering new insight into the molecular basis of inter-organ signaling during exercise. Given the substantial interindividual variability typically observed in human studies addressing circulating miRNA expression, along with sex-specific differences and the influence of menstrual cycle status, a larger sample size would have been required to adequately address these sources of variation; thus, it is probable that there are additional exercise-responsive miRNAs that would respond to this type of training. The results presented here underscore the role of miR-136-3p in mediating inter-organ communication and highlight its potential as a target for enhancing exercise-induced metabolic adaptations in skeletal muscle.
