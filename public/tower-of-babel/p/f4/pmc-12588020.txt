Validity of the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision codes for lithium toxicity in adult patients at hospital admission: a cohort study in Canada
Authors: Fatemeh Ahmadi, Flory Tsobo Muanda, Jennifer Ehiwario, Eric McArthur, Racquel Jandoc, Justin Slater, Akshaya Vasudev, Matthew A Weir, Edward G Clark, Soham Rej, Nathan Herrmann, Amit X Garg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo assess the validity of the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) healthcare database diagnosis codes for lithium toxicity at hospital admission in Ontario, Canada.DesignPopulation-based retrospective validation study.SettingA total of 152 hospitals linked to a provincial laboratory database in Ontario, Canada, from 2007 to 2023.ParticipantsPatients 50 years of age or older taking lithium with hospital-based serum lithium laboratory measurements during admission to the hospital (n=2804).Outcome measuresSensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) comparing an ICD-10 diagnostic coding algorithm for lithium toxicity to a serum lithium concentration of 1.5 mmol/L or more. The codes used in the algorithm were T568, T435, Y495, X41 and X49. Introduction

Large, population-based healthcare administrative databases are an invaluable source of information for health services research and quality improvement. These databases allow large samples to be analysed and avoid the need for primary data collection. However, data accuracy may be limited since such data are collected for administrative purposes. Typically, trained personnel use the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) coding system to record diagnoses and procedures when people present to the emergency department or are admitted to a hospital. These personnel review information written by physicians in patients’ medical charts to assign codes; they do not review or interpret symptoms or test results. In some settings, physicians may assign ICD codes directly. Regardless of who assigns the codes, inaccuracies can still occur due to documentation limitations, variability in coding practices or the complexity of the clinical presentation.1,3 As a result, the accuracy of codes is not guaranteed, and the coding process may introduce measurement errors and misclassification. Thus, it is crucial to understand the accuracy of codes used to identify characteristics and outcomes when interpreting the analysis of healthcare administrative databases.

Lithium has a very narrow therapeutic range. Lithium is primarily eliminated from the body by the kidneys, and conditions which reduce its renal clearance, such as older age or certain concomitant medications, increase the concentration of serum lithium and cause toxicity. Annual reports of the American Association of Poison Control Centers’ National Poison Data System reveal almost 7000 cases of lithium toxicity per year.4 5 A study in Ontario estimated that nearly 4% of chronic lithium users had at least one hospital admission with lithium toxicity within a decade of use.6

Although there is no simple relationship between serum lithium concentration and the presence or severity of clinical symptoms in chronic lithium users,7 most chronic lithium users show clinical symptoms of toxicity at lithium levels of 1.5 mmol/L or more.8 9 To date, our search of bibliographic databases has failed to identify any validation of recorded ICD-10 codes within administrative databases for lithium toxicity. We conducted this study to determine the diagnostic accuracy of an algorithm consisting of several ICD-10 codes for lithium toxicity to detect a serum lithium concentration≥1.5 mmol/L during a hospital admission. This was done in hospital admission settings in Ontario, Canada. Based on our findings of the validity of other ICD-10 codes to detect laboratory conditions,10 11 we anticipated that the codes for lithium toxicity would demonstrate moderate sensitivity and the serum lithium concentration would be much higher in the presence of the codes versus when the codes are absent. We restricted our analysis to patients admitted to the hospital (either directly or through an emergency department visit), as serious lithium toxicity is a life-threatening condition that requires hospitalisation.12 13

Methods

We first searched for the keywords and subject headings related to ‘lithium’ and ‘international classification of diseases’ in Medline and Embase to find studies assessing the validity of ICD-10 codes within administrative databases for lithium toxicity. The search strategy is presented in the online supplemental appendix A.

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective population-based validation study using health administrative databases, which are linked through unique encoded identifiers and analysed at the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences (ICES) in the province of Ontario, Canada (2007–2023). During the study period, over 15 million people lived in Ontario, of which 3.7 million were 65 years of age or older.14 All Ontario residents have universal access to hospital care and physician services through a government-funded single-payer system. All individuals 65 years of age or older receive universal outpatient drug coverage. Individuals under the age of 65 receive such coverage if they have high drug costs relative to their income or are collecting disability support or social assistance. We used a diagnostic test assessment framework to obtain metrics of sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) by comparing ICD-10 diagnostic coding algorithms for lithium toxicity to serum lithium laboratory measurements as a reference standard in the hospital admission setting (either direct hospital admissions or hospital admissions that came through an emergency department visit) (see online supplemental appendix B). We also compared serum lithium concentration and the change in serum lithium concentration from baseline levels in patients who were positive for the code algorithm to those who were negative.

The use of data in this study was authorised under section 45 of Ontario’s Personal Health Information Protection Act, which does not require review by a research ethics board or informed consent from patients. The reporting of this study follows STARD (The Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic accuracy studies) guidelines set out for studies of diagnostic accuracy and The REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data (RECORD) statement for pharmacoepidemiology (see online supplemental appendix C and D).15 16

Data sources

We obtained information on patient demographics, prescription drug use, baseline characteristics and the study outcome from six databases. The datasets were linked using unique encoded identifiers and analysed at ICES. We used the Ontario Laboratories Information System (OLIS) to identify laboratory measurements, including serum lithium levels. This dataset contains laboratory test orders and results from hospital and community laboratories. The Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI) National Ambulatory Care Reporting System (NACRS) contains ambulatory care information on emergency department visits. The CIHI Discharge Abstract Database (CIHI-DAD) contains diagnostic and procedural information during hospitalisations. The Ontario Drug Benefit Plan database includes records of prescriptions from outpatient pharmacies. The dispensing of medications for patients aged 65 and older is accurately recorded in this database with an error rate of less than 1%.17 The Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP) database contains all physician claims for medical services covered under the provincial health insurance plan. The Registered Persons Database includes demographic information, such as birth date and sex, for all Ontario residents who have ever been covered by OHIP. We have previously conducted several diagnostic accuracy studies using Ontario health administrative databases for important laboratory-based clinical outcomes, including acute kidney injury, hyponatraemia and hyperkalaemia.1018,22

Participants

To create the cohort, we included all patients who had at least one serum lithium measurement during a hospital visit between 1 April 2007 and 30 September 2023. We had information on hospital visits for both direct hospital admissions and emergency department visits. A hospital visit was defined as the individual’s first encounter with the hospital, marked by the date of the ED visit or, for those directly admitted, the hospital admission date. Given that ED visits can lead to either discharge or transfer to a hospital ward and our aim was to focus specifically on admitted patients, we only included individuals who were transferred from the emergency department to a hospital ward either on the same day or the day after their emergency department visit. As a result, our final cohort comprised patients admitted directly to the hospital as well as those admitted through the ED. The date of the hospital visit was considered the index date. In the case of multiple hospital visits for a patient, the first visit was selected. The highest lithium value was selected when multiple lithium measurements were taken during that hospital visit. We restricted the cohort to patients 50 years and older. This threshold was chosen for its clinical relevance: prior studies have identified age>50 years as a risk factor for lithium toxicity,23,25 and 50 years is commonly used to define older-age bipolar disorder,4 one of the primary indications for lithium therapy.26 We excluded patients who were not continuously receiving lithium before their index date (ie, those having less than two lithium prescriptions within the 210 days prior to the index date). To ensure that we captured the whole hospitalisation period of every patient, we restricted the cohort to patients admitted for no longer than 90 days. Considering that physicians will order lithium measurements promptly if a patient is suspected of lithium toxicity at the time of hospital presentation, we restricted the cohort to those who had their first lithium measurement within the first 3 days after the index date.

Diagnostic test (lithium toxicity International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision coding algorithms)

For hospital encounters in Canada, trained coders use ICD codes to describe diagnoses and procedures written by physicians in patients’ medical charts. The databases in this study used the ICD-10, which has been used in Canada since 2002. Several diagnosis codes may be recorded for each hospital visit, with one diagnosis identified as the most responsible diagnosis for the hospital encounter. We considered algorithms where lithium toxicity was recorded either in any field or in the most responsible diagnosis field. Within the emergency department (NACRS) database, coders are allowed to include up to 10 diagnoses per visit. In comparison, the CIHI-DAD provides the ability to record up to 25 diagnoses during a hospital admission. For hospital admissions that occurred through emergency department visits, we investigated all 35 diagnosis records, 10 from NACRS and 25 from CIHI-DAD, and we used the main diagnosis record from CIHI-DAD for the most responsible diagnosis analysis.

Since no specific ICD-10 code exists for lithium toxicity, we identified a set of plausible codes for non-intentional lithium poisoning through review of ICD-10 coding guidelines, with input from two investigators and a consultant psychiatrist. These codes cover toxicities related to medications used in psychiatric and neurologic disorders, as well as metals and chemicals that are unspecified or not elsewhere classified. After compiling these codes, we conducted a pilot study in a subset of patients examining median lithium levels for each code to refine the algorithm and remove codes unlikely to indicate true lithium toxicity. A full description of this process is provided in the online supplemental appendix E. For our algorithm, patients were considered to have a diagnosis of lithium toxicity if any of the codes T568, T435, Y495, X41 or X49 were present in the datasets, while T432 was not present. Reference standard (serum lithium values)

Serum lithium laboratory tests were performed either in an emergency department or during a hospital admission. Since lithium levels of 1.5 mmol/L or more in chronic lithium users are considered potentially toxic,23 we assessed how well the diagnosis codes identified patients with this serum lithium level.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise demographics, recorded conditions which suggested the indication for lithium use (ie, bipolar disorder), comorbidities, baseline laboratory measurements and concurrent medications. Descriptive statistics were also used to summarise several elements of hospital admission, such as receipt of acute dialysis and length of stay, and to compare the severity of the admitted events between code-positive and code-negative patients.

Sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV were calculated for the ICD-10 diagnostic coding algorithm to identify a serum lithium level≥1.5 mmol/L in the hospital admission setting, either directly or through an emergency department visit. The estimates were reported with 95% CIs. We reported lithium levels at hospital admissions as a median with IQR and compared the values in those who were code positive and code negative. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare the distributions of lithium values between groups, and p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. We performed all analyses with SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Patient and public involvement

No patients were involved in setting the research question or the outcome measures, nor were they involved in the design and implementation of the study. There are no plans to involve patients in the dissemination of results beyond our general media communication plan.

Discussion

In this population-based validation study, the sensitivity of ICD-10 codes for identifying a serum lithium measurement≥1.5 mmol/L at hospital admission (ie, either directly or through an emergency department visit) was moderate (84%). The median serum lithium measurement at hospital visits was significantly higher in those who were positive for the codes (1.7 (1.2 to 2.2) compared with those who were negative (0.6 (0.4 to 0.9)). The median change from prehospital lithium levels was also significantly higher in code-positive patients (0.7 (0.2 to 1.3) compared with code-negative patients 0.0 (−0.2 to 0.2)).

Based on the results of this study, the proposed ICD-10 coding algorithm works effectively in the hospital admission setting. Based on the median lithium levels at the hospital visit and the increase in lithium levels from prehospital baseline lithium levels, the algorithm seems to effectively distinguish between those with higher and lower serum lithium levels.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to validate the ICD-10 codes for the detection of lithium measurements≥1.5 mmol/L. In this study, we developed an algorithm that included the most related codes for the detection of lithium measurements≥1.5 mmol/L and used serum lithium laboratory measurement as a reference standard.

Our study does have several limitations. We did not have access to clinical information for the patients included in our analysis, preventing us from validating cases against a comprehensive definition of lithium toxicity, which does not rely solely on serum lithium concentration. We only reported the performance of ICD-10 codes for detecting a serum lithium concentration≥1.5 mmol/L, which, in the chronic lithium use setting, is a value that suggests toxicity. However, we do know that patients with lower levels of serum lithium can demonstrate symptoms of lithium toxicity, and rarely, this also occurs when the serum lithium concentration is in the normal range.27,29 Additionally, our datasets did not allow us to differentiate between acute and chronic lithium toxicity, which are distinct categories of lithium toxicity. Second, our study used lithium results from the OLIS, which compiles data from multiple laboratories using different assay methods. Because OLIS does not capture assay methodology, small variations in lithium measurements may affect the classification of toxic levels and the agreement between laboratory results and ICD-coded diagnoses of lithium toxicity.

Third, as the primary analysis in this study, we used a single lithium concentration from the index hospital admission as the reference for ICD code validation. In some patients, baseline values were also compared with admission levels, but these were used only descriptively. Since baseline and admission tests may not have been performed in the same laboratory, differences in assay methods could introduce variation; however, this would likely affect ICD code-positive and code-negative patients similarly.

We considered the codes’ performance in patients 50 years of age and older. Since the algorithm proposed in this study could only be validated in older adults due to data availability in this age group, its results may not be generalisable to younger patients, who may be less likely to develop lithium toxicity than older adults. Furthermore, this study was conducted in Ontario, Canada, and the codes may operate differently in other jurisdictions.

Conclusion

The diagnostic performance of the ICD-10 coding algorithms to identify lithium levels≥1.5 mmol/L was moderate at hospital admission. The presence or absence of the ICD-10 code for lithium toxicity successfully differentiates two groups of patients who had distinct higher and lower serum lithium values at hospital admission. The findings of this study inform the interpretation of ICD-10 lithium toxicity diagnosis codes when used in research and quality improvement initiatives.
