Optical demodulation and multi-dimensional identification via a monolithic perovskite heterojunction
Authors: Siyang Guo, Jihong Liu, Haoxuan Sun, Juan Wang, Jinfang Fan, Jingzhao Yu, Zicai Zhang, Shufang Wang, Liang Li, Shuang Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Position sensitive detectors (PSDs) based on the lateral photovoltaic effect (LPE) are distinguished by their non-contact sensing capabilities and exceptional self-powered properties, showcasing significant potential for diverse applications. However, the development of high-performance detectors that meet the demands of intelligent perception, particularly those capable of acquiring complex information from optical fields, remains a significant challenge. In this work, we propose a PSD utilizing an ITO/MAPbI3:P(VDF-TrFE)/Si heterojunction, which successfully achieves a broadband self-powered response ranging from 405 to 1064 nm. The modulation of the pyroelectric field enables a position sensitivity (PS) of 688.10 mV/mm, representing a substantial enhancement of 144.13 %. Simultaneously, the pyroelectric effect is found to significantly enhance the wavelength resolution. The device effectively decodes the digital information encoded with position-related ASCII code by directly evaluating the lateral photovoltage (LPV) values of the PSD across the visible to near-infrared spectrum, facilitating position and wavelength-resolved photoelectric demodulation. Furthermore, the PSD can mutually distinguish the wavelength, position, and intensity of incident light based on the LPV values and their enhancement ratios, without the need for additional systems, thus enabling the identification and coding of multi-dimensional optical information.

Introduction

Position detection technology is a critical component across various application domains. With the rapid advancements in modern science and technology, it has become essential in fields such as automation systems, medical imaging, precision control, and real-time monitoring [[1], [2], [3]]. In particular, position sensitive detector (PSDs), which work based on the lateral photovoltaic effect (LPE), presents promising application potential due to their advantages of high sensitivity, rapid response, non-contact measurement capabilities, and self-powered operation [[4], [5], [6]]. Nevertheless, traditional single-function position detection technologies have struggled to meet the diverse and precise requirements of complex applications. As a result, introducing novel modulation methods for PSDs and enhancing their ability to process composite information has become an urgent challenge that needs to be addressed.

The working mechanism of the LPE relies on the output voltage generated between two ipsilateral electrodes of a heterojunction or p-n junction under non-uniform illumination. The intensity of the LPE response is primarily influenced by the interface built-in electric field and surface transport capability [7]. In recent years, substantial advancements have been made in LPE performance through optimized material selection, innovative structural designs, and effective modulation strategies [5,8,9], such as utilizing piezo-optoelectronic coupling in n-3C-SiC/n-Si heterojunction to improve the low power consumption and high response characteristics or constructing double heterojunction to enhance the separation and migration efficiency of photogenerated carriers, and using nanowires and pyramid structures to enhance light absorption [[10], [11], [12]]. However, these methods mostly rely on external mechanical stimulation or complex interface engineering, and there are still limitations, such as relatively complex structural design and difficulty in achieving high functional integration. Recently, the organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite CH3NH3(MA)PbI3 has garnered considerable attention due to its exceptional photoelectric properties, including a suitable band gap, high light absorption coefficient, and long carrier diffusion length, alongside advantages such as low cost and ease of solution-based preparation [[13], [14], [15]]. When combined in heterojunctions with established narrow-bandgap semiconductors like Si, this perovskite can exhibit outstanding broadband self-powered photoelectric detection capabilities [16,17]. Furthermore, research indicates that the coupling of the pyroelectric field with the built-in field can efficiently modulate the longitudinal separation of charge carriers, thereby regulating the LPE response without the need for additional complex preparation processes or external power supplies [7,18]. Thus, the tunability of the PSD performance is expected through the introduction of a pyroelectric polymer into the perovskite precursor, which can optimize the crystallinity of the perovskite, the conductivity of the composite film, and, importantly, apply a pyroelectric field. Specifically, poly(vinylidene fluoride-tetrafluoroethylene) (P(VDF-TrFE)), a polymer of significant interest, has emerged as an ideal candidate for this modulation due to its pronounced pyroelectric response, flexible structure, straightforward processing, and exceptional chemical stability [19,20].

Inspired by the highly integrated characteristics of biological vision systems, the development of novel multi-functional integrated devices has become a central theme in contemporary research [21,22]. Especially in the field of artificial intelligence (AI), simulating and drawing on the capabilities of biological vision systems in multi-dimensional perception and information processing has become essential for promoting intelligent perception and cognitive systems. The human visual system, as a typical representative of the biological vision system, is capable of perceiving, processing, and integrating multi-dimensional information from the external environment in real time. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, optical signals are initially captured by photoreceptors in the retina and then converted into an electrical signal. These signals are conveyed to the visual cortex of the brain through a neural network, enabling advanced signal decoding and processing [6,23,24]. Different from the traditional photodetectors, the PSDs not only sense the wavelength and intensity of the incident light, but also capture the spatial position information, significantly expanding their potential applications in multi-dimensional information processing. This capability holds significant promise in the field of AI, particularly in multimodal data fusion and intelligent systems. However, significant deficiencies remain in the efficiency and capacity of PSDs when handling complex data and high-dimensional information. In this work, inspired by the high integration and versatility of the human visual system, we developed a PSD with position and wavelength-resolved photoelectric demodulation, along with multi-dimensional identification and code capabilities across the visible to near-infrared spectrum, as illustrated in Fig. 1b The device is constructed on an ITO/MAPbI3:P(VDF-TrFE)/Si heterostructure. The pyroelectric effect induced by instantaneous temperature changes in the MAPbI3:P(VDF-TrFE) film results in a position sensitivity (PS) of 688.10 mV/mm, reflecting a substantial increase of 144.13 %. Moreover, by correlating specific positions with ASCII codes, we achieve effective position and wavelength-resolved photoelectric demodulation by evaluating the LPV of various wavelengths in a broadband spectrum. Importantly, the PSD can simultaneously discern the wavelength, position, and intensity of the incident light by analyzing the differential LPV values and their enhancement ratios. With its ability to capture and process multi-dimensional information, the PSD offers significant advantages in complex application environments, providing reliable support for future multi-functional and multi-dimensional signal decoding and processing. This work presents a feasible technical solution to meet the demands of modern science and technology.

Materials and methods

Materials

The n-type Si wafer (100, 1–10 Ω cm, 500 μm) was purchased from Suzhou Research Materials Microtech Co., Ltd. Lead(II) iodide (PbI2, 99.99 %) and methylammonium iodide (MAI, 99.9 %) were purchased from Xi'an Yuri Solar Co., Ltd. Poly(vinylidene fluoride-tetrafluoroethylene) (P(VDF-TrFE)) was purchased from Beijing Apslong Technology Co., Ltd. N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF, AR, >99.5 %, CAS: 68–12–2) were bought from Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co., Ltd.

Methods

Fabrication process of the ITO/MAPbI3:P(VDF-TrFE)/Si heterojunction PSD

The n-type Si wafer with a resistivity of 1–10 Ω·cm was utilized as the substrate and was thoroughly cleaned with acetone, ethanol, and deionized water. To prepare solutions of different concentrations, various amounts of P(VDF-TrFE) (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 mg) were dissolved in 250 μL DMF. Subsequently, PbI2 and MAI powders were added to these P(VDF-TrFE) solutions to prepare perovskite precursor solutions, achieving P(VDF-TrFE) concentrations of 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1 wt %. The resulting mixtures were stirred at room temperature for 24 h to ensure complete dissolution. The deposition of the perovskite film onto the substrate was carried out in a glove box under controlled conditions. Spin-coating was performed at 4000 rpm for 20 s to achieve a uniform coating of the precursor solution. The samples were then annealed at 90 °C for 6 h. Following this, an approximately 20 nm-thick ITO conductive layer was prepared onto the MAPbI3:P(VDF-TrFE) composite film using radio frequency (RF) magnetron sputtering at room temperature. Finally, two Ag electrodes, each 50 nm thick and separated by 0.6 mm, were fabricated onto the ITO layer to complete the device.

Material characterization

The crystalline structure of the films was determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Bruker, D8 Advance). Absorbance spectra were obtained through a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Hitachi, U-4100). Additionally, the morphology of the MAPbI3:P(VDF-TrFE) film was characterized with a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (FEI Nova NanoSE M450).

Photoelectrical performance measurements

The longitudinal and lateral current-voltage (I-V) characteristics of the PSD were tested using a Keithley 4200 source meter. The position-dependent LPV curves were systematically collected using a customized measurement system that included a voltmeter (Keithley 2700) and an electrically operated displacement stage. Illumination for the measurements was provided by five continuous wave (CW) lasers, with wavelengths of 405, 532, 671, 780, and 1064 nm. The output power range of each laser during the test is 0.01–10 mW, and the laser spot diameter is about 0.1 mm. The output power of lasers was evaluated with a Thorlabs PM100D optical power meter. Pulsed light illuminations were conducted using a mechanical chopper. The time-dependent LPV (LPV-t) response characteristics of the PSD were recorded and monitored with a Tektronix Series 3 mixed domain oscilloscope.

Results and discussion

The structural configuration and fundamental photoelectric characteristics of the ITO/MAPbI3:P(VDF-TrFE)/Si heterojunction PSD are initially described and analyzed. Fig. 2a illustrates the device structural diagram, which consists of an n-type silicon layer, a p-type MAPbI3 layer, and an ITO conductive layer. Details of the fabrication process are outlined in the experimental section. In this study, the purpose of employing the ITO/MAPbI3:P(VDF-TrFE)/Si structure is mainly attributed to three factors: enhancing carrier extraction and collection efficiency, optimizing lateral charge transport behavior, and improving the environmental stability of MAPbI3. Fig. 2b presents the band structure of the ITO/MAPbI3:P(VDF-TrFE)/Si heterojunction, highlighting the carrier transport mechanism within the device. The MAPbI3 material has a band gap of approximately 1.5 eV, with the conduction band minimum (CBM) and valence band maximum (VBM) at −3.9 eV and −5.4 eV, respectively [25]. In comparison, Si has a band gap of about 1.12 eV, with CBM and VBM levels positioned at −4.0 eV and −5.12 eV [26]. This stepwise band alignment facilitates the transport and separation of carriers. Considering that the band gaps of MAPbI3 and Si are approximately 1.5 eV and 1.12 eV (corresponding to absorption edge wavelengths of about 830 nm and 1100 nm), light with a wavelength below 830 nm is mainly absorbed by the MAPbI3 layer. In the wavelength range of 830–1100 nm, Si becomes the primary absorbing layer. The photogenerated electron-hole pairs are efficiently separated by the built-in electric field at the heterojunction. Holes migrate towards the ITO layer and are ultimately collected by the top electrode, while electrons move into the Si layer and are transported to the bottom electrode, resulting in a pronounced photoresponse. Fig. 2c presents the lateral current-voltage (I-V) results of the device measured in the dark, along with the diagram illustrating the measurement setup. Devices with varying concentrations of the P(VDF-TrFE) exhibit distinct linear behaviors in the ITO/MAPbI3:P(VDF-TrFE)/Si heterojunction, indicating ideal Ohmic contact for the electrodes. The longitudinal I-V results of the MAPbI3:P(VDF-TrFE)/Si heterojunction (typically doped with 0.2 wt %) under dark and illuminated conditions, along with a schematic diagram of the longitudinal photoresponse test, are shown in Fig. 2d and Fig. S1. The observed rectification in the dark confirms the typical p-n heterojunction behavior. To explore the effect of P(VDF-TrFE) doping concentration on the LPE response, devices with concentrations of 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1 wt % were fabricated and systematically characterized. The ITO/MAPbI3:P(VDF-TrFE)/Si heterojunctions were prepared using the optimized parameters thickness established in previous studies, ensuring optimal device performance while maintaining consistency in the understanding of thickness-dependent photoelectric processes in perovskite-based devices. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to examine the crystalline structure of the composite films at each doping level, with the results shown in Fig. S2a. The observed diffraction peaks correspond to the MAPbI3 phase, indicating that the inclusion of P(VDF-TrFE) has a negligible effect on the crystalline structure of the perovskite. Additionally, the increased diffraction intensity reflects an improvement in crystalline quality of the MAPbI3:P(VDF-TrFE) composite films. The absorption spectra for perovskite films with different doping concentrations are presented in Fig. S2b. Each film shows a distinct absorption peak around 780 nm, aligning with the MAPbI3 band gap of approximately 1.5 eV. Top-view scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the composite films at a doping concentration of 0.2 wt % are shown in Fig. S2c, while Fig. S2d illustrates the lateral resistances of the heterojunction at different P(VDF-TrFE) concentrations. The results indicate that the lateral resistance of the device increases with rising P(VDF-TrFE) concentration.

The PSDs were subsequently subjected to LPE response measurement, with their working principle depicted in Fig. 3a. The photos of the fabricated device and the schematic diagram of the LPV test experimental setup are shown in Fig. S3. Under non-uniform laser illumination on the heterojunction surface, the MAPbI3 layer or Si efficiently absorbs photons and generates electron-hole pairs. The strong built-in electric field at the heterojunction interface facilitates effective charge separation, with holes being rapidly transported into the ITO layer while electrons are effectively confined. This process creates a substantial carrier concentration gradient along the ITO layer between illuminated and non-illuminated regions, driving lateral diffusion of holes within the ITO layer. The diffusing holes are ultimately collected by the two top Ag electrodes with different efficiencies due to their distinct distances from the illumination spot. This asymmetric collection creates a measurable LPV signal. As demonstrated in Fig. 3b, the LPV exhibits a linear correlation with the illumination position as the light moves across the device surface. Moreover, in previous reports, due to the excellent conductivity and long carrier diffusion length of ITO, a larger working distance of 28 mm can be obtained while maintaining low LPV response loss [27]. Fig. S4 shows the LPV response curves of 78 measuring points with the change of illumination position. The results show excellent agreement with the LPV theoretical model, demonstrating good linearity and sensitivity, which highlights the potential of the ITO/MAPbI3:P(VDF-TrFE)/Si heterojunction for high-density spatial positioning applications. Fig. 3c and Fig. S5-S7 illustrate the variations in LPV and PS across different doping concentrations, with PS calculated as LPV divided by the corresponding laser position. The observed photoresponse trends at each doping concentration are consistent with the above findings, confirming that the device with a 0.2 wt % concentration shows an optimal LPE performance. In addition, by comparing the response times of ITO/MAPbI3:P(VDF-TrFE)/Si heterojunction PSDs with 0 wt % and 0.2 wt % concentration of P(VDF-TrFE), it is found that the introduction of the pyroelectric effect caused by P(VDF-TrFE) can significantly improve the response time of the device, as shown in Fig. S8. The generation of LPV primarily depends on longitudinal separation and lateral diffusion, with the concentration of P(VDF-TrFE) playing a crucial role in the diffusion process. As shown in Fig. S2d, P(VDF-TrFE) concentration significantly influences the lateral resistance characteristics of the PSD. According to the diffusion theory of separated carriers, the LPV can be well deduced, with the equation expressed as: LPV(x)=κ(NLeft- NRight), where κ represents a constant coefficient, NLeft and NRight are the hole densities collected by the left electrode and the right electrode, respectively. Theoretically, when the lateral resistance of the device is either too low or too high, the difference in carrier collection between the two electrodes remains minimal, leading to a suppressed LPV output. An optimal lateral resistance maximizes this carrier collection asymmetry, thereby enhancing the LPV response [28]. Consequently, a moderate increase in lateral resistance can effectively improve LPV. To thoroughly evaluate the photoelectric performance of the PSD, a series of measurements were conducted. Fig. 3d presents the LPV response curves across a power range of 0.01 to 10 mW under 780 nm laser irradiation. The responses exhibit consistent linearity throughout this power range, increasing from 2.06 to 83.08 mV as laser power rises, indicating strong tunability and stability of the PSD under illumination of varying optical intensities. The inset in Fig. 3d shows that PS significantly increases in the low-power range but approaches saturation at high power levels. This behavior is likely due to increased carrier generation at higher laser power, which raises the recombination probability and leads to saturation of the response [29,30]. To further examine the spectral response characteristics, the heterojunction PSD was tested with lasers of various wavelengths. Fig. 3e and Fig. S9, S10 display the broadband LPE response of the device across wavelengths from 405 to 1064 nm. The PS and nonlinearity values for each wavelength are summarized in Fig. 3f. The PS peaks at 305.26 mV/mm at 780 nm, with nonlinearity remaining below 8.5 % across the entire spectral range, indicating that the heterojunction PSD achieves excellent performance in wide-spectrum position detection.

As previously discussed, the fundamental mechanism of the PSD is the non-uniform distribution of carriers resulting from uneven illumination. P(VDF-TrFE) is known for its unique pyroelectric properties, generating a pyroelectric field through transient temperature changes [[31], [32], [33]]. This effect can enhance carrier separation and transmission efficiency, significantly tuning the photoresponse of the PSD. To further study the impact of the pyroelectric effect on the LPE response characteristics at different illumination positions, multi-period time-dependent LPV (LPV-t) curves were measured at various positions (−0.3, −0.24, −0.16, −0.08, 0, 0.08, 0.16, 0.24 and 0.3 mm) under 780 nm laser illumination at 10 mW. The results shown in Fig. 4a demonstrate a consistent four-stage LPV response across all positions, highlighting the significant pyroelectric property of the MAPbI3:P(VDF-TrFE) film [30,31,34]. Fig. 4b shows the stable LPV values (LPVphoto) during continuous laser illumination, aligning with the laser position-dependent LPV values at the corresponding positions. When the laser illuminates near the electrodes, LPVphoto reaches a maximum value of 71.33 and −74.46 mV, respectively. The slight differences in absolute values for the two positions may be attributed to non-ideal symmetrical positioning or electrode contacting. Concurrently, the LPVphoto+pyro also reaches the peak values of 81.84 and −86.67 mV. Notably, the transient LPV values resulting from the pyroelectric effect (LPVpyro) vary depending on the laser spot position. Further analysis reveals that the LPV enhancement ratio ((LPVphoto+pyro-LPVphoto)/LPVphoto) progressively decreases as the spot approaches the electrode. As depicted in Fig. 4c, the LPV enhancement ratios at different positions are calculated to be 14.70 %, 27.55 %, 55.81 %, 144.82 %, 162.43 %, 53.91 %, 28.58 %, and 16.41 %, respectively, indicating that the influence of the pyroelectric effect on the LPE diminishes as the spot approaches the electrode. This phenomenon illustrates the unique interplay between the pyroelectric effect and the LPE, which varies with the illumination position. Generally, there is a limited photoresponse for a given heterostructure. While the pyroelectric effect can enhance the LPE, this improvement also depends on the original LPE response. When the illumination is close to the electrodes, the LPE response is relatively strong, resulting in only a slight enhancement from the pyroelectric effect. The schematic diagrams in Fig. 4d-f indicate notable variations in how the pyroelectric effect regulates the LPE response at different illumination positions. When the heterojunction is irradiated with light, electron-hole pairs are excited, and the built-in electric field facilitates their separation. Holes migrate into the ITO layer, where they diffuse, creating a stable LPVphoto induced by the LPE. Simultaneously, the transient change in the internal temperature of the composite film generates a pyroelectric field (Epyro). When the light is turned on, this pyroelectric field aligns with the built-in electric field. When the light is turned off, it opposes it [35]. This interaction results in the formation of LPVpyro. Under specific wavelength and illumination conditions, the number of photogenerated electron-hole pairs remains constant. When the light irradiates near the electrodes, the LPE dominates, allowing the built-in electric field to effectively separate and collect a larger number of carriers. Consequently, LPVphoto reaches its maximum, while the LPVpyro decreases, as shown in Fig. 4d and 4f. In contrast, when the illumination spot is positioned farther from the electrodes, the LPVphoto diminishes primarily due to limited carrier collection in the LPE. At this point, the pyroelectric effect becomes more influential. The pyroelectric field enhances the separation and collection of charge carriers, thereby increasing the value of LPVpyro, as illustrated in Fig. 4e.

The analysis of LPV-t curves at different laser wavelengths enabled an investigation into the influence of the pyroelectric field on the transient LPE response across the visible to near-infrared spectrum. Throughout the experiment, the irradiation position was maintained at −0.08 mm to ensure consistent spot placement, thereby minimizing the positional impact on the results. The experimental setup is illustrated in Fig. 5a. A pulsed light was generated using a continuous-wave laser source and chopper, and the PSD was irradiated at a constant frequency and power. Fig. 5b shows the LPV-t curves of the PSD under pulsed light irradiation at 10 mW and 5 Hz across various wavelengths. Results reveal a consistent four-stage photovoltage response for all wavelengths, indicating that the pyroelectric effect modulates the LPE response over a broad spectral range. Fig. 5c-e demonstrate the dependence of LPV, PS, and enhancement ratio on laser wavelength. Under irradiation at 405, 532, 671, 780, and 1064 nm, the LPVphoto values were recorded as 13.36, 14.59, 18.94, 22.55 and 12.18 mV, respectively. With the introduction of the pyroelectric effect, LPVphoto+pyro values increased to 20.00, 22.33, 35.38, 55.04 and 27.09 mV. The PS factors were calculated to be 167.01, 182.35, 236.78, 281.86, and 152.27 mV/mm for PSphoto, and 249.98, 279.13, 442.30, 688.10, and 338.67 mV/mm for PSphoto+pyro, corresponding to enhancements of 49.69 %, 53.08 %, 86.80 %, 144.82 % and 122.41 %, respectively. Especially at the wavelength of 780 nm, both the LPE and its enhancement from the pyroelectric effect show optimal responses. This pronounced enhancement is primarily attributed to increased carrier generation efficiency at a wavelength close to the MAPbI3 bandgap energy, facilitating more efficient utilization of photogenerated carriers [16]. Moreover, as the concentration of carriers rises, the modulation effect of the pyroelectric field on these carriers becomes more significant, substantially boosting the LPE response at 780 nm [36]. This is because a higher carrier concentration results in more active carrier migration and interaction, thereby amplifying the modulation effect of the pyroelectric field. From the above discussion, it is suggested that incorporating the pyroelectric field effectively modulates the PSD’s LPE response across the entire wavelength range, thereby improving detection sensitivity and enhancing the device’s ability to differentiate optical signals at distinct wavelengths. By analyzing the position-sensitive output characteristics of the ITO/MAPbI3:P(VDF-TrFE)/Si heterojunction PSD across various wavelengths, this study establishes a foundational approach for position and wavelength-resolved photoelectric demodulation. Positional markers on the PSD surface are shown in Fig. 5f, with four typical positions labeled as P1, P2, P3, and P4, respectively. Upon laser irradiation at these designated positions, distinct LPV values were generated, corresponding to the respective positional signals. Fig. 5g shows that the output signals matched the binary codes “00”, “01”, “10” and “11” for positions P1, P2, P3, and P4, respectively, thus establishing an effective correlation between position signals and digital information. For instance, when the laser sequentially irradiates P2, P1, P3, and P1, the PSD output “01001000”, which was demodulated into the letter “H” using the ASCII code. This position-encoded approach enables real-time data interpretation, transforming photoelectric signals into readable digital information by controlling the illumination position. Under 780 nm illumination, the LPVphoto values at the four specified positions were 71.33, 41.39, 45.04, and 74.46 mV, respectively. The corresponding values of LPVphoto+pyro were 81.84, 64.43, 69.33, and 86.68 mV. Furthermore, Fig. S11a-d illustrate the enhancement of the LPV values due to the pyroelectric effect under laser illumination at different wavelengths. In the range of 405–1064 nm, the pyroelectric effect significantly increases the LPV values at each position, confirming the modulation effect of the pyroelectric field across different wavelengths and irradiation positions. A series of signals were simulated, as shown in Fig. 5h, Fig. S11e, and Fig. S12-S13: signal 1 (01001000), signal 2 (01000010), and signal 3 (01010101), which were demodulated to “HBU” (representing abbreviation of Hebei University). Specifically, the total values of LPVphoto and LPVphoto+pyro at 780 nm were 623.53 and 852.61 mV, while the total values of LPVphoto at 405, 532, 671, and 1064 nm were 403.75, 441.41, 544.22, and 370.66 mV. The corresponding total values of LPVphoto+pyro were 435.36, 483.20, 639.84, and 494.64 mV. To further quantify the PSD’s wavelength discrimination capability, wavelength discrimination is defined as the absolute value of the LPV change per unit wavelength change. Based on the LPVphoto values, the wavelength discrimination was calculated as follows: 0.30 (532–405 nm), 0.74 (671–532 nm), 0.73 (780–671 nm), and 0.89 mV/nm (1064–780 nm). For LPVphoto+pyro, the wavelength discrimination values were 0.38 (532–405 nm), 1.13 (671–532 nm), 1.95 (780–671 nm), and 1.26 mV/nm (1064–780 nm). These results further indicate that modulation of the pyroelectric field significantly enhances wavelength resolution, thereby improving the wavelength discrimination ability during the signal demodulation process.

Finally, the potential application of the heterojunction PSD in multi-dimensional optical information identification is discussed by analyzing LPV values and their enhancement ratios across various wavelengths, positions, and intensities of the light. Fig. 6a shows that four distinct irradiation positions were selected at X1 = −0.08 mm, X2 = −0.16 mm, X3 = −0.24 mm, and X4 = −0.30 mm, respectively. The LPE response to laser irradiation across wavelengths from 405 to 1064 nm and intensities between 0.01 and 10 mW was systematically examined, with results shown in Fig. S14-S18. The results, displayed in Fig. 6b-e, reveal a significant increase in LPVphoto and LPVphoto+pyro values at all positions as incident light power increases, reaching their maximum values as the spot moves closer to the electrodes. For instance, under 10 mW illumination at 780 nm, LPVphoto and LPVphoto+pyro are 22.55 and 55.04 mV at position X1, 41.39 and 64.43 mV at X2, 57.87 and 73.78 mV at X3, and near the electrode, reaching 71.33 and 81.84 mV at X4. The LPV enhancement ratios, attributed to the pyroelectric effect, also show an upward trend with higher incident power, with notable ratios at positions further from the electrodes. As presented in Fig. 6f-i, under 780 nm and 10 mW irradiation, enhancement ratios for positions X1 to X4 are 144.82%, 55.81%, 27.55%, and 14.70%, respectively. Under 405 nm laser illumination, the enhancement ratios are 49.69%, 13.63%, 5.68%, and 3.90%, respectively. Similarly, at 532 nm, they are 53.08%, 16.98%, 7.65%, and 4.59%; at 671 nm, 86.80%, 27.55%, 10.60%, and 7.32%; and at 1064 nm, 122.41%, 47.46%, 23.83%, and 15.53%. Additionally, other device positions (marked as X5 = 0.08 mm, X6 = 0.16 mm, X7 = 0.24 mm, and X8 = 0.30 mm), shown in Fig. S19, display a similar trend to those discussed above. According to experimental observations, LPVphoto, LPVphoto+pyro, and enhancement ratios are highly sensitive not only to the wavelength and intensity of incident light but also exhibit significant variations at different irradiation positions. This confirms that the heterojunction devices possess the ability to simultaneously detect light intensity, wavelength, and position, enabling multi-dimensional optical detection. Furthermore, based on the rich experimental dataset, we established a multi-parameter response database. To evaluate the comprehensive detection performance, 20% of the data were randomly selected for testing. As shown in Fig. S20, the evaluation results demonstrate that the detection accuracy of the PSD across the three dimensions (wavelength, position, and intensity) reaches 100%, highlighting the excellent multi-dimensional optical detection capability of the device. This special characteristic enables the ITO/MAPbI3:P(VDF-TrFE)/Si heterojunction PSD to function as an effective multi-functional optical demodulator, with applications extending to high-performance optical sensing and precision light signal monitoring systems. Conclusion

Drawing inspiration from the integrated structure and complex information-processing capabilities of the human visual system, this research developed a multi-functional PSD that operates across the visible to near-infrared spectrum, based on the ITO/MAPbI3:P(VDF-TrFE)/Si heterojunction. By incorporating a pyroelectric field and coupling it with the built-in electric field, the PSD achieves a sensitivity enhancement of 144.13%, reaching 688.10 mV/mm. Furthermore, the pyroelectric effect significantly enhanced the wavelength resolution. A logical operation function was established based on the LPV values of different illumination positions and light wavelengths. Utilizing the ASCII coding table, the designed digital information can be decoded, facilitating position and wavelength-resolved photoelectric demodulation. Finally, a detailed analysis of LPV values and enhancement ratios further revealed correlations among light wavelength, position, and intensity, enabling the decoding and processing of multi-dimensional optical information. This study presents an effective strategy for modulating PSD performance and establishes a pathway for future applications of optoelectronic devices in complex information acquisition and processing.
