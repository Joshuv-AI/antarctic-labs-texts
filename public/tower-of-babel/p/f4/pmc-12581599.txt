Clinical value of quantitative PCR in diagnosis of suspected mycobacterial pulmonary infections
Authors: Jiali Chen, Chunyu Chen, Yongping Xie, Guanghui Xu, Meng Lin, Xu Gong, Xin Zhang, Xiaohua Chen, Jianyong Chen, Liang Zhang, Gang He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDue to the difficulty in clinically distinguishing pulmonary infections caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) and Non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM), the study utilizes quantitative PCR (qPCR) technology to simultaneously detect MTB, Mycobacterium abscessus complex (MABC), Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), and Mycobacterium kansasii(M. kansasii),to explore its clinical value in diagnosing of patients suspected mycobacterial pulmonary infections.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on a cohort of 102 patients suspected of mycobacterial pulmonary infections. Samples of sputum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid were extracted for testing with acid-fast staining (AFS), qPCR, and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS). Assess the diagnostic performance of AFS, qPCR, and mNGS for four types of mycobacteria based on comprehensive pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) CRS composite criteria, non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) diagnostic and treatment guidelines, as well as clinical observations.ResultsThe sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and AUC of qPCR for mycobacteria (MTB, MABC, MAC) were 90.00% (76.33–97.20), 100.00% (94.22–100.00), 100.00% (99.90–100.00), 93.93% (85.95–97.51), and 0.950 (0.888–0.983), respectively. For mNGS, the corresponding estimates were 87.50% (73.19–95.81), 96.77% (88.82–99.60), 94.59%(81.66–98.56),92.30% (84.07–96.46), and 0.921(0.851–0.965), respectively. The research showed that the sensitivity and specificity of qPCR and mNGS method for detecting mycobacteria are higher than AFS; and there was no statistical difference in the diagnostic performance for mycobacteria between qPCR and mNGS, but qPCR was superior to mNGS in specific values.ConclusionCompared with AFS, qPCR has higher sensitivity and specificity but statistical significance needs to be assessed with larger sample sizes for mycobacteria identification. In this regard, qPCR and mNGS demonstrate exhibit similar performance. However, qPCR is less expensive and more convenient for pathogen detection, which make it a promising lower-cost alternative diagnostic method for patients suspected of mycobacterial pulmonary infections in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Non-mycobacteria tuberculosis, Quantitative PCR, Diagnosis performance

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is a chronic infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) and represents a significant global health challenge, characterized by high morbidity and mortality [1]. Primarily affecting the lungs, MTB leads to pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB). Concurrently, the prevalence of Non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) infections is on the rise. To date, researchers have identified 14 subspecies and over 190 species of NTM. NTM infections can affect various body parts, notably Mycobacterium perfringens in the skin and Mycobacterium scrofululaceum in lymph nodes. However, the primary pulmonary pathogens among NTM are Mycobacterium abscessus complex (MABC), Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), and Mycobacterium kansasii (M.kansasii). These three species account for over 80% of non-tuberculous mycobacterial pulmonary disease (NTM-PD) cases [2–4]. PTB and NTM-PD share similar clinical and radiological features, often leading to misdiagnosis of NTM-PD as PTB, but their treatment plans are vastly different. Most NTM-PD patients are resistant to first-line anti-tuberculosis drugs, which delays their diagnosis and treatment and seriously affects their prognosis [5]. Therefore, early and accurate diagnosis of PTB and NTM-PD is clinically critical. Currently, NTM and MTB diagnoses primarily rely on bacterial smears and cultures. The bacterial smears have low sensitivity and cannot differentiate between MTB and NTM. The cultures method requires extensive laboratory equipment, a lengthy detection cycle, and complex procedures [6, 7]. Consequently, there is a pressing need for early, accurate, and rapid diagnostic technologies for MTB and NTM. Recent advancements in molecular biology, particularly quantitative PCR (qPCR) and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), have enhanced the diagnosis of PTB and NTM-PD. These techniques provide essential tools for accurate diagnosis.

mNGS is a powerful technique for sequencing DNA and RNA from clinical samples, enabling detailed analysis of microbial communities’ abundance and diversity at the genetic level. This method offers the advantages of speed and precision, capable of identifying a wide array of microorganisms, including viruses, fungi, and bacteria [8–10].However, it is important to note that mNGS testing can be expensive, and its effectiveness in identifying mycobacteria is not yet fully established. In contrast, qPCR excels in detecting a specific, limited range of pathogens.Previous research indicates that qPCR is highly sensitive and specific for detecting MTB and NTM, providing rapid results [11–15]. Nonetheless, most studies using qPCR have either focused solely on identifying MTB and NTM without distinguishing specific NTM species [11, 12], or they have used qPCR to identify MTB and over 10 NTM species in clinical and cultured isolates [13, 14]. Given the prevalence of MTB, MABC, MAC, and M.kansasii in pulmonary infections, our study employed Taqman multiplex qPCR technology to simultaneously detect for MTB, MABC, MAC, and M.kansasiiin respiratory tract samples from patients of suspected mycobacterialpulmonary infections. We then compared the results with acid-fast staining (AFS) and mNGS. Our findings aim to offer efficient, accurate, and cost-effective detection methods for patients clinically suspected of mycobacterialpulmonary infections.

Methods

Research subjects

This study involved a retrospective analysis of 301 patients suspected of mycobacterialpulmonary infections at Jiangmen Central Hospital and Jiangmen Tuberculosis Prevention and Control Institute, spanning from July 8, 2020, to May 18, 2023. The inclusion criteria for these patients were: (1) Age ≥ 18 years old. (2) Presence of systemic symptoms such as fever and exhaustion, along with respiratory symptoms including cough, expectoration of phlegm, and hemoptysis.(3) Chest imaging findings indicative of mycobacterialpulmonary infections, such as fibrous cavitation, multifocal bronchiectasis, infiltrative consolidation, the tree-in-bud sign, or multiple small nodular lesions. The exclusion criteria were: (1) Absence of AFS, qPCR, and mNGS testing.(2) Patients lost to follow-up. (3) Incomplete clinical data. Electronic medical records provided demographic and clinical data, including gender, age, clinical symptoms, imaging features, and results from AFS, qPCR, mNGS, and other relevant tests. All participants were followed up for a minimum duration of three months.

Diagnostic criteria

(1) Diagnosis of PTB: a composite reference standard (CRS) we used as the diagnosis standard. (1) Definite cases: MTB cultivates positive or molecular biology MTB positive twice. (2) Clinical diagnosis cases: The imaging features and clinical manifestations of patients consistent with pulmonary tuberculosis, combined with consideration of tuberculosis (immunological, biochemical, or histopathological, and response to anti-MTB treatment), or detection of a single positive molecular biology MTB, and follow-up for at least 3 months, anti-MTB treatment is effective. (2) Diagnosis of NTM-PD: The diagnosis of NTM-PD follows the established guidelines for NTM diagnosis and therapy [2]. It requires ruling out other pulmonary diseases and involves identifying characteristic chest CT or X-ray findings, such as cavity shadows or multiple nodular lesions, along with respiratory and systemic symptoms, and multiple bronchiectasis. The diagnosis is confirmed if it meets one of the following criteria in the absence of specimen contamination: (1) Two separate sputum samples testing positive for NTM culture, identified as the same pathogen; and/or confirmation of the same pathogen by NTM molecular biological detection. (2) Detection of NTM in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) or bronchial lavage fluid by molecular biological testing, and/or one positive NTM culture. (3) Lung tissue samples obtained via bronchoscopy or other methods showing typical pathological changes indicative of NTM infection (such as AFS or granulomatous inflammation), with concurrent NTM detection by molecular biological methods and/or NTM culture. (4) Examination of lung tissue by bronchoscopy or other techniques revealing pathological alterations characteristic of NTM infection, with at least one or more sputum samples, BALF, or bronchial lavage fluid testing positive for NTM by molecular biological detection or culture. Clinical follow-up outcomes are integrated with these criteria to establish the final diagnosis. (3) Non-mycobacterial infection: This diagnosis is made when mNGSor other traditional detection methods identify pathogenic microorganisms (such as bacteria, fungi, viruses, etc.), and pulmonary infection shows improvement after 14 days of antibiotic treatment. Alternatively, the diagnosis is considered when no pathogen is identified, but symptoms improve following hospital treatment and there is no recurrence of symptoms for at least three months during follow-up.

Ethics statement

The Ethics Committees of Jiangmen Central Hospital and Jiangmen Tuberculosis Prevention and Control Institute (protocol code: No.2023132) approved this retrospective study.

Sample collection and sample size estimation

For all eligible patients, a minimum of 3 mL of sputum should be collected, or alternatively, 10 mL of BALF can be retrieved using electronic bronchoscopy. The remaining sputum and BALFspecimens were stored at-80℃for use in AFS, qPCR, and mNGS. In this study, to investigate the comparability of analytical efficiency between two diagnostic methods for suspected pulmonary mycobacterial infections, we performed sample size calculations for non-inferiority testing of qPCR versus mNGS using PASS 15 software (Power Analysis and Sample Size Software, version 15). Based on published literature validating mNGS performance, we established a reference accuracy of 95% for mNGS detection [16, 17]. A one-sided proportional non-inferiority test was configured with a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a target statistical power of 90%. The non-inferiority margin was set at 10% absolute difference in accuracy metrics, defining the threshold for clinical equivalence between methodologies. Through these statistical parameters, the software calculation determined a required sample size of 108 clinical specimens to adequately power the comparative analysis.

Acid-fast staining

Medical professionals at the Jiangmen Central Hospital laboratory and Jiangmen Tuberculosis Prevention and Control Institute utilize the Ziehl-Neelsen staining procedure to process specimens. Respiratory specimens are uniformly spread on a glass slide to form an oval-shaped film, then air-dried and placed on a staining rack.Following thermal fixation, Ziehl-Neelsen staining is applied. The assessment of acid-fast bacilli is then conducted by drying the stained glass slides and examining them under a microscope [18].

DNA extraction and quantitative PCR

Respiratory specimens were processed using a magnetic bead-based nucleic acid extraction kit for total nucleic acid extraction kit (Guangdong Bright-Innovation BioMed Co., Ltd., Shunde, China). The identification of three NTM species and MTB was conducted using a commercial test kit from the same company. This kit has been used for detecting skeletal tuberculosis from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissues [19], while it also targets M.kansasii in this assay.The test included detecting the IS6110 gene specific to TB, the HSP65 gene for the MABC, encompassing Mycobacteriumabscessus (M. abscessus), Mycobacteriummassiliense(M. massiliense), and Mycobacteriumbolletii(M. bolletii), the ITS gene for the MAC, including Mycobacterium intracellulare (M. intracellulare) and Mycobacterium avium (M. avium), and the RHO gene for M.kansasii. Fluorescence detection in three channels was performed for each reaction tube. One set of assays targeted MTB and M. kansasii, along with the human reference gene Actb, while another focused on MABC, MAC, and the Actb gene. Samples with a cycle threshold (Ct) value above 38 were deemed negative. Samples with a Ct value above 38 were deemed negative. In cases where qPCR results were negative but AFSresults were positive, duplicate qPCR testing was undertaken to rule out false negatives due to experimental errors.

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing

After respiratory sample collection, the specimens were centrifuged, and the 600 µL processed sample was mixed with glass beads. Subsequently, BioPrep-24 (Beijing TianGen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was utilized for lysis. DNA extraction, including the removal of host DNA, was performed using the magnetic bead method (Guangzhou Meiji Biotechnology Co.,Ltd., Guangzhou, China). The metagenomic library construction employed the NextEra XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Library quality was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA). High-throughput sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NextSeq 550 DX platform. Post-sequencing, short and low-quality reads (less than 35 bp) were removed, and the remaining reads were aligned against the human reference genome sequence. Human-related reads were excluded using Bowtie2 (BWA) to ensure high-quality sequencing data. Non-human sequences were then mapped to a classification reference database from the NCBI (http://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/). This database comprises 4152 viral species’ whole genome sequences, 3446 bacterial genomes or scaffolds, 206 fungi associated with human diseases, and 140 parasites linked to human illnesses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29.0. The t-test was employed for comparing continuous variables between two groups, while the chi-square test was used for categorical variables between two groups. Sensitivity, specificity, negative predictive value (NPV), positive predictive value (PPV), and the 95% confidence intervals for AFS, qPCR, and mNGS were determined using MedCalc version 20.0. Additionally, the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Result

301 patients who may havemycobacterialpulmonary infections were included in this study. After excluding 197 patients who didn’t do mNGS or qPCR, one who was lost to follow-up, and one who didn’t have enough clinical data, only 102 patients who might have mycobacterial pulmonary infections were left, as shown in Fig. 1. 40 cases of infection with mycobacteria, including 21 cases of PTB and 18 cases ofNTM-PD. Additionally, there was one case of co-infection with both MTB and NTM. Among the cases, 18 cases were definite PTB and 3 cases were clinical diagnosis with PTB; 9 cases were MAC, and 9 cases were MABC, with no cases of M. kansasii. Furthermore, 62 cases of non-tuberculous mycobacterial infection were identified, of which 61 included infectious disorders and one involved a non-infectious condition.

Discussion

PTB and NTM-PD are the most prevalent forms of mycobacterium infection, capable of causing severe lung damage in some individuals. Therefore, early detection of these infections is critical for patient prognosis and treatment. Presently, conventional laboratory diagnostic methods for PTB and NTM-PD exhibit certain limitations and deficiencies [6, 7]. This study employed mNGS and qPCRbased on nucleic acid amplification to detect MTB and NTM. The diagnostic efficacy of these methods surpassed that of AFS. This study detected MTB, MABC, and MAC were identified, with no additional mycobacterium species detected. This finding is consistent with previous research, which indicates that MTB, MABC, MAC, and M.kansasii are the most common types of mycobacterialpulmonary infections [2–4]. Therefore, employing qPCR to evaluate mycobacterial presence in patients with mycobacterialpulmonary infections is justified. Compared to AFS, qPCR demonstrated superior performance in detecting MTB, MABC, and MAC. the sensitivity and specificity of the qPCR method for detecting MTB were 90.47% and 97.50%, respectively. For MABC and MAC, the sensitivity and specificity were 88.88% and 97.59%, respectively, aligning with previous research findings. Shen et al. [11] using qPCR for TB and NTM diagnosis, reported sensitivities of 83.0% for TB and 82.0% for NTM, with specificities of 79.9% and 99.6%, respectively. Kalaiarasan E et al. [13] employed qPCR for detecting NTM in clinical isolates, achieving a sensitivity of 88.9% and specificity of 100%. Previous studies have utilized qPCR to identify over ten different mycobacterium species, including MAC, MTB, and MABC. Kalaiarasan et al. [13] focused on three DNA sequences (IS6110, ITS, and 16 S rRNA) to detect over ten NTM species. Kim et al. [14] employed a step-by-step qPCR approach for identifying MTB and more than ten NTM types. However, the operation process is complex and time-consuming, and it detects only cultured and clinical isolates, which cannot achieve early diagnosis and delay patient treatment. In our research, we directly detected MTB and the three most prevalent pulmonary NTM pathogens using respiratory specimens and monitored the sampling process with the human internal reference gene Actb. Two separate reactions were conducted: one for MTB and M.kansasii, and the other for MABC and MAC. Our study offered a more straightforward operation and faster detection compared to other research. Unfortunately, due to the absence of patients with pulmonary infections caused by M. kansasii, the study could only evaluate the diagnostic performance for MTB, MABC, and MAC.

By comparing the diagnostic performance of qPCR in AFS-positive and AFS-negative, it can be seen that the sensitivity of qPCR in AFS-positive patients is higher than in AFS-negative patients. It is worth noting that although the sensitivity of qPCR in AFS-negative patients is lower than in AFS-positive patients, qPCR significantly increases the sensitivity of detecting mycobacteria in AFS-negative patients. AFS-negative mycobacterial pulmonary disease is more challenging to diagnose than AFS-positive, often leading to possible misdiagnosis, inadequate treatment, and irreversible lung damage, seriously affecting patient prognosis. Therefore, qPCR has crucial diagnostic value for AFS-negative patients.

In this study, the sensitivity and specificity of the mNGS method for detecting MTB were 85.71% and 96.25%, respectively. For MABC and MAC, the sensitivity and specificity were 94.44% and 93.97%, respectively. In line with other studies [20, 21], mNGS has proven more effective in detecting MTB. However, the majority of published diagnostic research on NTM using mNGS comprises case reports, with fewer prospective or retrospective clinical studies. Zhang et al. [22] applied mNGS to BALF of patients suspected of NTM-PD, achieving a sensitivity of 90.2% and specificity of 100%, outperforming both AFS and culture methods. Similarly, Wei et al. [23]reported a sensitivity of 81.4% and specificity of 97.8% in diagnosing NTM-PD using mNGS, indicating superior performance over culture methods.

Based on the sample size estimation, 88 patients with mycobacterial infections were needed.Due to the limited number of patients who underwent both mNGS and qPCR, only 102 cases of probable mycobacterial infections were collected. As a result, only 40 positive cases of MTB and NTM were included in the current investigation, which is lessthan the expected number of cases according to the sample size formula.In previous investigations, the sample size for MTB and NTM detection usingmNGSwasrelatively modest. For example, Wang et al. enrolled 27 patients for mNGS testing and identified 27 cases of positive NTM, withsix different NTM species [24]. In a separate study, 55 of 118 suspected NTM patients were shown to be NTM-positive using mNGS [22]. Shi et al. recently conducted a simultaneous analysis of 110 BALF samples using Xpert and mNGS.Xpert and mNGS found 22 and 23 cases of MTB, respectively [20]. Our sample size and positive detection rate for mycobacterial infections aresimilar tothose of this investigation. Instead, we employed a simple qPCR assay to detect MTB and three forms of NTM, and then compared the qPCR method to the mNGS test.Despite the small sample size in the present study, our findings may provide useful information for comparing qPCR and mNGSin identifying mycobacterial infections.

In this study, no significant differences were observed in the sensitivity and specificity of mNGSand qPCR in detecting MTB, MABC, and MAC. This finding contrasts with previous studies by Liu et al. [25] and Wang et al. [26] which indicated superior performance of mNGS over conventional PCR in TB diagnosis. A contributing factor to this discrepancy could be the use of conventional PCR in those studies, known to be less sensitive than qPCR. In this investigation, mNGS incorrectly identified two cases of non-mycobacterial infections as MAC and MTB.An analysis of the mNGS data revealed three reads for MAC and one read for MTB. The low number of reads for both types of mycobacteria suggests the possibility of contamination during the experiment, leading to unintentional positive results.

This study did not compare the frequently employed clinical methods for detecting MTB and NTM. Previous research has shown that qPCR offers similar diagnostic accuracy for MTB as conventional clinical methods like culture and GeneXpert MTB/RIF (Xpert) [27]. However, Xpert lacks the capability to detect NTM. Common clinical approaches for identifying NTM typically include conventional PCR and culture. Research indicates that qPCR surpasses culture in NTM diagnosis, offering comparable or enhanced diagnostic effectiveness. Nevertheless, NTM culture requires strict laboratory conditions due to its prolonged incubation period and specific culture requirements, such as varying temperature and pH for different bacterial species [28]. Although conventional PCR can detect NTM species, its detection efficacy is not as high as qPCR [29].

In addition to MTB and NTM, other pathogenic microorganisms like bacteria, fungi, and viruses were detected using mNGS in this study.Lung infections caused by those non-mycobacterial pathogens such asAspergillus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa often present non-specific clinical symptoms and radiological signs similar to mycobacterial pulmonary infections [30]. mNGS has identified pathogenic bacteria including Candida albicans and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, which are opportunistic pathogens that can coexist harmlessly in the human body but may become pathogenic when the immune system is compromised. Whereas, organisms such as Enterococcus faecium, typically considered non-pathogenic, are rarely associated with pulmonary infections. While mNGS can detect a broad spectrum of pathogens, it cannot distinguish between background microbiota, commensal microbes, and pathogenic bacteria. Consequently, clinical judgment based on a patient’s presentation remains essential. However, the high cost of mNGS increases the financial burden on patients. Therefore, mNGS testing is particularly beneficial for individuals with multi-pathogen infections, severe pulmonary conditions, or atypical symptoms [31, 32]. For patients with mild symptoms or probable typical tuberculosis, mNGS is not recommended. qPCR is a less costly alternative and is effective in detecting MTB, MAC, and MABC. Additionally, the turn-around time for qPCR is less than four hours, compared to 36 and 24 h for mNGS and AFS, respectively, making qPCR aeffective and powerful method.The diagnostic performance of qPCR compared with mNGS for PTB and NTM-PD has not been reported in any study. In this study, the two assays were compared, which can provide a reference for patients with suspected mycobacterialpulmonary infections.

The World Health Organization’s target product profile (WHO TTP) for TB diagnosis includes diagnostic speed, accuracy, cost-effectiveness, etc., and this study’s qPCR meets these criteria.Firstly, the qPCR technology exhibits high sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy and enables rapid pathogen detection within 4 h, allowing for the prompt identification of positive patients, which facilitates disease monitoring and management. Secondly, qPCR technology is cost-effective and can be utilized in resource-limited medical settings, with easy operation and the capacity for automated PCR amplification. Real-time monitoring of the amplification curve through a fluorescent signal enables the determination of Ct values, and the obtained test results are reliable and unaffected by external factors. Lastly, qPCR technology can detect MTB and common NTM, making it suitable for screening in high TB-burden areas and the differential diagnosis of MTB and NTM.

This study is subject to several limitations. First, future prospective experimental studies with larger sample sizes are necessary to more accurately evaluate the diagnostic capabilities of mNGS and qPCR for MTB, MABC, MAC, and M.kansasii, given the relatively small sample size of the current research. Second, the limitation of qPCR in this study to only detect MTB, MABC, MAC, and M. kansasiiprecludes an assessment of its efficacy in diagnosing other NTM. Lastly, only a small proportionofthe patients in this study underwent cultivation. China’s hospitals are divided into specialized tuberculosis hospitals and general hospitals.In many general hospitals, there is insufficient capacity to perform mycobacterial culture. However, a considerable majority patientswith suspected mycobacterial infections made their first visit in general hospitals, highlighting the importance of a simple qPCR assay for detecting MTB and NTM. In addition, although the study incorporated treatment outcomes and relevant clinical test data, the possibility of misdiagnosis and missed diagnoses cannot be entirely ruled out.

Conclusion

In summary, our work shows that qPCR has excellent diagnostic performance for NTM-PD (MABC and MAC) and PTB. Compared with AFS, qPCR had the advantages of higher sensitivity and specificity for mycobacteria identification, however, a larger sample size is still needed to assess whether it is statistically significant. Although qPCR and mNGS exhibited similar sensitivity and specificity, qPCR is less expensive and more convenient for pathogen detection, which make it a promising lower-cost alternative diagnostic method for patients suspected of mycobacterial pulmonary infections in resource-constrained and underserved areas.
