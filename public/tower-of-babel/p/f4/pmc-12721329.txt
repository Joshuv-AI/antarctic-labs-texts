A 5′-UTR cis-acting RNA element targeted by RNase III is essential for DNA simple sequence repeat-dependent phase variation in Haemophilus influenzae
Authors: Beatriz Rapún-Araiz, Begoña Euba, Joshua C Mell, Alejandro Toledo-Arana, Junkal Garmendia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSimple sequence repeats (SSRs) are insertion-deletion mutational hotspots causing phase variation in bacterial genomes. When located in intergenic regions, SSR variation impacts transcription and may change post-transcriptional regulatory element targeting. Here, we show that transcription, 5′-UTR structure, and messenger RNA (mRNA) processing are key to bacterial SSR phase variation. Expression of the Haemophilus influenzae HMW1A adhesin is inversely proportional to the (5′-ATCTTTC)n SSR length upstream of the hmw1A gene. This repeat region is transcribed as part of the hmw 5′-UTR, which acts as a cis-acting regulatory element via a long hairpin structure involving 10 repeats on its left arm and a G-rich region on its right arm. The double strand-specific endoribonuclease RNase III processes hmw1 mRNA at this 5′-UTR hairpin increasing HMW1A expression. Deep-sequencing-based SSR quantification in bacterial populations showed that RNase III is also required for changes in SSR count. Moreover, mutations that disrupt the 5′-UTR hairpin in the repeat sequence or in the G-rich region impair HMW1A expression and repeat length changes. This supports hmw 5′-UTR and RNase III contribution to the mechanism of SSR number expansion and contraction. Overall, we present novel regulatory roles for the hmw 5′-UTR in bacterial adaptation, where its transcription, folding, and RNase III processing are essential to HMW1A expression and phase-variable changes.

Introduction

Many human-adapted pathogens take up long-term residence in or on the body as part of the normal healthy microbiome, but they also cause acute and chronic infections depending on host health and immune status. Understanding the mechanisms responsible for genetic variation, regulation of gene expression, and phenotypic variation of bacteria that arise within individual human hosts is key to shedding light on how bacteria adapt during long-term chronic infections, and results may point to novel approaches to treating or preventing them [1–5]. Phase variation is the ability of many bacteria to rapidly switch between phenotypic states by switching on or off specific genes called “contingency loci” due to rapid and reversible genetic changes [6]. The mechanisms of phase variation differ among bacteria but often involve changes in the number of simple sequence repeats (SSRs) within the coding sequences or promoters of contingency loci, a strategy that relies on high-frequency on–off switching of gene activity to generate phenotypic variation within bacterial populations [7–10]. Slippage of the DNA strands during DNA replication within such SSR tracts has been proposed to drive variation in repeat copy number in populations thus resulting in the phase-variable modulation of contingency gene expression. In turn, count changes in intergenic SSRs can influence transcription by changing the spacing of promoter elements or transcription factor binding sites [11–14]. Also, length variation of intergenic homopolymeric repeats in 5′-UTRs can determine small RNA (sRNA)-mediated post-transcriptional regulation [15, 16]. The mechanisms and roles of SSRs on gene regulation at the RNA level are poorly understood.

Nontypeable Haemophilus influenzae (NTHi) is normally an asymptomatic colonist of the human upper airways, but also a leading cause of chronic infections in the lower airways of patients suffering chronic lung diseases, especially chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [17–19]. One major driver of how NTHi strains adapt to the lung environment is through phase-variable changes at many contingency loci containing SSRs whose copy number dictates expression level [20]. Many SSR tracts are found within protein-coding genes whose products modulate cell surface structures, and where copy number changes in the SSR switch the gene between functional in-frame and non-functional frameshifted versions. Several other SSR tracts are located upstream of genes, within promoter elements where they can act as “rheostats” controlling gene expression levels. One major complication in understanding the genetic and regulatory processes that dictate NTHi’s fitness during long-term lung infection is that phase variation at many loci contributes to exceptionally high potential for phenotypic heterogeneity within even apparently fully clonal populations [20].

NTHi bacterial cells invade a variety of human cell types, a trait thought to contribute to persistent lung infections [21–27]. The high molecular weight (HMW) adhesins of NTHi mediate adherence to respiratory epithelial cells and—depending on the allelic variant—confer epithelial hyper-invasiveness, as found in an earlier study using natural transformation to map NTHi genes involved in intracellular invasion [3, 27–29]. Genes encoding the HMW adhesins and their accessory proteins are present in about half of NTHi strains, nearly always found as two paralog loci at different chromosomal positions, hmw1ABC and hmw2ABC [3, 4, 30–39]. The hmw1A/2A genes encode the adhesin proteins HMW1A/2A; hmw1B/2B genes encode the pore-forming translocator proteins HMW1B/2B; and hmw1C/2C genes encode the glycosyltransferases HMW1C/2C that glycosylate the HMW1A/2A adhesins [40–44]. The hmw1A/2A adhesin genes themselves display extensive allelic diversity within and among strains, which affects how they mediate interactions with different human cell types. Furthermore, the hmw operons are under phase variable control due to heptameric SSR tracts (5′-ATCTTTC)n located upstream of the hmw1A/2A coding sequences [3, 20, 33, 36, 38, 45–47]. The SSR count can also change due to a ∼400-kb inversion polymorphism between the hmw1 and hmw2 upstream regulatory regions that can arise during long-term persistent infection [48]. As we have previously shown, as the SSR number increases, hmw1A transcript and HMW1A protein levels are attenuated, which leads to a phenotypic switch toward decreased bacterial intracellular invasion and increased biofilm formation [3].

An earlier study using fusion reporter plasmids where we coupled variants of the of the hmw1 promoter-regulatory region to a fluorescent reporter suggested the presence of post-transcriptional cis-acting regulatory elements in the 5′-UTR of the hmw1 mRNA [3]. Here, we dissected this putative regulation and discovered not only that hmw1 mRNA processing at the 5′-UTR by the double strand-specific RNA endonuclease RNase III is key to protein production, but also that it is required for phase variable changes in the length (SSR number) of the heptameric repeat. This provides a novel mechanistic model where regulatory features in the hmw 5′-UTR contribute to genetic phase variability and post-transcriptional gene regulation of this major bacterial adhesin.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

Strains used in this study are listed in Table 1. Haemophilus influenzae strains were grown at 37°C, 5% CO2 on PolyVitex agar (PVX, bioMérieux, 43101), or on Haemophilus Test Medium agar (HTM, Oxoid, CM0898) supplemented with 10 μg/ml hemin and 10 μg/ml nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD), referred to as sHTM agar. Bacterial strains used in this study

Generation of plasmids

Plasmids and primers used in this study are shown in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, respectively. All plasmids were generated using standard cloning techniques. Gel purification kit (Macherey-Nagel, 22740609.250) following the manufacturer instructions, and appropriate FastDigest restriction enzymes and Rapid DNA ligation kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, K1423) were used. Escherichia coli TOP10 was used as an intermediate host, plasmids were extracted and purified (Qiagen, 12143), and transformed into H. influenzae electrocompetent cells [50]. Transformants were selected on sHTM agar with the corresponding antibiotic and confirmed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Specifically, to generate pTBH03-Phyper-(SSR)13 and pTBH03-Phyper-(SSR)23 plasmids, two DNA fragments of 231 and 301 bp containing (SSR)13-downstream region and (SSR)23-downstream region, respectively, were commercially synthesized (GeneArt Gene Synthesis, Invitrogen). These DNA fragments were excised from the provided plasmids pMA:UTR14 and pMK:UTR24 with EcoRI and SpeI restriction enzymes and cloned into a previously linearized pTBH03 plasmid using the same restriction sites. Strain Rd KW20 expressing pTBH03-Phyper-SSR4 (P1309) was naturally obtained during clone checking of the pTBH03-Phyper-(SSR)13 and pTBH03-Phyper-(SSR)23 plasmids by Sanger sequencing. pTBH03-Phmw-86–028NP (used for the mRACE assays) was previously generated by SphI-SpeI cloning a 491 bp fragment containing the Phmw promoter-regulatory region from NTHi 86-028NP strain (260 bp), plus 214 bp upstream from the predicted P2-Phmw promoter region, and an RBS from Gram-negative bacteria (17 bp) into pTBH02 [3].

Multiple sequence alignment of Phmw, 5′-UTR, and HMW binding domain regions

The genomes of 51 NTHi strains previously sequenced using the long-read PacBio technology were downloaded from the NCBI. The paralogous hmw loci located adjacent to the yrbI or nearby the radA genes were located, and the sequences from all hmw 5′-UTR regions were aligned using Genious aligner and the MEGA11 software. The hmw186–028NP promoter and 5′-UTR nucleotide sequence (adjacent to the yrbI gene) were used as reference in both alignments. To perform HMW1/2 assignments, the HMW binding domains (BD) defined in the R2846 strain (amino acids 555 to 914 for HMW1A; amino acids 553 to 916 for HMW2A) were used to calculate percent amino acid identities of the 102 HMW BDs present in the 51 available fully assembled genomes by using Clustal Omega (Dataset S1).

Generation of Rd KW20Δrnc mutant strain

A 2713 bp DNA fragment containing the rnc gene (HI0014) and its respective flanking regions was amplified by PCR with Phusion polymerase (Fisher Scientific) using Rd KW20 genomic DNA as template and primers rnc-F1/2319 and rnc-R1/2320, and cloned into pJET1.2/blunt, generating pJET1.2-rnc plasmid. The cloned PCR product was disrupted by inverse PCR with Phusion polymerase using primers rnc-F2-Spc/2344 and rnc-R2-Spc/2345. An internal fragment was replaced by a blunt-ended Spec resistance cassette excised by EcoRV digestion from pRSM2832, generating pJET1.2-rnc::spec. This plasmid was used as a template to amplify the rnc disruption cassette with primers rnc-F1 and rnc-R1, and transform the H. influenzae Rd KW20 strain following the M-IV method [51, 52]. Transformants were selected on sHTM agar with Spec50 and gene inactivation was confirmed by PCR. The hmw1A promoter-regulatory region was Sanger sequenced was Sanger sequenced in the Rd KW20Δrnc-generated strain for SSR number confirmation.

Molecular beacon assay

The molecular beacon (MB) assay was performed as previously described [53], with the following modifications. Briefly, the hmw1 5′-UTR mRNA was commercially synthesized (a 122-base sequence containing the RNA hairpin only) and labeled with the 6-FAM and BHQ-1 molecules at the 5′ and 3′ ends, respectively (GenScript). Five picomoles of MB were mixed with 2.5 µl of 10× reaction buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 300 mM KCl, 200 mM NH4Cl, 15 mM DTT, 50 mM MgCl2) and DEPC water up to a final volume of 25 µl. FAM fluorescence emission was measured using the Bio-Rad CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System and the following incubation periods: 13°C, 10 min; 24 cycles of 5 min each starting at 13°C and with a 3°C increase per cycle. RNase III processing was demonstrated by using five picomoles MB mixed with 2.5 µl of 10× buffer RNase, 0.5 µl RNase III (AM2290, Invitrogen) and DEPC water up to a final volume of 25 µl; five picomoles of MB without RNase III were used as a control. Samples were incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Next, FAM fluorescence was measured as above indicated, with 24 cycles of 5 min each starting at 13°C, and increasing 3°C per cycle. In all cases, assays were performed in triplicate, on three independent occasions (n ≥ 3).

RNA electrophoretic separation on polyacrylamide gels

Five picomoles of MB (see earlier) were mixed with 2.5 µl of 10× buffer RNase, an RNase III concentration range consisting of 0.5, 0.25, 0.125, or 0.065 µl, and DEPC water up to a final volume of 25 µl; five picomoles of MB without RNase III were used as control. Samples were incubated at 37°C for 15 min, immediately mixed with equal volume of 2× Sample Loading Buffer (Norgen, 15096), and incubated at 65°C for 15 min before loading into 6% polyacrylamide TBE-urea gels (Invitrogen, EC6865BOX). After electrophoresis, RNA fluorescence was visualized with UV light using a ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Two independent replicates were performed.

Mapping of 5′-end from RNA molecules

The mapping of mRNA molecules’ 5′-ends was performed using a modified version of the Rapid Amplification of cDNA Ends (mRACE) method [54]. RNA was isolated from H. influenzae strains grown for 12 h on sHTM agar with Erm11. Two to five colonies were inoculated into 10 ml sBHI with Erm11 and grown at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 12 h at 100 rpm. Cultures were then diluted into 20 ml fresh sBHI to OD600 = 0.07 and grown to OD600 = 0.5–0.6. Seven ml/culture were then pelleted (4000 rpm, 10 min, 4°C), flash frozen, and stored at −80°C. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Then, total RNA samples were treated with the Cap-Clip Acid Pyrophosphatase (Tebu-Bio) following the manufacturer’s recommendations, for 45 min at 37°C. RNA was extracted by phenol–chloroform and precipitated with sodium acetate and cold ethanol. Next, 500 ng of Cap-Clip and non-Cap-Clip-treated RNA were ligated using the T4 RNA Ligase I (New England Biolabs) at 37°C for 45 min. The ligated RNA was used as a template for RT-PCR carried out using the SuperScript™ III One-Step RT-PCR System with the Platinum Taq DNA polymerase (Invitrogen), and the outward primers RACE.A/2199 and RACE.B/2200. PCR products were run in 2% agarose gels, and bands were purified and ligated into pGEM-T Easy (Promega). The resulting reactions were used to transform E. coli TOP10 cells. Isolated white colonies were first checked by PCR using universal primers M13_FW and M13_RV, and then analyzed by Sanger sequencing. Transcript putative initiation and processing sites were determined by sequence analysis, and nucleotide frequencies representing the 5′-ends were recorded as percentages (%).

Protein immunodetection

To determine HMW1A or GFP protein levels, protein extracts were prepared from bacteria grown for 12 h on PVX agar or on sHTM agar with Erm11, recovered with PBS, and adjusted to OD600 = 1. Alternatively, H. influenzae strains were grown for 12 h on PVX agar. Two to five colonies were inoculated into 10 ml sBHI and grown at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 12 h at 100 rpm. Cultures were next diluted into 20 ml fresh sBHI to OD600 = 0.07 and grown to OD600 = 0.6. Next, 1 ml of each culture was centrifuged (14 000 rpm, 5 min), pellets were washed with PBS, resuspended in 400 µl PBS, and sonicated for 30 min. In all cases, protein extracts were quantified using BCA assay (Thermo Fisher, 23227). Samples were diluted with protein loading buffer, incubated for 5 min at 95°C, and for 1 min on ice before gel loading. Samples were separated in sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis 7.5% (HMW1A) or 12% (GFP) CriterionTM TGX Stain-Free™ Precast Gel (Bio-Rad, 1610181, 1610185) on a mini-PROTEAN Tetra Cell system (Bio-Rad) and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane for immunoblotting. HMW1A was detected by using a primary guinea pig anti-HMWA (gp85) antibody diluted 1:2000 and incubated for 4 h at RT [56], and a secondary goat anti-guinea pig IgG (Santa Cruz, sc-2438) antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase, diluted 1:5000 and incubated for 1 h at RT. GFP was detected by using a primary mouse anti-GFP (Living Colors, Clontech, 632381) antibody diluted 1:5000 and incubated for 2 h at RT, and a secondary goat anti-mouse IgG (Fisher Scientific, 32430) antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase, diluted 1:2500, and incubated for 1.5 h at RT. ECL Supersignal West Dura Extended Duration Substrate (Thermo Fisher, 34075) was used for development; visualization was performed in a ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Experiments were performed on at least three independent occasions (n ≥ 3). Band densitometry analyses were performed using QuantityTools from ImageLab 6.1 software (Bio-Rad). Bands were detected manually, the average pixel intensity was measured, and a background pixel intensity subtraction was applied in all cases. Quantification values were standardized to the sample used as a control in each case to determine a relative ratio of detection.

Bacterial RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis

H. influenzae strains were grown for 12 h on PVX agar. Two to five colonies were inoculated into 10 ml sBHI and grown at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 12 h at 100 rpm. Cultures were next diluted into 20 ml fresh sBHI to OD600 = 0.07 and grown to OD600 = 0.5–0.6. In all cases, 7 ml/culture were then pelleted (4000 rpm, 10 min, 4°C), flash frozen, and stored at −80°C. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Reverse transcription was performed using 1 μg of RNA by PrimerScript RT Reagent kit (Takara). Complementary DNA (cDNA) diluted 1:10 was used as template for quantitative PCR (qPCR). In all cases, 20 μl reaction mixtures were prepared, containing 1× SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (Takara) and adequate primer pairs. Fluorescence data were analyzed with QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher). The comparative threshold cycle (Ct) method was used to obtain relative quantities of mRNA that were normalized using the gyrA gene as endogenous control (mean ± SD). Bacterial cultures were grown at least three times, and all samples were processed with technical triplicates (n ≥ 3).

Cell culture and bacterial infection

A549 human alveolar basal epithelial cells (ATCC CCL-185) were maintained as described [26], seeded to 1.5 × 105 cells/well on 24-well plates for 32 h, then serum starved for 16 h before infection. For infection, PBS-normalized bacterial suspensions (OD600 = 1, ∼109 CFU/ml) were prepared using H. influenzae strains freshly grown on PVX agar for 16 h. A ∼90% confluence was reached by the time of infection. SSR phase-variable HMW1 expression undergoes post-transcriptional regulation

The previously described P2 and P1 promoter elements, located upstream of the hmw1A gene, are separated by the (5′-ATCTTTC)n heptamer [47]. In previous work using transcriptional reporters, we showed that hmw1 mRNA expression is predominantly driven from the P2 region, whereas the P1 region was transcriptionally inactive in the conditions tested [3]. As a result, transcription from P2 (hereafter Phmw) generates mRNA that includes the 7-nt repeats (Fig. 1A). Thus, the 5′-UTR length varies according to the number of repeats, in addition to transcript levels decreasing with increasing heptamers [3, 47]. To assess whether SSR-mediated changes in hmw1 mRNA expression rely on the promoter activity of Phmw or on an alternative mechanism, we generated a set of GFP transcriptional fusion reporter plasmids in which we exchanged the Phmw element from the hyperinvasive clinical strain 86–028NP [58] for an unrelated constitutive promoter, Phyper [59], also varying the heptameric SSR count from 13 to 23 repeats. 86–028NP was selected as reference based on two previous studies of our research group. In [29], we identified acquisition of the hmw1ABC locus from the 86–028NP as responsible for gain of epithelial cell invasiveness. In [3], we characterized HMW86–028NP-driven cell invasion by generating a set of transcriptional fusion reporter plasmids coupling variants of the Phmw element to GFP. From those, plasmids carrying the native Phmw promoter and 5′-UTRs carrying either 13 or 23 repeats are now used as controls (Fig. 1B, upper panel). Reporter plasmids were introduced into the Rd KW20 strain [49], which naturally lacks the hmw genes, and heterologous GFP protein levels were detected by immunoblot. GFP protein levels were strongly decreased from plasmids with 23 compared to 13 repeats with either promoter (Fig. 1B, lower panel). This observation indicates that SSR phase variation affects hmw1 mRNA expression independently of promoter identity.

The hmw 5′-UTR folds into a stable long hairpin structure

Long 5′-UTRs often carry cis-acting regulatory elements that control gene expression. 5′-UTRs can fold into alternative structures in response to temperature (thermosensors), contain translationally active small upstream open reading frames (uORFs), or bind trans-acting factors such as metabolites (riboswitches), tRNAs (T-box), RNA-binding proteins, or small regulatory RNAs [60–64]. Moreover, homopolymeric guanine (G) repeats in long 5′-UTRs can be targeted by trans-acting sRNAs in such a way that length variation may determine post-transcriptional regulation [15, 16]. Given the potential regulatory role of the long hmw 5′-UTR, we asked whether this 5′-UTR may fold into an mRNA secondary structure, and whether SSR number impacts the predicted structure. We used the 5′-UTR sequence from the hmw1 of 86–028NP strain carrying 13 SSRs as a reference and the RNAstructure software, version 6.5 (https://rna.urmc.rochester.edu/RNAstructure.html). We predicted the formation of a long RNA hairpin comprising 122 nt of the hmw186–028NP 5′-UTR (Fig. 2A). Nearly identical predicted hairpin structures were obtained when increasing the SSR number (Supplementary Fig. S1). Interestingly, the left arm of this structure (from −175 to −116, taking the ATG start codon as reference for nucleotide positions) includes ten repeats that pair with the right arm (from −108 to −54), which in turn contains a G-rich nucleotide stretch (almost 50% of Gs). This G-rich region could form a G-quadruplex (G4) secondary structure according to QGRS Mapper, a web-based server for predicting such structures in nucleotide sequences [65].

Repeats are labeled in green, flanked by red dashed lines, and numbered; the G-rich region is labeled in pink; the start codon (ATG) and the position of each 5′-AUCUUUC repeat are indicated. Repeats are numbered in reverse to normalize numbering among variants with different repeat numbers (see Supplementary Figs S1 and S2). Otherwise, numbers would differ depending on total repeat number, making it difficult to visualize that all hairpin structures contain 10 repeats. To demonstrate the formation of this 5′-UTR hairpin in vitro, we used a synthesized molecular beacon (MB) system, as previously described [53, 66]. This MB consisted of a 122-mer RNA oligonucleotide, which includes the whole RNA sequence required to fold into the predicted hairpin structure. In addition, the carboxyfluorescein fluorescent dye (FAM) and the Black Hole Quencher 1 (BHQ 1) molecules were attached to the 5′ and 3′-ends of the RNA oligonucleotide, respectively. If the MB folds as expected, meaning a long double-stranded RNA hairpin, both FAM and BHQ 1 molecules will fall in close proximity to each other, preventing fluorescence emission [53]. In contrast, denaturing the MB structure by increasing incubation temperature would allow fluorescence emission (Fig. 2B). We started incubating the MB at 13°C for 5 min and then increased temperature in intervals of 3°C every 5 min up to 85°C. Fluorescence emission was measured during the entire process. Lack of fluorescence was registered from 13°C to 46°C, indicating that the MB is folded into a hairpin. When reaching temperatures above ∼46°C, FAM fluorescence emission was registered, indicating the melting of the hairpin structure (Fig. 2C). These data strongly support that the hmw 5′-UTR folds into a very stable structure compatible with the predicted RNA hairpin.

A minimum of eleven SSRs are found in nature correlating with the hmw 5′-UTR hairpin structure

NTHi isolates encode two HMW paralogs, and besides SSR count number, 5′-UTR polymorphisms are observed outside the SSR. We next asked whether this natural variation may affect the 5′-UTR structures. Because short-read assemblies often have contig breaks in and around the hmw1 and hmw2 operons, and since typical Illumina read lengths are insufficient to span long heptameric repeats, we used all publicly available genomes of hmw-containing NTHi isolates that were sequenced using long-read Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) technology (Dataset S1). A total of 51 PacBio-sequenced NTHi genomes contained both hmw loci. The HMW1/2A adhesins consist of four domains: a signal peptide; the pro-peptide containing the secretion domain that mediates interaction with the HMW1B/2B translocator before cleavage and release; the mature adhesin that contains the host binding domain (BD); and a C-terminal anchor [41, 46, 67]. Distinction between paralog hmw1 and hmw2 loci involves comparative analysis of their BDs to those of the prototype adhesins in strain R2846, since assignment based on synteny is unreliable because gene conversion can swap BDs between the two loci [3, 29, 46]. Here, BD comparative analyses were performed for paralog assignment on the 5′-UTRs from the 51 genomes, referring to them by whether the hmw locus corresponds to HMW1ABC or to HMW2ABC (Dataset S1, sheet 2). We examined the sequence of 102 Phmw promoter and 5′-UTR regions belonging to 102 hmw1 or hmw2 loci across this set of 51 NTHi genomes. Although 27 of 51 strains had evidence of the inversion polymorphism across hmw loci that was previously described [48], the Phmw promoter element (P2 region) was highly conserved, as was the heptameric repeat sequence itself (Dataset S1, sheets 1 and 3). Repeat number had a large range, between 11 and 63, with the most common being 16, 17, and 18 SSRs (found in 20, 18, and 14 of the analyzed 5′-UTRs, respectively) (Fig. 3A). Within each strain, paralog 5′-UTRs had uncorrelated repeat numbers. Of the 51 genomes, 17 were from strains that had been serially collected from 7 COPD patients (indicated for patients 3, 48, 6, 10, 67, 5, and 93 in Fig. 3A [31, 48]). A trend toward increasing SSR number over time could be observed agreeing with previous observations [3, 33]. Finally, 27 single-nucleotide and short indel variants were found in the downstream G-rich segment (Fig. 3B; Dataset S1, sheets 1 and 4).

RNA secondary structures were predicted for the full set of 5′-UTRs (representative examples are shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Figs S1 and S2). The long hairpin involving ten repeats was predicted in all cases. No alternative structures were found due to SSR number or to nearby polymorphisms. It is noteworthy that the minimum SSR number found in the 51 analyzed genomes was 11, which may always have ten SSRs allocated in the left arm of the RNA hairpin. These observations suggest an unknown conserved biological function for a minimal SSR count to maintain this hmw 5′-UTR hairpin structure.

RNase III cleaves the hmw 5′-UTR to modulate HMW-mediated H. influenzae-host airway interplay

The high conservation and stability of the hmw 5′-UTR hairpin led us to propose that it might be a target for double-strand-specific endoribonucleases. Since processed mRNA fragments would be generated by the endonucleolytic cleavage of the hmw 5′-UTR by a ribonuclease, we carried out a modified rapid amplification of cDNA ends (mRACE) assay [54] using RNA samples to in vivo map 5′-ends of hmw mRNA molecules by circularization. Considering that the 5′-end of the primary transcripts retains the triphosphate, which avoids RNA ligation for mRNA circularization, mRNAs were subjected to Cap-Clip acid pyrophosphatase treatment to hydrolyze 5′-triphosphate to 5′-monophosphate; processed mRNAs already include 5′-monophosphate. Thus, comparison of Cap-Clip-treated and untreated samples will allow discrimination between primary and processed transcripts.

Given that both the length and low quantity of the hmw1 transcript may limit RNA circularization efficiency, we took advantage of a previously generated reporter plasmid pTBH03-Phmw-86–028NP [3] that contains a transcriptional fusion coupling the hmw186–028NP upstream region to GFP. This plasmid was introduced into the Rd KW20 strain, which, as mentioned above, naturally lacks the hmw genes, to perform 5′-end mapping. The mRACE analysis gave similar results with or without Cap-Clip treatment suggesting a processing event occurring in the hmw 5′-UTR. Specifically, we mapped a monophosphorylated 5′-end at −138 nt from the ATG start codon, resulting in a processed hmw mRNA containing four complete 5′-ATCTTTC repeats (Fig. 4A).

RNase III is a highly conserved endoribonuclease that specifically cleaves both intramolecular stem structures and cis or trans intermolecular RNA duplexes to regulate gene expression in bacteria [68–71]. To test if hmw 5′-UTR processing is driven by RNase III, a Δrnc mutant was generated in Rd KW20 (Rd KW20Δrnc), transformed with pTBH03-Phmw-86–028NP, and used for mRACE 5′ end mapping. This showed that a full primary mRNA is generated in the Rd KW20Δrnc strain. Moreover, the likely transcription start site for the putative non-processed mRNAs was mapped downstream of the Phmw promoter element, corresponding to the first base of the first 5′-ATCTTTC repeat (Fig. 4A). This result confirmed previous data [3] indicating that the so-called P2 region, here renamed Phmw, is the only promoter responsible for hmw1 transcription under the conditions tested.

This mRACE assay does not rule out whether the observed hmw 5′-UTR cleavage is directly catalyzed by RNase III at the hairpin structure or if, alternatively, it may involve a separate ribonuclease with an indirect requirement for RNase III. We took advantage of the MB system shown above to next evaluate the direct effect of RNase III on the hairpin structure. The MB was incubated at 37°C in the presence of an E. coli RNase III for 1 h. Then, to gradually denature the MB, we increased the temperature in intervals of 3°C every 5 min until 85°C, and quantified fluorescence emission. Figure 4B shows that fluorescence was registered much earlier in the RNase III-treated (19°C) than in the untreated (46°C) MB, suggesting that RNase III destabilizes the hairpin structure. To further confirm that hairpin destabilization is due to a direct RNase III cleavage, the MB was incubated with increasing concentrations of RNase III for 15 min at 37°C. Resulting reactions were run into polyacrylamide gels under denaturing conditions to allow fluorescence emission and tracking of RNA molecules. As shown in Fig. 4C, a lower band was obtained when the MB was incubated with RNase III, and this processed RNA band yield directly correlated with RNase III concentration. These results confirm that the hmw 5′-UTR is directly cleaved by RNase III, also suggesting a possible conformational change in the mRNA structure, which, in turn, could modify RBS availability (Fig. 4D). In fact, we next asked if such processed hmw mRNA has an effect on protein expression. To do so, we constructed a GFP transcriptional fusion reporter plasmid coupling the hmw 5′-UTR carrying four 5′-ATCTTTC repeats. This plasmid was introduced into the Rd KW20 strain and GFP levels were compared to those produced by the reporter plasmid carrying 13 SSR repeats. This reporter plasmid mimicking the RNase III-processed hmw mRNA produced higher GFP levels (Supplementary Fig. S3), suggesting that hmw mRNA processing may facilitate protein expression.

Given the direct processing of the hmw 5′-UTR by RNase III, we next assessed the role of such processing in HMW-mediated host-bacteria interactions. We used the Rd KW20 derivative strain rRdS, previously generated by hmw1ABC86–028NP recombination [29], which has a single hmw1 locus with 14 repeats of the 5′-ATCTTTC sequence in its upstream region. rRdS shows an airway epithelial hyperinvasive phenotype comparable to that of 86–028NP [29]. Since Rd KW20 naturally lacks both the hmw1 and hmw2 loci, rRdS allows us to uncouple those two loci, thereby facilitating genetic manipulations, simpler construct design, and more straightforward interpretation of phenotypic data [3]. rRdS was used to inactivate the rnc gene encoding RNase III, yielding the rRdSΔrnc mutant strain. Inactivation of rnc in rRdS had no impact on bacterial growth (data not shown) but led to reduced hmw1 transcript and HMW1A protein levels (Fig. 4E), confirming that RNase III-mediated hmw mRNA processing increases HMW expression. We made the same observations with overall lower expression due to phase variation when using rRdS WT and Δrnc strains carrying seventeen 5′-ATCTTTC repeats in the hmw1 upstream region (Supplementary Fig. S4), likely uncoupling RNase III processing from SSR number. In terms of host interplay phenotypes, decreases in hmw1 transcript and HMW1A protein levels reduce intracellular invasion rates and increase biofilm formation levels [3]. Here, as expected, inactivation of rnc also decreased intracellular invasion and increased biofilm formation when compared to the rRdS isogenic WT strain (Fig. 4F).

These results provide evidence that the hmw 5′-UTR contains the heptameric repeat as part of a hairpin structure, which normally undergoes mRNA processing by the endoribonuclease RNase III. Such processing may lead to an mRNA structural reorganization. Inactivation of the rnc gene limits intracellular invasion and increases biofilm formation by altering HMW expression, highlighting RNase III as a key factor in the HMW-mediated NTHi-host airway interplay.

The long RNA hairpin is a key contributor to HMW-associated phenotypes

As shown earlier, the hmw 5′-UTR structure remained unchanged for repeat numbers ≥10 and was unaffected by naturally occurring polymorphisms in the G-rich region that base pairs with the SSR (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Figs S1 and S2). To further investigate how this long hairpin structure modulates HMW1A protein expression in vivo, we generated a panel of chromosomal mutants in the rRdS strain aiming to modify the 5′-UTR folding. First, we tried reducing the number of SSRs to eliminate the stable hairpin structure. However, we were unsuccessful at generating any rRdS derivative with ten repeats or fewer due to either construct instability, or unavoidable sequence mutation during the DNA synthesis process (data not shown). Although not formally tested, this may contribute to explaining the minimum of eleven repeats found in nature (Fig. 3A).

Second, four rRdS derivative strains were made by design. By maintaining repeat number at 14, we modified the left or the right arm of the hairpin to block or to reconstitute its folding, respectively. Specifically, the 5′-UTR sequence was modified by: (i) inverting the 5′-ATCTTTC heptanucleotide sequence to 5′-GAAAGAT (rRdS-SSR-invert); (ii) changing between purine and pyrimidine nucleobases from 5′-ATCTTTC to 5′-GCTCCCT (rRdS-SSR-change); (iii) changing the right arm sequence (5′-GGGAAGGGAGGGAGGGG to 5′-TTGAAGTTAGTGATTGT) to simultaneously eliminate the G-rich region and modify the RNase III cleavage site [rRdS-Grich-(G::T)]; or (iv) changing the right arm sequence (5′-GGGAAGGGAGGGAGGGG to 5′-AAGAAGAAAGAGAAAGA) to eliminate the G-rich region but without affecting the hairpin structure [i.e. a compensatory mutation, rRdS-Grich-(G::A)] (Fig. 5A). These rRdS derivative strains were successfully generated and had no growth defects (data not shown). RNA structures of the 5′-UTR SSR-invert and SSR-change derivatives were predicted to not produce the long RNA hairpin with the G-rich region. The Grich-(G::T) structure affected the hairpin structure at the RNase III cleavage site, otherwise maintained in the Grich-(G::A) structure (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S5). We hypothesized that elimination of this RNase III-targeted structure may have consequences comparable to those observed upon rnc gene inactivation. Indeed, this was the case as SSR-invert, SSR-change and Grich-(G::T) 5′-UTR sequence modifications led to reduced hmw1 transcript and HMW1A protein levels, and to subsequent decreased airway epithelial invasion and increased biofilm formation compared to the rRdS-ermC WT isogenic strain. In contrast, the Grich-(G::A) 5′-UTR derivative, designed to eliminate the predicted G-quadruplex but preserve the hairpin structure, showed phenotypes comparable to those of the WT isogenic strain (Fig. 5C and D).

Thus, the 7-nt repeat region at the 5′-UTR of the hmw loci may also be key for the hairpin structure to be correctly formed, besides its already known involvement in hmw1A gene expression by phase-variable changes. Moreover, having a heptameric repeat is insufficient, as the proposed regulation requires generating the 5′-UTR hairpin structure compatible with processing by RNase III, which in turn involves the downstream complementary G-rich region. Indeed, maintenance of both the hairpin structure and the RNase III processing site are key for post-transcriptional regulation to occur, as shown by the (G::T) and (G::A) G-rich region modifications. Also, the rRdS-SSR-invert mutant maintained the 5′-ATCTTTC sequence in the complementary DNA strand but its phenotypes correlate to those of the Δrnc mutant strain, which may reinforce an RNA-mediated post-transcriptional regulatory mechanism, rather than a transcriptional one where a putative DNA-binding protein may target the SSR region to modulate HMW expression.

Phase variation at the HMW SSR depends upon RNase III processing and 5′-UTR cis-acting element structure

The molecular mechanism of SSR expansion and contraction is typically thought to be due to slippage of the DNA strands during DNA replication or from recombination between copies of the same SSR sequence [9]. Previous evidence supports a role for expansions of the SSR in the hmw1 5′-UTR in attenuating its expression [20, 47]. Here, we provide new evidence that the ten hmw1A-proximal heptamers are part of a conserved hairpin structure required for post-transcriptional processing by RNase III and thus normal expression. SSR length variation and post-transcriptional processing may somehow be in conflict if RNase III activity leads to a processed mRNA containing four repeats in its 5′-end; in other words, if this RNA processing activity is independent of the SSR number found in the DNA and in the primary unprocessed transcript, as it leads to a four-repeats-processed mRNA anyhow. Testing this hypothesis would require performing mRACE using a higher SSR number reporter plasmid; however, higher SSR number involves decreased gene expression, which impairs the assay by the insufficient mRNA yield (data not shown).

Our data suggest that ten repeats are integral to normal expression via their role in the RNA structure required for processing, with changes to the number of additional copies “tuning” hmw1 expression levels. These findings led us to speculate that the rate of phase variation itself—i.e. genetic changes to the copy number of the SSR—may be directly affected by the transcriptional or post-transcriptional processing machinery passing through the heptameric repeat, e.g. due to collisions between the replication and transcription machinery or from transcription-induced DNA damage [10, 72].

To test whether population-level changes to the heptameric repeat in the 5′-UTR of hmw1 could be detected within bacterial cultures, and whether such phase variation may change in mutants, we carried out a 5-day serial passaging experiment in sBHI broth (days 0 to 4; passages 0 to 4, i.e. P0 to P4). We reasoned that if phase variation events were frequent enough, the variance in SSR copy number would change over passages. For each passage (Fig. 6A), overnight agar-grown bacteria were inoculated into 5 ml of rich liquid medium and cultured for 8 h before aliquoting three ways: (a) 0.1 ml were plated to agar to initiate the next cycle 16 h later; (b) 0.1 ml were serially diluted and plated to agar to collect 10 single colonies for single-clone Sanger sequencing of PCR amplicons across the repeats tract; and (c) the remaining 4.8 ml were pelleted and used for deep sequencing of PCR amplicons across the repeat by PacBio single-molecule long-read sequencing. The assay was performed in duplicate (two biological replicates, 1R and 2R). Prior to deep sequencing, Sanger reactions of PCR amplicons were performed on 20 isolated colonies from the last passage (P4; 10 colonies per replicate, 1R and 2R) from each genotype tested. Results in Supplementary Table S3 indicate that WT clones showed variable heptameric repeat counts as expected; however, mutant strains—including the rnc gene inactivation—appeared to have little to no variation in their SSR copy number. Thus, we used deep sequencing on PCR amplicons from the bulk culture (as a pool) at each passage to examine whether and how SSR copy number changed in each strain over passages.

Since copy number in the heptameric repeat of hmw1 can exceed the length of typical short-read Illumina sequences, we used PacBio to collect low-error circular consensus sequences that spanned the full repeat. After filtering for reads between 900 and 1500 base pairs, we collected on average 49 477 reads per sample (range 22 900 to 116 124), of which nearly all contained a heptameric repeat corresponding to the one expected (Dataset S2), according to the Tandem Repeats Finder tool [57], which we used to identify and count repeats within individual sequence reads (Dataset S3). This identified clear systematic changes over serial passages for both replicates in the rRdS WT strain, changing from 14.4 ± 0.8 heptameric repeats at the initial P0 passage to 17.8 ± 2.3 at P4, changing an average of 3.4 repeats (Fig. 6B). Notably, the frequency of reads with <10 repeats was rare (<0.5%), consistent with their absence from the complete genomes surveyed. This indicates that under these conditions, selection favors expansion of the hmw1 5′-UTR heptameric repeats, and that rates of phase variation are sufficiently high to see an increasing variance over subsequent passages.

We next examined whether RNase III affected SSR copy number change in the 5′-UTR of hmw1, and remarkably, we found that it did. In contrast to rRdS WT, in the rRdSΔrnc mutant strain, the 15.9 ± 1.4 repeats at P0 remained relatively unaffected by serial passages, with 15.5 ± 0.9 repeats at P4 (Fig. 6B). These data indicate that phenotypes seen in Fig. 4 cannot be solely due to an expanded repeat in the Δrnc mutant, since the high repression in hmw1 transcript and HMW1A protein levels is not consistent with the small increase in the mean repeat number. Previous work assessing mechanisms of SSR phase variation in H. influenzae showed that inactivation of polI and mutS, needed for mismatch repair, destabilized tetranucleotide and dinucleotide repeat tracts, respectively, but mutations in genes required for genome maintenance had no effect [73–75]. Expression of the polI, mutS, and recJ genes was not modified by rnc gene inactivation, excluding changes in their expression as indirectly affecting HMW phase variation (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Inheritance of genetic information in all cells relies on RNA–DNA hybrid formation during replication and transcription [76, 77]. RNA–DNA hybrid formation is necessary, but persistent hybrids compromise genome integrity, such that ribonuclease H (hybrid) enzymes incise RNA within RNA–DNA hybrids, contributing to their removal [76]. We tested whether inactivation of the rnhA gene—encoding an RNase H that acts on RNA:DNA duplexes—was required for phase variation over serial passages. These passages also showed increased mean and variance over serial passages with an average of 2.2 repeats, from 15.1 ± 0.8 to 17.3 ± 1.4 (Fig. 6B; for completion, phenotypic analysis is shown in Supplementary Fig. S7). Variation of the SSR length was clearly observed, with slightly less change than WT. This does not rule out a role for RNases acting on RNA:DNA duplexes since, moreover, the rnhB gene was still intact. Together, these data strongly suggest that the activity of the RNase III enzyme is required for high rates of phase variation in the 5′-UTR of hmw1, which further indicates a role for the 5′-UTR hairpin structure in genetic changes to the heptameric repeat.

Since Δrnc eliminated observable phase variation in the serial passaging experiment, we also tested length variation in the SSR-invert, SSR-change, Grich-(G::T), and Grich-(G::A) mutant strains. rRdS-SSR-invert and rRdS-SSR-change strains showed no observable phase variation over serial passages; the Grich-(G::T) strain showed less phase variable change than WT, with an average of 2.8 repeats, from 15.7 ± 1 at P0 to 18.5 ± 1.2 at P4. In contrast, the Grich-(G::A) strain—designed to maintain hairpin structure—behaved in terms of SSR variation by changing an average of 4.8 repeats, from 14.5 ± 1.1 at P0 to 19.3 ± 1.2 at P4 (Fig. 6B). Overall, when looking at SSR number heterogeneity at the final passage P4 across all tested strains, such heterogeneity is extensive in WT, ΔrnhA, and Grich-(G::A), but lower for the rest of strains (Dataset S3).

These data strongly indicate that RNase III-dependent processing of the hairpin RNA not only controls hmw1 expression, but is also required for hypervariability of the heptameric repeat within the genomic DNA itself, further supported by the Grich-(G::A) hairpin-preserving chromosomal modification. Also aligned with this notion, hairpin RNA alteration by modifying the heptanucleotide sequence severely impairs hmw1 expression and phase variation, while the Grich-(G::T) change rendered milder effects with an intermediate phenotype in terms of hmw1 expression and SSR variation.

Transcription of the hmw mRNA is required for phase variable changes in the heptameric repeat

Results shown in Fig. 6 indicate a role for RNase III in phase variability of hmw1, and the SSR- and Grich-(G:T) variants may have affected DNA structure itself such that phase variation was affected (e.g. if the repeat is involved in branched DNA structures, and slippage occurs solely as a result of DNA replication slippage with no contribution from transcription). Although this might reveal an unknown role for RNase III in DNA replication, we instead sought to test whether transcription from Phmw was required for high rates of phase variability at hmw1. To address this, a rRdS-ermC derivative strain was generated that maintains the full native 5′-UTR but deletes the Phmw promoter region (rRdS-ΔPhmw). As expected, compared to the isogenic WT strain, the rRdS-ΔPhmw had undetectable hmw1 transcript and HMW1A protein, decreased epithelial invasion, and increased biofilm formation (Fig. 7A and B). Moreover, serial passage of the rRdS-ΔPhmw strain showed strongly impaired phase variation, comparable to that of the rnc and other repeat mutants (Fig. 7C). These results support that hmw transcription, the 5′-UTR hairpin in the transcript, and processing by RNase III are all required for heptameric repeat number changes in the H. influenzae hmw1 5′-UTR element.

The hmw RNA hairpin is required for phase variation in response to both SSR expansion and contraction selective pressures

Above experiments show that H. influenzae growth in sBHI medium selects for lower HMW expression, and the mean repeat number increase is a result of this selection. But low HMW expression may in fact remove sBHI selective pressure, in turn eliminating the need for SSR variation. Thus, the observed lack of phase variation could be the result of abrogating such selective pressure without the need for RNA-mediating changes in SSR length. As such, our sBHI serial passage observations would not allow deciphering if hairpin/RNase III coupling to increased SSR number relates to bacterial response to selective pressure, or if a processed mRNA is required for phase variation to occur.

We next hypothesized that a pressure selecting for increased HMW expression would lower the repeat mean number. In this case, if phase variation is a regulatory process confined to the DNA level, repeat number should decrease independently of HMW expression level; however, if RNA is required to mediate changes in the SSR length, repeat contraction would be impaired if transcription is reduced. Based on our previous TREP gain-of-function assay [29], we performed serial selection by lung epithelial cell gentamicin protection to enrich for invasive clones of the rRdS-24SSR strain. As previously shown, this high SSR number leads to low rRdS HMW expression and invasion rate [3]. We carried out a 5-day serial invasion experiment (days 0 to 4; cycles 0 to 4, i.e. pools from cycles 0 to 4, C0 to C4) (Fig. 8A). Epithelial invasion rates and phase variation frequency of the bacterial intracellular population were quantified over serial cycles, which, importantly, selected for increased invasion (Fig. 8B), and decreased SSR repeat number, which declined by 11.1 repeats on average, from 24 ± 1.5 at C0 to only 12.9 ± 2 repeats at C4 (Fig. 8C).

Same serial cell invasion cycle assay was performed with the low-invader rRdS SSR-invert strain (it contains the 5′-ATCTTTC repeats in a reverse orientation impairing hairpin structure, and hmw is transcribed at a low level; see Fig. 5). We selected this mutant as a control where the SSR sequence is maintained but inverted, which should contribute to distinguishing between DNA-dependent or hairpin-dependent SSR variation. In this case, quantification of the intracellular population did not select for changes in epithelial invasion (Fig. 8B). Notably, HMW phase variation was impaired (Fig. 8C), which supports the coupling of hairpin/RNase III/processed mRNA to phase variation in response to selective pressure.

Together, these results fully support that hmw transcription, the 5′-UTR hairpin, and its processing by RNase III are needed for HMW phase variation to occur. Moreover, hairpin structure and processing are coupled to phase variation independently of directionality, i.e. SSR expansion or contraction, which depends on selective pressure, thus contributing to H. influenzae patho-adaptation during airway infection.

Discussion

Human-adapted bacterial pathogens have evolved specific 5′-UTR-mediated regulatory mechanisms to control expression of genes involved in virulence. Long 5′-UTRs can form complex alternative RNA structures through base pairing, which might be reconfigured by external stimuli to affect mRNA stability and/or translation initiation [66, 78–85]. Here, we demonstrate that the folding of a long RNA hairpin in the 5′-UTR of the hmw mRNA modulates the HMW-mediated H. influenzae bacterial-host interplay and SSR phase variation. HMW is a major adhesin for airway infection by H. influenzae, involved in both host cell invasion and biofilm growth. The hmw RNA hairpin structure includes a range of features with key roles in modulating both hmw post-transcriptional regulation and SSR phase variation.

First, the left arm of the RNA hairpin includes ten 5′-ATCTTTC repeats, and RNA folding predictions with higher SSR numbers do not alter significantly the predicted RNA structure. Interestingly, hmw 5′-UTR sequence comparative analyses revealed no naturally occurring strains with fewer than eleven repeats upstream of hmw, suggesting that H. influenzae genomes maintain a minimum number of these repeats for functional expression. When trying to evaluate the effect of lowering the SSR number below eleven, we failed to construct strains carrying the Phmw promoter and 4, 9, or 10 heptameric repeats. Synthetic constructs accumulated mutations in the hmw promoter region when cloned into E. coli. Fluorescent reporter plasmids containing such mutated promoters showed no GFP production, supporting that only transcriptionally inactive constructs could be cloned (data not shown). These observations lead us to conclude that hmw 5′-UTR sequences carrying <11 SSRs might affect bacterial cell viability for currently unknown reasons.

Second, the right arm of the RNA hairpin contains a G-rich nucleotide stretch, which pairs the SSR repeats, creating the RNase III processing region. Alternatively, this G-rich stretch may also form a G-quadruplex (G4) secondary structure. G4s are non-canonical DNA or RNA structures that can form in G-rich stretches of nucleic acids. In bacteria, G4s regulate transcription through blocking RNA polymerase from binding to promoter sequences or through helping single-stranded DNA formation, thereby increasing transcription, and can also regulate translation by interfering with ribosome binding to the G4-containing mRNA [86]. Potential G4-forming sequences are overrepresented in regulatory regions of bacterial pathogen genomes [87], and a G4-forming sequence is essential for antigenic variation of Neisseria gonorrhoeae [88]. Decreased HMW expression related to progressive SSR expansion over time in COPD patients has been suggested to be a pathoadaptive trait to high serum titers of HMW1/HMW2-specific antibodies [33]. Additional regulatory elements may contribute to such antigenic variation as, e.g. natural variation in the hmw 5′-UTR. However, the natural variation observed in the G-rich sequence did not involve changes in the hairpin structure (Supplementary Fig. S2), and the specific mutation Grich-(G::A), which changes the G-rich sequence but maintains hairpin structure, did not modify hmw-associated phenotypes. Although this highlights the likely relevance of hairpin structure over G-rich sequence, we acknowledge a faster and wider SSR number increase in the Grich-(G::A) strain compared to WT (see Fig. 6C). Further investigations are needed to understand whether the folding of one RNA structure (RNA hairpin) may crosstalk with the formation of another structure (G-quadruplex) in the same RNA molecule and vice versa, and how this may affect post-transcriptional regulation and/or SSR phase variation.

Third, the hmw 5′-UTR hairpin is targeted by RNase III according to in vivo mapping of mRNA boundaries by mRACE and in vitro molecular beacon assays. RNase III cleaves at the left arm of the hairpin instead of generating a double-strand cleavage, as often occurs for perfectly complementary double-stranded RNAs. This is a common phenomenon when the intramolecular stem-loop structures are not perfectly complementary, as the one predicted for the hmw RNA hairpin, which includes some mismatched base pairs and bulges in the stem structure. In these cases, RNase III may cleave only one strand of the RNA hairpin depending on the location of these irregularities [71, 89]. Inactivation of the rnc gene decreased HMW production, which correlates with results obtained with strains carrying mutations that affect hairpin folding. We may also speculate that RNase III processing is needed to increase translation efficiency by facilitating the access of ribosomes to its binding site, or by modifying the action of trans-acting factors such as a putative regulatory sRNA and/or RNA-binding protein, which might target the hmw 5′-UTR. This later hypothesis has been proposed for the long 5′-UTR of the Staphylococcus aureus cspA mRNA, which analogously folds into a long hairpin, targeted by both RNase III and its own encoded CspA RNA chaperone. CspA interacts through a U-rich motif located at the RNase III cleavage site in the cspA mRNA, showing CspA as a putative RNase III antagonist [68, 82]. RecJ, ExoI, and RecG exonucleases participate in H. influenzae genome maintenance but do not influence SSR variation [75]. Further investigation should attempt to decipher whether hmw mRNA is structurally reorganized after RNase III processing and/or if H. influenzae RNA helicases or RNA chaperones are participating in this process.

We also present evidence that, for phase variation to occur, the hmw 5′-UTR needs to be transcribed, folded, and processed by RNase III. We speculate that this RNA-mediated phase variation mechanism could be in conflict with the DNA polymerase slippage hypothesis proposed to explain SSR variation [20], as the SSR-invert mutant (same DNA stretch of repeats but reversely cloned) did not present repeat variation, suggesting that neither DNA polymerase slippage nor a DNA recombination phenomenon may play a role in hmw phase variation. We acknowledge a caveat when forming conclusions from our sBHI serial passage assay, as if the mean repeat number increase is a result of selection for lower HMW expression, we may have removed such selective pressure in the mutant strains where HMW1 is poorly expressed, and the lack of selective pressure may eliminate SSR variation without RNA necessarily mediating changes in SSR length. However, our epithelial cell invasion enrichment assay eliminates this caveat, as it is a pressure selecting for increased HMW expression that lowers the mean repeat number and requires transcribed and structured 5′-UTR RNA to mediate such contraction in the SSR length (see Fig. 8).

In conclusion, we present novel regulatory and genetic roles for RNase III in bacterial adaptation, where the processing of the 5′-UTR heptameric repeat in hmw transcripts operates as a cis-acting regulatory element essential to HMW1A expression and changes in the SSR copy number, therefore modulating H. influenzae epithelial intracellular invasiveness and biofilm growth.
