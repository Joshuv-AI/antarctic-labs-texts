Real‐world comparative effects of slow‐release oral morphine vs. methadone on healthcare use for opioid use disorder: A population‐based target trial emulation
Authors: Vivienne Y Zhou, Nadia Fairbairn, Jingxin Lei, Rupinder Brar, Paxton Bach, M Eugenia Socias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo examine the comparative effects of slow‐release oral morphine (SROM) vs. methadone for the treatment of opioid use disorder (OUD) on healthcare use.DesignThis retrospective cohort study emulated a target trial using electronic medical record data from Vancouver Coastal Health Authority linked with population‐based administrative health data from British Columbia (BC), Canada.SettingVancouver Coastal Health region in BC, between 1 July 2017 and 30 June 2024, operating under a universal healthcare system, where the majority of illicit opioids are contaminated with fentanyl.ParticipantsOUD patients aged between 18 and 65 years.InterventionsIntent‐to‐treat (ITT): receiving a new prescription of SROM or methadone for the treatment of OUD (regardless of treatment initiation or adherence). Per‐protocol (PP): initiating and adhering to the prescribed SROM vs methadone (real‐world on‐treatment exposure).MeasurementsThe daily rate of healthcare use—including all‐cause and opioid‐specific emergency department (ED) visits, all‐cause and opioid‐specific hospitalization days, all‐cause physician encounters/services, OUD‐related physician visits, and non‐OUD‐related primary care physician visits—over 1 year following treatment prescription or initiation was modelled using weighted modified Poisson regression with generalized estimating equations. Cumulative healthcare use at 1 year and corresponding adjusted rate ratios (aRRs) were estimated.FindingsWe identified 3254 unique individuals [median (Q1–Q3) age 37 (30–46) years, 64.2% male] contributing to 4059 person‐trials (eligible treatment episodes) for the ITT analysis (32.6% SROM) and 1992 unique individuals contributing to 2276 person‐trials for the PP analysis (27.4% SROM). ITT analysis shows that receiving a SROM vs. methadone prescription was not associated with statistically significant differences in the rates of all‐cause [aRR = 1.10, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 0.997–1.20] and opioid‐specific ED visits (aRR = 1.00, 95% CI = 0.90–1.13), all‐cause (aRR = 0.99, 95% CI = 0.81–1.18) and opioid‐specific hospitalization days (aRR = 0.97, 95% CI = 0.78–1.20), all‐cause physician encounters/services (aRR = 1.00, 95% CI = 0.94–1.06), OUD‐related physician visits (aRR = 0.94, 95% CI = 0.87–1.01) and primary care physician visits (aRR = 1.11, 95% CI = 0.98–1.24). PP analysis also did not observe statistically significant differences across healthcare outcomes among those who initiated and remained on SROM vs. methadone treatment, with aRRs of 1.24 (95% CI = 0.91–1.50), 1.01 (95% CI = 0.67–1.36), 1.06 (95% CI = 0.65–1.58), 0.95 (95% CI = 0.53–1.58), 1.03 (95% CI = 0.90–1.17), 0.97 (95% CI = 0.82–1.14) and 1.06 (95% CI = 0.73–1.46), respectively.ConclusionsThis emulated trial on opioid use disorder patients under a universal healthcare setting in Canada did not observe statistically significant differences in risk of healthcare use outcomes between the patients receiving slow‐release oral morphine and those receiving methadone following treatment prescription and initiation. This supports slow‐release oral morphine as a viable alternative to methadone with similar real‐world effectiveness in preventing all‐cause and opioid‐related acute healthcare needs and the potential to expand treatment options for opioid use disorder without increasing health system burden.Keywords: healthcare utilization, medications for opioid use disorder, methadone, slow‐release oral morphine, target trial emulation

INTRODUCTION

Over the past 25 years, opioid‐related overdoses and deaths in North America have escalated through multiple waves, reaching an unprecedented scale [1]. In Canada, British Columbia (BC) has been disproportionately affected, with a public health emergency being declared following an alarming rise in opioid‐related deaths in 2016 [2]. In 2024, eight years into this emergency, the opioid toxicity death rate in BC reached 41.0 per 100 000 population, compared with a national average of 17.6 per 100 000 [3]. Amid complex social and economic factors, the toxic and unregulated drug supply has been identified as the primary driver of these deaths [4, 5].

In the United States (US), the total cost of excess health care utilization associated with opioid use disorder (OUD) is estimated at approximately $90 billion per year, primarily attributed to costs associated with hospital and emergency department (ED) encounters [6, 7]. The most recent data available in Canada shows, in the year 2024 alone, there were 24 587 opioid‐related poisoning ED visits, averaging 67 visits per day and 5514 opioid‐related poisoning hospitalizations, averaging 15 hospitalizations per day [3]. Beyond opioid‐related poisoning, people with OUD are also more likely to develop serious bacterial infections requiring long‐term intravenous antibiotic therapy and often resulting in hospitalization [8, 9]. These hospitalizations are associated with longer length of stay and higher odds of discharge to post‐acute care facilities compared with people without OUD [10, 11]. Comorbid mental health disorders are also common [12], further increasing clinical complexity, healthcare use and mortality [13, 14].

Pharmacological treatment for OUD is a cornerstone of the public health response in addressing the opioid crisis. Initiating and adhering to effective medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD) is consistently associated with reduced overdose risk and improved health outcomes [15], as well as reduced healthcare use over time [16, 17]. However, uptake and engagement remain low because of individual‐ and structural‐level barriers [18].

Slow‐release oral morphine (SROM) has been used as an off‐label form of MOUD in Canada since 2017 [19, 20]. In BC, SROM is prescribed and dispensed in a manner similar to methadone, suggesting a broadly comparable health‐system burden for treatment delivery. Specifically, both are initially prescribed in office‐based settings by trained clinicians as once‐daily dosing and dispensed via daily witnessed ingestion at community pharmacies; take‐home doses are considered after at least 4 weeks of dose stabilization and adherence [21]. Despite SROM's increasing role in treatment guidelines and rising rates of adoption, its evidence base remains limited, with existing primary evidence mainly coming from four small European randomised controlled trials (RCTs) suggesting similar clinical efficacy (e.g. treatment retention or reduced unregulated opioid use) compared to methadone [22, 23, 24].

Studies on clinical efficacy may not fully capture the broader impact(s) of MOUD relevant to patients and health systems, underscoring the need to examine healthcare utilization as a separate complementary outcome domain. For example, ED visits and hospitalizations could reflect patients' overall acute care needs and morbidity, whereas OUD‐related ED visits and hospitalizations more specifically capture those related to opioid‐related harms and can serve as surrogate markers of real‐world treatment effectiveness. Physician encounters/services reflect general outpatient healthcare use and burden, whereas OUD‐specific physician visits capture OUD care engagement, reflecting proactive disease management, but could also reflect potential complications. However, most existing studies examining healthcare utilization have compared individuals receiving any MOUD with those receiving no treatment and have shown reduced acute care utilization and increased outpatient care use [25]. Direct comparative evidence on healthcare utilization across MOUD remains limited, especially in the fentanyl era, with no available evidence for SROM.

Using observational data to emulate the pRESTO RCT [26], our recent work has shown that SROM was associated with a higher risk of treatment discontinuation and lower adherence compared to methadone, but a similar mortality risk [27]. In this present study, we evaluated the comparative effectiveness of SROM vs. methadone on healthcare utilization (a secondary outcome of the study)—a policy‐relevant and public health‐relevant marker of morbidity, care engagement and system burden in supporting evidence‐based approaches to OUD care.

METHOD

Study setting, design and data sources

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using an observational target trial emulation framework to evaluate the comparative effectiveness of SROM vs. methadone in patients receiving clinical care and services provided by the Vancouver Coastal Health Authority (VCHA) in the province of BC, Canada. See Table S1 for key protocol components. VCHA is one of the five public health authorities in BC, serving more than 1.25 million people (~25% of BC's population). Its service area includes one of the epicentres of Canada's overdose crisis—the Vancouver Downtown Eastside, a marginalised urban neighbourhood with high overdose risk, healthcare needs, poverty and homelessness. Under Canada's universal healthcare system, patients with OUD who live or stay in the Vancouver Coastal Health region have free and low‐threshold access to a large network of health and harm reduction services, including drop‐in and office‐based care, dispensation of MOUD through community‐based pharmacies, as well as numerous harm reduction supply outlets and withdrawal management facilities.

Our study used de‐identified patient‐level longitudinal data accessed from VCHA's intrahealth electronic medical record (EMR) system, which captures detailed information on all treatment prescriptions from VCHA's primary care and community clinics, linked with BC's high‐quality administrative data on all provincially funded healthcare services. See File S1 for databases used. Together, the linked data produced a comprehensive record of patients' socio‐demographic characteristics, medical and psychiatric comorbidity diagnoses, physicians and ED visits, hospitalizations and prescribed and dispensed medications, enabling longitudinal assessment of treatment and healthcare utilization.

Patient eligibility

In this study, adults aged between 19 and 65 years who met the following criteria are included: (1) have a diagnosis of OUD, defined as having an International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 9th/10th version code for opioid dependence/abuse (see Table S2) or at least one episode of MOUD prescription as recorded in the EMR; (2) have a ‘new’ prescription [i.e. no dispensation of MOUD in the past 7 days (accounting for take‐home doses) or 42 days for extended‐release buprenorphine) of the 24‐hour formulation of SROM‐ (i.e. Kadian) or methadone‐based MOUD [see Table S3 for Drug and Product Identification Number (DIN/PIN) used] from a primary care or community clinic between 1 July 2017 and 30 June 2023 as recorded in the EMR to allow for at least 365 days of follow‐up; (3) have no cancer diagnosis in the past year; (4) have no evidence of prolonged corrected QT interval (QTc) (defined as QTc >500 ms, using the Fridericia formula) in the past 3 months; and (5) have not been prescribed with more than one MOUD on the same day. Individuals could contribute multiple person‐trials, defined as eligible SROM or methadone treatment episodes if they met eligibility criteria more than once; however, only the first qualifying episode per calendar year was included to prevent overlap of the 365‐day follow‐up period. For example, if a patient initiated MOUD on 1 September 2021, any new eligible episode occurring before September 2022 would not be included.

Outcomes of interest

Our study examined a total of seven healthcare utilization outcomes across acute and outpatient care: (1) all‐cause and (2) opioid‐specific ED visits; (3) all‐cause and (4) opioid‐specific hospitalization days; (5) all‐cause physician encounters/services; (6) OUD‐related physician visits; and (7) non‐OUD–related primary care physician visits. See File S2 for detailed outcome definitions and Table S4 for ICD codes used to identify OUD‐related outcomes.

Treatment strategy and assignment

We sought to examine the comparative effect of two treatment strategies: SROM vs. methadone‐based MOUD for the treatment of OUD on the identified outcomes. Unlike the original RCT, where individuals were randomised into each treatment strategy, individuals in this trial emulation design using observational data were assigned to the treatment strategy that was compatible with the prescription data as recorded in their EMR at baseline.

Causal contrast

We estimated the observational analogues of the intent‐to‐treat (ITT) and per‐protocol (PP) effects. In the ITT analysis, the causal estimand was the comparative effect of receiving a new prescription of SROM vs. methadone for the treatment of OUD (regardless of treatment initiation or adherence). In the PP analysis, the causal estimand examined the comparative effect of initiating the prescribed SROM vs. methadone, while adhering to the assigned treatment. A 4‐day grace period was applied to allow patients sufficient time to dispense the treatment following the prescription date. The interval was chosen in alignment with the BC pharmacies' policy of cancelling a MOUD prescription if not picked up within 4 days.

Follow‐up

In the ITT analysis, follow‐up for each patient started on the date of treatment prescription (i.e. index date) and ended on the date of death or 1 year of follow‐up, whichever occurred first. In the PP analysis, follow‐up started on the date of treatment initiation (i.e. the date of first treatment dispensation following the index date) and ended on the date of treatment discontinuation (i.e. defined as a 7‐day gap in treatment based on dispensation data), switching to another type of MOUD, starting dual treatment, death or 1 year of follow‐up, whichever occurred first. Starting follow‐up after treatment initiation, allows us to assess the impact of healthcare utilization while patients were actively treated. See File S3 for diagrams illustrating the study index date and start/end of follow‐up.

Covariates

We accounted for a number of covariates based on existing literature [28, 29]. Socio‐demographic characteristics included age at index date, sex (female vs. male) as recorded on EMR prescription data, degree of urbanicity (metro/urban vs. rural/remote; classified as metro/urban if missing; 2% of ITT patients) [30], health authority of residence (VCHA vs. others; classified as VCHA if missing; 2% of ITT patients) and homelessness status (yes vs. no) within 1 year before index date. Comorbidities and measures of disease severity included overdose, injection drug use, modified Elixhauser Comorbidity Index (excluding substance use and psychiatric disorders assessed separately), chronic pain, alcohol use disorder, stimulant use disorder, sedative use disorder, psychotic and bipolar disorder, depressive, anxiety and trauma‐related disorder, hepatitis C virus (see Table S2 for ICD codes used) and low income status defined as being on BC PharmaCare plan C/G (i.e. for individuals with annual income <$42 000) (yes vs. no) within 1 year before index date. History of healthcare utilization included under the care of a regular general provider (yes vs. no), admissions to hospital and ED (counts) and use of virtual care (yes vs. no) within 1 year before index date. Medication use included MOUD use in the past 5 years, dispensations of other medications within the last 90 days, including other opioids, sedative medications, other psychiatric non‐sedative medications (Table S5). We also included prescription characteristics, including co‐dispensation of hydromorphone as ‘safer supply’ [21] (a policy initiative implemented during the pandemic to reduce dual exposure to both the toxic drug supply and coronavirus disease 2019; yes vs. no), the year and quarter of MOUD episode initiation and daily doses of MOUD dispensed. All covariates were first measured at baseline. For the PP analysis, a pre‐specified subset of baseline covariates (listed in Method S1) was remeasured 30 days before treatment deviation and treated as time‐varying.

Identifying assumptions

The biggest assumption in trial emulation is that patients in treatment groups are conditionally exchangeable [31], allowing causal inferences to be drawn from observational data. To mimic the randomization of the clinical trial, statistical methods were used (e.g. inverse probability of treatment weights to account for differences in baseline patient characteristics) with the assumption that all confounding variables have been properly identified and adjusted and that no unmeasured covariates influence both treatment and outcome. Sensitivity instrumental variable (IV) analysis was conducted to account for unmeasured confounding; however, this approach relies on its own assumptions (see Sensitivity analysis below for more details).

Statistical analysis

ITT analysis

We followed patients daily (i.e. equal interval of 1 day) from the index date until the date of death or 1 year of follow‐up. During the entire follow‐up time, patients remained in the assigned treatment group regardless of actual treatment initiation or adherence. Healthcare utilization outcomes were assessed as to whether or not the event occurred during each person‐day follow‐up (i.e. binary).

We applied stabilised inverse probability of treatment weights (IPTW) to account for baseline differences between treatment groups and censoring weights (IPCW) to account for potential informative censoring [32] because of death (see Method S1 for more details). In our outcome model, a weighted modified Poisson regression with generalised estimating equations [33] on pooled person‐day data was used, accounting for within‐person correlation and informative cluster sizes across repeated daily observations. In addition to treatment strategy and baseline covariates, the outcome model also included the calendar year and quarter of index date, a spline term of time, as well as the interaction of time and treatment strategy.

PP analysis

Using medication dispensation data from PharmaNet, patients from ITT cohort who dispensed the prescribed medication within 4 days (i.e. initiators) were identified. Next, we applied stabilised inverse probability of initiation weights (IPIW) [34] to mimic a pseudo‐population of the initial cohort in which everyone initiated the assigned treatment (Method S1). Initiators were followed daily from treatment initiation date to assess healthcare utilization outcomes for a maximum of 1 year until treatment deviation, defined as treatment discontinuation (more than 7‐day gap in treatment), treatment switch or starting dual treatment or death. To account for short gaps between treatment dispensations, we established continuous SROM and methadone treatment episodes for each patient, as shown in File S3.

In addition to (1) IPIW, we computed (2) IPTW similarly as in the ITT analysis to balance baseline covariates and (3) IPCW to address informative censoring because of deviations from the assigned treatment strategy (Method S1). We, then, fitted the same outcome model as the ITT analysis, incorporating the weights.

We estimated the marginal cumulative healthcare utilization over 1 year from each weighted outcome model under each treatment strategy (Method S1) and reported the comparative treatment effects in adjusted rate ratios (aRR) at 1 year, with CIs obtained by resampling patients with replacement 500 times and calculating the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution. All analyses were done using SAS version 9.4 and R version 4.5.1.

The protocol of the original pRESTO RCT (target trial) and the emulated trial have been described before [26]. Some of the specific outcomes and operational definitions reported in this manuscript were not fully pre‐specified in the protocol and should, therefore, be interpreted as exploratory. The study was approved by the University of British Columbia/Providence Health Care research ethics board. This manuscript is prepared following the Transparent Reporting of Observational Studies Emulating a Target Trial (TARGET) guideline.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted several sensitivity analyses. First, to test and account for within‐person correlation arising from multiple episodes per patient, we randomly selected one episode per unique individual and repeated the primary ITT and PP analyses. Second, we repeated analyses with a follow‐up period of 6 months to align with the original RCT duration [35].

We also repeated the primary PP analyses using alternative discontinuation thresholds (i.e. gaps of 14/30 days) based on previous studies [36, 37]. Given a number of drug shortages of the 24‐hour formulation of SROM (i.e. Kadian) in BC occurring during the study period, we also considered switches from this formulation to BID (i.e. twice a day) dosing of the 12‐hour version (i.e. M‐Eslon) as advised specifically during these shortages as being retained on SROM [38]. Kadian shortages were identified from Drug Shortages Canada and BC websites and confirmed with the BC Ministry of Health (Table S6) [39, 40].

In addition, to adjust for potential unobserved factors influencing medication assignment, we conducted an IV analysis in the PP cohort using the prescriber's past 6‐month preference before the current prescription as an IV, consistent with methods of prior studies in our setting [28, 41]. See File S4 for a more detailed description and violations of assumption tests.

Finally, we conducted a ‘repeated episode’ analysis where we sought to examine the ‘as‐treatment’ effect of SROM vs. methadone by relaxing the PP criteria to allow individuals to contribute repeated episodes, that is allowing restarts and switches from methadone to SROM or vice versa during follow‐up time, under a marginal structural modelling framework with treatment treated as a time‐varying exposure (File S5).

RESULTS

Baseline sample characteristics and follow‐up

In total, we identified 3254 eligible patients [median (Q1–Q3) age 37 (30–46) years; 64.3% male] contributing to 4059 person‐trials (32.6% SROM) between 1 July 2017 and 30 June 2023 (Figure 1; Table 1). The distribution of baseline characteristics was relatively balanced across treatment arms, except that the median age for the SROM group was older, and patients were less likely to have recent dispensation of opioid analgesics and more likely to be co‐dispensed hydromorphone as ‘safer supply’. After applying IPTW weighting, baseline covariates were balanced between treatment groups (all standardised mean difference <0.1).

ITT analysis

Overall healthcare utilization was high in both groups (Table S8). Estimated outcome rate per person‐year over 1 year under SROM vs. methadone was 5.36 vs. 4.89 for all‐cause ED visits (opioid‐specific: 0.92 vs. 0.92), 7.12 vs. 7.22 for days in all‐cause hospitalization (opioid‐specific: 5.06 vs. 5.22), 25.34 vs. 25.35 for all‐cause physician encounters/services, 14.35 vs. 15.31 for OUD‐related physician visits and 3.01 vs. 2.72 for primary care physician visits, respectively (Figure 2; Table S9). In the ITT analysis, no statistically significant differences were observed between SROM and methadone across healthcare utilization outcomes, with corresponding aRRs at 1.10 [95% confidence interval (CI) = 0.997–1.20], 1.00 (0.90–1.13), 0.99 (0.81–1.18), 0.97 (0.78–1.20), 1.00 (0.94–1.06), 0.94 (0.87–1.01) and 1.11 (0.98–1.24), respectively (Table 2).

PP analysis

Treatment initiators had overall higher rates of physician encounters/services, but lower acute healthcare utilization compared with the ITT population. Estimated rate per person‐year under SROM vs. methadone was 5.29 vs. 4.26 for all‐cause ED visits (opioid‐specific: 0.79 vs. 0.78), 2.70 vs. 2.55 for days in all‐cause hospitalization (opioid‐specific: 1.81 vs. 1.90), 33.80 vs. 32.88 for all‐cause physician encounters/services, 20.69 vs. 21.33 for OUD‐related physician visits and 3.67 vs. 3.47 for primary care physician visits (Figure 3; Table S9). Our results show that initiating and remaining on SROM vs. methadone was not associated with statistically significant differences in healthcare utilization across all outcomes, with aRRs of 1.24 (0.91–1.50), 1.01 (0.67–1.36), 1.06 (0.65–1.58), 0.95 (0.53–1.58), 1.03 (0.90–1.17), 0.97 (0.82–1.14) and 1.06 (0.73–1.46), respectively (Table 2).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were broadly consistent with the primary findings (Table S10). However, in the repeated‐episode analysis, SROM was associated with a higher rate of all‐cause ED visits [aRR = 1.31 (1.07–1.51)], while other outcomes remained similar. Repeated‐episode analysis results also show that patients with a greater number of repeated episodes during follow‐up had a higher proportion of SROM episodes and higher rates of all‐cause ED visits (Table S11). Importantly, the association between treatment type and ED visit rates varied with episode burden, with a significantly steeper increase in rates observed for SROM as number of episodes increased (P = 0.003).

DISCUSSION

In this target trial emulation using observational EMR data linked with large administrative health data, we examined the comparative effectiveness of SROM vs. methadone on healthcare utilization outcomes in routine practice in the Vancouver Coastal Health region, BC, Canada. Both primary ITT and PP analyses did not show significant differences between SROM and methadone in rates of ED visits, days hospitalised, all‐cause physician encounters/services, OUD‐related physician visits or non–OUD‐related primary care physician visits at 1 year.

In contrast, sensitivity analyses allowing for treatment discontinuation/re‐initiation/switching demonstrated a higher rate of all‐cause ED visits while individuals were receiving SROM. This repeated episode analysis targeted a dynamic as‐treated estimand, which is different from our main ITT and PP estimands. The higher risk observed may reflect increased statistical power because of additional person‐time and events, but also could be driven by differences in treatment trajectories (reflecting selection) rather than a direct pharmacologic effect of SROM, as patients with more frequent treatment cycling were more likely to receive SROM and have higher ED utilization overall.

Comparison with existing literature

In our study, healthcare utilization rates overall were high among OUD patients. A previous BC‐based study [42] using data up to 2017 reported higher utilization within VCHA OUD cohort compared to provincial cohort, with a median (Q1–Q3) of 3.8 (0.9–12.3) days hospitalised and 2.0 (0.8–4.5) ED visits per person‐year of follow‐up. In our cohort of patients receiving methadone/SROM prescriptions from VCHA services, median days hospitalised were shorter [0 (0–4)], whereas ED visits were comparable [3 (1–6)]. The shorter hospitalised days likely reflect differences in the study period and cohort composition, as our cohort represents a more treatment‐engaged subset of the OUD population, less likely to experience prolonged hospitalizations. In addition, our results suggested that hospitalization rates were lower among individuals who initiated and remained engaged in treatment (PP population) compared with the ITT population, highlighting the importance of sustained engagement in opioid agonist therapy (OAT) in reducing acute care utilization.

Existing studies on healthcare utilization have primarily focused on comparisons between patients receiving MOUD and those who did not. Direct comparative evidence on different MOUDs is limited, with many studies emphasizing healthcare costs rather than measuring healthcare utilization directly. A US study [43] using Veteran Health Administration and a United Kingdom study [44] using electronic health records found patients who received methadone had higher healthcare utilization than buprenorphine. One research abstract [45] comparing methadone, buprenorphine and naltrexone reported naltrexone initiators had the highest all‐cause utilization, whereas methadone initiators had higher OUD‐specific outpatient utilization. Studies using US claims databases have found lower healthcare utilization among patients treated with extended‐release naltrexone compared with buprenorphine and methadone [46, 47, 48] as well as oral naltrexone [48].

As an emerging MOUD, SROM's real‐world effectiveness evidence remains sparse. To our knowledge, this is the first large‐scale study to compare SROM vs. methadone, the two full oral opioid agonists, from a health system impact perspective. In our main emulated trial study [27] using the same data sources, SROM showed slightly higher treatment discontinuation and lower adherence, but similar mortality risk compared to methadone. In this present study, we did not observe significant differences in risks of healthcare utilization between SROM and methadone when evaluated at treatment initiation and under continuous treatment exposure, whereas higher ED use was observed only in analyses capturing complex treatment cycling trajectories. Together, these findings suggest that although SROM initiators had slightly lower treatment retention and adherence, this did not appear to translate into higher acute care or meaningful changes in outpatient healthcare utilization compared with methadone. Similar all‐cause and opioid‐specific acute care use provides indirect support for the potential comparable real‐world effectiveness of SROM, whereas comparable rates of outpatient healthcare utilization outcomes suggest no clear reduction in OUD‐related care engagement or increase in outpatient healthcare burden. Hence, it may still be appropriate to select either medication based on patient factors and preferences within a patient‐centred care framework that emphasises treatment engagement in decision‐making, as long as measures are in place to support retention.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths and limitations. First, having access to prescription data directly from patient EMRs enabled us to study the ‘crude’ effect of treatment assignment at the point of clinical encounter when prescription was written. By comparing patients prescribed SROM vs. methadone, this is more analogous to the ITT analysis in the target trial compared with studies with dispensation data only [49]. ITT analyses are particularly relevant in clinical practice because they account for real‐world variability in treatment adherence and delivery of care. Additionally, to our knowledge, this is one of the first population‐level studies comparing health system‐level outcomes between SROM and methadone. The use of population‐based data within a universal healthcare setting ensured comprehensive capture of publicly funded healthcare services. We used a marginal structural approach to estimate the PP effect reflecting the real‐world treatment usage and adherence and can account for the effect of time‐varying covariates.

Our study has key limitations. First, establishing causal inference from observational data relies on key underlying assumptions and we assume that patients in treatment groups are exchangeable and all covariates have been correctly identified and adjusted for. We acknowledge that some key covariates, such as gender, race, concurrent opioid use and incarceration, are not available in our administrative data, but could act as confounders and affect our results. We further used an IV approach to address unmeasured confounders. Although we have empirically tested for violations of some key IV assumptions, not all can be empirically proven [50]. Instead, they can be supported by subject knowledge and/or falsified through extensive testing. In addition, IV estimates reflect a ‘complier average causal effect’ among patients whose treatment choice was influenced by their prescriber's preference and, therefore, may not generalise to patients whose treatment would be unaffected by prescriber preference. Although statistical power from IV results was limited, especially for less common outcomes, the intervals overlapped substantially with those from primary PP analysis and were compatible with the main finding.

The lack of observed differences in healthcare utilization outcomes should be interpreted with caution. We did not adjust for multiple testing as outcomes were pre‐specified to represent distinct dimensions of healthcare use, and no statistically significant differences were observed. Many individuals in the ITT analysis did not initiate treatment, which could bias estimates toward the null. The shorter follow‐up in the PP analysis may have limited statistical power, particularly for hospitalization and OUD‐specific outcomes, where estimates were imprecise. Countervailing mechanisms within treatment groups are also possible, such as increased physician visits because of OUD‐related complications being offset by overall decreased healthcare engagement. However, from a health‐system perspective, these estimates remain informative as they capture the net comparative impact of initiating SROM vs. methadone on each outcome. Given structural inequities in overdose risk and MOUD access, our aggregate results may obscure important disparities among structurally marginalised groups. Future studies with extended follow‐up, larger sample sizes and more complete intersectional demographic data are warranted to improve precision and to examine heterogeneity in treatment access, adherence and outcomes across patient populations.

Our study was conducted in an urban setting with low‐threshold access to office‐based MOUD prescribing and community pharmacy dispensing under a universal healthcare system, where the cost of SROM and methadone is fully covered. Many patients in our study cohort also received prescribed hydromorphone through safer supply programs, reflecting BC's response to the fentanyl‐dominated unregulated drug supply. These contextual factors limit the generalizability to rural settings or jurisdictions with different healthcare systems or treatment delivery models. Nevertheless, the real‐world evidence on SROM in the fentanyl era remains useful for settings considering expanding evidence‐based treatment options.

CONCLUSION

This target trial emulation using observational EMR data linked with administrative health data did not observe significant differences in risk of ED visits, hospitalizations and physician encounters/services between SROM and methadone. Comparable real‐world effectiveness of SROM to methadone on acute, high‐cost healthcare use supports SROM as a viable alternative, expanding treatment options without increasing health system burden. Despite overall high healthcare utilization rates among OUD patients receiving care at Vancouver Coastal Health clinical sites, individuals who initiated and remained engaged in treatment had lower acute healthcare utilization compared with the ITT population, highlighting the need for strategies that support sustained engagement in MOUD.
