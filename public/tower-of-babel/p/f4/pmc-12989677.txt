Defining Korean Epidemiologic Cutoffs for Helicobacter pylori: from Minimum Inhibitory Concentration Distributions to Clinical Implications
Authors: Bokyung Kim
Published: 2026

Helicobacter pylori is a major cause of various gastrointestinal diseases and a well-known carcinogen for gastric cancer.1 Recently, the increase in antibiotic resistance poses a significant limitation to successful H. pylori eradication therapy. Despite the clinical significance of H. pylori and its escalating resistance rates, Korea has yet to establish validated interpretive thresholds capable of translating minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) distributions into actionable resistance data.2-4

In a timely response to this gap, Noh et al.4 report a comprehensive, model-based analysis of Korean MIC distributions, proposing Korea-specific epidemiological cutoff values (ECOFFs) for six commonly used antibiotics. ECOFFs, introduced by the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST), indicate MIC thresholds that distinguish wild-type populations from those with acquired resistance, facilitating standardized resistance reporting and supporting the establishment of clinical breakpoints.5

By analyzing a large-scale dataset of 5,925 primary H. pylori isolates collected nationwide between 2015 and 2023, the authors employed standardized serial 2-fold agar dilution and automated ECOFFinder modeling to determine MIC distributions and cutoff values. The proposed ECOFFs were 0.125 μg/mL (amoxicillin; tentative), 0.125 μg/mL (clarithromycin), 0.5 μg/mL (levofloxacin; tentative), 8.0 μg/mL (metronidazole), and 0.25 μg/mL (tetracycline), with corresponding resistance rates of 17.9%, 31.9%, 40.9%, 24.7%, and 11.5%, respectively; rifabutin did not yield a stable cutoff in the dataset.4

For amoxicillin, the authors adopted a provisional ECOFF of 0.125 μg/mL despite a lower ECOFFinder estimate, aligning with prior reports while acknowledging limited evidence for high-level resistance. Regarding clarithromycin, although EUCAST included more very-high MIC isolates (≥128 μg/mL), the expected impact on Korean resistance estimates is likely small because Korean isolates were uncommon in the intermediate MIC range (0.5–2.0 μg/mL). For metronidazole, several earlier Korean studies classified MIC 8 μg/mL as resistant, whereas the present analysis suggests that MIC 8 may still fall within the wild-type range, resulting in a lower resistance estimate than a representative 2019 Korean report (24.7% vs 29.5%). For rifabutin, a robust microbiological cutoff could not be derived from the current dataset because the MIC distribution was too narrowly clustered. As rifabutin becomes increasingly important in the setting of rising resistance, future studies may benefit from enriched datasets with broader sampling such as isolates from pretreated or eradication-failure patients in whom non–wild-type subpopulations may be better represented.

Several points deserve emphasis in this study. First, it is crucial to distinguish ECOFFs from clinical breakpoints. While ECOFFs serve as a microbiological tool to differentiate wild-type from non-wild-type populations, they do not inherently define clinical resistance. Clinical breakpoints incorporate pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic properties and treatment context, whereas ECOFFs primarily standardize microbiological categorization.6,7 Methodologically, the strengths of this research lie in the scale and rigor of the dataset. The authors included a large MIC dataset by a high-volume tertiary center and restricted inclusion to isolates tested under standardized conditions. The application of the automated ECOFFinder algorithm enables analysis of MIC distributions in a standardized manner. This is valuable since ECOFF estimation is highly sensitive to sample size, MIC dilution ranges, and the presence of mixed/overlapping subpopulations.

This study carries significant implications for practice and public health policy in Korea. The finding that clarithromycin resistance is around one-third and levofloxacin resistance exceeds 40% under the proposed ECOFFs argues against empiric clarithromycin- or fluoroquinolone-based regimens in most clinical settings unless susceptibility is known.4,8-10 A standardized ECOFF framework may also enable valid comparisons across centers and over time, strengthening surveillance and allowing reliable assessment of antimicrobial stewardship efforts. However, ECOFFs should be viewed as a starting point rather than endpoint; the key next step is clinical validation to determine how well these microbiological cutoffs predicts eradication failure under current regimens.

The issue of representativeness remains a consideration when interpreting this study. Since a single tertiary center contributed most of the data (86.1%), the observed MIC distributions might reflect localized referral patterns, specific prior antibiotic exposure histories, or laboratory-specific variables rather than true nationwide ecology. These issues support the necessity for periodic, geographically balanced updates to keep national cutoffs aligned with evolving resistance patterns. Furthermore, the reliance on MIC data alone introduces analytical constraints. Without clinical outcomes and resistance genotypes, defining ECOFFs becomes particularly challenging where distribution curves overlap—most notably with amoxicillin and metronidazole. While the authors’ decision to adopt a provisional amoxicillin ECOFF based on expert consensus (despite a lower estimate from ECOFFinder) is a pragmatic response to imperfect data separation, it highlights a need for transparent rationale and outcome-linked validation. Finally, comparisons with EUCAST datasets warrant a cautious interpretation, as inherent discrepancies in study populations and susceptibility testing methodologies can influence MIC distributions and resistance rates.

Despite these limitations, the study by Noh et al.4 provides a timely, Korea-specific ECOFF proposal based on a large MIC dataset and quantify resistance under a standardized method. When paired with outcome-based validation and updating, this approach can improve comparability of Korean resistance surveillance, support rational empiric regimen selection, and strengthen the evidence base for future guideline revisions.
