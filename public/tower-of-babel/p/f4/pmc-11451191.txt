The effectiveness of Balint groups at improving empathy in medical and nursing education: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Bin Gong, Xiaochen Zhang, Chen Lu, Chengcheng Wu, Jin Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveEmpathy is crucial for patient health. The Balint group is a commonly used method for empathy training. However, the impact of Balint groups on empathy remains unclear. This systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) aims to assess the impact of Balint groups on empathy training among medical and nursing students, as well as doctors and nurses.MethodsThis review involved searching multiple databases for relevant articles. Rigorous eligibility criteria were applied during the screening of titles and abstracts, and during the selection of records. Following a full-text eligibility evaluation, two reviewers independently extracted data from the final selection of studies, and a meta-analysis was conducted. The standardized mean difference (SMD) was calculated to assess the systematic outcomes.ResultsA total of 11 studies were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. Participants in Balint groups demonstrated a significant increase in empathy than those in the control group (SMD = 1.46, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.86–2.06; p < 0.001). Studies conducted in China (SMD = 2.13, 95% CI 1.27–2.99; p < 0.001) revealed a greater impact of Balint groups on empathy than those conducted in France (SMD = 0.24, 95% CI 0.12–0.37; p < 0.001). The impact of Balint groups was significantly greater among physicians (SMD = 2.50, 95% CI 1.79–3.21; p < 0.001) and nurses (SMD = 2.88, 95% CI 1.34–4.43; p < 0.001) compared to medical students (SMD = 0.71, 95% CI = 0.35–1.06; p < 0.001). Participants who attended ten or more sessions (SMD = 2.37, 95% CI 1.35–3.39; p < 0.001) demonstrated better outcomes compared to those who attended fewer than ten sessions (SMD = 0.79, 95% CI 0.30–1.29; p < 0.01).ConclusionBalint groups are effective for empathy training among doctors, nurses, and medical students. Future research should incorporate patient-led measurements to evaluate empathy and ascertain the long-term impact of Balint groups on empathy training.Trial registrationPROSPERO registration number CRD42023488247.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06098-3.

Introduction

Empathy has many definitions, with one widely accepted definition in the medical context being the ability of a healthcare provider to understand a patient’s feelings, concerns, and situations and to demonstrate this understanding through appropriate behavior [1, 2]. It incorporates perspective-taking, emotion management, and emotional resonance [3]. For a more nuanced understanding, empathy is a multifaceted concept that encompasses both an affective component and a cognitive component, as well as a motivation to help others [4].

Empathy is a fundamental component of the healthcare professional-patient relationship, encompassing both doctors and nurses. There is substantial evidence supporting its positive effects on patient satisfaction and health outcomes [5–12]. In both medical and nursing literature, empathy is increasingly recognized as an essential condition for effective patient care [11]. Studies have underscored the critical link between empathy and patients’ adherence to treatment and medication plans [8, 13, 14]. Futhermore, empathy is associated with lower rates of physician burnout, improved well-being [15, 16], less malpractice [17], and higher ratings in clinical skill evaluations [18]. Empathy is a crucial skill in healthcare, therefore, it is essential to emphasize its cultivation [19]. However, a disheartening trend observed in recent studies indicates a decline in empathy levels during medical and nursing education [20–23]. This decline has been linked to the clinical learning environment and patient interactions, which may hinder effective patient interactions and outcomes [24, 25]. Despite this concerning trend, researchers concur that empathy can be cultivated through various methods [26–28], including communication skills training [29], narrative medicine [30], simulation-based education [31], reflective writing [32], and Balint groups [33].

Initially, a Balint group is defined as a small group of clinicians who regularly analyze cases from their medical practices to gain a deeper understanding of the doctor-patient relationship. In recent years, the adoption of Balint groups has expanded within the nursing community [34, 35]. It allows members to share their inner experiences, thoughts, and self-reflections by presenting specific patient cases in a psychoanalytic context [36]. Balint groups have been implemented at various stages of medical education, including undergraduate medical education, residency training and continuing medical education. A study conducted in Ireland showed that the introduction of Balint groups to fourth-year undergraduate medical students resulted in a significant increase in empathy scores [37]. Likewise, a RCT suggested that Balint groups may initially help medical students develop clinical empathy [33]. However, there was disagreement regarding the efficacy of Balint groups in enhancing empathy. For instance, a study found that Balint groups failed to increase the occupants’ empathetic abilities [38], while another study revealed that empathy scores remained unchanged following a 12-month Balint intervention [39]. Previous research has not comprehensively examined the potential of Balint groups to enhance empathy among medical and nursing students, as well as among doctors and nurses. Although these studies have illuminated the various roles of Balint groups [40–42], they are lacking meta-analysis, which could potentially undermine their conclusions.

Due to the uncertain effect size of Balint groups training, educators lack substantial evidence to support the integration of Balint group into the curriculum. Furthermore, the implementation of Balint groups necessitates a considerable investment of time and resources, prompting educators to carefully evaluate the costs in relation to the potential benefits. Consequently, it is imperative to ascertain the effectiveness of such training. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to assess the effectiveness of Balint groups in empathy training by synthesizing existing RCTs in the education of medical and nursing students, doctors and nurses. This study seeks to address the current knowledge gap in this domain.

Methods

Protocol and guidance

The research was conducted following the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA).

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive search across multiple databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Cochrane Library, PsychInfo, Embase, CINAHL, and ClinicalTrials.gov. Addtionally, we searched another four databases: The Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data, and vip.database. The search strategy employed the MeSH thesaurus alongside free terms to enhance comprehensiveness. The term “empathy” was used for outcome and “Balint group” for intervention. Free terms encompassed concepts such as compassion, caring, Balint psychoanalytic therapy. The complete search strategy is detailed in Supplementary Appendix 1.

Study selection

The studies included in this systematic review and meta-analysis should meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) Medical and nursing students, as well as doctors and nurses. (2) The intervention for the experimental group was Balint groups. (3) The studies examined quantitative data on empathy levels as either a primary or secondary outcome, using self-reported, observer-reported, and/or patient-reported measures. (4) RCTs. (5) Scientific publications written in English or Chinese. The following were excluded from the analysis: (1) observational studies. (2) studies that lacked control groups. (3) reviews, editorials, and conference abstracts.

The search results from various databases were aggregated using EndNote X9 with duplicates removed. Two reviewers (BG and XZ) performed a hierarchical screening of publications by analyzing article titles and abstracts to assess their relevance, followed by a thorough examination of the full texts. This multi-step selection process aimed to identify RCTs involved Balint groups as an intervention.

Date collection

Two researchers (BG and XZ) utilized a data extraction template to independently extract the data with a third team member (JY) resolving disputes until a consensus was achieved. Detailed study elements were collected from each individual study. For instance, details such as publication country, publication year, participant type,, sample size, control intervention,, intervention sessions, intervention duration, outcome measures, and main outcome were extracted. The study outcomes were recorded directly, including the mean, standard deviation, and post-test scores. Otherwise the mean and standard deviation were determined from relevant published statistical data, including the median, p values, CIs and the differences between the mean and standard deviation before and after the intervention [43, 44].

Quality assessment

The quality of each study was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool [45]. Additionally, the quality of the evidence was quantified using the Grades of Recommendation, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) instrument with the GRADEprofiler (GRADEpro) program. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion within the research group.

Data analysis

The characteristics of the included studies were summarized through descriptive analysis. The primary outcome of the study was the empathy scores reported by participants, standardized patients (Individuals trained to portray medical conditions for educational assessments and clinical training), observers, or patients. Due to variations in the assessments of empathy across the included studies, SMDs and their 95% confidence intervals were calculated to estimate the effect size based on Cohen’s guidelines: 0 to 0.2 (trivial), 0.2 to 0.5 (small), 0.5 to 0.8 (moderate), and greater than 0.80 (large) [46]. The I2 statistic was employed to assess statistical heterogeneity. An I2 value exceeding 50% indicates substantial inconsistency in the evidence. A random-effects model was recommended for the meta-analysis to calculate the pooled weighted effect sizes [47]. Conversely, a fixed-effects model was utilized. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, including publication country, participant type, and the number of Balint group sessions. Sensitivity analyses were conduct to evaluate the impact of individual studies by omitting each study from the overall analysis. Publication bias was assessed using a funnel plot and fail-safe number. The significance level was set at a p value less than 0.05. The meta-analysis was conducted using Review Manager (RevMan 5.3) [48].

Results

Study selection

Initially, 1,962 articles were retrieved from the electronic database search. An additional potentially relevant paper was identified through reference searching. After removing duplicates, 1,405 articles were included for further analysis. However, upon screening the titles and abstracts, 1,327 records were deemed irrelevant. A total of 78 full-text papers were evaluated for eligibility, resulting in 11 studies that met the inclusion criteria. The search process and reasons for exclusion are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Study characteristics

A comprehensive summary of the key components of the included papers is presented in eTable. 1 of Supplementary Appendix 2. The study included 11 studies, with a total of 1355 participants. Seven studies involved medical students, three involved nurses, one involved physicians, and none involved nursing students. Seven studies were conducted in China, while the remaining three took place in France. Each study employed a RCT design with Balint groups as the intervention. The durations ranged from 8 weeks to one year, with frequencies varing from once per week to once per season. Seven studies did not implement any intervention in the control group, while three included reading or lectures. Among the reviewed studies, six involved voluntary participation, two were mandatory, and one employed a hybrid model with partial mandatory participation. The participation status for the remaining studies is unspecified. Five studies did not differ from usual Balint group sessions. The other studies incorporated the Balint group experience with role-playing, preliminary training, or additional educational activities. Notably, three of the studies specifically targeted participants with lower baseline empathy levels, potentially to enhance the intervention’s impact. The remaining studies recruited participants from the general population. The outcomes of each RCT were assessed following the intervention. The Jefferson Scale of Empathy (JSE) was used in 9 studies to measure empathy. One study used the College Students’ Empathy Ability questionnaire, while the final sudy combined the JSE with the Consultation and Relational Empathy Measure (CARE) scale, which was completed by standardized patients (see eTable 1 of Supplementary Appendix 2).

Risk of bias

The risk of bias assessment for the 10 studies is summarized in Fig. 2 and Figure. S1 (see Supplementary Appendix 2). All 11 studies described their randomization methods, but most lacked specific details regarding allocation concealment. Additionally, due to the nature of Balint groups, it wasn’t feasible to blind participants during the research. Only one study employed CARE reported by standardized patients to measure empathy. The remaining studies relied on self-reported measures of empathy. All studies showed low risks for incomplete data and selective reporting, indicating minimal attrition bias and reporting bias.

Meta-analysis

A meta-analysis was conducted on 11 studies. In the study that assessed empathy using both the JSE and CARE scale, data from both instruments were included in the meta-analysis. As depicted in Fig. 3, the pooled effect size was calculated using a random-effects meta-analysis of SMDs, indicating a significantly positive impact on enhancing empathy following Balint groups compared to the control group (SMD = 1.46, 95% CI 0.86–2.06; p < 0.001). Heterogeneity in empathy was observed (I2 = 96%), as indicated by a substantial Q statistic (p < 0.001). A sensitivity analysis was conducted to confirm the reliability of the findings, and the primary outcome remained largely unchanged even after excluding specific studies (refer to Figure. S2, Supplementary Appendix 2). Collectively, these results demonstrated the robustness of the findings. Based on the GRADE criteria (see eTable 2, Supplementary Appendix 2), the quality of evidence for the impact of Balint groups on enhancing empathy compared to the control group was considered low.

Subgroup analysis

Planned subgroup analyses were conducted. The categorical variables encompassed region (France or China), participant (medical students, doctors, or nurses), and the number of sessions (< 10 sessions or ≥ 10 sessions). As depicted in Figure S3a (Supplementary Appendix 2), studies conducted in China yielded a large effect (SMD = 2.13, 95% CI 1.27–2.99; p < 0.001) with 92% heterogeneity, while studies in France indicated a small effect (SMD = 0.24, 95% CI 0.12–0.37; p < 0.001) with 13% heterogeneity. The test for subgroup variances revealed a significant difference between the results from China and France.

In relation to the participants, as demonstrated in Figure S3b (Supplementary Appendix 2), studies involving nurses exhibited the highest effect size (SMD = 2.88, 95% CI 1.34–4.43; p < 0.001), followed by doctors, who showed a substantially large effect (SMD = 2.50, 95% CI 1.79–3.21; p < 0.001) and medical students, who demonstrated a moderate effect (SMD = 0.71, 95% CI 0.35–1.06; p < 0.001).

With respect to the number of sessions, as illustrated in Figure S3c (Supplementary Appendix 2), studies involving fewer than 10 sessions of Balint group interventions demonstrated a significantly smaller effect size (SMD = 0.79, 95% CI 0.30–1.29; p < 0.01) compared to those with more than 10 sessions (SMD = 2.37, 95% CI 1.35–3.39; p < 0.001).

Publication bias

The study analyzed all available research to evaluate potential publication bias. A funnel plot is presented in Fig. 4, showing potential bias in the publications. Furthermore, the classic fail-safe number (fail-safe N) was computed, which ideally should exceed 5k + 10, according to Rosenthal [49], where k represents the total number of reported effect sizes. In this study, the fail-safe N was 1190 (α = 0.01), while 5k + 10 was calculated to be 17.3, indicating that the fail-safe N exceeded the required threshold. The findings indicated that an additional 1190 missing studies with a mean effect size of zero would be necessary to render the overall effect statistically insignificant. It seems highly unlikely that all 1190 studies would have gone undetected. Based on the funnel plot and fail-safe N, we conclude that publication bias has not distorted the overall mean effect size.

Dicussion

To our knowledge, this is the first report that consolidates evidence regarding the effectiveness of Balint groups in enhancing empathy among medical students, doctors, and nurses. Given the current context, it is imperative for educators and students to evaluate the effectiveness of Balint groups. The literature reviewed in this analysis comprised exclusively RCTs, thereby proving a higher level of evidence.

This meta-analysis examined the benefits of Balint groups in enhancing empathy by reviewing RCTs. In contrast to the control groups, Balint groups exhibited a significant improvement (SMD = 1.46) in empathy scores, which aligns with previous research suggesting that targeted training can enhance empathy skills in medical students and physicians [50, 51]. This review contributes to the growing body of evidence regarding the effectiveness of Balint groups among healthcare providers,, including two systematic reviews on the Balint groups’ application in undergraduate and medical education [35, 36]. Meanwhile, another study showed improved empathy in medical students who attended 8 sessions of Balint groups [52]. However, direct comparisons could not be performed due to the lack of a meta-analysis.

Empathy development is influenced by an individual’s intrinsic ability to respond to another person’s mental state, emotional condition, and perspective [53]. It is innate and nurtured through positive interactions and role-modeling, such as constructive patient interactions, continuity, and encouragement from others. Socialization plays a crucial role in either fostering or diminishing empathy [54]. Balint groups offer opportunities to explore diverse perspectives on emotions and the interactions between patients and healthcare providers, such as doctors or nurses [55]. This exploration leads to a greater understanding of both their own emotions and those of their patients. Additionally, Balint groups focus on enhancing self-awareness and understanding of transference and countertransference within a supportive environment [56]. Transference occurs when a patient unintentionally attributes their emotions to a doctor or nurse, while countertransference refers to the reactions and feelings of the doctor or nurse towards the patient [57]. By effectively managing these dynamics, group members can customize their approaches to meet the specific needs of their patients [58]. This process can be perceived as a part of the empathetic engagement, facilitating the cultivation of empathy.

Studies conducted in China showed a larger effect size than those in France. Previous research has indicated significant variations in empathy across nations [59, 60]. This discrepancy may be attributed to variations in multiple aspects of each individual study. Firstly, when examining the dynamics of Balint groups, a voluntary participation model, as commonly observed in Chinese studies, tends to show higher acceptance rates and educational value than its mandatory counterpart [41]. While mandatory participation may ensure a more consistent level of commitment, a voluntary approach is more likely to engage individuals with a genuine interest or specific need for the training. This self-selection bias could result in a more motivated participant pool, which may, in turn, account for the observed increase in effectiveness. Secondly, the incorporation of role-playing and preliminary training in Chinese studies may provide participants with unique opportunities for introspection and learning that are not typically found in traditional Balint group settings. These methodological differences could explain the heterogeneity in effect sizes observed across various studies. Futhermore, it has been noted that in some Chinese studies, the selection of participants has been skewed towards individuals with lower levels of empathy. Consequently, this approach could naturally lead to a more pronounced effect size post-intervention, given that their initial lower empathy scores suggest a greater potential for improvement Finally, within the context of China’s healthcare system, where doctor-patient and nurse-patient relationships are often fraught with trust and communication issues, there is an urgent need for training programs designed to promote more effective interactions. Balint groups provide a confidential environment for dialogue, enabling healthcare providers to navigate the stress that arises from strained relationships with patients and to explore the emotional aspects of their professional roles. Through this process, Balint groups are instrumental in enhancing empathy and communication skills.

Previous research has demonstrated that excessively short or long durations did not yield significant benefits [61, 62]. For instance, a study demonstrated that doctors benefited from training sessions lasting one to three days [63], while another study suggested that moderate to long-duration interventions were the most effective [64]. However, a meta-regression analysis revealed no correlation between the number of training hours and the effect size [65]. These findings suggest that intervention strategies and participant characteristics may influence the optimal duration of interventions. Our results indicate that Balint groups may produce better outcomes with more than 10 sessions, but further investigation is needed to determine the optimal number of sessions, as an excessive number of sessions could lead to high attrition rates [62].

A meta-analysis by Emily et al. found that the effectiveness of empathy training is significantly influenced by the type of trainee and their educational status [65]. Medical students tend to allocate less time to patient bedside care compared to doctors and nurses. This increased patient interaction may render doctors and nurses more susceptible to challenges in the doctor-patient or nurse-patient relationship. Balint groups offer a valuable approach to enhance doctor-patient communication and foster new insights, potentially providing greater benefits to physicians and nurses. Additionally, medical students have been reported to encounter more obstacles to compassion than doctors and nurses, who typically experience fewer barriers [66]. This suggests that empathy training may be particularly advantageous for medical professionals who face fewer barriers to compassion, as they may be more receptive to the training and better able to integrate empathetic practices into their patient interactions.

Limitations

This study has a few limitations. Firstly, the majority of the included studies relied on self-reported measures of empathy, which provide limited insights into their correlation with clinical behaviors. The focus of these studies was primarily on the second level of the Kirkpatrick model [67], which evaluates changes in knowledge or skills, without extending to higher levels such as learning transfer and patient outcomes. Furthermore, the scarcity of follow-up studies on empathy complicates the tracking of the long-term development of empathetic skills post-intervention. It is also important to note that this review may not have captured all relevant literature, particularly studies published in other languages, such as French. Additionally,, the meta-analysis revealed significant heterogeneity, which should be considered when interpreting the results. Moreover, medical education trials, which may not be subject to stricter regulation, could have contributed to the low GRADE score and resulted in an overestimation of the effect due to the tendency to publish favorable results.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings provide evidence for the effectiveness of Balint groups at improving empathy among medical students, doctors, and nurses, despite the considerable heterogeneity observed among the included studies. Furthermore, our results strongly support for the integration of Balint groups training into medical and nursing education. It is advisable for future studies to rely less on participants’ self-reported empathy scores for evaluation and incorporate additional measures, such as a method using patients’ perceptions, in order to derive more significant findings from research.
