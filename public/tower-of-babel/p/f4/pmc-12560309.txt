Should I play or should I watch: the effects of active and passive distraction methods on children’s pain, fear and anxiety during invasive procedures: a randomized controlled clinical trial
Authors: Ayşe Gökoğlu, Özge Sukut
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMinimizing pain, fear, and anxiety in children during invasive procedures is crucial for effective communication with both children and their parents, and for facilitating cooperation during medical interventions. Identifying and implementing effective distraction techniques can improve the quality of pediatric care.MethodsThis randomized controlled clinical trial evaluated the effectiveness of two distraction methods—watching cartoons (passive distraction) and playing video games (active distraction)—on pain, fear, and anxiety levels in children aged 3 to 7 years undergoing invasive procedures. A total of 105 hospitalized children were randomly assigned to one of three groups: control (n = 35), cartoon (n = 35), or video game (n = 35). After obtaining ethical approval, data were collected between 26 November 2019 and 8 January 2020 using a demographic information form, the Oucher Pain Scale, the Children’s State Anxiety Scale, and the Children’s Fear Scale. Data were analyzed using SPSS software, employing chi-squared tests, one-way ANOVA, and repeated measures ANOVA. Additional ANCOVA analyses were conducted to control for baseline differences in pulse and respiratory rate, and effect sizes (partial η²) were calculated for all primary outcomes.ResultsChildren in the control group reported significantly higher levels of pain, fear, and anxiety before, during, and after the procedure compared to those in the cartoon and video game groups (p < 0.001). Both distraction methods were effective, but the video game group consistently showed the lowest scores for pain, fear, and anxiety across all time points (p < 0.01).ConclusionsBoth cartoon watching and video gaming are effective, low-cost, and practical methods to reduce procedural pain, fear, and anxiety in young children. Video games proved to be more effective.Trial registrationThe trial registered on ClinicalTrials.gov with numberNCT06826352 on 26/11/2019.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06188-1.

Introduction

Invasive medical procedures are a routine yet often distressing component of pediatric care. Among these, procedures such as venipuncture and intravenous cannulation are known to trigger significant emotional and physiological distress in young children [1]. The pain and fear associated with these procedures are frequently compounded by a child’s limited ability to understand the situation, the unfamiliarity of the hospital environment, and anticipatory anxiety. Research indicates that children who harbor medical fears are more likely to exhibit distress behaviors such as crying, screaming, or verbal resistance during invasive procedures. These reactions can complicate the intervention and negatively affect both short- and long-term psychological well-being. Prior research has shown that such fears are associated with heightened distress behaviors during procedures [1, 2] and that fear levels can be even higher than pain levels during needle-related interventions, with a positive correlation between fear and pain intensity [3]. Moreover, healthcare-induced anxiety and distress in pediatric patients can have lasting negative effects on emotional development and coping with future medical procedures [4].

In hospital settings, children face multiple stressors beyond the procedures themselves. These include disruptions in daily routines, separation from parents, unfamiliar faces and environments, restricted physical activity, and difficulties in communication [2, 5, 6]. Additionally, seeing other children cry or observing medical equipment may further heighten their anxiety. One of the most challenging aspects of pediatric venous access is the technical difficulty posed by children’s smaller vessel diameters, vascular hyperreactivity, and behavioral resistance. These factors increase the complexity of establishing intravenous access compared to adults [7, 8].

Effectively managing procedural pain, fear, and anxiety is essential not only for improving the child’s experience but also for facilitating cooperation during the procedure and building trust between healthcare professionals, children, and their families. While pharmacological interventions have long been used to manage pain and anxiety, non-pharmacological approaches, especially distraction techniques, are increasingly favored due to their safety, ease of use, and cost-effectiveness [9, 10].

Distraction methods can be broadly categorized into active and passive techniques. Active distraction requires the child’s engagement and interaction with the distraction source and includes methods such as interactive video games, virtual reality, therapeutic play, and augmented reality [11–13]. Passive distraction, on the other hand, does not require active participation and typically includes watching cartoons, listening to music, holographic display or viewing calming visuals [14–19].

Both distraction strategies have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing children’s procedural pain and anxiety. However, active distraction is thought to engage more cognitive resources, thereby offering a higher level of sensory and emotional immersion, which may lead to more significant reductions in distress [12]. Nevertheless, the feasibility and suitability of distraction methods may vary depending on the clinical setting, the nature of the procedure, and the child’s developmental stage and preferences. Previous research comparing passive and active distraction in pediatric procedural pain management has produced mixed results. Dahlquist et al. (2007) found that interactive distraction methods, such as handheld video games, resulted in greater reductions in distress than passive viewing of videos in children undergoing painful procedures [20]. Similarly, Koller and Goldman (2012), in their review of psychosocial interventions, concluded that active engagement often yields better outcomes for distress reduction [21]. However, MacLaren and Cohen (2005) reported that children in a passive distraction condition were more distracted and less distressed than those in an interactive condition during dental treatment [22]. Bellieni et al. (2006) also observed that audiovisual distraction could be as effective as, or even superior to, active methods in certain contexts [23]. These divergent findings may reflect differences in the type and duration of the medical procedure, the age group studied, and the nature of the distraction tool. Given these inconsistencies, further research is warranted—particularly in younger children undergoing short invasive procedures—to clarify which approach is more effective. Despite the growing body of literature supporting both active and passive distraction strategies, few studies have directly compared their effectiveness in a standardized manner during routine invasive procedures with age-specific pediatric populations. The present study addresses this gap by evaluating and comparing the effects of watching cartoons (passive distraction) and playing video games (active distraction) on pain, fear, and anxiety in children aged 3–7 years, using validated and age-appropriate measures. Based on prior research, we anticipated that both distraction methods would significantly reduce pain, fear, and anxiety compared to routine care, with active distraction through video games producing a more pronounced and sustained effect than passive distraction with cartoons.

Methods

This randomized controlled experimental study was conducted to determine the effects of two distraction methods—watching cartoons (passive distraction) and playing video games (active distraction)—on the levels of pain, fear, and anxiety in children aged 3 to 7 years undergoing invasive procedures. The study was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of the T.C. İstanbul Health Science University, Umraniye Training and Research Hospital (Date: 20/11/2019, No: B.10.1.TKH.4.34.H.GP.0.01/212).

Participants

A power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1.0 to determine the appropriate sample size, assuming a medium effect size of 0.39, a statistical power (1–β) of 0.80, and a significance level (α) of 0.05. The required sample size was calculated to be 35 children per group. A total of 105 children who were hospitalized in the general pediatric unit between November 26, 2019 and January 8, 2020, met the inclusion criteria, and consented to participate along with their parents, were recruited into the study.

Data collection tools

Information form

A structured information form developed by the researchers was used to collect demographic and clinical information on the children, such as age, sex, and previous hospital experiences.

Oucher pain scale

The Oucher Pain Scale is a validated visual tool for assessing pain intensity in children. It includes six photographic facial expressions ranging from “no pain” to “worst possible pain” [24]. During the study design phase, we carefully considered both the FPS-R and the Oucher. Since our sample included children as young as 3 years old, we concluded that the Oucher Pain Scale would be more appropriate for the lower end of our age range. The cognitive developmental stage of 3-year-old children can limit their ability to interpret abstract or stylized facial representations, and the Oucher’s photographic format may be easier for them to understand. Previous literature indicates that the Oucher has been validated for use with children aged 3–12 years, and its realistic images can enhance comprehension among the youngest participants [25]. While the FPS-R remains an internationally well-supported alternative, particularly for children aged 4 years and above [26] the Oucher was selected to allow children to quickly view and compare the facial expressions on their own.

Children’s state anxiety scale (CSAS)

The CSAS-S is a visual analog scale designed for children aged 4–10 years. It resembles a thermometer with ten horizontal lines, where children mark the point corresponding to their current (“state”) anxiety level [27, 28]. Before administration, the child’s ability to count and understand numerical order (e.g., recognizing that five is greater than two) was assessed. If the child could not demonstrate this ability, self-report was not attempted and observational scoring was conducted instead.

For self-report, children were instructed to imagine that all of their anxious or nervous feelings were located at the “bulb” at the bottom of the thermometer and could rise toward the top as anxiety increased. They then marked a line on the scale to indicate their current level of anxiety. After data collection, the child’s mark was scored by placing a transparent ruler marked in 0.5-point increments over the scale and rounding to the nearest whole or half number, yielding a score from 0 to 10.

Children’s fear scale (CFS)

The CFS evaluates a child’s level of fear using five facial expressions that depict varying degrees of fear, ranging from 0 (no fear) to 4 (severe fear). While designed for children aged 5–10 years, it has also been successfully used with 3- and 4-year-olds who have adequate cognitive and language development [29, 30] such as understand basic verbal instructions; communicate their choice clearly; and demonstrate basic counting and ordering skills. Children capable of self-reporting were asked to point to the face that best matched their fear level.

Procedure

Children and parents who met the inclusion criteria were informed about the study and asked to provide written consent. All procedures followed ethical standards set by the Declaration of Helsinki. After obtaining ethical approval, both parents and children were informed about the study’s aims, procedures, and ethical considerations. Participants who consented to take part in the research were included in the study. Written and verbal informed consent was secured from both parents and children, ensuring the confidentiality of all data. For written consent, parental consent forms were collected from families, while child consent form were gathered from the children. Data were stored securely, anonymized, accessible and, analyzed without knowing the groups only to authorized researchers.

Eligible children were randomly assigned to one of three groups using sealed, opaque envelopes prepared by an independent researcher who was not involved in data collection. The envelopes were sequentially numbered and handed to the parents, who were asked to select one. After group allocation, children in the cartoon and video game groups were allowed to choose from a list of age-appropriate options. Each group included 35 children: one control group (routine nursing practices), one passive distraction group (watching cartoons), and one active distraction group (playing video games). In both experimental groups, the distraction device (tablet or smartphone) was provided and operated by the attending pediatric nurse immediately before the procedure. The same nurse was responsible for positioning the child, initiating the distraction activity, and monitoring safety throughout the procedure. A second pediatric nurse, trained in data collection and blinded to group allocation, recorded the outcome measures before, during, and after the procedure according to the standardized protocol. Whenever the child was able to understand the instructions and respond, they provided their own ratings directly on the relevant scale. In all cases, self-reports were completed directly by the child. The same pediatric nurse for all three groups observed the child’s behavioral and verbal cues while also asking the child about their perceived pain, fear, or anxiety level. The nurse then indicated the score on the scale and confirmed it with the child. This procedure ensured that the observational rating reflected both professional judgment and, when feasible, the child’s own input. Parent or other caregiver ratings were not collected in order to maintain methodological consistency. All assessments—whether self-reported or observational—were performed following the same standardized protocol.

The invasive procedures performed in this study included venipuncture and intravenous cannulation carried out for diagnostic or treatment purposes. Each procedure typically lasted between 2 and 5 min from tourniquet application to needle withdrawal, depending on the child’s cooperation and the ease of vascular access.

In our clinical setting, all children—regardless of group allocation—were accompanied by a parent or primary caregiver during the procedure. These standard care measures were applied to every participant before the study-specific interventions were initiated.

Prior to the procedures, all children had their oxygen saturation and pulse rates measured via pulse oximetry, and blood pressure measured with a pediatric sphygmomanometer and stethoscope.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) software, with a second researcher conducting a blind analysis. This researcher was unaware of the group assignments, as the groups were defined in the SPSS program using a different code. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and baseline variables. Chi-squared tests assessed intergroup homogeneity. One-way ANOVA tested between-group differences. Repeated Measures ANOVA analyzed pain, fear, and anxiety across time points. Post-hoc analyses were conducted using Bonferroni correction. Effect sizes were calculated for all ANOVA and repeated measures ANOVA analyses using partial eta squared (η²), in accordance with conventional guidelines. Values of η² were interpreted as small (0.01), medium (0.06), or large (0.14) effects. In addition to the primary analyses, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted for pain, fear, and anxiety outcomes, with baseline pulse and respiratory rate included as covariates, to control for potential confounding effects. Group differences were then reassessed after adjusting for these variables. A significance level of α = 0.05 (two-tailed) was used for all statistical tests.

Results

The participants’ introductory characteristics, including age, gender, history of bloodletting or vascular access within the last 24 h or six months, presence of chronic illness, previous hospitalization, prior experience with distraction methods during invasive procedures, and their reaction to prior procedures, were evaluated to assess baseline homogeneity across groups. No statistically significant differences were found between the groups for these variables (all p >.05), except for a significant difference in the variable of crying during previous procedures, χ²(4, N = 105) = 10.960, p =.027, indicating potential baseline emotional sensitivity in one or more groups. Age did not significantly differ across groups, F(2, 102) = 2.199, p =.116 (see Table 1).

Discussion

This study aimed to analyze the effects of distraction methods on 3- to 7-year-old children’s pain, anxiety, and fear during invasive procedures. In this section, the findings are discussed in relation to the existing literature.

The robustness of our findings was further supported by additional analyses controlling for baseline pulse and respiratory rate, which differed significantly between groups at the start of the study. The ANCOVA results demonstrated that these physiological variables were not significant predictors of pain, fear, or anxiety scores, and the group effect remained statistically significant in all cases. This suggests that the observed benefits of distraction methods, particularly active distraction via video games, were independent of initial physiological differences. By controlling for these covariates, we have strengthened the validity of our conclusions and reduced the likelihood of confounding effects.

The experimental and control groups were compared before, during, and after the procedures. The findings showed that both experimental groups (cartoon and video game) experienced significantly lower pain, fear, and anxiety levels compared to the control group at all measurement points. While both methods were effective, video games showed a longer-lasting effect, particularly in reducing fear and anxiety after the procedure. Although several group differences were statistically significant, it is important to interpret these findings in light of the observed effect sizes. In this study, partial eta squared (η²) values ranged from 0.14 to 0.51, which correspond to medium to large effects according to Cohen’s (1988) benchmarks [31]. These results indicate that the observed differences are not only statistically significant but also likely to be meaningful in clinical practice. However, in cases where smaller effect sizes were found, the clinical impact may be limited despite statistical significance. Therefore, the results should be applied with consideration of both statistical and clinical significance, particularly when adapting these interventions to different clinical settings.

Invasive needle-based medical procedures often trigger fear and anxiety in children, which may lead them to resist the intervention. Cognitive-behavioral treatments (CBT) are commonly used psychological interventions for managing procedural distress. Distraction methods like video games and cartoons, considered behavioral strategies, help shift attention away from the procedure [32]. Supporting the current findings, previous studies have demonstrated the efficacy of distraction techniques. For example, Krishnegowda et al. (2023) reported that cartoon videos effectively reduced perceived pain in preschool-aged children undergoing venipuncture [17]. Similarly, Özkan et al. (2021) found that children watching cartoons during intravenous procedures exhibited reduced anxiety and fear [18]. Additional studies also confirm the effectiveness of both cartoons and video games in lowering pain during venipuncture [33, 34]. These results are consistent with findings from other studies that support the use of both active and passive distraction techniques in pediatric pain management [10, 20, 35–38].

Similarly, the significantly lower fear levels in the cartoon and video game groups before and during the procedure, and the sustained effect of video games afterward, confirm the effectiveness of distraction in managing fear. The literature corroborates these findings. Various distraction techniques have been shown to reduce fear and anxiety in children during invasive procedures [9, 39–42].

In terms of anxiety, both cartoon viewing and video game playing significantly lowered anxiety before and during the procedure, with video games maintaining their effect afterward. A systematic review by Mathias et al. (2022) supports the effectiveness of interactive distraction tools like video games and tablets in alleviating procedural anxiety [13].

Other studies also demonstrate similar outcomes. Mason et al. (1999), MacLaren & Cohen (2005), Bellieni et al. (2006), and James et al. (2012) reported that distraction—whether through storybooks, toys, TV, or cartoons—can effectively reduce children’s anxiety during painful procedures [9, 22, 23, 35].

In conclusion, both distraction methods were found to be rapid and effective in reducing pain, fear, and anxiety in young children. Compared to children who received routine care, those in the experimental groups experienced significantly less distress.

Playing video games resulted in the lowest pain, fear, and anxiety scores across all time points. Although anxiety and fear levels decreased in all groups after the procedure, the video game group showed the most sustained and pronounced reduction. This confirms that active distraction (video games) is more effective than passive distraction (cartoons).

This result is supported by Koller & Goldman (2012), who found in their critical review that active distraction methods reduce distress more effectively than passive ones [21]. Studies comparing active and passive methods [20, 22, 23] consistently show that active engagement—such as playing games—leads to better outcomes in terms of pain, fear, and anxiety reduction.

Primack et al. (2012) also highlighted that video games have a 42% effectiveness rate in pain management and could serve as viable psychological and physical interventions in healthcare [43]. In the current study, video games quickly engaged children’s cognitive and motor functions, distracted them effectively, and prevented focus on the painful procedure.

Our findings are consistent with the conclusions of several high-quality systematic reviews and meta-analyses on distraction for pediatric procedural pain. The most recent Cochrane review Birnie et al., 2018 synthesized evidence from 59 trials involving 5,929 children and adolescents undergoing needle-related procedures, concluding that distraction interventions significantly reduced self-reported pain and distress compared to standard care [44]. By focusing on children aged 3–7 years, our study extends these findings to a younger age group, reinforcing the clinical value of active distraction techniques in pediatric pain management.

In addition to statistical significance, it is important to consider the clinical relevance of the observed differences. In our study, the reductions in pain scores between the experimental and control groups exceeded the minimum clinically important difference (MCID) commonly cited for pediatric self-report pain scales, which is approximately 2 points for both the FPS-R and the Oucher [25, 26]. For example, during the procedure, the mean pain score in the video game group was more than 5 points lower than in the control group, far surpassing the MCID threshold. Similar patterns were observed for fear and anxiety, suggesting that the interventions provided not only statistically significant but also clinically meaningful benefits. The sustained reduction in fear and anxiety in the video game group after the procedure may translate into improved cooperation in future medical encounters, potentially reducing procedural delays and distress for both children and healthcare providers.

Despite the scarcity of studies focusing on 3- to 7-year-olds, this study contributes valuable insights. Assessing abstract emotions like pain and fear in this age group is inherently challenging, and behavioral observations are often necessary. When children are relaxed and less anxious, the procedure is smoother for both parents and nurses. Video games, especially free ones installed on smartphones, offer a practical and low-cost solution for managing distress during procedures.

Additionally, the implementation of distraction interventions such as video games requires some logistical preparation in clinical settings. In this study, the pediatric nurses ensured that devices (tablet or smartphone) were fully charged, the selected games were installed and functioning properly, and the content was age-appropriate and free from violent themes. At least two devices were kept ready for use each day to accommodate consecutive procedures and to avoid delays in case of technical issues. These preparations required minimal additional time in the daily workflow and were easily integrated into routine nursing care. Highlighting these logistical considerations may help other healthcare professionals plan and implement similar interventions effectively in their own settings.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of the study include randomized group assignment, the use of distraction methods suitable for young children, and the application of the same procedures by the same nurse using standardized equipment. The children were on their first day of hospitalization, minimizing previous influences. Moreover, both distraction techniques were practical and inexpensive, and this is the first study in Turkey to evaluate them using tools appropriate for 3- to 7-year-olds.

Limitations include the use of self-reported background data and the inability to standardize exposure duration for video games. Furthermore, no pre-intervention baseline measurement was taken immediately before the distraction started. Although potential confounding variables such as prior experiences and baseline anxiety were recorded, they were not statistically controlled. Another limitation is the use of single-item measures for some variables (e.g., Oucher Pain Scale for pain and Children’s Fear Scale for fear), which may not capture the full complexity of these constructs and could reduce measurement reliability. In addition, the sample included children aged 3–7 years, a relatively wide developmental range, which may have affected comprehension of the instruments and variability in responses. Although all tools were previously validated for the relevant age groups and used according to each child’s developmental level, this factor should be considered when interpreting the findings. Another limitation of this study is that, in accordance with the local standard of care in our country, topical anesthetic creams (e.g., lidocaine) were not used prior to venipuncture or intravenous catheterization. While international guidelines frequently recommend the combined use of topical anesthetic, positioning, and coping strategies to minimize procedural pain, in most public hospitals in Turkey the routine application of topical anesthetic is not feasible due to cost, availability, and workflow constraints. Nevertheless, all participants in our study, including the control group, received the other two core components—appropriate positioning and coping strategies such as parental presence and verbal reassurance—as part of routine nursing practice. Future studies should consider incorporating topical anesthetics when possible to align more closely with international standards.

Although recent procedural exposure (within the last 24 h) was balanced between groups, IV cannulation and phlebotomy were considered equivalent based on prior literature and our clinical experience indicating that these procedures are similar in nature and elicit comparable distress levels in children [3, 44]. We did not specifically compare the distribution of these procedures across groups at baseline, which could be addressed in future studies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that both watching cartoons and playing video games were effective in reducing pain, fear, and anxiety in 3- to 7-year-old children during invasive procedures. Among these, video games, as an active distraction method, proved to be more effective than cartoons, a passive method, with the effect continuing after the procedure.

The use of distraction methods facilitated better communication and cooperation between children, parents, and nurses, while also mitigating the children’s negative feelings such as pain, fear, and anxiety during the procedures. This highlights the efficacy of distraction methods, particularly video games, in managing pediatric anxiety, fear, and pain during invasive procedures.

Given their ease of use, cost-effectiveness, and practicality, we recommend that both cartoons and video games be employed as distraction strategies to alleviate pain, fear, and anxiety in children undergoing invasive procedures. Furthermore, due to their more prolonged effect, video games should be considered as the primary method for this purpose.
